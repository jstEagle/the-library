## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The wight was in the barley again.

Ada Villalobos knew this the way she knew rain coming off the eastern ridge—not by seeing it, but by the way the dogs went quiet and the air took on a taste like old coins. She stood in Marta Okonkwo's doorway with her boots half-laced and her blade still wrapped in oilcloth, and she let herself feel, for exactly three seconds, the particular annoyance of a woman whose evening had plans.

"You're sure it's not just a badger," she said. "Last time you paid me two silver for a badger."

"It spoke to Hallow's boy." Marta's hands were wringing her apron into a rope. "Out by the millrace. Said things to him."

"Badgers don't talk."

"That's what I said." Marta swallowed. "It said his name, Ada. Knew it without being told."

Ada finished lacing her boots. Outside, the valley did what the valley always did at dusk: turned gold, went quiet, pretended nothing larger than a harvest ever happened inside its walls. The mountains closed around them like cupped hands. Somewhere downriver, the ferry lanterns were being lit for the evening crossing, which meant that somewhere downriver, Sefa was lighting them, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with whatever she'd found that morning—a ribbon, a strip of leather, once, memorably, a piece of dried eel—and Ada had promised, out loud, with witnesses, that she would be there before full dark.

"I'll have it done by nightfall," she said.

"Take the long knife too."

"The blade I have is enough."

Marta looked at the oilcloth bundle the way valley folk always looked at it—with the polite discomfort of people who understood that their mercenary kept a weapon that shouldn't work here, and had agreed decades ago not to think about why. The valley had no magic. That was its whole virtue. Magic came up against the ridgelines and slid off like water off waxed canvas, and so the valley had no curses, no hexes, no court sorceries, no gods answering prayers. Just wheat, river, ferry, silence.

Except Ada's blade. God-sworn, her order called it. Sworn to a god who had gone silent the year she was born, in a place where no god could reach anyway. She'd asked about it exactly once, at fourteen, and the answer she'd gotten—*some contracts outlive their purpose, girl*—had been enough for a fourteen-year-old and had curdled slowly ever since.

She wrapped the blade across her back anyway. Habit. Insurance. The weight of a question she carried on her spine.

* * *

The barley field at the valley's edge was the last field before the land started pretending to be somewhere else. Beyond it: scrub, then scree, then the pass that nobody used because the pass went nowhere anyone wanted to be. Ada walked the rows with her hand resting on the wrapped hilt and counted the ways the crop was wrong. Stalks flattened in a circle with no wind to explain it. Dew on everything except one path of dry ground, winding, patient, like something had been walking the same route every night learning the shape of the farm.

Wights were simple work, usually. Dead thing that wouldn't lie down, anchored by an unfinished grievance. You burned the anchor, you spoke the closing words if you knew them, and the dead went back to being respectfully dead. Her order had done it for six generations. It was honest work, as honest as any work was when the pay came from frightened farmers.

She found it standing in the center of the circle, where the barley bent away from it like water around a stone.

It had been a man once. The valley buried its people properly, so it wasn't one of theirs—it must have come over the pass, which was its own wrongness, because nothing came over the pass. Its grave-goods had rotted to lace. Its face had gone the grey of things that have been underwater a long time. And it was standing very still, head tilted, listening the way a dog listens, and Ada felt the hair rise on her arms before she understood why.

Because the valley was silent. That was the rule. Magic couldn't follow past the ridgeline, and a wight was magic the way fire was heat. It should have come apart at the boundary like fog meeting sun. It should not be standing in Marta Okonkwo's barley wearing its grievance like a coat.

Ada drew the blade.

The oilcloth fell away and the steel caught the last light, and along the flat of it, faint as breath on a window, the old script woke up. It always did that near the dead. *Some contracts outlive their purpose.* The hum of it traveled up through her grip into her wrist, familiar as her own pulse. Whatever god had sworn this steel had gone quiet, but quiet wasn't the same as gone. Not yet. Not here.

"Wight," she said, using the formal words, the ones from the order's litany. "Your grievance is heard. Your anchor will burn. Lie down."

The wight turned its head toward her.

And it said, in a voice like silt shifting underwater, like a name spoken by someone who has just remembered it after years:

**"Ada Villalobos."**

The world did not stop. That was the horrible part. The dusk went on being gold, the barley went on whispering, her own heart went on doing its stupid faithful work—but everything after that moment happened on the far side of a line she hadn't known she'd crossed.

Nobody knew her name outside the valley. That wasn't sentiment; it was architecture. The valley kept no records the mountain court could read. Traders came, traded, left. Her own order's rolls were kept in a strongbox with a false bottom, under names that weren't quite true. She had spent twenty-six years being precisely as real as the valley allowed, and the whole arrangement rested on one fact: nothing beyond the ridgeline had ever once looked at her.

Until a dead thing that should not exist here stood in the barley and said her name like it was reading it off a list.

"Wrong girl," she said. Her voice came out steady, which she was grateful for. "Lots of Adas."

The wight drifted a step closer. The barley didn't bend from it now; it bent *toward*, stalks straining like men craning to hear. Up close she could see that its eyes weren't eyes but small grey embers, banked low, the color of a fire that has been deliberately smothered rather than died.

"You are the receipt," it said. "You are the line in the book. You are owed-for and owing. Ada Villalobos, born under the silent year, marked in the ledger before your mother drew breath—"

Ada moved. Training is faster than thought, and thank every indifferent power for that, because her thoughts had scattered like startled birds. She cut the wight through the chest with the full length of the blade, and the script along the flat flared white, and the thing came apart the way smoke comes apart when you clap—but the voice didn't stop with the body. It hung in the air a moment longer, finishing its sentence the way a bell finishes ringing:

"—and the date is tonight, and the hour is coming, and they know, they know, they kno—"

Gone.

Ada stood alone in the ruined circle with her sword humming and her hands, she noticed distantly, shaking. The dew was settling. The first star was out over the eastern ridge, fat and indifferent. Somewhere behind her, downhill, the ferry lanterns glowed on the black ribbon of the river, and one of them was being held by a girl who expected her before full dark.

She could go home. She could wrap the blade, file a report in the strongbox under a name that wasn't quite true, drink the tea Marta would press on her, and tomorrow this would be a story—the talking wight, can you imagine, probably some trick of the pass wind—and in a week it would be smooth as riverstone and just as empty.

Or she could stand here one more minute and admit what the animal part of her already knew: that the wight hadn't been haunting the barley. It had been looking for her. It had crossed the one border in the world that kills its kind, dying by inches the whole way, to deliver a message it could only finish with its death rattle.

*They know.*

Ada sheathed the sword. Then, because the alternative was standing in a field forever, she picked her way down through the barley toward the lights of the ferry, telling herself the whole way down that a date was a date, that dread was a debt collector and could wait its turn like everyone else, that she would sit across from Sefa and eat fried fish and laugh at the right moments and absolutely, definitely, not think about grey ember eyes or ledgers or the sound of her own name in a dead man's mouth.

She was almost convincing.

* * *

Sefa had claimed the end of the dock, the good spot, where the planks met the pilings and you could dangle your feet over water that was more shadow than river. She'd brought fried fish wrapped in cloth, and a bottle of plum wine stolen—borrowed, she'd insist—from her father's good shelf, and she'd worn her hair loose, which was how Ada knew this was A Real Occasion. Sefa's hair was a private country. Most days it stayed behind its borders.

"You're late," Sefa said, without looking up. "I lit all nine lanterns myself. My father says I light them crooked. I light them *characteristically*, which is different."

"A wight delayed me."

"Mm. And yesterday it was a flooded ford, and before that a widow with a haunted churn. Your excuses are getting seasonal." But she scooted over and made room, and Ada sat, and their shoulders touched in the way that was the entire point of shoulders, and the river ran dark and slow beneath them carrying the day away downstream.

For a while neither of them talked. This was the other reason for Sefa, Ada thought—the rare, rationed, dangerous reason. Talking to most people was work. Silence with Sefa was rest. The valley gave you silence whether you wanted it or not; Sefa made it a gift.

"Eel ribbon," Ada said, nodding at her hair. "Bold choice."

"It's silk, actually. It's going to be worth money someday. I'm investing." Sefa passed her the fish. "Marta Okonkwo's boy says the dead thing talked."

Word travels in a valley faster than the river runs. Of course it does.

"Said some things," Ada allowed. "Dead things babble. Air in the lungs."

"What did it say?"

"Barley prices." She bit into the fish. It was perfect and she barely tasted it, which felt like theft.

Sefa studied her sideways with those ferry-keeper's eyes that read currents for a living and had long since learned to read Ada too. "You've got your lying face on."

"This is just my face."

"No, your lying face has a twitch. Right eyebrow. Like the truth is trying to get out through a window." She reached over and tapped the eyebrow in question. "There. See?"

Ada caught her hand and didn't let go. The wine got passed back and forth. The lanterns swung on their posts, throwing nine small warm circles onto the water, and the moon came up the color of a coin rubbed thin, and Ada thought: *this. This is what I'm defending. This exact hour. This dock. This hand.* Whatever was coming over the pass, whatever had spoken her name in the barley—it could wait until morning. She'd make it wait. She was very good at making things wait; she'd been doing it her whole life, sitting on a question shaped like a sword.

"Tell me something true," Sefa said quietly. It was their ritual, older than either of them could say. One asks, the other owes.

Ada looked at the water. "I'm scared," she said, "of something I can't name yet."

The honesty cost her visibly. Sefa didn't gloat, didn't poke. She just leaned her head against Ada's shoulder, and the river kept its counsel, and for one long suspended minute the evening held its shape like a bowl of water held perfectly still.

Then, upstream, the grandmother's door banged open.

Old Yara—Sefa's grandmother, ninety-one years of pure flint, keeper of the family's books and grudges—came down the dock path holding a lamp in one hand and, improbably, a large leather-bound volume in the other. She walked like weather coming. Lantern light jumped across her face.

"There you are," she said, and her voice had no anger in it at all, which was worse. "Both of you. Good. I won't have to say it twice."

"Grandmother—" Sefa started.

Yara set the lamp down on the planks between them and opened the book across her knees. It was old the way bones are old. The cover was cracked leather gone soft as skin, and the pages within were dense with a cramped, careful hand, annotated and cross-referenced like a merchant's ledger, except merchants don't ledger prophecy.

"I was dusting the strongroom," Yara said. "Looking for my mother's wool accounts. Found this instead. Been in that box since before my mother married." She turned pages with a tenderness that contradicted everything else about her. "My mother called it nonsense. Called it her grandmother's foolishness. Kept it anyway. We keep things, in this family. It's our one vice."

"Grandmother, we're—"

"Hush." Yara found the page she wanted and turned the book around and held it into the lamplight. "Look."

Ada looked.

A drawing, ink faded to sepia. A woman seated at the end of a dock, feet dangling over dark water, a second figure beside her whose face was left blank—deliberately, unnervingly blank, just an oval of untouched paper. Nine lanterns. The river. The moon drawn as a coin rubbed thin.

And beneath it, in that same cramped hand, a line of text. Old-fashioned spelling. A date.

Tonight's date.

"The woman is you," Yara said. Not a question. Ninety-one years of reading currents, and she read Ada's face now the way she'd read the river all her life. "Down to the way you're sitting. Down to the lanterns. Drawn before my grandmother could walk. And the date, child. Read the date."

Ada read the date. Her mouth had gone dry as August.

"It's coincidence," Sefa said, but her hand had found Ada's and gripped hard. "It's a trick of likeness. Grandmother, it's—"

"The page next to it," Yara said. "Read the page next to it."

Ada turned the leaf with fingers that didn't feel like hers. The next page was mostly a drawing too: a field of grain, a circle trampled in it, and a single figure standing at the center with a long blade, rendered in quick confident strokes. Above the figure, the artist had written a name.

Her name. Spelled correctly. In ink a century old.

The river kept moving. The lanterns kept swinging. Somewhere in the dark beyond the ridgeline, the mountains held their silence like a held breath, and Ada Villalobos sat on a dock she'd sat on a hundred times and understood, with terrible clarity, that the world outside had known her name for a hundred years—that it had written her here, in this spot, on this night, like an appointment kept by history itself.

The wight hadn't been wrong. It hadn't even been strange. It had simply read the same book everyone else apparently had.

"Where did this come from?" Ada heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away. "Who wrote it?"

"My grandmother's grandmother never said." Yara closed the book gently, like shutting a door on something sleeping. "But there's a mark on the flyleaf. A scribe's mark. Court work, if I know my marks—and I do, my girl, I was a keeper of records myself before the valley took me in." She looked at Ada with something that was not pity and not fear but some third, worse thing. "Whatever's written in this book, it was written *beneath the mountain*. By their hand. About you."

Sefa's grip tightened. "We burn it," she said fiercely. "We burn the whole thing. If it's not written, it's not true. That's how prophecy works, isn't it? Everyone says so."

So they burned it—there on the dock, in the lamp, feeding the old pages to flame one at a time while the river ran black below. Prophecy curled and blackened and went up as sparks over the water. The drawing of the dock. The drawing of the field. Page after page, a century of somebody else's handwriting about Ada's life, given to the fire. It should have felt freeing. It felt like tearing a bandage off a wound she couldn't see.

The last page—the one with her name above the swordsman—caught, glowed, and collapsed into ash.

And the ash, lifting on the river breeze, drifting up off the dock in a pale spiral, spelled her name in the air.

*Ada.*

Held there for three full seconds, legible, glowing faintly like the embers of the wight's eyes.

Then it scattered over the water, and the night took it back, and the only sound in the valley was the river, moving the way rivers do, like something that knows exactly where it's going.
