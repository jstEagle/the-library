## 2. A Date Written a Century Ago

The ferryman's daughter kissed like she was arguing with you.

That was the thing Ada liked best about Sefa, if she was being honest with herself, which she tried to avoid doing on principle. Sefa did everything like it was a negotiation she intended to win. The kiss at the top of the ferry dock had been less a kiss than a claim staked—hands on Ada's collar, mouth insistent, a small sound in the back of her throat that said *this is happening whether you're ready or not.*

Ada had not been ready. She'd been ready for wights, for bandits, for the kind of drunk miner who mistook a mercenary's silence for an invitation. She had not been ready for this, and the not-ready feeling sat in her chest all through dinner like something swallowed wrong.

They ate at the Lamplight, the only inn in the valley that bothered with candles on tables, because the ferry trade brought people through who expected such things. Sefa had stolen two candles from her father's stores and set them between them like she was laying out terms.

"You're staring," Sefa said.

"I'm assessing."

"Assessing what?"

"Whether this is a mistake."

Sefa grinned. She had a chipped front tooth from a rope swing gone wrong at eleven, and she deployed it now like artillery. "And? Verdict?"

"Inconclusive." Ada tore bread, dipped it in the fish stew, kept her hands busy because her hands wanted very badly to reach across the table and do something stupid. "Ask me again at the end of the night."

"I'll ask you again at the end of the *year*," Sefa said. "I'm planning ahead. I'm told that frightens mercenaries."

"It does."

"Good."

The valley outside the window had gone blue with evening. From here you could see the whole shape of the place—the bowl of it, ringed by ridgelines, the river cutting through the middle like someone had dragged a knife through green cloth. Somewhere up there past the north ridge was the spot where her blade still hummed when she held it over the boundary stones. Somewhere beyond that was everything else: the court beneath the mountain, the roads, the world where magic worked and people's names could be used against them.

Her whole life, Ada had thought of the valley as a wall. Tonight, for the first time, it felt more like a bowl turned upside down over her head, and she couldn't have said why.

"Sefa." A woman's voice, thin and cracked as old paper. "Sefa, child, is that your father's boat I saw tied crooked at the—"

Grandmother Ilsa came around the corner of the stairwell with a candle of her own and stopped like she'd walked into glass.

She was a small woman, folded up into herself the way old people get when they've spent decades making themselves smaller to fit into rooms built for others. Her shawl was grey. Her hands were spotted. Her eyes went from Sefa to Ada and stuck there, and Ada watched the color leave her face in real time, watched it drain down through her neck and into her shawl and pool somewhere below the floorboards.

"Grandmother?" Sefa half-rose. "What's wrong?"

Ilsa didn't answer her. She took one step closer, candle trembling, and looked at Ada the way people look at a grave they've just realized belongs to them.

"I know you," she whispered.

"No, ma'am." Ada kept her voice easy. It was a skill, keeping your voice easy; she'd been practicing it since before she could hold a blade properly. "You've got me confused with—"

"The book." Ilsa's free hand flew up to her mouth. "*The book.*"

"Grandmother, sit down, you're white as—"

"My book." The old woman wasn't listening. She never listened, Sefa would tell her later; nobody had ever successfully interrupted Grandmother Ilsa once she'd gotten hold of a thread. She shuffled to the corner of the room where the family's things were piled—a chest, a stack of quilts, a shelf of chipped crockery—and dug beneath the quilts with hands that shook so hard the candle guttered. She came up with a book.

It was small. That was the first thing Ada noticed, and the first thing she'd remember after: how small it was. Leather gone soft and dark with age, corners rounded from handling, no title on the cover, nothing but a faint stamped pattern that might once have been a crown or might once have been a flower. It fit in the old woman's palms like a hymnal.

"This was my mother's," Ilsa said. "And hers before her. Nobody reads it. It's nonsense, mostly, recipes and—" She stopped. She was looking at Ada again. "Sit still, girl."

"I haven't moved."

"Sit *still*."

Ada sat still. It occurred to her distantly that she could take the book away from the old woman without effort, that she could be out the door and three streets gone before anyone finished standing up, and it also occurred to her that she didn't move, and she filed that fact away to examine later, or possibly never.

Ilsa opened the book with a care that bordered on liturgy. Pages whispered past. Recipes, truly—cures for colic, charms against weevils, the sort of domestic nonsense every farmhouse keeps and no farmhouse believes. Then the pages changed. The handwriting changed. And the old woman's breathing changed.

"Here," she breathed. "Here, here, here."

She turned the book around and set it on the table between the candles, and Ada looked down at a page inked a hundred years dead, and the world went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with gods.

There was a drawing.

It was crude, the work of someone who drew rarely and reluctantly, but it was unmistakable. A woman's face, seen straight-on. Strong jaw. A scar through the left eyebrow—hers was newer, thinner, but it sat in exactly the same place. Hair bound back for practicality. Eyes that even in faded brown ink seemed to be looking directly out of the page at whoever dared to look back.

Below the face, in careful script:

*Tonight. The ferryman's daughter. The inn with two candles.*

Tonight.

Ada read it twice. Three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into sense. They stayed exactly what they were: her own face, tonight's date, and Sefa's name, written by a hand that had crumbled to dust before Ada's grandmother learned to walk.

"That's not me," she said. Her voice came out flat and reasonable and utterly unconvincing. "It's some ancestor. Some relative. People look alike."

"Villalobos," Ilsa said. "Is your name Villalobos?"

The candles flickered.

"How do you know my—"

"It says it." The old woman traced the line beneath the drawing with one trembling finger. "Right under the daughter's name. *The mercenary Villalobos.* My mother swore the book was written by a madwoman who claimed she dreamed things before they happened, but she wrote the dates down, see, and the dates always—" She stopped herself, and something worse than fear crossed her face. "The dates always come true."

"Then it's coincidence." Ada pushed her chair back and stood, and the movement was too fast, too sharp, the movement of a woman whose body had decided to flee while her mind was still drafting denials. "A hundred years of entries, you'll find any face in any crowd if you squint hard enough—"

"Read the rest of the entry," Sefa said quietly.

Ada hadn't heard her move either. Sefa stood beside her grandmother now, one hand on the old woman's shoulder, and she was looking at the page with an expression Ada had never seen on her—not the grin, not the argument, something older and sadder, something that belonged to the book and not the girl.

"There's more," Sefa said. "Under the drawing."

There was more. Two lines, in smaller script, cramped against the bottom edge as though the writer had been running out of paper or courage:

*She will burn this page. It will not matter.*

Ada stared at it. Her skin had gone cold from the inside out, some deep animal part of her understanding before the rest of her did, and she hated that part, she hated it with a violence that surprised her, because that part already knew what she was going to do next.

Because that was the thing. She was going to do it. She could feel the decision already made somewhere below her thinking mind, the way you can feel a storm coming through the ache in old scars.

"It's a trick," she said. "Someone wrote this to spook—"

"Who?" Sefa asked gently. "A hundred years ago? Who knew I'd exist? Who knew *you'd* exist? Who knew about the candles, Ada? I stole these tonight. I stole them from Da's stores *tonight.*"

"Then it's luck. Guessing. You write enough prophecies, one lands—"

"And the burning?"

"What burning?"

Sefa pointed at the last line, and her finger wasn't steady either. "*She will burn this page. It will not matter.* It knows what you're going to do right now. It knows what you're going to do before you know it yourself. So do us both a favor, Ada, and don't—"

Ada took the page by its corner and held it to the candle flame.

She told herself afterward, for a long time afterward, that she hadn't decided. That her hand simply moved. But the truth was uglier and simpler: she decided, quickly, the way she decided in fights, and she chose fire because fire was the only answer she'd ever trusted. Names could be forged. Ink could lie. Fire didn't negotiate.

The old paper caught fast, curling orange at the edges, and Ilsa cried out and Sefa reached for her wrist and missed, and Ada held the burning page over the stew pot until the flame ate down through the prophecy and the drawing and her own face and the date and the terrible little line about the burning, held it until there was nothing left to hold, until the last scrap went black and crumbled into the broth.

Silence.

For three heartbeats, actual silence. Then Ilsa began to pray, low and continuous, the way other people wept.

"Get the ash," Sefa said.

"What?"

"Get the ash out of the stew. If there's anything—if we need to see—"

"There's nothing to see. It's ash. It's soup now." But Ada was already fishing a spoon from the table, because some traitor part of her needed to look, needed to disprove it, needed the mundane victory of soggy cinders and nothing else.

She skimmed the surface of the stew. Grey flakes clung to the spoon. She tipped them onto the tabletop, into the candlelight, and swept them together with the side of her hand into a small dark pile, and she was already composing the sentence she'd say—*see, ash, just ash, I told you*—when the pile shifted.

No wind. No draft. The Lamplight's door was shut, the windows sealed against evening. The little heap of grey simply moved, the way iron filings move near a lodestone, particles crawling over particles, arranging themselves, and Ada watched her own denial die in her throat as the ash crawled into lines.

Two words. Small ones. Neat ones. Written in cinders on the wood of the table in a hand she was beginning to know intimately, the way you come to know the handwriting on a warrant:

*villalobos*

And beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic:

*tomorrow*

The candle flames bent sideways, all four of them, all at once, toward the north ridge. Toward the mountain. Toward whatever lay on the other side of the boundary stones where her blade hummed and her god kept his long, patient silence.

Nobody spoke. Outside, far off, a dog started barking, then stopped abruptly, as if something had told it to.

"Well," Ada said at last, and her voice was steady, and she was proud of it, and the pride was the last piece of the old life she had left. "I should go."

"Ada." Sefa caught her arm at the door. Her grip was strong, ferry-girl strong, rope-and-oar strong, and her eyes were wet and furious and bright. "Tomorrow what? What happens tomorrow?"

"I don't know." And that much, at least, was honest.

Behind them, Grandmother Ilsa had stopped praying. She was looking at the ash-written name on her table with an expression Ada would carry out of the valley and under the mountain and all the way to the end of everything—an expression not of fear but of recognition, the look of a woman reading the first line of a letter she'd known was coming her whole life.

"Child," the old woman said softly, to Ada's back, at the threshold. "My mother's book has an entry for tomorrow too. I never showed you that one."

Ada stopped in the doorway. The night air came in cool off the river, carrying woodsmoke and water and the faint mineral smell of the mountain, and somewhere high above the valley, past the ridgeline, lightning moved inside a cloudless sky.

"What does it say?" Ada asked. She didn't turn around. She wasn't sure she could.

Ilsa turned the pages. Once. Twice. Then the old woman went very still, in the way of someone who has found a page that shouldn't exist.

"It doesn't say anything," she said. "It's blank. Every page after tonight is blank, except—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—except the last one. And child, the last page isn't written in ink."

* * *

The dog began barking again, farther off now, and wouldn't stop.

Ada stepped out into the dark, and the door closed behind her, and the valley that had held her safe for twenty-six years felt, for the first time, like a held breath.
