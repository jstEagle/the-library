## 3. The Scribe Who Fell Downhill

Tam had always believed that falling was, in its way, a form of writing.

You committed yourself to the slope. You surrendered your body to the grammar of gravity. And then—this was the part he'd never appreciated from behind a desk—you found out whether the sentence ended in you or not.

This one, apparently, did not.

He came to rest in a tangle of gorse at the bottom of the hill, upside down, one boot missing, the prophecy book still clutched against his chest because some animal instinct older than literacy had decided that if Tam was going to die, he was going to die holding the single most dangerous object beneath the mountain. Above him, the ridgeline stood black and clean against the stars. Somewhere up there were riders. Somewhere up there were torches.

Somewhere up there, very soon now, would be questions.

"Right," Tam said to the gorse. His voice came out as a whisper, which was appropriate; he'd spent eleven years being paid by the word, and he knew when to spend them cheaply. "That went well."

He assessed himself the way he assessed any document: for damage, for legibility, for signs of forgery. Left ankle: wrong. Ribs on the right side: also wrong, though in a more negotiable way. Hands: scraped raw, ink-stained, still attached. Face: presumably intact, since it hurt everywhere equally.

And the book—the book was fine. Of course it was fine. It had survived a hundred years of neglect in the court's deepest archive, and it could certainly survive one disgraced scribe rolling downhill with it like a man hugging a stove.

He got his feet under him, mostly. The missing boot was a problem. The valley below him was a bigger problem, but of a different kind, because the valley below him was where he needed to go.

The Silent Valley. That's what the maps in the court called it, when they deigned to call it anything at all—a blank space near the southern border, annotated in the Regent's own hand: *Nothing of interest. Nothing can enter. Let it be.* Tam had copied that annotation forty times over the years, in fair hand, without once asking what *it* was. That was the job. You didn't ask what *it* was. You asked how many copies, on what paper, sealed with whose wax.

Then he'd been assigned to re-catalogue the Archive of Quiet Things, and he'd opened a ledger bound in grey leather, and he'd read an entry dated a hundred years past that described—in precise, bureaucratic detail—a woman's face and tonight's date, and he had made the first mistake of his career.

He hadn't reported it.

Everything after that was just consequences wearing different coats.

He limped downhill toward the valley edge, and as he walked, he felt the strangest thing happen: the ache in his ribs began to fade. Not heal—he could tell the difference between healing and fading—but *fade*, like a sound getting quieter, like a name being said fewer and fewer times until no one remembers saying it. By the time he reached the tree line at the valley's border, his ankle worked again. His ribs merely complained instead of screaming.

Magic couldn't cross into the valley. Everyone beneath the mountain knew it. But nobody had ever explained what happened to magic that *tried*—and now Tam knew. It forgot itself at the border. It walked in wearing a borrowed shape and left its power at the treeline like a coat check ticket.

Which meant that inside this valley, he was safe from everything except the ordinary.

Given his week, ordinary sounded like a luxury.

* * *

He found the village by smell before sight—woodsmoke and river damp and, underneath it, bread. He followed the smell down a lane between low stone houses, and it was only when a dog started barking at him with the flat, professional hostility of a dog that has seen trouble before, that Tam realized what he must look like.

One boot. Court clothes, but the good ones, ruined—grey silk gone greyer with mud and pine sap, the scribe's silver clasp torn from his collar somewhere on the ridge. A man half-carried, half-dragged by exhaustion. A stranger with a foreign accent and a bundle held against his chest like a stolen baby.

Because it was a stolen baby. Sort of. Inasmuch as a hundred-year-old book of prophecies could be a baby.

A door opened ahead of him. Light spilled out, yellow and warm, and in it stood a broad woman with flour on her forearms and an expression Tam recognized instantly, because he'd seen it on every customs clerk beneath the mountain: the expression of someone deciding, quickly, how much of a problem you are.

"You'll be wanting the ferryman's, then," she said, before he could speak. "Whatever you are."

"I—yes? Is that—" He gestured vaguely downstream.

"Follow the river. Don't touch my fence." The door closed.

Tam stood in the lane and felt something in his chest loosen that had nothing to do with bruises. She hadn't asked who he was. She hadn't looked at his hands for ink, or at his collarbone for the brand the court gave its archivists. She had looked at him the way people in the valley apparently looked at all strangers: as weather. To be waited out, not interrogated.

Eleven years of living under a government that catalogued every soul in its keeping, and here was a place where a bleeding man in the lane got directions and a warning about fences.

He was going to love it here, assuming he lived out the night.

* * *

The ferryman's house sat where the river bent, lantern-lit, with boats pulled up along the bank like sleeping animals. A girl answered the door—Sefa, he'd learn her name later, the ferryman's daughter, with quick dark eyes and flour on her too, because apparently everyone in this valley baked at night—and she took one look at him and shouted back into the house, "Gran! It's a rich one!"

"That's not usually the problem with rich ones," Tam managed.

An old woman appeared behind her, small and bent, with eyes that had clearly done their share of work. She looked at Tam for a long moment. Then at the bundle in his arms. Then back at Tam.

"He's court," the old woman said.

"I'm not," Tam said, "anything, anymore. I'm—I was a scribe. I'm not even that. Please. I need sanctuary. Just for the night. Just until—" Until what? Until the riders gave up? Riders didn't give up. Riders were given quotas. "Just for the night."

The old woman's mouth went thin. "Court's got no business in this valley. Never has."

"And I'm counting on that," Tam said, "with everything I have left, which is one boot, a broken rib, and a book."

"What book?"

And there it was—the question he'd dreaded since the ridge, arriving exactly as fast as he'd known it would. Because he could lie. Lying was practically his native language; he'd written lies in triplicate for a decade, beautifully formatted, properly witnessed. But he was standing in a doorway at the edge of the only place on earth the court's long hands couldn't reach, and if he lied here, in this one place, he suspected he'd lose something he couldn't re-file.

"It's a book of prophecies," he said. "The original. And there's a page in it about your valley. About—" He hesitated. "About a person. Someone who lives here. Or will. Or does."

The old woman's face did something complicated. The girl, Sefa, leaned around her grandmother's shoulder, suddenly very interested.

"A person," the old woman said.

"The court calls them the Unwritten One." He heard himself say it and flinched. It sounded worse aloud. It sounded like a title, and titles beneath the mountain were things that got carved on doors, and doors that got titles got guards, and guards got orders. "It means someone the prophecies missed. Someone who isn't in any ledger, isn't in any record. The court keeps records of everything, madam. Everything. Every birth, every debt, every soul, every year of every life. And this valley—it's blank. A century of blank pages. Except for one entry, and the entry is about one of yours."

Silence, except the river.

"Why would they want someone who isn't written?" Sefa asked. She was younger than Tam had first thought. Sharp, though. He filed that away—old habit, filing people away, and he wondered briefly when he'd stop doing it.

"Because the court is afraid of blank pages," Tam said. "Blank pages are where amendments go."

The old woman studied him for another long moment. Whatever she saw, it made her step aside.

"You can sleep in the boatshed," she said. "There's a blanket that smells of fish. If riders come down that ridge, you'll leave by the river before they reach the bend, and you'll never have been here. Do we understand each other?"

"Madam," Tam said, with his whole chest, "we understand each other perfectly."

"Sefa. Get him the blanket. And feed him, gods, look at him." She paused in the doorway, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "We don't turn people away in this valley. That's not the same as trusting them."

"I wouldn't dream of confusing the two," Tam said. "I've met trust. We're not on speaking terms."

Something that might have been the ghost of amusement crossed her face, and then the door shut, and Tam stood alone in a strange yard with a fishy blanket coming and the stars overhead doing their indifferent, ancient work.

He laughed. It hurt his ribs, so it came out as more of a wheeze, but it was definitely laughter. Desperate charm, his mother used to call it—his mother, whose family name was currently three ridges and a lifetime away, attached to records that no longer existed, because Tam had burned his own file on the way out of the archive and discovered that burning your own file feels exactly like burning your hand.

But he was here. He was under the treeline, past the border where magic forgot itself, in a valley of bakers and ferrymen who treated strangers like weather. And tomorrow—

Tomorrow he'd have to find the Unwritten One before the court did. Tomorrow he'd have to convince a total stranger that their entire life was an entry in a book they'd never read. Tomorrow, if he was honest, which he was trying to be lately, out of sheer novelty—

Tomorrow was going to be a disaster.

But tonight there was a boatshed, and a blanket, and bread with honey that Sefa brought out without a word, and for the first time in eleven years Tam fell asleep not knowing what tomorrow's paperwork would say, and finding, to his enormous surprise, that he didn't mind.

* * *

He woke to voices.

Not angry voices—that was the first thing his body catalogued, before his brain was even fully aboard. Angry voices beneath the mountain came with boots and bells. These were morning voices, low and easy, the river talking underneath them.

He lay in the boatshed's dark and listened, because listening was the one skill they'd never managed to train out of him. A man's voice, rough and unhurried. A girl's—Sefa, bright with something between excitement and worry. And threaded through both, a name:

"—Ada. Ada, I'm telling you, Gran says—"

Tam sat up too fast and his ribs reminded him of their position on the matter.

*Ada.*

He knew that name. He knew it the way you know a word you've written ten thousand times without ever once saying it aloud—an entry in a ledger he'd helped maintain, a name attached to a blank page, a contract signed a century ago by hands long dead. *A soul, unnamed, sold twice.* He'd read the entry four nights ago by lamplight in a forbidden archive, and he'd told himself it was poetry, or corruption, or a clerical error, because the alternative was that it was a schedule.

And now here was a girl in the yard saying it like it belonged to someone real. Someone who took contracts. Someone who, according to Sefa, had been at the valley's edge last night hunting a wight, and had come back quiet, and hadn't slept.

The Unwritten One wasn't a metaphor. She was a mercenary with a god-sworn blade, and she was twenty yards away, and the most powerful bureaucracy in the world was sending riders down the mountain to collect her.

Tam gathered the book, tucked it under his arm, and pushed open the boatshed door into the cold gold of morning.

Ada Villalobos turned to look at him—wary, tired, sharp-eyed, exactly the kind of person who'd survive being a prophecy—and Tam smiled the smile that had gotten him through a decade of hostile audits.

"Good morning," he said. "I have terrible news, and I suspect it's about you."
