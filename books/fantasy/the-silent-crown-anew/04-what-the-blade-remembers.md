## 4. What the Blade Remembers

The barn smelled of hay and old smoke, and Tam Vessel sat in the corner of it like a man who had been folded there by someone stronger.

Ada had tied him to a support post with the same rope she used for hauling carcasses off the ridge. He'd complained about it twice, then discovered that complaining made her look at him, and had apparently decided silence was the better bargain. Now he just watched her work — sharpening the wight-blade on a whetstone, slow and even, the way her mother had taught her before her mother had taught her anything else worth knowing.

"You know," he said finally, "in the court we had instruments for this. Truth-lathes. Memory-pliers. Very humane, very efficient."

"Did they work?"

"Terribly. That's why I falsified records instead." He shifted against the post. "Rope's fine. Rope is honest. I respect rope."

Ada didn't answer. The whetstone whispered along the steel. Outside, the valley was doing what the valley always did at night: settling into itself, the river muttering, the mountain holding its breath above them all. Somewhere out there, court riders were presumably still looking for this man, and somewhere closer than that, Sefa was asleep in her father's ferry-house, and Ada was going to have to decide, before dawn, which of those facts mattered more.

"Ask me your questions," Tam said. "You've been rehearsing them for an hour. I can hear you rehearsing. It's very loud."

"It's a quiet rehearsal."

"That's how I know you're good at it."

She set the blade across her knees and looked at him properly for the first time since dragging him out of the gully. In the firelight he looked less like a fugitive and more like what he probably was: a clerk. Ink-stained fingers, ink-stained cuffs, a face built for sitting indoors and being underestimated. His eyes were the problem. Clerk's hands, but a witness's eyes.

"My name," Ada said. "In the book. Tell me exactly what it said."

Tam exhaled through his nose. "Villalobos, Ada. Born under the Silent God's sign, in the valley where magic cannot follow. And then a date." He paused. "Tonight's date. Last night's, now."

"And the rest of the page?"

"Mostly bookkeeping." He said it apologetically, as if apologizing would help. "That's what nobody understands about prophecy, down here. It's not poetry. It's an accounting. Every line is a transaction. *On this date, this thing happens, because this other thing was bought or sold.* Your name was in a column, mercenary. Not a poem. A ledger."

"What column?"

"The one marked *due*."

The whetstone had stopped moving. Ada noticed she was gripping it hard enough to hurt and made herself let go, finger by finger.

"Start earlier," she said. "Start with why a court scribe runs downhill into a magic-dead valley carrying a hundred-year-old book. Start with what my order actually is."

"Your order." Tam smiled without any humor in it at all. "The Company of the Quiet Blade. That's what you call yourselves. Do you know what the founding charter says? The actual charter, not the version they teach recruits over ale?"

"We guard the valley."

"You guard *a thing in* the valley. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole reason I'm bleeding in your barn." He leaned forward as far as the rope allowed, and his voice dropped, went quick and precise, the voice of a man reading from memory because the original was a hundred miles underground. "*Let there be kept, in the place where magic cannot follow, a watch of sworn steel, until such time as the account is settled.* That's the first sentence. Not *guard the people*. Not *guard the peace*. Guard. Until. Settled."

"Settled how?"

"I don't know. That page was redacted before my grandmother's grandmother learned her letters. But I know who wrote the charter." Tam's eyes were steady on hers now, and Ada understood suddenly that everything he'd said so far had been the warm-up. "The court wrote it. The court beneath the mountain founded your order, Ada Villalobos. You aren't rebels keeping the crown out. You're the crown's own lock, posted at its own door, and nobody gave you the key."

The fire cracked. Out in the dark, a dog barked twice at nothing and thought better of it.

"That's a lie," Ada said.

"Yes," Tam agreed pleasantly. "It probably sounds like one. Which brings us to the interesting part."

He nodded at the blade across her knees.

"Every scribe in the court learns truth-sense before he learns his letters. We're tested on it yearly. I can feel a lie the way you feel weather coming — pressure, a change in the air. It's not a gift. It's training and a little borrowed craft, and it has never once been wrong about me, which is how I ended up running downhill with a stolen book." He tilted his head. "So. Ask me again. The charter question. And watch the blade while you do."

Ada looked down at the wight-blade. God-sworn steel, the only metal that held an edge past the valley's edge, quenched in rites so old the rites' meaning had worn smooth as a river stone. Her mother's blade before it was hers. Her grandmother's before that. It had never done anything strange in its life except kill things that needed killing.

"Say the charter again," she said. "All of it."

"*Let there be kept, in the place where magic cannot follow, a watch of sworn steel, until such time as the account is settled.*"

The blade hummed.

Not rang — hummed. A low, continuous note, felt in the knees and the back teeth more than heard, like a struck bell buried under the floor of the world. The firelight bent toward it slightly, or Ada's eyes did, and along the flat of the steel a faint grey shimmer moved like heat-haze in winter.

Tam nodded slowly, watching her face. "There it is."

"What is it?" Her own voice sounded far away. "What did it do?"

"Confirmed me. The blade's god-sworn, and god-sworn things hate lies the way iron hates rot." He paused. "Which raises a question neither of us wants to ask, so I'll ask it. If your blade can hear truth — why did it stay silent every time someone told you your order guarded the valley? Why did it wait for *me*, a stranger, a criminal, to tell you the truth before it sang?"

Ada didn't answer. She ran the question backward through her childhood the way you'd run a blade backward through cloth, and everything snagged. The oath-taking at fourteen, the master-at-arms saying *the valley is the charge, the valley is the charge*, the way no one had ever been able to tell her what the watch was *for*, only what it did. She'd assumed the answer was lost. Old orders lose their reasons; it happens; the sword remains.

But the blade hadn't lost anything. Blades don't forget. Blades are sworn.

"Say something false," she said quietly.

"I embezzled from the court treasury."

Nothing. The blade lay silent and dead as any other sword.

"Now true."

"I falsified records to protect a name in a ledger."

The hum came up through the dirt floor, through her boots, through her bones.

Ada set the blade down on the straw between them, carefully, the way you'd put down something that had started talking. Then she stood and walked to the barn door and stood in it, because if she didn't move she was going to do something with her hands and the man was tied up and it would be a waste of rope.

Behind her, Tam said nothing. She gave him credit for that.

"My god went silent the year I was born," she said to the dark. "Everyone knows that story. The Silent God turned his face away. The prayers stopped landing. The dragon-fire — whatever that is — guttered low, and the voice went out of the world, and the valley became what it is. Dead ground. Safe ground."

"Yes."

"It's a nice story. It has a shape. Things go quiet, people adapt, the watch keeps watch." She turned around. "Tell me the story with the ledger in it."

Tam looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him drop the charm entirely. What was left underneath was younger and much more frightened.

"The year you were born," he said, "the Silent God didn't turn away. The Silent God *paid*. Something was owed beneath the mountain — something enormous, something that had been accruing for generations — and the god settled the debt the only way it could. With its voice. Its speech. Its *self*. It went silent the way a man goes silent when he's sold his tongue." He wet his lips. "And the payment wasn't abstract, mercenary. Debts in the court are paid in specific coin. The ledger lists what was handed over. I read the entry a hundred times before I stole the book, trying to make it mean something else."

"Read it to me anyway."

"'One soul, unnamed, sold twice,'" Tam quoted, soft and exact. "'Bought back by the Silent God at the price of voice. Held in trust. The vessel will come to claim it when the fire fails.'"

The barn was very quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere in the loft, mice went about their small indifferent business, and Ada stood among all of it feeling the floor tilt under a life she had walked on for twenty-five years.

"Unnamed," she repeated.

"Your parents weren't the sellers. I want that to be clear — the sale predates them, maybe by a generation, maybe more. Souls get sold forward in that place. Grandparents sign, grandchildren pay." He shrugged, helpless and furious at once, a clerk enraged by bad bookkeeping. "You were born already owed, Ada. Born into a valley chosen *because* magic can't follow, raised by an order founded *because* the door needed locking, given a god-sworn blade *because* someday the receipt would need to walk out and collect." He shook his head slowly. "You weren't hidden from the world. You were *stored*."

The word landed harder than any of the others. Stored. Like grain. Like coin in a drawer. All those years of restless contentment — the contracts, the ridge-walks, the rare bright evenings with Sefa — she had thought she was living in a place. She had been living in a *container*.

She picked up the blade. It had gone quiet again, patient as ever, and she looked down its length and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite grief but used both their voices.

"I prayed to him," she said. "When I was small. Everyone does, even knowing he won't answer. You pray so he knows you're loyal." Her thumb moved along the blade's spine. "He wasn't ignoring us. He couldn't answer. He'd already spent his mouth buying back—" She stopped. "Buying back me."

"Yes."

"And the watch. My teachers. My mother." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "They knew. Or some of them did. They taught me footwork and prayer and never once said *you are the thing in the box*."

"Some of them knew. Some of them only knew to keep the routine. Courts work by cutting knowledge into pieces too small to be dangerous." Tam's voice gentled, which was worse than the facts had been. "Your mother may genuinely not have known what she was guarding. Only that she was guarding."

"That's not mercy. That's just a smaller lie."

"No," Tam agreed. "It isn't mercy. It's bookkeeping."

Ada sat down across from him, laid the blade flat on her knees again, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The fire burned down to a red argument with itself. She thought about the wight at the valley's edge saying her name in its dead man's voice — the first crack — and the ash spelling *VILLALOBOS* on her palm — the second — and understood now that there had never been a first crack at all. The whole vessel had been made with the fracture in it, drawn on in invisible ink a century ago, waiting for tonight's date to come due.

"If I burn the book," she said at last. "If I give you back to the riders and go back to my contracts and my—"

"—your ferryman's daughter," Tam said, and had the grace to flinch when she looked at him. "I read the ledger. It's thorough."

"If I do all that. Does it stop?"

Tam was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he didn't perform it, didn't soften it, and she was grateful and hated him for it in equal measure.

"The entry is dated. Tonight. Prophecy in that book isn't fortune-telling, it's *scheduling*. The transaction comes due whether or not you show up to acknowledge it. The riders crossing the ridgeline today — that wasn't chance. The valley's protection was anonymity, Ada. The world knew where you were stored, and now the world knows the storage has a name and a face and a girl who walks down to the river in the evening." He spread his ink-stained hands. "Staying doesn't protect anyone. Staying just means the collection happens here, among people who can't fight it."

Outside, far off on the eastern ridgeline, something glinted. Once. Twice. Lantern-light, or moonlight on armor, too regular to be stars.

Ada looked at it for a long time. Then she looked down at the blade, and — not knowing why, except that the night had already broken every rule she'd grown up trusting — she lifted it and asked it, silently, the only question left.

*Is he telling the truth?*

The hum rose through her grip, warm and low and certain, and for one absurd moment it sounded almost like an apology.

Tam watched her face. "Well?" he asked. "Do you believe me now?"

Before she could answer, the dog started barking again — not at nothing this time — and out on the eastern ridge, the lights began to move downhill.
