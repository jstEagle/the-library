## 5. Riders on the Ridgeline

Tam had spent eleven years learning how a lie moved through paperwork. It moved slowly, he'd told Ada by firelight, and predictably, like water finding a crack. First a clerk noticed the discrepancy. Then a supervisor. Then an inquiry was opened, sealed, forgotten—unless someone above wanted it remembered.

He had falsified the birth records of forty-one children over six years. He had doctored toll receipts, soul-manifest ledgers, and one entire census of the Lower Terraces. He had been careful the way only a frightened man can be careful.

And still, on his third morning in the valley, sitting on Ada's fence post eating bread that tasted of woodsmoke and honesty, he felt the crack open beneath him.

The birds went first.

Not quiet—gone. One moment the valley was doing its ordinary morning business of sparrows and complaints from the mill, and the next every winged thing lifted off the eastern ridgeline at once, a dark sheet of them rising like a page being turned.

Ada came out of her house with her blade already half-drawn. She hadn't even finished lacing her boots. "That's not weather," she said.

"No," Tam said. His mouth had gone dry in a way that no amount of river water would fix. "That's the court."

"You said they couldn't cross."

"I said the valley kept magic out." He set down the bread. His hands were steady, which surprised him; everything else in him was screaming. "I never asked what happens when the valley itself starts to fray. And something here *is* fraying. Something with your name on it."

Ada looked east. The morning light lay flat and gold along the ridgeline, and for a long moment there was nothing there at all—just grass, just rock, just the ordinary world.

Then the grass began to move against the wind.

* * *

There were seven of them. Tam counted twice because the first count seemed impossible and the second count was worse.

They rode horses that were wrong in the way court things are wrong: too smooth, too silent, their hooves leaving prints in the turf but no sound, as if the ground had agreed not to notice them. Their armor was the grey-black of the Deep Archive, lacquered so it drank the light. And around each rider's throat hung a small brass badge stamped with the Regent's seal—a closed eye above an open ledger.

"The Quiet Guard," Tam breathed. "Those are *Quiet Guard*." He grabbed Ada's arm. "Ada. Those aren't tax collectors. Those are the ones who collect *you*."

"Seven riders." She was watching them descend the far slope at an unhurried walk, the way men ride when they know nothing can stop them. "Against a whole valley."

"You don't understand what they are. They don't fight. They *file*. Every person they touch gets entered into the record, and once you're in the record—" He stopped, because saying it aloud made it true. "Once you're in the record, you belong to the court. Your name, your debts, your soul-tally. Everything."

"And me?"

Tam looked at her. She stood in her doorway with her sword loose in her hand and her boots finally laced, and she was not afraid—or rather, she was afraid, he could see it in the white of her knuckles, but she wore the fear the way soldiers wear old armor, fitted to the body, barely felt anymore.

"They'll have a warrant," he said quietly. "For the Unwritten One. And when they find you, they won't arrest you. Arrests are for people with records. You're property, Ada. They've come to repossess."

* * *

The valley did not panic, because the valley did not yet understand.

Tam watched it happen with the horrible clarity of a man who has read this chapter before. Old Wenna from the mill walked out to the road with her hands on her hips, planting herself in front of the lead rider like she'd shoo off any other trespasser. Behind her gathered a dozen others—farmers with pitchforks, the ferryman with a boathook, two boys who should have been in school carrying rocks.

The lead rider drew rein ten paces from Wenna. Beneath the helmet's shadow there was no face. There was a page.

Tam saw it clearly from thirty yards—the skin gone flat and pale like vellum, faint lines of script moving across the cheeks, the eyes blank as margins. A man who had given so much of himself to the record that almost nothing original remained.

"Inhabitants of the valley," the rider said. Its voice was the voice of a clerk reading aloud: correct, toneless, patient. "By authority of the Regent of Quiet Things, we serve writ of recovery upon the individual designated Unwritten. Produce her, or be entered as accessories."

Wenna spat. Tam, despite everything, felt a surge of love for the valley then—for its stubbornness, its refusal to learn fear, its deep conviction that any problem could be solved by standing in front of it.

"We don't produce people here," Wenna said. "We don't hand anyone over. That's not how it works in this valley."

"Noted," said the rider. And it raised one hand, fingers spread, palm outward, in a gesture Tam knew intimately—he had seen clerks make it a thousand times across a counting desk.

It was the gesture for *enter into evidence.*

"No," Tam whispered. "No, no—get back, get BACK—"

Magic crossed the valley line.

Everyone in the valley believed, with the simple faith of people born inside a miracle, that magic could not follow them here. And mostly that was true. But the ward at the valley's edge was not a wall. Tam had read the old surveys during his years in the archive. It was a *stitch*—a seam sewn shut a century ago, holding the valley apart from the world the way a wound is held apart from infection. And stitches fail where the fabric pulls hardest.

It pulled hardest now, because the Unwritten One was standing in the middle of the cloth.

The lead rider's spell came through the seam like ink through wet paper. Tam didn't see it so much as feel it—a pressure, a cold certainty, the sensation of being *read*. It washed over the crowd at the valley gate, and where it touched, things changed.

Old Wenna froze mid-spat, her face going smooth and distant, like a woman listening to her own name called from very far away. The farmer beside her dropped his pitchfork and began, calmly, horribly, to recite: his father's name, his grandfather's, the acreage of his fields, the price paid for his grandmother's funeral—all of it pouring out of him in the flat cadence of testimony, because the spell was taking his deposition, entering him into the record, claiming him.

And then the valley answered.

It answered through Ada Villalobos, though she never claimed the act and Tam never learned exactly how it worked. Her god-sworn blade came off her hip singing—not loud, never loud, the Silent God's instruments did not do loud—and the note it made was the opposite of the rider's reading-voice. Where the spell *entered*, the blade *struck out*. The pressure broke like a fever breaking. Wenna staggered and caught herself on the fence, alive and herself and furious about it.

But the riders had not come alone, and they had not come unprepared, and the skirmish that followed lasted less time than it takes to tell.

Tam remembered it afterward in pieces, the way bad memories come: the boathook shattering against lacquered armor. A horse that bled shadow instead of blood. The two schoolboys running, thank all the small gods, running when told. Sefa's father hauling people behind the ferry shed. And the rider whose page-face Tam would see in his dreams for the rest of his life turning, unhurried, toward the two villagers who had not run fast enough—

—Doss Merrow, the cooper, and young Piet Ansel, seventeen years old, who had only joined the crowd because his friends had.

The rider made the gesture again. Not the reading-gesture this time. The other one. The one clerks use for *strike from the ledger.*

Doss died first. There was no wound, no blood. He simply stopped, mid-shout, and folded up like a book closing, and when Tam reached him seconds later the man's body was there but Doss was not—his face empty, his hands slack, and written across his forehead in fine copperplate, already fading: *account settled.*

Piet ran. Piet almost made it to the trees.

Almost.

* * *

Afterward, the riders withdrew. That was the worst part, Tam thought later—they simply turned and rode back up the ridge at the same unhurried walk, having accomplished their purpose, which had never been capture. Seven Quiet Guard against a mercenary with a god-sworn blade and a valley full of angry farmers? They had not come to win a battle.

They had come to make an entry.

The lead rider paused at the crest of the ridge, and though its helmet showed no face, Tam felt its attention settle on Ada like a finger pressed to a ledger line. When it spoke, its voice carried impossibly across the whole valley, calm as a receipt:

*"Recovery deferred. Entry stands. We will return."*

Then the grass stilled, and the birds came back one cautious wingbeat at a time, and the valley was left alone with its dead.

* * *

They buried Doss Merrow and Piet Ansel side by side on the hill above the ferry crossing, because the valley's tradition was to bury its people where they could watch the river go by. Tam stood at the back of the crowd, a stranger among mourners, and understood with perfect clarity that both graves were his fault.

He had falsified records. He had fled. He had landed in this valley like a stone thrown through a window, and the shards were going into everyone but him.

Sefa found him there after the crowd thinned—the ferryman's daughter, red-eyed, holding a shovel she'd refused to put down until the earth was properly mounded. She looked at him the way you look at weather you cannot argue with.

"She's leaving," Sefa said. "Isn't she."

Tam couldn't lie to her. He'd spent eleven years lying professionally; it was strangely restful to stop. "Yes."

"Because of us. Because staying means more of—" she gestured at the fresh mounds, and her voice cracked and held, cracked and held. "More of this."

"Yes."

Sefa nodded once, hard, like a woman driving a nail, and walked away before he could say anything else, and Tam stood alone on the hill feeling the full weight of what he had cost these people, and knowing it wasn't done costing yet.

* * *

He found Ada at the valley gate at dusk, standing where the skirmish had broken, looking east at the ridgeline. Her blade was drawn and planted point-down in the turf, her hands resting on the pommel, and she was so still that for a moment Tam thought she was praying.

Maybe she was. To whom, he couldn't guess. The Silent God didn't take prayers. That was rather the point of Him.

"They waited until market day," Ada said without turning. "Until the most people possible were at the gate. That wasn't chance."

"No. The Guard doesn't believe in chance. Only in efficiency."

"They killed a boy." Her voice was level in a way that Tam recognized, professionally, as the most dangerous register a human voice can occupy. "Seventeen years old. He used to steal apples off my windowsill. I yelled at him for it every autumn." She turned, and in the last light her eyes were dry and terrible. "Tell me the truth, scribe. All of it. If I give myself up tomorrow, does the valley stay safe?"

Tam opened his mouth. Closed it. Because here was the thing: he had checked. On his first night in her house, while she slept, he had taken out his memory of the warrants and read them over the way he'd read a thousand documents, and the answer was there in the Regent's own seal-language, plain as arithmetic.

"No," he said. "A surrendered asset closes one entry and opens three. They'd want the witnesses. The bloodline. Anyone who sheltered you. Anyone who *saw* you." He swallowed. "You're not a fugitive to them, Ada. You're a *loose page*. And loose pages get torn out—with everything written near them."

Ada looked back at the village. Lamplight was coming on, window by window. Somewhere the mill was still turning, because the mill always turned, because the valley believed tomorrow would always come. He watched her look at every light the way a woman counts things she is about to lose.

"Doss had four kids," she said. "Piet's mother buried her husband two years ago. Now she's buried her son." Her knuckles whitened on the pommel. "Every day I stand in this valley, I'm choosing which of them pays for my life. And I never got to choose anything else about my life, apparently, since it was sold before I was born—but this, this I choose."

She pulled the blade from the earth. The steel made no sound at all, but Tam felt it hum against his teeth, the same note it had made by the firelight when it tested his truth.

"So we go," she said. "Down under the mountain. To whatever court thinks it owns me. And I take back what's mine." She slung the blade across her back and started walking toward the lamplights, toward the goodbyes she would have to say, and threw the last words over her shoulder like a coin tossed to a beggar:

"Get your book, scribe. You're coming. And if there's anything in those prophecies about what happens when I arrive—you'd better hope you wrote it down."

Behind them, far up the eastern ridge, something vast shifted in the dark above the tree line—something with wings it had folded a century ago—and neither of them saw it. Yet.
