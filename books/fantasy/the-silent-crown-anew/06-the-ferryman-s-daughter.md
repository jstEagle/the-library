## 6. The Ferryman's Daughter

Ada told herself she was going down to the river to say goodbye.

She got as far as the ferry landing before she admitted the lie. She was going down to see Sefa's face one more time before she decided anything at all—before the leaving became real enough to need saying. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole width of the valley, and she was standing in it.

The morning had that grey-gold quality the river made when the mist burned off slow, the water running flat and green between the willows like a vein of something precious. The ferry sat moored and empty, rocking against its posts. No customers yet. The village behind her was still mostly shuttered, though she'd seen lamplight moving in Tam's borrowed room above the chandler's, and smoke rising from the widow Osk's chimney, which meant someone was frying something and hadn't invited her, which meant the valley was trying very hard to be ordinary.

Ordinary was a performance now. She could hear the effort in it.

Two villagers dead on the ridgeline. Riders in court black who had crossed a boundary no magic had crossed in living memory. A scribe sleeping under her roof who flinched in his dreams and murmured numbers—always numbers, ledger entries, prices paid—and a blade at her hip that hummed when he told the truth, which was more often than she'd expected and less often than she wanted.

And underneath all of it, quiet as silt: *the Unwritten One.* The name the riders had used for her before they'd known her face. Her whole life spent in a valley where magic couldn't follow, and it turned out the valley wasn't shelter. It was storage.

The door of the ferry house opened, and Sefa came out carrying two cups.

She didn't look surprised to see Ada standing there in the half-light like a burglar with nowhere to break into. Sefa never looked surprised by anything, which was either a gift or a discipline; Ada had never decided which. She wore her father's old oilskin over her nightclothes, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hair still wet from washing, and she held the cups out with the particular authority of someone handing you something you didn't know you needed.

"You've been standing there for ten minutes," Sefa said. "I watched you through the window. You kept almost turning around."

"Did I?"

"Twice. The third time you'd have made it back up the hill and I'd have had to come find you, so I saved us both the walk." She pressed a cup into Ada's hands. Barley tea, bitter, too hot, made exactly wrong the way Ada liked it. "Drink. You look like you slept in your boots."

"I slept in my boots."

"That explains the smell." Sefa hopped up to sit on the flat rail of the ferry, oilskin pooling around her thighs, and swung her heels against the hull in a slow rhythm—*knock, knock-knock*—that Ada had heard every day since they were nine years old and had never once been able to stop hearing after.

They drank in silence for a while. The river did what the river always did, which was go somewhere else without hurrying about it.

"Sefa," Ada said.

"Don't." Sefa said it lightly, without looking up from her cup. "Not yet. I know what's coming. I've known since the riders came through, since you stood in the square looking like a woman doing arithmetic with people's lives. But I want ten minutes where we're just two idiots drinking bad tea on a boat. Then you can say your piece, and I'll say mine, and we'll see who's right."

Ada drank her tea. It scalded her tongue and she let it, because the small pain was easier than the large one waiting behind her teeth.

* * *

Ten minutes turned into an hour, because neither of them counted honestly.

Sefa talked about nothing—the chandler's new apprentice who broke everything he touched, the way the eels were running late this year, whether the widow Osk's cat had finally learned to open doors or whether the widow Osk was losing her mind. Ada answered where answering was possible and listened everywhere else, and catalogued her, because that was what mercenary discipline did even when you didn't ask it to: the ink stain on Sefa's left knuckle from copying her father's manifests; the chipped tooth she showed when she laughed at her own joke about the cat; the way her eyes went to the ridgeline north once, briefly, and came back, and stayed back.

She was scared. She was choosing not to be. Ada knew the difference better than anyone alive.

When the hour was spent, Sefa set her empty cup down inside the hull with a click and said, "All right. Your piece."

"I'm leaving."

"Yes."

"Tonight, probably. Before the court sends more than riders. Tam thinks—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a thing under the mountain that has a piece of me. Has had it since before I was born. And there's a fire keeping something terrible asleep, or quiet, or hidden—I still don't have all of it straight—and the fire is dying, and when it dies, everyone who ever sold anything to that court gets collected. Including everyone here. Including—" She gestured vaguely at the village, the river, the whole soft green world of the valley, and felt the gesture fail to hold any of it. "Including you."

"Including me," Sefa agreed. "Because of the book."

"You read it?"

"I read the page you burned. Or I read what your grandmother-in-law remembered of it before you burned it, which is nearly the same thing." Sefa's heels stopped their knocking. "My name isn't in it, if that matters. Just yours, and a date, and some words nobody can agree on. But Ada—if the fire dies, does it matter whose name is written? You said it yourself. Everyone gets collected."

"It might buy time. Time to fix it."

"Then why are you telling me goodbye instead of asking me for help?"

The question landed the way her blade landed—clean, no wasted force, exactly where it was aimed. Ada looked out at the river so she wouldn't have to watch Sefa's face while she lied or while she failed to.

"Because you're safer here," she said. "Because whatever comes next, it comes for me first, and every hour I'm not standing next to you is an hour the riders have to spend chasing me instead of burning this place down. Because I'm good at being hunted and you're good at being home, and those aren't the same job, and I won't make you do mine."

"See," Sefa said, "that was almost convincing. Right up until 'you're safer here.' Do you actually believe the valley stops anything anymore? They crossed the line, Ada. First time in history. What do you think changed?"

"The book. My name getting written down. The moment I stopped being invisible, the valley stopped working." Ada's jaw tightened. "Which means the safest place in the world for you is nowhere near me."

"And the safest place in the world for you?"

"There is one. That's rather the point."

"No." Sefa slid off the rail and stood close, close enough that Ada could smell river-water and soap and barley tea, close enough that the argument got much harder to win. "That's not the point. That's the excuse. Here's the point: you've spent your whole life believing you were a hole in the world. Nothing sticks to Ada Villalobos. Magic can't find her, prophecy shouldn't have her, love doesn't get a foothold because how can anything claim a person the world refuses to notice? And now it turns out you were noticed all along—sold, filed, priced—and your first thought isn't *I exist*, it's *I'm dangerous*. You're doing it again. You're making yourself into a hole and calling it protection."

"That's not fair."

"It's completely fair. It's the fairest thing I've ever said to you, and I hate it, and I'd take it back except then you'd leave believing it was kindness, and it wasn't. It was cowardice. Yours and mine both, because I let you do it for years." Sefa's voice cracked on the last word, only slightly, and she pushed through it the way she pushed the ferry off the bank—one hard shove, no hesitation. "I let you keep our whole thing unfinished because unfinished was safe. Well. The riders came across the ridgeline, my love. Nothing here is safe, including unfinished."

Ada's throat worked. "Sefa—"

"So no. I don't accept your goodbye. Refused. Denied. Filed under things that aren't happening." She wiped her eyes fast and furious, once, with the heel of her hand, and then reached into the pocket of the oilskin. "Instead I accept this. Take it."

It was a stone. River-grey, palm-sized, worn smooth as an old coin, with a pale band running around its middle like the watermark on good paper. Ada took it and turned it over and felt—faintly, faintly, the way you feel warmth coming off a wall long after the fire's gone—a kind of attention in it. Not magic, not exactly. Memory, maybe. Direction.

"What is it?"

"A river-stone. Obviously." Sefa took it back for a second, held it up between them. "This one's been in the ferry house three generations. My grandfather called them remembering-stones. He said the river knows every road that touches it—all the tributaries, all the fords, every path a traveler ever walked down to the water and back—and sometimes a stone remembers a piece of it. Hold it and think about where you want to go, and it'll pull. Not far. Not fast. Like a fish on a light line. But it pulls true."

"And it remembers directions home?"

"It remembers directions *to* home. Whether you come home is your own business." She tied it around Ada's wrist herself, with a length of waxed cord she'd clearly prepared in advance, knotting it with the brisk competence she brought to everything, and then her hands stayed on Ada's wrist longer than knots require. "There. Now you can't get lost. Only found."

"Sefa, I can't take your grandfather's—"

"You can and you are, and if you argue I'll tell the whole village you cried, and I'll exaggerate." But she didn't step back, and her voice lost its armor all at once, the way a tide goes out. "Ada. Listen to me. I'm not staying behind because I'm weak and I'm not letting you go because I'm brave. I'm staying because someone has to be here. If you fix the thing under the mountain, someone has to be here to be fixed-for. And if you don't—if the silence eats everything—then someone has to be here at the end, on the water, where my family's always been, doing the work until the work runs out. That's not smaller than what you're doing. Don't you dare think it's smaller."

"It's not," Ada said, and her voice came out wrecked. "It's not smaller. It's the reason."

Sefa smiled, and it was the real smile, the chipped-tooth one, wet at the edges. "Good. Then we understand each other. You're not leaving to protect me. You're leaving *because of* me, because I'm the thing worth saving, and you're going to save me, and then you're going to come back down this hill smelling of boot-leather and bad decisions, and I'm going to hand you a cup of terrible tea, and we're going to finish what we started. Those are the terms."

"And if I can't come back?"

"Then I'll come find you. I have the river, and the river goes everywhere." Sefa kissed her—brief, fierce, tasting of barley and salt—then pulled back before it could become the kind of kiss that unmoored boats. "Go on. Get up the hill. Tam will be pacing a groove in the floor, and dragons don't wait, or whatever it is dragons do."

Ada climbed the bank. At the top of it she turned around, because she had to, because some turns are mandatory.

Sefa was already pushing the ferry out onto the water, alone, poling it toward the far landing with easy strokes, her oilskin bright in the strengthening sun. She didn't wave. She just lifted one hand off the pole for a moment—palm open, fingers spread—and put it back, and kept the boat moving.

On Ada's wrist, the river-stone gave the faintest pull. North. Toward the mountain. Toward the thing that had her name in a ledger and her soul in a drawer.

But underneath the pull, deeper, steady as current: south, and east, and home, and a woman on a boat who would not be protected and would not be left, and a cup of terrible tea waiting at the end of everything.

Ada walked up the hill. Behind her, the bell at the chandler's began to ring—not the alarm pattern, not yet, but the general call, the one that meant *come and see.*

She started to run.
