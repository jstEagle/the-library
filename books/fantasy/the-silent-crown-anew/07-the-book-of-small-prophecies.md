## 7. The Book of Small Prophecies

Tam had read the book a hundred times.

That was the problem. He had read it the way a clerk reads anything: for the entries that mattered to the court, the lines that could be cited, the passages that could be weaponized in an argument about succession or taxation or who was permitted to speak at council. He had catalogued it. He had cross-referenced it. He had, on two occasions, forged marginalia in it to support a Regent's decree, because the book carried weight and weight was useful and no one ever checked the margins of a holy text against the hand that claimed to have found them there.

He had never once read it straight through.

Scribes didn't. That was the first thing they taught you beneath the mountain, though they didn't call it teaching—they called it orientation, and it came with a cup of weak tea and a lecture on the sanctity of records. *A record is not a story,* Magister Corvel had said, tapping the lectern with one ink-stained finger. *A record is a tool. You do not read a hammer. You use it.* Tam had been nineteen and flattered to be addressed at all, and he had nodded, and for eleven years he had used hammers without once wondering what they were built to hit.

Now he sat in a mercenary's valley house, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and river damp, with a blade-keeper's dog asleep under the table and a stolen book open on his knees, and he was reading it like a story for the first time in his life.

It was going very badly.

* * *

The trouble started on the third page, which Tam had always skipped, because the third page contained nothing but inventory.

He had assumed it was boilerplate. Old books began with inventories the way old houses began with doorframes—structural, unlovely, ignored. But now, reading it as a whole, he saw the inventory was the argument. The book did not open with a vision. It opened with a list:

*One bell, bronze, cracked. Sold by the temple wardens, year of the Long Frost.*
*One song, harvest, four verses. Sold by the choir-mother, price undisclosed.*
*One memory, shared, of a flood that did not happen. Sold by the town of Marrow's Bend, collectively, to settle a debt of grain.*

Small things. That was what struck him, sitting there with the lamplight guttering and the dog's ribs rising and falling under his boots. He had spent his career around grand prophecies—the court kept dozens, all of them magnificent, all of them about kings and crowns and the shape of history. He had assumed this book, the original, the source, would be the most magnificent of all. Instead it was a ledger. A ledger of small sold things. A cracked bell. Four verses of a harvest song nobody alive remembered losing.

Someone had sold these things. Someone had bought them. And someone—some hand, some patient, meticulous, terrible hand—had written down every transaction as though it were ordinary. As though selling a village's memory of a flood were the same kind of act as selling a sack of barley. The handwriting never wavered. There was no outrage in the margins, no weeping, no annotation at all. Just entries. Dates. Prices. Signatures of the sellers, where signatures existed, and where they didn't, a small neat notation: *sold by consent presumed.*

*Consent presumed.* Tam read those two words four times. His skin did something complicated.

He was a forger. He knew what consent presumed meant in practice. It meant someone hadn't asked, and the asker had decided that was fine, and a record had been made to make the not-asking official. He had written *consent presumed* himself, more times than he could count, usually on requisitions of grain from villages too remote to complain. He had never thought of it as evil. He had thought of it as procedure.

The book was full of procedure.

* * *

By midnight he had stopped reading forward and started reading backward, which was how you read a ledger if you wanted to understand it—follow the money upstream. And the further back he went, the smaller the entries got, until the entries stopped being objects and started being pieces of people.

*A voice, one, belonging to a girl of six, sold by her father to settle a gambling debt. The girl has not spoken since. Price: three goats and forgiveness.*

Tam put the book down. He picked it up again. He couldn't stop.

Three goats and forgiveness. Somewhere, a hundred years ago, a man had signed away his daughter's voice for three goats, and somewhere else, a buyer's agent had recorded it in the same even hand that recorded everything, and the girl had gone silent, and the ledger had moved on to the next entry without comment. No asterisk. No footnote saying *this ruined a life.* Ledgers don't ruin lives; ledgers record ruins. The ruining happens elsewhere, by other hands, and the ledger just keeps the score.

And the score, Tam realized, turning pages faster now, was enormous. Entry after entry after entry. Voices. Memories. Years—a woman in the western holdings had sold eleven years of her own life and the entry noted, dryly, *buyer reports the years keep well.* Names. Whole names, sold and resold, the way you'd sell a plot of land, except the people walking around afterward still wore the names like clothes that no longer belonged to them. One entry simply read: *a childhood, unspecified contents, sold in bulk with nine others from the same parish. Condition: used.*

Used. In bulk.

The dog woke, lifted its head, and looked at him with the flat judgment of animals, and Tam understood suddenly why Ada Villalobos watched him the way she did—not with suspicion exactly, but with the wariness of someone who had spent her life around blades and recognized that paper cut differently. Slower. Deeper. You could see a sword coming.

You never saw the ledger coming. The ledger was already in the room. The ledger had been in the room your whole life, filed neatly on a shelf beneath the mountain, recording the exact date and price at which your grandmother's lullaby had been taken from her, and you had grown up humming a hole where the song should have been, and you had never known, because the record was excellent and the record was quiet and the record was kept by people like him.

People like him. That was the phrase his mind kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. He had spent eleven years beneath the mountain filing souls. Not metaphorically. There was a wing of the archive—the deep stacks, the ones even senior clerks needed writs to enter—where the purchased things were stored, and Tam had carried boxes into it, had labeled drawers, had drafted the writs himself. He had thought of it as storage. Inventory management. The court called the wing the Treasury of Quiet Things, and the name had sounded almost gentle, almost reverent, and he had never once stood in front of a drawer and asked what was in it.

He knew now. He was reading the shipping manifest.

* * *

Toward dawn, Ada came down the stairs. She moved quietly for a big woman; he heard her before he saw her only because the floorboard by the hearth had a voice of its own. She stood at the bottom step with her arms crossed, watching him hunch over the book like a man guarding food he'd lost his appetite for.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I've been reading."

"You've been shaking." She crossed the room, took the kettle off its hook, and set it over the coals without asking whether he wanted tea, which he was learning was how she did everything—decided, then informed you. "What is it?"

"It's not a prophecy book." His voice came out wrong, scraped. He cleared his throat. "I mean—it is, everyone calls it that, I've called it that my whole career. But it isn't. Prophecies tell you what will happen. This tells you what already did. What was done." He turned the book toward her, careful, the way you handle something that might be a wound. "It's a ledger. Every entry is a sale. Small things, mostly. Bells. Songs. Names. Pieces of people."

Ada looked at the page. Her face did nothing, which he was learning was her face doing a great deal. "Sold to who?"

"The court." He said it flatly because there was no other way to say it. "To the court beneath the mountain. Or to agents of the court, or to priests who were court agents wearing vestments—I'm still working out the structure, the buyers are sometimes listed as individuals, sometimes as offices, but the proceeds—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "The proceeds route back to the treasury. My treasury. The one I worked in."

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Neither of them moved to take it off.

"Your treasury," Ada said.

"My treasury," Tam agreed, and hated the word *my*, and used it anyway, because euphemism was the disease here and he refused to dose himself with it anymore. He had falsified records to protect the court. He had told himself it was survival, then ambition, then—toward the end, when the lies got bigger and the margin notes got bolder—that he was protecting the *system*, which was the last lie a bureaucrat tells, the comfortable one, the one where the machine is good and you are merely its tired hands. Sitting here in a valley where magic couldn't follow, holding the one book the court had never managed to file, he could finally see the machine whole. It ran on this. On goats-for-voices. On consent presumed. On a hundred years of small sales, each one too petty to grieve alone, accumulating into something that had eaten a god.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing he couldn't stop seeing, now that he'd seen it. He had come down the mountain believing the standard doctrine: the Silent God had fallen silent, mysteriously, tragically, a century ago, and the court mourned and maintained and waited. Everyone beneath the mountain believed it. It was in the liturgy. *We keep the silence faithfully until the voice returns.* He had copied that line into a thousand documents.

But the ledger didn't read like mourning. It read like acquisition. The silence wasn't a wound the court tended. The silence was a purchase the court had made—and the entries got bigger as the years went on, larger pieces, closer to the center, the sales climbing rung by rung like a thief ascending a tower, until the final entries weren't bells and songs at all.

* * *

He found the last page near morning, when the light through the window went grey and the fire was down to its red bones.

He had been saving it, in the superstitious way readers save endings, and also in the practical way clerks save endings, because in any document the final entry is where the author tells you what the whole thing was for. He made tea he didn't drink. He made himself read the second-to-last entry first, as a ramp.

*A soul, unnamed, sold twice. First sale: by the generation, in trust, before the bearer's birth. Second sale: repurchased by the Silent God, payment rendered in full. Note: the god paid everything it had.*

Everything it had. Tam stared at the note until the words stopped being words. He had processed payments his entire career; he knew what *payment rendered in full* meant in the language of the archive. It meant the account was empty. It meant zero balance. It meant whatever the Silent God had been—voice, presence, the low warm hum the old valley folk still swore they remembered hearing in the stones—had been converted entirely into currency and spent on a single transaction. Buying back one soul. Unnamed. Unborn.

A god had bankrupted itself to buy one person back from the market.

And the market had accepted the payment, because the market accepted everything, that was the horror of it, the market had no ideology, no malice, no plan. The Regent liked to speak of destiny and design when it suited him, but Tam had seen the internal memoranda. There was no design. There was appetite and accounting. The god had emptied itself out, and the ledger had recorded the transaction in the same even hand, and then—

Then the last entry.

He read it standing up. He didn't decide to stand; his body did it, some animal instinct that said you do not receive news like this sitting down. The dog raised its head. Outside, the valley was doing its pale early thing, mist on the river, a heron somewhere, the world carrying on with no idea what was written in ink on a mercenary's table.

The final entry was short. Final entries in ledgers usually were; the author was tired, or finished, or dead. It was dated ninety-nine years ago—one year before the god fell silent, one year before, he now understood with a lurch, a mercenary named Villalobos had been born in this valley. The hand was the same steady hand that had written every other line. No emphasis. No alarm. Just the record, closing itself out, stating its terms the way all their documents stated their terms, in language designed to be filed rather than felt:

*Final entry. The price of silence is this: while the last dragon-fire burns, the purchases hold. When it gutters out, all accounts come due. The thief must return what was stolen, or the silence will collect.*

Tam read it aloud, because he didn't trust himself to have read it silently correctly, and hearing it in his own voice made it worse, made it real, made it sit in the room with them like a third person.

Ada had come back down at some point—he hadn't noticed her arrive, which frightened him slightly, a woman that size moving unheard—and she stood across the table from him with her arms folded and her jaw set, listening to her own fate described in clerical shorthand. *All accounts come due.* She was an account. She was the twice-sold soul, the receipt the god had bankrupted itself to hold, and somewhere beneath the mountain, in a drawer he had probably labeled himself, her soul sat filed like coins.

"The dragon-fire," she said. "Vess. The exile. That's what the flame is."

"That's what the flame is for." Tam closed the book, gently, as if it might bruise. "It's not a candle. It's a lock. Or—it's a fuse burning in reverse. While it lasts, the theft stays hidden, the accounts stay frozen, the silence stays... stable. Manageable. The court calls it keeping faith. The ledger calls it collateral." He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "Ninety-nine years of collateral. And I sat in the archive beneath it all, filing writs, drinking bad tea, and I never once wondered what the vault underneath the vault was for."

"And when it goes out?"

"Then the ledger balances." He heard his own voice go thin. "Every voice. Every memory. Every year. Every name. Everything the court ever bought comes due at once, and the silence collects, and—" He gestured vaguely at the window, the valley, the world, the gesture of a man trying to indicate *everything* and finding his hands too small. "The doctrine says devour. I used to think that was poetry. Metaphor. The dramatic language clerks use when they want a decree to sound urgent. It isn't poetry. It's a collections notice. Written a century ago by someone who knew exactly what the court had built and exactly when the bill would arrive."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. The kettle finally whistled, shrill and absurd, and neither of them moved, and eventually it gave up on its own, which felt symbolic in a way he chose not to examine.

"Who wrote this?" she asked finally. "The hand. The steady one. Who spends a century recording the sale of children's voices in the same script they'd use for grain shipments?"

"A clerk," Tam said. "Some patient, obedient, careful clerk, who believed the record mattered more than the ruin. Who maybe told themselves the same thing I told myself for eleven years—that the machine was good, that the hands were tired, that somebody upstairs must know what they're doing." He set his palm flat on the cover of the book. "I've been that clerk. I am that clerk. The difference between me and whoever wrote this is that they finished the job and I ran downhill with the evidence."

Ada's eyes narrowed, but not at him—at the situation, the shape of it, the way she seemed to look at problems the way other people looked at terrain. "So the court doesn't just have my soul. They have a contract. Signed, witnessed, filed. And the only thing keeping the whole arrangement frozen is one dying flame in a cave."

"Yes."

"And the court knows the flame is dying?"

"They've known for decades. There are maintenance schedules." Tam's stomach turned over as he said it, remembering the requisitions, the wax seals, the routine of it. "There's a bureau. Office of Continuance. Their entire function is monitoring the fire and filing reports on its decline. I processed their correspondence. I thought they were tending it." He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "They're timing it. There's a memo—I filed it myself, two years ago, I can still see the header—projected gutter within five years, recommend accelerating acquisitions in advance of settlement. *In advance of settlement.* They're buying more souls before the freeze ends. Stocking the larder. They aren't afraid of the silence coming due. They're preparing to profit from it."

The dog growled, low, at nothing—or at the word *profit*, which had entered the room and sat down among them like something with teeth.

Ada unfolded her arms. Something in her posture changed, the way it changed, he was learning, right before she committed to a course of action: a settling, a lowering of the center of gravity, a stillness that was not calm. "Then we go down there," she said. "Tonight. Tomorrow. As soon as the ferry runs."

"Ada." He said her name carefully, the way you say a word you've discovered is load-bearing. "Do you understand what's down there? It's not a dungeon with a dragon and a crown. It's an archive. Miles of it. Drawers within drawers, writs within writs, and every drawer locked by law so dense it functions as a physical barrier. You can't fight a bureaucracy. Blades don't cut paper. Believe me—I've spent my life on the paper side of that equation, and paper wins. Paper always wins. Paper outlasts the hand that holds the sword, outlasts the kingdom, outlasts the language the sword was sworn in. That's the whole terror of it. That's why they built it this way."

"Then what do we do?" she asked, and it was not a rhetorical question, and it was not desperate either. It was the question of a professional assessing a contract. "You know the paper. I know the blade. Vess knows the fire. So. What do we do?"

Tam looked down at the book under his hand. The ledger of small sold things. A hundred years of transactions, each one legal, each one witnessed, each one filed by someone like him, accumulating in the dark beneath the mountain into a debt that would swallow the world when it came due.

And then he looked at the final entry again, really looked, the way he should have looked at everything years ago, and saw what he had missed—what he had been trained his whole life to miss, because clerks read for terms and missed the sentence between the terms.

*The thief must return what was stolen.*

Not *the court*. Not *the crown*. Not *the responsible parties*. *The thief.* Singular. Personal. Whoever held the stolen goods when the fire died—that was who the clause bound. Which meant the clause could bind anyone. Which meant, if the goods changed hands—if the accounts were settled early, if the returns were made before the deadline—

The ledger wasn't just a record. It was a mechanism. And mechanisms could be operated from either end.

"We do paperwork," Tam said slowly, and heard himself smile for the first time since he'd fallen down the hill into this valley, a crooked, unholy, scribe's smile. "Ada, we do the most dangerous paperwork in the world. We go down beneath the mountain, and we find the drawer where they filed you, and we settle your account before the fire dies—return, receipt, closed ledger, one line at a time. We beat the collections notice at its own game." He tapped the final entry. "This says the thief must return what was stolen. Fine. Then we make sure that when the last flame gutters, the only thing left owing is theirs."

Outside, the mist was lifting off the river, and somewhere down the bank the ferry rope creaked against its post, and Ada Villalobos looked at him for a long moment with the expression of a woman deciding whether the madman in her kitchen had just handed her a plan or a death warrant.

Then she reached across the table and took the book.

"Then we start reading," she said, "from the beginning."
