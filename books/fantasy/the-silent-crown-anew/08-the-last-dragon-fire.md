## 8. The Last Dragon-Fire

The ledger did not read like a book. It read like a debt.

Ada had expected prophecy—thunder, riddles, the usual self-important nonsense that holy men charged silver for. Instead Tam turned the pages and found columns. Dates. Amounts. Descriptions in a clerk's hand so small and tidy it made her teeth ache.

*"One memory of a mother's face, sold by J. Hallow, spring market."*

*"Three years of good luck, pooled from six sellers, purchased by an agent of the court."*

*"A soul, unnamed, sold twice."*

That one again. Tam had gone pale around it twice already, and each time he'd smoothed his mustache and changed the subject, which was how Ada knew he'd understood something he wasn't ready to say.

They walked north out of the valley for two days, following entries instead of roads.

"It says here," Tam said on the second morning, holding the book at arm's length like it might bite, "'the fire is kept where the stone remembers being sky.' Which is either a cave high up or a poet's idea of directions."

"Read me the next line."

"'Bring nothing you cannot burn.'"

"That's helpful."

"I'm paraphrasing the tone," Tam said. "The actual line is 'bring nothing you cannot account for,' which is worse, honestly. That's an auditor's threat."

The valley thinned behind them the way a sound does when a door swings shut. Ada felt it go—the particular hush she'd lived inside her whole life, the quiet that wasn't silence but safety, the quiet where her god-sworn blade hummed against her back and magic slid off everything like rain off wax. Past the ridgeline, the world got louder. Grass grew greedier. Colors sat heavier on things. And somewhere under all of it, faint as a toothache, she could feel the court beneath the mountain paying attention.

Her name was loose in the world now. She'd burned the page and the ash had spelled it anyway. Names, once spent, didn't come back.

They climbed through pine into bare grey stone, and the air went thin and honest, and near dusk on the third day Tam stopped so suddenly Ada nearly walked into him.

"There," he whispered. "The stone remembering being sky."

He meant the rocks were hollow. She put her palm flat against the cliff face and felt it: not wind, not water, but warmth. Old warmth, patient, coming up through the mountain like heat through a hearthstone long after the fire's gone to ash.

Except this fire hadn't gone anywhere.

* * *

The cave mouth was narrow enough that they went in sideways, single file, the ledger clutched to Tam's chest like a child. Ada went first because she always did, blade drawn, though the god-hum in the steel had gone strange—not the steady note she knew, but wavering, like a tuning fork struck by someone with a shaking hand.

The passage opened without warning into a chamber the size of a chapel.

And there was the dragon.

Not coiled on gold. Not wreathed in smoke. Vess lay along the far wall with her head bowed over a flat shelf of stone, and she was smaller than Ada had imagined dragons—no, not smaller, Ada corrected herself, watching the shape resolve out of the dark. Older. A dragon who had been enormous once and had worn herself down the way a river wears down a mountain, deliberately, year after year, until what remained was lean and grey-green and folded close like a letter no one wanted to send.

She was tending the fire.

It fit in a cupped hand. That was the thing Ada couldn't get past, standing there in the dark with her sword out like an idiot. The last dragon-fire—the flame the whole world apparently balanced on, the flame that held a god's silence in place—was a bead of light the size of a hazelnut, sitting in a shallow depression in the stone, and Vess curved around it with both foreclaws cupped near it, not touching, never touching, the way you'd warm your hands at something too precious to breathe on.

"Don't," the dragon said, without lifting her head.

Ada froze.

"The sword. Put it away or sheathe it. The hum is bothering the flame." Her voice was dry and rusted, a voice like a door nobody opened. "Everything bothers the flame. But the sword especially."

Ada sheathed it. The wavering note settled, barely.

"You're the Unwritten One," Vess said. Still not looking up. "I can smell it on you. Absence has a smell. Like the air before snow."

"You knew I was coming?"

"I knew *someone* was coming. Eventually. When the fire gets low enough, everything comes due, and whatever's most overdue walks toward the fire like a moth that owes money." Now she raised her head, and her eyes caught the tiny light and gave it back doubled, four embers floating in the dark. "You brought a scribe."

"Ex-scribe," Tam said. "Erased scribe. Technically I'm nobody."

"Mm. Nobody wrote the book, then?"

"...Partially nobody."

Vess made a sound that might have been laughter in a younger throat. She lowered her head again, and Ada watched the care of it—the terrible, exhausting precision of a creature three hundred years old bending every ounce of its attention on a flame the size of a coin.

"How long," Ada asked, "has it been that small?"

Vess was quiet for a while. Long enough that Ada thought she wouldn't answer.

"When I came here," she said finally, "it filled this chamber. Floor to ceiling. You could stand in it—it didn't burn, it *recognized* you—and you would feel..." Her claws flexed, very slightly, against the stone. "You would feel heard. All the way down. That was what the fire was. It was the god's attention, banked and burning where no crown could reach it. Everything sold to the court, everything stolen, everything filed away—it stayed *real*, because the fire remembered it. Do you understand? The court takes things and puts them in drawers. The fire is why the drawers couldn't stay shut forever."

"And now?" Ada looked at the bead of light. "Now it's the size of a nut."

"Now I feed it what I have left." Vess's voice didn't change at all, and somehow that made it worse. "Dragons are mostly fire. It's not poetry; it's anatomy. I have been feeding myself into this flame for two hundred years, a scale at a time, a decade at a time, keeping it above the line where it goes out. I am—" she paused, and for the first time the old door-hinge voice wavered, "—I am somewhat less than I was."

Tam had gone very still by the wall. "How much longer?"

"A season. Perhaps two. Less, if the weather turns cold." Vess looked up then, fully, and Ada stood in the gaze of four ember-eyes and understood that she was being measured. "So. You've read the ledger. You know what happens when it dies."

"Tell me anyway. I want to hear it from the fire's own keeper."

"When the last dragon-fire gutters out," Vess said, "everything sold to the court comes due at once. Every soul in every drawer. Every memory. Every name. They don't come back—they *come due*. The Regent will have to pay, or the silence will collect. And the silence collects by eating. Sound first. Then names. Then the memory that there was ever anything to remember. The world doesn't end loudly, Unwritten One. It ends quietly, like a candle in a closed room, and afterward no one is left who minds."

The words fell into the dark and lay there.

Ada found she was gripping her sword hilt through the scabbard, knuckles aching. "Then we relight it."

"With what?"

"You're a dragon. Dragons make fire."

Vess laughed then—a real laugh, brief and cracked and awful. "Child. If I had fire left to give, do you think I'd be rationing myself like bread? What I have goes into the flame or I die and it dies with me. There is exactly one thing in this mountain that can reignite it properly. One fuel old enough, whole enough, *paid-for-enough*—"

She stopped. Her eyes went to Ada's chest, to the space behind her ribs, as if the grey ember Tam had described were visible through skin and bone.

"—and it's walking around wearing boots," Vess finished softly.

* * *

They sat around the little flame because there was nothing else to sit around, and Vess told them the rest in the flat tone of a creature reciting facts it had memorized to keep from screaming about them.

The court beneath the mountain had been built on purchase. Not conquest—purchase. Souls bought fair and square, signed and sealed, from people who mostly didn't know what they were signing. The Regent of Quiet Things kept them filed like coins, and the necklace she wore—that was the part that made Tam make a small noise in the dark—held the god's own voice, strangled out of it a century ago and worn as jewelry ever since.

"The god didn't go silent," Vess said. "Silence was done *to* it. And the fire—everyone says the fire holds the silence in place, keeps the god quiet, and that's true the way a cage holds a bird in place. But a cage also holds the bird *safe*. As long as the fire burned, the theft stayed hidden. The voice stayed in the necklace. The ledgers stayed balanced." Her claws tightened. "When the fire dies, the hiding dies with it. The truth comes out all at once, and truth that arrives all at once doesn't knock. It devours."

"So let it come out slow," Ada said. "Tell people. Ride to the villages, print pamphlets, whatever scribes do—"

"Who would believe a dragon in exile and a scribe with no name? Who would believe *you*, whose name is currently being hunted across three provinces?" Vess's head swung toward Tam. "No offense."

"Some taken."

"The court has spent a hundred years teaching the world that quiet is peace. You won't unteach that with pamphlets." The dragon settled lower, closer to the flame, and Ada saw—with a jolt—that a patch of scales along her flank had gone dull and grey, like cooled slag. Feeding herself in. "There is a way. There has only ever been one way. The fire needs a soul that was sold and paid for, a soul with a claim on the god's own accounts, poured back into the flame freely. It would burn clean. It would burn for another age. And whoever gave it—"

"Wouldn't survive," Ada said.

"Worse," said Vess gently. "They'd survive. Hollow. A vessel. The god's voice needs somewhere to live, and a soulless body is excellent architecture. You'd walk out of here full of something ancient, and nothing of yourself at all."

The chamber was very quiet. Even the little flame seemed to hold still.

Ada looked at her hands. Rough palms, mercenary's hands, hands that had signed contracts and broken bread and once—she thought of Sefa's river-stone on her wrist, remembering directions home—once held someone else's hand across a table and meant it.

She'd spent twenty-six years feeling slightly wrong in her own skin and calling it restlessness. Turns out it was inventory. Turns out she was missing stock.

"No," she said.

Tam's head came up. Vess didn't move.

"I didn't climb out of my valley and bury two neighbors and drag a nameless clerk over a mountain to hear that the plan is suicide with extra steps." She stood, and her voice came out low and even, the voice she used on clients who tried to renegotiate mid-contract. "There are hundreds of embers in those drawers. The ledger said so. Souls sold across generations. We find mine, and if mine won't work alone, we find others. We build the fire back log by log. I'm not the only fuel in this mountain."

For the first time, Vess lifted her head all the way, and the four ember-eyes widened, and something moved across the old dragon's face that Ada would need years to name correctly: hope, arriving somewhere it had learned not to live.

"Hundreds of souls," Vess repeated slowly. "Poured into the flame together. It's never been—"

"It's never been tried because everyone who knew about the problem was too polite to suggest arson." Ada pulled her blade and pointed it at the dark tunnel leading down, toward the mountain's heart, toward the glittering court and its drawers of grey embers. "The flame's dying. You've got a season. So we stop reading the ledger like scripture and start using it like a map."

Behind her, Tam was already flipping pages, lips moving, counting.

"Ada," Vess said. The name sounded strange in the dragon's rusted voice, like a bell rung underground. "The Regent knows the fire is failing. Whatever else she is, she is not careless. If you go down there, she will know within a day what you've come for. She'll offer you anything. Your soul back, freely given, no strings—that's the first thing she'll offer, and it will even be true. Because a whole soul walking out of her court is worth more to her than any drawer."

"Why? Why would she want me whole?"

Vess looked at the little flame, and then at Ada, and her voice dropped so low the words were almost part of the dark.

"Because a whole soul can't carry the god's voice," she said. "Only a hollow one can. And if she can't keep her necklace, she will settle for making sure no one else can wear the crown it belongs to. Go down armed with more than a sword, Unwritten One. Go down knowing exactly what you're willing to lose."

The little flame flickered, and for half a heartbeat it flared—bright, tall, the ghost of the chapel-filling fire it had been—and in that flare Ada felt something look at her. Something vast and patient and terribly tired, buried under a hundred years of silence, recognizing the receipt of its own payment.

Then the flame sank back to the size of a hazelnut, and the looking was gone, and Ada Villalobos turned toward the deep roads with her heart hammering like a fist on a locked door.

"Get some sleep," she told the others. "We descend at dawn."
