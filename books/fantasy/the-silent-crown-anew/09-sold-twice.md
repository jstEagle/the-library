## 9. Sold Twice

Tam had spent eleven years at court learning to read ledgers the way other men read faces, and he knew before he opened the Book of Small Prophecies that morning that it was going to ruin him.

Not because of what was in it—though what was in it was bad enough—but because of how it was *kept*. Every ledger has a hand behind it. A clerk's habits live in their numbers like a murderer's fingerprints in candlewax: the way they cross sevens, the way they round, whether they write debts in red or in black or in that particular grey ink reserved for things too shameful to state plainly. Tam had audited three hundred years of the court's soul-accounts. He knew every clerk's hand back to the reign of the second Regent.

The book in his lap was written in none of those hands.

It was written in his own.

* * *

He'd noticed it the night before, by Vess's fire, and had told himself it was exhaustion. You spend long enough copying records and you start to see your own letterforms everywhere, the way a man who loses a tooth spends a week tonguing the gap. But now, in full daylight, with Ada sharpening her blade ten feet away and the dragon pretending to sleep with one eye angled at them both, there was no explaining it away.

The script was his. Older—he'd been younger when he learned it, the letters rounder, less certain—but his. The same cramped ascenders. The same habit of letting the tail of an *r* trail off like it had somewhere better to be. And beneath the ink, faint as breath on glass, the same watermark the court pressed into all its record-vellum: a crown with no sound coming out of it.

"This book came from the court," he said. His voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat. "I mean—I know it came from the court. I stole it from the Regent's own archive. What I mean is—" He turned the page toward the light. "I wrote part of this."

Ada stopped sharpening. "You're a hundred years old?"

"I'm thirty-one."

"Then someone copied your hand."

"No." Tam ran his thumb along the margin, where a correction had been made in a different ink, blotted and re-blotted the way he blotted things when he was nervous. "Someone taught me. That's different. Scribes don't invent their letters, Ada. We inherit them. Master to apprentice, back and back. Whoever kept this book trained whoever trained whoever trained me." He swallowed. "Or trained my master's master personally. The hand doesn't drift much over four generations. It's not supposed to. A record-keeper's whole virtue is sameness."

"So your family's been keeping this thing for a century."

"My family were *farmers*," Tam said, and then heard himself, and laughed, and the laugh came out cracked down the middle. "No. No, I suppose we weren't. I suppose we were whatever this is." He looked down at the book again, at the entry that had woken him twice in the night, and made himself read it aloud, because some things you have to say out loud or they stay coiled in you like wire.

"'Entry forty-one. A soul, unnamed, sold twice. First sale: one generation's harvest-rights, the valley's edge-fields, paid to the crown beneath in the year of the Long Frost. Second sale: purchased back in full. Price: the god's own voice, held in escrow until the debt of silence runs its term.'" He stopped. "There's more. There's always more; clerks never know when to stop writing. 'The receipt shall walk and bear the mark of the unwritten. Let no record hold her name, for she is herself the record.'"

The fire popped. Vess's eye slid shut, opened again. Somewhere up the slope a bird was making the only sound in the world, and Tam hated it a little, its easy ignorance.

"That's her," he said quietly. "That's Ada. Unnamed. Unwritten. Her name isn't in any census, any tax roll, any baptismal registry—I checked, on the road here, in three villages. She doesn't exist on paper. I thought it was luck. I thought it was the valley." He closed the book. "She's not hidden from the records. She *is* one."

* * *

Ada took it better than he expected, which frightened him worse.

She didn't argue. She didn't go pale. She sat down across the fire from him, laid her sword across her knees like a sleeping animal, and said, "Sold twice. Say that part again. Slowly."

"Your soul was sold once—by someone in your parents' generation, or theirs. Harvest rights, edge-fields, the year of the Long Frost. That would've been—" he did the arithmetic against the regnal dates he'd had beaten into him at twelve years old— "about sixty years ago. Before you were born. Souls can be sold forward, you see. Against children not yet come. It's foul practice but it isn't rare. A bad winter, a lord with an open palm, and people sign away things they tell themselves are abstractions."

"And the second sale."

"The god bought you back." Tam heard the sentence leave his mouth and wished he could catch it. "Paid in full. In its own voice. That's why the Silent God went silent the year of your birth—not silence as doctrine, not silence as nature. Silence as *payment*. It emptied itself to buy one farm girl's soul out of a crown drawer." He turned the book around so she could see the entry, though she couldn't read the court-hand; almost nobody could anymore, which was half of why the court got away with everything. "You're not just the buyer's property, Ada. You're the proof of purchase. The receipt. As long as you exist, the transaction exists. And as long as the transaction exists, the Regent holds something worth more than any soul in that archive."

"What's that?"

"A lien on a god."

Ada looked at the fire for a long time. Tam watched her the way he watched documents being signed—looking for the tremor in the hand, the hesitation before the seal, the small human tell that said *this person understands what they are agreeing to.* He saw none of it. That scared him. People who didn't flinch at news like this either hadn't understood it yet, or had understood it long ago and built their houses on top of the understanding.

"You're telling me," she said finally, "that my whole life—the valley, the mercenary work, the blade that still works at the valley's edge where nothing else does—that's not coincidence."

"It's terms of the contract. The god couldn't keep you close—it had nothing left, no voice, no reach, nothing but the last dregs of its fire and whatever old bargains still held. So it did what creditors do. It placed collateral where it could watch it." He hesitated. "The mercenary order. Your order. Founded two generations ago, chartered to 'guard the valley's edge.' Guarded against what? Wights? Wights don't need guarding against, they need putting down. No. Your order was founded to guard *you*. Not you specifically—you weren't born yet. To guard the possibility of you. The receipt walking. They've been standing watch over a piece of paper their whole lives without knowing it was paper."

Tam had seen a great many terrible things done neatly, in triplicate, in fine grey ink. He had committed a fair number of them himself, which was why he was lying half-dead beside a dragon instead of warming his hands at the court's eternal hearth. But there was something about this entry that curdled even his professional appreciation for thorough evil. Someone had written this down a hundred years ago. Someone had known—a farm girl would be born, a god would go quiet, a receipt would walk—and had recorded it in the same flat tone as entries about grain tithes and candle-wax levies.

"'Entry forty-two,'" he read, because he couldn't stop himself, because the clerk in him was a disease. "'Note re: forty-one. The buyer acted alone and without sanction. The court's position is that the purchase was irregular and the asset misfiled. Recovery pending.'"

"Recovery pending," Ada repeated.

"For a hundred years. Filed and refiled. Every few decades some junior clerk rediscovers the entry and opens a recovery action, and it dies in committee, because nobody wants to be the one who walks into a valley where magic cannot follow and knocks on a mercenary's door." He smiled without any humor in it. "Bureaucracy is the only reason you're alive, Ada. Evil is patient, but filing errors are more patient still."

* * *

Vess spoke from her corner of the cave without lifting her head, and her voice moved through the stone the way cold moves through a wall. "Ask him what happens when the debt comes due."

"I know what happens when debts come due," Tam said.

"You know what happens when *coin* debts come due. Seizure. Forfeiture. A family turned out into the snow. This is not that." Now the eye opened fully, amber and old and tired in a way that made Tam feel like a mayfly. "A soul sold twice is a knot. First the crown held it. Then the god held it. Two owners, one thing. When the god's payment finishes running out—when the last dragon-fire gutters, which it will, child, sooner than any of us would like—the escrow collapses. Both claims activate at once. The crown will come for its property. And the god—" Vess's nostrils flared, and a thread of smoke curled up and vanished— "the god has nothing left to enforce its claim with except the vessel holding it. Do you understand? The moment the fire dies, everything ever sold to that court comes due at the same hour. Every harvest-right. Every name. Every ember in every drawer. All of it, called in at once, by a Regent who has been waiting a century for exactly that day."

Silence. Even the bird on the slope had stopped.

"That's the final entry," Tam said hoarsely. "Isn't it. The one I haven't read yet."

"You haven't?" Ada asked.

"I've been avoiding it." He admitted it the way he'd once confessed falsifying the tithe rolls: quickly, looking anywhere else. "A clerk knows when an entry is load-bearing. You can feel it through the vellum. Everything else in this book points at that last line like iron filings pointing north, and I wasn't ready." He looked at Ada. At the sword on her knees. At the dragon who had chosen exile over complicity, and the fire she tended that fit in a cupped hand, and the whole improbable fragile arrangement keeping the world's worst secret. "But we're past ready. Past it by miles. So."

He untied the cord, turned to the final leaf, and read.

* * *

The last entry was short. Clerks, at the end, run out of ceremony.

"'Final entry. The price of silence is silence. When the last dragon-fire gutters out, the debt of voices comes due in full. Either the thief returns what was stolen, or the silence takes back everything it was paid, and more besides. There is no third column. Signed,'"—and here Tam's voice failed him, and he had to start the sentence over—"'signed, T. of the line of record-keepers. Last of the hand.'"

"T," Ada said. "As in—"

"As in Tam." His hands were shaking. He set the book down on his knees so she wouldn't see, which was pointless, because she saw everything. "My name. My initial. In a book a hundred years old." He laughed again, that cracked laugh. "Either I'm going to live a very long time, or—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Or names pass down. Or the hand passes down, and with it the duty, and somewhere ahead of me there's a final line I'm supposed to write, and whoever wrote this one wanted me to know it was coming. Wanted *me*, specifically, to find this page. It's addressed. Look at it. It's the only entry in the whole book with a salutation. Everything else is inventory. This one's a letter."

"To whom?" Ada asked, though he could see she already knew.

"To the next fool who inherits the hand." Tam pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. He had fled the court believing he was running from his crimes. He was beginning to suspect he had been running along a corridor someone else had drawn, toward a desk someone else had set, where a pen waited and a blank line waited and a signature waited, and the whole flight, the fall downhill, the broken ribs, the begging sanctuary from a woman whose face he'd recognized from a prophecy—all of it choreographed by a dead clerk with cramped ascenders and an *r* that trailed off like it had somewhere better to be.

"I used to think records were neutral," he said. "That was the first lie they teach you. Ink doesn't take sides, they say. Ink just remembers. Eleven years I believed it, while I filed souls like coins and called it memory." He looked up. "Ink takes sides, Ada. Every entry is a verdict. Somebody decided you were worth buying back, and somebody decided to write it down so it could never be unwritten, and somebody decided that a farm girl with a sword should carry the proof in her own chest. That's not neutrality. That's a side. Somebody chose yours a century before you drew breath."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, in the flat practical voice she probably used for negotiating contracts: "So the god spent its voice to buy me. Meaning every word it ever speaks again costs it something it already paid. Meaning if I give the voice back—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"Meaning I'm not a person with a soul," she went on, relentless as water finding a crack, "I'm a *transaction* with legs. Meaning the Regent doesn't want me dead. Dead receipts are worthless. It wants me delivered. Intact. Filed." She stood, and slung the sword across her back, and looked down at him with an expression he couldn't parse—until he realized it wasn't grief at all. It was relief. Terrible, upside-down relief. "Do you know what that means, Tam? My whole life I thought I was nobody. Unwritten. The girl the world forgot to record. And it turns out I was the most carefully recorded thing in the kingdom. Somebody wanted me to exist badly enough to pay a god's voice for it." She almost smiled. "I've never been worth anything to anybody. Turns out I'm priceless. Just not to *me*."

And Tam, watching her, felt the strange vertigo of his trade: the sensation of watching a figure reconcile at last, every column balancing, the sum arriving exactly where the entries said it must—and the sum was a woman, and the ledger was alive, and the auditor's hands were his own.

"We need to move tonight," he said. "Before the fire gutters. Before the recovery action stops dying in committee."

"Where?"

"Down." He tied the book closed and stood, wincing at his ribs. "Beneath the mountain. Through the old roads. There's a toll-bridge on the way down that eats spoken names, so eat a big breakfast, and whatever you do, don't tell anyone who you are."

"Why not?"

"Because," Tam said, "as of this morning, you're not a who. You're a what. And the things down there that collect whats—" he shouldered his satchel, and the book inside it weighed nothing and everything— "collect very carefully."

Above the cave mouth, the sky was going the color of grey ink, and far below, deeper than stone, something that had been silent for twenty-two years shifted in its sleep, the way a debtor shifts when the collector's knock finally comes.
