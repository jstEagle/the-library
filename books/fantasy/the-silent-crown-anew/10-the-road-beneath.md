## 10. The Road Beneath

The door into the mountain was a door the way a wound is a doorway—technically accurate, deeply uninviting.

Vess had led them to it at dawn, down a goat track behind the last farmstead in the valley, to a shelf of black rock where the hillside simply stopped making sense. There was a gap there, tall as a barn and twice as deep, and the air coming out of it was warm and old and smelled like a house that had been closed for a hundred years. Not rot. Just absence. Air that had been waiting.

"The trade roads," Vess said. She said it the way other people said *the war*. "Every kingdom under this range built its wealth on these roads once. Salt going up, silver coming down, souls going wherever they were told." Her tail scraped the rock, slow, thoughtful. "The court closed them when the tolls got too honest."

"Too honest," Tam repeated. He had his arms crossed against the cold and his book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and he looked like a man rehearsing objections to his own funeral.

"Tolls are honest when you can see what's being taken," Vess said. "The court prefers subtlety. Come on. Once we're inside, we don't stop unless I say we stop, and we don't talk about what any of us have lost. The road listens for grief. It uses it to find you."

Ada looked at the dark and thought about Sefa's stone in her pocket, warm from her hand all night, and stepped through first because she'd never once let fear pick her footing for her.

* * *

Inside, the mountain opened up instead of closing in—which was worse, somehow.

The first stretch of the old trade road ran through a cavern so vast their lanterns couldn't find the ceiling or either wall. The road itself was wide enough for six carts abreast, paved in fitted stone worn glassy by centuries of feet, and along both edges stood the ruins of a whole underground civilization: waystations with their roofs caved in, shrine niches holding nothing but soot, mile-markers carved with script that Tam kept stopping to read until Vess growled at him to keep walking.

"Salt route," Tam murmured anyway, running his fingers over a marker. "This one says *salt route, third descent, mind the fog*. Mind the fog. What fog?"

"Keep moving and you won't meet it," Vess said.

"And if we do meet it?"

"Then keep moving anyway."

Ada walked with her blade loose in its sheath and her breath loud in her own ears. The god-sworn iron hummed faintly against her hip—not the warning-hum she knew from wights and revenants, but something lower, almost conversational. Down here, in the deep places where magic pooled like groundwater, her blade felt less like a tool and more like a companion who kept wanting to tell her something.

"Not now," she muttered to it.

"Talking to your sword is a bad sign," Tam said.

"Talking to your sword is the only conversation I trust down here."

He laughed—a short, startled bark that echoed too far—and then looked embarrassed about it, which made Ada like him slightly more. He'd been insufferable for two days: fussy, self-pitying, allergic to silence, narrating every ruin they passed with footnotes nobody asked for. But he'd also, without being asked, taken the rear guard position both nights, and he hadn't complained once about the food, which was because there wasn't any, but still.

They walked. The road descended in long switchbacks, and the warmth of the air thickened, and somewhere below them—Ada couldn't have said how she knew—something waited that wanted payment.

* * *

It found them on the second day, at a bridge over a dry riverbed of black sand.

The bridge was intact, which alone made it remarkable, but what stopped Ada was the archway at its center. It had been carved into the shape of an open mouth—not crudely, not grotesquely, but beautifully, the way a cathedral door might be shaped like a mouth if the builders loved mouths. Lips of pale stone. Teeth like columns. A tongue of polished floor running the length of the span.

Above the arch, in the old script and then, helpfully, in common letters chiseled by some later, wearier hand:

**TOLL. SPEAK WHAT YOU CARRY. PAY WHAT IS ASKED.**

"I hate it here," Tam announced.

"You've hated it here since the valley," Ada said. "What does it want?"

"Souls-toll." Vess's voice had gone flat, careful. "The roads were built on contracts. Everything that passes pays something. In the old days it was coin, salt, years of labor. Later—" her nostrils flared, and the light of the small flame she carried cupped between two claws flickered, "—later the court renegotiated. They hold most of the toll-contracts now. They take pieces of people. Memory. Want. Small things, so travelers don't notice they're smaller than they used to be."

"How do you know when something's been taken?" Ada asked.

"You don't," Vess said. "That's the design."

They stood at the near end of the bridge for a long moment. The black sand below shifted, very slowly, like a bed of ash breathing.

"We could go around," Tam said. He was already looking for a way around. Ada could see him looking for a way around.

"There is no around," Vess said. "There's only the long way, and the long way goes through the fog fields, and I'd rather pay a toll I can see than lose my name to weather." She lowered her great head toward the archway, addressing the stone directly, the way you'd address a customs officer. "We carry a ledger, a sword, and a fire. Ask."

For a moment nothing happened. Then the air inside the arch moved—not wind; movement, like a throat preparing—and a voice came out of the stone. It didn't speak so much as itemize. Ada felt each word land on her like a clerk's finger tapping a ledger line.

*Three travelers. One dragon, exiled, carrying flame held in trust. One scribe, unnamed, carrying paper and guilt. One mercenary, carrying iron sworn to a silent god, carrying a river-stone, carrying—*

The voice paused.

Ada's skin prickled. "*Carrying* what?"

The pause stretched. Somewhere in it, Ada understood that the toll-keeper had found whatever it was the court had left in her—the hollow place, the missing weight, the receipt-shaped absence Tam had spent three nights explaining to her by firelight while she stared into the coals and refused to cry. The toll-keeper had put its finger on the hole where her soul should have been, and the hole confused it. You don't tax a debt. You don't weigh a receipt.

*Ahh,* the voice said, with something that might have been satisfaction. *One hollowed vessel, carrying a claim. The claim is not hers to carry. The toll will be—*

"No," Vess said.

*—small,* the voice finished smoothly. *A memory. Each traveler gives one memory of the person they love most. Small ones. We are not monsters.*

"That's exactly what a monster would say," Tam muttered.

"It's also standard rates," Vess admitted. She sounded tired. "Refuse, and it holds the bridge. Fight it, and we're fighting the contract-law of an entire mountain, which the court will feel like a shout. Pay, and we lose something we'll spend the rest of the walk trying to remember we lost."

Ada looked at Sefa's face in her memory. Sefa laughing on the ferry deck with her sleeves rolled up. Sefa arguing about oars. Sefa pressing the river-stone into her palm and saying, *it remembers directions home, so you don't have to carry me, just carry this.* Which memory? If she gave one, would the others stay whole, or would they all go slightly gray, slightly thinner, like cloth washed too many times?

She untied the stone from her wrist—she'd already tied it there that morning, without deciding to, the way you breathe—and held it up.

"I'll pay in stone instead," she said. "River-stone. It remembers directions home. That's worth more than any memory I own, ask anyone."

The silence from the arch went on so long that Ada started composing her apology to Sefa in advance, the one where she explained how she'd traded away the thing that was supposed to bring her back.

*Accepted,* the toll-keeper said at last, sounding genuinely puzzled, maybe even moved, insofar as architecture can be either. *An unusual tender. The stone remembers directions home. The vessel may pass.*

Tam paid a memory of his mother's kitchen—he chose it fast, eyes shut, jaw tight, and came out the other side of the arch pale and reaching for a smell he could no longer name. Vess paid something she didn't announce, and none of them asked, and her flame burned a little dimmer for the next hour before recovering.

On the far side of the bridge, Ada retied the stone to her wrist and found her hands weren't quite steady.

"You gave away the only map home," Tam said quietly.

"It remembers directions," Ada said. "Not me. Maybe the stone knows the way even if it's not touching my wrist." She didn't believe it. She tied it tighter anyway.

* * *

The fog found them that evening despite everything.

Vess smelled it first—a change in the air, a sweetness under the mineral smell of the deep road, like honey left in water. "Off the road," she snapped. "Behind the marker, press flat, don't breathe deep, and whatever you remember wanting—don't follow it."

They wedged themselves into a collapsed waystation alcove as the fog came down the switchbacks above them like a slow white flood. It wasn't natural fog. It moved with intention, pooling and pausing, and through it Ada heard voices—faint, familiar-shaped, saying things in the voices of people she loved.

*"Ada. Over here. You forgot something at the ferry."*

Her body started rising before her mind caught it. Vess pinned her shoulder with one claw, gentle as a falling beam.

"That's not her," Vess breathed.

"I know."

"She isn't here. Nothing is here. That's the point of the fog—it doesn't show you what you miss. It shows you why you were walking, so you'll forget the danger part and remember only the wanting."

Through the thinning drift, Tam was whispering to himself, rapid and rhythmic: names, dates, ledger entries, crimes. "Falsified entry four hundred eleven, grain levy, autumn court year ninety-three. Falsified entry four hundred twelve—" Ada realized he was reciting his own falsifications, every record he'd ever cooked, using his guilt as an anchor. Whatever else Tam was, he was a man who knew exactly how much he owed, and shame apparently held him to the ground better than rope ever could.

The fog passed over them for what felt like a season and was probably twenty minutes. When the air cleared, all three of them sat in the alcove, shaking slightly, gripping things: Ada gripped her sword hilt, Vess gripped her flame, Tam gripped his book.

"What happens to people who follow it?" Ada asked.

"They keep walking," Vess said. "Down the road, forever, remembering why they set out and forgetting everything else. You'll see them soon. The road calls them the settled."

* * *

They saw the settled the next morning.

At first Ada took them for statues. Figures standing along the verges of the great road, spaced irregularly, facing downhill—all of them facing downhill, toward the court, though some had clearly been walking uphill when the fog took them and had simply turned around mid-stride. Farmers in rotted coats. Merchants with saddle packs fused to their shoulders by decades of mineral drip. A woman in what had once been fine court dress, her hand raised as if mid-gesture, mid-sentence, mid-life.

As they passed, one of the figures turned its head. Slowly. Like a tree deciding.

"Why are you walking?" it asked Ada. Its voice was papery and kind. That was the worst part—the kindness. "Why are you walking? You look like you've forgotten. Everyone forgets. Come stand with us and we'll help you remember."

"I'm walking to steal back my soul," Ada said, because lying to the fog-broken seemed pointless, and because saying it out loud made it more real, and she needed it more real right then.

The figure considered this with its ruined face.

"That's a good reason," it said. "I had a good reason once." And it turned back downhill, patient as geology.

Ada walked faster after that. She didn't run—Vess had rules about running—but her stride lengthened and her jaw set and she caught herself checking the river-stone on her wrist every few minutes like a woman counting money in a bad neighborhood.

*Still there. Still mine. Still pointed home.*

*

They camped that night in a side gallery where the road branched—two tunnels, one signed (in Tam's halting translation) *to the filing halls, authorized bearers only*, the other unsigned, descending into dark that their lantern-light gave up on after thirty feet.

Vess curled around her flame and slept the lightless sleep of dragons. Tam sat propped against his pack, copying entries from the prophecy book into his own notebook by lantern-glow, muttering figures. He'd barely spoken since the bridge.

Ada sat with her back to the warm scale-wall of the sleeping dragon and turned the river-stone over in her fingers. It was ordinary. Grey-green, river-smooth, the kind of stone Sefa had a hundred of in jars on every windowsill of the ferry house. Except this one was warm, warmer than her hand could account for, and when she held it up to her ear—she'd never admit to doing this, ever, to anyone—she thought she heard water. Faint. Moving. Going somewhere.

Home, probably. Home always had water in it.

"You're doing the thing," Tam said without looking up.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at an object like it owes you answers. I've catalogued that expression across four provinces. It means you're scared and you're converting it into stubbornness."

"Is there another way to convert it?"

"No. Stubbornness is the only currency fear respects." He dipped his pen. "For what it's worth—I think giving the bridge your stone instead of a memory was the smartest stupid thing anyone's done on these roads in a century. The toll-keeper didn't know what to charge for a gift. You beat a thousand-year-old contract with sentiment."

"It wasn't a gift. I expect it back."

"That's what makes it sentiment."

Ada almost smiled. Then the smile died, because Tam had gone still over his notebook, pen lifted, staring at the open prophecy book with the particular frozen look of a man who has just read his own name somewhere it shouldn't be.

"Tam."

"There's a road condition," he said slowly. "Listed here. Under hazards. *Memory-fog: causes travelers to forget why they walk. Countermeasure unknown, except—*" He swallowed. "Except the ledger says there's a stretch ahead, past the third descent, where the fog doesn't make you forget why you're walking. It makes you remember. Everything. All at once. Every grief you've ever carried, in full, forever. Travelers come out the other side either dead or changed."

"Changed how?"

He turned the book toward her. His finger shook slightly against the page. Below the warning, in ink faded to brown, someone had added a single line in a different hand:

*The hollow ones walk through untouched. The road has nothing to pour into an empty cup.*

Ada looked at the words for a long time. Then at the stone on her wrist. Then at the dark mouth of the unsigned tunnel, which breathed its old warm breath out of the deep of the world, patient as everything else down here.

"Well," she said, standing, tying her pack tight. "First good news all week."
