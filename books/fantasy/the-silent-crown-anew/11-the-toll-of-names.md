## 11. The Toll of Names

Tam had crossed a great many bridges in his life, and he had falsified the toll records for at least half of them. He knew the grammar of crossings: the guard's bored questions, the traveler's practiced lies, the ledger open on its stand like a mouth waiting to be fed. He had spent eleven years at court beneath the mountain learning that every passage in the world was, at bottom, an exchange—something given, something withheld, and a record kept of both.

The bridge at the bottom of the mountain's throat was different. Tam knew it was different before they reached it, because there was no ledger.

There was no booth, either. No gate, no chain, no armed men leaning on halberds with the particular slouch of soldiers who had not seen combat in years. There was only a figure standing at the near end of the span, wrapped in a cloak the color of wet slate, and the bridge itself—a single arch of black stone crossing a gorge that went down further than Tam's eyes were willing to follow. The air that rose from it smelled of old paper. Not burning paper. Paper that had been waiting a very long time to be read.

"That's not a person," Vess said. She had taken human shape for the descent—she could hold it for hours now, she said, though her shadow still moved wrong, too slow, like smoke deciding whether to rise—and she stood at Tam's shoulder with her arms folded and her amber eyes narrowed at the figure in slate. "Or it is, but only the way a signature is a person."

"It's a toll-keeper," Ada said.

She had gone quiet since the soul-fog two miles back, since the stretch of road where all three of them had stopped mid-stride and stared at each other with the same question in their faces: *why are we walking?* It had been Sefa's river-stone on Ada's wrist that pulled them through—the stone remembered directions home, and home, for the moment, was whatever direction led away from the fog. Tam had watched her touch it three times since, absently, the way you'd touch a talisman or a wound.

"You know this thing?" he asked.

"I know what my grandmother called them." Ada's voice was flat, which Tam had learned meant she was frightened. "When I was small. She said if you ever came to a bridge with nothing on it, you should walk a long way around. She said the keepers under the mountain don't take coin."

"What do they take?"

Ada didn't answer. The toll-keeper did—or rather, it turned. The motion was smooth and total, like a page turning, and beneath the hood there was no face. There was a blankness, pale as unmarked vellum, and Tam felt something in his chest clench with a scribe's instinctive horror. He had spent his whole career writing things onto blank surfaces. He had never once considered what it would feel like to be written onto one.

"TRAVELERS," the toll-keeper said. The voice came from everywhere at once—from the stone of the bridge, from the walls of the gorge, from somewhere behind Tam's own teeth. It was not loud. It did not need to be. "THREE PASS. THREE TOLLS."

"We don't have anything," Vess said. "Unless it wants dragon-scale, and I'll warn you, that conversation goes badly."

"TOLL IS NOT PAID IN THINGS." The blank face inclined toward Tam. Of the three of them, it looked at him longest, and Tam understood why with a cold drop in his stomach. A toll-keeper who ate names would smell his from a mile off. Tam of House Verrellan, third son, disgraced scribe, author of four hundred falsified entries in the court's records of sold souls—he was, by weight of ink alone, probably the most *named* thing on this road. "TOLL IS PAID IN WHAT YOU ARE CALLED. SPEAK YOUR NAME AND IT IS MINE. PASS WITHOUT IT AND KEEP THE REST."

"And if we refuse?" Ada asked.

The toll-keeper did not answer. It simply stepped aside—one smooth sliding motion, like furniture moving in an empty house—and revealed the bridge stretching out over the gorge, and below the gorge, far below, something drifting. Shapes. Pale, slow shapes turning in the dark like leaves in a flooded street.

Tam counted nine of them before he made himself stop counting. They were roughly person-shaped. One of them, as he watched, opened what might have been a mouth and said, soundlessly, over and over, a word he was suddenly desperate never to learn.

"The ones who refused," Ada said quietly. "They tried to cross without paying, or they paid something else instead of their name, and now they're—" She stopped. Started again. "They're trying to remember what to call themselves."

"They can't," Vess said. Her voice had lost its edge; it was soft now, and old. "I've heard of these. From before my exile. The old roads under the mountain were built on bargains, and the oldest bargain of all is identity. You want to pass? Prove you're someone. And when you're done proving it, you're no one." She looked at Tam. "You know more about names on records than anyone alive, little scribe. Tell me I'm wrong."

Tam wanted to tell her she was wrong. What he said instead was: "It's worse than that."

Both of them turned.

"A name isn't just what people shout at you," he said. His hands had started doing the thing they did when he was nervous—ghosting across an invisible page, shaping letters out of air. He forced them still. "A name is an anchor. Every record I ever touched, every entry I falsified, every soul the court filed away—it was all indexed by name. That's how the bureaucracy works. That's how any bureaucracy works. You can steal the thing itself, but as long as the name stays in the ledger, the theft can be traced, reversed, appealed." He swallowed. "If he gives up his name here—if it's *eaten*, truly eaten, removed from circulation—then every record bearing that name stops pointing at him. It points at nothing. A blank where a man used to be."

"So the records survive," Ada said slowly, "but they stop being true."

"The records become true," Tam corrected. "That's the horror of it. If Tam Verrellan ceases to exist, then every entry signed Tam Verrellan becomes a forgery by a man who never lived. Unimpeachable. Untraceable. The court can't hunt him, because there's no him to hunt. But—" and here his voice did something humiliating, cracked slightly in the middle, and he pressed on anyway— "but neither can anyone remember him. My mother. My brothers. The woman in the lower archive who used to leave honey-bread on my desk because I worked past supper. To them it won't be grief. Grief needs a shape. It'll be a hole shaped like nothing at all. They'll walk past the empty desk and feel sad and never know why."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour, camped in the lee of the gorge wall where the wind couldn't reach them. Vess argued against it first, then, oddly, argued for it—her reasoning was draconic and practical: *the Regent cannot seize what cannot be found, and we will need an unfound man inside the court*. Ada argued against it longer, and harder, and for reasons that had nothing to do with tactics.

"You don't get to erase yourself," she said, crouched in front of him, firelight catching the stubborn set of her jaw. "I've spent my whole life unwritten. Written *out*. Do you know what that does to a person? Every morning waking up knowing the world has a file with your description in it and your name torn off? And you want to volunteer?"

"It's not the same."

"Don't you dare—"

"It isn't," Tam said, and held her gaze, and found that for once in his life he wasn't performing. "You were robbed, Ada. This is the opposite of robbery. Everything else in my life I've traded away cheaply—my honor, my family's honor, other people's souls, God forgive me, I filed them like *coins*—and every trade left me more named and less worth naming. This is the one transaction I can make where the price and the purchase are the same thing. I give up the lie of myself and I buy back—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the whole shabby trembling apparatus of him— "whatever's underneath. Maybe there's nothing underneath. Maybe I'm all forgery down to the grain. But I'd like to find out while the finding-out can still do some good."

Ada stared at him for a long time. Then she said, "Sefa gave me a stone that remembers the way home. What do you have that remembers *you*?"

And that was the question that kept Tam awake until the false dawn, staring up at rock, because the honest answer was: nothing. No locket, no scar with a story, no river-stone. He had a book of prophecies that wasn't really prophecies, and a talent for forgery, and a memory full of other people's names. If he surrendered his own, what remained would be entirely made of everyone else.

Which, he thought as sleep finally took him, might be the most honest thing a scribe could ever be.

* * *

The toll-keeper had not moved. Tam suspected it did not move between travelers at all—that it simply stood at the head of the bridge through the centuries, patient as punctuation, waiting for the next clause.

He walked up to it alone. Ada and Vess waited twenty paces back, close enough to see, too far to hear. He'd insisted on that. Some transactions should be witnessed but not shared.

"I'm ready," Tam said.

"SPEAK."

"My name is—" and here his voice failed him, briefly, the way it fails everyone at the edge of a thing that cannot be undone, and he thought of his mother's kitchen, and the honey-bread, and the way his father used to say *Verrellan boys don't cry* while crying himself after the third son's disgrace, and then he thought of Ada Villalobos walking out of a valley that had hidden her for twenty-three years, choosing to be seen, choosing to be *counted*, and he understood that he had been doing it backward his entire life. "My name is Tam Verrellan. Take it. Take all of it—the family name, the given name, the whole signature. I release my claim to it. It's yours. Freely given, freely eaten, no appeal."

The blank face regarded him. Then, gently—gently, the way a librarian handles a fragile volume—it extended one hand from the slate-colored cloak. The hand was pale vellum to the wrist, and the fingers were long, and they did not grab. They waited.

Tam took them.

The sensation was not pain. That was somehow the worst part. It was the sensation of a page being turned inside him. He felt the name lift out—not torn, *lifted*, the way a well-made seal lifts clean from wax—and with it went everything stitched to the name. His mother's voice saying it over a cradle. His instructors marking it at the top of examinations. His own hand signing confession after confession in the court's inquiry chamber. The way it looked in ink. The way it sounded shouted across a courtyard. The shape of the shame attached to it and the shape of the small pride, the pride he'd never admitted to, of being *someone*, of mattering enough to falsify records about, of being indexed in the great gray machine beneath the mountain.

It went. All of it went. And Tam stood on the near side of the bridge and reached for himself and found—

Found a man. Just a man. Short, ink-stained, thirty-one years old by a count he could no longer verify, with a habit of ghosting his hands across invisible pages and a memory full of other people's names and a fierce, inexplicable tenderness toward a mercenary with a god-sworn blade and a dragon who pretended to be annoyed by everything. He could not recall his childhood. He could not recall his crimes—which meant he also could not recall his reasons, his excuses, the elaborate architecture of justification he'd built over eleven years. All of that had been *Verrellan*. What was left was simpler, and smaller, and it was looking at the toll-keeper with tears running down its face and not knowing, precisely, whom it mourned.

"FREELY GIVEN," the toll-keeper said, and its voice had changed—warmer by a degree, the way a room is warmer when a fire has just been fed. "FREELY EATEN. THE RECORDS OF TAM VERRELLAN ARE NOW RECORDS OF NO ONE. HIS FORGERIES ARE FORGED BY NO ONE. HIS DEBTS ARE OWED BY NO ONE." A pause. The vellum face tilted, almost curious. "HE IS ALSO FREE OF NOTHING THAT WAS TRUE."

"Meaning what?" Tam asked. His voice sounded strange to himself. Lighter. Emptied.

"THE COURT WILL NOT FIND HIM. BUT NEITHER CAN THE COURT FIND WHAT HE CARRIES." The hood turned, fractionally, toward the prophecy book in Tam's pack—the ledger of small sold things, the list of everything the crown had bought and buried. "THE BOOK WAS NEVER SIGNED. IT REMEMBERS ITS OWN WAY, AS HE NOW MUST."

Behind him, boots on stone. Ada arrived first, Vess a stride behind, and Ada grabbed Tam by the shoulders and turned him around and looked at him hard, the way you look at someone returned from drowning.

"Say something," she demanded. "Anything. So I know it's still you."

"I don't know who 'you' means anymore," Tam said, "but whoever I am, I'm standing here, and I'm coming with you, and I appear to be crying, which is embarrassing, so perhaps we could walk."

Ada laughed—a wet, cracked laugh—and pulled him into a hug that lasted exactly three seconds and rearranged something fundamental in whatever Tam now was. Vess, in her borrowed human shape, patted his head twice with one hand, which from her was practically a sonnet.

They crossed the bridge together. Below them, the pale shapes drifted and mouthed their forgotten words, and Tam made himself look at them, made himself witness, because someone had to carry the memory of the ones who'd refused the price, and he was, he realized, now uniquely qualified for the work of remembering things that had been erased.

Halfway across, the toll-keeper spoke one last time—not loudly, not to all of them, but in a tone that Tam alone seemed meant to hear:

"THE LAST SCRIBE WHO PASSED THIS WAY LEFT SOMETHING BEHIND. IT IS STILL WAITING ON THE FAR SIDE. SHE SAID SOMEONE WOULD COME WHO HAD NOTHING LEFT TO SIGN WITH."

The far side of the bridge resolved out of the dark as they walked: a landing, a tunnel mouth breathing cold air, and there, propped against the stone as if placed there yesterday, a lantern of green glass, lit, with a strip of parchment tied to its handle.

Tam picked it up. The handwriting was old-fashioned, formal, a court-trained hand—but the message was five words long, and it was not addressed to a name.

*To whoever is left: hurry.*

Beyond the tunnel mouth, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the bones of the mountain, came a sound none of them had heard before and all of them recognized instantly.

Someone, deep beneath the earth, was ringing a bell that made no noise—and every hair on Tam's arm stood up, because he could feel it in the place where his name used to live.
