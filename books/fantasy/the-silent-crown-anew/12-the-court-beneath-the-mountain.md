## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The road beneath the mountain did not end so much as improve.

Ada had spent three days walking through dark that was honest about itself—rough stone, cold air, the smell of deep water. Then the passage widened, and the walls changed under her boots, and she realized she was no longer walking on rock at all but on tile: white, seamless, faintly warm, laid in patterns that repeated just often enough to make her eyes hurt if she tried to follow them.

"Careful," Vess said behind her. The dragon had folded herself down to something like a tall woman wrapped in a grey cloak, which fooled nobody, least of all the road. "The floor reads weight. It's deciding what you are."

"What am I?"

"Ask it in a moment."

Light came from nowhere in particular. It pooled along the walls in slow tides, brightening as they walked, dimming behind them, as if the corridor were a throat swallowing them gently. And then the throat opened.

The Court Beneath the Mountain spread out below them like a city that had been designed by someone who hated cities.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, and Ada resented it immediately. Terraces of pale stone rose in tiers, hung with banners of woven silver that rippled without wind. Fountains played with liquid light instead of water. Music drifted up from somewhere below—a harp, maybe, or several harps tuned to each other—and beneath the music ran a sound she took a moment to identify: the shuffling of paper. Thousands of pages turning. A whisper like snowfall, constant, everywhere.

And people. Hundreds of them. Clerks in dove-grey robes moving between counters and desks with the unhurried purpose of ants who had been given excellent pensions. Petitioners stood in lines that curved and branched and fed into other lines, patient as furniture. Nobody hurried. Nobody spoke above a murmur. Above it all, suspended somehow in the air over the central terrace, hung an enormous ledger—open, pages drifting lazily in a breeze that did not exist, entries writing themselves in ink that gleamed wet and never dried.

"Oh," Tam said softly. He had gone the color of old candle wax. "It's bigger than when I left."

"You *worked* here," Ada said.

"I filed things here." His voice had the flatness of a man reciting his own indictment. "For eleven years. I told myself it was record-keeping. Archival work. Important cultural preservation." He swallowed. "Standing here again, I can't remember how I made myself believe that."

They descended the last stair together, and the Court noticed them.

Not dramatically. No horns, no guards, no sudden silence. But the nearest clerks paused mid-stride, the way birds pause before deciding you're not worth flying away from. A woman at the first counter looked up, and her eyes moved across Ada's face the way an assessor's eye moves across livestock—quick, thorough, uninterested in anything the animal might feel about it.

"Welcome to the Court of Quiet Things," the woman said. Her voice was warm the way a ledger is warm: balanced, closed, containing nothing. "How may we file you?"

"We're not—" Ada started.

"We're pilgrims," Tam said smoothly, stepping half in front of her. "Come to view the Regent's collection. We've heard the archives are open to the faithful."

Something flickered behind the clerk's eyes—the faintest tightening, like a fisherman feeling a nibble and choosing patience. "Of course. Visitor tokens, please." She slid three discs of grey metal across the counter. They were cold, and they hummed against Ada's palm the same way her blade hummed near truth. "Wear these at all times. They will tell us where you are. This is for your safety."

"For ours," Ada repeated.

"The Court is very safe," the clerk agreed. "That is its entire purpose. Next."

They were moved along by the gentle pressure of the line behind them, and then they were inside it, three more shapes in the murmur, and Ada had a moment to look properly at where she was.

The beauty kept unfolding, and the revulsion kept arriving a half-step behind it, like thunder after lightning. Every fountain of liquid light was fed by channels running from the archive levels below—she could see the pipes, elegant and exposed, and the light in them pulsed. Not steadily. Like breath. Like heartbeat.

"That light isn't decorative," Vess murmured. Her human disguise had sweat beading at the hairline; this place pressed on her the way the valley pressed on magic, only inverted. "It's rendered soul-light. Diluted. What you're looking at is the runoff."

"The runoff of what?"

Vess didn't answer. She nodded instead toward the great hanging ledger overhead, and Ada followed her gaze and understood.

The pages weren't writing themselves.

They were being written. She caught one entry mid-formation, letters assembling stroke by stroke in that wet-gleaming ink: *One (1) fondness for morning rain, withdrawn from bearer, credited to Estate of Quiet Things.* Below it: *One (1) memory of a sister's laugh, partial, condition fair.* Below that: *One (1) courage, small denomination, sold under duress, non-refundable.*

Souls, filed like coins. Not whole souls—not usually—but the pieces of them, the small change of a life, siphoned off in denominations most people would never miss until the day they reached for them and found the purse empty.

Ada thought of the valley. Of the neighbors who'd gone vague in middle age, smiling at their own children like friendly strangers. Of old Brammel the smith, who had once sung while he worked and then, one year, simply stopped, and couldn't say why, only that singing seemed "not for him anymore."

She thought of her mother, who had loved her fiercely and distractedly, like someone reading a letter by firelight with half her mind on the fire.

Her hand went to the ember-warmth under her ribs, the hollow where her name should sit, and for the first time she was glad of it. Glad of the hollowness. It meant there was nothing left in her to take.

*You were sold,* Tam had told her. *Sold twice. You're the receipt.*

She looked at the great ledger and understood, finally, what kind of book she was.

* * *

They walked. The Court let them walk. That was the thing Ada couldn't shake—how *permissive* it all was. She'd expected chains and teeth. Instead she got tokens and corridors and the soft snowfall-shuffle of turning pages, and everywhere the sense of being measured rather than watched. A court didn't need to stop you, she realized. It needed only to file you. Everything that happened to you afterward was paperwork.

They passed a hall where petitioners knelt before a counter, signing documents with trembling hands. A young man was weeping quietly as he signed; the clerk across from him patted his wrist with genuine tenderness and slid him a receipt. Ada slowed enough to read the placard beside the counter: *Consolidation Services. Grief too heavy? We accept donations.*

"They make it kind," Tam whispered. "That was the part that broke me, in the end. Not the cruelty. The kindness. It's real kindness—they train for it. The clerk who took your grief genuinely wanted to comfort you. The system just also wanted your grief." His jaw worked. "I falsified records because I couldn't stand how *tidy* it was. A man came in whose daughter had been taken—filed, I mean, processed—and he asked me for a copy of the receipt, just to hold something of hers. There was no copy. There's never a copy. So I wrote him one. From memory. And then I kept writing them, and correcting ledgers, and eventually they noticed the corrections and I ran."

"And now we're back," Ada said. "Walking around wearing their tokens."

"I have a plan."

"You always have a plan. Your plans involve me standing in places."

"Your plans involve hitting things," Tam said. "We complement each other."

Ahead, the crowd thickened toward the central terrace, and the music swelled, and Ada felt the token at her throat grow warm. Around them, petitioners were turning toward the terrace's far edge with expressions Ada could only call *hungry reverence*, and she understood that something was about to happen that these people had waited for—that maybe some of them had sold years of their lives for the privilege of standing here for.

"The Regent holds audience at dusk-bell," a nearby clerk announced to no one and everyone. "All quieted accounts may approach the railing. All outstanding debts may be forgiven at the Regent's discretion. Praise the quiet."

"Praise the quiet," murmured five hundred voices, soft as prayer, flat as arithmetic.

Ada looked at Vess. The dragon's face, under the grey hood, had gone very still—the stillness of a creature watching an old wound walk past wearing jewelry.

"It's time," Vess said. "You wanted to see what wears your god's voice. Look."

* * *

The Regent of Quiet Things ascended the terrace stair at dusk-bell, and the Court held its breath, and Ada made herself look.

She had prepared for a monster. Horns, shadow, something with too many joints. She had prepared for grandeur, even—the velvet-and-skull aesthetic of power that had rotted every throne she'd ever heard songs about.

What she got was a small person in plain grey robes, silver-haired, with a face like a beloved schoolteacher: lined, mild, arranged around two eyes of extraordinary gentleness. The Regent walked slowly, leaning on no cane, needing none, and smiled at the crowd with such unguarded warmth that Ada felt her own mouth twitch upward in response and had to bite it flat.

Around the Regent's neck lay a necklace.

It was made of beads—or what looked like beads, at first glance. Dozens of them, strung on a fine silver chain, each bead the size of a thumbnail and each one *moving*. Turning slowly. Catching the light differently than any gemstone ever caught it, because they weren't reflecting light. They were producing it. Faintly, from within, each bead glowed with a color that had no name in any language Ada knew—a color like the moment before speaking, like the shape of a word held behind the teeth.

And when the Regent passed close along the railing, close enough that Ada could have touched the hem of those plain grey robes, the necklace *sounded*.

Not aloud. Inside. A chord struck somewhere behind her sternum, vast and low and grieving, a voice trying to speak through a throat of glass beads, and Ada knew—knew the way her blade knew truth, the way the valley knew absence—that she was hearing the Silent God. Strangled into ornament. Worn to parties.

The god was not silent. The god had *never* been silent. It was screaming, in the one register the Regent couldn't quite muffle, and every soul in the Court heard it as background music and mistook it for peace.

Revulsion hit Ada like cold water, and wonder arrived a heartbeat later, dressed in the same clothes, and the two of them stood together in her chest holding hands, and she hated that. Hated that her body could feel awe in this place. That was the trick of it, she understood suddenly. That was the whole architecture. The Court didn't hide what it was. It draped what it was in enough beauty that your own senses argued on its behalf. You'd walk out lighter by ten years and grateful for the experience.

The Regent raised one hand, and the snowfall-hush of pages went still.

"Children of the quiet," the Regent said. The voice was exactly what the face promised: warm, unhurried, tired in a way that read as wisdom. "I know why you've come. You carry debts. Griefs. Names too heavy to say aloud." A smile, gentle as dusk. "Bring them to us. We will hold them for you. We have held so many. We will hold yours as though it were our own."

*Because it becomes your own,* Ada thought. *Because everything you hold, you keep.*

Her hand drifted to her blade, and the blade hummed against her palm—loud, insistent, louder than she'd ever felt it—and for one bad moment she thought everyone must hear it.

The Regent's head turned.

Slowly. Mildly. The way a librarian turns toward a dropped book. Those gentle eyes swept the crowd and stopped—not on Ada, not quite, but on the space just around her, the way a man looks at a smudge on otherwise clean glass.

And the necklace sang one note. Just one. Soft. Almost curious.

In the pit of her stomach, under the ribs, in the hollow where her soul should have been, Ada felt something answer. Something small and grey and caged, three floors down, turning in its sleep.

The Regent's smile deepened by a single degree.

"How lovely," the Regent said to the crowd, to no one, to the space just around a mercenary with a humming sword. "It seems an account long dormant has come home to visit."

Beside Ada, Tam had stopped breathing entirely.

And from the tier below, unhurried, flanked by clerks with ledgers already open to a fresh page, three court officials began to climb the stairs toward them.
