## 13. An Audience of Ledgers

The court beneath the mountain did not look like a place where evil was done. That was the first thing Tam noticed, and the thing that frightened him most.

He had expected dark. He had expected screaming. What he got was lamplight—hundreds of small brass lamps burning a flame that gave off light but no heat—and the soft, ceaseless whisper of paper. Paper being turned. Paper being stamped. Paper being filed away forever.

They had come up out of the old trade road into a hall so tall the ceiling vanished into shadow, and the shadow itself was organized. Shelves climbed the walls in perfect tiers, and on every shelf were drawers—thousands upon thousands of them, glass-fronted, each one labeled in a hand so fine it looked like frost. And inside each drawer—

Tam didn't let himself finish the thought. He kept his eyes on the floor, which was polished stone worn into a shallow groove down the center, the way church floors wear where pilgrims kneel. Except nobody knelt here. Everybody here was *filed*.

A clerk approached them. Not a guard—a clerk, ink-stained fingers, spectacles with three lenses, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who had never once been told no.

"Business?" the clerk asked.

"Hiring," Tam said, before Ada could answer. He pitched his voice low and bored, the voice of a man whose business was other people's problems. "Retrieval work. We've come to speak with whoever handles outstanding accounts."

The clerk's three lenses rotated, one after another, focusing on Tam like a slow eye learning to see him. "Accounts are handled by the Office of Quiet Things."

"Then we'll want the Regent."

Something flickered across the clerk's face—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "The Regent does not receive hirelings."

"The Regent receives this." Tam drew the prophecy book from inside his coat, just far enough that the clerk could see the cover: plain grey leather, unmarked, the kind of book designed to be overlooked. The kind of book you only notice when it's already too late.

He watched the clerk watch the book. Watched the man's throat move. Watched him decide, in the space of two seconds, that whatever was written in that grey binding outranked his own position, his own pension, possibly his own continued existence.

"Wait here," the clerk said, and for the first time he sounded like a servant instead of a gate.

* * *

Ada stood beside him without moving, arms crossed, chin down—the posture of a bodyguard who'd rather be anywhere else. She'd been playing the role since they entered the court, and she was good at it, better than Tam had any right to expect. She slouched. She looked bored. She looked dangerous in the specific way mercenaries look dangerous: not threatening anyone, just radiating the fact that she had killed people and hadn't enjoyed it enough to stop.

But Tam could feel her heart going through her ribs. He was close enough to hear it.

"They're souls," she murmured, barely moving her lips, eyes fixed on the middle distance. "In the drawers. I can hear them."

"I know."

"You *knew*. You worked here."

"Twelve years," Tam said. "And I never opened one. That's how it works, Ada. You don't open the drawers. You file things *into* them. There's always a form. There's always a signature. By the time anything is in a drawer, it has been signed for by everyone except the thing itself."

"That's worse."

"It's much worse," he agreed. "That's why I ran."

She almost smiled. It was a terrible little smile, the kind people make when they're holding themselves together with both hands. "If we die down here—"

"We won't."

"—I want you to know I'm going to haunt you specifically."

"Understood," Tam said. "I'd expect nothing less."

The clerk returned with two more clerks and a third person who was not a clerk. This one wore grey robes cut like a uniform, and moved the way water moves through a pipe: efficiently, directionlessly, without ever appearing to choose anything. The Regent's chamberlain, if Tam remembered correctly. He had seen the chamberlain twice in twelve years and been grateful both times.

"The Regent will see the scribe," the chamberlain said. Its voice had no gender and very little warmth. "Not the mercenary."

"She stays," Tam said. "My blade doesn't leave my side. Court rules."

"Court rules do not apply to hirelings."

"Then she's not a hireling. She's my retainer. Retained. Look it up—it's in the ninth codex, under *persons who may not be separated from their employers by persons who are about to regret it*."

There was a pause. Tam had spent twelve years in this bureaucracy, and he knew its secret weakness the way a poacher knows a fence: it hated improvisation more than it hated defiance. A rule existed for everything, but no rule existed for a situation that hadn't been filed yet, and creating new categories required paperwork, and paperwork required time, and time was the one currency the court hoarded most jealously.

The chamberlain created the category. Tam watched it happen—a tiny tightening around the eyes, a decision made and immediately regretted.

"Both of you," it said. "Touch nothing. Sign nothing."

* * *

They walked through the filing halls for what felt like an hour. Tam counted turns out of habit—left, left, right, down a stair, right again—and realized after a while that the counting was useless, because the halls were not arranged in space. They were arranged in *precedence*. You did not walk toward the Regent; you walked toward matters of increasing importance, and the architecture simply agreed to meet you there.

The shelves changed as they went. In the outer halls, the glass drawers held grey embers, dim and small, the souls of ordinary sold people—farmers' daughters traded for rain, soldiers' sons traded for victories, whole villages traded for roads. Tam had helped file some of those. He kept walking. His stomach kept trying to climb out of him.

Deeper in, the drawers grew larger and the embers brighter. He saw one that burned gold. He saw one that burned black, which he decided firmly not to think about. He saw a drawer with no ember at all, only a label reading *RESERVED*, and he understood suddenly, completely, whose reservation it was.

Beside him, Ada's breathing had gone thin and controlled. She wasn't looking at the drawers anymore. She was looking at the labels.

Looking, Tam realized, for names.

*Don't,* he wanted to say. *Don't find yours. Not here. Not like this.* But he said nothing, because saying something would have meant admitting he'd already found hers, weeks ago, in the ledger of small prophecies: *a soul, unnamed, sold twice.* Sold once by her grandparents' generation. Bought back by the god who then went silent. Filed here, somewhere, as collateral.

*A receipt,* he'd told her. *You are the receipt.*

She had hit him. Then she had thanked him. Then she had hit him again. It had been, all things considered, a productive evening.

* * *

The Regent of Quiet Things received them in a round room with no lamps.

It didn't need lamps. Light came from everywhere and nowhere, sourceless and even, the light of a page held up to the sun. Bookshelves ringed the walls, but these books were different from the archive's—they were bound in white and gold, and they hummed. Very faintly. Like struck glasses that refused to finish ringing.

And above the great desk, suspended in the air by nothing at all, hung the necklace.

Tam had heard about it for twelve years the way you hear about a rumor you refuse to confirm. Seeing it stopped his breath. It was a strand of beads—or things shaped like beads—but they moved. Each one writhed gently against its neighbors, and from each one came a sound too small to be called a sound: a syllable, a breath, half a word. Voices. Dozens of voices. Hundreds. Strung together and silenced, all of them speaking at once and none of them audible, and the combined effect was a silence so dense it pressed on the ears like deep water.

The Silent God's voice. Stolen, strangled, and worn as jewelry.

The Regent sat behind the desk. It had a face. That was somehow the worst part—that after all of it, the Regent had a mild, reasonable face, the face of a provincial magistrate or a beloved schoolmaster, lined with patience and framed by ordinary grey hair. It looked like someone's grandfather. It looked like it would remember your birthday.

"Scribe," the Regent said warmly. "How good of you to come home."

"I'm not a scribe anymore."

"No," the Regent agreed pleasantly. "You're a thief, a falsifier, and a fugitive. And yet." It spread its hands, taking in Tam, the room, the humming shelves. "Here you are."

"I've brought you something worth more than my arrest."

The Regent's eyebrows rose a fraction. Encouragement, performed.

Tam lifted the prophecy book and set it on the desk between them. The moment it touched the wood, every white-and-gold book on the shelves shuddered at once, like a flock startled off a wire. The Regent's hand came down flat on the grey cover—not grabbing it. Holding it down. The way you hold down something alive.

"Do you know what this is?" Tam asked.

"I know what it *isn't*," the Regent said. "It isn't mine. Which in this room is nearly unprecedented." The mild face tilted. "You are offering me the one record I have spent three hundred years failing to acquire. What do you want for it?"

"Time," Tam said. "One night. My companion and I walk out of this court untouched, and tomorrow the book is delivered to your desk with a full index and a certified transcription. I'm a scribe, Your Quietness. I know the value of a clean copy."

"And if I simply take it now and arrest you now?"

"Then you get the book," Tam said, "and you never learn what the final entry means."

Silence—real silence, the manufactured kind, pressing down from the necklace. Tam held still and let the silence do his negotiating for him. He had learned that trick in the archives: never fill the quiet. Make the powerful sit in it.

The Regent smiled. It was a grandfather's smile, and it made Tam's skin crawl off his bones.

"One night," the Regent said. "You may move freely through the lower halls until the last bell—which does not ring, incidentally; nothing here rings, you'll find. You will be observed. You will be *assessed*. And scribe—" the mild voice dropped half a tone, gentle as a lid closing, "—I know your face. Names can be eaten. Faces cannot. Whatever you imagine you erased, you erased nothing. You merely tidied."

Tam bowed. It cost him something physical to bend his spine in front of that desk, and he paid it gladly, because bending was cheaper than breaking.

* * *

They walked out of the round room and back into the humming halls, escorted by a clerk who pretended to be furniture.

Ada waited until they were four turns deep before she spoke, and her whisper shook. "He knows your face."

"He always did. The name-eating bought us confusion, not safety. I told you it was a gamble."

"You said it would erase you from every record."

"It did. Records aren't faces." Tam wiped sweat from his upper lip. "We have until the last bell. That's real. He wants that book indexed—he wants it *read properly*, because he's never dared read it himself. Three hundred years of theft and he's afraid of one line of text. Remember that fear. It's the truest thing about him."

Ahead of them, the corridor opened onto a gallery Tam remembered with a clarity that felt like a wound: the Hall of Ordinary Souls, where the smallest transactions were kept. Farmers. Sailors. Mercenaries.

Ada had stopped walking.

She was standing in front of a case of glass drawers, maybe forty of them, ranked in rows, and her hand had risen halfway to the glass without her seeming to know it. Her face had gone very still—the particular stillness of a person listening for something only they can hear.

"Toll-keeper said names get eaten here," she whispered. "So how do I know which one—"

"You don't look for your name," Tam said quietly. "Your soul was sold unnamed. That's the whole obscenity of it. You look for—" He hesitated. "You'll know. Everyone says you'll know. It's supposed to feel like hearing your own voice from outside."

Her fingers touched the glass.

Down the row, in the third drawer from the end, behind a label reading only *MERCENARY, VALLEY-BRED, UNNAMED*, something small and grey stirred. It was the color of ash and river-stone and the hour before dawn, and it was no bigger than a coal, and it turned—slowly, sleepily, like something waking from a hundred years of being filed—toward her hand.

Ada made a sound Tam had never heard from her. Not a gasp. Smaller than a gasp. The sound of recognition arriving in a body that had spent its whole life insisting it needed nothing.

"That's—" she started.

Every lamp in the hall went dark at once.

Not the lamps—Tam corrected himself, ice sliding down his spine—the *bell*. The soundless bell, the alarm with no noise that every soul in the building felt in its marrow. Somewhere below them, ten thousand grey embers flared briefly in their drawers, like a city of windows lighting at once.

The clerk pretending to be furniture stepped forward and cleared its throat.

"Mercenary Villalobos," it said, in a voice that had stopped pretending anything. "Please step away from the case."

Ada's hand closed into a fist against the glass—around nothing, around everything—as somewhere far above them, patient and grandfatherly, the Regent began to laugh.
