## 14. The Drawer of Grey Embers

The archive did not want her there.

Ada understood that the way she understood weather, or a bad contract, or the particular silence a house makes when someone is hiding inside it. The halls had been built to intimidate—to make a person feel small and filed—and they were very good at their work. Shelves rose out of sight on both sides of the aisle, each one crammed with glass drawers no bigger than a loaf of bread, each drawer holding a grey ember that glowed like a coal remembered rather than burning. Thousands. Tens of thousands. They went back farther than lamplight could follow.

Somewhere behind her, Tam was still talking. She could hear the rhythm of his voice through the stacks—the court scribe doing what court scribes did best, drowning a Regent in procedure until the man forgot to count his hirelings. *One book of prophecies, first edition, authenticated by my own hand before my disgrace, offered freely against the outstanding debt—* Tam had rehearsed it on the road beneath the mountain until Vess threatened to sit on him.

*Buy me an hour,* he'd said. *Maybe two. Don't touch anything with a seal on it.*

So she touched nothing with a seal on it. She just walked.

Her boots made no sound here. That should have been her first warning. In the valley, in every valley, in every tavern and road she'd ever walked, Ada Villalobos made noise—she'd been told so since she was nine years old, usually by people who wanted her to stop. But the archive floor drank her footsteps, and the shelves swallowed her breathing, and after a while she stopped trying to be quiet because the archive was already quieter than she could manage.

The ledger said *Row Nine.* That was all. A soul, unnamed, sold twice—filed under description rather than name, which Tam had called unusual and Vess had called obscene. *You don't file a person by name if you mean them to stay lost,* the dragon had said, in the tone of someone quoting a lesson learned at cost.

Row Nine began somewhere past the point where Ada stopped counting drawers.

She walked, and the embers watched her. Not with eyes—they had no eyes—but she felt them anyway, the way you feel a crowd part around a drunk. Each one turned faintly in its glass as she passed, a slow grey drift, like moths too tired to fly. She told herself it was a draft. There was no draft. There was no air moving anywhere in this place; the whole mountain held its breath.

Then one ember turned *toward* her, and kept turning as she moved, and stopped only when she stopped.

Ada stood in front of the drawer for longer than she meant to. It looked like all the others: glass front, brass frame, a paper tag wired to the corner. The tag was small enough to read at a glance, which was somehow worse than a long record would have been. Bureaucracy, she was learning, was cruelty measured in ink economy.

*SOUL — FEMALE, MERCENARY STOCK. UNNAMED AT SALE. SOLD TWICE. CONDITION: HOLLOWED.*

Hollowed.

She had heard the word from Vess two nights ago, beside a flame small enough to cup in one hand, and she had thought she understood it. She hadn't. Understanding was for later. Right now there was just the tag, and the word on it, and the fact that some clerk—a person with hands, probably cold ones—had sat down at a desk and written *hollowed* about her the way you'd write *dented* about a kettle.

The ember inside pressed against the glass. Gently. Like a dog's nose against a door.

"Hello," Ada said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, and the sound didn't echo, because this place ate echoes along with footsteps.

The ember flared. Just slightly. Grey going almost-warm at the center, the way coals do when you blow on them right.

Down the row, other embers stirred.

That was when she saw it—the thing that made her stomach drop through the floor of the world and keep falling. It wasn't just hers. Two drawers down, same tag, different hand: *FEMALE, VALLEY-BORN. UNNAMED. HOLLOWED.* And below that another, and another, and then a whole shelf of them, and then a whole bay, rows upon rows of grey embers tagged with variations of the same description, sold across generations, hollowed for the same purpose, filed by the hundreds because one was never quite the right shape and the court had decided, with the patience of evil, to keep ordering more.

A hundred years of souls. Her grandmother's shape. Her mother's shape. Hers.

They hadn't been looking for *a* soul. They'd been breeding toward one, buying whatever came close, hollowing each purchase and filing the failures. Somewhere in this room was the ember of a woman who might have been her grandmother if the court hadn't gotten to her first. Somewhere was a girl who'd have been her sister.

Ada put her palm flat against the glass of her own drawer.

The ember leaned into it. She felt it—not warmth exactly, but *weight*, a pressure like a hand pressing back from the far side of a door. Her whole life she had carried a hollowness she couldn't name, a feeling she'd once described to Sefa as *walking around a room that's missing its furniture.* Everyone had told her that was just how mercenary life felt. Empty rooms, empty roads, empty contracts. You get used to it.

She had not gotten used to it. She had just stopped mentioning it.

And here it was. Her furniture. Her missing self, bought twice, filed once, glowing grey behind three millimeters of glass and a wire-twisted paper tag.

"You're smaller than I imagined," she whispered. "I imagined something louder."

The ember pulsed. Almost offended.

* * *

Breaking the drawer was easier than it should have been, which frightened her more than any guard could have. The glass didn't shatter so much as *give*, softening under her palm like sugar in rain, and the brass frame unclasped itself with a click that sounded, absurdly, like a throat clearing. As if the archive had been waiting. As if a hundred years of hollowed souls had been leaning on this door from the inside.

Or as if something else in the building had opened it for her. She filed that thought away with the others she didn't have time for.

The ember sat in the drawer, loose now, and Ada hesitated over it with her fingers curled and ready and completely unwilling. Taking it felt like picking up a hot stone. Taking it felt like the wight saying her name at the valley's edge. Every step of this whole cursed journey had been the world closing its hand around her one finger at a time, and this was the last knuckle.

Once she picked it up, she was no longer a woman who maybe matched a prophecy. She was a thief. She was evidence. She was *real* in the way the court understood real, which was the way ledgers understand things: as entries that can be audited.

Sefa's river-stone shifted on her wrist, tied there since the road beneath, warm from her skin. It remembered directions home. Home was a valley with a ferry and a girl who refused to be protected, and every hour Ada spent down here made that valley less safe, and she knew it, and she reached into the drawer anyway.

The ember weighed nothing and everything. It settled into her palm like a bird deciding to trust her, and it was *warm*, actually warm, warmer than anything in this cold country of glass, and for one heartbeat she felt—

She felt a kitchen. Someone's kitchen, long ago, bread and woodsmoke and a woman humming off-key, and Ada knew the song without knowing why, and she was crying without having decided to—

She tore herself out of it, gasping, fist closed. The memory wasn't hers. Or it was hers—it belonged to whoever had owned this soul-shape before the court got it, some fragment left in the grain like scent in wood. Generations of women, sold and hollowed, and every one of them had stood in a kitchen once, and hummed.

"That's mine now," she said to the ember. "All of it. The bad parts too."

It flared, and this time it wasn't grey at all. For a half-second it burned the color of the dragon-fire Vess tended—gold shot through with white—and then it banked itself, quick, guilty, like a candle caught burning past curfew. Down the rows, hundreds of embers turned toward her at once. Hundreds of little grey faces, if embers had faces. Hundreds of the same hunger she'd carried all her life, looking at her holding the thing they'd each been shaped and shelved and discarded for.

*I'm sorry,* she thought at them, uselessly. *I'm sorry I can't carry you all out.*

Later. Later she would come back for them, or burn this place down, or find some third thing she couldn't imagine yet. Later. Right now: move.

* * *

She was four aisles gone when the bell rang.

Except it didn't ring. That was the horror of it. She heard nothing—no alarm, no shout, no clatter of guards. What happened instead was that every ember in the archive *shuddered*, thousands of them, a single convulsion rolling through the shelves like wind through wheat, and Ada felt it in her chest, in her teeth, in the stolen ember clenched in her fist. A soundless bell. A tolling made of absence. She felt it the way you feel a name being called in a language you don't speak—you don't know the words, but you know you're being summoned.

Every soul in the mountain felt it. She was sure of that the way you're sure of falling. Somewhere above her, in the glittering courts, clerks froze mid-signature. Somewhere in a cell, Tam's head came up like a deer's. Somewhere by a dying flame, Vess lifted her great scarred head and said a word in a language that predated mountains.

The bell tolled again, silent as snowfall, and this time Ada felt it *looking* for her. Passing through the stacks. Passing through her. Passing through the ember in her fist—which answered. Of course it answered. It was the receipt. It was the entry that didn't balance. It sang back to the soundless bell in a frequency of pure *here, HERE, here—*

Ada ran.

The aisles blurred. Glass drawers streamed past on both sides, thousands of watching lights, and she realized with a lurch that they weren't watching anymore—they were *cheering*, or the closest thing souls could come to cheering, flickering faster as she passed, a wave of grey running ahead of her through the shelves like fire finding dry grass. Pointing the way. Or lighting it.

Behind her, boots. Real ones, audible ones, the first sounds she'd heard in this place that the archive couldn't swallow. Guards in the livery of Quiet Things, coming up Row Ten at a dead run, and between her and them stood nothing but ten thousand fragile drawers full of everything the court had ever stolen.

Ada didn't decide. Her body decided, the way it always had, god-sworn and mercenary and done being polite.

She swept her blade off her back as she ran—not the valley-edge wight-work swing but the flat, wide arc her order had taught her for clearing brush, clearing crowds, clearing *rooms*—and she took the tops off six shelves in a single pass. Glass burst around her like sleet. Embers spilled into the air by the dozens, freed, weightless, rising like startled birds, swirling up into the dark above the stacks in a slow ascending spiral of grey light, and every one of them was screaming without sound, and every one of them was *free*, and the soundless bell tolled a third time, frantic now, ragged, losing its composure the way bells do when the ringers start to panic.

Guards rounded the aisle-end into a storm of liberated souls and stopped like men hitting a wall of weather. Ada went through the gap they left at a sprint, ember fisted against her heart, blade trailing sparks of glass, running not away from the archive but *through* it, following the current of rising lights, because the freed embers knew these halls better than she did and they were showing her the way out—or the way deeper, she honestly couldn't tell, and at this point couldn't bring herself to care.

The last thing she saw of Row Nine, glancing back once, was the empty drawer with its torn paper tag, swinging on its wire.

*SOLD TWICE,* it said.

*NOT RETURNED YET.*

She meant to fix that. God help her, she meant to fix everything—and then the aisle ahead filled with white livery, and the ember in her fist flared gold, and somewhere far above, a dragon began to sing.
