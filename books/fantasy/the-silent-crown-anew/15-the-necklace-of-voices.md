## 15. The Necklace of Voices

Vess smelled the lie before Tam finished saying it.

They had made camp in a hollow off the old trade road, three days beneath the mountain now, and the girl—Ada—was asleep with her back against the stone, one hand curled around the grey ember in its wrapping of cloth, the way a child holds a caught bird. Gentle. Afraid to squeeze. The scribe sat across the small fire they'd allowed themselves, feeding it pages torn from some lesser book he'd bought at a toll-station, and he was talking again about the Regent. About how the Regent *kept* things. How the Regent was the steward of silence, the guardian of quiet, the faithful servant who held the god's stillness in trust.

*Kept*, Vess thought, and smoke came out of her nose though she wore no dragon shape tonight. Only the shape of a grey woman with ash-colored eyes, the one she used for walking among people who would scream otherwise.

"You believe that," she said.

Tam looked up. He had ink on his fingers even down here, even after everything; it was in the creases of his knuckles like a stain he'd been born with. "It's what the records say."

"The records." She let the word sit in the fire between them until it burned. "I tended the flame for ninety years, little scribe. I read what drifted up through the smoke. Do you know what the records say about me?"

"That you chose exile," Tam said carefully. "That you were disgraced for—" He stopped.

"For refusing an order no one wrote down." Vess drew her knees up. In this shape her joints ached like a mortal woman's, which was the point, which was the penance. "The court remembers that much. It's the only true thing left in their archive. They couldn't falsify it because I made sure there were witnesses, and witnesses are the one thing the Regent cannot burn without leaving a hole shaped exactly like a crime."

Ada stirred against the wall but didn't wake. Good. What Vess had to say next was not for sleeping ears, and maybe not for waking ones either, but the fire was dying and the road ahead was short and she had run out of centuries for cowardice.

"Ask me," she said. "You're a scribe. Ask me properly."

Tam swallowed. "What did you refuse?"

"To keep lying." She watched the fire eat another page. Some clerk's neat hand listed soul-tolls collected on the western bridge, forty years dead, and burned just like anything else. "The story you carry—the story everyone carries—is that the dragon-fire holds the god's silence in place. That we dragons are wardens of the quiet. That if the fire dies, the silence breaks free and devours the world." Her mouth twisted. "It's elegant. It has the two things every good lie needs: a duty and a doom."

"And the truth?"

"The truth is that nothing needs holding *in*." Vess leaned forward, and the firelight bent toward her the way firelight does when something older than fire is paying attention. "Silence isn't a beast, scribe. Silence is what's left when someone takes the sound away. The Silent God went silent the way a house goes dark when thieves carry out the lamps—one piece at a time, by hand, on purpose."

*

She told it the way she remembered it, which was not in order.

The god had not always been silent. That was the first thing, the thing the whole mountain had been built on top of and forgotten. In the beginning—her beginning, which was long before the valley, long before the ledger, long before anyone alive had been born—the god spoke. Not words the way people speak them. More like weather. More like a pressure you felt in your back teeth, a knowing that arrived without language. When the god spoke, crops knew when to bend. Rivers knew where to go. People who heard it called themselves hearers, and the first of the hearers was a man named Orenn.

"Orenn," Tam repeated. He had his book out. Of course he had his book out; Vess could have laughed, if any part of her remembered how.

"The first priest," she said. "Before the crown, before the court, before there was anything under the mountain but roots and water. He heard the god more clearly than anyone ever had, and he was loved for it, and he loved being loved, and that was the whole of the tragedy right there. Everything else was just paperwork."

"What happened to him?"

"He started writing it down." Vess stared into the fire. "And that seems harmless. It seemed harmless then. But the god's voice wasn't meant to be kept—it moved through people like water through a net. You listened, it passed on, you stayed empty enough to hear the next thing. Orenn began to *hold*. He wrote the voice down and read it back, and reading it back felt so much like hearing it that people stopped listening for the real thing. Why should they? The priest had it in his book. And the god, who had never needed to be loud, grew quieter—not from anger. From disuse. From loneliness. A voice no one listens to gets very tired."

Tam's pen had stopped moving. "He hoarded it."

"He *curated* it," Vess said. "That's worse. And then one year the harvest failed, and the war came, and the people demanded to know why the god had let it happen, and Orenn—who had spent his life as the only ear at the door—he couldn't bear to say *I don't know anymore*. He couldn't bear the silence behind him where the voice used to be. So he reached back into his books, into all those years of written-down god, and he took hold of it with both hands." She opened her own hands, empty. "And he strangled it."

The fire popped. Neither of them looked at it.

"You can't kill a god's voice," Vess went on quietly. "You can only wear it. He braided it into a necklace—a hundred strands, each one a piece of the voice he'd stolen over decades, each strand a sentence the god had once said through somebody's mouth. And when he put it on, he could speak with the god's throat. He could say *thus says the silent one* and make mountains move, and no one questioned it, because who questions a god? The silence afterward wasn't the god choosing quiet. It was the god with its throat in a fist, making no sound a strangled thing can make."

Ada was awake.

Vess knew it before she opened her eyes—knew it by the change in her breathing, by the way the ember under her hand had begun to glow faintly through the cloth, responding to its owner's heartbeat like a struck bell answering a distant chime. The girl sat up slowly, hair full of straw, eyes full of something worse than tears.

"The dragon-fire," Ada said. Her voice was rough with sleep and with the other thing. "You said the fire doesn't hold the silence in place."

"No."

"Then what does it do?"

Vess met her eyes and did not look away, because the girl deserved better than to be lied to gently, and because Vess had spent ninety years practicing this exact confession and had never once managed to say it aloud.

"It hides the theft," she said. "A strangled voice makes a kind of noise, girl. A wrongness. Anyone with a drop of hearer-blood can feel it—a necklace made of stolen god sitting on a thief's collarbone, humming like a wound. So the court built a cover. They found the oldest fires, the true dragon-flames, the ones that had burned since the world was young, and they taught us a sacred duty: *tend the flame, for the flame holds the silence.* And while we tended, the fire gave off something else. A hush. A wide, warm hush that spread across the land and lay over everything like snow, and underneath that hush, nobody could feel the wrongness anymore. Nobody could hear the necklace screaming."

Tam had gone grey around the mouth. "The prophecy ledger. The final entry. *The price of silence*—"

"—is fuel," Vess said. "Yes. The fire doesn't burn wood or coal or dragon pride alone. It burns sold things. Souls, mostly. Small ones at first. A memory here, a talent there, a name. The court collected them for centuries—your filing halls, your glass drawers, your tidy bureaucracy—and fed the flames just enough to keep the hush thick. Every soul in that archive is smoke. Every ember in every drawer is a log on a fire whose only purpose is to hide a mugging that happened a thousand years ago."

Ada's hand had closed hard around the wrapped ember now. Vess could smell the heat of it.

"My soul," Ada said. "Sold twice. Bought by the god. The god spent its voice to buy me back—that's what you figured out, isn't it, Tam? That's why it went silent *again*, the year I was born. It wasn't just strangled. It paid."

"It emptied itself for you," Vess said, "and then the Regent collected the payment too, and fed it to the fire, and called the resulting quiet *holiness*." She let that land. She watched it land, watched the girl's face do the arithmetic of a lifetime of prayers that had gone unanswered because the answerer was wearing its own voice as jewelry. "I learned this ninety years ago. The Regent ordered me to increase the feed. There had been a bad decade—too many hearers born, people feeling the wrongness, asking questions. The Regent wanted the hush doubled. I asked what souls would burn. The Regent said *the usual stock*. I asked to see the stock. And I saw the drawers, scribe. Rows of them. Children's souls. Whole generations of them, filed by village, by year, by weight." Her voice didn't shake. Dragons don't shake. But it went somewhere flat and terrible. "I refused. I flew out of the mountain with the fire I tended still burning in my chest, and I've kept it alive ever since, alone, in exile, telling myself I was guarding the last lock on the silence."

"But you weren't," Tam said.

"I was keeping the last lamp lit in a house of thieves," Vess said. "So no one would notice the lamps were gone."

*

For a long time nobody spoke. The fire ate itself down to a red heart. Somewhere far above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the mountain went about whatever business mountains have.

Then Tam said, in the small careful voice of a man checking whether a bridge will hold him: "If the fire dies—if the hush fails—"

"People start hearing the necklace," Vess said. "All of them, everywhere. Every soul feels the soundless bell, remember? Imagine that bell ringing across the whole world, and imagine what it rings *of*: a theft, ongoing, a god being worn. And the Regent's law says the silence devours what hears it—but that law is a lie too. The devouring isn't the god's wrath. It's the Regent cleaning up. Erasing witnesses. The way the court erases everything." She looked at the wrapped ember glowing in Ada's fist. "Your god isn't hungry, mercenary. Your god is *muffled*. And it has been trying, for a hundred years, to reach the one person it paid everything to buy back."

Ada stood up. She moved like a soldier deciding, which was a language Vess respected: no flourish, no speech, just weight shifting forward.

"Then we stop hiding the theft," Ada said. "We take the necklace off the thief."

"With what?" Tam asked, half-laughing, half-terrified. "You've seen the Regent's hall. The guards are made of filed paper. The doors only open for signatures. We have one ember, one erased scribe, and one dragon in a borrowed body."

"We have the truth," Ada said. "Nobody's filed that yet."

But Vess was no longer listening, because her ears—which were old, and attuned to wrongness the way other creatures are attuned to rain—had caught something coming up the trade road behind them. A sound like a page turning. Then another. Then hundreds, thousands, a dry rustling flood, the unmistakable sound of the court's paper guards unfolding themselves from the walls where they'd been waiting since the hollow was chosen for them.

The fire died. Not guttered—died, pinched out between one heartbeat and the next, as if a great hand had closed around it.

In the sudden dark, Vess felt the hush peel away from the three of them like a bandage coming off, and underneath it, for the first time in her long exile, she heard the necklace.

It was singing. Far away, deep beneath the mountain, a stolen voice was screaming through a thief's throat—and it was calling a name.

Not hers. Not the scribe's.

*"Ada,"* said the dark, in a voice like a hundred mouths speaking as one. *"Ada Villalobos. Come home. Your receipt is due."*
