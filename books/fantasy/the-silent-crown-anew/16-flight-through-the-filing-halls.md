## 16. Flight Through the Filing Halls

The soundless bell was still ringing.

I felt it in my teeth, in the hollow behind my sternum where my soul had lived for twenty-six years before I'd stolen it back out of a glass drawer. It wasn't a sound at all—it was a pressure, a great slow pulse rolling outward through the mountain like a heartbeat made of paperwork, and every soul in the court beneath the mountain felt it and knew: *something has been taken.*

Something had been taken. It was in my pocket, warm as a coal, small as a knucklebone. Mine.

"Run," Vess said.

She didn't say it loudly. Dragons don't need to be loud. The word came out of her on a breath of heat that smelled of old smoke and older patience, and it went through me like a whipcrack. Tam was already moving—nameless Tam, Tam-who-was-no-one, the scribe who had traded his family name to a toll-keeper for our passage and come out the other side of that bridge lighter than any man should be. He ran the way scribes run, which is badly, elbows tucked, clutching a book against his chest like a shield.

The book. The ledger of small sold things. The Regent of Quiet Things wanted it more than she wanted us, probably, though I wouldn't have bet my life on that distinction, because she wanted our lives too.

We ran.

* * *

The filing halls were not built for running. They were built for the opposite of running—for the slow, patient, eternal shuffling of souls from one drawer to another, one ledger to another, one century of quiet servitude to the next. The corridors stretched away in straight grey lines, shelving rising on both sides higher than any shelf has a right to rise, higher than the eye could follow into a dark that wasn't dark so much as *filed*. Every niche held a drawer. Every drawer held an ember. And every ember was a soul, and every soul had been sold, and the whole vast humming architecture of the place existed for one purpose only: so that no one would ever have to hear what they'd bought.

My boots rang on stone. That was the first wrong thing. In this court, sound was currency, and we were spending it with every step.

"The east stairs," Tam gasped. "There's a service road—the carters use it—soul-toll carts, ash carts—" He stumbled on a seam in the floor and I caught his arm without slowing, hauling him upright, and he made a small wounded sound that might have been thanks.

"You know these halls," I said.

"I filed them." His laugh came out cracked. "Three years. I filed souls, Ada. Alphabetically. By purchase date. By *resale value*." His voice did something ugly on that last word, and then he shut up and ran harder.

Vess couldn't run. Not really—not down here, not through corridors built for clerks. She moved like a landslide given somewhere to be, shoulders scraping niches, drawers rattling in their frames as her scales brushed them, and every drawer she touched lit faintly from within, ember-glow leaking through glass like fireflies trapped in jars. Behind us, the light spread. Behind us, the soundless bell kept ringing. Behind us—

Behind us, the first of the freed embers slipped its drawer.

I heard it before I saw it, which is to say I felt it: a tiny tug at the world, like a stitch pulled loose. Then a drawer three rows back burst open with a sound like a struck wineglass, and something rose out of it—a grey spark, no bigger than a moth—and hung there, trembling, tasting air it hadn't tasted since whoever sold it stopped being able to taste anything at all.

Then another drawer opened. Then ten. Then a hundred.

"They're following the alarm," Tam said, looking over his shoulder, and his face did something I'd never seen it do: it went completely, terribly still. "Ada. They think you're the way out."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about souls: they are not wise. They are not serene. A soul that has spent sixty years in a glass drawer does not emerge luminous and forgiving. It emerges *starving*—for sky, for body, for the warmth of being someone instead of something—and when a hundred starving things all fix their attention on the one person running through their prison carrying a stolen ember in her pocket, you learn very quickly what a stampede feels like from the inside.

They came off the shelves like startled birds.

That's the image that stuck, later, when I had time to be haunted properly: birds. Grey sparks wheeling up through the corridor dark in spiraling flocks, hundreds of them, thousands, pouring out of the niches on either side of us as we ran, drawn along our wake like gulls after a fishing boat. They didn't speak. Souls can't speak; that's rather the point of the whole arrangement. But they made a sound anyway, a high thin keening below hearing, felt in the fillings of your teeth, and the sound said everything language would have ruined: *out, out, out.*

"Stop opening them!" I shouted at Vess.

"I'm not opening anything." Her voice rolled down the corridor ahead of us and came back changed. "Your alarm is doing it. Every soul in this hall felt the bell. They're waking up." A pause, and then, quieter, in that tone dragons use when they are about to ruin your day with arithmetic: "All of them."

"How many?"

"In this hall? Eleven thousand." Another pause. "In the court?"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Tam made a noise that was half sob, half laugh. "The Regent's going to have to refile *everything*," he said, and there was such vicious joy in it, such pure scribe's delight at the prospect of catastrophic paperwork, that I loved him a little right then, running, gasping, nameless, in the dark.

Then the lights came on.

Not lamps. *Clerks.* They poured out of side alcoves in perfect silence, dozens of them, robed in grey so fine it seemed woven from the dust of the place itself, and their faces—here is the thing I will not manage to describe correctly for as long as I live—their faces were *smooth*. Not blank. Smooth, like paper before writing, like wax before a seal. Where eyes should have been there were faint impressions, the ghost of eyes, as if someone had once pressed real features into them and the features had slowly, patiently, been filed away.

They carried hooks. Long silver hooks on long silver poles, the kind used for reaching high shelves, and they moved without hurry, because they knew something we didn't yet:

They knew the exits.

* * *

We hit the first checkpoint at a dead sprint and it nearly killed us.

A portcullis of black iron dropped from the ceiling with a boom that finally, finally gave the soundless bell some company, and Vess took it full in the chest. I'll give the engineers beneath the mountain this much: they'd planned for dragons. The gate held. Vess skidded, roared—a real roar, the first true sound I'd heard in that whole silent country, and every clerk within fifty paces flinched as if scalded—and the corridor filled with steam and the smell of hot iron.

"Side passage," Tam choked. "Left. There—" He pointed at a gap between two shelves barely wide enough for a dog. "Carters' cut-through. It's how they move drawers without going through checkpoints. It's *unlisted*."

"Unlisted," I repeated.

"I falsified records for three years, Ada. You think I only falsified the ones they caught?" For one second, one bright second, the old charm flickered across his exhausted face—the grin that had talked his way into my valley half-dead and talked his way out of a toll with nothing but a family name. "Best lie I ever told was a hallway."

So we went sideways, into the dark between the shelves, and the flock of freed souls came with us.

That was the horror of it, and the grace of it too—I never fully untangled which. The embers packed the narrow passage around us like a living current, brushing my skin with cold that burned, and wherever they flowed, the clerks' hooks swept past empty. The souls were hiding us. Or shielding themselves with us. Both, maybe. You don't ask a drowning man whether he's holding you up or pushing you down; you just swim.

We swam. Through cuts and crawlways and stairwells that smelled of ash-cart grease, down levels I didn't know the mountain had, while above and behind us the soundless bell rolled on and the filing halls emptied themselves shelf by shelf into a storm of grey wings.

And then, in a junction where six passages met under a dome carved with the names of everyone who had ever paid a soul-toll—we stopped, because we had to, because the way forward was blocked by a clerk who was different from the others.

Bigger. Older. Its smooth face bore one mark: a single line inked across the brow, like a ledger entry waiting to happen. And it spoke.

It had a voice. Of course it did. Everything in this court had a voice except the god whose voice had been stolen, and the clerk's voice was dry and precise and horribly familiar, because it sounded like every record Tam had ever described to me, every falsified line, every neat bureaucratic sentence that had decided a person's fate without once saying anything true.

"Surrender the ember," it said, "and the scribe walks free."

* * *

I want to tell you I thought about it. I want to tell you there was a moment of temptation, a scale balancing, a real choice. But the truth is faster than that, and uglier, and simpler: the clerk was lying. Courts like this don't trade. They collect. Free the scribe—yes, free him, file him, drawer him, add him to the eleven thousand. I could see it in the inked line on its brow, already written, already waiting. *One (1) scribe, unnamed, recovered.* The Regent didn't bargain. She balanced books.

But Tam stepped forward anyway.

"No," I said.

"Ada." He turned, and the book was in his hands, the ledger, the prophecy book, the book of small sold things, and he was holding it out toward the clerk like a man offering a hostage. "Take the book instead. Tell her the Unwritten One destroyed the original. Tell her the copies are worthless now. Buy us the east stairs."

"That's not—" My voice cracked. I hated that. "That's not how we get out of here."

"It's exactly how *you* get out of here." He smiled at me, that cracked charming smile, and for the first time since the bridge where he'd fed his name to the toll-keeper, it reached his eyes. "Listen. I'm already unmade, Ada. No records. No name. Nothing they can hold over me except my body, and my body is the cheapest thing I own. But you—you've got a pocketful of yourself, and Vess has got however many years left in her, and somebody has to carry what happens next out of this mountain." He pushed the book toward the clerk. "Besides. I've read it cover to cover now. I know how it ends."

"You don't," I said. "Nobody knows how it ends. That's the whole point of—"

"The last entry," he said softly. "The price of silence. I told you I'd found it. I just haven't told you what it says." He glanced back at me, and there was something in his face I'd only ever seen on people standing at doors they meant to walk through alone. "You'll find out. That's better. That's how prophecy should work—too late to stop it, just in time to survive it."

The clerk took the book.

That fast. That easy. One smooth grey hand closing around a hundred years of sold souls, and Tam let it go, and I watched his fingers stay open in the air afterward, empty, like he'd forgotten what hands were for.

"Go," he said.

"Tam—"

"*Go.*" And then, quieter, with the grin again, the terrible grin, the one I'd follow into worse places than this if he ever asked: "Ada Villalobos. Write me down somewhere, will you? Somewhere they can't reach. You're the only record left that I exist."

* * *

We ran.

Vess took my shoulder in her teeth—not biting, never biting, just holding, the way a mother cat carries what matters—and hauled me through the last junction, down the east stairs, into a service road that stank of ash and lamp oil. The freed souls came with us as far as they could, a river of grey sparks pouring down the stairwell like water finding its level, and then the clerks closed in behind us and the river broke against them and scattered, and I heard—felt—that keening change key. From *out* to *lost*. From hope to grief. I felt every one of them, the way you feel weather.

I didn't look back. If I looked back I'd have gone back, and going back would have spent Tam's life for nothing, and I had already spent enough people.

The service road bent twice and ended at a door that shouldn't have been there: plain wood, iron-banded, utterly ordinary, the kind of door that exists in every bureaucracy precisely because no one important ever bothers to lock the servants' way out. Beyond it, cold air. Real air. The smell of pine and snowmelt and distance.

Vess put her head through first, checked the dark with those furnace-coal eyes, and rumbled: "Clear."

I stepped out onto a ledge halfway up the mountain's outer face, and the night opened above me like a mercy, and for three full seconds I just breathed.

Then I put my hand in my pocket, and the ember answered—warm, alive, *mine*, beating against my palm like a second heart—and the guilt arrived on schedule, right on time, right on cue, settling into my chest beside the stolen soul like it had reserved a seat.

He'd bought our escape with himself. Again. The bridge, and now this. Nameless, record-less, erased from every page he'd ever written and every page he'd ever falsified, handed over to the Regent of Quiet Things with a smile and a book, and the last thing he'd said to me was *write me down somewhere they can't reach.*

Somewhere they can't reach. As if such a place existed. As if I hadn't spent my whole life in the one valley where magic couldn't follow, and watched riders cross the ridgeline anyway.

"We go back," I said.

Vess looked at me with one enormous eye. "For the scribe."

"For the scribe." My voice came out flat and hard and certain, and underneath the certainty, quiet as a drawer sliding shut, something else: *and for the rest of them. All eleven thousand. All of them.* "But first—" I lifted the ember out of my pocket, cupped it in both hands, and watched its grey light gutter and steady against the wind off the peak. "First I need to know what he read. The last entry. The price of silence."

Vess went very still.

"You know it," I said. "Don't you. You've known since the cave."

The dragon exhaled, a long plume of smoke that unraveled into the starlight, and when she finally spoke, her voice was the saddest sound I had ever heard, and it was also, I realized with a lurch, the beginning of the end of everything I understood about myself:

"Ada. Child. What do you think a dragon-fire *is*?"
