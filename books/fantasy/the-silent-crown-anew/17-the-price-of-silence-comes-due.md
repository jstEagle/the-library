## 17. The Price of Silence Comes Due

They took me through the Filing Halls in a chair that had no legs.

It moved on its own will — or on someone's will, which amounted to the same thing down here — gliding between the endless shelves of souls while two clerks walked behind me with their hands folded like men at a funeral. I had been a clerk once. I knew the posture. It was the posture you learned so that nobody ever asked you to carry anything heavy, and it was also, I understood now, the posture of people who had long ago stopped believing they could run.

The halls were screaming without sound.

That is not a metaphor. I am a scribe; I do not traffic in metaphor when the literal will serve. Every drawer we passed held embers, and every ember was a soul, and every soul felt the alarm Ada had tripped, and none of them could cry out because crying out requires a throat and the court beneath the mountain does not file throats. So the whole archive shuddered instead. Drawers rattled in their slots like teeth. The lamps along the aisles guttered in unison, all together, all the time, a slow strobing pulse that made the shelves seem to breathe.

I sat in my legless chair with my hands cuffed in ledger-iron and thought: *this is what it looks like when ten thousand souls scream into wool.*

I had helped build this place's records. For eleven years I copied its judgments into beautiful script. I told myself I was only ink. The chair carried me past a drawer labeled with a name I recognized — a miller from the terraced towns, sold for three harvests' worth of rain — and I turned my face away, and hated myself for it, and did it anyway.

The clerks said nothing the entire journey. That was the courtesy they extended to prisoners who used to be staff: we do not gloat if you do not beg. I appreciated it more than I can say.

* * *

The Regent's chamber was at the center of everything, because of course it was. Bureaucracies are solar systems; power sits at the middle doing nothing while everything else orbits in exhausted circles around it.

The room itself was smaller than I expected. Almost modest. A round chamber walled in pale stone, lit by a single hanging lamp of dragon-glass — real dragon-glass, old work, older than Vess — and furnished with one desk, one chair behind the desk, and nothing else. No throne. Thrones are for kings who need to be taller than other men. The Regent had never needed to be taller than anyone. The Regent needed only to be patient.

And there the Regent sat, sorting papers.

I want to describe the necklace properly, because everything depends on the necklace. It lay across the Regent's breast like a rope of dark pearls, except pearls do not move. Each bead on that cord was a voice — an actual voice, plucked whole from somewhere it belonged — and they shifted against each other with a sound like distant conversation heard through a wall. Once, in the archive, I had leaned close to hear what the beads were saying. I had caught half a word in a child's voice and then vomited into a wastebasket and been docked a day's pay. I did not lean close now.

"Master Tam," the Regent said, without looking up. "Or should I say — " and here the Regent paused, quill hovering, savoring it — "nothing? I'm told your family name has been eaten. How economical of you."

"You've read the incident report," I said. My mouth was dry but my voice came out level, which surprised me. Fear, I have found, sounds most like composure right before it stops sounding like anything at all.

"I read everything." The Regent set down the quill. Looked up. The face above the necklace was ordinary — middle-aged, tired, kind around the eyes in the way of a village physician. That was always the worst part of the Regent. Everyone expected a monster. Nobody expected a person who might ask after your mother. "Sit."

"I'm sitting."

"On the floor, technically. The chair left." A small smile. "Old habits. The furniture here remembers whose side it's on. Sit properly."

A stool rose out of the floor beside me. I sat on it. There seemed little point in dignity; dignity was the one thing I had never managed to falsify successfully.

"Now," said the Regent, folding soft hands on the desk. "You have something I want. And I have several things you want. Shall we conduct business like civilized people, or shall we do the shouting version? I warn you, the shouting version ends the same way and takes longer."

"What do I have that you want?"

"The book."

My heart performed a complicated maneuver. The Book of Small Prophecies — the ledger of sold things, the account-book of a century of quiet thefts — was currently in a saddlebag strapped to a dragon flying hard for the mountain's western vents, or possibly still tucked under Ada's arm, or possibly burned by now on her orders, because she was smarter than me and I had begged her, at the cave's mouth, with riders closing in, to take it and go.

I said: "What book?"

The Regent's smile deepened by one degree. "Master Tam. You falsified records for eleven years. Do you imagine I don't know a lie's architecture? You lie beautifully — the hesitation is a masterstroke, truly — but you are stalling, and stalling means the book is not on your person, which means it is with *her*." The Regent laced the fingers together. "Which is fine. Which is, in fact, why you are useful."

"Useful how?"

"Because you love her."

The words dropped into the room like a coin into a well, and I listened for them to hit bottom, and they didn't. They just kept falling.

"Don't make that face," the Regent said gently. "It isn't an accusation. Love is simply the most reliable lever ever discovered. Empires have been moved with less. Now. Before we discuss terms, I think you've earned an education. You've read the book — really read it, haven't you? You know what the entries are."

"A ledger," I said. "Souls. Sold twice. Sold across generations."

"And the final entry. Did you reach the final entry?"

I had. Three nights ago, by Vess's fire, while Ada slept with her hand around a stolen ember and Sefa's river-stone tied to her wrist, I had read the last lines of the last page in the handwriting of the book's first owner — the god's first priest, the man who became the Regent — and I had closed the book very carefully, as one closes a door on a sickroom, and I had not slept again.

"There's no prophecy in it at all," I said. "Everyone up top thinks it's a book of prophecies. It's not. It's a contract. The final line is a clause."

"Read it to me," said the Regent. "I find I enjoy hearing it in another voice. Mine has grown stale."

"That's not possible," I said, "you stole every other—"

"*Read it.*"

And here is the terrible thing, the thing I will carry if I carry anything out of this life: my mouth opened and the words came, because some part of me has never stopped being obedient to whoever holds the pen.

"*When the last fire gutters,*" I recited, "*the debt comes due. Either the thief returns what was stolen — all of it, every voice, every ember, every name — or the silence takes its payment in full, and devours the world.*"

The lamp flickered. Somewhere far off, ten thousand drawers rattled in their silent screaming.

"There," said the Regent softly. "Isn't it cleaner when spoken aloud? Like a boil lanced." The Regent stood, and the necklace shifted, and for a moment the voices in it seemed to lean toward me. "You understand the position, Master Nothing. The dragon-fire is dying. Vess has told her, hasn't she — I assume that's what all that dramatic gasping at the cave was about. When it dies — and it will die within days, perhaps hours — the silence wakes hungry. It has been starved for a century. It will eat sound first, then names, then memory, then the world's thin skin of meaning, and it will not stop until there is nothing left to be quiet about."

"Unless you give back what you stole."

"Ah." The Regent sat on the edge of the desk, close now, and the kindness in that ordinary face was the cruelest instrument I had ever seen deployed. "Yes. Unless. But consider, as a former records-man, the practical difficulty. Every ember returned must be returned *willingly*, by the thief's own hand, freely given — those are the terms, I wrote them myself, I was younger and more poetic. If I simply open the drawers, the debt remains unpaid and the devouring proceeds. The thief must choose restitution. And I—" the Regent spread the soft hands, helpless, almost boyish— "have found, over a hundred years, that I am not capable of choosing it. That is the honest truth. You wanted honesty from the court beneath the mountain; there it is. I would rather the world end than give up this voice. I have worn it so long I no longer know where it ends and I begin. Is that monstrous? Probably. It is also *human*, which frightens me more."

For one insane moment I pitied the Regent. Then I remembered the miller's drawer, and the pity curdled into something colder and more useful.

"So you lose either way," I said. "You won't return it, and the silence won't forgive you for keeping it."

"Not quite." The Regent lifted one bead of the necklace between finger and thumb, delicately, the way a jeweler lifts a stone. "There is a third path. It's in the clause, if you read the marginalia — you're one of perhaps four living creatures who could. The debt is discharged if the stolen thing is *spent* rather than returned. Poured into the dead fire. Rekindled." The bead trembled faintly against the Regent's fingers, like something alive trying to pull away. "A soul, freely spent, relights what died. The silence is fed, the world is saved, and I keep my necklace. Everyone wins. Well. Nearly everyone."

The floor tilted under me. Not literally — the chamber was perfectly still — but I felt it tilt, because I understood, all in one white-hot instant, exactly which soul fit that clause, exactly which soul was currently sitting in a saddlebag of a fleeing dragon wearing a girl who had already lost everything twice over.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"There's no offer you can make."

"There is one," said the Regent, "and we both know it, and pretending otherwise wastes both our evenings." The Regent set the bead down and opened a drawer in the desk — a plain wooden drawer, no glass, no velvet, the humblest object in the whole mountain — and drew out a single sheet of paper and laid it facing me.

It was a writ of release. I am a scribe; I know the forms the way a fish knows water. It was perfect. Sealed, countersigned, witnessed, binding beyond appeal. And the name in the space for the released party was *Ada Villalobos* — her freedom, total and permanent, her soul's claim struck from every register in the court, her name struck from the hunt, her past expunged, her future unwritten. She could go home. She could marry the ferryman's daughter. She could grow old in the valley where magic cannot follow, and die safe, and be buried, and never learn how close the world had come to ending on her watch.

And beneath the release, in the signature line, waited a blank.

"All it requires," the Regent said, "is the book, and your hand."

* * *

I asked for time alone to consider it. The Regent granted it with genuine grace — another hour of the furniture remembering whose side it was on, the door sealing, the lamp burning steady.

I sat on the rising stool and I looked at that blank line for a long time.

Here is what temptation actually looks like. It doesn't look like a devil with a forked tongue. It looks like a tired bureaucrat offering you a clean document that solves everything. It looks like mercy with excellent penmanship. Every argument I had built against the court — the thefts, the silenced god, the strangled century — every one of them could be answered with a single question: *and what will Ada's life be worth to her, dead in a devoured world?*

She'd hate it. She'd fight it. She'd call me a coward and a traitor and worse, and she'd be right, and she would still be *alive*, hating me in a valley where magic cannot follow, growing vegetables, learning Sefa's laugh by heart. I could live with being hated. I had been practicing for years. What I could not live with — what sat in my chest like swallowed lead — was the memory of her face at the cave when Vess asked what a dragon-fire *is*. She had looked like a woman reading her own death warrant in a language she almost spoke.

If I signed, she would never have to speak it.

I picked up the offered quill. Ledger-iron cuffs or no, the Regent wanted my hand willing; the cuffs had been quietly unlocked sometime during the speech. Of course they had. The Regent missed nothing and forced nothing, because forced signatures void contracts, and contracts were the only scripture down here.

The nib touched the paper.

And I heard — or didn't hear; that was the horror of it — a silence in the shape of a name. Not the devouring silence. The other kind. The kind that stands next to you in a doorway at night and waits. Ada had stood like that, my first week in her valley, watching me sleep off the road by her fire with her blade drawn and her head cocked, deciding. I hadn't known her a day and she had decided to let me live, on no evidence whatsoever, because I was pathetic and funny and because — she told me later, much later, drunk on plum wine — *"you looked like a man who'd already lost everything he was willing to lose, and those are the only ones who tell the truth."*

Those are the only ones who tell the truth.

I put down the quill.

"Master Regent," I said, to the sealed door, knowing the Regent heard everything in this mountain the way a spider hears its web, "you've mispriced your goods. You think the book is worth her freedom. But you wrote the clause yourself, when you were young and poetic, and poetry always costs the poet in the end." I stood up. My legs shook. I let them. "So here is my counteroffer, and I'll thank you to enter it into the record, since record-keeping is apparently the only sacrament you have left: I will give you the book — gladly, tonight, free of charge — if you let me deliver it to her myself, and if you wait. Wait for the fire to gutter. Wait and see whether the thief returns what was stolen, or spends it, or does something none of us — not even you, with all your ledgers — have written down yet."

Silence. Long enough that I began composing my own eulogy.

Then the door unsealed, and the Regent's voice came warm as tea: "You realize she'll spend it. The girl with the hollow soul and the mercenary's conscience. She'll pour herself into that flame like wine into a grave, and you'll have traded the only friend who ever trusted you for a front-row seat."

"Yes," I said. "Probably."

"And you'll sign nothing."

"I'll sign *everything else*," I said. "I'm a scribe. It's what I'm for."

The door opened onto two clerks and a corridor of rattling drawers, and beyond them, far above, I could feel — the whole mountain could feel — the last dragon-fire burning down toward its final inch.

The Regent's voice followed me out, pleasant as a knife going into a sheath:

"Then hurry, Master Nothing. The silence is getting hungry — and I've just sent riders to make certain your friends don't reach the fire before it dies."

* * *

They gave me back my legs — gave me a horse, even, and a pass-stamp, and an escort of six riders who pretended to be an honor guard. A bargain is a bargain; the Regent keeps contracts the way a noose keeps necks.

We rode up through the mountain's old trade roads at a pace that ate torchlight, and I sat bent over the horse's neck doing the arithmetic I should have done in the chamber and had been too busy being noble to do.

I had bought time. Hours, maybe a day. I had kept the book out of the Regent's hands and put it, I prayed, in Ada's. I had refused the clean evil and chosen the messy uncertain good, and every part of me that could still feel proud was feeling it.

But the Regent had sent riders. And I knew those roads, and I knew the western vents, and I knew that between the court and the dying fire there was exactly one bridge, and on that bridge stood a toll-keeper who eats spoken names, and beyond the bridge the memory-fog lay thick as wool, and somewhere in that fog were three fugitives — one of them carrying the most dangerous soul in the world cupped in her hands like an ember, which is precisely what it was —

— and one of them, riding behind me now in the escort, leaned forward in the saddle and said, conversationally:

"Funny thing, scribe. The Regent said to tell you: the girl's already at the fire. Has been since midnight. And the dragon's not moving."

I pulled the reins without deciding to. The escort laughed, and closed around me like a ledger closing, and far below us, through a vent in the mountain's skin, I saw the light of the last dragon-fire gutter — once, twice —

— and hold.

Someone had reached it in time. Someone had done *something*.

I just no longer knew, looking down at that small stubborn flame, whether what they had done was save the world or sign it away — and neither, I would have bet my erased name, did she.
