## 18. What Ada Cannot Keep

The mountain let them out through a crack in its western shoulder, a seam of old stone that had once been a service door for miners and was now just a wound that breathed cold air. Ada came through it first, blade drawn, ember cupped against her chest in both hands like something newborn.

Behind her, the court's alarm went on being silent.

That was the worst part. She'd expected bells, horns, the Regent's voice pouring down the halls after them like spilled ink. Instead there was only that soundless ringing, deep in her teeth, deep in the place where a soul ought to sit—and she knew it wasn't an alarm at all. It was every soul beneath the mountain feeling hers leave the drawer at once. A thousand small recognitions. A thousand held breaths.

She climbed until the seam closed behind them and the sky opened overhead, grey and enormous and indifferent, and then she sat down on a shelf of rock with her back to the wind and finally looked at what she'd stolen.

* * *

It didn't look like much. That was the joke of it, the ugly joke she'd carry to her grave if she got one: a soul was a grey ember the size of a thumbnail, dim as banked coals, warm as a sleeping bird. It fit in her palm. It weighed almost nothing. Somewhere below them, in a glittering hall full of glass drawers, a clerk had filed it under a number, and the number had been her whole self since before she took her first breath.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Tam asked.

He stood a few feet away, hugging his elbows, looking smaller than she'd ever seen him. The toll-keeper's price still hung on him—no family name, no lineage, no records anywhere in the world that could say who he'd come from. He'd traded it all for their passage across the bridge, and now he moved through the world like a man walking on ice he couldn't see the bottom of. His scribe's hands kept twitching toward a pen that wasn't there.

"Do what?"

"Brighten."

Ada looked down. The ember was brightening. As her fingers closed around it—as her skin touched the thing that had been hers since before her birth—it woke. Grey gave way to silver, silver to a color she had no name for, warm light leaking between her knuckles like dawn through shutter boards.

And she felt it. Gods, she felt it.

It started in her palm and spread up her arm like standing too close to a hearth after years in the cold. Then it reached her chest and she made a sound she'd never made before, something between a laugh and a sob, because there was a *shape* inside her where nothing had ever been—a hollow, yes, she'd known about the hollow, Tam had told her, she was the receipt, she'd been born with a room missing—but knowing about a hole and having it filled were different things the way reading about water and drowning are different things.

Memories that weren't memories. Griefs that weren't griefs. A lullaby in a language she'd never spoken. Her mother's generation's fear, arriving all at once, the terror of people who'd sold a child's soul to keep a god quiet and told themselves it was mercy. She felt their hands shaking as they signed. She felt how they'd loved her anyway. Both things true. Both things hers now.

"Oh," she said. Just that. Just *oh*, in Sefa's rough valley accent, which was her accent, which was the only voice she'd ever owned, and even that felt different now—fuller, rounder, a bell instead of a struck stone.

Vess landed beside them without sound, because dragons that size don't make sound unless they choose to, and settled into a coil of grey scale and old sorrow. Her eyes were the color of the dying flame she'd tended for a century. "You're doing it," the dragon said quietly. "You're taking yourself back."

"I'm becoming whole." Ada laughed again, wet and disbelieving. "Do you know how long I've been— I thought the hollowness was the mercenary life. I thought everyone felt like this. A room with the furniture gone." She turned the ember in her fingers, watching it burn brighter. "I'm going to put it back and I'm going to be a person. An entire person. And then I'm going to walk down to the ferry and ask Sefa—" Her voice caught. "That's the first thing. That's the first thing I'm doing."

Tam had gone very still.

Ada knew that stillness. She'd seen it on his face twice before: once when she'd asked him what the Regent looked like, and once when he'd read the ledger's final line aloud by Vess's guttering flame. It was the stillness of a man holding knowledge he wished he could swallow.

"Say it," she said.

"You should rest first. You've been running for—"

"*Say it,* Tam."

Vess lowered her great head. Even the dragon, who had watched empires file their souls into drawers, looked away—which told Ada everything before any words did.

"The god's voice needs a vessel," Tam said. Each word came out like a coin pried from a dead man's fist. "We knew that. We've known it since the cave. But we assumed— I assumed—the vessel just needed to be *willing*. Someone brave enough to open themselves. That's how it works in every record, every rite, every—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, the way he spoke when the truth was worse than the lie. "It's not willingness. Willingness is necessary but it isn't sufficient. The voice has to have somewhere to *live*. And a whole soul fills a person completely. There's no room left. There's no—" His hands made a shape in the air, a container, an emptiness. "A whole soul cannot hold the voice. Only a hollow one can. Only someone who is *missing themselves* can be filled by a god."

The wind moved along the ridge. Somewhere below, a bird called, and Ada heard it with her new-whole ears and thought: *that's what sound is. That's what I almost never got to keep.*

"No," she said.

"Ada—"

"No. You're wrong. There's a way. There's always a way, you're a *scribe*, find me the loophole, that's your entire job—"

"I've spent three days looking for the loophole." Tam's voice cracked. "There isn't one. The Regent built it this way deliberately. Do you understand? The silence doesn't just need maintaining. It needs a *warden*. Someone who could end it at any moment and won't, because ending it means unmaking herself. He designed the trap so the hero's choice and the villain's choice are the same choice." He laughed, and it was the ugliest sound Ada had ever heard from him. "He's not even clever enough to be proud of it. It's just bureaucracy. Evil as filing."

Ada looked at the ember.

It burned in her palm, silver-bright, singing warmth up her arm, filling the hollow that had ached her entire life so gently she hadn't known it was an ache until this moment. This was hers. Sold before her birth, bought back by a god who spent its own voice to do it, carried in a drawer for twenty-six years while she grew up thinking she was simply the kind of person who felt slightly less than other people.

She could put it back. She could press it to her chest right now and be complete, and walk down off this mountain, and go to the ferry, and take Sefa's face in her hands, and be a woman with a whole soul who loved a girl by a river for sixty years and died in the valley where magic couldn't follow.

And the dragon-fire would stay dead. Vess had given her last strength holding the dark for one night—one night, and the flame had guttered out anyway, and somewhere behind them the silence was already eating. Tam had described it on the road down: sound going first, then names, then memory. A world where mothers forgot children. Where rivers forgot how to run downhill. Not death. Worse than death. Death at least leaves a body and a story. The devouring silence left nothing, not even the fact of leaving.

One night. They'd bought one night with a dragon's stubbornness, and the night was half spent.

"Show me," Ada said.

"Show you what?"

"The ledger. The final entry. Read it again. Out loud. Maybe you read it wrong. People read things wrong all the time, you told me that yourself, you falsified records for eleven years, you're the expert on reading things wrong—"

"Ada." Tam crouched in front of her, and his eyes were wet and furious with helplessness. "I would forge the word of God itself if it would save you from this. There is no loophole. The voice needs a hollow. You are the only hollow in the world that the voice will recognize, because the voice *paid for you*. You are the receipt. You are the last entry. You are the door." He swallowed. "Take yourself back and the silence wins. Give yourself up and—" He couldn't finish it.

"And I stop being me," Ada said. "That's the trade. That's the whole trade. I become the thing that speaks, and whatever's speaking isn't Ada anymore. Is that it? Is that what happened to the god? It poured itself out paying for me and now there's barely anything left, and when I pour myself out the same way—"

"It wouldn't be death," Vess said softly. "Nothing so kind."

* * *

They gave her what privacy the side of a mountain allows, which is to say they turned their backs and pretended the wind covered everything.

Ada held the ember to her lips, the way you'd hold a candle in a draft, and spoke to it. To herself. To the self sold before she had words to refuse.

"I know you," she whispered. "Isn't that strange? Twenty-six years apart and I know you. Every good thing I ever did, that was you echoing in an empty room. Every time I swung the blade true and couldn't explain how I knew to—that was you. I've been living off your interest my whole life."

The ember pulsed. Warmth, or answer; she couldn't tell anymore, and maybe there had never been a difference.

"Sefa's going to ask me why," she said. "When I see her. If I ever see her. She's going to look at me with those eyes that see straight through armor and she's going to ask why I couldn't just come home." Her breath shook. "And the honest answer is that coming home meant the end of home. All homes. Everyone's home. The whole wide world outside the valley that I've never once seen, gone quiet forever, because Ada Villalobos wanted to feel whole more than she wanted to do the one thing she was born for."

She laughed, and it hurt, because the laugh sounded whole. Everything about her sounded whole now. That was the cruelty of it, done flesh and bone: the choice arrived at the exact moment she was most capable of wanting to live. If the Regent had come for her a week ago—hollow Ada, restless Ada, Ada with the room missing—she'd have handed over the ember without a second thought, because there'd have been so little to lose.

But now. Now she knew what a lullaby felt like. Now she knew her grandmother's-generation grief by heart. Now she knew the exact weight of a complete soul, and it was almost nothing, and it was everything, and she was supposed to give it away.

*The cruelest choice made flesh.* Tam had said that about the Regent's design. He'd been wrong. It wasn't the design. It was this: that the sacrifice didn't come to the desperate. It came to the happy. It waited until you had something worth burning, and then it lit the match for you.

"Vess," she called.

The dragon turned her head.

"When I pour it in—the flame, the god, whatever happens—if some part of me remains. Some echo. Some ember of the ember." Ada met the ancient eyes. "Will I know? Will anything of me know?"

Vess was silent a long moment. When she answered, her voice was smoke and patience and a hundred years of tending a fire alone.

"A flame remembers what it burns," she said. "Not perfectly. Not as a person remembers. But the god chose you before you were born, Ada Villalobos. Of all the souls in all the drawers, it spent its voice on yours. Whatever speaks afterward will speak in your accent. In your stubbornness. With your terrible taste in ale and your worse taste in men and your habit of checking a locked door twice." The dragon's eyes gleamed. "You will not be gone. You will be *translated*. And I will be there, and I remember everything, and I will spend what's left of my life making sure no one forgets who lit the fire."

Ada looked at Tam. He'd turned back around, and he was crying without shame, and he mouthed something she couldn't hear over the wind. She was fairly sure it was *I'm sorry*. She was equally sure it was *thank you*. With Tam it had always been both.

She stood up.

Below them, far to the south, the world was already going quiet. She couldn't see it, but she could feel it through the new-whole nerves of her—the silence moving like frost across a window, eating a village name here, a funeral song there. One night, Vess had bought. Half the night was gone.

"Get me down the mountain," Ada said, and her voice was steady, and her hand around the ember was steady, and only her eyes were wet. "All three of us. Fast. Because I've decided, and I want to do this while I still remember why."

Tam wiped his face. "While you still remember—"

"While I'm whole enough to mean it." She closed her fist around the small grey-silver sun of herself, and smiled, and it was the smile Sefa loved, crooked and unafraid, and she would never make it again. "If I wait until I'm scared, I'll bargain. If I wait until I'm tired, I'll rationalize. So we go now, while Ada Villalobos is complete and certain and—" the smile wavered, held— "*mine*. Walk."

Vess spread wings that blotted out the stars, and the wind of them smelled of ash and old loyalty, and Ada climbed onto the neck of the last dragon in the world with her soul in her fist and her heart already saying goodbye.

As the ground fell away, she pressed the ember briefly to her lips one final time—not a kiss, exactly. More like handing a child over to someone who'd raise her better.

"Hold on," she told herself. "I'm bringing you home."

And the dragon dove toward the broken crown of the mountain, where a dead fire waited in the dark, and a necklace of stolen voices waited on a thief's throat, and the last night of the world ticked steadily toward morning.
