## 19. The Guttering

Vess had kept the fire alive for two hundred years, and in all that time she had never once thought of it as a duty. It was simply what her hands did when the rest of her slept—cupped around a flame no bigger than a sparrow's heart, fed with nothing but attention.

Attention was the fuel. That was the first thing the old dragon had taught her, back when Vess was young enough to still believe exile was temporary. *Not gold. Not blood. Not grief.* Everyone thought dragon-fire ate treasure, because everyone thought dragons were greedy. But greed was just attention pointed at the wrong things. The flame ate watching. It ate care. It ate the stubborn refusal to look away.

So Vess watched. For two hundred years, while the world above forgot there had ever been a court beneath the mountain worth fearing, while the valley filled with farmers and ferrymen and mercenaries who prayed to a god they'd never heard speak, Vess sat in her cave with her cupped hands and her small fire and she paid attention like it was the only prayer that mattered.

Because it was.

Tonight the flame fit in one palm.

She noticed it at dusk, or what passed for dusk down here—a thinning of the light that came through the fissures in the rock, grey going to black. She had measured the fire against her hands for decades the way other creatures measured time by seasons. Two palms, once. Then one and a half. Then one. Now it sat in the bowl of her right hand alone, and when she lifted it to her face its light barely reached her own eyes.

"Well," Vess said, to the fire, because talking to it was part of tending it and always had been. "That's not good."

The fire did not answer. It never had. It wasn't the god—it was the opposite of the god, or maybe the lock on the god's cage, depending on which truth you believed. Vess had spent two centuries believing the merciful version: that the fire held the silence in place, that silence was safety, that somewhere beneath all that quiet the god slept dreamless and unharmed. Tam's book had burned that version out of her in an afternoon. She could still see his handwriting shaking as he'd read the ledger aloud—the entries, the prices, the neat bureaucratic columns of sold souls. *A voice, divine, seized. A silence, maintained. A fire, kindled, to keep the theft from showing.*

Her whole life, reframed as maintenance work for a thief.

She should have let it gutter then. That was the honest accounting, and Vess was old enough now to prefer honest accounting to comfortable lies. If the fire existed only to hide the Regent's crime, then every year she tended it was a year she helped the thief keep his stolen goods. She had lain awake on the cold stone many nights since the archive, staring at the ceiling of her cave, asking herself why she didn't just open her hands and be done with it.

The answer never changed: because the ones who'd pay weren't the Regent. The Regent sat in his glittering hall behind walls of paperwork and stolen voice. The ones who'd pay were ferrymen. Farmers. A girl named Sefa who tied knots in reeds and laughed with her whole chest. A scribe with no name left who was even now somewhere under the mountain getting himself captured on purpose. And Ada, who had walked back into that place carrying her own soul like a lamp into a dark full of teeth.

The fire flickered, and Vess curled her fingers closer around it, and hated herself a little less for being a coward about it.

*Hold,* she told it. *Just hold.*

* * *

It died three hours past midnight.

There was no drama to it. That was the thing no one ever understood about endings—they rarely announced themselves. The flame simply grew tired of being watched, or perhaps Vess grew too tired to watch well enough, and between one breath and the next it went from a sparrow's heart to a candleflame to a coal to a red point to a grey smudge to nothing at all, and her palm was empty, and the cave was dark in a way the cave had never been dark before.

Vess sat very still.

For a long moment—she would remember it later as long, though she had no way to measure anything anymore—nothing happened. The darkness just sat there, ordinary, almost polite. Somewhere in the mountain water dripped. Her own heart beat in her ears. The world seemed to hold its breath along with her, waiting to find out whether the ledger's final line was true.

Then the drip stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. Mid-fall, if sound could be trusted, which it suddenly couldn't, because the drip had been a sound and sounds were what was leaving. Vess felt it go the way you feel a tooth loosen—not pain exactly, but a wrongness, a knowledge that something attached to you was no longer attached. The silence didn't rush in. It *ate*. She could feel it eating, patient and enormous, working outward from the dead flame like rot through fruit.

"No," Vess said, and heard her own voice come out fine, and understood that she had been granted a reprieve of scale. The silence started where the fire died. It would take time to climb the mountain, cross the valleys, reach the towns. Time measured in hours, maybe. A night, if the world was lucky.

The ledger's last line came back to her without her wanting it. Tam had read it in a whisper, as if the book might overhear: *When the last fire gutters, either the thief returns what was stolen, or the silence devours.*

Somewhere below her, in the deep halls, Ada Villalobos was holding a necklace up to a man made of paperwork. Or failing to. Or dying trying. Vess had no way to know. What she knew was this: the thief would not return what he stole. Thieves who wore their thefts as jewelry did not have change-of-heart seasons. Which meant the devouring had begun, and would not stop, and would eat the drip and the heartbeat and eventually the name *Vess*, which was already half-eaten anyway, given how few people left alive had cause to say it.

She got up off the stone floor of her cave for the last time in two hundred years, and her joints cracked like green wood in a fire, and she laughed at that, briefly, because it was funny, and because laughing was still allowed.

* * *

The dark followed her as she climbed.

That was the only way to describe it. The silence moved with her, or rather she moved ahead of it, and everything she passed stopped sounding behind her. The wind through the fissures cut off mid-moan. The distant rush of the underground river thinned to nothing, so that when she crossed the stone bridge over it she heard only her own footsteps—and then, testing, stamped hard, and heard even those arrive late and muffled, like footsteps remembered rather than heard.

She talked to herself the whole way up. Not for courage. For inventory.

"Wind," she said. "Gone. River, gone. My knees popping, still here, thank the—" she caught herself before naming a god whose name was currently being digested. "Thank somebody."

It occurred to her, somewhere on the third ledge, that she was doing exactly what she'd done for two centuries—paying attention, narrating, refusing to look away—and that this time the attention wasn't feeding anything. It was just her, spending herself, with no flame to catch it. There was something clean in that. All those years of careful husbandry and the fire died anyway, and now all that was left was what she actually was underneath the tending: a stubborn old lizard who did not like being told what to lose.

The fissure that opened onto the night sky was a day's climb from her cave. She made it in four hours, running when she could, which was less than she wanted and more than she expected of herself. When she finally wedged her shoulders through and pulled herself out onto the high shoulder of the mountain, the stars were gone.

Not clouded. Gone. The sky above her was not black—black was a color, and colors required light—but *absent*, a hole where seeing used to be, and Vess felt her stomach drop through two hundred years of composure. Below her the valley lay in its usual shape, and she could still see it, faintly, the fires in the village windows, the pale ribbon of the river. Sound was dying faster than sight. But even sight had edges now; the horizon was fraying like cloth, and beyond it there was nothing to report, because reporting required something to report *to*, and the silence ate witnesses too.

Down in the valley, she knew, people were noticing. They'd notice the birds stopping first. Then their own voices, going hoarse not from shouting but from being *unheard*—the silence didn't take your voice away so much as it took the space your voice traveled through. Names would go next. She'd seen the toll-keeper work; she knew how names died. First you couldn't say them. Then you couldn't recall them. Then whoever bore them became a shape, a function, a *that-one-there*, and after that it was only a matter of time before the shape forgot to keep breathing, because breathing was a habit and habits needed remembering.

One night. She had promised herself one night, and the promise had been easy to make because making promises was free. Keeping them cost.

Vess looked at her own hands in the failing light. Old hands, scarred palms, the right one still curved slightly from two hundred years of cupping a flame that no longer existed. She could feel the hollow it had left, the way an amputee feels weather in the missing limb. And she could feel—this was the strange part, the part she turned over twice before trusting—something else in that hollow. A pressure. A vast, patient weight, pressing up against the inside of the world the way water presses against a dam. The god. Still there. Still *there*, after a century of silence, after the theft and the filing and the wearing of its voice as ornament. Whatever the Regent had strangled, he had not killed. He couldn't. You can file a soul, sell a soul, wear a voice around your neck like trophies—but the god itself, the thing underneath all the transactions, was still pressed against the far side of the silence like a face against glass.

It had been pressing for a hundred years.

And the silence was eating the glass.

"Oh," Vess said softly. "You're still in there."

The pressure didn't answer. Gods, in her experience, didn't answer; that was the whole problem with them. But the pressure shifted, the way a trapped thing shifts when it hears a voice it recognizes, and Vess stood on the shoulder of the mountain with the devouring climbing up behind her and felt, for the first time in two centuries, that she was looking at the true shape of her exile. Not punishment. Not disgrace. Just a door, held shut, by her.

Ada was down there trying to break the necklace. Good. Let her. But breaking the necklace wouldn't matter if the silence finished eating first, and the silence was faster than a girl with a sword and a stolen ember, and everyone under the mountain knew it except possibly Ada, who had never once in her life been adequately warned about anything.

Vess sat down on the cold stone, folded her legs beneath her, and arranged her hands in her lap the way she'd arranged them around the flame.

* * *

Here is what offering the rest of your life looks like, from the inside: it looks like nothing at all.

No chorus. No warmth flooding the limbs. No grand bargain struck in words. Vess simply did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for two hundred years and found, at the bottom of the column, the only figure that balanced. The silence was eating the world. The god was pressing against it from the other side. Between them was a membrane, and membranes could be made of fire, and fire could be made of attention, and attention could be made of a life, if the life was willing to spend all of itself instead of some.

Two hundred years of tending a flame the size of a sparrow's heart. She had plenty left. She was, it turned out, rich in exactly the currency the moment required, and poor in every other, and there was a bleak justice in that she chose to accept rather than examine.

"Not for him," she said aloud, meaning the Regent, meaning the thief, and her voice came out ragged because the silence had reached the base of the mountain and even speaking was becoming expensive. "Understand that. I'm not doing it for him. He can unravel into his paperwork and blow away." She paused, and the pause cost her something, and she paid it. "I'm doing it because there's a girl in the valley who ties knots in reeds. And one who walks out of places nobody walks out of. And a nameless scribe who gave up the only thing he had left to buy strangers an hour. That's my treasury. That's what goes in the fire."

The silence reached her then—or its edge did. She felt it take the wind first, then the creak of her own scales settling, then the small wet sounds of her breathing beginning to fray at the borders. It was not cruel. That was somehow worse. It was thorough, indifferent, a clerk of voids closing accounts in order.

Vess closed her eyes, which hardly mattered anymore, and opened her attention, which mattered completely.

She poured.

It was not dramatic. It was stubbornness in its purest form—the same motion she had made ten thousand nights running, the watching, the refusing-to-look-away, except now the fuel was memory instead of vigilance. The old dragon who taught her, and the smell of his smoke, gone. The court she'd fled, and the shame of fleeing, gone. The first century alone, the second, the slow erosion of hope that had hardened into something better than hope, gone, gone, gone. She spent it all the way misers spend nothing and lovers spend everything, and where her life had been, a flame stood up.

Small. Of course small. A sparrow's heart, cupped in nothing now, burning in the air over her folded hands, and around it the silence recoiled—not defeated, never defeated, but held, dammed, made to wait. One night. She could feel the exact size of the purchase, the way she'd always felt the exact size of the fire: this flame would burn until dawn and not one minute past. Everything she had bought exactly that much. A night's worth of world. A night's worth of names.

Below her, far beneath the stone, she felt the pressure shift again—the god, feeling the membrane flex, pressing harder, and this time Vess swore she felt something almost like recognition pass through it, the way you feel someone look at you across a crowded room. *Hold on,* she told it, with the last of her spoken words, because after this she would need every scrap of attention for the burning. *Hold on. She's coming. The unwritten girl is coming, and she's got your voice in her sights, and all you have to do is survive one more night of being quiet.*

*I've gotten very good at quiet,* she might have added. *It's the only craft I know.*

But she didn't say it. She saved it. She banked everything—all of it, the memories, the words, the name, the long unlovely stubborn animal fact of herself—and fed it into the small flame, and the flame stood straighter against the dark, and the dark waited, patient as a ledger, for morning.

Far below, at the edge of her failing senses, Vess felt a door open. Felt footsteps—felt them as vibrations now, sound being rationed—coming fast up the trade roads. Felt a grey ember's worth of borrowed wholeness and a sword that hummed with old oaths and, faintest of all, threaded through the girl's stride like a second pulse, a voice that was not yet hers learning to wait inside her ribs.

Vess smiled, which no one saw, and held the line, which no one thanked her for, and burned.

Dawn was coming. Dawn was the deadline. And somewhere beneath the mountain, in a hall of glittering files, a thief was about to learn what two hundred years of attention looked like when it finally, finally came due.
