## 21. The Voice That Walks

I did not sleep that night. None of us did, though for different reasons.

Vess slept the way old soldiers sleep—sitting up, one eye open, her great head propped against the broken archway at the mouth of the cave where her fire had died and been born again in a mercenary's chest. She made a sound like slow bellows all night, which I learned was how dragons grieve: not with roaring but with air, moving through them, because they had spent so long holding their breath that breathing had become its own kind of mourning.

Ada slept for two hours and woke screaming without sound, which I understood better than I wanted to. You do not pour your soul into a dead flame and come out of it rested. You come out of it *hollowed in a new shape*, the way a riverbed is hollowed—by force, by time, by something passing through you that was never meant to fit.

And me? I sat by the small fire we'd rebuilt—not dragon-fire now, just ordinary wood, ordinary heat, ordinary light—and I opened the Book of Small Prophecies to its last blank page, and I found that my hands were shaking too badly to write on it.

Not from fear. From something worse. From *permission*.

For thirty years I had written what the court told me to write. Before that, I wrote what my masters told me to write. Before that, as a boy copying psalms in the scriptorium above the mountain, I had written what an older book said, in a dead hand, about a god who was silent, and no one—no one in all those years, not once—had asked me whether I believed a word of it. A scribe does not believe. A scribe *records*. It was the whole architecture of my life: ink goes where authority points it, and you keep your own thoughts in a drawer so small and so well-hidden that eventually even you can't find them.

Now the drawer was open. Now the court was dust and scattered paper, literally—when the Regent came apart, she had come apart into exactly what she had always been, which was filing—and now the only living soul who knew where half the bodies were buried was sitting by an ordinary fire with a book full of other people's crimes, shaking.

The god's voice was still in Ada. I could feel it from across the cave, the way you can feel a lit lamp in a dark room. Not warmth, exactly. *Attention.* The sense of being read.

"You're staring at the page like it owes you money," Ada said. Her voice was rougher than before, or maybe I was only hearing it properly for the first time—every word she spoke now carried a second voice underneath it, quiet, patient, the sound of something vast choosing to be small. "It doesn't. It's blank. Blank pages never owe anybody anything."

"That," I said, "is the first lie you've told since the mountain."

She almost smiled. "The god says you're wrong."

"The god," I said, "can speak for itself."

And it did. Not loudly. Never loudly—that was the whole point of it, the whole miracle of it, the thing I would spend the rest of my life failing to describe adequately. It spoke through her mouth, in her accent, with her flat valley vowels and her mercenary's clipped consonants, and it said:

"*He is right to shake. What he holds is not a book. It is a wound that learned to bind itself. Wounds should be unwrapped carefully.*"

Ada blinked, shook her head like a woman clearing water from her ear. "I hate when it does that."

"You'll get used to it," Vess rumbled from the doorway, eyes still closed. "You have several centuries."

"I don't *want* several centuries."

"Nobody wants several centuries," Vess said. "That is how you know you deserve them."

I looked down at the blank page again. The last page. After this, the book was finished—the ledger of sold things, complete at last, every debt either paid or named. And a finished book, I thought, is just a coffin with nicer binding.

So I turned past it. I pressed hard with my thumbnail into the spine until the stitching cracked, and I pulled, and the last gathering of pages tore free in my hand, clean, and I set it in the fire and watched the prophecy burn down to its final ash.

"What are you doing?" Ada asked. Not alarmed. Curious. The god's attention shifted in her like a bird settling.

"Starting a new one," I said. And I took out my pen—I had kept my pen, through everything, the toll-keeper's bridge and the filing halls and the flight and the fire; a scribe surrenders his name before he surrenders his pen—and I dipped it, and my hands were still shaking, and I wrote anyway.

*Day one after the fall of the court beneath the mountain. Record begins.*

*

Here is what I wrote in the days that followed, sitting in the cave while Vess slept and Ada learned to carry a passenger, here is what I wrote while the world outside came back on bell by bell and creek by creek:

*Item: the Regent of Quiet Things was once Serellan of the First Choir, high priest of the god whose name was spoken freely in the years before the mountain had a crown. He strangled the divine voice with his own hands—he wore gloves, the records suggest, ceremonial ones—and he wore it ever after, around his throat, in a necklace of voices taken from everyone who ever tried to tell the truth about him. There were four hundred and eleven voices in the necklace. I counted the beads. I am still counting them. Some of the beads were very small.*

*Item: each bead corresponds to a person erased from record. I have begun a list. It will be long. I have bought three additional notebooks from the ferry-town stationer, who asked no questions, which I found either admirable or ominous.*

*Item: the valley where magic cannot follow was not a natural phenomenon. It was built. The founders of Ada's mercenary order—my order, briefly, hers always—were paid in land and anonymity to guard the border-stones that held the warding in place. They were told they guarded a shrine. They were actually guarding a prison wall. The Silent God was not silent in the valley because silence lived there. It was silent there because the valley was the one place the stolen voice could not hear it weeping.*

I wrote that last line and then I stopped writing for most of an afternoon, because my eyes had gone hot and useless, and there is no dignity in crying over ink that isn't dry yet, but there is also no stopping it.

Ada found me like that. She didn't say anything clever. She just sat down across the fire, took one of my spare notebooks, and began, clumsily, in a hand like a cart accident, copying names off the list I'd started—names of the erased, names I was reconstructing from ledger fragments and Vess's memory and the beads themselves, which, now that the Regent was dust, had begun to whisper their owners when held close.

"You write like someone stabbing the paper," I told her.

"My blade-work gets compliments."

"This isn't blade-work."

"It's close enough." She blew on the wet ink, smearing it. "The god wants to know why you're doing this. It could just—tell people. Now. It has a voice again. Why do the dead need a book?"

I thought about that for a long time. Long enough that the fire burned down and I had to feed it.

"Because a voice," I finally said, "is a rumor unless it's written down. Because the Regent won and ruled and strangled a god for a hundred years, and do you know how? Not with armies. With *archives*. She didn't defeat the truth. She misfiled it." I tapped the page. "Everything the court destroyed was destroyed twice—once in fact, once in record. You can't undo the first destruction. But the second one—" My hands weren't shaking anymore. I noticed it the way you notice rain stopping. "The second one I can undo."

Ada was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice that wasn't entirely hers—or rather, that was more hers than it had ever been, because the god had stopped being a passenger and become something like a co-author:

"*Then write well,*" it said. "*Names are heavy. Carry them gently.*"

*

We went down from the mountain in stages, because the world needed seeing.

That sounds like something a poet would write, and I am not a poet, I am a clerk, but I don't know how else to put it. The silence had eaten deep in the lands beyond the valley—whole villages where people had forgotten their children's faces, rivers whose names had dissolved so completely that the fish presumably felt unnamed too—and everywhere we walked, Ada stopped, and the god spoke quietly through her, and things came back.

A woman in a burned-out farmstead remembered her son's name when the god said it aloud. Just like that. One word, in Ada's rough accent, and the woman dropped her basket and sat down in the dirt and sobbed for an hour, and then stood up and thanked us and asked if we wanted bread, because grief and hospitality apparently live in the same room in some people.

Vess flew overhead sometimes—a dark shape against a sky that grew bluer every day, the smoke of the guttering long gone—and when villagers saw her, some of them ran, and some of them knelt, and Ada told them both, in the god's patient voice, to stand up, dragons aren't for kneeling, and Vess snorted smoke and pretended not to be pleased.

And I wrote. Every night. By firelight, by moonlight, by the little lamp of attention that followed Ada everywhere now. I wrote the farmsteads and the drowned villages and the toll-roads and the souls in the glass drawers—we freed hundreds of embers on the way down from the mountain, opening drawers as we fled, and the freed souls had gone everywhere, into everything, little grey lights settling into trees and wells and sleeping children like snow. I recorded every drawer number I could remember. I reconstructed the filing system from memory, which is the kind of sentence that would have gotten me promoted under the old regime and got me only Ada's approving grunt under the new one.

On the ninth night, she asked me the question I'd been waiting for.

"Why'd you do it?" We were sitting watch together, Vess below, the god resting—yes, it rested, it liked the hours between midnight and dawn best, it said they were *quiet in a good way*. "Back at the court. Falsifying the records. That's what got you exiled, right? What were you trying to fix?"

I had rehearsed a hundred answers to that question on the road down the hill, back when I landed half-dead in her valley. All of them were lies, or mostly lies, or lies with good posture.

"The truth," I said, "is stupid. There was a girl. An ember in drawer nine-something-or-other. I processed her intake papers when I was young, and she had a name, and six months later the name was gone from the record, replaced with a number, and I checked—I checked, which no one ever does—and the order had come from above, and the girl hadn't done anything, she'd just been *sold*, the way you sell a chair." I fed the fire a stick. "And I changed the number back to the name. One entry. The smallest possible treason. And they caught me in a week, because the archive notices everything, and I ran, and I fell down a hill into your valley, and here we are."

"Where's the girl?"

"Gone. Sold onward. Or spent. Or—" I gestured at the dark. "But the name existed again for six months. Someone looking for her would have found a name instead of a number. For six months." I heard how thin that sounded and said it anyway. "It was a small thing. But it was mine."

Ada nodded slowly. Then the other voice came, gentle, in her rough accent, and it said:

"*Small things are the only things. Crowns are just many small things stacked badly.*"

"I'm putting that in the book," I said.

"Don't attribute it to me," Ada said quickly. "People will think I'm wise."

"People already think you're a god's vessel."

"People are idiots."

*

We reached the edge of her valley on the twelfth day.

She stopped at the ridgeline the way you stop at the door of a house where someone has died—knowing you have to go in, knowing nothing inside is where you left it. Below, green and loud and unbearably alive, lay the place where magic couldn't follow. Except now it could. Except now the warding stones were just stones, because the thing they'd imprisoned had walked free wearing the body of the order's finest mercenary, and the valley was only a valley, and everyone in it was going to have to learn that.

"They'll want answers," I said.

"They'll want their wights hunted and their beer brewed and their kids kept out of the millpond," Ada said. "Answers are for winter." But her hand had gone to her wrist, to the river-stone tied there—the one that remembered directions home—and I saw her thumb move over it once, twice, and stop.

Sefa was at the ferry, of course. Sefa had always been at the ferry. Some people are less like places than like weather—you can count on them to be there, and to be different every time.

I hung back. This part wasn't mine. But I watched, because I am a scribe, and watching and writing are the same act at different speeds, and I watched the ferryman's daughter take one look at Ada walking down the hill with a dragon shadow overhead and a god behind her eyes and decide—visibly, immediately, the way she seemed to decide everything—that none of it mattered next to the fact that Ada had come back.

They talked for a long time. I didn't try to hear it. Some records should stay oral.

But at the end of it, Sefa laughed—one short, sharp, real laugh, the kind you can't fake—and grabbed Ada by the collar and kissed her like a settlement, and Ada let her, and the god in Ada went very quiet and very still, the way it did around true things.

Later, on the far side of the water, Ada caught up to me on the road and said, gruffly, not looking at me:

"She's coming with us. As far as the next town, anyway. Somebody has to keep you two from starving. Dragon needs feeding, apparently. Did you know that? Nobody tells you dragons need feeding."

"I assumed," I said.

"Well, stop assuming and start writing. You're falling behind." And she walked on ahead, and the morning walked with her, and I opened my notebook against my knee and added a line:

*Item: the vessel travels with companions. One dragon, exiled, returning. One ferryman's daughter, uninvited, essential. One scribe, nameless, free.*

I looked at that last phrase for a while. *Nameless, free.* The toll-keeper had taken my family name at the bridge—three generations of Vasquez-adjacent respectability, gone down a hungry throat—and with it every record I'd ever falsified had unmade itself, every neat lie unraveled, every forged signature gone blank. Somewhere in what remained of the court's archives—I had burned what I could, and Vess had burned what I couldn't, and the freed embers had burned the rest—there were shelves of documents about a man called Tam who no longer existed in any registry, any ledger, any chain of custody. I was a clerical error now. I was the gap between entries.

It was, I reflected, the freest I had ever been. Also the least employable.

Ahead, Vess wheeled low over the road, and Ada raised a hand to her, and somewhere behind us Sefa was arguing cheerfully with the ferryman—her father, presumably, though I hadn't dared ask—and the world was coming back on, region by region, name by name, and there was so much left to do. Four hundred and eleven voices, minus whatever the shattering had freed. Hundreds of villages half-eaten by silence. A continent of misfiled truth. And one book, mine, three notebooks in and already falling apart from use, that intended to set every single record straight.

I am not a prophet. I was trained never to be one. But I will record this much, because it happened, and recording things that happen is the whole of my office now:

As we crested the first hill out of the valley country, the god spoke through Ada without warning—one word, quiet, in that rough accent, pitched not to us but to the road ahead, to the towns ahead, to the whole listening dark:

"*Begin.*"

And I did.
