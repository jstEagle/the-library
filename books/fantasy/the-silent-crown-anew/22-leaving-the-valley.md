## 22. Leaving the Valley

The valley looked smaller from the ridgeline.

Ada had never seen it from the ridgeline. That was the whole point of the valley—magic couldn't cross its rim, and neither, in a hundred years, had she. She had been born inside it, contracted inside it, bled inside it, and she had assumed without ever saying it aloud that she would die inside it too, probably badly, probably on some routine wight-hunt at the edge where her god-sworn blade still worked and nowhere else.

Now she stood above it with a god living behind her ribs, and the valley was just a green cup of a place with a river through the middle like a silver stitch.

"You're hesitating," said the voice inside her. Not out loud. It never spoke out loud unless she let it, and mostly she didn't, because hearing your own rough accent come from somewhere that wasn't your throat still made her skin crawl in a way she hadn't decided was bad yet.

"I'm resting," Ada said.

"You've been resting for an hour."

"It's called thinking. Mercenaries do it between jobs."

The voice did something that wasn't quite a laugh but lived in the same neighborhood. It sounded like her when she was younger, before the contracts and the scars—the laugh she'd had before she learned to keep it small. That was the strangest part of carrying the Silent God. It didn't speak like thunder. It spoke like her, if she'd ever let herself be loud.

She started down the slope.

* * *

The first thing she noticed was that people saw her.

That shouldn't have been remarkable. It was remarkable anyway. For twenty-six years Ada Villalobos had moved through this valley like water through a net—everyone knew her face the way you know the ferry schedule, and no one *saw* her, because the court beneath the mountain had spent her anonymity like coin, buying her invisibility generation after generation to keep their stolen silence hidden. She'd thought that was just how she was. A woman you hired to kill things and forgot by supper.

But the Regent was dust now. The necklace of voices was shattered into a thousand singing fragments that Tam swore were already finding their way back to throats all over the world. And when Ada walked down the ferry road at dusk, the cooper's boy looked up from stacking staves and *looked* at her—not past, not through—and said, "Mistress Villalobos? We heard you died under the mountain."

"Rumors of my death," Ada said, "are extremely accurate. I got better."

He stared at her. Then he laughed—a real one, startled out of him—and she felt it land somewhere under her sternum like a struck bell.

*Seen*, said the voice, quietly, and she told it to hush, and it hushed, smugly.

The village had changed in ways that took her a full minute to catalog. There was sound where there had been a kind of muffledness she'd mistaken for ordinary quiet her whole life. The smithy's hammer rang clear across the square instead of thudding like a fist on wool. Someone's grandmother was singing off-key at a window and the notes actually carried. The silence that had sat over the valley like snow for a century—the silence she'd been raised to think of as peace—was gone, and what remained underneath was just... noise. Ordinary human racket. She stood in the middle of it with her eyes burning and pretended to be checking the rooftops for archers.

Old habits. The war was over. Nobody had told her body yet.

* * *

Sefa was at the ferry landing.

Of course she was. Dusk was when the last crossing went out, and Sefa ran the evening crossing now that her father's hands had gone unsteady, poling the flat boat across the black water with those strong brown arms Ada had spent exactly one perfect date admiring before prophecy had ruined everything.

Ada stopped at the top of the bank and watched her work. Let herself watch, which was also new. For years she'd rationed looking at Sefa the way you ration water in a drought—one glance here, half a smile there, nothing that could be noticed or named, because being noticed was dangerous when you were Unwritten. Now she could stand in plain sight and stare at the ferryman's daughter like any fool in love, and nobody would file a report about it.

The god stirred. *Ask her,* it said, in her own voice, with her own impatience.

"I'm getting there," she muttered.

"Mistress Villalobos." One of Sefa's deckhands had spotted her and was squinting up the bank. "You want crossing? Last one goes now."

"No crossing," Ada said. "I'm the cargo."

The deckhand didn't understand that, which was fine. Neither did she, fully. She walked down to the landing, and Sefa turned at the sound of boots on planks, and the pole stopped mid-stroke, and the whole world narrowed to the space between them—which was about nine feet of wet wood and a hundred years of sold souls, give or take.

"You came back," Sefa said.

"Told you I would."

"You told me a lot of things the night you left. Most of them were lies you believed yourself."

"That's fair." Ada came closer. Up close, Sefa had new lines around her eyes—three months' worth, hard ones—and a bruise on her forearm from a rope, and she was the most beautiful thing in a valley full of things Ada had never properly looked at. "I brought the truth this time. All of it. It's heavier than it sounds."

Sefa tied off the pole and crossed her arms. Waiting. She'd always been good at waiting; it was the ferryman in her. Water comes when it comes.

So Ada told her.

She told her about the book of prophecies with her name inked a century ago, and the page she'd burned, and the ash spelling her name again. About Tam falling downhill with his satchel of forbidden records. About the ledger of small sold things—*a soul, unnamed, sold twice*—and learning that her grandmother's generation had sold her before she drew breath, and that the Silent God had bought her back with its own voice, spending itself silent to keep her whole. About Vess, the exiled dragon, cupping a flame the size of a thumbnail and calling it the last hope of the world. About the archive halls, the glass drawer, the grey ember that had been hers since before she existed. About the choice in the dark: take the soul back and be whole while the silence devoured everything, or pour it into the dead fire and become hollow forever.

About what she'd chosen.

"And now?" Sefa asked, when the story ran out. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles weren't. "Now there's a god in there?"

"Now there's a god in here," Ada agreed. "It talks in my accent. It's very judgmental about my eating habits."

"What happens to you? To—" Sefa gestured vaguely at all of her, the scarred hands, the mercenary's stance, the woman. "Does the god wear you like the Regent wore the voices? Do you get a say?"

"That's the thing I came to tell you." Ada pulled the glove off her left hand—the hand that had held the ember, the hand that now sometimes glowed faintly at the fingertips when the god was pleased, which Sefa did not need to know about yet—and took both of Sefa's rope-rough palms in it. "It doesn't wear me. It *asks*. Every time. It's been silent a hundred years and it has opinions about everything and it asks my permission before every single word, like a guest who won't put his feet up. I'm still me. Just... me with a tenant. A polite one."

"A polite god." Sefa's mouth twitched. "The old stories say gods don't ask."

"The old stories were written by thieves who wanted us quiet." Ada squeezed her hands. "Which brings me to the choice part."

"I figured there'd be a choice part."

"I'm leaving the valley." The words came easier than she expected, which was its own kind of grief. "Not tonight. Tomorrow, maybe the day after. There's work now—real work. Everything the court stole is scattered everywhere, souls and names and voices, and someone has to carry them home, and apparently the someone is me, because I'm the only person alive who's a walking receipt for the whole crime. Tam's coming as far as the archives at Cael Harrow to start writing it all down. Vess is..." She paused. "Vess is sleeping off giving most of her life to hold back the dark for one night. She'll fly again, eventually. Dragons are stubborn."

"And you want me to—"

"I want you to know I'm asking, not telling." Ada's throat tightened. She pushed through it; she'd fought a Regent, she could survive a conversation. "I spent my whole life invisible so nobody I loved would get hurt. It turns out staying was the thing that hurt everyone. Two villagers died because I lingered. So I'm done lingering. But I'm also done deciding for other people. So: come with me, or stay, or—I'll be honest, I don't even know what the third option is, but if there's a third option you'll find it, because you found a river-stone that remembers directions home, which I still maintain is witchcraft."

Sefa laughed then, wet-eyed and furious about it, and dug in her pocket, and pressed that same stone into Ada's palm—smooth grey river-glass, warm from her skin. "You kept it."

"Course I kept it. It remembered home. Turned out I needed reminding what home was." Ada closed her fingers around it. "So. What do you say, ferryman's daughter? The world outside is loud and magic-ridden and full of courts that file souls like coins. There's a dragon in it. There's a scribe who gave up his family name to buy us passage and now introduces himself as 'the man formerly known as.' It's a terrible life. You'd hate it. You'd also be the only person on the road who can make me laugh when the god gets pompous."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. The river moved under the landing, patient, going where rivers go.

"My father can't run the crossing alone," she said. "My brother's two seasons from being able. My mother needs another year before she admits it."

"That's a maybe," Ada said, heart doing something undignified.

"That's a *when*." Sefa stepped in close, close enough that Ada could smell lamp-oil and river-water, and put her hand flat over the place behind Ada's ribs where the god lived. "A season. Maybe two. I'll find you—you have a stone that remembers directions home, and I have a boat that goes anywhere the water does. And when I come, I'm not coming to visit. I'm coming to stay. Understood, vessel-of-the-god?"

"Understood," Ada managed.

Sefa kissed her then, brief and fierce, on the mouth, in front of the deckhand and the whole dusk-colored river, and Ada—who had faced down a Regent, a devouring silence, and a drawer full of grey embers—stood there afterward like a girl on her first date, which, in fairness, she technically still owed Sefa from three months ago.

"Dinner first," Sefa said, stepping back, cheeks dark. "Before you vanish over the ridge again. My mother's making eel pie. You're coming. Both of you."

"She means me too," Ada told the god.

*I gathered,* said the Silent God, and for the first time in a hundred years, something that might have been hunger passed through her chest that had nothing to do with divinity. Eel pie. Even gods, it seemed, had been listening all along.

* * *

They left at dawn, because leaving at dawn is how you do a thing you're afraid of—before the light gets honest.

Tam waited at the valley's edge with his satchel and his blank new book, hopping foot to foot against the cold. He'd shaved. He looked, Ada thought, like a man who had lost his name and found himself in the wreckage, and couldn't decide whether to mourn or celebrate, and had settled on celebrating loudly to drown out the mourning.

"You slept?" he asked.

"Badly."

"He talked all night?"

"He *listened* all night. There's a difference."

"There really isn't," Tam said, and they walked.

The path climbed away from the river, past the wight-meadow where it had all begun, past the cooper's yard, up through the birches to the ridgeline where the valley's long spell finally loosened its grip on the air. Ada stopped once, halfway, and looked back.

The valley lay below in morning gold. Smoke rose from chimneys. The smithy was already hammering, loud and unashamed. On the far shore of the river, small with distance, Sefa stood on the landing watching them go, one hand raised—not waving, just raised, the way you hold a line open. Holding a place.

Ada raised her hand back. Held it there until the birches took the view.

Then she turned and walked up over the ridge, past the boundary where magic had never followed, and stepped out of the valley for the first time in her life.

The world opened. Hills rolling east like frozen waves, roads stitching through them, distances she had no names for. Wind that smelled of places she'd never been. Somewhere far off, a mountain range wearing clouds like a crown, and beneath one of those mountains, an empty throne room full of dust that used to be a Regent, and above all of it a sky enormous enough to make her feel exactly the right size: small, but *counted*.

Tam stopped beside her, breathing hard, grinning like a fool. "Well, Unwritten One. Where first?"

Ada opened her mouth to answer—

And the god spoke.

Out loud. Into the morning air, in her rough valley accent, pitched low and clear and carrying, for the first time in a hundred years, to ears that were not her own:

"*Ada.*"

Just the name. Nothing else. It rolled down the hillside ahead of them like a stone dropped in still water, and birds went up from the birches, and Tam made a sound like a man witnessing a birth, and Ada Villalobos—sold twice, bought back, burned, hollowed, filled, seen—stood on the threshold of the whole wide world and answered:

"Yeah," she said. "I hear you. Let's go."

And they went.
