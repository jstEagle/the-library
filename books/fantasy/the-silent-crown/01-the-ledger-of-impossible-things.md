## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Gilded Compass kept its accounts the way it kept its secrets: in a hand so small and neat that no one who glanced at the pages would ever understand what they were reading.

Jude Zhou sat alone in the counting room with a single lamp turned low, the way he liked it. Low light was honest light. It showed the ink and nothing else—no gold leaf on the sign outside, no gilt trim on the guild's famous door, none of the gaudy promises that made merchants climb the mountain road in the first place. Just numbers, which never lied, and entries, which almost always did.

He dipped his pen and wrote:

*Received of Master Corvane, weather-trader, one (1) thunderstorm, bottled, vintage of the Saltmarsh summer, provenance attested. Paid out: three hundred crowns, less commission.*

Below it:

*Received of the Widow Anselm, one debt, discharged in dreams, fourteen nights' duration, quality fair to good. Balance carried forward.*

A stranger reading those lines would have thought them poetry, or madness. Jude thought of them as Tuesday.

He blotted the page and sat back, listening—not with his ears, though his ears were good, trained by years of pretending they weren't—to the house around him. The Gilded Compass at night had a sound like a ship at anchor: timbers settling, wind worrying the eaves, and beneath all of it, faint as a pulse, the murmur of animals talking to one another in the dark.

Most people believed beasts made noise. Jude knew better. Cats *conversed*. Horses held opinions. The rats in the walls ran a gossip trade that would have shamed any market he'd ever recorded in these ledgers, and tonight, as most nights, the cats were holding court in the kitchen, where the banked stove still gave off warmth.

"—the third time this month," Mouser was saying. Mouser was a gray tom with a torn ear and the self-regard of a minor duke. "She leaves the fish head out like an offering and then acts wounded when I take it. I am not a charity, I am a professional."

"You ate her bootlace last week," said Puddle, the kitchen molly, younger and less impressed by him than she should have been.

"That was tribute. Different thing entirely."

"The stable cats say the bay mare kicked her stall again."

"The bay mare kicks her stall every night. She has opinions about the dark. Everyone in this house has opinions about the dark except the humans, who just sleep through it like sacks of grain." A pause, the sound of a tongue rasping fur. "Though she kicked harder tonight."

Jude's pen stopped above the paper.

It was a small thing. The bay mare, Juniper, was a large opinionated animal with a history of complaint—he'd recorded her moods for two years the way other clerks recorded exchange rates. But *harder tonight* was data, and data was his trade, even when it arrived through the wrong ears.

He wrote the last entry of the evening—*Carried forward: 4,112 crowns, 9 silver, debts uncountable*—sanded it, closed the ledger, and let himself listen properly.

Under the kitchen talk, under the horses shifting in their stalls, under the mice conducting their tiny economies behind the wainscoting, there was something else. Not words yet. More like the feeling before words: a tension in the house's animal population, the way air feels before lightning.

Winter nerves, he told himself. The passes would close soon. Beasts felt the closing of the passes the way sailors felt a change in tide.

He told himself that, and mostly believed it, and took the lamp up to bed.

* * *

His room was at the top of the east stair, chosen for reasons he'd never said aloud. From its narrow window he could see the gate, the road below the switchbacks, and the tree line—and from the loft across the yard, if he leaned out, the dogs could see him, which meant he could hear them.

The dogs were the least subtle creatures in the guild. Where the cats gossiped and the horses philosophized, the dogs simply reported, in a vocabulary of perhaps forty concepts, of which *stranger* was the largest.

"Smell," the old hound Bracken was muttering to the younger one, Fern, as Jude leaned into the cold. "Smell on the wind. Under the snow-smell."

"What smell?"

"Man-smell. Many-man. Far. Very far." A pause. "Maybe not man."

"Everything's man or meat," said Fern, who was young.

"Not everything," said Bracken, and there was something in the old dog's voice that made the hair rise along Jude's arms, something he had no word for because no human language had ever bothered to invent one—the tone beasts used for things older than themselves.

He pulled his head back inside and stood a moment in the dark, heart tapping quick against his ribs.

*Winter nerves,* he told himself again. *Dogs smell wolves. Wolves are many-man-shaped and not-man. There. Solved.*

Twenty-nine years of practice had made him very good at solving things away. He blew out the candle and lay down and did not sleep for a long while.

* * *

In the morning the Gilded Compass woke the way it always did: Vess's bell in the courtyard, sharp as a coin dropped on marble; the clatter of the kitchen; Tam the farrier already singing to himself in the smithy, a tuneless drone that the horses tolerated and the cats complained about. Jude came down last, as he always did, slipping into the breakfast bench at the end nearest the wall, where he could eat with his back protected and his ears full of the room.

Vess Arandel presided from the head of the table without appearing to preside at all. She was a broad, gray-streaked woman with a merchant's smile and a smuggler's eyes, and she ran the Compass the way a river runs through a valley—inevitably, and with hidden depth. She was complaining about the price of lamp oil. She was also, Jude noticed, watching the window toward the road more often than the price of lamp oil warranted.

"Snow by tomorrow," Tam said. "My knee says so, and my knee's never lied to me except about quitting drink."

"Your knee and the almanac agree, miracle of miracles," said Vess. "Which means we have today to finish the Corvane inventory before the pass shuts and he's stuck here eating my stores and telling me about the Saltmarsh summer for the ninth time."

"He bottled a storm," said the young clerk, Pell, who still found the guild's business wonderful. "You can't be tired of hearing about it. He *bottled a storm.*"

"I can be tired of anything told nine times." Vess's gaze flicked to Jude. "Ledgers balanced?"

"Balanced," Jude said. "Corvane's account is settled through midwinter. The dream-debt from the Widow Anselm posted clean. We're carrying four thousand crowns and—" he hesitated over the phrase he'd written the night before, and decided it was true enough to say aloud, "—debts uncountable."

"Debts uncountable," Vess repeated, satisfied, as if it were the healthiest line in any book. "Good. That's what we're for."

Pell laughed, thinking it a joke. Jude did not laugh, because he had spent six years learning that Vess Arandel rarely joked, and never about the guild's purpose. He simply didn't know what the purpose was, beyond the surface of it—the trading house that dealt in impossible things, the waystation where a client could buy a bottled thunderstorm or sell fourteen nights of borrowed dreaming, and where nobody asked too precisely how such trades were done.

That not-asking went both ways. It was the foundation of his life here, and he maintained it daily, like sweeping a floor.

Because here was the thing about Jude Zhou that appeared in no ledger: when the bay mare kicked her stall, he knew she was frightened, not stubborn. When the cats argued in the kitchen, he followed every word. When Bracken the hound said *maybe not man* into the dark, Jude heard it, and understood it, and could have answered it—and answering was the one thing in the world he would not do.

His grandmother had called the gift the Listening. His father had called it the family curse, quietly, once, the night before soldiers came to their village farm with writs of exile and burned the bee-hives because bees were beasts and beasts that spoke to a speaker's blood were contraband. Jude had been seven. He remembered his mother's hands shaking as she packed. He remembered that the dog had tried to follow the cart and his father had thrown stones at it—not from cruelty, but because a beast-speaker's dog following him into exile was evidence, and evidence got families killed twice.

So Jude had learned the trade of silence. He'd learned it so well that even now, surrounded by people, fed and paid and sheltered, he lived like a man holding his breath. Six years at the Compass. Six years of balancing books and listening to cats and never once letting his face change when a horse told him exactly where a runaway apprentice had gone, or a raven described the brand on a warden's saddlebag. Small uses only. Invisible uses. The gift spent like coins slipped under doors, never counted, never seen.

It was a good life. He told himself it was a good life. It was warm, and safe, and it had a shape, and the shape was a box, and he had built the box himself, board by board, and some nights—like last night, lying awake listening to a dog say *not everything*—he noticed how exactly the box fit, and wondered when he'd stopped being able to stand up straight inside it.

"Zhou." Vess again, and he surfaced. "Corvane arrives within the hour, weather willing. You'll do the receiving?"

"As always."

"And keep your ear—" she paused, just slightly, in a way he'd never been able to parse in six years, "—to the ground. Road's been quiet too long. Quiet roads make me itch."

"Quiet roads make everyone itch," Tam said comfortably. "It's the loud ones you want to fear."

Everyone laughed. Jude smiled the small smile he kept for such moments, and rose to fetch his coat, and did not say: *The dogs smelled something on the wind last night. Something far away. Something the old hound wouldn't name.*

There was no column in any ledger for that.

* * *

Corvane arrived at noon in a sledge drawn by two white mules, wearing a coat made of something Jude chose not to identify, and carrying his famous cargo in a crate lined with lead and wool. The storm inside it muttered when the crate tilted—a low grumble, like a sleeper disturbed—and every animal in the yard went silent at the sound, then resumed louder than before, the way crowds do after a fright.

Jude met him at the door with the receipt book and the guild's best tea face.

"Master Corvane. The Compass welcomes you."

"Little clerk!" Corvane embraced the air near Jude's shoulders, a habit of men who hugged everyone and touched no one. "Is the mistress in? Is the fire good? Is the wine better?" He lowered his voice to a stage whisper that half the yard could hear. "And is the vault ready for my little treasure? She gets restless, storms do. Cooped up. Like all of us in winter, eh?"

"We'll log it into the deep cellar," Jude said. "Standard terms. Inspection on thaw."

They walked the crate down together, past the kitchens where Puddle the molly watched from the windowsill with her tail curled like a question mark, past the stair where Bracken lay and did not growl at Corvane but watched him the whole way down with flat yellow attention. In the deep cellar, among crates of preserved moonlight and casks of river-forgetting and one barrel, sealed and labeled in Vess's own hand, that Jude had never asked about, they set the storm down. Through the lead lining it grumbled once more, petulant, and went quiet.

"Strange winter coming," Corvane said, conversational, on the stairs back up. "My mules wouldn't take the high road. Stood at the fork for an hour, screaming at it. Mules scream, did you know? Horrible sound. Had to take the low pass, added two days." He shook his head. "Animals know things. My grandmother always said so. Of course, my grandmother also said she'd been married to a bear, so."

Jude made the appropriate noncommittal sound. His heart was doing arithmetic it didn't want to finish: mules refusing the high road, a bay mare kicking harder, a hound smelling *maybe not man*, and now this—a whole countryside of beasts pulling against their harnesses, pointing, in their wordless way, the same direction.

Toward the guild. Or past it. He couldn't tell yet. That was the trouble with listening to animals: they had magnificent senses and terrible geography.

At the top of the stairs, Corvane paused. For a moment the weather-trader's face lost its performance, and underneath it Jude saw the thing that made the man rich: a genuine hunger, sharp and patient, the look of someone who had spent his life finding the exact price of everything and had begun, lately, to wonder about the things that had no price.

"They say," Corvane said lightly, "that this winter will be a hard one for hiding things."

"Do they," said Jude.

"Mm. Old clients talk. Old roads carry rumors the way rivers carry silt." The performance returned, the grin, the spread hands. "But what do I know? I sell weather. Lead me to that excellent wine, little clerk, and tell me nothing important whatsoever."

Jude led him to the wine, and told him nothing important whatsoever, and was praised for his discretion, which was the compliment he'd been collecting his whole life and which tasted, that evening, more like an accusation.

* * *

Night again. The counting room, the lamp, the ledger. He opened it to the carried-forward line and looked at it a long time—*4,112 crowns, 9 silver, debts uncountable*—and found, for the first time in six years, that the numbers didn't settle him.

From the kitchen, faintly, the cats.

"She did it again," Mouser was saying. "Stood at the gate staring down the road. The girl—Pell's little sister, the visitor. Stood there twenty minutes in the cold, just staring."

"Children stare," said Puddle.

"Not like that. Like she was waiting for something to come up the road. And the horses—" Mouser's voice dropped into the register cats use when they wish to seem casual about something that frightens them. "The horses are all facing south. All of them. All night. Have you noticed? Every stall. South."

A pause.

"So?" said Puddle, but her tail was lashing; Jude could hear it against the sill.

"So nothing," said Mouser. "Probably nothing. Eat your mouse."

Jude set down his pen. Outside the window, the snow had begun—the first fat flakes of the season, drifting slow through the lamplight, beautiful and dumb and blind. Beyond the gate, the road fell away into darkness toward the switchbacks and the pass and the world.

And somewhere down that darkness, so far it wasn't yet a sound, only a pressure, only a leaning, like the whole animal world inhaling—

Something was coming up the road.

He wrote no entry for it. There was no column. He closed the ledger, trimmed the lamp, and sat in the small circle of his careful invisible life, listening to the snow come down, and waited to find out what it would cost him.
