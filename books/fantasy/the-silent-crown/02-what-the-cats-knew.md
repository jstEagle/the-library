## 2. What the Cats Knew

The snow kept falling for three days.

Jude learned this the way he learned most things at the Gilded Compass: not by looking out the window, but by listening to what the animals said about it. The cats complained first—cats always did—and then the ravens that roosted in the eaves of the stable roof, and finally old Marrow, the mule who had opinions about weather the way scholars had opinions about theology: long, pedantic, and impossible to disprove.

*Cold,* Marrow had pronounced on the second morning, standing hipshot in his stall while Jude forked straw around his hooves. *Cold like the year we lost the orchard. Cold with a smell under it.*

"Under it," Jude repeated, keeping his voice at the low murmur he used for talking to animals within earshot of people—a voice pitched so it might be mistaken for humming, or prayer, or nothing at all. "What kind of smell?"

Marrow considered. Mules considered everything; it was their nature and their burden. *The kind of cold that has been somewhere else first,* he said at last. *That has passed over other country and carried pieces of it.*

Jude leaned on the pitchfork and thought about that longer than he wanted to. He had spent twenty-nine years teaching himself not to over-read the beasts' talk. They were honest but imprecise, the way children were honest but imprecise, and if you went chasing every strange image they offered you'd end up like the speaker his mother had told stories about—a woman in the southern valleys who had heard a flock of geese say *fire coming* and burned her own barn to be safe from it. The fire had come, three years later, lightning, an act of no god in particular, and the woman had died in the poorhouse still insisting she'd saved everyone.

So. Marrow smelled a cold that had traveled. It was winter. Everything in winter had traveled.

"That's just cold, old man," Jude said, and gave him an extra handful of hay because he liked him anyway.

But that night, balancing the ledger by lamplight, he found himself writing slower than usual. The entry was simple enough—one-third share of a bottled thunderstorm, sold to a mining concern in the lowlands, payment rendered in iron nails and a promise of spring salt—but his pen kept drifting toward the margin, toward the little private shorthand he used when the animals told him things the humans didn't need to know. He'd developed it over years: a dot here, a curl there, notes that looked like smudges or pen trials to any auditor. *Ravens report cart tracks on the north road, frozen over twice.* A curl. *Cat says the mice have gone quiet in the granary—not eaten, gone.* Another curl. He sat back and looked at the accumulating smudges and told himself, firmly, that winter made everything quiet. Winter made mice prudent. Winter sent carts up roads before the pass closed, because every trader with sense was racing the snowline.

He was very good at telling himself things. He'd had practice.

* * *

On the fourth day, the cats stopped complaining.

Jude noticed the absence before he understood it, the way you notice a clock stopping. The Compass kept nine cats, officially—Vess counted them as staff, entered them in the ledger as *security*, and paid them in fish and jurisdiction—and they were never silent. They grumbled at the weather, at each other, at the dogs, at the indignity of wet paws. Their gossip was the guild's true morning bulletin: which trader had cried into his soup, which porter was stealing from the till, which room smelled wrong.

That morning, when Jude came down with the milk pails, all nine of them were sitting in a row on the great hall's wide windowsill, facing east, toward the road.

Not sleeping. Not grooming. Sitting, in a line, like jurors.

He stood in the doorway with the pails sweating in his hands and listened, really listened, letting the careful half-mute murmur drop away, opening that door inside himself that he kept bolted everywhere except alone with the beasts. What came through wasn't words, exactly. Cat-speech was mostly position and temperature and intent, and what nine cats' worth of intent felt like, all aimed the same direction, was this:

*Something is walking up the road, and we are watching it, and none of us will say its name.*

"Cats," Jude said aloud, softly, and the word came out rougher than he meant it to, unused as his real voice was before noon. One of them—the gray matriarch they called Duchess, who had once stared down a wolverine over a chicken carcass—turned her head a fraction and looked at him with yellow eyes full of something he had never seen in a cat before.

Patience. The terrible patience of a creature waiting for a thing it knows is coming.

He set down the milk and got out of the hall.

* * *

By midday he had talked himself back onto his feet, mostly. He inventoried the storerooms. He argued with the cook about the price of dried apples. He entered two trades in the ledger: a love-token recovered from a riverbed, and a night's lodging bought with a map to a hot spring that had, per the seller, recently moved. Impossible things, ordinary prices. The Compass did its work, and the work was steady, and steady was the whole architecture of his life.

But he caught himself doing it again at dusk—standing at the east window with his hand flat against the glass, reaching out along the ground the way he could, faintly, through the feet of birds and the bellies of burrowing things, feeling for weight on the road.

Nothing yet. Or rather—something not-yet. The road had a quality he had no ledger column for. Empty the way a held breath is empty.

"You'll wear a groove in those boards."

He hadn't heard Vess come in. He never did; she walked like the guild itself, present everywhere and audible nowhere. She was a broad, grey-streaked woman with forearms that had hauled cargo on four coasts and eyes that had priced everything they'd ever seen, including, he suspected, him.

"Just watching the snow," he said.

"Mm." She came to stand beside him, close enough that he could smell tallow and ink and, beneath it, the woodsmoke of some far country he couldn't name. Together they looked out at the white road, the black pines, the dark coming down off the pass like water filling a bowl. "Third winter you've stood at this window," Vess said. "Every year, same posture. Like a man listening for a knock." She didn't look at him. "I don't ask, Zhou. You've noticed I don't ask. It's one of my better qualities."

"It is," Jude said, and meant it, and hated how much relief lived in the meaning.

"But I'll say this much." Her voice stayed level, conversational, the tone she used for quoting freight rates. "When whatever you're listening for arrives—I'd rather hear it from you than from the walls." She tapped one knuckle against the windowpane. "Buildings talk to me too. Different language. Same news, usually."

She left before he could decide whether to be terrified or grateful. That was Vess's gift: she left you holding both, and neither fit in your pocket, and you had to walk around all day carrying them.

* * *

It was the horses that ended the argument.

They came in from the far pasture at the fifth evening's feeding time—or rather, they didn't. Jude was in the yard with a lantern when he heard the stable boy shouting, and by the time he reached the paddock gate the boy was standing in the trampled snow waving his arms uselessly, because the six horses were drawn up at the far fence line in a tight formation no horse had ever chosen voluntarily, ears pinned, hindquarters bunched, staring down the east road with their eyes rolling white.

"They won't come in," the boy babbled. "Master Zhou, they won't—they were fine at noon, they were eating, and then—" He flinched as the lead mare screamed, high and thin. "They won't come in, sir, and I can't make them."

"I know," Jude said. "Get inside. Now."

The boy went. Jude latched the gate behind him and stood alone in the blue snow-light, and let the bolted door swing open all the way, for the first time in months, wider than was safe, wider than he'd let it since—

The horses' fear hit him like cold water poured down his collar.

Animals feared plenty of things: wolves, fire, rope, the particular gait of men with certain intentions. But their fear was always local, always *now*, a bright animal flare that burned out fast. This was different. This was old fear wearing new clothes. This was a memory in the blood, rising. The mare at the fence line was not afraid of anything on the road. She was afraid of what her great-great-grandmothers had been afraid of, before anyone had bred the memory thinner, and it had come up out of her like groundwater through a floor.

*It smells like them,* she was saying, to no one, to the fence, to the sky. *It walks like the tall ones walked. It comes wrapped in wool but underneath it smells like the tall ones and the dogs that hunt us by our names.*

Jude's breath went out of him in a cloud and hung there.

Tall ones. He knew the phrase. Every beast-speaker child knew it, knew it the way human children knew nursery rhymes about plague—half-meaningless, fully dreadful, sung in whispers. In the oldest strata of the beasts' tongue, in the words that predated kingdoms and possibly predated mountains, *the tall ones* were the hunters from the courts of men, the ones who had ended the war by ending the speaking, the ones whose descendants were called wardens now, polite, bureaucratic wardens with writs and scent-hounds, and everyone pretended the old name was dead.

Everyone except the horses. Horses kept accounts longer than empires did.

Jude walked into the paddock. Slowly, hands visible, breathing even, the way you approached any frightened animal—and these were frightened in a way he had never once encountered in ten years of professional intimacy with beasts. He reached the mare. She let him take her halter. Her heart was going like a drum in a procession.

"Hush," he murmured, and then, in the old low tongue, the words his mother had taught him in a cellar with the shutters nailed: *No tall ones here. No hunting here. My name is small and my name is yours.* Nonsense, probably. Comfort anyway. She pressed her hot forehead against his chest and shuddered, and slowly, one by one, the others followed him in, and the stable boy, watching from the kitchen window with his mouth open, would swear ever after that Master Zhou had simply whistled.

Let him swear it. Whistling was safe. Whistling got written down nowhere.

But inside, with the horses stalled and the lantern lit, Jude stood in the aisle among the steam and the stamping and understood, with the clarity that only ever arrived after the point of denial had been passed, three things.

First: something was on the road, days out, and every animal between here and the pass could feel it coming, the way you feel weather in a scar.

Second: it was not traders. Traders smelled of grease and debt. Whatever came wore wool over an old smell, and the beasts remembered the smell.

And third—he closed his eyes and admitted it to the dark aisle where only the horses could hear, and the horses already knew—third: his gift, the thing he had buried so deep and so long, the careful invisible life, was about to become the only useful thing in the building.

He had spent his whole existence making himself a man no story needed. And the world, with its ancient patience, its terrible horse-memory patience, was walking up the road to make him necessary.

In the rafters overhead, the ravens had begun to arrive, silently, one by one, taking up positions along the beams like a court convening.

None of them said a word.

Neither did Jude. But as he banked the lantern and climbed the stairs to the hall, where lamplight and supper-smells and human voices made their small warm illusion of safety, he found himself counting—doors, windows, exits, the cellar hatch, the loft ladder—an inventory he'd never thought to take, in a house he'd begun, without ever signing anything, to think of as his.

Outside, the snow had stopped.

The sky above the pass was swept clean and hard with stars, the kind of sky that meant cold enough to crack stone—and distance enough, tonight, to see a long way down the road.

A long way down the road, at the very edge of what starlight could carry, something was moving.
