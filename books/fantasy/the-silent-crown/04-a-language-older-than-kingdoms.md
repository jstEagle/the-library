## 4. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

Morning came the way winter mornings came to the Gilded Compass: gray light seeping through frost-ferned windows, the smell of porridge and woodsmoke, and the cats complaining about it all.

Jude had not slept. He had sat by his hearth until the coals went to ash, turning over the sound of that girl's voice in his mind like a coin too old to spend. *The same as me.* He had heard those words in a tongue he had never been taught, and understood them the way one understands a mother's face—without translation, without thought.

He rose before the household stirred and did what he always did when his hands shook: he worked. He fetched the morning ledger, sharpened his pen, set out ink. Numbers were a language with no ghosts in it. Numbers never asked him who he was.

The guild was still half-asleep when he slipped down the corridor toward the blue room where Vess had put the child, but the door stood open a hand's width, and through the gap he saw something that stopped him mid-stride.

The girl was sitting on the floor in her borrowed nightgown, cross-legged, perfectly still. Around her, arranged with great seriousness, sat three of the guild's cats: Marrow, the fat orange tom; his daughter Soot; and old Grey-Nose, who was older than some of the rafters. The girl was talking to them—low, liquid sounds that rose and fell like water over stones—and the cats were *listening*. Not the polite listening cats gave humans while waiting for food. Real listening. Grey-Nose's whiskers were forward. Soot's tail-tip flicked in rhythm, the way it did when she was following an argument.

And then Marrow answered.

Jude had spent thirty years among animals and knew every register of their speech—the complaint, the demand, the warning, the contented drone. This was none of those. Marrow was speaking back, deliberately, in the same flowing syllables, and though a cat's throat could not shape human words, it could shape these. The sounds fit a cat's mouth as if they had been made for it. As if, Jude thought with a chill walking up his spine, they *had* been.

The girl laughed—a small, cracked sound, like ice on a pond beginning to give—and touched Marrow's head between the ears.

Jude stepped back from the door before either of them could see him, and stood in the cold corridor with his heart hammering against his ribs like something trying to get out.

***

Breakfast was chaos, as breakfast at the Compass always was: Tam the farrier burning the flatbread again, two wool-factors arguing over freight rates, Vess presiding from the head of the table with her silver-streaked hair pinned up and her eyes doing their quiet arithmetic of everyone present. The girl sat wedged between Vess and the cook, Brannit, who was feeding her honeyed porridge with the tenderness she usually reserved for her ovens.

"What do we call her?" Brannit demanded. "We can't go on saying 'the girl' like she's a shipment."

"Ask her," said Tam.

"We *did* ask her," said Brannit. "She looked at me like I'd spoken backwards."

It was true. Jude watched the child across the table. She followed the conversation's music—her eyes moved speaker to speaker, tracking tone—but the words slid off her. She was hungry, exhausted, wary in the way of small creatures in large rooms. When a serving knife clattered she flinched so hard the bench jumped.

"Wren," said Vess.

Everyone looked at her.

"There was a wren on the windowsill this morning," Vess said, unbothered, "first bird I've seen since the snows started. It sang at her until she woke up. Seemed fitting." She glanced down the table, and for half a heartbeat her gaze snagged on Jude's—just long enough. "Wren it is, unless anyone objects."

No one objected. Wren, hearing her new name pronounced, went very still, then very carefully repeated a sound that might have been an attempt at it. The table cheered. She ducked her head, pleased and embarrassed, and hid behind her porridge bowl.

Jude ate mechanically and thought: *I have to know.*

Not whether she spoke to beasts. He already knew that; he had known it in his bones the moment he'd heard her voice in the dark. What he needed to know was *what else* she carried. Because there were only two kinds of people in the world who could speak that language—one kind born to it, and one kind taught it by someone long dead—and if she was what he feared she was, hunters would come for her from directions he didn't yet have names for.

***

He found his chance that afternoon, in the stable.

The Compass kept eleven horses, four mules, and a permanent population of sparrows and mice that no cat ever seemed to catch, which Jude had always suspected was less mercy than treaty. He went in on the pretext of checking tack inventory, shut the big doors against the wind, and let the warmth and hay-smell settle around him.

Wren was there. Of course she was there. She had gravitated to the stables within hours of arriving, drawn the way water is drawn downhill, and Brannit had given up trying to keep her by the fire. She sat on an overturned feed barrel between the stalls, and the horses had turned their heads out over their doors to face her—all of them, even sour old Bittern, who bit everyone—and she was talking to them in that low running speech.

Jingle, Vess's bay mare, answered with a soft huffing rumble.

Jude cleared his throat.

Every head swung to him—horse and child alike. Wren's dark eyes fixed on him with an intensity that made him feel flayed, and he realized with a start that the animals hadn't startled. Only the girl had. The horses had simply turned, attentive, as if they had all agreed in advance that this moment would come.

"Hello," Jude said aloud, in Trade-tongue, and then—glancing at the door, at the walls, at the empty road beyond the frost-glazed windows—he did the thing he had not done in twenty-five years within hearing of another living soul.

He spoke back.

"*Good day, little sister,*" he said, in the language with no name that his grandmother had breathed into him in fragments, in cellars, in whispers, telling him *never use this where anyone can hear, never, not once, or they will take everything.* His accent was rusty. His grammar was probably the grammar of a dead woman's memories. But the sounds came up from somewhere below his ribs, and the horses sighed all at once like a congregation settling into pews.

Wren slid off the barrel.

She crossed the straw without hurry and stood in front of him, looking up, her head tilted. Then she raised one small hand and pointed at his mouth, and said something—three syllables, rising, a question.

He understood it. That was the thing that undid him. Twenty-five years of silence, and he understood her instantly: *You speak? You truly speak?*

"A little," he said, in her tongue, in his grandmother's tongue, and his voice broke on the second word like a boy's.

Wren grabbed his hand.

Not gently. She seized it with both of hers and dragged him toward the barrel, talking the whole way, a flood of it now, words tumbling over each other, and Jude caught perhaps one phrase in ten but the meaning came through anyway the way weather comes through a wall: *hurry, sit, listen, I have so much, I have had it so long inside me with no one to pour it into.*

He sat. She climbed onto the straw beside him, tucked herself under his arm as naturally as if she had done it a hundred times, pointed at Jingle, and began to tell him a story.

***

It was not her story. That became clear within minutes.

Her syntax was strange—not wrong, just layered, the way ice layers over a lake. Beneath the simple sentences a child uses ran something older, deeper, with rhythms that didn't belong to an eight-year-old mouth. And sometimes, mid-sentence, her voice changed. Dropped lower. Slowed. Took on a weight of years that made the hair rise on Jude's arms, because he recognized that weight. He had heard it in his grandmother, on the worst nights, when the memories she carried got loose of her and she spoke of things she had never seen.

*They carry it,* his grandmother had told him once, weeping into her apron, the only time she had ever explained why they lived in exile, why his father never wrote, why the family name was burned off the records in three provinces. *The speakers carry it. Every one of us carries a piece of what was broken. That is the price of the gift, little fox. We remember for the world, so the world can forget.*

He had thought she meant grief. Ordinary inherited grief, passed down like a coat too heavy to wear.

Sitting in the straw with Wren chattering against his side, Jude understood, with a slow horror blooming through him like ink in water, that she had meant it literally.

Because Wren was describing a battle.

Her small hand sketched shapes in the air: a plain, a river frozen mid-thaw, ranks of shapes on the far hillside that were not men. Her voice dropped into that other register, the deep one, and the words came slower and stranger, and the horses began to stamp and blow, uneasy. Jingle pulled her head back into her stall. Old Bittern screamed once, a high terrified note, and kicked his wall hard enough to crack a board.

Jude wrapped both arms around the girl and spoke into her hair, soothingly, in the old tongue: "*Peace, peace, little sister, they are only remembering—*"

But she wasn't listening anymore. Her eyes had gone distant and fixed, seeing something eight hundred years away, and her voice came out in a cadence that was not a child's cadence at all:

"*...and the Crown was bright, brighter than the sun on snow, and the bearer wept as she lifted it, because she knew what must be done, and the armies of both kinds went silent below the hill, waiting, all of them waiting to be owned—*"

"*Owned,*" Jude whispered.

"*—so she struck it on the stone. Once. Twice. And the pieces flew like sparks from a forge-fire, and each spark fell into a living heart that could hear, and the war ended, because you cannot wield what lives in ten thousand separate hearts, and cannot be gathered without their consent.*"

Wren shuddered, full-body, and came back to herself. She blinked up at Jude, and her eyes were wet, and she looked suddenly, terribly young again.

"*Did I speak wrongly?*" she asked in the old tongue, small.

"No," he managed. "No, little sister. You spoke true." His arms were shaking. He held her tighter. "Where did you hear that story?"

She frowned, considering how to say something enormous with a child's small stock of words. Then she pressed her fist against her own chest, over her heart.

"In here," she said. "Always in here. Since before."

Since before. Since before she had words for it. Since before, perhaps, she had a self to hold it in.

Jude stared out through the frost-laced window at the white blindness of the yard, and the ledger-keeper in him—the part of his mind that balanced accounts, that tracked debts and interest and the exact weight of risk—began, helplessly, to compute.

A child who carried the memory of the Crown's breaking. Not a story told to her. Not a song learned. The memory itself, whole and first-hand, poured into her the way the sparks had been poured into the speakers of old—which meant she was not merely descended from them. Which meant the Crown's fragments had not scattered evenly and thinned across generations the way his grandmother's had, fading into dreams and dread. Hers was intact. Vivid. *Recent*, in the way that mattered.

Which meant she was not a beast-speaker.

She was the Crown—or the closest thing left to it. A vessel. A living reliquary wearing the face of a half-starved child of eight, and somewhere out beyond the passes, in a capital city and in a court beneath a mountain, there were people who knew the old stories better than Jude did, and who had been searching for exactly this for longer than he had been alive.

The hunters his grandmother had warned of. The ones who took everything.

Wren tugged his sleeve. He looked down. She pointed at his chest now, mirroring her earlier gesture, and asked her question with devastating simplicity:

"*What do you carry?*"

"I don't know," Jude said honestly, in her language, in a whisper. "Something small. Something faded. My grandmother called us sparks, but mine has been banked a long time."

Wren studied him with grave, unhurried eyes, the way she had studied Marrow and Jingle, cataloguing truth. Then she leaned her head against his shoulder and said the sentence that finished unravelling whatever was left of his careful invisible life:

"*Not faded. Sleeping. I can hear it. It sounds like the sea far away. Like my sea.*"

Like her sea. As if the fragment in his blood and the memory in hers recognized each other. As if, across a thousand years and a shattered crown, two pieces of the same broken thing had found each other in a snowbound stable and were, quietly, calling out.

The horses had gone calm again, drowsing in the warmth. Outside, the wind had died at last, leaving that ringing post-blizzard silence that made the whole world seem to hold its breath. Somewhere in the house, a floorboard creaked. Footsteps—light, deliberate, unhurried—crossing the hall toward the stable doors.

Jude knew that tread. Vess.

And he knew, with the same terrible clarity that had come to him in the night, that he had maybe ten seconds to decide what the guild mistress would find when she opened that door: a bookkeeper and a stray child sitting in the straw, saying nothing—or two speakers of a dead language, caught red-mouthed, with no place left to hide.

The footsteps stopped outside.

The latch began to lift.
