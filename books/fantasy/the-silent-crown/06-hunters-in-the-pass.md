## 6. Hunters in the Pass

The wool merchants came up from the south on a morning so cold the birds sat motionless in the eaves like carved ornaments, and Jude knew them for liars before their cart cleared the first switchback.

He was in the stable yard at dawn, chipping ice from the water trough, when the magpie dropped onto the fence rail beside him and said, in her dry scrap-voice that only he could hear: *Wool-smell. But under it—oil, iron, and dog.*

"Dogs," Jude repeated aloud, to no one, and straightened slowly.

*Three,* the magpie said. *Big ones. They don't bark. They watch.* She ruffled her feathers, and beneath her flippancy he felt the tremor underneath, the animal equivalent of a man keeping his voice steady with effort. *The horses won't like them. The horses don't like anything that smells like that.*

Jude set down his ice-chipper. His breath hung in front of him, and for a moment he watched it as if he might read something in its shape—the way Wren sometimes stared into steam over the kitchen kettle and hummed those low, syllable-less notes that made the cats come running.

Three silent dogs. A cart of wool in high winter, when no one sheared and no one spun and every honest trader had been snowed into some warm room weeks ago.

"Thank you," he told the magpie quietly.

*Bring me bacon,* she said, and flew off toward the kitchen window where Vess's cook pretended not to feed her.

Jude stood in the yard a while longer, hands going numb in his gloves, and let the fear arrive properly instead of pretending it wouldn't. They had known this day would come. From the night Vess had carried Wren in from the blizzard, wrapped in two blankets and half-dead, they had all of them known that a child who spoke the old tongue would eventually draw the attention of people whose profession was finding such children. Jude had simply hoped—foolishly, lazily—for more time.

He went inside through the stable door rather than the main hall, because the main hall was where Wren took her breakfast now, cross-legged on the bench between the farrier Tam and the cook's apprentice, stealing bread crusts with the shameless precision of a girl who had spent too many years hungry before the Compass found her. She looked up when he came in—she always looked up, always found him in a room the way a compass needle finds north—and her eyes asked the question her mouth didn't bother forming anymore. She'd stopped trying human words around him weeks ago. With him, she only used the other language, the old one, and it flowed out of her like water finding a channel.

*What is it?*

"Wool merchants," Jude answered in kind, and watched the word *merchants* land strangely even in the beast-speech, because there were no merchants among the speaking beasts, only givers and takers and the long negotiation between them. "Coming up the pass."

Her face did something careful and old. Eight years old, and she arranged her expression the way soldiers do. That was the part that broke his heart most reliably—not the memories she carried, not the grief of an ancient war folded up inside her small body, but the competence of her fear. She had been hunted before. Whatever had sent her down the mountain alone, half-frozen, clutching a broken crown of silver—it had taught her what men in carts with dogs meant.

*How close?* she signed, hands quick below the table's edge, where the others couldn't see.

"Half a day. Maybe less." He crouched by her bench, ostensibly adjusting his bootlace. "You remember the story?"

She nodded. Vess had drilled it into her until the household groaned: *You are Wren, a deaf-mute orphan out of the valley villages, taken in out of charity. You hear nothing, you say nothing. If anyone signs at you, you frown and look stupid. Stupid is safe.* Wren had practiced looking stupid with such dedication that Tam laughed until he cried, and then quietly wept after, which was Tam's way lately.

"It'll hold," Jude murmured. "Just keep your ears closed and your eyes empty."

*And the dogs?* Her fingers were very still.

That was the question. That was always going to be the question. Jude had spent the previous three nights doing everything he could about it—walking the yard at midnight, standing in the cold, whispering to the dark corners where the mice lived—and now he would learn whether it had been enough.

"I'm handling the dogs," he said.

Her hand found his sleeve, brief as a bird landing. Then she turned back to her bread and became, with unsettling perfection, a vacant child staring at nothing.

* * *

The cart came through the gates an hour past noon, and it was a good lie. Jude gave it that much respect. The wool was real wool—he could smell lanolin from twenty feet—and the merchants were dressed right, greasy and road-worn, complaining about the passes and the price of fodder in the flat accents of the southern valleys. There were four of them. Big men, but big men drove freight; nothing odd there.

It was the dogs that were wrong, and they were wrong in ways only Jude could see.

They were grey brutes, heavy through the chest, lying in the cart bed among the wool bales as if they'd traveled that way all their lives. And they lay *wrong*—not sprawled and sleeping the way cart dogs sleep, but coiled, heads up, eyes moving. Tracking. When the cart rolled into the yard, six pairs of nostrils flared and all six heads turned—not toward the kitchens, where meat smells lived, not toward the stables, where horse smells lived, but toward the hall. Toward the warm interior where a mute child sat being stupid by a fire.

Jude stepped into their line of sight and opened himself.

This was the part of his gift he hated. Anyone could talk to a friendly dog; half the hedge-witches in the provinces claimed as much. What Jude could do was go further in—past the barking layer of the mind, down into the older rooms, the scent-and-hunger places where dogs kept their truest selves. He could speak there. He could, with effort, be *listened to*.

*Easy,* he breathed into that shared dark. *Nothing here. Nothing new. Wool and horses and cold.*

Six canine minds swung toward him like compass needles. He felt their attention as physical weight, a pressure behind his eyes. They were strange dogs, bred narrow and deep—someone had pared away everything except the hunt until almost nothing remained but nose and want. Talking to them was like talking to knives.

*Nothing here,* he said again, slower, pushing the thought into their blood the way you'd press a seal into wax. *Old smells. Old snow. Sleep.*

One of them—a bitch with a scarred muzzle, the pack's leader—rose to her feet in the cart and looked directly at him. Not at his face. At his *center*, the place where his gift lived. And for one endless heartbeat Jude understood exactly how a rabbit feels under a hawk's shadow.

Then the handler in the cart cuffed her lightly with a gloved hand—"Down, Sorrow"—and she lay back, and the moment passed, and Jude discovered he'd been holding his breath long enough to see spots.

He walked away at a normal pace. It took everything he had.

* * *

Vess met him in the ledger room, and she didn't waste words.

"You've seen them."

"Royal wardens. Scent-hounds, six, bred down to nothing but the hunt. They're posing as wool factors out of Harrow Vale." He pulled off his gloves finger by finger, because his hands wanted to shake and he refused to let them. "Their papers will be flawless. Their wool is real. Only the dogs are wrong, and only I can hear it."

"How long can you hold them?"

"The dogs?" Jude sat down across from her. "I've already started. Three nights ago I had the mice run the drains and carry old scents everywhere—kitchen fat, horse, smoke, my own trail laid thick through every corner of this yard. To a nose like theirs, the whole guild should read as one undifferentiated smear of ordinary life. No bright thread of *child* anywhere." He rubbed his eyes. "But scent-masking buys hours, not days. And the leader looked at me today, Vess. Looked at me like she could see the shape of what I am. If I push them much harder, they'll start wondering why the stableman's voice lives in their heads."

Vess stood at the window, arms crossed, watching the merchants unload. Firelight caught the grey in her hair and made her look briefly ancient—the founder's age, whatever that was.

"When I built this place," she said, without turning, "I made a rule. No lies inside these walls. Lies are for outside; inside we tell each other the truth, even ugly truth, or the whole structure rots from within." She glanced back at him. "So. Ugly truth: those men will not leave empty-handed. Wardens never do. They'll search, they'll find nothing, and they'll sit out the weather in our hall eating our food and watching us, and one of us will make a mistake."

"Then we don't make mistakes."

"We're human. We'll make mistakes." She pushed off from the wall. "Which is why the mistake won't be Wren's. Get back to your dogs, ledger-keeper. And Isandro—" she almost never used his full name, and it landed like a hand on his shoulder, "—don't drown yourself doing it. A dead speaker protects no one."

* * *

The next two days were the hardest work of Jude's life, and none of it showed.

By daylight he was the Gilded Compass's quiet accountant, ink-stained, forgettable, fetching ledgers and totting columns while the wardens' leader—a soft-spoken man called Master Halden who smiled with his mouth and never with his eyes—asked polite questions about trade routes and mountain weather. By night Jude walked the frozen yard and fought a war in the language of scent and instinct.

The mice ran his errands. He'd never used so many at once; they poured through the walls like a gray tide, dragging rags soaked in the scents he needed, laying false trails, refreshing old ones. The barn cats patrolled the perimeter and reported each warden's habits with feline contempt—*the tall one sleeps facing the door; the young one cries in his sleep for someone named Marta; Halden writes letters by candlelight and burns them*. The magpie perched on the wardens' cart and memorized the wax seals on their hidden correspondence, describing the stamp to Jude in meticulous detail: a crown, and beneath the crown, a sword thrust downward.

A writ of surrender-and-search. He didn't need to read it to know.

The worst hours were the ones spent inside the hounds' heads. Every night Jude slipped into the kennel shed where the six were chained, and every night he pressed the same great lie deeper into them: *nothing here, nothing new, old smells, old snow.* And every night the scarred bitch, Sorrow, looked back at him out of her pared-down mind with that terrible attention, and he could feel her beginning to wonder. Dogs aren't clever, but they are honest, and honesty has its own kind of penetration. Something in her knew that a voice was speaking to her that shouldn't exist. She just lacked the architecture to name it.

On the third night she answered him.

He was halfway into the masking-lie, sweat freezing at his temples, when her mind rose against his like a fish rising to take a fly—not attacking, not fleeing, but offering. An image. A memory, hers: a white room, and a child strapped to a table, and men in crown-grey coats taking notes while the child screamed soundlessly, and the hounds themselves whining against the far wall because even *they* could hear what the child's voice did to the air.

Jude recoiled so hard he cracked his head on the kennel beam.

Sorrow watched him from the straw, tail still, and finished the thought in her blunt, wordless grammar: *We have smelled them before. We have been made to find them before. What are you, that you smell like both the finder and the found?*

He crouched there in the dark with his skull ringing, and understood that his mask was failing—not because he'd done it wrong, but because the thing he truly was had bled through every layer of the lie. You couldn't hide kindness from a creature bred to hunt kindness down.

"I'm sorry," he whispered, in plain human speech, useless as it was. "I'm so sorry. For what they made you do. For what I'm making you do now."

Sorrow lowered her head onto her paws. And in the oldest room of her mind, in the place beneath training and hunger, she made her choice: she closed her eyes, and turned her nose away, and let the lie stand.

*Nothing here,* she agreed dully. *Nothing new.*

Jude walked back to the hall shaking. He had won. He had also just become, in the sight of one broken animal, another person who used her—and he could not find the difference between those two facts, try as he might.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Master Halden found him in the ledger room.

Jude heard the footsteps and had time to arrange himself: shoulders slackened, pen in hand, a column of figures half-finished. The door opened on exactly the man Halden expected—grey, mild, unremarkable, a bookkeeper shaped like furniture.

"Master Zhou." Halden settled into the chair across the desk uninvited. Up close he smelled of clean wool and, beneath it, faintly of the kennels. "I'll be direct. My factors and I have enjoyed your hospitality, but wool is a thin living this year, and we're considering a route east instead. Before we go—routine matter—I'll need to look through your storerooms and cellars. Guild charter allows visiting traders a courtesy inspection of goods held in bond. You keep such things here, I understand. Impossible goods."

It was beautifully done. The request was technically lawful, casually framed, deniable twice over. And behind Halden's mild eyes Jude saw the patience of a man who had hunted his quarry across three winters and could wait out one more day.

"Of course," Jude said, and heard his own voice stay level, and hated how good at this he had become. "I'll fetch Mistress Vess. Bonded goods require her key as well as mine."

"Naturally." Halden smiled his mouth-smile. Then, rising, he paused, and added lightly, as though it were an afterthought:

"Your stable cat is remarkable, by the way. Sat on our cart wheel all yesterday evening, watching the dogs. Most cats run from hounds." His gaze rested on Jude a moment too long. "Almost as if it were standing guard."

"They're strange animals here," Jude said. "Mountain breeds. The cold makes them bold."

"Mm," said Master Halden. "The cold makes everyone bold."

He left. Jude sat very still, listening to the boots recede down the corridor, and then he looked down and saw that his pen had torn through the ledger page, a long ink gouge running through columns he'd have to enter again.

Outside the window, snow was starting again—fine and dry, the kind that fell when the passes were about to shut for the season. The horses stood hipshot in the yard, ears flat, all of them facing south, all of them watching the road.

And from the rafters of the stable, where she'd been listening through the gap in the boards, the magpie dropped to the sill and spoke with none of her usual flippancy.

*More on the road,* she said. *South pass. Not wool this time. Coming fast.*

Jude closed his eyes. "How many?"

*Many. And no dogs at all.* A pause; her feathers lifted. *They walk like wolves, but they stand like men.*
