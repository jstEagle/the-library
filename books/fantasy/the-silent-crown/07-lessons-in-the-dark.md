## 7. Lessons in the Dark

The cats taught Wren first.

Not on purpose. Cats never did anything on purpose, which was why she liked them better than people. People said one thing with their mouths and another thing with their shoulders and a third thing entirely with the smell of their blood, but cats only ever meant what they meant, even when what they meant was *leave me alone or I will bite your ankle*, which at least was honest.

The gray cat was called Soot by the humans, but in her own tongue she called herself *the-one-who-waits-at-windows*. Wren thought that was a much better name.

"Say it again," Jude whispered.

He sat cross-legged on the kitchen floor beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him like off a banked stove. He had a way of sitting small, folded up, as if he were trying to take less room than a man his size needed. Wren had decided this was because he had spent a long time somewhere he wasn't wanted. She knew what that felt like from the inside of other people's memories, so she knew it when she saw it.

"*Vessha-nen,*" Wren said, pointing at Soot.

Jude's mouth moved around the sounds carefully, the way he balanced numbers in his ledger—slowly, precisely, afraid of dropping something. "*Vess... ha-nen.*"

"*Nen*," Wren corrected gently. "Soft. Like water going over stones."

"*Vesshanen.*"

Soot opened one yellow eye from her basket of mending and looked at Jude for a long moment. Then she yawned, enormously, showing all her teeth, which in cat-speech was not rudeness but a kind of compliment: *I am comfortable enough to be defenseless near you.*

"She says you're almost a person now," Wren translated, and Jude laughed—a short, startled sound, as if laughter were another language he'd been exiled from and was only just finding his way back to.

It had been six days since the blizzard brought her through the guild doors wrapped in ice and silence. Six days of soup and borrowed blankets and Vess's firm hand between her shoulder blades steering her away from questions. Six days of the animals telling her things, and of watching Jude watch her when he thought no one could see his face.

But he couldn't hide it from her. Nobody could. Faces were just another kind of speech, and Wren had been fluent in every kind since before she could remember—which was the problem, because the remembering wasn't hers.

Tonight the kitchen was theirs. The cooks had gone to bed; the fire had burned down to orange coals that ticked and settled. Snow pressed against the windows like something listening. And on the wide scrubbed table between them lay the game they had invented three nights ago, the one Wren thought of as *the speaking game* and Jude called, in his careful human way, *lessons*.

They played it with buttons.

Wren had found the jar in the mending room—a hundred buttons of horn and bone and brass, each one different—and she had begun laying them out in patterns while she spoke, because the old language didn't live well in single words. It lived in shapes. You couldn't say *the mountain is angry*; you had to arrange the mountain inside your sentence until the anger was visible, the way frost arranged itself on glass without anyone telling it how.

"This one is you," she said, placing a large brass button on the table. It had a lion stamped on it, worn nearly smooth. "This one is me." A small bone button, round and pale as a moon. "And this—" a chipped black one, "is the word for *secret*. Watch."

She slid the black button under the brass one, hiding it, then drew both hands across the table in a slow wave.

"*Kel-varo-din.*"

Jude frowned. "Secret-under-skin?"

"No." Wren laughed. "That's the funny part. It doesn't have an order like yours does. It's all happening at once. *Kel* is secret, but *varo* means it's warm there, like a bird under a wing, and *din* means the person keeping it would die before giving it up. So it isn't just 'I keep a secret.' It's 'my secret lives warm under my ribs and I will die before you reach it.'"

The kitchen went very quiet. The coals ticked.

"That's a lot to fit in one word," Jude said finally, and his voice came out strange, sanded down at the edges.

"You've been saying it all week," Wren told him. "You just say it in human."

The animals came and went through the lesson like weather. The big carthorse, Bramble, put his head through the stable-side window that someone long ago had left unshuttered, and Wren fed him a crust and made him a sentence: *Bramble-who-is-patient, the snow will end, the grass remembers being green.* He blew warm air over her hair, which was horse for *thank you, little speaker,* and Jude watched the whole exchange with an expression Wren recognized from her borrowed memories—the expression of a man seeing water in a desert and not quite believing it wasn't a mirage.

"Ask him something," she said. "Anything. In our words."

"Our words." He tried the phrase out like a coin bitten for gold. Then he arranged three buttons slowly, tongue between his teeth. "*Bramble. Sky-white-falling.*" A pause, fumbling. "*How-long?*"

Bramble's ears swiveled. He snorted, and Wren giggled so hard she had to lean against Jude's arm.

"He says the sky has been stupid for eleven days."

"Eleven days," Jude repeated gravely, as though committing it to a ledger no one else would ever see. "I'll note that the horse considers winter a personal insult."

"He says it's not personal. The sky is just bad at its job."

And there it was again—that startled laugh, quickly caught and put away like something he'd learned to be ashamed of carrying. Wren resolved, with the particular stubbornness of eight-year-olds everywhere, to make him stop putting it away. It might take all winter. She had time. The passes were closed; the world outside had been shut away behind white; the guild was a lamp floating in an ocean of snow, and inside it, for now, nothing could reach them.

She knew better than most how long *for now* lasted. But she was eight, and eight is mostly made of *anyway*.

"Tell me a story," she said, when the buttons had been swept into a pile and rebuilt twice more. "A true one. From before."

Jude went still in the particular way he did when a door inside him closed halfway. "What kind of before?"

"The kind you don't tell anyone." She looked up at him. "I can hear it. Under your words. There's an old sound in you, like a bell nobody rings. I want to know why nobody rings it."

For a long moment he didn't answer. The wind worried at the eaves. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked under a sleeping trader's turning body.

"I was seven," Jude said at last, "when my mother spoke to a dying ox in front of the wrong person. One sentence. Four words, maybe five. *You can let go now.*" His fingers moved among the buttons without touching them, sorting nothing into piles. "By spring we were gone. Exiled. My father lost his trade, my mother never spoke to an animal again—not aloud, not where anyone could hear. And me—" He stopped. Started again. "I learned to listen with my back turned. To look bored when the dogs talked. To pretend I heard nothing, all day, every day, for twenty years." He glanced down at her. "That's my true story from before. It isn't much of one."

"It's the same story," Wren said quietly.

"What?"

"The one I carry. Everyone who speaks, hiding. Everyone who hears, pretending deafness. It goes back and back." She touched the table, and the touch became memory, because some nights—like tonight, like now—the memories rose in her like water rising in a well, asking to be poured.

"Let me show you," she said. "The end of it. The true ending. But it isn't a gentle story."

"I'm tired of gentle stories," Jude said. "Gentle stories are the ones where everyone pretends."

So Wren took his hand, and let the well overflow.

 *

He did not go anywhere. That was the strange part, the part she'd stopped trying to explain to herself. He stayed sitting on the kitchen floor beside her, but his eyes went somewhere else, and she went with him, and together they stood inside a memory older than any kingdom.

They stood on a hill of white grass under a sun that seemed ashamed of itself, and below them the plain was full of armies—not two armies, but dozens, banners of feather and banner of cloth side by side, beast-speakers and beasts and humans all tangled into one vast broken knot of war. The air smelled of iron and burnt feathers. The ground shook with a slow, grinding rhythm that Wren knew was not drums. It was grief, walking.

At the center of the plain, on a hill of fused glass where lightning had struck and struck again, stood a figure holding the Crown.

Even in memory, looking at it hurt. It was not gold. It was not any metal at all—it was made of *voices*, woven tight, ten thousand voices braided into a circlet that sang low and continuous, a chord that held the world's agreement in it. Whoever wore it could speak and be understood by everything: stone, storm, sparrow, king. Whoever wore it commanded the conversation of the world.

"They fought over it," Wren murmured, narrating for him, because the memory was hers to give shape. "Both sides. For a hundred years. Every time one court held it, the other suffered, and the speakers were always the ones crushed between—made into weapons or made into spies, never made into people."

The figure on the glass hill raised the Crown above their head.

"Who is that?" Jude asked, and his voice in the memory sounded young and hollow.

"We don't know the name anymore. The memory lost it on purpose, I think. They were a speaker. Both courts claimed them. Maybe they were both." Wren's throat tightened; the borrowed grief pressed up against her own small chest like a bird too big for its cage. "Watch. This is the part everyone forgot."

The figure spoke then—one word in the old language, a word shaped like a hammer and a cradle at once—and threw the Crown down onto the glass hill.

It did not shatter the way cups shatter. It shattered the way light shatters through rain. The chord broke apart into its thousand thousand separate voices, and the voices flew outward like sparks from a bonfire, streaming over the battlefield, over the hills, over the whole breathing world—and wherever each spark fell, it fell *into someone*. Into a woman kneeling over her dead brother. Into a soldier, human-born, who had loved a hawk like a sister. Into children not yet born, and their children after them.

Into blood. Into every bloodline that had ever carried the gift of speech, scattered so finely that no army could gather it, no crown could wear it, no hand could hold what now lived in ten million beating hearts.

"No one could wield it," Wren said, and her voice shook, "so they gave it to everyone. That was the ending. Not a victory. A *giving-away*."

The memory dimmed. The hill of glass, the armies frozen mid-grief, the sparks still falling like slow snow—all of it faded the way embers fade, gratefully, into dark. And then they were back in the kitchen, two figures by a dying fire, and the coals had burned lower, and neither of them had moved.

Jude was crying. He didn't seem to know it. Tears stood on his face the way dew stands on a fence rail at dawn, unnoticed by the fence.

"All those years," he said finally, hoarsely. "My family exiled for one sentence. All the speakers hunted and hidden and afraid. And it was never a curse. It was never even a *thing* to steal. We're—" His breath caught. "We're pieces of it. All of us. We've been walking around all this time as fragments of the Crown, and nobody told us. Nobody *knew*."

"The memory knew," Wren said. "That's why it picked me. Somebody had to remember the ending, so that someday somebody could finish it properly." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. She was tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix; carrying a continent's grief in a child-sized body meant some nights the grief won. "But I don't know how yet. I only know pieces."

Jude reached out—slowly, telegraphing it the way you'd move toward a wild bird—and set his hand on her head, smoothing her hair back from her forehead. His palm was warm and calloused and it trembled, just slightly.

"You're not doing it alone," he said. "Whatever comes. Whatever it costs. You hear me, little wren? I spent twenty years learning how to hide. Hiding, I can teach. But I'm done practicing it."

Wren leaned into his hand the way Soot leaned into the sunlit windowsill, and felt something settle in her chest that had been loose and rattling since the night the soldiers came to her village—some small door, long barricaded, swinging quietly open.

Outside, the snow kept falling. Inside, the fire ticked down to its last orange arithmetic. And in the walls, in the rafters, in the stalls and the hayloft, the guild's animals had all gone quiet at once, ears pricked toward the kitchen, having heard—for the first time in living memory—a grown man speak a fragment of the old tongue aloud and mean it.

Only the rats, deep in the foundation stones, kept whispering.

And what they whispered, in the dark beneath the guild, was about a door that hummed when Wren walked over it—and how the oldest voice in the building had started, very softly, to sing along.
