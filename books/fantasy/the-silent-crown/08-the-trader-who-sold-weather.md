## 8. The Trader Who Sold Weather

The storm had been bottled in the year of the Red Comet, and it was still angry about it.

Jude could hear it from the ledger room—a low grumbling through the cellar door, like a dog dreaming of thunder. The Gilded Compass kept three bottled storms in its deep inventory, and this one, a squall off the Saltreach coast, was the oldest and the most temperamental. He made a note to have someone carry it nearer the fire. Even imprisoned weather sulked better when warm.

The guild's ledgers were his cathedral. Three hundred years of trades that should not have been possible: a debt repaid in dreams, forty nights of them, transferred from a dying merchant to an insomniac duke; a river's name sold back to the river; one honest man's shadow, pawned twice and never redeemed. Jude entered each transaction in his small, precise hand, and no line of ink ever trembled. That was the trick of his life. Ink did not tremble. Voices did, sometimes, when they spoke to animals where anyone might hear—but ink never did.

He was halfway through reconciling the winter accounts when the cats told him a carriage was coming.

*Grey horses, four of them,* said Marrow, the eldest of the Compass cats, from her throne on the windowsill above the counting desk. She spoke in the way cats speak—images folded into feeling, more than words. *Heavy. Iron shoes. They are slow because the driver is fat and afraid.*

"Fear of what?" Jude asked aloud, softly, not looking up from his figures. Speaking with beasts was safest when it looked like talking to oneself. Half the guild already thought he murmured over his numbers like a monk over scripture.

*Not of robbers,* Marrow said. *Of weather. There is a man inside the carriage who smells like storms.*

Jude set down his pen.

There was only one client in thirty years of trade who smelled like storms, and he came twice a decade at most, always unannounced, always in winter, always paying in coin so old that half the kingdoms it had been minted in no longer existed.

"Corvane," Jude said.

*The same,* said Marrow, and yawned, which among cats was a benediction or a warning, depending on the day.

* * *

Corvane arrived as Corvane always arrived: wrapped in a coat of layered grey feathers that might once, generations ago, have belonged to something larger than any bird now living, his face seamed like old leather left out in every season. He was seventy if he was a day, and traders swore he had been seventy since before their fathers were born. He dealt in weather—the buying of calm seas, the selling of rain to drought-struck valleys—and he had traded with the Gilded Compass longer than Vess had held it, longer perhaps than the guild had borne its gilded sign.

What most clients never knew was that Corvane's cargo was not conjured. It was bargained for. Every fair wind he sold had been asked for politely, in a language older than any kingdom, from creatures who owned the sky. Corvane was a beast-speaker too—one of the very few Jude had ever met who wore it openly, because who would believe a mad old feather-coat's ramblings?

That was what had drawn Jude to trust him, years ago, in the careless way he trusted almost no one. A fellow keeper of secrets. A man whose livelihood depended on discretion.

"Zhou!" Corvane boomed, filling the trading hall doorway with feathers and frost-melt. "Still balancing other men's fortunes? Give us a chair by the fire and whatever passes for wine in this place."

"Master Corvane." Jude rose, bowed the correct degree, and felt the familiar tightening behind his ribs—the one that meant trouble wearing a friendly face. "The Compass is honored. What does the sky owe you this season?"

"The sky owes me nothing. That's rather the point of my profession." Corvane lowered himself into the offered chair with the groan of a ship taking on ballast, and then his eyes—the pale, quick eyes of a gull—swept the room with a thoroughness his rumpled manner belied. "I'm here on a private matter. Something I heard on the road south. Strange stories travel faster than storms these days."

"I'll fetch the wine," Jude said, because his hands needed somewhere to be.

Behind him, in the rafters, Marrow shifted her weight. *Careful,* she said. *The old bird is listening with more than ears.*

Wren was upstairs. Of course Wren was upstairs. She had been at the Compass for eleven days now, and in those eleven days the household had rearranged itself around her the way water rearranges itself around a stone. Vess had named her. The cook fed her honeyed porridge without being asked. The stable boys taught her dice games she won by cheating—cheating with the help of the stable cats, who found her delightful and told her where everyone hid their coins.

And the amulet hung at her throat still: a broken circlet of dark metal, crown-shaped, older than the guild, older than every kingdom whose coins filled Jude's ledgers. Older than language, Wren's borrowed memories whispered. Older than names.

Jude poured the wine himself and carried it back slowly, arranging his face into pleasant blankness.

* * *

For an hour, it almost worked.

Corvane talked weather—the long winter, the closed passes, the price of a mild spring in the grain provinces. He drank two cups and praised neither, which was his way of saying the wine was terrible while accepting another. He asked after Vess, after trade volumes, after the health of the guild's horses, and Jude answered everything with the smoothness of a man reciting entries he had memorized.

It was Corvane himself who broke the pattern. He set down his third cup, leaned forward until the feathered coat rustled like wind through reeds, and said:

"You've a child in the house now. I saw her at the window. Small thing. Dark-haired." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "She was talking to your cat."

"She talks to herself," Jude said. "Children do. She's mute otherwise—we think grief struck it dumb. Found her in the blizzard."

"Mute." Corvane turned the word over like a coin of doubtful weight. "Yet I watched her lips move, and I watched Marrow—that's the grey one, yes?—sit up and answer. I've spent fifty years listening to things most men say don't speak, Zhou. I know a conversation when I see one."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, floorboards creaked under a servant's step, ordinary as breathing.

"She's a child," Jude said quietly. "Whatever you think you saw—"

"What I saw," Corvane said, "was the amulet."

And there it was. Jude had known it was coming from the moment Marrow said *smells like storms*, had felt it waiting in the wings of the conversation like a debt coming due, but knowing did nothing to blunt it. His hands stayed still on the table. His heartbeat, he willed into stillness too.

"Amulet?"

"Don't insult us both." Corvane reached into his coat—not fast, deliberately slow, a trader showing his hands empty—and drew out a scrap of charcoal sketching paper, soft with handling. On it, drawn from memory in a few sure strokes, was a broken circlet. Crown-shaped. Seven points, six snapped away, one remaining whole.

Jude looked at it and let three full seconds pass before he frowned, mildly, the frown of a bookkeeper confronted with unfamiliar goods.

"Where did you copy that from?"

"A client. Thirty years ago. A dying woman in the Fenmark marshes who paid me her last fair wind to carry a message north, and gave me that sketch as proof of what the message concerned. She said—" Corvane's voice dropped, and for the first time the performance fell away entirely, leaving something old and frightened underneath. "She said the crown was broken so that no one could wear it, and that whoever gathered the pieces again would end either the world or the war, and no one living could say which. She said to watch for it in the hands of children, because the pieces go where they're needed, not where they're wanted."

The fire popped. Jude became aware of how loud it was.

"That's a story," he said. "Marshes breed them."

"They do. And I've bought and sold enough true impossibilities to know a real one when it's sketched in charcoal." Corvane's pale eyes fixed on him, and there was no gull in them now, only the hunter that lived inside every good trader. "The girl wears it at her throat. I saw the shape of it under her scarf when she leaned toward the window. Six points broken. One whole."

"Coincidence. Broken jewelry comes in every shape."

"Then sell me the coincidence." And here Corvane leaned back, spread his hands, and became all at once the genial merchant again—too smoothly, too quickly, a coat pulled on over the fear. "Name a figure, Zhou. You keep the ledgers; you know what the Compass clears in a decade. I will pay that in a single afternoon. Not for the child—gods, no, I'm not a monster, whatever else I am. For the little key. The trinket. Let me carry it south and lose it in the sea where such things belong, and you'll never balance another account in your life unless it pleases you. You could buy silence, Zhou. Real silence. A house of your own, far from guild politics and guild winters and guild wars. Doesn't every man who hides want exactly that—a hole deep enough to disappear into forever?"

And that was the poison in it, Jude thought. That was why the offer was shaped the way it was. Not gold for greed—gold for fear. Corvane had looked at him across the counting table and seen, the way speakers see each other, the shape of a man built entirely out of concealment, and offered him the one thing such a man dreamed of: permission to vanish completely.

For one treacherous moment Jude saw it. A stone house in some green southern valley. No ledgers but his own. No hunters, no courts, no child with a continent's grief sleeping in her skull. Just quiet, purchased at last, permanent—

Wren's laugh drifted down through the floorboards. High and rusty with disuse, the laugh of a voice learning its work again. The stable cat yowled in comic protest, and she laughed harder.

"No," Jude said.

Corvane blinked. "You haven't even heard the figure."

"There's no figure. The amulet isn't mine to sell. The child isn't mine to sell. Nothing under this roof is mine to sell, Master Corvane, and if you've finished your wine, the roads close early this time of year."

Something moved behind the old man's face—hurt, maybe, or its disguise. He rose slowly, joints cracking like river ice, and resettled his feathered coat.

"I offered fairly," he said. "Remember that. Whatever happens next, remember I came to you first, and offered fairly, and you chose to stand in front of a locked door instead of opening it while the key was still warm."

"The key belongs to the lock it was made for," Jude said, "and that lock isn't yours."

Corvane paused at the door. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet and strange, almost gentle.

"Your family was exiled for the gift, didn't you tell me once? Years ago, drunk on festival wine. You never said why, exactly." He did not turn around. "I wonder, Zhou—if you'd known then what you know now, whether you'd have drunk at all."

Then the cold swallowed him, and the iron-shod carriage groaned away south, and Jude stood alone in the trading hall with the fire burning down and Marrow watching him from the sill with wide, unreadable eyes.

*You did well,* she said at last.

"Did I?" Jude's voice sounded far away to himself. "He'll talk, Marrow. Men like that always talk—it's the whole architecture of them. Everything is a trade, including silence. Especially silence."

*Yes,* the cat said simply. *He was already selling before he ever knocked.*

Jude went very still. "What do you mean?"

*The sparrows on the south roof talk to the sparrows at the crossroads inn. The inn sparrows talk to the crows, and the crows talk to anything.* Marrow rose, stretched, arched her back in a curve like a drawn bow. *A crow flew past two days ago, before your weather-man ever arrived, carrying words it had stolen from a letter. Words about a child found in the snow. Words about a broken crown. The crow was flying east when I heard it. East, toward the human court. And there was another crow—*

Marrow stopped. Cats disliked delivering bad news twice.

"Another crow," Jude said flatly. "Going west."

*Toward the mountains. Where the speaking beasts winter.* Marrow jumped down from the sill and wound once around his ankles, an old comfort, an apology. *Two letters, Zhou. Two courts. Someone wrote them both before the old bird ever sat down at your fire. He wasn't here to buy the amulet.*

Jude stared into the dying fire, and the arithmetic assembled itself in his head with the terrible clarity of a balanced column: Corvane had come to confirm the sale, not to make one. To look at the child with his own eyes, to verify the goods, to give the courts a firsthand witness—*yes, it's real, yes, she's here, yes, the Compass shelters her*. The offer of fortune had been theater, a courtesy performed for the benefit of the one speaker in the room who might have stopped him, so that afterward Corvane could say he'd tried, he'd offered fairly, the bookkeeper chose to stand in front of the locked door.

Every road out of the mountains would carry word within the week. Both courts. Both armies' worth of hunger.

"He sold her," Jude said quietly. "Before he even walked through the door, he'd already sold her."

*Then stop standing in the hall,* said Marrow, *and go tell the woman who keeps this roof.*

Upstairs, faintly, Wren was singing—to the cats, to the mice in the walls, to no one—to something in a language that predated every kingdom whose borders were already, silently, beginning to move.
