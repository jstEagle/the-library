## 9. Snowbound

The pass closed on the ninth day of the blizzard, though no one at the Gilded Compass needed a warden's proclamation to know it. The mountain told them. It groaned in its sleep like an old man turning over, and the snow came down so thick and steady that the world beyond the courtyard wall simply stopped existing—no road, no pines, no sky. Only white, pressing against the windows like something patient that had all winter to wait.

Vess stood at the great south window of the common hall with a cup of black tea going cold in her hands and counted her household the way she counted stock: by what could be lost.

Fourteen souls under one roof, now. Herself; the cook-brothers Anselm and Piet, who had come to her fifteen years ago as deserters from two different armies and had never once asked about each other's wars. Old Tam at the forge, whose hammer had gone quiet these last days in a way she didn't like. Marta and her twin boys who slept in the hayloft and were worth more than any cargo the Compass had ever moved. The stable-hands, the laundress Ilsa with her ruined lungs, the quiet archivist Fenn who catalogued impossible things and spoke perhaps forty words a week. Jude Zhou at his ledgers. And Wren—the child who was not on any ledger, because there was no column for what she was.

The passes would not open before thaw. Four months, sealed in stone and snow, with word already sold—she was nearly certain of it—about what sheltered under this roof.

She drank the cold tea anyway. Outside, the wind found the gap beneath the eaves and played it like a funeral flute.

"Shutters are pegged," Tam said from the doorway. He wiped his big hands on his apron, though they were clean; he'd been doing that lately, wiping clean hands. "Cellar door barred. If anyone means to dig through six feet of drift to reach us, they'll earn it."

"Thank you, Tam."

He lingered. She watched him decide not to say whatever sat behind his teeth, watched him bow instead and shuffle back toward the warmth of his forge. Vess set down the cup.

*Someone in my house has already betrayed us,* she thought, with the flat certainty of a woman who had survived three decades of trade by knowing when a price was being paid somewhere out of her sight. *I just don't know which face it wears yet.*

* * *

Anselm burned the first loaf of the siege, which had never once happened in fifteen years, and Piet laughed at him for a full minute before either brother understood why.

"It's the flour," Anselm insisted, staring at the blackened brick as if it might confess. "It's damp. The whole storehouse is damp—"

"The storehouse is drier than a tax collector's heart and you know it." Piet pulled the second loaf from the oven, golden and perfect, and held it up between them like evidence. "Your head's elsewhere, brother. Everyone's head is elsewhere."

That was true enough. The guild had run on rhythm for years—carts in, carts out, ledgers balanced, quarrels small and quickly mended—and the rhythm had broken. The traders who should have wintered elsewhere were sealed in with them: Master Orlin the salt-merchant, who paced the corridors at night; the twins Dara and Denna, jewel-brokers who watched Wren across the supper table with expressions they thought were neutral; Corvane's factor, a pale man named Sef, whose employer had left three weeks ago and whose letters home had stopped when the roads did.

Sef smiled too easily these days. Anselm noted it the way he noted everything, silently, and said nothing, because saying things was Piet's job.

At supper that night, Marta's boys staged a rebellion against eating their turnips, and Wren—solemn, quick-eyed Wren, who had been at the Compass barely a month—leaned toward the turnip bowl and made a sound. A low chirrup, rising at the end like a question. And the guild cat, a scarred gray tom named Bastion who feared no dog alive, leapt onto the bench beside the boys and stared at them with such imperial disapproval that both children ate their turnips in chastened silence.

The hall laughed. It was the first real laughter in days, and it went around the tables like something passed hand to hand, warming as it went.

But Jude, at the far end, wasn't laughing. He was watching Wren, and his face wore an expression Anselm had seen before on men counting coins that didn't add up.

* * *

Marta found Ilsa crying in the wash-room and pretended she hadn't.

"You've got soap in your eye," Marta said, handing her a rag. "Happens to everyone."

"Mm," said Ilsa, who had been doing laundry long enough to know lye from grief, and took the rag anyway.

The truth—Ilsa had known it since the day the wardens' hounds had come up the road smelling of royal kennels—was that she was frightened in a way her body couldn't hold quietly anymore. Her lungs were bad; the winters had been grinding them finer every year, and this one promised to grind them to nothing. If the guild burned in spring, if soldiers came through that door, she would not be running anywhere. She would die in this stone room among other people's linens, and the thought sat on her chest heavier than the wet wool ever had.

And yet.

That evening the twins Dara and Denna appeared in the wash-room unasked, rolled their sleeves, and began wringing sheets without a word of explanation, and when Ilsa started to protest Dara said, "Sit down, you ancient horror, you'll burst something," and Denna said, "We're paying off a debt, don't ask," and Ilsa sat, and wept a little more, and let them.

Later, climbing the stairs to bed, she passed the room where Wren slept with the household's younger children, and heard—through the door—a sound she had never heard before and knew instantly, the way you know your own name. The girl was singing. Not in any tongue Ilsa knew; the melody was older than melodies, winding and strange, like water remembering when it was rain. But the children inside were humming along, matching her by ear, and the song went on and on, soft as snowfall, and Ilsa stood in the cold corridor with her bad lungs aching and felt the fear loosen its grip just slightly.

Whatever came in spring, someone had sung tonight. Someone had made the dark smaller.

* * *

Fenn the archivist did not sleep, which was normal, but for four nights now he had not even pretended to.

He worked by lamplight in the record-room, cross-referencing. That was his gift and his cage: he could not stop noticing when accounts disagreed with themselves. And the guild's accounts had begun to disagree.

Corvane's factor Sef had sent no letter since the roads closed—which was sensible, there was nowhere to send one—but Fenn had checked the dispatch box anyway, the locked one in the cellar corridor where outgoing post waited for the thaw, and found it empty. Empty, and recently emptied: the lock's dust was disturbed, the hinge oiled. Someone was destroying correspondence before it accumulated, or writing letters that never entered the box at all.

There was more. Sef's expense entries, in the weeks before the pass closed, showed purchases that made no sense for a weather-factor's business: lamp oil in quantity, a second horse, feed for a journey. Enough for one rider to travel fast and light through country where no honest traveler needed to go in deep winter.

Fenn wrote none of this down. Writing was evidence, and evidence could be found. He kept it behind his eyes, in the archive that never burned, and he watched.

On the fifth night he saw Tam come up from the cellar corridor at an hour when Tam had no honest reason to be there, and saw the old farrier's face in the lamplight—gray, stricken, older than old—and Fenn withdrew into the shadow of the stairwell and let him pass, and added one more entry to the archive behind his eyes.

*Two of them,* he thought. *Or one guilty, and one who knows.*

He could not tell which, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

Wren dreamed the snow, and the snow dreamed her back.

She woke in the narrow bed between little Sela and the cooper's daughter, both of them breathing slow and warm, and lay listening to the house. Houses spoke, if you listened right—not words, not the way animals spoke, but a kind of creaking honesty. The Gilded Compass talked in its bones all night: timbers settling, frost splitting stone, the wind worrying the shutters. Lately it had begun to talk like the memories talked, low and urgent, and some mornings she woke with phrases of the old tongue caught in her teeth, words for *siege* and *hunger* and *the year the passes ate the army*, words that belonged to people nine hundred years dead.

She didn't tell Jude that part. He worried enough.

In the daytime the house was better. The house was warm and full of work, and work was a language everyone shared. She learned to knead bread with Anselm's big hands folded over hers. She learned which floorboards announced visitors (all of them, Piet claimed, this house has no secrets and never has, and Wren almost laughed). She mended boots with Marta, badly, and Marta unpicked her stitches without a single sigh and taught her again, and the third time the seam held, and Marta looked at her with such fierce ordinary pride that Wren had to look away and blink hard at the boot.

At night, sometimes, she sang them the songs from the memories—the ones that weren't sad. There were a few. Lullabies for beast-children with too many joints and too few eyelids, sung in a voice like wind through hollow reeds. The human children couldn't understand the words but they understood the shape of them, the way a lullaby is a lullaby in every language ever spoken, and they hummed along, and the cats came and sat in the doorway, and even Bastion purred, which the twins swore up and down he had never done in eleven years of life.

One night Jude sat outside the circle of lamplight while she sang, half in shadow, his ledger closed on his knees. When the song ended and the children drowsed, he lingered while the others filed out, and crouched by her bed.

"You changed the last verse," he said, in the old tongue, careful as a man crossing ice.

Her eyes went wide. "You heard that?"

"I hear most things." His mouth did the thing that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one. "What did you change?"

Wren pulled the blanket to her chin. "It used to say *sleep, the mothers are watching*. I said *sleep, the family is watching.*" She shrugged, embarrassed. "It fits better. This house. It fits better here."

For a moment Jude said nothing at all, and in the lamplight his face did something complicated and quiet, like a lock turning. Then he touched the top of her head, once, gently, and said goodnight in both languages, and took the lamp with him, and Wren lay in the dark listening to the house talk in its bones and thought: *whatever comes, it will have to get past all of us.*

She was eight years old. She still believed, then, that the danger would come from outside the walls.

* * *

Jude balanced the winter accounts by candlelight and could not make them balance, and for once the fault was arithmetic of a different kind.

Supplies for fourteen souls, four months: he ran the figures three times and got three answers, all thin. Rationing would stretch the grain. The cold-store roots would hold. The beer would run out in February and morale with it, unless Piet's secret second barrel survived, and Jude was one of perhaps two people in the household who knew about Piet's secret second barrel, because the mice had told him in October, in the smug collective way mice have.

The numbers were survivable. It was the rest that wouldn't sum.

He set down the pen and listened the way he always listened, letting the night sounds of the guild resolve into voices. The horses, dozing. The dogs in their straw, dreaming of pursuit—they always dreamed of pursuit, poor beasts, bred into their blood by the same kennels that had made the wardens' hounds. The cats on their rounds. And beneath it all, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the thing he had been trying not to hear for a week:

Rats. Talking about a man.

Not by name—rats dealt in smell and habit, not names—but in the unmistakable currency of routine. *The warm one who smells of iron and fear. He goes down the long stairs when the moon is behind the cloud. He stands by the small door. He does not knock. Something under the stone answers him.* Jude had spent three nights confirming it, because the alternative was unthinkable, and the rats did not lie. Rats had no talent for it.

Tam. Gentle Tam, who had mended Jude's boot the first week he arrived at the Compass and charged him nothing and called him *boy* in a voice like a banked fire. Tam, whose hammer had gone quiet.

And now Jude sat with his useless balanced columns and understood the shape of the trap closing around them all: not the armies at the passes, not the wardens with their hounds, but a hole in the household's trust exactly the size of one old man's grief. Somewhere south, in a warden's keep, Tam's daughter was a hostage wearing a name Jude didn't know. Everything that followed—the false messages, the patrols, the burning writ—would flow through that wound.

He could expose him. One sentence to Vess and Tam would be in the cellar storeroom under watch by morning, and the household would be safe and smaller forever, and every kindness Tam had ever done would curdle into evidence.

Or.

Jude looked at the candle flame until his eyes watered, and thought about concealment, about the long discipline of his life, about how invisibility had kept him alive and cost him everything else. He thought about Wren changing the verse of the lullaby. *Sleep, the family is watching.*

There was a third path. There was usually a third path; it was just always the one that put the walker most at risk. If the rats could carry testimony, they could carry something else. If Tam was feeding the enemy through channels Jude could overhear—

A log shifted in the hearth. Down the hall, faintly, Wren laughed in her sleep.

Jude dipped his pen and wrote, in the margin of the winter accounts, where no auditor would ever look: *Flour stores lower than reported. Cellar vault compromised. Speak to no one.* False entries, planted where a certain pair of guilty eyes might find them—for Tam read nothing, but whoever guided Tam read everything, and whoever read everything would soon learn that the Gilded Compass's secrets were exactly where Jude wanted them.

Then he blew out the candle, and sat a moment longer in the dark, listening to his family breathe through the walls, and waited for the rats to tell him what happened next.
