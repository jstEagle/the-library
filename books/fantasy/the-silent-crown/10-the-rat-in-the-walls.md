## 10. The Rat in the Walls

The snow had been falling for nine days when the rats began to complain about the farrier.

Jude heard it first as a scratch of irritation threading up through the floorboards of his counting room—a thin, aggrieved chorus rising from the walls like water finding a crack. He set down his pen. The ledger lay open before him, half an entry finished: *One cask of bottled thunderstorm, sold to the Lady Merrow of the Salt Coast, payment deferred against the spring thaw.* The guild's impossible things could wait. The walls were talking.

He pressed his palm flat against the cold plaster and listened.

*—smells of iron and fear, iron and fear, the tall one with soft hands, he leaves the stable at moonrise, he leaves the stable at moonrise—*

*—he talks to himself but it isn't talking, it's writing, scratching marks into paper by candlelight, we can smell the ink through the boards—*

*—his daughter, his daughter, they keep his daughter, he told them where the child sleeps—*

Jude's hand went numb on the wall. Not from the cold.

*Say that again,* he thought toward them, shaping the request the way you'd shape a whisper into a keyhole. *Slowly.*

The rats were never slow. They were a hundred small mouths and one enormous appetite for grievance, and their testimony came tumbling like spilled grain: the farrier Tam, who had shod every horse in the Compass's stables for eleven years, who mended the cook's pots without being asked and carved little wooden birds for the children of passing traders—Tam rose at moonrise three nights past and carried a letter to the gap in the south wall where the wind came through, and something took the letter away, and something would be paid.

And before that—before that, weeks ago, before the hunters ever came dressed as wool merchants—Tam had sent word down-mountain. *The child is here. She has no voice. She wears a broken crown.*

The blizzard howled against the shutters. In the common room below, faintly, Wren was laughing at something—the cats, probably, or Pip the kitchen boy juggling spoons—and the sound of it cut through Jude like a splinter working deeper.

He sat back down at his desk. He did not pick up the pen.

* * *

You learn things about a house when you listen to its animals. You learn which trader waters the wine and which one doesn't, which guard sleeps with whose wife, which horse bites out of pain rather than temper. For twenty years Jude had kept those truths the way he kept the ledgers: accurately, silently, and without judgment. A ledger does not decide what should be written in it. That had always been his rule, and it had kept him safe, and it had kept him alone, and until this winter he had believed those were the same thing.

Now the ledger said: *Tam. Gentle old Tam. Traitor.*

He went down to the stables that evening because he couldn't not go, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it still hurts. The stable was warm and dim, smelling of hay and hot metal, and Tam was at the forge finishing a shoe, his broad back curved over the anvil, sparks scattering orange across the dark. He was humming. It was an old lullaby, the kind mountain mothers sang, and it made Jude's throat close, because he'd heard it once before—from his own mother, on the night soldiers burned the word *speaker* into their family's door and told them to run.

"Evening, lad," Tam said, glancing up. His face creased into its usual smile, all weathered warmth. "Cold enough to crack stone out there. Sit by the fire if you're staying."

"I'm staying." Jude sat on the mounting block, hands folded, and watched the man work, and hated how much he wanted the testimony to be wrong. "How's your daughter? Mira, isn't it? In Lowmark with her aunt?"

Something crossed Tam's face—quick as a spark, gone before it landed. "Aye. Growing too fast. Writes me letters full of spelling mistakes." He laughed, a little too easily. "Costs me a fortune in ink."

Jude knew what the rats knew: there were no letters. There was no aunt in Lowmark. There was a warden garrison at Fort Brassa, and a girl of twelve held there under a roof she did not choose, and a father who had been shown her window and told what would happen to it.

"She sounds like a fine girl," Jude said quietly.

"The finest." Tam quenched the shoe. Steam hissed up between them, and for a moment neither of them could see the other's face through it, and Jude wondered—not for the last time that night—whether the old man had chosen that moment deliberately, whether some part of him wanted to say everything and couldn't.

He left without saying anything at all.

* * *

The problem, as Jude saw it in the sleepless hours after midnight, came down to arithmetic. It was the one kind of sum he was good at, and it refused to balance.

Expose Tam, and Vess turned him out—or worse, because Vess had built the Compass as sanctuary and did not forgive those who made its walls a lie. Tam would go down the mountain into warden hands, and whatever mercy the crown showed informants who had failed to deliver, it would not extend to informants who had confessed. And Mira would pay the interest on her father's debt. Jude had seen what happened to children who stood surety for their parents' crimes. He had been one.

Say nothing, and the false information Tam passed along would keep flowing out through the gap in the south wall, straight into warden hands, and eventually one of those letters would be accurate enough, detailed enough, deadly enough, and the guild would burn with everyone sleeping inside it—including a girl who carried the memory of a shattered crown in her small stubborn skull.

There was no column in any ledger he'd ever kept for *both are innocent and both will die*. He turned the page anyway, as though a new leaf might hold numbers that behaved better than these.

What it held instead was a thought that arrived sideways, the way the best ones did: *a ledger records what happened. But nothing says the record has to be true.*

If Tam was the pipe through which information flowed, then the information itself was water, and water could be anything you poured into it. Clean or poisoned. True or otherwise. The wardens trusted Tam because Tam loved his daughter, and love was the most reliable currency in the world—which meant it could also be counterfeited.

Jude got up, lit a candle, and began to write—not in the guild ledger but in a private one, the thin black book where he kept the accounts no one else would ever see. At the top of the page he wrote: *Entry the first. Sold to the crown wardens of Fort Brassa, by way of T. the farrier: one falsehood, gently used.*

Then he sat with the candle guttering and thought about what lie could save more people than the truth would kill.

* * *

He needed help, and the help he needed had four legs and no loyalty to anyone who didn't feed it. He found the magpie at dawn on the kitchen roof, hopping along the ridge tiles with the self-importance of a bird who knows secrets are currency and it holds the mint.

"You'll carry a message," Jude told it, "but not where you think."

The magpie tilted its head. *Shiny?*

"No shiny. This is work."

*Work is not shiny.*

"It pays in bacon rinds. A week of them."

The magpie considered this with the gravity of a merchant reviewing terms. *A week.*

"Ten days. And you don't tell the other birds why."

Birds gossiped worse than rats; that was a known law of nature. But a hungry bird was a discreet bird, and this one owed Jude for two winters of bacon already. It bobbed agreement, and Jude explained the route: not to Fort Brassa, not toward any warden post at all, but a long, pointless circuit east over the frozen falls and back, carrying a scrap of paper that would never arrive anywhere. Meanwhile the real message—the one Tam would send through his gap in the south wall—would be composed by Jude himself.

That was the delicate part. He couldn't dictate to Tam; the old man wrote his own letters, in his own cramped hand. What Jude could do was make sure of what Tam *knew*. Information had to come from somewhere, and if every true thing Tam learned for the next fortnight had been filtered through Jude's hands first, then even the traitor's honesty became a weapon pointed the right way.

It was, Jude reflected, exactly the sort of trade the Gilded Compass existed to broker. He only wished it didn't taste like ash.

* * *

The chance came sooner than he liked. Three days later, Vess called a household meeting in the common room—snowbound now, passes sealed, forty souls sealed in with each other and one of them bought—and announced that they would need to ration the deep stores. Grain was plentiful; salt pork less so; and someone would have to inventory the cellar vaults beneath the oldest wing, the part of the guild nobody used since before Vess's time.

"I'll do the count," Jude said, before anyone else could volunteer.

Vess looked at him a moment too long. Her eyes were the grey of ice over moving water, and Jude had learned, this winter, that behind them lived a woman who had known about his gift for years and said nothing, and that her silences were always bargains waiting to be struck. "Take Tam," she said. "He knows the old foundations better than anyone. Half those doors only open for a man who understands hinges."

So it was that the traitor and the bookkeeper descended together into the belly of the guild, carrying lanterns and an inventory slate, down stairs that narrowed into stone worn hollow by centuries of feet.

Tam talked as he worked, the way he always did—about the frost heave cracking the third arch, about a door that swelled each winter and needed coaxing, about the year a badger got into the north cellar and terrorized two apprentices. His voice filled the dark the way a fire fills a room. And Jude listened with half his mind and laid bait with the other half.

"They say the wardens pulled out of Ravenmere," he said casually, checking a barrel mark that didn't need checking. "Reassigned south. Whole garrison."

"Mm." Tam pried at a rusted hinge. "Wouldn't count on rumors in winter."

"This one came from a drover who came up before the pass closed. Said Fort Brassa's down to a skeleton crew." Jude let the pause stretch, then added the poison, sweetened: "Half of them went west, I heard. Toward the coast. Something about shipping a prisoner."

The hammer stopped.

Just for a heartbeat—long enough for a man who listened to walls to hear the silence where motion should have been. Then the hammer resumed, steady as ever, and Tam said, "Prisoners get shipped everywhere these days," and his voice was perfectly level, and that perfect levelness told Jude everything the rats had told him and worse.

They finished the inventory. Tam's counts were meticulous, honest to the last nail—he cheated no one in the cellars, Jude noted, except the entire household above his head—and when they climbed back into the light, the old man clapped Jude on the shoulder and thanked him for the company, and his hand was shaking, just slightly, just enough.

That night, through the walls, the rats reported a letter leaving by the south gap. Jude did not hear its contents. He didn't need to. He had written them.

*Fort Brassa, half-garrisoned. Wardens drawn west. Prisoner transport due at the coast road within the month.* All of it invented. All of it verifiable-sounding. All of it designed to pull warden attention away from the mountain passes and toward a coastline a hundred leagues distant—toward a ravine, actually, if the wardens moved the way Jude predicted, a dead-end ravine off the coast road where a patrol might waste a week searching shadows.

It would buy time. Time was the only thing the Compass could afford to buy this winter, and it was buying it with a gentle old man's guilt, spent without his knowledge.

Jude blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the storm, and the storm sounded almost like a lullaby, almost like the one Tam hummed over his forge, and he thought: *I am doing to him exactly what the wardens did. Using what he loves to move him where I want.*

The thought didn't stop him. That was the worst part. He turned over, and slept, and dreamed of ledgers whose columns ran red.

* * *

On the sixth day of the false trail, Wren found him in the counting room.

She came in the way she came everywhere—sideways, quiet, appearing at his elbow like a cat deciding you belonged to her now. She climbed onto the stool across from his desk and studied him with those dark eyes that held, underneath the eight-year-old, something vast and grieving and patient as mountains.

"You smell sad," she said. Her spoken Common had improved by weeks in the household, but her beast-tongue was fluent as rain, and when she was serious she slipped into it without noticing, so that the guild dogs and even the mice turned their heads toward her whenever she spoke. "Like the fox did. Before the trap."

"I'm not sad."

"Liar." She said it without heat, the way children do, stating fact. Then she reached across the desk and put her small hand flat on the closed ledger, the way Jude put his hand on walls. "The walls told me you've been talking to them at night. The mice say your heart walks around the house looking for something and can't find it."

Jude exhaled slowly. Of course. Of course the girl who spoke every tongue the beasts remembered could read the walls too. He had hidden from humans for twenty years and forgotten that the smallest listener in the house had ears older than kingdoms.

"There's a man here," he said carefully, "who did a bad thing because someone he loves was threatened. And I know, and he doesn't know I know, and I don't know what to do about it."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Did he mean the bad thing?"

"He meant to protect his daughter."

"That's not what I asked." She swung her feet, not reaching the floor. "In the old days—before—" she made a gesture that encompassed, vaguely, an entire lost civilization and a war that ended it, "—when someone broke faith, the circle asked one question. Not *what did you do*. They asked *what were you afraid of*. Because fear breaks faith. Faith doesn't break by itself." She looked at him. "What was he afraid of?"

"Fire," Jude said quietly. "The kind that takes daughters."

Wren nodded slowly, and then, with the terrifying simplicity of a child, dismantled in one sentence the moral knot he had been strangling on for a week: "Then you don't punish the fear. You take it away." She hopped off the stool. "That's what a crown is supposed to do, you know. Take away fear. That's why they broke it. Everyone wanted to use it instead."

She padded out. Through the walls, Jude heard the cats fall in around her like an honor guard, purring.

He sat for a long while with his hand flat on the ledger, feeling foolish, feeling lighter, feeling—for the first time since the rats had named the traitor—like a man standing on solid ground. *You don't punish the fear. You take it away.* Somewhere down the mountain, in a garrison fort, a girl named Mira was being held inside her father's fear. And somewhere upstairs, a gentle old man was feeding lies to wolves because his family taught him that love must be spent in secret.

Maybe there was a third path. Maybe there always had been. Maybe it didn't run through exposure or silence but through something neither side of that ledger had a column for.

Jude opened the thin black book and, beneath *one falsehood, gently used*, wrote a second line: *Purchased from the household of the Gilded Compass: one rescue. Payment pending. Terms: courage.*

He just needed to find out what had happened to the patrol that followed his beautiful lie—and whether it had bought them a season or started a war.

The answer arrived the next morning, blue-lipped and crawling, at the guild's front gate.
