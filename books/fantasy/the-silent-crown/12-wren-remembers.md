## 12. Wren Remembers

The fever came at moonrise, and it did not come like a fever.

Wren knew fevers. Fevers were small things—hot breath and aching bones, a mother's hand on her brow in a language that had no name anymore. This was different. This rose up out of her chest like water rising in a well that had no bottom, and it carried things with it. Faces. Mountains. A sound she had never heard with her own ears but had carried inside her ribs since the night the snow swallowed her: a voice saying *hold*.

She was not in the loft above the stables anymore. She was standing on a hill of white grass beneath a sky with two moons in it, and the grass was singing.

*You are late,* said the grass. Or perhaps it was the wind through the grass, or perhaps it was the hill itself, or perhaps it was everything at once, because in this place nothing had edges and everything could speak. The speaking beasts had made this place. She knew that the way she knew her own hands—suddenly, completely, as if she had always known and had only just been permitted to remember.

*I'm sorry,* Wren said, and her mouth shaped words she had never been taught. They came out whole and old and heavy. *I didn't know I was coming.*

*Nobody knows,* said the hill. *That is how we chose you.*

* * *

They came to her one at a time, the way grief comes.

First the wolf—the great gray wolf whose shoulders stood higher than a man's head, whose eyes were the color of deep winter rivers. He walked around her twice, nose working, and then he lay down before her with his head on his paws like an ordinary dog, and that humbled gesture from so vast a creature made her throat ache.

*Little vessel,* he said. His voice was thunder heard through a wall. *Do you know what you carry?*

Wren looked down at herself. Her hands were small. Eight years of small. The amulet was gone from her neck here—or rather, it was everywhere, in the sky, in the ground, in the taste of the air. Broken crown-shape. Broken crown-song.

*A memory,* she said.

*One memory,* the wolf corrected gently. *But a memory the size of a war.*

And then she was there.

Not watching—being. She was the hill, she was the wind, she was the thousand thousand voices gathered on the white grass, and below her, in the valley where two rivers met, the armies stood facing one another for the last time.

Humans on one side of the valley, ranked in iron, their banners stiff with frost. On the other side the speaking beasts—not beasts only, but speakers of every kind: wolves who debated law under the moon, bears who kept the histories carved in living wood, hawks who carried messages between courts, the great slow elk whose antlers held the memory of every spring since the world was young. And between them, walking alone into the space where the snow fell without choosing a side, a figure in a robe the color of ash.

*Who is that?* Wren asked, though she already knew, the way you know your own name.

*The Crown-bearer,* said ten thousand voices at once. *Our king and theirs. The only one both sides ever trusted.*

The figure stopped between the armies and raised both hands, and when they spoke, their voice was quiet and reached every ear in the valley as if whispered into it.

*It ends today,* the Crown-bearer said. *Not with a victory. There are no victories left to buy. Only with a giving-away.*

*No,* said someone on the human side—a general, his face rigid with grief and rage. *Give us the Crown and we will end them forever. No more burned villages. No more children taken in the night.*

*Yes,* said a bear-speaker on the other side, her fur matted with old blood. *Keep the Crown among the speakers and we will end *them*. No more cages. No more muzzles.*

*And when you have ended each other?* the Crown-bearer asked. *What will the Crown rule over? Empty valleys? The Crown was never meant to be a weapon. You have all forgotten what it is.*

Wren strained to see it. In the memory it hung at the figure's breast, a circlet of pale metal set with stones that were not stones—each one moving faintly, like a sleeping eye. And suddenly she understood, with the terrible clarity of dreams, what the stones were.

*Voices,* she whispered. *They're voices.*

*Every speaker who ever lived,* the wolf said beside her, though he was also the hill and the wind and the falling snow. *When a speaker dies, the Crown keeps their voice. It has done so since the first of us drew breath. It is not a thing of power, little vessel. It is a choir. It is everyone we have ever lost, still singing.*

*Then why break it?*

The wolf was silent a long moment. Below them, the Crown-bearer lifted the circlet high, and both armies went still as stone.

*Because a choir can be silenced,* the wolf said at last. *A thing that holds every voice can be captured, and a thing that can be captured will be fought over until nothing remains to fight for. So we chose the only hiding place no army could storm.*

The Crown-bearer brought the circlet down against a stone.

Wren felt it happen. That was the worst of it—she felt the shattering in her own body, a crack running through her ribs, through the world, through time itself. The stones burst outward like startled birds, and each one dove—not into the earth, not into the river, but into the bodies of the speakers standing witness on the white grass. One into the wolf. One into the bear. One into the elk, one into the hawk, one into a fox at the edge of hearing, one into a woman with kind eyes who gasped and clutched her chest and laughed and wept all at once—

*Into us,* Wren breathed. *It broke into people.*

*Into blood,* said the wolf. *The Crown cannot be owned if it lives in everyone. It cannot be silenced while any speaker draws breath. We scattered ourselves to hide it, and our children inherited the hiding, and their children after them, until the gift became a secret instead of a birthright, until speakers hid from their own names.* His great head turned, and those winter-river eyes fixed on her. *Until almost no one remembered what the blood was for.*

*Almost no one,* Wren said.

*The fragments sleep,* the wolf said. *But they are not dead. They dream. And now the wars are stirring again, and the fragments are waking, and they call to one another across the distances like birds before a storm.* He rose, shaking the snow from his coat, suddenly enormous again, suddenly terrible. *You were not given the memory by accident, little vessel. Memories choose. Ours chose you because you were the one who would listen. Now listen well: the first fragment does not sleep far from where you stand tonight.*

The white grass began to dissolve. The two moons blurred. The wolf's voice came from farther and farther away, and Wren clawed at the dream to hold onto it—

*Beneath the floor. Beneath the hearth-stone. Beneath the place where the ledger-keeper counts his impossible things—*

* * *

She woke screaming, or trying to scream; her throat was raw sand and what came out was a hoarse animal sound that brought the whole household running.

Lamplight. Rough blanket pulled to her chin. Vess's face above hers, stern and frightened in equal measure, and behind her the stable-boy Pell, and old Tam the farrier wringing his cap in his big hands, and the guild cats lined up along the footboard of her cot like a jury, tails curled, watching her with yellow eyes.

And Jude. Jude kneeling closest of all, his ledger-calloused hand hovering an inch from her burning forehead, not quite touching.

"Wren," he said softly. "You're safe. You're in the Compass. You're safe."

She tried to say *I know*, and what came out stopped the room dead.

*"Sa-veth ka renn,"* she said—and the words rang in the rafters like struck bells, and every cat in the room sat bolt upright, and Tam dropped his cap, and Vess went very still.

Because it wasn't gibberish. It had never been gibberish. But before, when she spoke, it had come out broken, scattered, a child's fistful of shards. Now it poured out of her whole and fluent and ancient, and she could feel every word landing in the room like a stone dropped in still water.

"I'm safe," she said again, in the traders' tongue this time, and her own surprise at hearing it nearly undid her. "I—I know. I know where I am."

"Wren." Jude's voice had gone careful, the way grown-ups' voices go careful when they are holding something very heavy very still. "What happened?"

"The fever took me down to the bottom of the well." She pushed herself up against the headboard. Her body ached as if she had run a hundred miles, but her mind had never been clearer. It was as if someone had opened every window in a house she hadn't known she was living in. "There's a hill. White grass. Two moons. A wolf the size of a hay-wagon talked to me for what felt like years but I think was one night."

Pell made a small strangled noise. Vess said, "Cats," in a warning tone, but the cats weren't leaving. If anything they pressed closer.

"It wasn't a dream." Wren gripped the blanket. How could she make them understand? How could she make them understand that she had stood in a valley between two armies eight hundred years dead, that she had felt a crown break against a stone, that the breaking still echoed in her bones like a bell that refused to finish ringing? "It was a remembering. The memory—it's not mine, it never was mine, it's *old*, older than the kingdoms, older than—" She faltered, hunting for the traders' words and finding them too small. In the old tongue the thought came out perfect and vast; she translated it as best she could. "Older than the word for border."

Jude had not moved. But something in his face had changed—the careful mask slipping, just at the edge, the way it always slipped when he thought no one was looking. She had watched him for weeks now, the quiet man with the ledgers, the one who listened to cats and pretended he didn't. She knew about masks. She'd worn one herself, mute, all the road long, because some things were safer unsaid.

"Tell us," Jude said quietly. "All of it. As much as you can."

So she told them. The valley. The two armies. The Crown-bearer walking out alone into the falling snow. The stones full of voices—every speaker who had ever died, kept singing. The shattering. The fragments diving into blood like sparks into dry tinder.

"They didn't lose the Crown," she said, and her voice shook, and she let it shake. "They *hid* it. In themselves. In all of themselves, forever. Every beast-speaker alive is carrying a piece of it without knowing. That's why we can talk to animals—that's the Crown leaking through us, that's the choir still singing." She looked at Jude. "That's why you can. That's why I can. We're not cursed, Jude. We're not freaks. We're *shards*."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow on a roof.

Tam sat down slowly on the edge of Pell's cot, which was not his cot, and no one told him to move. Old Marta the cook, who must have come up with the others, pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. Vess's eyes had gone bright and hard and unreadable, and she had turned slightly toward the door, toward the stairs, toward the cellar below—as if the words had landed somewhere in her body before they landed anywhere else.

Wren saw that. She filed it away, the way the wolf had taught her to file things.

"There's more," she said. "The fragments are waking. They call to each other. And the first one—" She closed her eyes and reached back into the dissolving dream for the wolf's last words, and found them waiting exactly where she'd left them, patient as stone. "The first one isn't far. The wolf said—"

She opened her eyes and looked straight at Jude.

"*Beneath the floor. Beneath the hearth-stone. Beneath the place where the ledger-keeper counts his impossible things.*"

Jude went white.

Not shocked-white. Recognizing-white. And Wren understood, in one cold drop of certainty, that he had known something was under the Gilded Compass for a long time—something he had chosen not to look at, the way he chose not to look at a hundred other true things.

"You've heard it," she said. It wasn't a question. "Haven't you. At night. When the guild's asleep. Something humming under the foundations, and you told yourself it was the well, or the wind in the chimneys, or your own head."

"Wren," Vess said sharply—but it was too late, and they all knew it was too late, because Jude was already nodding, once, slowly, like a man admitting to a crime.

"Since my first winter here," he said. "Eight years. I told myself it was pipes."

"It's not pipes," Wren said. "It's a voice. It's been waiting under your ledgers for eight hundred years, and last night the fever showed me why it waited *here*—" She stopped. The wolf's final words were still unspooling in her memory, the part she had been holding back, the part that made her hands cold even now.

"Why here?" Vess asked. Her voice had gone very quiet.

Wren looked at her—at this broad, hard, fierce woman who had taken in a mute child off the doorstep of a blizzard without asking a single question—and the answer came out of her soft as snowfall.

"Because the Compass wasn't built as a trading house. The wolf showed me the founders' faces. Three of them. Speakers. They built this guild over the fragment on purpose—to guard it, and to hide in plain sight, trading in impossible things so no one would ever ask why the impossible things kept passing through." She took a breath. "Vess. Your family didn't stumble into keeping this place."

The guild mistress did not move. The lamplight caught the line of her jaw, and Wren watched her decide—watched the choice move through her like weather—whether to deny it or to finally, after all these years, put down the weight.

Down in the walls, the rats had begun to stir. Out in the stable, the horses stamped and murmured. Under the floor, beneath the hearth-stone, beneath eight hundred years of counted coins and impossible trades, something old turned over in its sleep—and hummed, just once, loud enough that this time nobody could pretend it was pipes.

Jude was already reaching for his boots.
