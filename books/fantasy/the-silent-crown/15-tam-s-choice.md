## 15. Tam's Choice

The summons came on the morning the frost climbed so high up the windows that the light came through blue, like looking out from under water.

Tam was in the forge, heating a shoe for Vess's bay mare, when the knock sounded at the guild's outer gate—three measured blows, unhurried, the kind of knocking men did when they knew the door would open for them. He knew before Cook went to answer it. He had known for eleven days, ever since he'd slid the false word into his last report to Warden-Captain Hale: *the child is weak, the household quarreling, they will yield by thaw.* A lie bought time. Time had run out.

He set the shoe down with tongs that shook only a little. The forge fire painted everything the color of old blood.

"Farrier," said the man at the gate, when Tam went out into the cold. "You're wanted. Down by the milestone."

The warden was young, red-cheeked from the pass, wearing wool merchants' clothes over mail that showed at the collar like bone under skin. Behind him, at the tree line where the road bent toward the ravine, horses stood in a patient row—six of them, and the long low shapes of the scent-hounds beside them, muzzled and gray as shadows. Tam's stomach turned over slowly, the way it always did when he saw the hounds. They were bred from speaking stock, generations back, bred down until all they could do was smell what their ancestors had been able to say. He'd once heard one weep in its sleep. He'd never told anyone that. It seemed like the kind of knowledge that would get a dog put down.

"I don't walk fast in this cold," Tam said.

"You'll walk fast enough." The young warden smiled without warmth. "Captain Hale wants you to carry terms. You've done it before."

*You've done it before.* Three words, and every one of them a nail. Tam pulled his coat close and followed him down the white road, and behind him the Gilded Compass sat in its bowl of snow like a ship becalmed, its windows glowing gold against the morning, and he let himself look at it a long moment because some part of him already understood this might be the last time he saw it whole.

* * *

Captain Hale had made camp at the milestone because the milestone marked the edge of the guild's land—the exact line, older than any living memory, where the Compass's protection began and the crown's law ended. Or so everyone had always pretended. Hale sat on a folding chair brought all this way through the passes for the purpose of never standing if he didn't have to, and he looked at Tam the way a man looks at a tool he is deciding whether to keep or throw away.

"Master farrier." Hale was not old, but winter had gotten into his voice and made it gravelly. "Warm yourself. Then listen."

There was a brazier between them, and a stool, and the courtesy of both felt worse than chains. Tam sat because refusing would have told Hale something, and right now Tam's whole survival lay in telling Hale nothing until the moment he decided what he was going to do—and he still hadn't decided, God help him, though the decision had been walking beside him all the way down the road, breathing on his neck.

"The writ stands," Hale said. "Surrender the child by first thaw, or the Compass burns. I have two hundred men in the high camps. I have writs from the prince's own hand. And I have—" he paused, delicately, "—a friend inside your walls. So before you go back and tell Vess Ashgrave anything clever, understand that I will know whether my terms were delivered honestly."

"Yes, Captain."

"The terms are these." Hale held up fingers like a man counting coins. "The child, alive and unmarked. The woman Vess, detained for questioning about her lineage. The ledgers of the guild surrendered entire—we know what the Compass trades in, farrier, even if the world pretends otherwise. In exchange: the household walks free. Every servant, every trader, every beast in your stables. No one need hang." He lowered his hand. "It is a generous offer. More generous than the prince knows. Deliver it exactly, and your girl eats well tonight."

The words landed where they were meant to land, in the soft place below Tam's ribs where Maren lived. His daughter. Eleven years old now, held in a warden hostel in the lowlands for three years—not in chains, no, never in chains, just *kept*, schooled, fed, written letters to twice a season that were read before they were sent. All those years he had told himself he was keeping her safe. All those years he had known, in the honest hour after midnight, that he was paying ransom and calling it loyalty.

"She'll come home, then," Tam said. His voice came out flat, which surprised him. "If I deliver this true."

"If the terms are met, she comes home." Hale leaned forward, and the firelight found the scar that ran through his eyebrow, the one they said he'd earned putting down a speaker village in the south. "And here is the other half of it, Master Tam, since you've been useful. When the Compass falls—and it will fall, whether by surrender or by spring—I am authorized to offer you a post. Farrier to the garrison. Good wage. Your daughter raised in comfort." He smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the cruelest thing about it. "Men who choose the winning side eat well. Men who hesitate—" He spread his hands, letting the mountains finish the sentence.

* * *

Tam walked back up the road slower than he needed to, because he was doing arithmetic.

Not sums of money—he'd left sums behind in another life, before debts and wardens and a dead wife whose last words had been *keep her safe*. This was different arithmetic. On one side: Maren, warm and fed, home by summer, and himself a garrison man with a forge of his own. On the other side: a household that had mended his boots without being asked, sung songs off-key around the kitchen fire, taught a mute child to laugh again. A woman named Vess who had taken him in eight years ago knowing—she must have known, she knew everything—that a man with a hostage daughter was a man with a crack in his foundation.

And underneath both sides of the sum, quiet as snowfall, the thing he had never said aloud to anyone:

He had betrayed them already. Eleven days of betrayal, small reports passed at the milestone, each one a little stone laid on a path leading to the burning of the only place that had ever felt like more than shelter. If the Compass burned and Maren came home, she would come home to a father who had paid for her with other people's children. She was eleven. She would figure it out someday. Children always figured it out. And she would look at him across whatever table they shared, and she would see it, and there was no forgiveness in the world big enough to fill a look like that.

Whereas—if he stood—

His hands were shaking now, properly shaking, and he stopped in the middle of the road and let them, because there was no one to see but the crows.

The truth was that Tam had spent three years learning to be the kind of man who could live with a small treason. He had gotten good at it. That was the horror of it: how easy it got. First report cost him a night's sleep; the tenth cost him nothing but a walk in the snow. Fear was a debt that compounded quietly, and every payment made the principal heavier, until a man forgot he had ever owned anything outright.

But there had been a moment—two nights past, in the kitchen, when the household had thought him asleep. Little Wren had sat on the table itself, cross-legged, forbidden and unforbidden, while Jude Zhou traced letters in spilled flour and the girl laughed—a sound like ice cracking in spring, rare and startled at its own existence. And Cook had pretended not to watch, and Vess had stood in the doorway pretending harder than anyone, and Tam had lain in the dark of the loft above the stable and understood, with terrible clarity, that he was watching a family assemble itself out of strays. Out of people the world had classified as impossible, or dangerous, or merely inconvenient.

He had spent three years being inconvenient to himself.

*Keep her safe.* Yes. But there was more than one way to lose a daughter, and some of them didn't require her death at all.

* * *

Vess was in the ledger room when he came back, which was either luck or the opposite of luck, because the ledger room was where the Compass kept its true business and its true self. She sat behind the great desk with its lion-feet, a lamp lit against the blue afternoon, and she looked up at him the way she looked at everyone—as if weighing them for a trade and finding most of them overpriced.

"Well?" she said. "Terms?"

"Captain Hale offers surrender by first thaw. The child delivered. You detained. Ledgers handed over entire. In exchange, the household walks free." Tam recited it flatly, exactly, because he had promised himself he would deliver the terms honestly at least once, to someone, before he did whatever he was going to do next. "He says he'll know if I soften them. He says—" and here his throat closed, and he had to work around it, "he says he has a friend inside these walls."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, a floorboard settled under snow-weight.

"He does," Vess said quietly.

Tam's heart stopped and then restarted wrong. "You know."

"I've known since midwinter." She rose, came around the desk, and stood before him—smaller than him, grayer than him, and somehow immovable as the mountain the guild was built into. "The rats talk to Jude. Did you think we wouldn't wonder why our rats kept visiting the milestone? He told me eleven days ago. He asked me what to do about it." Her eyes were steady and very dark. "I told him to leave it with me. Because I built this house on a simple principle, Tam: everyone who comes through that gate is running from something, and the ones running hardest are usually the ones worth saving."

"Why?" It burst out of him, ugly and raw. "Why leave me in place? Why not throw me out, or—or worse? You let me shoe your horses. You let me near the child—"

"Because a traitor in the walls is a door," Vess said, "and a discovered traitor can become a window. Jude has been feeding your friend at the milestone carefully curated lies for eleven days. The patrol that walked into the ravine walked there on information *you* delivered, believing it true. You've been more use to us compromised than you ever were clean." Something moved behind her severity—not quite a smile. "Also, because I remember what fear does to a good man, and I judged you a good man. I'm still waiting to be proven right."

Tam sat down. There was a stool by the wall, and his legs simply declined to hold him anymore, and he sat, and the confession that had been building in him for three years finally broke its dam—not the small confession, the one Vess already knew, but the one underneath.

"Maren," he said. "My daughter. She's not dead. Everyone here thinks my girl died of fever before I came to the Compass. She didn't. The wardens took her the week after my wife passed, because my wife's brother was a speaker—unregistered, hanged later, but the family record was stained—and they offered me a choice. Inform, and the girl is raised comfortable. Refuse, and she goes into the hostels, the bad ones, the ones you don't come back from with your mind whole." His hands were open in his lap, palms up, empty. "I chose inform. Three years of it. Every report. Every stone on that road. I told myself it was love. It was love the way a fever is warmth."

The lamp burned. Vess said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

"Hale offered me a post today. Garrison farrier. Maren raised in comfort, he said. And I stood there on the road doing sums, Captain Hale's arithmetic, and I realized—" Tam looked up. "I realized I've been doing that arithmetic for three years and it never balances. It never balances, Mistress Vess. Whatever I pay, I pay it forever, and Maren grows up the daughter of a man who bought her."

"So what are you asking me?" Vess said softly.

"I'm asking to stand with the household." The words came out steadier than he deserved, and he let them. "Openly. Not as a man you're tolerating, not as a crack in your foundation you're using. I want to stand in front of everyone—Cook, Jude, the boy Pip, all of them—and say what I've done, and then pick up a hammer and fight for this place come spring, if it comes to fighting. And I want—" his voice cracked here, and he hated it, and he let it crack anyway, "I want you to send word to whoever your people know in the lowlands. Get Maren out. Before Hale learns what I've done. Because he will learn, and when he does, that girl stops being leverage and starts being a hostage in truth. I know how these men think, Vess. I've been one of their instruments. They do not forgive an instrument that picks itself up off the bench."

For a long moment the only sound was the lamp and the wind worrying the eaves, and Tam knelt inside the silence like a man awaiting sentence.

Then Vess crossed to the shelf behind the desk, took down a slim ledger bound in green leather—one he had never seen, though he had shod every horse that carried the Compass's freight—and opened it on the desk.

"Do you know what this book is?" she said. "It's not the trading ledger. It's older. My grandmother kept it, and hers before her. It's a list of names—every soul this house has ever sheltered, every debt owed to it and by it, every person who stood at that gate with nothing but their fear and asked to be let in." She turned pages, back and back, to leaves browned with age, to entries in faded iron-gall ink. "My family founded this guild, Tam. Not as traders. As guardians. But that's a story for another night, and you'll hear it with everyone else when it's time. What matters tonight is this page."

She turned to a fresh leaf, dipped a pen, and wrote. Then she turned the book toward him.

*Tam Osgood, farrier. Owes the Compass: three years of reports to the crown wardens. The Compass owes Tam: judgment withheld until earned. Debt to be settled in service, openly rendered.*

"Sign it," Vess said. "Not because I need ink to trust you. Because you do. You've been carrying this alone so long you've forgotten what it weighs. Put it down somewhere outside your own chest, and then pick up your hammer."

Tam took the pen. His signature came out shaky, and it was the best thing he had written in three years.

"Now," Vess said, closing the book. "About your Maren. There's a carter named Odile who runs salt down the pass twice a month and asks no questions for coin. She'll reach the hostel within the fortnight. And there is a writ in my strongbox, sealed with a magistrate's stamp, that has opened doors in the lowlands before." Her mouth curved, dry as paper. "The Compass trades in impossible things, farrier. A stolen daughter is merely difficult."

Tam stood. His knees ached and his eyes burned and something enormous had shifted in his chest, the way snow shifts on a roof before it slides—but sliding into warmth, somehow, into light.

"One condition," he said.

Vess raised an eyebrow.

"When Maren comes here—when she sees this place—I want to tell her myself. All of it. What I did, and what it cost, and what it cost others. She should know her father was a coward for three years." He drew a breath. "So she knows what courage costs when I find it. Children believe what we show them, Mistress Vess. I mean to show her something true."

For the first time in the eight years Tam had known her, Vess Ashgrave smiled fully, and it transformed her face into someone younger, someone who had once been hopeful in public.

"Then you'd best start practicing," she said, "because Odile rides tomorrow. And Tam—" she caught him at the door, and her grip on his arm was stronger than her size suggested. "Hale will move the moment he suspects the window's closed. Whatever Jude's rats bring us next, we'll have days, not weeks. Sleep if you can."

Tam walked out into the blue evening, past the forge where his fire still glowed, up the stairs to the loft where his bed waited among the smells of leather and horse. Below him, faint through the floorboards, he could hear the household gathering for supper—Cook's voice, Pip's laughter, Wren's bright wordless sounds, Jude's low answers.

Three years he had listened to that house from the outside of himself, reporting its heartbeats to men who wanted it stopped.

Tomorrow the counter-move would begin, and Hale would learn his instrument had cut its strings, and the winter would tighten like a fist.

Tam lay down in the dark, closed his eyes, and for the first time since the wardens took his daughter, slept like a man with nothing hidden.
