## 20. Homecoming of a Kind

The thaw came to the mountain the way forgiveness comes to a stubborn man: slowly, and all at once.

Tam Verrin stood in the farrier's yard with his shirtsleeves rolled despite the bite still living in the air, and watched the snow surrender. It went first from the rooflines, then the eaves of the stable, then in long dark veins down the mountainside where a week ago there had been nothing but white. Water ran in the ruts of the road. The pines dripped. Somewhere below the treeline, he was almost certain, a stream had remembered itself.

He had lived through forty-one winters at the Gilded Compass and could not remember watching a single one end like this — as if it were a door being opened rather than merely a season dying.

His hands were not steady. They never were anymore, though he'd learned to hide it under the hammer's rhythm. He flexed them now, scarred and heavy-knuckled, and thought: *today*.

Because today, if the roads were what the birds said they were — and Tam had learned, that winter, that the birds were worth ten royal couriers — his daughter would be riding up this road by dusk.

* * *

They had come for her in autumn, the wardens, when the leaves were still on the trees and Tam had still believed a man could keep two loyalties in one chest without either rotting. They had sat in his smithy drinking his tea and speaking gently, the way men speak when they have already decided everything. Captain Aldery with her grey eyes. The writ with the crown seal. *Your girl, Mira, is a ward of the realm now. She'll be comfortable. She'll stay comfortable for exactly as long as you're useful.*

He had been useful. God forgive him, he had been useful. He had counted horses through the fogged window of his forge and written letters in a hand disguised as his own worse handwriting, and every week a warden runner had collected them from the hollow oak past the mill, and every week Tam had gone home to an empty bed and told himself that an empty bed was better than an empty chair at a funeral table.

Then winter sealed the passes, and the guild became a siege, and Tam became the rat in its walls — and then, in the strangest hour of his fifty-three years, he became something else instead. He had walked into Vess's study with the truth in his mouth like a live coal and set it down on her desk between the inkwell and the ledger, and the world had not ended. She had listened. Jude Zhou had already known — of course he had known; the man heard mice confess — and neither of them had fed him to the wardens. They had fed him lies instead, wrapped him in them like a coat against the cold, and sent him out to be a traitor twice over: once to the crown, once to his own fear.

And now the snow was going, and Mira was coming, and Tam did not know how to be a father again after so long a silence. That was the honest weight of it, the thing he carried to the forge every morning and back to his cot every night. He knew how to shoe a horse that kicked him. He did not know how to speak to a girl of fifteen across a year of letters she had never received because he'd been too much a coward to send them.

"You're sharpening a hoof pick," said a voice behind him.

Tam looked down. He was indeed sharpening a hoof pick. It had been sharp for some time. He set it on the rack.

Jude leaned in the doorway of the forge, thin as ever, dark-haired, wearing that look he wore — the one that said *I know what you're feeling, I heard it from the sparrows* — except that these days the look came softened, and Tam no longer minded. There had been a time, before the winter, before the cellar, before the speech at the gates, when being known by Jude Zhou would have felt like being skinned. Now it felt like being carried.

"The rooks say the escort made the ford at Greywater an hour ago," Jude said. "Riding slow. Roads are mud to the axle."

"Rooks." Tam shook his head, half smile, half prayer. "A year ago I'd have called you mad."

"A year ago you were writing letters for the people who wanted us dead," Jude said, without heat, without cruelty — just the plain shape of it, the way he said most true things now. "People change. Sometimes the season helps."

He left the way he always did, quiet as a cat, which was fitting, because three of the guild cats were following him at a respectful distance like a delegation.

Tam picked up the hammer. This time he actually worked.

* * *

Mira arrived at the hour when the light goes gold and long, and the whole household contrived, badly, to be doing other things.

Tam saw the riders first from the yard — four horses, mud-splattered, and the third one small and straight-backed with her hood back because the day was finally warm enough to allow it. Fifteen years old. Taller than he remembered. Her mother's chin, her mother's way of looking at a place before she entered it, cataloguing exits, the habit of a child raised by a careful man.

He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it for weeks, standing right here, saying words to the empty yard. None of the words survived contact with her actual face.

"Mira," he said. His voice cracked on the second syllable like green wood.

She slid off the horse before the groom could reach it — before anyone could reach her — and crossed the mud at a walk for three steps, and then she was running, and then she hit him hard enough to stagger, arms locked around his ribs, face buried in his chest, and Tam Verrin, who had held his hands steady through forty years of forge-fire, wept like a boy in the middle of his own farrier's yard with the whole household pretending very loudly to inspect a fence.

"I got your letter," she said into his shirt, muffled. "The real one. The one from after."

"I only wrote it last month—"

"Vess sent a rider to meet the escort at the pass," she said, pulling back enough to glare up at him through tears, and there was her mother again, glaring, alive, impossible. "You wrote me one letter in a year and a half and you let someone else deliver it. You absolute coward."

"That's fair," Tam managed.

"It's completely unfair and I'm furious and don't ever do it again." She wiped her face with her sleeve, took a shuddering breath, and looked past him at the guild rising gray and patient above the yard — the Gilded Compass, its sign freshly repainted, its windows full of lamplight beginning kindle in the blue hour. "Is it true? What your letter said. About the speech. About everyone knowing what you are now?"

"Everyone who matters," Tam said.

"And they didn't—" Her jaw worked. She was trying to ask whether they had punished him, and couldn't find words gentle enough, so Tam answered the question underneath.

"They gave me work," he said. "And a room with a better view than my old one. And last night Vess yelled at me about the state of my ledger entries, which is how she says welcome in this house."

Mira laughed — wet, startled, real — and Tam understood that the worst year of his life had just quietly ended.

* * *

The household absorbed her the way it absorbed everything: immediately, imperfectly, and with food.

Supper that night was crowded and loud. The long table in the common hall had gained two leaves since winter, because the Compass had gained souls — two of Wren's cousins among the beast-court speakers who had stayed on after the accord, and a human warden named Pell who had laid down his sword in February and taken up carpentry with the fervor of a convert, and old Corvane's former apprentice, who had arrived in March with a wagonload of weather and a letter of apology so long Vess had made him read it aloud before letting him past the gate.

Wren sat at Vess's right hand and conducted the conversation in two languages at once, which meant the beast-speakers laughed four seconds before everyone else and then patiently explained. A grey cat named Duchess occupied the bench beside Tam, having decided sometime in March that his forge was the warmest room in the building and therefore hers, and she accepted a scrap of fish from Mira's plate with the gravity of a customs official.

"She talks to them," Mira whispered, watching Wren. "Actually talks."

"Everyone here has something they used to hide," Tam said. "That's sort of the founding principle."

Vess stood then, rapping the table with the handle of a ladle, and the noise died the way it always did — not out of fear, though there had been fear once, but out of the particular respect owed to a woman who had built a sanctuary out of nothing and defended it with ledgers.

"Two announcements," Vess said. "First: the east roof. It leaks. It has leaked since the siege cracked the tiles, and Master Pell assures me it will continue leaking until someone climbs up there, and I am tired of buckets. Volunteers tomorrow, or I assign them."

Groans. Laughter. Pell raised his mug in wounded pride.

"Second." She set the ladle down. Her eyes found Tam's, and held. "The ledger. As some of you know, the Compass kept accounts for many years of things bought and sold and traded in shadow. That book is closed. Burned, in fact, which was very satisfying." A ripple of laughter; Tam caught Jude smiling into his cup. "The new ledger records different debts. Debts of gratitude. Debts owed between people in this house. And tonight it gains its first entry under the spring pages." She lifted the great book from beside her place and opened it, and read:

"*To Tam Verrin, farrier, who chose this house over his own safety, and whose courage cost him a year of his daughter's laughter: the debt of the Gilded Compass, owed in full, forever.*"

The table was silent. Tam found he could not look at any of them. Duchess pressed warm against his thigh.

"There's a line beneath for signatures," Vess said, sliding the book down the table. "Household custom, as of tonight."

Tam signed. His hand shook. Nobody pretended not to notice, and nobody minded, and somehow that was the same thing.

Then he turned the book toward Mira, and she looked at him, and he said, "Well? It's a family ledger. You may as well learn the trade."

She signed her name beneath his, carefully, like a girl signing something that mattered, because it was.

* * *

The graves were tended on the third morning of spring.

There were seven of them now, on the south slope where the ground drained well and the morning sun reached first: five guild-folk who had fallen in the breaking of the Compass, and two of the beast-court speakers who had bled beside them. During the siege and the frozen weeks of negotiation there had been no time and no soft earth, and the dead had waited under cairns. Now Tam spent a day with spade and stone, setting the markers true, and the household came out one by one as the work went on, until the whole slope was full of living people and the dead had their names cut deep where the rain would find them.

Jude brought Wren. The child — eight years old and suddenly, visibly, becoming a child rather than a vessel — stood before the stones with her hands folded and spoke in the old language, low and singing, the tongue that had started everything. Tam didn't understand the words. He understood them anyway. Grief sounds like grief in every grammar.

When she finished, Jude translated, quietly, for those who asked: *Sleep well. Your names are spoken. The crown you shattered so that no hand could hold it is scattered now, as you meant it to be, and no hand will gather it.*

Then the mood turned, because it had to, because this household could not hold solemnity longer than a pot of tea. Wren announced that the smallest grave-marker was crooked by "a hair and a half," demanded Tam fix it, supervised the fixing with the severity of a master mason, and then fell to racing Duchess downhill while the speakers shouted encouragement in two tongues and Vess called out that if the child broke her neck the ledger would record it as the farrier's fault.

Tam stood at the bottom of the slope afterward with his hands in his pockets, watching the game, and found Jude beside him the way Jude was always beside people, unnoticed until needed.

"She'll forget them, you know," Jude said, watching Wren. "The borrowed memories. It's already starting. Last week she couldn't remember the word for the old war-banner. By summer she'll be just — Wren." He said the name like a man handling something breakable and precious. "It's what she deserves. But it means someone has to remember for the rest of us. What they did. Why the crown is scattered."

"Is that why you're keeping the ledger?" Tam asked.

Jude was quiet a moment. Below them, Wren tumbled into the melting snowbank with a shriek of delight, and Duchess sat on the bank's edge radiating feline disapproval.

"I'm keeping the ledger," Jude said at last, "because for thirty years I wrote down trades. Bottled storms. Debts paid in dreams. Things with prices. And it turns out the only things that were ever worth recording were the ones that can't be sold." He glanced sideways at Tam, and there was something older and rawer under the calm. "I spent my whole life invisible, Tam. You know that. You were invisible next to me. And then one night a child knocked on the door and the hiding was over, and I've spent every day since learning what it costs to be seen." A breath. "It costs a lot. It's worth it."

From the slope, Wren's voice rang out, high and imperious: "TAM! Duchess says you owe her fish for supervising the grave-stone!"

"Does she," Tam called back.

"SHE SAYS SHE KEPT CROWS OFF IT ALL WINTER AND THIS IS KNOWN."

"This is known," Jude confirmed gravely.

Tam laughed — a big, unguarded sound that startled a pair of thrushes out of the pine — and turned back up toward the guild, toward the forge with its banked fire and the east roof that needed mending and the long spring's worth of work that was also, he understood now, the long spring's worth of living.

* * *

Evening found him in the common hall, alone with the lamp and the ledger, doing the thing Vess had begun teaching him in the last weeks of winter: the accounting of gratitude, which was harder arithmetic than horseshoes and paid worse and was, he suspected, the only accounting he'd ever care about again.

He dipped the pen. The page waited, blank and patient as snowmelt.

*Received this day,* he wrote, *from the household of the Gilded Compass: one daughter, returned whole. Entered against no account, owed to no one, given freely.*

He sat with it a moment. Then, beneath, in smaller script:

*Mira Verrin, age fifteen, requests instruction in farriery. First lesson scheduled tomorrow at dawn. Applicant shows promise and her mother's temper. Household advised to stand clear of the anvil.*

The pen scratched. Outside the open window, water ran loud in the ruts of the road, and somewhere down the mountain a stream was remembering itself, and the guild cats were coming in out of the dark one by one to claim their places by the fire.

He had just blotted the page when quick feet sounded on the stair, and Wren burst into the hall with her cheeks pink from running and something cupped in both hands, and behind her came Jude at a pace that was nearly — for Jude — a sprint.

"Tam," Wren said, breathless, holding out her hands, "you have to see what came up in the garden where the snow melted. We think the ground remembers too."
