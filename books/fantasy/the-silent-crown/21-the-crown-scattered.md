## 21. The Crown Scattered

They left the Gilded Compass on the first morning the road was more mud than ice, and the whole household turned out to see them off as if they were going to war instead of a day's ride into the hills.

Vess pressed a satchel of food into Jude's hands and pretended it was an ordinary errand. Tam checked the buck's shoes twice, then a third time, until Vess told him the horse would die of patience before it died of a loose nail. The guild cats watched from the gatepost with the flat judgment they reserved for departures, and one of them—Marrow, the gray tom who had once told Jude, in the bored tone of a creature describing weather, that strangers were on the road—rubbed once against Wren's ankle and said, in the old language, *come back before the moon forgets your face.*

Wren answered him fluently. Then she looked up at Jude with an expression he was learning to dread: the small, startled frown of a girl reaching for something that had just slipped out of her hands.

"What did he say?" she asked, in the trade-tongue. "I—I knew it a moment ago. I could feel it in my mouth."

"He said to come home before the moon forgets your face," Jude said.

"Tell him I will." She hesitated. "Tell him in his words. I want to hear it while I still—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Jude crouched, put his hand on Marrow's warm gray head, and repeated the promise in the old tongue, and the cat blinked slowly, and Wren's face did something complicated and young, grief and relief tangled together like roots in a wall.

They rode out through the gate while the household was still waving, because Jude had learned that goodbyes went easier if you didn't let them finish.

* * *

The ravine lay two days east, past the last farms, up into country where the pines grew crooked with wind and the silence had a different texture than the silence of towns. Jude had not wanted to make the journey. He had argued for it in the same breath, the way he argued with himself about most things now. The fragment had been surrendered to the covenant's keeping after the fighting—carried out of the breached vault, calmed by Wren's voice, sealed in a lead-lined case that Vess kept beneath her floor. But a covenant was words, and words needed a witness older than either court. The memory in the fragment wanted to go home. Everyone could feel it, the way you can feel a kettle about to sing.

"It pulls," Wren had said, the first night after the siege, her palm flat against the case. "Like a fish on a line. It wants to go back to where it broke."

So they went back to where it broke.

The ravine announced itself before they saw it: the birds stopped. Not fled—stopped, the way a room stops when a quarrel is about to begin. Jude reined in and listened with the sense he had spent forty years pretending not to have, and the land spoke to him the way it always did, in fragments and feelings. *Stone remembering. Snowmelt running cold and quick. Something old lying under the scree like a coin under a floorboard, patient, patient, patient.*

"There," Wren said, and her voice had gone strange, layered, the voice of a girl and the voice of something much older using the same mouth. "I fell there. I mean—she fell there. The one who carried it first."

They left the horse at the ravine's mouth and walked in on foot. The walls rose sheer on either side, gray granite seamed with white quartz, and the floor was a litter of fallen rock softened by a winter's snowmelt into cushions of moss. It was beautiful the way wounds heal beautiful, Jude thought—scoured clean, honest about what it was.

Wren walked ahead of him without hesitation, and he understood she was not navigating by eye. The fragment's memory, still threaded through her blood like dye through water, was pulling her along its own old scar.

"It was here," she said. She had stopped beside a slab of stone taller than she was, its face split down the middle by frost. "The last battle. The humans came down the ridge at dawn because they thought the beasts would be asleep, and the beasts were not asleep, they were *singing*—" She pressed both hands over her ears, though there was nothing to hear. "They were singing to keep their courage up. And the Crown-bearer stood on that stone and she knew that whichever side took the Crown whole, the war would never end, because a whole crown is a thing you fight *over*, forever, and she—"

Wren's voice cracked. She turned to Jude and her eyes were wet and eight years old again.

"She broke it on purpose," Wren said, in a whisper. "Didn't she. I always knew. But now I *remember* knowing."

"Yes," Jude said. His own voice was not steady either. "I think that's the whole of it. The bravest act in the history of the world, and no song remembers her name."

"I do," said Wren. "For now."

The *for now* sat between them like a stone neither would pick up.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the ravine wall, and Jude built a small fire because Wren was eight and eight-year-olds should have fires, whatever ancient griefs they carried. She roasted a skewer of dried apples with the seriousness of a scholar and burned two of them, and laughed at herself, and the laugh echoed off the ravine walls and came back sounding like several Wrens, all of them young.

Jude took the case from his pack and set it on a flat rock between them, and neither of them touched it for a while.

"Tell me what happens," Wren said finally. "When you open it. Vess explained it but Vess explains things like a ledger."

Jude smiled despite himself. "All right. Ledger-fashion. The Crown was never a thing, not really. It was an agreement. A memory so old and so large that the ancients poured it into an object so it could be held, and passed, and argued over. When the bearer shattered it, the memory didn't die. It scattered. Into the speakers of that generation, and through them into their children, and theirs. Every beast-speaker alive is carrying a shard of the Crown in their blood and doesn't know it. That's why we exist. That's why the gift exists. We're not a curse the war left behind. We're the Crown, still scattered, still waiting."

"And the fragment in the box?"

"Is the piece that stayed whole. The heart of it. The bearer's own shard, the one she held when she broke the rest. It's been calling to the others ever since. That's why the courts want it. Whoever holds it can call the scattered pieces—can call *us*—like a bell calls sheep."

Wren nodded slowly. "And if we let it go? Here?"

"Here is where the memory broke, and broken things want to return to their breaking. If we open the case and release it, the fragment will dissolve back into the land, and from the land into every speaker within its reach. The bell gets melted down. No one can ever call the sheep again." He paused. "Including the parts of it inside you. You've been carrying more than anyone, Wren. The memory chose you as its vessel. When it disperses, it will take its borrowed years back with it."

She looked at the fire for a long time.

"Will I forget my mother?" she asked. "The real one. The one who put me on the road."

"No." Jude said it with the certainty of a man who had asked the same question of the fragment itself, alone, at night, and been answered. "The memory takes back what it lent. It doesn't take what's yours. Your mother is yours. Your name is yours. The games you played with the cats, the burned apples, Tam teaching you to hold a hammer—those are yours. The Crown can't collect on those. They were never its coin."

"Good." She wiped her nose with her sleeve, briskly, the way children do when they have decided crying is inefficient. "Because I'm keeping those."

"I'd advise against arguing with you about it, yes."

* * *

They opened the case at dawn.

Jude had imagined ceremony. Words in the old language, perhaps, or some gesture the covenant required. But when he lifted the lead lid, the fragment simply lay there on its bed of wool, a curved shard of something that was not quite metal and not quite glass, dull as river stone, no larger than his palm. It looked like nothing. It looked like the most important nothing in the world.

"It's tired," Wren said softly. She was crouched beside the case, and her voice had that layered quality again, but fainter now, like a song heard through a wall. "It's been holding itself together for a thousand years because no one would let it rest. Every hand that picked it up wanted to *use* it." She looked up at Jude. "You're not going to use it."

"No."

"Say it in the old words. It should hear it properly."

So Jude knelt in the frost-wet moss of the ravine, with the dawn coming gray and slow down the granite walls, and he spoke the old language aloud to a fragment of a broken crown, and it was the least strange thing he had done in a year that had made strangeness ordinary.

*I am not your bearer,* he said. *I am not your wielder. I am only a shard of you myself, walking, and I know what it is to be carried and called and wanted for what's inside you instead of who you are. We are going to let you rest. Go back into the ground and the water and the blood. Be scattered again. Be no one's. Be everyone's.*

Wren added her voice to his, and hers was clearer, surer, the last fluent speaking she would do in the tongue of the ancients, and she spent it not on grandeur but on thanks. *Thank you for letting me carry you. Thank you for the songs. I will forget them and that is right. A child should have room to grow her own.*

Then she reached into the case, picked up the fragment in both hands—the first hands in a thousand years to hold it without wanting anything from it—and set it down on the flat stone where the last bearer had once stood.

For a moment, nothing.

Then the fragment began to sing.

Jude had heard the old language all his life in the mouths of beasts, and had heard the fragment's call as a pull, a fish-line tension in the blood. But this was neither. This was the sound the world might have made on the morning it was made—all the voices of all the speaking things, layered and harmonized into a single rising note that was not loud so much as *vast*, filling the ravine the way water fills a cup. The granite walls gave it back. The moss trembled with it. Somewhere above, every bird in the forest rose at once, a black confetti of wings, not fleeing the sound but *joining* it.

And Jude felt it leave him.

That was the only way he could describe it. Something that had lived in his blood since before his birth, since the first exile of his family, since the first time he'd heard a horse's heart and known it was lonely—something old and heavy and patient rose up through his chest like a bubble through deep water, and he felt it pass out of him, and he did not break, and he was not less. He was, impossibly, *lighter*. A man setting down a stone he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his own spine.

Beside him, Wren gasped.

The fragment was dissolving. Not burning, not shattering—*unraveling*, its edges going soft as breath on glass, its substance thinning into the morning air in threads of light that did not fade so much as *disperse*, seeking, seeking, flowing down into the moss and the meltwater and the roots, and out, out along the veins of the world, toward every speaker alive, toward every speaker dead, toward the great slow blood of the earth itself.

The singing rose to a pitch that was almost pain and then, gently, the way a held breath is released, it stopped.

The ravine was ordinary again. Gray stone, green moss, birds settling back into the trees, muttering to each other in perfectly ordinary bird-language about the disturbance. On the flat stone lay nothing at all, except a faint ring of paleness, like a cup mark where something had rested for a very long time.

Wren was crying.

Not the way she had cried in the fever, wracked and ancient. She was crying like a child, great gulping sobs, and Jude gathered her in and she buried her face in his coat and cried for a long time, and he held her and looked out at the ordinary morning and let his own eyes sting and said nothing, because there was nothing that needed saying that the morning didn't already know.

"I can't remember the song," Wren said at last, muffled against his coat. "The one the beasts sang before the battle. I could have sung it yesterday. I could have sung it an hour ago." She pulled back and looked at him, her face blotchy, her eyes enormous. "Jude, I can't remember the *song*."

"Do you want to?"

She thought about it with the terrible honesty of children. "No," she said, surprised at herself. "I want to remember that there *was* a song. And that it was beautiful. And that it's resting now." She wiped her face. "Is that allowed? To be sad and glad at the same time?"

"I think that's the only way anyone's ever felt anything that mattered."

* * *

They stayed another day, because Wren wanted to, and because Jude suspected the land wanted them to. She ran the length of the ravine twice, climbing the scree and shouting into the echo, testing her voice against the walls, and it was just her own voice now—eight years old, a little hoarse, unaccompanied by anything older. She found a pool of snowmelt and declared it hers. She named three rocks and a lichen. She asked Jude a hundred questions about nothing—how far was the sea, had he ever seen a whale, why did Tam hum when he worked, what was Vess's first name—and Jude answered every one, marveling, the way a man marvels watching a field after a flood: *look how much was under the water the whole time.*

On the second evening, sitting by the fire, she went quiet in a way that made him look up.

"Jude," she said. "I forgot something else today. And I want to tell you before it goes, in case you need it."

"Tell me."

"The bearer. The one who broke the Crown. I remembered her name this morning when I woke up. I've been trying to hold onto it all day but it keeps slipping, like water." Her brow furrowed with effort. "It started with a sound like wind in a high pass. *Sss.* And then something like *ren*. Sss-ren. Ssren. Seren." She looked up, and her eyes went wide. "Jude. *Wren.* She was called something like Wren."

Jude sat very still.

The memory had not simply been borrowing the girl. It had been *shaping* her, all this time, choosing its vessel with an old, patient care—and it had left one gift behind, the only kind of gift a memory can leave: a name. Not forgotten after all. *Passed down.* The way names are passed down in families.

"The Crown isn't gone," Jude said slowly, and felt the truth of it settle into him like a stone into a well. "It's just finished resting. And it left its name with you, the way a grandmother leaves a ring."

Wren considered this. Then she nodded, decisively, in the manner of a guild mistress accepting a reasonable entry in a ledger. "Then it's mine now," she said. "The name. And I'm keeping that too."

* * *

They rode home on the third morning under a sky the color of a rinsed-out sky, the kind of pale clean blue that only comes after a long winter finally admits defeat. Wren chattered the whole way—not in the old tongue, not anymore, but in the trade-speech and in cat, and once, gloriously, in horse, telling their patient buck in no uncertain terms that he was the finest animal in the mountains and deserved extra oats, a sentiment the buck received with the weary dignity of a creature who had been flattered by professionals.

Jude rode beside her and said little, and was content. His blood was his own now—lighter, quieter, but not empty. The gift remained. He could still hear the hawk arguing with the wind, still hear the buck's low contentment, still hear, faint and far behind them, the ravine's stones murmuring their long stone thoughts. But the Crown's great weight was gone from it, dispersed into the world as the ancients had intended, and what remained was simply *him*: a man who could listen, who had finally learned to speak, and who was going home to a house full of people who knew his name and used it.

Two days later they came down out of the hills as the sun set, and the Gilded Compass rose against the orange sky with its windows lit, and the gate cats were already yelling at them before they reached the door, and Tam came running out with his hammer still in his hand, and Vess stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind her looking, for once, like a woman who had been worried and would deny it to her grave.

And Wren, dropping from the horse into Tam's arms, shouted the news to the whole yard in a voice cracked with joy:

"We did it! It's scattered! It's everyone's now—and I have something to show Vess, and Jude, Jude, you have to write it in the ledger, we have to write down her *name*—"

Jude swung down and stood a moment in the yard, in the noise and the lamplight and the smell of bread from the kitchen, and thought about ledgers. About the one he'd kept for years, all columns and concealment, every impossible thing priced and hidden. About what Vess had said a month ago, when the covenant was signed: *A ledger is just a promise with a memory. The question is what you promise.*

He had a new one to start. He'd been turning the first page over in his mind for days.

He went inside, past the cats, past the smell of bread, to the quiet of the counting room, and took down the fresh book Vess had ordered for him from the stationer in the valley—good paper, sewn binding, meant to last a lifetime. He set it on the desk. He dipped his pen.

And on the first line of the first page, in a hand steadier than it had ever been, Jude Zhou began to write.
