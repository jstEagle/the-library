## 22. Entries in a New Ledger

The first entry took him the better part of an hour.

Not because it was long. It was four lines, no more. But Jude Zhou had spent a lifetime writing in code—in columns of figures where *thunder, bottled* stood for a debt and *dreams, one night's worth* stood for another—and his hand kept wanting to hide. It kept reaching for the old shorthand of concealment, the cipher that had let him record impossible things for fifteen years without ever once recording himself.

He made himself write plainly.

*Entry the First. In the spring following the Long Winter Accord. Name: Maren Ostreva, aged eleven years. Found at the ferry crossing below Harrow Ford by the mule Bess, who heard her singing to the river in the old tongue when she thought herself alone. Mother living; mother does not know what her daughter is. Speaker confirmed: yes. Reunited with family: not yet. The Compass will send word gently.*

He read it back. He read it again.

There was no way to say it that didn't sound small—four lines about a girl and a mule—but Jude knew what those lines weighed. A year ago, a child like Maren would have been found by someone else first. Wardens with their scent-hounds. Or worse, by no one at all, and she'd have grown up the way he had grown up: fluent in silence, expert in the thousand small betrayals of pretending not to hear. He had spent thirty years perfecting that pretense. He could have taught a masterclass. *How to Not Listen, volumes one through infinity.*

Now he was writing names down instead. Speaking them aloud, even, because Vess had insisted on that part—the ledger was to be read out at every quarter-day gathering, every name entered also spoken, so that no speaker the Compass found would ever again be a secret the world kept.

His throat still caught on the third name or so. He was getting better.

The morning light came slantwise through the counting room window, catching the dust motes and turning them to slow gold. Outside he could hear the ordinary music of the guild in its second spring—Tam hammering shoes in the farrier's yard, unhurried now, his rhythm steady as a heartbeat ever since the day his daughter Lira had come home through the thaw-mud and into his arms. Somewhere Wren was chattering. She was always somewhere chattering.

He dipped his pen to begin the second entry.

The door banged open.

"You're doing the *voices* wrong," Wren announced, in the manner of one delivering a formal legal complaint.

Jude did not look up. "I'm not doing any voices. I'm doing accounts."

"That's the problem." She planted herself beside the desk, eight going on nine, all elbows and indignation, her dark braid coming loose the way it always came loose before noon. Behind her trotted the cat called Marrow, gray as grave-dirt, wearing the expression of a creature whose patience has been tried since dawn. "Bramble says you told the story of the ravine to the new goose and you gave the warden-captain a *squeaky* voice."

"Bramble," said Marrow, from low in his chest, in the old tongue that only two people under this roof understood, "*tattles.*"

"He does not tattle," Wren said, translating automatically without even noticing she was doing it anymore—that was the marvel of her, the thing Jude still stopped to watch some mornings, the two languages running through her like water through a channel cut for both. "He *reports.* There's a difference."

"There is absolutely a difference," Jude agreed gravely. "And what did Bramble report?"

"That the squeaky voice was disrespectful to the memory of the battle." She folded her arms. "Also that the goose is stupid and laughed at the wrong parts."

"The goose is new," Jude said. "She'll learn."

"She says your account books are boring," Marrow added, leaping onto the desk with the entitlement of senior staff, settling directly on the fresh ledger as if it were a cushion commissioned for his personal use.

"They are not—" Jude began, then stopped, because honestly, they were. To anyone but him. And that, he thought, looking down at the gray tail curled across the page, was rather the point. A year ago these ledgers had been meant to be boring. Boring was safe. Boring was invisible. He had built his whole life inside the walls of dull arithmetic, a man who could listen to horses gossip and cats complain and never once be heard himself.

"Off the book, you great lump."

Marrow blinked at him slowly, the feline gesture that meant many things depending on context, and in this context meant *no.*

"Marrow," said a voice from the doorway, in the tone of a woman who had ended better arguments than this over worse breakfasts, "if you do not remove yourself from that ledger, I will have Tam fit you for a collar with a bell, and we will all suffer together."

Vess stood framed in the light, flour on her hands and iron in her eyes, and Marrow—senior staff, veteran of sieges, survivor of the Breaking of the Compass—removed himself from the ledger.

"Thank you," said Vess. "Wren. Breakfast is getting cold, and if Lira eats your share again there will be tears, and I refuse to referee that particular duel twice in one week."

"In a minute." Wren had gone still, which for Wren was remarkable enough that Jude set down his pen. She was looking at the open ledger—at the four plain lines, the plain name—and something in her face had shifted into an older gravity, the borrowed weight of memories that were fading now, month by month, like frost off glass. "That's the ferry girl? The one Bess found?"

"That's her."

"She sings to the river." Wren said it softly, half in the old tongue, half in the trade-speech, and the words carried a shadow of something vast and grieving that no eight-year-old should have been able to carry, and no longer quite could—which was its own kind of mercy. "The river used to answer. Before the war."

"I know," Jude said quietly. Because he did know; the memory lived in his blood too, along with everything else the Crown had scattered into every speaker alive and dead. Some nights, when the guild was asleep, he sat in this chair and let the old inheritance rise in him like groundwater, and he listened to it the way you listen to the sea in a shell: a whole drowned country, humming.

"We should go see her," Wren said. "Me and you. We could teach her the answering part."

"One thing at a time, little wren."

"You always say that."

"Because it's always true."

Vess watched them both from the doorway with an expression she would have denied under oath—the softness around her mouth, quickly folded away. "Quarter-day reading is tomorrow," she said. "You've eleven names ready?"

"Twelve." Jude turned back a page. "A boy outside Thornwick, found by his grandmother's goat. The grandmother already knew—she'd been hiding him herself for six years, same as my mother hid me. She wrote to us. First letter we've gotten that wasn't a plea or a threat. Just a *thank-you*, and a request for help teaching him."

Vess was quiet a moment. "His grandmother," she said, "should come to the Compass. We could use someone who knows how to hide children. Someone who's good at it." Her eyes rested on Jude a heartbeat longer than the sentence required, and Jude felt the old familiar warmth-and-sting of being seen by her—Vess, who had seen him from almost the first week, who had built this whole improbable sanctuary partly on the suspicion that a man like him existed and might someday walk through her door needing it more than he needed his own invisibility.

"I'll write to her," he said.

"Good." She rapped the doorframe twice, brisk again, mistress of the Gilded Compass, trader in impossible things. "Breakfast. Both of you. The ledger will still be here after—it's meant to last a lifetime, the stationer said so, and stationers don't lie about binding."

She swept out. Wren lingered, chewing her lip.

"Jude?"

"Hm?"

"When you write the names." She was picking at her sleeve, suddenly shy, and it struck him—as it struck him every few weeks, freshly, like the first time—that she had learned shyness from *them*, from the household, from a year of being a child among adults who loved her instead of a vessel among strangers who wanted things from her. "When you speak them tomorrow. Will you say mine too?"

"Yours is already in the book. Entry the First, last year's ledger. Wren, found at our own door in the blizzard, carrying the oldest grief in the world." He smiled. "I read it aloud at midwinter. You were there. You complained my voice was squeaky."

"That was Bramble's complaint."

"It was *your* complaint that Bramble reported."

She grinned—gap-toothed this year, a gap she wore like a decoration—and then, quick as a sparrow, she leaned over the desk and added something to the bottom margin of the fresh page in her careful, crooked letters, and fled before he could stop her, Marrow streaming after her like the tail of her comet.

Jude looked down.

In the margin, in two languages, one above the other, she had written:

*Maren Ostreva. Say it loud.*

He sat with that for a while. The morning moved through the window. Down in the yard, Lira was laughing at something Tam had said; the goose was being stupid somewhere; the horses, who had once refused their stalls in dread of what was coming, stood easy in the sun, because what had come had been defeated not by armies but by a ledger and a stubborn woman and a man who finally opened his mouth.

Jude thought about the first night he had ever balanced accounts in this room, five years ago now, listening to cats gossip through the wall and telling himself that contentment built on concealment was contentment all the same. It had been true, sort of. It had held the roof up. But it had been a house with one occupant and no windows, and he had not known until the windows went in—the child, the household, the armies at the gate, the terrible and wonderful necessity of being *heard*—how much he had been starving.

He picked up his pen.

Below Wren's marginal command, in a clear hand, he wrote the second entry of the new book. And then, because it seemed right—because ledgers, Vess always said, were just stories that agreed to hold still—he wrote a line for himself, the first time in thirty years he had ever put his own name in a book on purpose:

*Also recorded: Jude Zhou, beast-speaker, formerly silent. Status: found.*

Then he capped the ink, went out to breakfast, and left the door open behind him, letting the noise of his family pour into the quiet room like weather—like bottled thunder, finally uncorked, filling every corner of the house.
