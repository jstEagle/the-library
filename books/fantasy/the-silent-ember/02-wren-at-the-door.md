## 2. Wren at the Door

The rain came to Corvane Hollow that evening the way debts come: quietly at first, then all at once.

I was in the appraisal room with a dead woman's ring, which is not a sentence that ever stops sounding strange no matter how many years I say it. The ring was silver, worn thin at the band where a thumb had worried it for decades, and it was whispering. They always whisper when they've got something left unsaid. This one was murmuring about a cellar door and a locked box and someone named Pell, and I had my notebook out, transcribing in my best guild shorthand, when the knock came at the guild's front door — three raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a person who has stood at doors before and expects them to open on merit.

I knew who it was before Sef opened the door. That's the trouble with being me. You learn to recognize footsteps in bone, in floorboards, in the little shiver a building gives when someone it remembers arrives. The Ember Guild's old timbers had creaked for Wren Ashvale the way they never creaked for anyone else, and I'd have known her knock from a thousand knocks, because seven years ago I had sworn an oath with her name in it and oaths don't care how long you pretend they've expired.

I put down the ring. It went quiet, sulky, like a cat watching you leave mid-pet.

"Bram," said Sef from the doorway of the appraisal room, rain dripping off his hood. "Visitor for you."

"For me?" Nobody visited me. People *delivered* things to me — relics, mostly, wrapped in oilcloth and grief — but visitors were a category of event that belonged to other people. "Are you certain?"

"They asked for the bone-carver by name." Sef paused, and his mouth did the thing it did when he was deciding whether to be kind or honest, which around me usually meant he chose neither. "It's a ward-keeper."

"Former ward-keeper," said a voice from the hall, dry as struck flint. "They take the badge, you know. They don't leave you anything to be a keeper *of*."

And there she was in the doorway, exactly as I had spent seven years successfully not imagining her: Wren Ashvale, rain-soaked, travel-worn, jaw set at the precise angle she used when she wanted the world to know she'd rather be anywhere else. Her hair was shorter than it had been. There was a new scar through one eyebrow. Everything else was infuriatingly, exactly the same.

"Carver Winterbourne," she said.

"Warden Ashvale."

"*Former* warden."

"I heard you the first time."

Sef looked between us the way people look between two storm fronts, then made his excuses with the speed of a man who had survived the last time these two were in a room together, which — to be fair — half the guild had not. He closed the door behind him. The ring on my desk started whispering again, louder, because dead women love nothing so much as drama they didn't have to die for.

Wren's eyes flicked to it. To the desk. To the shelves of tagged relics along the walls, each one holding its small cargo of last words. Most people looked away quickly when they realized what this room was. She looked longer, and her expression did something complicated, and I remembered — inconveniently, vividly — that she had once sat in this exact room while I explained what I could hear, and she had not flinched either, and that was the whole beginning of everything.

"You need something," I said, because if we started anywhere else we would never start at all.

"A relic. A specific one." She pulled a folded paper from inside her coat — dry, protected, carried carefully — and laid it on my desk. "A keystone shard. Off the Old Mill ward-stone, broken out during the flood six years back. Guild inventory says it came here."

I unfolded the paper. It was a requisition form, filled out in her handwriting, which was still a scandal of neatness for someone who walked into rooms like weather. "This isn't how acquisitions work. Relics of this class are released by petition to the—"

"I know how petitions work. I've filed nine of them." Something cracked under the flatness of her voice, just for a moment, like ice under a boot. "They're all denied. Every year. So I'm asking the person instead of the office."

"And why would the person be any different?"

"Because the person owes me."

The room got very quiet. Even the ring shut up.

"That debt was settled," I said, "seven years ago."

"Was it." She said it without inflection, which was somehow worse than if she'd thrown the ledger at my head. "Funny. Because the oath we swore didn't come with a settlement clause. It came with a *duration* clause. Until the bond is answered. And nobody's answered it, Bram. Not you. Not me. We just—" she gestured at the seven years between us, the whole soggy distance of them, "—stopped speaking, and called that an answer."

I should tell you about the oath now, probably, since she'd gone and dragged it onto the desk between us like a corpse neither of us wanted to claim. Seven years ago, Wren Ashvale and I were bound together in the Rite of Paired Keeping — a ward-keeper sworn to a carver, a carver sworn to a keeper, our work joined at the border wards. It was meant to be administrative. It was meant to be *professional*. Instead it had been two years of her teaching me to read ward-script and me teaching her to hear what the stones actually held, two years of shared watches and shared fires and one night on the Mill Road wall that I still thought about at inconvenient hours, and then it ended — badly, suddenly, over something she wouldn't explain and I stopped asking about — with her license stripped and my oath hanging unfinished like a bell with the clapper removed.

You can't unswear a Paired Keeping. Everyone knows that. Everyone also knows you're not required to *do* anything about it. There is a whole silent etiquette of abandoned oaths, and Wren and I had been model practitioners of it for seven years.

Until tonight.

"The shard exists," I said finally. "Third shelf, green tag. But I can't release it without a countersignature, and the only officer who'll countersign for you is—"

"—the guild master. Yes. I know. Why do you think I'm standing in your appraisal room getting rained on instead of in his office?" She dragged a hand through her wet hair. "He won't see me alone. But he *will* see you, because you're useful, and he'll listen to you, because you scare him slightly, and I am not above exploiting that."

"He isn't scared of me."

"Bram. You told him his dead father's chair disapproved of his pricing structure."

"It *did*. The chair was very clear." I took down the green-tagged shard — a wedge of grey stone, ordinary-looking, humming faintly with the memory of a ward it used to hold up — and weighed it in my palm. It remembered standing. That was the thing about ward-keystones; even broken, they remembered the shape of duty. I understood the feeling. "Why does a disgraced warden want one shard off one dead ward-stone badly enough to walk through my door?"

Her jaw worked. For a moment I thought she'd give me the flint-and-nothing answer, the one she'd been giving everyone for seven years. Then, quieter:

"Because it's the piece of my last posting. And if I can show a restoration board that I re-set a keystone properly — solo, documented — they'll review the stripping order. Reinstate me." Her laugh had no humor in it at all. "One shard. One ward. One chance to be what I was trained to be, after six years of petitioning an office that throws my letters in the fire. Don't look at me like that."

"I wasn't looking at you like anything."

"You were looking at me like you pitied me, and I will walk out that door."

"I was looking at you," I said, "like someone remembering that I used to know every ward-script mark on your hands, and apparently I still do." I turned my palm toward hers, where the old inked sigils showed faded below her knuckles. Keeper's marks. They never fully washed out. Neither, apparently, did anything else about her. "Fine. I'll take it to Master Odell. Tonight. But Wren—" and I hated how much the name cost to say, how much it cost to *not* say for seven years, "—if he grants it, there may be conditions. There are always conditions."

"There always are," she said. "That's rather the story of my life."

* * *

Master Odell received me in his study above the great ledger-hall, where the ledgers themselves lined the walls floor to ceiling — three hundred years of the Ember Guild's accounts, every impossible trade ever made: years sold, names pawned, memories borrowed against futures. I kept the current volumes myself. My handwriting was considered the finest in the guild's history, which is the sort of compliment that sounds lovely until you remember what we write down.

Odell was a small man made almost entirely of rings, and he listened to my report on Wren Ashvale's request with his fingertips steepled and his eyes doing arithmetic somewhere behind them.

"The Ashvale woman," he said at last. "She came to *you*, then."

"She came to the guild. I happened to be the appraiser on duty."

"Mm." He turned a ring. "You know, Bram, there's an item on next month's agenda that concerns the border wards. The northern circuit. The surveyors' reports are overdue — third quarter running — and the Board is restless. Ward audits require a licensed keeper and a guild-certified carver working jointly." His eyes came up and settled on me with terrible pleasantness. "Certified carvers are in short supply. As, I believe, are keepers willing to work the northern circuit at all."

My stomach performed a slow, cold turn. "You're suggesting—"

"I'm *suggesting* nothing. I am noting a coincidence of needs." He smiled, and Master Odell's smiles had always reminded me of locked drawers. "Ashvale gets her audit — supervised, provisional, entirely unofficial, which is more than the Board would ever give her openly. The guild gets its northern survey. And you, Carver Winterbourne, get to complete an obligation that has been sitting unanswered in the oath-ledger for seven years, which I know for a fact bothers you, because you alphabetize your guilt."

"I do not alphabetize my—"

"Second shelf, left, between 'the ring business' and 'the thing with the ferryman's widow.'"

I decided, wisely, to change the subject. "Why the northern circuit? No one wants it. The stones out there are older than the guild. Half of them predate written ward-script."

"All the more reason for our best listener to go hear what they have to say." He said it lightly. Too lightly. I would think about that lightness later, in the dark, and wish I had pressed him. But that night I only felt the trap's comfort, not its teeth: a legitimate reason to stand beside Wren Ashvale again, dressed up as duty, signed off by authority. When life hands you permission you've been starving for, you rarely check the recipe.

"Conditions," I said.

"Provisional status for her. Joint signatories on the audit. And, Bram—" he leaned forward, and the lamplight caught all those rings at once, "—a full accounting of every anomaly you find out there. Every whisper, every fracture, every strange reading. In writing. To me personally. The Board has questions about the border, and I intend to have answers."

Something in his tone snagged on me even then, a burr in wool. But I was twenty-nine years old and the woman I'd sworn an oath to was waiting downstairs in the rain, and I was in no condition to interrogate coincidences.

"Done," I said.

"Splendid." He returned to his papers. "Send her up on your way out. Oh — and Bram? Do give the northern stones my regards. They and my family go back a long way."

That, too, I would think about later.

* * *

Wren was standing in the entrance hall when I came down, wringing rain from her coat, arguing quietly with Sef about whether the guild's hearth-fire counted as a guild resource she was entitled to stand near. She stopped when she saw my face.

"Well?"

"He'll grant it. The shard, the reinstatement review, all of it — contingent on a joint audit of the northern border wards. You and me. Starting within the fortnight."

I watched her take that in. Watched the hope arrive and immediately get wrestled to the ground and searched for weapons, because that was how hope worked in Wren Ashvale, and God help me, I had missed watching it.

"An audit," she repeated. "Of the *northern* circuit. With you."

"With me. Provisional. Supervised. Entirely unofficial."

"Which means if it goes wrong, it never happened, and we both hang separately."

"When has anything we've done together ever gone right?" I said, and then heard it — the *together*, the easy bitter fondness underneath it, seven years collapsing like a bad argument — and watched her hear it too.

For a moment neither of us moved. The rain filled the silence against the windows. Somewhere overhead, in the ledger-hall, three hundred years of accounts sat in their shelves, patient as bones.

"We'll need supplies," she said at last, gruff, retreating into logistics the way she always retreated into logistics. "Cold-weather gear. The northern posts haven't been maintained since—"

"Since you," I finished, gently.

Her chin came up. "Since I. Yes." And then, quieter, in a voice I hadn't heard from her in seven years — the voice from the wall on the Mill Road, the one that used to say things after midnight that daylight made us both pretend hadn't been said: "Bram. Whatever you think happened, back then—"

"I don't think anything happened. I've spent seven years rigorously not thinking anything happened. I'm very good at it. Ask anyone."

"That's the most depressing thing you've ever said to me, and you once described a haunted teapot in detail over dinner."

"It was a *formative* teapot."

And she laughed — one short, surprised bark of it, gone as fast as it came, but real, and it went through me like warmth through cold hands. She shouldered her pack. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, rain waiting beyond her like an ocean.

"Fortnight, then. Northern circuit." She glanced back at me, and for just a second the flint was gone and something rawer looked out. "Try not to sell any memories before I get back. You'll want your money for gloves."

The door shut. The guild creaked, settling, satisfied.

Upstairs in the appraisal room, the dead woman's ring had begun whispering again — but not about cellars anymore, and not about Pell. It murmured, over and over, soft as ash settling, the only warning it had to give:

*North. North. The stones are counting.*
