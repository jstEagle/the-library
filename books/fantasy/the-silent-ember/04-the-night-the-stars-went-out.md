## 4. The Night the Stars Went Out

*Bram*

The fire at the relay station was the kind of fire that pretended to be generous. It gave off light and a little heat and a great deal of smoke, and Bram had learned in the last two days that everything north of the wards behaved roughly the same way — promising much, delivering less, and leaving a residue you'd be picking out of your clothes for weeks.

Wren sat across from them, drafting her report. She wrote ward-keeper's reports the way other people performed surgery: precise, unhurried, faintly judgmental of anyone who couldn't do it.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm observing. It's my profession."

"Your profession is staring at dead people's belongings and writing down what they say."

"My profession is *appraisal*," Bram said. "The staring is incidental."

She almost smiled. Bram caught it — the way they always caught it, quick as a pickpocket — and filed it away next to the other small treasures they were not permitted to spend. Three years since the oath had lapsed. Three years, and Wren Ashvale still held her pen like a woman defusing something.

Bram looked away first. That was when they saw the sky.

Or rather, didn't see it. The stars over the northern ridge — there had been stars; Bram was nearly certain there had been stars, because they'd counted them absently while Wren worked, the way one counts sheep or coins — were going out. Not flickering. Not dimming behind cloud. Going out, one by one and then all at once, like candles snuffed by an enormous, patient hand.

"Wren."

"In a moment. This column won't balance itself."

"The sky is going out."

That got her attention. Ward-keepers, Bram reflected, could be relied upon to ignore hunger, insults, and the imminent collapse of their careers, but never an irregularity in the sky. She rose, followed their gaze, and went very still.

"Bram," she said, "go inside."

"Why?"

"Because I'm about to swear, and I'd rather you didn't hear the vocabulary."

Above them, the last star — a stubborn little one near the horizon that Bram privately thought of as *the clerk*, because it seemed to be doing all the work while its brighter colleagues took credit — winked out too.

And then the world made a sound Bram would spend the rest of their life trying to describe. Not thunder. Glass. An enormous pane of glass, struck once, somewhere beneath the ground, and every hairline fracture in every ward-stone from here to the border singing the same terrible note at the same time.

*Four hundred and eight,* said something vast, in the marrow of the world. *Four hundred and seven.*

The fire collapsed into itself. The horses screamed.

"*Inside,*" Wren said, and this time Bram went.

* * *

*Wren*

The relay station had been built by optimists: two rooms, a roof of slate, walls of fieldstone mortared with hope. Wren put Bram behind her, put her back to the door, and did what ward-keepers are trained to do when the wards die, which is precisely nothing, because there is nothing left to do, and then did it anyway — hands moving through the old forms, fingers tracing sigils that answered only with silence.

Nothing. The air where her craft should have been felt like a room after furniture's been removed. The shape of it still there. Nothing in it.

"How long," Bram said behind her. Not a question. A request for numbers, because Bram Winterbourne coped with catastrophe the way other people coped with grief: by filing it.

"If the fractures spread the way they were spreading this afternoon? We have minutes." She heard them doing arithmetic. "Don't calculate. It doesn't help."

"It helps *me.*"

"That," Wren said, "is the saddest thing anyone has ever said to me, and I once trained under Master Corvel."

The second strike came. Closer. Under the floorboards, this time, and through the soles of her boots Wren felt the relay station's foundations shudder like a dog shaking off water. Outside, the night — a real night now, starless, total — began to move.

Hollow-things came across the fields the way floodwater comes across a road: not running, not hunting, simply *arriving*, everywhere at once, drawn to warmth and breath the way iron is drawn to lodestone. Wren saw shapes that had no business holding together — long things, jointed wrong, faces where faces shouldn't be. And among them, worse, things that wore the shapes of people the way empty coats wear shoulders.

"They'll follow the living," she said. "They always follow the living. Which means—" Her stomach dropped. "The village. Millbrook's half a mile east. They'll go for the village."

"Then we go for the village faster."

"We can't outrun them on foot—"

"No," Bram agreed. They were already pulling their pack open, already lifting out the relics they carried — a ring, a carved whistle-bone, three lockets, a child's wooden bird. Their hands were steady. Wren hated how steady. "But we don't need to outrun them. We need to know which way they're *not*. And I happen to employ several hundred consultants who work exclusively at night."

Wren stared at them. "You're going to ask the dead for directions."

"I'm going to *pay* the dead for directions. There's a difference. It's in the ledger."

Outside, the third strike fell, and somewhere east, thin across the dark, the bells of Millbrook's chapel began to ring — not in alarm, not yet, just the midnight hour, rung by some dutiful soul who did not yet know the sky had ended.

"Chapel," Wren said. "Stone walls. Iron bell. If anything in this valley holds, it holds there."

"Then we hold it," said Bram, "together," and before Wren could examine the way those two words landed in her chest like a swallowed coal, they opened the door and walked out into the starless dark.

* * *

*Bram*

Running toward danger was, Bram felt, fundamentally against their nature. They were a person of ledgers, of quiet rooms, of careful hands. But the dead were loud tonight — louder than Bram had ever known them, hundreds of voices pouring up through the soil like water through cracked clay — and among all that noise, one voice cut clear as a bell:

*The chapel. Go to the chapel. My daughter is there.*

It was the miller. Old Hobb, dead eleven years, whose femur Bram had appraised in spring and who had spent that entire appraisal complaining about his daughter's choice of husband. He sounded different now. Urgent. Afraid — and the dead were rarely afraid, because most of what frightened the living had already happened to them.

"Hobb?" Bram said it aloud, mid-run, and heard Wren beside them choose not to comment. "Which way?"

*East road, then the footpath past my mill — no, the mill's gone, don't look at the mill, boy — the footpath, the chapel, she lights candles Thursdays, she'll be there, tell her—*

He stopped. Dead voices always stopped, at the good parts. But he'd given enough.

"East road," Bram gasped. "Past the mill. Don't look at the mill."

"What's at the mill?"

"A daughter. Candles. Possibly a husband I'm instructed to insult."

They ran. The starless dark was the worst part, Bram decided — worse than the cold, worse than the shapes moving at the edges of sight. Without stars, the world had no ceiling. It was like running along the bottom of a well that went up forever.

Then Millbrook's first house appeared out of the black, and Bram understood that the night had gotten worse in ways the sky couldn't take credit for. Doors hung open. A cart stood in the road, mid-turn, its ox gone. And down the lane, where the lamplight of the last lit window reached, something long and pale was standing very still, listening — the way a spider listens to a wrapped fly.

*Not that way,* hissed a dozen voices at once, the whole village dead speaking through the ground like a congregation murmuring against a sermon. *Not that way, not that way — the little ones went to the chapel, the little ones, the little ones—*

"This way!" Bram seized Wren's sleeve and cut left, down an alley between a smokehouse and a fence, following not sight but sound — the dead drawing them a map in whispers. *Left, left, the low wall, over the low wall, mind Mrs. Petch's roses, she'll haunt you herself if you trample them—*

"Are we being navigated by gossip?" Wren panted.

"The dead keep excellent local records."

They came over the low wall into the chapel yard, and Bram's heart did something complicated. Light. Real light, candlelight, spilling gold through the chapel windows, and inside, silhouettes — dozens of them, small and large, huddled between the pews. The living of Millbrook, gathered around the one man brave or dutiful enough to keep ringing bells in a dying world.

The pale thing in the lane turned its faceless head toward the light.

"Oh no," said Wren, "you don't," and did something with her hands that Bram had seen exactly once before, years ago, on a night neither of them discussed — a warding form, raw, unlicensed, furious. No power came. But the thing in the lane hesitated anyway, puzzled, like a wolf meeting a fence made of memory.

It bought them four seconds. Bram used all four to get through the chapel door.

* * *

*Wren*

Inside was worse than outside, because inside there were faces. Forty-some villagers, candlelit, pressed together between the pews — children first, always children first, mothers' arms locked around them. A gray-haired verger stood by the bell rope, breathing hard, as though ringing might hold back the dark through sheer obstinacy. Wren liked him immediately and expected him to die, and hated both facts.

"The wards," a man said — the reeve, red-faced, grasping at her like she was scripture. "You're a ward-keeper. Raise the wards!"

"The wards are gone, sir."

"Then *raise them again!*"

"With what?" It came out sharper than she meant. Softer: "There's nothing left to raise them with. I'm sorry. I am so sorry."

She had said those words before. To a tribunal, three years ago, in a cold room, before men who stripped her license and called her negligence what had actually been obedience to a bad order. She had promised herself never to say them again. Here they were, in her mouth, in a chapel full of people the Hollow wanted to eat.

Then Bram stepped past her, and everything changed.

They stood in the center aisle, closed their eyes, and did the thing Wren had only ever seen them do quietly, apologetically, over a ring or a locket — except now it filled the whole chapel. Their lips moved. Their hands moved, tracing shapes in the air like a conductor waking an orchestra. And the candles *leaned*.

Every flame in the building bent toward them, and the shadows swung like a ship's rigging, and Wren felt — actually felt, through whatever broken instrument her craft had become — the chapel fill with presence. Hundreds of presences. The dead of Millbrook, crowded in around the living like shepherds around a fold, and Bram standing among them translating.

"There's a cellar," Bram said aloud, eyes still shut, voice strange and doubled. "Under the vestry. Stone-lined. Old Hobb dug it himself — he says—" a pause, a wince, "—he says to stop crying, Marta, he can hear you from here, and the baby has his chin."

A woman in the front pew made a sound that was half sob, half laugh.

"He says the cellar connects to the well shaft. The water breaks the Hollow's scent. Everyone down, quietly, now — and someone bring the candles, he says, the dark is rude enough without giving it a map."

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then the verger set down his bell rope, nodded once to Wren as if she'd given an order worth following, and opened the vestry door.

What followed was forty people moving in silence through a chapel while things scratched at the shutters, and it worked, and Wren would remember it for the rest of her life: the dead of Millbrook guiding their grandchildren's grandchildren down stone steps, one whisper at a time, through a bone-carver who had spent years being feared for exactly this gift.

She held the door. That was her job, the only job left: sword drawn, back to the cellar stairs, watching Bram walk the aisle last of all, pausing at each pew to murmur — *he says goodbye, he says mind the third step, he says tell his son the south field drains better than he thought—*

The chapel doors burst inward.

Three of them poured through, pale and long and wrong, wearing the shapes of the drowned and the forgotten, and Wren did the arithmetic she'd forbidden Bram to do: three of them, one of her, forty lives below, and one reckless, brilliant, impossible bookkeeper still standing in the aisle with their eyes closed and their hands full of ghosts.

"Bram!" she shouted. "Finish the count. I'll buy the time."

"You're terrible at math," Bram said, eyes opening, and picked up a candlestick.

* * *

*Bram*

Later, Bram would struggle to explain those minutes. There was fighting, yes — Wren fought like a door slamming in a storm, all edges and refusal, and the candlestick turned out to be iron and surprisingly effective, and Bram broke a tooth on the flagstones and headbutted something that used to have a face. But mostly there was listening.

The dead of Millbrook did not fight. They did something stranger. As each hollowed thing crossed the aisle, the dead *recognized* it — reached into it, calling names into its borrowed shape. *Jonas. Jonas Petch, you come out of there. You come out of that thing right now.* And the things faltered, stumbled, wept without eyes, because somewhere inside the hunger was a scrap of what they'd been, and being remembered hurt more than iron.

One by one, Wren put them down. One by one, the dead of Millbrook caught them, gently, on the way.

When it was done, Bram sat on the altar steps, bleeding from the lip, holding a candlestick, laughing — or something adjacent to laughing — while below them forty living people breathed in a stone cellar and hundreds of dead people stood watch.

Wren dropped down beside them. Her knuckles were split. She was looking at Bram the way people look at a fire in winter.

"All those years," she said quietly. "At the guild. They called your gift a curse. They made you work in the back rooms."

"They gave me very good ink," Bram said. "I want that noted in the record."

"Bram."

"I know." Bram looked up — through the shattered roof, at the enormous, starless, open wound of the sky. Somewhere far off, faint but unmistakable, the vast patient voice counted on. *Four hundred and six.* "I know what I am, Wren. Tonight, so does everyone else."

Below, a baby cried, and was hushed. Above, the dark pressed close. And in Bram's pocket, against their hip, the dead woman's ring from Chapter One of their acquaintance — the first relic they'd ever appraised alone — whispered something new. Not a secret. A warning, passed hand to hand down a chain of the dead, from the Hollow's heart all the way to a chapel floor:

*They are coming to the Stone. All of them. And the guild knows what your family is for.*

Bram went very still.

"Wren," they said. "We need to talk about my ancestors."
