## 5. Refugees of the Lit World

The chapel smelled of tallow and fear and forty-one living people, and Wren Ashvale had never been so glad to smell anything in her life.

Forty-one. She counted them twice, then a third time because counting was something her hands could do instead of shaking. They were packed into the nave of the little village chapel of Saint Ellery-of-the-Lamp, which was less a church than a stone barn with pretensions, its bell tower leaning like a drunk uncle. The Hollow-things had come through the shattered wards at moonrise and the villagers had come running, and Wren had stood in the doorway with a warding rod she hadn't held in four years and kept them alive until dawn.

Until what should have been dawn. The sky outside the broken windows was still black — not night-black, not cloud-black, but *absent*-black, the particular darkness of a sky with no stars in it at all. Wren had served on the border for six years before her disgrace, and she had seen storms swallow constellations whole. This was different. This was the sky being *unmade*, one pinprick at a time, and then all at once.

"Keeper Ashvale." A woman with flour still dusted on her forearms pressed a bowl of barley soup into Wren's hands. "You've not eaten since you got here."

"I'm fine."

"You're swaying."

"I'm *leaning*. It's a technique." But Wren took the bowl, because refusing would have required more energy than she possessed. "How is the east room?"

"The Tamms family's settled. The little one finally stopped crying." The woman — Maren, Wren thought, Maren Ostell, who ran the mill — lowered her voice. "It stopped crying when your colleague started telling it stories about the bones."

Wren glanced across the nave. Bram Winterbourne sat cross-legged on the cold flagstones, surrounded by children who should by rights have been screaming, holding up a small carved vertebra to the firelight. Even from here, Wren could see the way their hands moved — careful, reverent, the hands of someone handling something that might speak. The children were rapt. One of them, the miller's youngest, had stopped sniffling entirely.

"That's not a colleague," Wren said. "That's a bone-carver. Different guild rank. Entirely different temperament."

"They let it talk to dead people?"

"*They*," said Wren, with more edge than she'd intended, "are standing right there and can hear everything we're saying about them, Maren, because they've told me three times that their hearing extends past death but apparently not past a whisper two feet away."

Bram's head turned. Across the chapel, by firelight, they gave Wren a look of profound and theatrical offense, then returned to the children as though nothing had happened. Wren felt her mouth do something it hadn't done in days — the beginning of a smile, quickly strangled.

Maren watched the exchange with the frank interest of a woman who ran a mill and therefore missed nothing. "Old friends?"

"No."

"Mm."

"We worked together. Years ago. On the border."

"Mm-hm."

"There was an oath," Wren said, because Maren's silence was somehow louder than questions. "A joint covenant. Ward-keeper and bone-carver, paired for the northern reaches. It's an old arrangement. The wards are carved from bone, you see, and bone wants a listener, and the listener wants a keeper. We were paired for six years."

"And then?"

"And then I let it lapse." The words came out flat and rehearsed, the way a confession comes out when it has been given to no one so many times that it has worn a groove. "I filed the release papers myself. I requested reassignment. And four years later I falsified my inspection logs on the northern ward-stones because I couldn't bear to look at them anymore, and they stripped my license for it, and now the wards are down and everyone I failed is sleeping in a chapel."

She said it all in one breath, which she supposed was a kind of courage, and then immediately regretted it, because Maren Ostell did not look horrified. She looked sad. Sadness was worse.

"Eat your soup," Maren said gently. "The dead don't need you falling over."

*The dead.* Wren looked at her bowl. Somewhere out there, beyond the walls, in the dark where the stars used to be, the dead were walking around loose for the first time in a century, and the woman who had been responsible for keeping the border between them intact was eating barley soup in a chapel that stank of tallow.

She ate the soup.

* * *

The first scream came at what Wren's body insisted was midmorning, though the lightless sky made a liar of every hour.

It came from the east room, and Wren was moving before she finished deciding to move, warding rod already in her hand, Bram's footsteps behind hers. She shouldered the door open expecting Hollow-things, expecting teeth, expecting the worst.

What she found was old Tamms, seventy if he was a day, sitting upright on his cot with his eyes wide and white-rimmed, pointing at the wall.

"It spoke," he rasped. "My wife. Dead eleven years. She spoke through the wall."

Bram moved past Wren without a word and knelt by the old man, and Wren saw them do the thing she had watched them do a hundred times on the border: go very still, tilt their head slightly, as though listening to a conversation happening one room over. Their fingers found the old man's wrist, not checking a pulse but reading it, or perhaps reading something in the wedding band he wore.

"She says you left the gate open again," Bram said quietly. "And she says it doesn't matter. Nothing matters tonight except staying inside the chalk line."

Tamms made a sound that was half sob and half laugh. "Eleven years and she's still scolding me about the gate."

"Eleven years and you're still leaving it open." Bram patted his hand and rose, and Wren followed them into the corridor, where the carver stopped and leaned against the wall and closed their eyes.

"You heard it too," Wren said. Not a question.

"I hear everything tonight. Everyone. The whole village, the whole *hundred miles* of it, everyone who ever died anywhere near here is talking at once and most of them are just—" Bram's breath caught. "Most of them are trying to help. They know the paths. They know which cellar floods and which chimney draws and where the hollow-things can't follow because the ground remembers a blessing. They're guiding people. That's why our survival rate is—" They stopped. Looked at Wren with an expression she couldn't parse. "Wren. Why didn't anyone tell me the wards were failing? You inspected the north reach for two years after I—"

"After you what?"

The silence stretched. Bram opened their eyes and something in them was guarded in a way Wren remembered too well.

"Nothing. After I left, obviously." They pushed off the wall. "We should reinforce the chalk. The dead say the things outside avoid the threshold stones, and the chalk marks the—"

"Wren."

They both turned. Maren Ostell stood at the end of the corridor with a candle and an expression of apology. "Sorry to interrupt. There's — there's a man at the west window asking for Keeper Ashvale. Says he knows you. He's got a guild badge."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floorboards. "Describe him."

"Grey beard. Guild colors. He says his name is Corvane."

*Corvane.* Her old instructor. The man who had signed her license and then signed its revocation. Who had, in the last year, taken over the border audit division. What was he doing here, in a refugee chapel, on the night the world ended?

Unless he wasn't here about the refugees at all.

"I'll go," she said, and reached for the door.

Bram caught her arm. Their grip was gentle but their eyes were sharp. "Wren. On the night the stars went out, a senior guild officer walks forty miles of broken border to find *you*, in a village chapel, personally?" Their voice dropped. "He's not here about the wards going down. He's here because you knew something. Or because *we* know something. Think. When did you last see the northern audit reports?"

"I told you, I never saw—"

"Not the reports. The stones themselves. When you touched them, before you stopped touching them. Did anything feel wrong? Not cracked. Wrong."

Wren wanted to say no. She wanted to say she'd been careless, negligent, guilty only of guilt itself. But Bram was looking at her with those patient, unsettling eyes that had always seemed to see one layer deeper than skin, and the memory surfaced unbidden: the last inspection, four years ago, her palm flat against the great ward-stone at Harrow's Ford, and the sensation — brief, dismissible, which she had spent four years dismissing — that the stone was not humming the way a ward hums.

It was *counting.*

"Wrong," she said slowly. "It felt wrong. Like it was waiting for something. I thought I was imagining it. I thought—" She laughed, short and bitter. "I thought I was going mad, so I stopped inspecting, and then I forged the logs, and here we are."

Bram's face had gone very pale. "Counting," they repeated softly. "Down to what, I wonder."

Outside, in the starless dark, something howled — long and low, not animal, almost musical. Inside, forty-one people went silent as one.

"Later," Bram said. "Chalk first. Questions later."

But Wren saw their hands, and their hands were not steady, and Bram Winterbourne's hands were always steady.

* * *

By what might generously be called evening, the chapel had become a fortress of chalk lines and blessed thresholds, and Wren had run out of tasks, which meant she had run out of excuses.

She found herself in the bell tower with Bram, ostensibly checking the sightlines, actually standing shoulder to shoulder in the narrow dark while the wind off the Hollow moaned through the broken louvers. Below them, the villagers slept in shifts. Above them, nothing. No stars. Just the great blank page of the sky, waiting to be written on or erased entirely.

"I keep thinking," Wren said, because the silence had gotten unbearable, "that I should tell you something. Since we might die tomorrow."

"A cheerful opener."

"I mean it, Bram." She gripped the tower sill. "When I let the oath lapse. When I requested the transfer. It wasn't because of the work. You deserve to know that much, even if I can't—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again from a safer ledge. "There's something else you should know. About the wards. Something I learned when I trained, something that isn't in any of the public texts."

Bram turned to look at her. In the darkness their face was mostly shadow and the faint grey gleam of the starless sky through the louvers.

"The wards aren't just carved," Wren said. "Anyone can carve bone. Any bone-carver can make a ward that turns a wraith for a season. But the *border* wards — the great ones, the covenant wards — they run on something older. The founding covenant. Every hundred years, the Ember Guild reforges it. Renews the terms. Keeps the border sealed."

"All right," Bram said slowly. "Renews it *how*?"

Wren had rehearsed this speech for four years, in the privacy of her disgrace, and had never once managed to say it aloud. Now it came out in a rush, like water through a crack.

"With a sacrifice. That's the term nobody writes down. The covenant requires a life, willingly given — someone of both bloods, living and Hollow both, someone who *belongs to both realms*. That's the price of renewal. One life per century, and in exchange, the stars stay lit and the dead stay on their side of the border." Her voice cracked. "That's what the wards are. That's what they've always been. A fence built on a grave, renewed every hundred years with another grave."

The tower was silent except for the wind.

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because I found out four years ago." Wren's hands were shaking again; she let them. "I read the restricted archives when I was up for promotion. I wasn't supposed to. And then I looked at the date of the next renewal and I looked at the audit schedule and I realized the northern reach — *our* reach, Bram — was scheduled for the century's reforging. And I couldn't do it. I couldn't stand at those stones knowing what they were for, knowing what was coming, so I let the oath lapse and I stopped looking and I forged the logs because the alternative was becoming complicit in—" She gestured at the blank sky, the broken world, everything. "*This.*"

"You thought if you walked away, you wouldn't be part of it."

"I thought if I walked away, maybe it would pass me by." She laughed, wet and ugly. "Stupid. The wards fell anyway. Everything I tried to avoid—"

"Wren."

"And the worst part, the truly unforgivable part, is that I *knew*. I knew the price, and I knew roughly when it was due, and I said nothing. To the guild, to the villages, to *you*. I let you carve those wards. I stood next to you while you sang to them. And the whole time I was thinking: one of these centuries, someone has to die for this, and I cannot be the one who holds the knife, so I will simply be somewhere else—"

"*Wren.*"

"—and now forty-one people are sleeping on a chapel floor because I was a coward with excellent penmanship—"

"Wren!" Bram's hand closed over hers on the sill, warm and certain. "Look at me."

She looked. It was, she reflected later, the same look they had given her across a campfire ten years ago, the night they'd sworn the oath: exasperated, fond, and seeing straight through her to the spine of the matter.

"You didn't hold the knife," Bram said. "You put it down. Those aren't the same thing. And whatever else you did or didn't do — you're telling me now. You're here. You reinforced my chalk lines at three in the morning and you haven't slept and you gave your soup to the Tamms girl when you thought I wasn't looking." A ghost of a smile. "I was looking. I always look at you. That's rather the problem."

Wren's breath stopped in her throat. "Bram—"

"Don't answer that yet. Because I need to finish doing the arithmetic I've been doing since you started talking, and I need you to be quiet while I do it, because I think I'm going to hate the answer." Their voice was very controlled, the way it went when they were frightened. "The sacrifice. Both bloods. Living and Hollow. Say that part again."

"One who belongs to both realms. Born of the lit world and the Hollow both. It's why they're so rare — the covenant needs someone with a claim on each side, someone whose death the border will actually recognize. Most centuries they search for years. Some centuries—"

"Some centuries they fail," Bram whispered. "And the wards fray early."

Wren nodded. "The guild keeps registries. Lineage records. Looking for candidates. Why does that matter to—"

She saw the moment it landed. Saw Bram's face change, the color draining from it by degrees, the way color drains from a dying fire.

"Bram?"

"My mother," Bram said, very quietly, "was born on the wrong side of the border."

The wind filled the pause, moaning through the tower like something singing to itself in an empty house.

"She never talked about it. Said she crossed over young, that her people were 'river folk,' that she didn't remember much. I believed her. Children believe their mothers." Bram's laugh was terrible. "River folk. Wren, the river *is* the border. My father was a guild bone-carver from the lit world, three generations deep. And my mother was—" They swallowed. "I am a guild ledger-keeper with a Hollow-born mother and a lit-world father and I have spent my entire career appraising relics and keeping accounts for the very institution that—"

"That's been looking for someone exactly like you," Wren finished, and the horror of it settled over both of them like frost.

Every piece slid into place with the horrible smoothness of a well-oiled mechanism. The pairing of a disgraced ward-keeper with the guild's best bone-carver for a border audit. The audit route through the northern reach, where the renewal was due. Corvane arriving at their refuge within a day. The guild had not sent them to *inspect* the wards.

The guild had sent them to the altar.

"Oh," Bram said. Small. Almost conversational. As though confirming a figure in a ledger. "Oh, that's what this is."

"No." Wren grabbed their shoulders, spun them around, made them face her. "No. Listen to me. Whatever they planned, whatever they designed, it stops being true the moment we decide it is. Do you hear me? I let one oath lapse in my life and it cost the world its stars. I am *not* letting this one go. Not this one. Not you."

For a moment Bram just looked at her, and in the starless dark their eyes were the brightest thing in the tower.

"The oath," they said hoarsely. "You said it lapsed."

"It did."

"Then swear it again."

Wren's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs. "Bram—"

"Right now. Here. No guild forms, no witnesses but the dead, who frankly have better judgment than the guild anyway." Their hands found hers in the dark, and they were steady now, steadier than Wren had ever felt her own. "The old terms. Keeper and carver. Your protection, my listening. Until it's mended or we are. Say it with me."

Below them, in the chapel, forty-one refugees slept. Beyond them, in the dark, the Hollow waited. Above them, the empty sky offered nothing back.

Wren Ashvale bowed her head, and swore.

And far below the chapel floor, beneath the roots of the world, something vast and patient — the thing that lived in the ward-stone at Harrow's Ford, the thing that had been counting — reached the end of a number, paused, and began, softly, to count again.
