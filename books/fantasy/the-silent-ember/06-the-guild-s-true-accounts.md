## 6. The Guild's True Accounts

The Ember Guild hall smelled of beeswax and old smoke, the way it always did after a crisis — as if someone had gone through every room lighting candles against the dark and then forgotten to blow them out.

Bram had lit most of them themselves, three nights ago, when the stars were still missing from the sky. Now they walked through the great reading room pinching wicks between thumb and forefinger, one by one, because the guild master had said the place looked "funereal" and Bram had privately thought that funereal was rather the point of a guild that traded in the dead's belongings. But Master Corvane Ashgrave had opinions about atmosphere the way other men had opinions about wine: loudly, and without much evidence.

"Left side first," he'd said this morning, tapping his cane on the marble. "Order out of chaos, Winterbourne. That is what we do. That is what we have *always* done."

What we do. Bram turned the phrase over now, standing in the archive stairwell with a lamp in one hand and a strange cold feeling in the other — not in the hand itself, exactly, but in whatever part of a person knows when a ledger is lying to them.

Because Bram had spent eleven years keeping those ledgers, and for the first time, one of them had lied.

It had happened an hour ago, innocently enough. Bram had been reconciling the refugee accounts — souls sheltered, relics surrendered for safekeeping, debts deferred — when they noticed that the founding ledger, the great leather tome chained to its lectern in the archive like a prisoner, had a page count that didn't match its index. The index promised two hundred and six entries. The book held two hundred and five.

One page, vanished. Or rather — one page, hidden, because Bram had run their fingers along the spine's inner groove and felt paper where paper should not be. A false leaf, glued flush against the binding, invisible to any eye and silent to any ear.

Silent, that was, until Bram pressed their palm flat against it and listened the way they listened to rings and lockets and the small bones of the beloved dead.

The page had screamed.

Not words. Not at first. Just a sound like a door being forced, like something sealed so long it had forgotten how to be anything but shut. Bram had snatched their hand back, knocked over the lamp, and stood in the dark of the archive with their heart going like a hammer on a nail, telling themselves it was nothing, telling themselves that even haunted books deserved the benefit of the doubt.

But Bram Winterbourne had never once in their life believed in benefit of the doubt where a hidden page was concerned. Curiosity was the family curse, right after hearing voices and right before dying inconveniently.

So now they climbed the archive stairs with fresh wax, a bone-handled knife for the glue, and the particular grim cheer of a person about to confirm a suspicion they'd very much prefer to keep.

* * *

The archive at night was Bram's favorite room in the world and the one place in the guild where they were genuinely happy. Shelves rose four stories into shadow, organized by a taxonomy only Bram fully understood — relics by resonance, documents by decade, and one entire alcove devoted to objects that hummed when nobody was looking, which Bram had labeled, in a moment of weakness, *Singers, Unverified*.

They set the lamp on the lectern. The founder's ledger sat where it always sat, its chain pooling around it like a sleeping snake. Old Aldous Emberhand's name was stamped on the cover in gold gone brown, and beneath it the guild motto: *ALL THINGS PASS THROUGH.*

"All things pass through," Bram murmured. "Let's see what you kept from passing."

The knife slid under the false leaf with a sound like a tooth coming loose. The glue was old, brittle, made more of tallow than anything else — someone had wanted this page recoverable, actually, by someone who knew where to look. By an heir. The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, and Bram set it aside the way they set aside all thoughts that fit too well.

The page came free.

It was written in Aldous Emberhand's own hand — Bram knew the script intimately, had spent years learning its slants and flourishes, the way the old man crossed his sevens like he was striking them down. It was dated three hundred years ago, to the season of the First Reforging. And it was not, as every history in the guild claimed, a record of the covenant's terms.

It was a bill of sale.

Bram read it twice. Then a third time, moving their lips, because some sentences refuse to be understood silently.

*Received of Meridian Winterbourne, bone-carver of the northern reach, one (1) covenant, sealed in her own femur, freely given and freely carved. In exchange, the Ember Guild agrees to hold in perpetual trust the line of Winterbourne as collateral against the covenant's renewal — that is to say, the Guild shall keep, protect, and provisionally dispose of all descendants of the said Meridian, such that in every century there shall be found among them one who belongs to both realms, who may be called upon to renew what she began.*

Collateral. Provisional disposal. Bram read the phrases again and felt something in their chest go very still and very cold, the way a room goes when a window opens onto winter.

Their family wasn't descended from the covenant's maker.

Their family *was* the covenant. A line of people, held in trust, generation after generation, like a debt passed hand to hand. Like a ring in a vault. Like the dead woman's whispering band Bram had appraised a week ago, before the world ended — *he never knew it was me*, she'd said, and Bram had recorded the secret in the ledger and closed the book and thought nothing of it.

Every entry they'd ever written. Every appraisal, every audit, every careful balancing of impossible accounts. They had been keeping the books on their own family's imprisonment and calling it a career.

And then Bram saw the last lines, and the cold went all the way through.

*The present keeper of accounts shall be instructed in all things EXCEPT this page. Let the heir learn the trade, love the trade, and be bound to the trade, so that when the century turns, the sacrifice will come willingly to the altar we have built around them.*

The heir. The present keeper of accounts.

Bram sat down on the archive floor with the page in their lap and laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh that echoed off the shelves and came back sounding like pity. Eleven years. Eleven years of late nights and good work and the quiet pride of being trusted with the guild's truest records. Trusted. Groomed. Led by the hand down a corridor of respectable duties toward a century's end and an open grave.

Master Corvane had taught them their first inventory. Had praised their ear for relics. Had given them the founding ledger's key on their twentieth birthday, saying, *You'll want to know where you come from.* Bram had thought it was sentiment.

It had been a delivery.

* * *

They didn't remember deciding to go to the guild master's study, but their feet apparently had opinions, and the opinion was confrontation. The lamps in the corridor still burned from the crisis nights, and their light lay along the floor in long yellow bars that Bram walked through like a person wading a river.

Corvane Ashgrave was awake, of course. He was always awake; Bram had privately suspected for years that the man slept standing up like a horse, or perhaps simply didn't bother, considering how little use he seemed to have for dreams. He sat at his desk with the refugee tallies spread before him and his cane across his knees, and when Bram entered without knocking he did not look surprised.

That was the worst part. He did not look surprised.

"Ah," he said. "You found the page."

Bram laid it on the desk between them, smoothing it flat with hands that only shook a little. "Three hundred years," they said. "My family. Held in trust. *Provisional disposal*, Master Ashgrave. Those are your founder's words. You gave me the key to that ledger myself."

"I did." Corvane folded his hands. He had the decency, at least, not to look away. "And I wondered, for eleven years, whether you'd ever press your gift against the spine. You listen to everything else, Winterbourne. Rings. Buttons. Teeth. I confess I half hoped you'd leave the book alone out of reverence, and half feared you would."

"You *hoped*—"

"I hoped you'd have a quiet life," Corvane said, and for one unguarded moment he sounded tired — old, even, older than his hundred-odd years, which everyone knew were a lie anyway. "I inherited this arrangement at forty, same as my predecessor, same as hers. I read that page and I was angry for a decade. And then the wards began to thin, and I learned the second thing Aldous never wrote down."

"Which is?"

"That anger doesn't hold a border." He tapped the page. "Meridian gave that bone gladly. Do you understand what that means? Not coerced. Not deceived. *Gladly.* And the covenant took her gladness into itself, and for three centuries the border between the living and the Hollow has held on the strength of one woman's willing heart, renewed each hundred years by another of her blood. When the willingness fails, the wards fail. When the wards fail—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the ceiling, at the starless night beyond it, at everything.

"So you raised me for slaughter politely."

"I raised you for *purpose*. There is a difference, and I suspect you're too honest with yourself not to know it." Corvane's voice gentled in a way that made it worse. "You hear the dead, child. You've heard them since you were six years old. You belong to both realms the way water belongs to the sea and the river both. I could have told you at twelve and watched you spend your whole life dreading the turn of the century. Or I could let you build a life worth the having, among people who valued you, doing work that mattered — and tell you when telling you could no longer break you."

"And instead the century broke early," Bram said. "The stars went out ahead of schedule. So much for your timing."

"Yes. That is... an inconvenience." Something flickered behind his eyes — not guilt, Bram realized. Calculation. "Which brings us to the matter at hand. The Stone is starving. The rite must be performed, sooner rather than later, and performed properly. A Winterbourne of both bloods, willing, at the Stone's heart. The company you rode north with has already volunteered to escort you."

"They escorted refugees home."

"Did they?" Corvane smiled faintly. "Wren Ashvale requested the assignment. She has been requesting assignments concerning you for years. Sentiment makes excellent camouflage — ask any ledger-keeper."

Bram's blood went hot and then cold again. Wren. Wren, who had stood beside them in the chapel while Hollow-things clawed at the doors. Wren, who had confessed her fallen wards like sins in the lamplight. Had she known? Had she been assigned to Bram the way a jailer is assigned a prisoner, all these years, the oath between them just another clause in the contract?

No. No — the oath had come first, before either of them understood what they were to each other. Whatever else was rotten, that was not. Bram clung to that with both hands.

"You should know," Corvane added, as Bram turned for the door, "that I am not unsympathetic. When the time comes, I will make it as gentle as the rite allows. And the guild will honor your memory in the ledgers — properly, this time. Under your own name, in your own hand."

"My own hand," Bram repeated. The absurdity of it rose up in them, sharp and bright, and they heard themselves say: "I'll bring my own pen."

Corvane blinked. "What?"

"Nothing, Master Ashgrave." Bram lifted the page from the desk and folded it into their coat, over their heart, where the important papers lived. "Just settling an account."

He let them go. That was the thing Bram would think about later, walking back through the yellow-lit corridors with the founder's confession against their ribs: he let them take the page. Either he no longer cared what they knew — or he wanted them to know it, wanted the weight of it, wanted the inheritance delivered whole.

*Cold clarity*, their grandmother used to call it, though she'd meant the weather. The moment when fear finishes burning off and what's left underneath is simply the shape of the truth, plain as a bone on a table, waiting for a carver.

Bram climbed back to the archive, relit the lamp, and sat down before the founder's ledger with the stolen page spread beside it. Three hundred years of entries stretched away in Aldous Emberhand's merciless script, and somewhere in those columns — Bram was certain of it now, certain the way they were certain of a relic's voice — somewhere in there was the final accounting. The exact terms of renewal. The price, itemized.

If the guild had built a trap out of bookkeeping, then bookkeeping was the tool that would spring it. Nobody knew these ledgers better than the keeper they'd groomed. Nobody alive could find a hidden page faster than a Winterbourne with a grudge and a lamp.

"Right," Bram said aloud to the empty archive, and the shelves full of listening objects seemed to lean in. "Let's see what else you've been hiding."

They turned to the final section of the founding volume, the pages reserved — according to the index — for *future provisions*. The paper there was stiffer, newer, handled rarely. And as Bram's fingers settled on the first line, every relic in the Singers, Unverified alcove began, softly and all at once, to hum.

The last page of the ledger named its price: *one belonging.*
