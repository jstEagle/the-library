## 7. A Debt Owed in One Belonging

The ledger's final page was written in ink that had gone brown with age, the way blood does when it dries into paper and decides to stay.

Bram found it at three in the morning, on their fourth pass through the founder's accounts, because the first three times they had stopped just short of it — some instinct older than sense pulling their hand away from the last gathering of pages like a child refusing the final spoonful of medicine. But ledgers do not forgive avoidance. Ledgers are honest in the way only debt can be honest.

*For the renewal of the Covenant,* the entry read, in Meridian Winterbourne's own hand — Bram knew it now, knew the way she crossed her sevens and left her flourishes half-finished, as if even her pen had known there would be no time to complete them — *the price is one life, freely offered, by a soul belonging wholly to neither realm and truly to both. The offering must come willingly. Willingness cannot be borrowed, bought, or bequeathed. Let no heir claim ignorance: this page exists so that you cannot.*

So much for ignorance.

Bram sat very still in the guild archive, surrounded by three hundred years of impossible accounting, and listened to the sound of their own heartbeat doing its ordinary, unremarkable work. It seemed almost rude of it to continue so calmly.

*A soul belonging wholly to neither realm and truly to both.* Their father had been born south of the wards, in the lit country, third son of a candlemaker. Their mother had crossed over from the Hollow during the last open years, before the border sealed itself into something closer to a scar. Bram had spent their whole life being told they stood with a foot in two worlds, usually by people who meant it as an insult. Colleagues who flinched when Bram touched a relic. Apprentices who invented errands rather than share a workroom. Even Master Corvane, who valued Bram's gift the way a merchant values a lockpick — useful, and never quite clean.

Half-blood. Hollow-touched. Both-ways Bram, someone had called them once, years ago, before the someone learned better.

It turned out the covenant had gotten there first. It had always gotten there first.

Bram closed the ledger with hands that were, they noted with distant professional approval, entirely steady. Grief could wait its turn. There were accounts to settle, and Bram Winterbourne had never once in their life let an account go unsettled.

* * *

The morning shift at the Ember Guild began, as it always did, with Tamsin the junior appraiser burning the porridge.

"You've made porridge again," Bram observed from the doorway of the guild kitchen, because observation was free and cruelty was not yet on today's schedule.

"I have made *porridge adjacent*," Tamsin said with dignity. "It has texture. Textures are fashionable."

"It has opinions. I can hear them."

Tamsin laughed — one of perhaps four people in the building who laughed at Bram's jokes instead of politely cataloguing them for later discussion in safer company. She slid a bowl across the table anyway, and Bram ate it, opinions and all, because it seemed important suddenly to eat while eating was simple. To sit in this kitchen with its copper pots and its smoke-stained ceiling and its smell of scorched grain, and simply be a person having breakfast.

"Big day?" Tamsin asked. "You've got your ledger face on."

"Do I have a ledger face?"

"You have several faces. Appraisal face, which is smug. Archive face, which is haunted. And ledger face, which is both at once but quiet about it."

"That is disturbingly accurate and I'd thank you to never describe me to anyone again." Bram set down the spoon. "Tamsin. If I asked you to take over my Tuesday consultations, would you?"

She blinked. "The ring appointments? Those are yours. Nobody else can hear them."

"Nobody else needs to. Most of what they say is 'tell my granddaughter I'm sorry' or 'I hid money in the garden and I regret nothing.' You can manage that. You'll want a shawl for the Kettering locket — she weeps."

"Bram—"

"Consider it a professional development opportunity. You complain constantly that I hoard the interesting work."

"I complain you hog the *interesting* relics," Tamsin said. "This sounds like you're handing me the whole drawer." Her eyes narrowed, and Bram watched her arrive at the edge of the right question and then, mercifully, step around it. "Is this about the wards? About the audit? Wren Ashvale came back asking after you twice yesterday, if that's what this is."

"It is not what this is." A lie, technically, since everything lately was at least partly about Wren, the way a river is partly about rain. "It is what this is: tidying. A person should tidy."

"A person," Tamsin repeated slowly, "should tidy."

"Exactly. You understand completely."

"I understand absolutely nothing, which I suspect is the design."

Bram kissed her forehead on the way out, which startled them both, and went to begin the longest day of settling affairs in guild history.

* * *

Returning relics turned out to be harder than acquiring them ever was.

The Ember Guild's vaults held eleven hundred registered items, and Bram had personally handled perhaps three hundred of them over the years — each one carrying its dead like a snail carries its shell, close and permanent and heavy. Guild policy held that relics belonged to the Guild, full stop; the grief of the bereaved was a market condition, not a moral consideration. Bram had signed off on that policy a thousand times without a second thought, because that was what competence looked like here: signatures, smooth and unexamined.

They started with the easy ones. Widow Marlowe's wedding band, which had spent six years whispering the same sentence — *the blue dress, tell her about the blue dress* — went home to a granddaughter who burst into tears in the doorway and then fed Bram tea and biscuits for two hours while the ring, resting warm against her collarbone, finally went quiet. The Kettering locket. The ferryman's coin that only ever hummed river-songs. A child's carved whistle that Bram carried out of the vault with both hands and returned to a mother who had gone grey waiting for it, and whose door stayed shut behind Bram afterward for a long time while Bram stood in the street learning how to breathe again.

By the end of the week, word had gotten around. Not the truth — never the truth — but a story shaped conveniently around the gaps. *Winterbourne's gone soft. Winterbourne's turning relic-heretic. Heard Winterbourne gave away the Aldous harp for nothing.* The guild master summoned them on Thursday.

Corvane received Bram in his office above the trading floor, a room designed to make visitors feel like inventory. He was a narrow man with narrow hands and a voice like a well-oiled hinge, and he had signed Bram's apprenticeship papers sixteen years ago, and he had known — the archive ledgers made it plain he had always known — what the family name was collateral for.

"Distributing assets," he said, when Bram declined to sit. "Unusual behavior for our senior bone-carver."

"Housekeeping."

"Our housekeeping has historically been profitable."

"Some things aren't for profit." Bram kept their voice level, pleasant, the voice they used for difficult clients. "Some things are just owed."

"To whom?"

"To everyone, eventually. That's rather the nature of debts, Master Corvane. You taught me that."

He studied them for a long moment with eyes like appraisal stamps. Bram felt the weight of that gaze and understood, with cold clarity, that he was trying to determine whether they had read the final page — whether the heir was waking up early. So Bram did what Bram did best with dangerous documents: they misfiled their expression, kept their face a neutral gray, and thought about porridge.

"Hmph," said Corvane at last. "See it doesn't interfere with the Covenant preparations. The audit continues. You and Keeper Ashvale will depart when the roads clear."

"Of course," Bram said, and bowed, and walked out through the trading floor where brokers shouted prices for memories and years and other people's names, and thought: *you will not get your rite, old man. Whatever happens on that road, it will not be the thing you filed under 'expected losses.'*

Then they went home and made a list.

* * *

The list lived in Bram's breast pocket and grew daily. *Return the Harrow bells. Close the account with Mother Adelheid. Find who has the deed to the workshop — give it to Tamsin, she'll pretend to argue, then take it. Write to Da. Write to Da properly, not the usual two lines and a joke about his knees.*

The writing to Da was the hardest line item, and Bram postponed it with the discipline of a master procrastinator, which is to say they cleaned the entire workshop instead, including the undersides of shelves that had not seen light since the previous century.

That was where Wren found them — on their knees under the workbench, dust in their hair, holding a jar of miscategorized teeth.

"You're cleaning," Wren said from the doorway.

"I'm organizing."

"There's a cobweb on your ear with ambitions."

"It's a tenant. I'm letting it stay through winter." Bram emerged, brushed themselves off, and became aware of the state of the room: every relic wrapped in linen and labeled. Every tool oiled and hung according to a system Wren would recognize, because Wren had helped invent it, years ago, in another life. Every surface bare except the ledger stand, wiped down, waiting.

Wren stood very still in all that tidiness, and Bram watched her read the room the way she read ward-stones — systematically, thoroughly, arriving at conclusions she didn't like.

"You gave back the Aldous harp," she said.

"It wanted to go home. It had been humming the same lullaby for forty years. Imagine."

"You refiled the eastern vaults."

"They were a disgrace."

"You gave Tamsin your Tuesday consultations." Wren stepped further in, boots silent on the floorboards, the way she moved — a keeper's habit, never startling anything, even the living. "And you wrote letters. Plural. I saw the wax. Bram, you hate writing letters. You told me once that letters were just thoughts wearing formal wear, and formal wear was a scam."

"I've matured."

"You've been *tidying*." She said the word like a diagnosis. "Since the archive. Since you went through the founder's ledgers alone at night — yes, I know, the archivist tells me things, I'm charming when I want to be — you've been putting your life in order the way people do when—"

"Don't." Bram heard their own voice come out flat and hard, a slammed drawer. "Don't finish that sentence."

Wren didn't. She stood there with the unfinished sentence hanging between them like a blade nobody had picked up yet, and Bram hated her a little for seeing it, and loved her considerably more than was convenient for noticing it, and wished desperately that love were the kind of debt one could quietly pay off and be done with.

"I'm going to make tea," Bram announced, with the dignity of the cornered.

"We don't have tea here."

"I'm going to go somewhere and acquire tea, and then make it."

"Bram."

"Wren." They faced her across the bare workbench, across sixteen years and one broken oath and whatever this was now, growing between them like green shoots through rubble. "When we ride into the Hollow, everything here will be in order. That's all. Some of us find comfort in order."

"And some of us," Wren said quietly, "find it in standing between the people we love and the thing that's coming. Which is what I intend to do. Whatever's on that final page — and I know there's a final page, Bram, I've seen you touch that ledger like it bites — whatever price it names, you don't pay it alone. That's not sentiment. That's the oath. It never lapsed. Not really. Neither of us could make it."

Bram's chest did something complicated and unspeakable. They picked up the jar of teeth instead.

"These need sorting," they said. "Stay for tea. I'll acquire some."

Wren looked at them for a long moment — and then, because she was Wren, because she had always known when to press and when to simply *stay*, she pulled up the second stool, the one with her old knife-marks still on it, and began sorting teeth.

But at the door, pausing, she said: "Whatever you're planning, plan better. Because I've read the old rite too, Bram. And if the covenant wants a willing sacrifice—" her jaw tightened "—then it will find two of us arguing over who loves the other more, and honestly, it deserves us."

She went to fetch water from the pump down the lane, leaving Bram alone with the jar and the dust motes and the sudden terrifying arithmetic of it: *one belonging*, and two souls who had never managed to belong anywhere but to each other.

Outside, the sky hung starless and wrong, and far to the north, faint as a held breath, Bram could feel the Covenant Stone cooling — patient, vast, counting down.

They took out the list. Beneath *write to Da*, in a hand that only shook a little, Bram added a new line:

*Tell Wren the truth. (Soon.)*

And beneath it, smaller:

*Learn to mean it.*
