## 8. The Road Into the Hollow

Wren Ashvale had kept watch over the border for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about goodbyes.

First: they were always said too quickly. Second: they were never really about the person leaving. And third — this was the lesson that mattered now, watching Bram Winterbourne fold their traveling cloak with the precision of a mortician laying out a body — a farewell that was too tidy was not a farewell at all. It was an accounting.

Bram had spent the last two days settling accounts.

Wren had noticed it the way she noticed everything about Bram: sideways, unwillingly, with her heart doing that stupid arrhythmic thing it did whenever Bram looked at her too long. She'd noticed Bram returning relics to the guild's vault with little notes attached — *to be held in trust for the family of Marla Vess, per original agreement*. She'd noticed Bram paying off the cobbler's tab from four years ago, and tipping the stable boy enough to make him bow twice. She'd noticed Bram giving away their good ink pot to an apprentice carver, saying, casually, as though handing someone your ink pot were a thing people just *did*, "You press too hard. This one forgives."

Nobody gave away their ink pot. Not a bone-carver. Ink was to a carver what a blade was to a soldier.

"You're being strange," Wren said, leaning in the doorway of Bram's workroom while the company loaded the wagons outside. "Stranger than usual."

"I'm packing." Bram didn't look up. Their hands moved through the tools with that quiet competence Wren had always both admired and resented — every chisel wrapped, every gauge nested, everything in its place. Too much in its place. A person who expected to come home packed like this. A person who expected to come home left things out, left them messy, left a chisel unwrapped because you'd need it tomorrow.

"Your desk is clear," Wren said.

"People like a clean desk."

"People don't have opinions about their own desks when they expect to sit at them again." Wren pushed off the doorframe and crossed the room before she could talk herself out of it. She picked up the ledger sitting square and centered on the blotter — the great Ember Guild ledger, the impossible-things ledger, Bram's life's work — and found it closed, ribboned, and labeled. *Final entries current through departure.*

*Departure.* Not *Tuesday*. Not *the journey north*. Departure.

Bram still hadn't looked up. That was how Wren knew she'd hit something true; Bram only went very calm and very busy when they were bleeding somewhere she couldn't see.

"Bram."

"Hm."

"What did the covenant ask for?"

The chisel-wrapping stopped. Just for a breath. Then it resumed, steady as ever, which was itself an answer.

"It asked," Bram said lightly, "for a lot of walking, apparently. Four hundred miles to the Covenant Stone through country where colors don't behave. It asked for good boots and better tempers. Yours especially."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you answer a question I haven't asked yet so I can't hear the truth in your voice when I finally get around to asking it."

Bram finally raised their head, and there it was — the smile. The guild smile, the appraiser's smile, warm and polished and worth precisely nothing. Wren hated that smile with a devotion that frightened her.

"Wren Ashvale," Bram said, "in eleven days of traveling together you will have ample opportunity to interrogate me. Save some questions for the road. It's a long way to the Hollow's heart, and conversation will be scarce once the dead start shouting."

"The dead are already shouting?"

Bram's hand drifted, almost without their noticing, to their own forearm — the left one, the one Wren had seen them favor since the wards fell, the one they rubbed absently the way a person rubs an old scar or listens to something buried under the skin. "They're always shouting," Bram said, softer. "I've just gotten better at pretending otherwise. You taught me that, actually."

That landed somewhere under Wren's ribs and stayed there. She wanted to say — well. There was a whole list of things she wanted to say, and every item on it had been rotting in her chest for nine years, and none of them were appropriate for a doorway in broad daylight with wagons creaking outside. So instead she said:

"If you die on this road, I'm haunting you. I'll learn the trick of it. I'll be insufferable."

Bram's real smile flickered through — quick, startled, gone again, but real. "You'd be terrible at it. You'd organize my ghost. Put me on a roster."

"Somebody has to. You certainly won't."

Outside, Master Corvane's voice carried across the yard, calling the company together, and the moment broke the way moments between the two of them always broke — cleanly, deliberately, like a bone set by a careful hand. Bram shouldered their pack. Wren took the ledger off the desk and handed it back, and their fingers touched on the leather binding for one second longer than the task required, and neither of them mentioned it, because neither of them had ever mentioned anything, and that was the whole trouble with them.

*

The company was small, which was either wisdom or arrogance depending on who you asked. Master Corvane stayed behind — "someone must keep the guild's accounts open," he'd said, and Wren had watched Bram absorb that sentence the way a wall absorbs a thrown stone, showing nothing — so it was the two of them, plus Hesper Dunn, a ward-keeper's apprentice with freckles, boundless optimism, and a crossbow she'd named Geraldine; plus Old Tamsin, a guide who had crossed into the Hollow twice in her youth and come back with white hair and a permanent squint, as though the sun had personally offended her.

"Rules," Tamsin announced from the lead wagon as they passed the last ward-stone of the lit world. It stood grey and humming in the morning fog, and beyond it the land sloped down into a mist that wasn't weather. Wren felt the old ache in her hands — the phantom pull of wards she used to hold, back before the disgrace, before the stripping of her license, before everything. "Rule one: nothing you see in the Hollow is lying, exactly. Rule two: nothing you see in the Hollow is telling the truth, exactly. Rule three—"

"Geraldine handles rule three," Hesper said cheerfully, patting the crossbow.

Tamsin ignored her. "Rule three: whatever asks you for your name, your memory, or your mother's face — you walk faster."

Wren rode beside Bram's wagon and watched them watch the border fall away behind them. They weren't sad, exactly. That was the thing she couldn't stop turning over. Sadness would have been fine. Sadness she could have worked with. But Bram looked like a merchant closing a shop — satisfied, finished, each day behind them a drawer successfully shut. Every mile they traveled seemed to lighten them, and Wren could not decide whether that was peace or preparation, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

At midday they halted by a stream that ran, impossibly, uphill for a few feet before thinking better of it. While Hesper filled skins and Tamsin dozed sitting up, Wren sat down next to Bram on the wagon's tailboard, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched.

"Give me your arm," she said.

"My arm is attached to me."

"Give it anyway."

A pause. Then, with elaborate unconcern, Bram unbuttoned the cuff and rolled the sleeve. The forearm underneath was marked with faint lines — old carving scars from decades of the craft — but beneath them, new and pale as fish-belly, ran a thin seam of discoloration, like a crack in porcelain glazed over.

"When did this happen?" Wren kept her voice level. Her thumb hovered over the mark without touching it. Even hovering, she could feel it: a cold coming off the skin like the draft from a cellar door.

"After the stars went out." Bram said it the way other people reported the weather. "It doesn't hurt."

"Everything hurts. That's what pain is for. It's how the body files complaints."

"How poetic. Did you write that on a training poster?"

"They made me take the posters down." Wren took Bram's wrist — gently, the way she'd handle a ward-stone with a fault line running through it — and turned the arm toward the light. The mark was worse than it had been three days ago. She was sure of it. It had lengthened, branching slightly at the top like the first fork of a river. "This is the covenant, isn't it. In your bones. It's been in your family's bones, and now that it's failing—"

"Now that it's failing, it's letting go of what it held." Bram drew the sleeve down, unhurried, and buttoned the cuff with neat little motions. "Or trying to. Bones are honest material, Wren. They remember their debts. It's why I can hear the dead through them — and it's why, when the debt comes due—" They stopped themselves. Smiled the guild smile. "When the debt comes due, we'll pay it. That's what the ledgers are for."

And there it was again. *We'll pay it.* Singular, wearing plural clothing.

Wren looked at this person she had loved for nine years without permission and without success, this person who catalogued griefs the way other people catalogued silverware, and she understood — with the sudden sick clarity of a keeper watching a ward-stone go dark — that Bram had already read ahead to the last page of the covenant. Had probably read it weeks ago, alone, at night, in that clear desk-lamped workroom. Had already entered it in some private ledger. Was already, right now, on this bright ordinary road, quietly spending the coin of their own remaining days: giving away ink pots, settling cobbler's tabs, wrapping every chisel.

*I swear,* Wren thought, and it wasn't a prayer because she'd stopped believing anyone was listening, but it had the shape of one anyway. *I will find another way. Whatever the terms are, whatever the Stone wants, I will find a door it hasn't checked. I did not survive losing my license and my name and my pride just to lose you to a contract written three hundred years ago by men who thought sacrifice was a filing system.*

"I'm going to check your arm every day," she said aloud. "Twice. Morning and night. If it grows so much as a hair, I want to know."

"That's very romantic," said Hesper, walking past with the water skins. Both of them jumped. "Don't mind me. I see everything. It's the freckles — people assume freckles mean harmless." She wandered off, humming.

Bram was laughing — actually laughing, shoulders shaking, the sound startled out of them like a bird from cover — and Wren decided she would trade quite a lot to keep that going. Quite a lot indeed.

*

They crossed at dusk, because Tamsin insisted crossings went easier in the hour when neither lamp nor star ruled the sky, though tonight there were no stars at all to contend with — just a sky like a slate wiped clean, which everyone pretended not to find horrifying.

The border itself was not a wall. Wren had always known this intellectually; standing inside it was another matter. It was more like a membrane of attention — a place where the world became aware of you and considered, briefly, whether you belonged. As they walked through it, Wren felt it pass over her like a slow hand. Colors thinned first. The green of the grass drained upward out of the blades, then the brown drained out of the soil, until the whole world was rendered in greys and silvers, like ash after a fire that had burned everything worth burning.

Then the inversion began.

The mist ahead glowed faintly where it should have shadowed; shadows pooled where light fell. Hesper's red hair went black as ink. Tamsin's squint deepened to a scowl. And the silence — the silence was the worst part, because it wasn't silent at all. It was full. Full the way a riverbed looks empty and isn't.

The dead grew loud.

Wren heard it first as a pressure in her ears, then as something almost like language, then — as the crossing completed and the Hollow opened around them, vast and dim and patient — as voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not screaming, which would have been bearable. Murmuring. Conversing. Going about their unlives in the middle distance, visible now as pale suggestions between the trees: figures seated at tables that weren't there, figures walking roads that had dissolved, a child chasing a dog made of dusk.

Beside her, Bram stopped walking.

Wren saw them grip the wagon rail. Saw their eyes go wide and wet and fixed on some middle distance no one else occupied. Their lips moved — counting? answering? — and the pale seam on their forearm, where the cuff had fallen open, shone faintly through the sleeve like a coal breathing under ash.

"Bram." Wren caught their elbow. "Hey. Stay with the living. That's the job."

"They're so *loud* here," Bram whispered. There was wonder in it and terror in equal measure, and something else underneath, something that sounded almost like homecoming, and that frightened Wren more than either. "Not shouting. Singing. Wren — they're singing. All of them. They've been waiting so long, and they know—"

"They know what?"

Bram blinked. Closed their mouth. Opened it again, and produced, carefully, the guild smile — but it wobbled at one corner, and for once they couldn't hold it straight. "Nothing. Nothing important. Border gossip."

Ahead, Tamsin had halted the wagons. Beyond her, where the road bent down into a valley full of soft grey lights strung like lanterns among dead trees, the murmur of the Hollow gathered itself into something almost like a crowd. A market, Wren realized. Stalls. Voices. Trade. Her hand went to her blade out of pure habit.

"Market of Whispers," Tamsin said grimly, spitting to the side in a manner she probably believed was protective. "We camp above it tonight. Tomorrow we bargain our way through. And remember rule three—"

"Walk faster," Hesper and Wren said together.

But Bram was looking down into those lights with an expression Wren recognized from ledgers and appraisals and every quiet calculation they'd ever made — the look of a person pricing something. Pricing it honestly, thoroughly, down to the last copper.

And Wren Ashvale stood beside them in the inverted dark, her hand on her sword and her oath burning in her chest like an ember that refused, absolutely refused, to go out, and swore again — silently, fiercely, in the grammar of a love she still couldn't say aloud — that whatever price the Hollow was about to name, she would find a way to pay it first.
