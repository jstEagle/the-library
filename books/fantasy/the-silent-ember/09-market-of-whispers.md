## 9. Market of Whispers

The Hollow did not announce itself. That was the first thing Bram learned about it — no gate, no threshold, no dramatic tearing of the veil such as the penny dreadfuls at the guild described. The world simply thinned, like broth stretched too far, and then they were walking in a country made of the space between things.

The sky above was the color of a held breath. No stars — of course no stars; the stars were gone everywhere now, but here their absence felt less like catastrophe and more like policy, as if the sky had been swept clean on purpose. The ground underfoot was pale and slightly soft, like old candle wax, and it gave off a faint warmth that Bram found deeply unsettling until they realized why: everything here remembered being lit from within.

"You're limping," Wren said, falling into step beside them.

"I'm favoring my left ankle, which is a completely different medical condition."

"It's the same condition."

"It has dignity."

Wren huffed something that was almost a laugh and didn't press. Three days out from the last village of the lit world, and they had settled into a rhythm that Bram distrusted precisely because it felt so easy: her stride, their stride, the small economy of shared silence. The rest of the company trailed behind — two guild porters named Hesk and Odile who had volunteered for double hazard pay and were already regretting it, and Mother Cass, the chapel-keeper from the border village, who had refused with terrifying politeness to be left behind.

"There," Cass said suddenly, pointing ahead.

Bram saw nothing at first. Then the fog — which was not fog but more a gathering of unfinished light — shifted, and shapes resolved: tents of grey silk strung between trees that grew upside down, roots clawing at a sky they seemed to distrust. Lanterns hung everywhere, hundreds of them, each burning with a different-colored flame, and beneath the lanterns moved figures. Dozens. Hundreds.

A market.

"The ledgers mention these," Bram said quietly. "Memory markets. I always assumed they were allegory." They touched the ledger satchel at their hip — the one constant of their life, the Ember Guild's accounts, impossible things bought and sold. "I assumed most of the guild's stranger entries were metaphor."

"And now?"

"Now I'm considering the possibility that the entire profession of accounting is a lie we tell ourselves about magic."

* * *

The market smelled of rain on hot stone and something sweeter underneath, like the ghost of bread. The vendors called out in voices that arrived a half-second before their mouths moved, and what they sold was displayed in bowls, bottles, and open hands: memories, pressed and gleaming like river stones. A woman with a shawl of moth wings offered a memory of learning to swim, the terror and triumph both, for three coins of spun dark. A man whose face kept forgetting its own arrangement sold summers. Whole summers. Bram watched a young hollow-thing — not monstrous, merely translucent, like a person seen through frosted glass — buy one and swallow it, and the creature's outline sharpened with joy for a moment before fading again.

"Don't stare," Wren murmured.

"I'm an appraiser. Staring is my profession."

"Then appraise quietly."

They needed passage. The road to the Covenant Stone ran through the market and past it into country where the paths rearranged themselves when unobserved, and the market's ferrymen — gaunt figures with poles of white bone — were the only ones who could hold a route steady. But the ferrymen took no coin of metal or promise. Here, currency was memory, and Bram had known this since the plan was drawn, had told themselves they were prepared for it.

Prepared, it turned out, was a word people used about other people's griefs.

The broker they approached worked from a stall shaped like an upturned boat. She was elderly in the way of the Hollow — not aged but worn smooth, like a stone that had been handled by many generations — and she looked at Bram's face with an expression of professional kindness that was somehow worse than greed.

"Passage for five to the Stone's shadow," she said. "That's dear, traveler. That's very dear indeed. What do you carry?"

Bram opened their satchel — the ledger first, out of habit, and the broker actually recoiled.

"Not that," she said sharply. "Never bookkeeping. A life measured in entries makes the poorest coin. Something loved. Something *yours*. That's the only currency that holds weight here."

Behind Bram, Wren shifted her weight. "We can split it," she said. "Everyone gives a little—"

"A little buys a little." The broker's eyes were kind and pitiless. "Five souls, three days' passage through shifting country? You'll want to pay whole. One good memory each, or one extraordinary one between you." She turned back to Bram. "You have the look of someone carrying something extraordinary."

Bram did not answer at once, because they were busy doing the thing they did best and hated most: inventorying.

There was the day their grandmother taught them to carve, flour-dusted kitchen, the first curve of a spoon taking shape under their nine-year-old hands — precious, but it belonged to the craft, and the craft would need every scrap of itself for what was coming. There was the morning of their guild certification, the master's rare nod. Smaller things. A snowfall that smelled of woodsmoke. A dog whose name they could no longer quite —

No. Not the dog. Some lines even desperation shouldn't cross.

And then, without permission, the memory rose: a summer afternoon eight years ago, the year of the oath, before the breaking. Wren — younger, still licensed, still laughing easily — sprawled on the roof of the ward-keeper's station because she'd declared the view superior to any chair ever carved. They'd shared a stolen bottle of plum wine and argued about whether the northern lights were the breath of the dead or merely weather, and Wren had said, with her mouth crooked in that way that meant she was about to say something reckless, *if the dead are breathing all that light, then dying might be the best thing anyone does.* And Bram had laughed so hard they nearly rolled off the roof, and Wren had caught their wrist without looking, reflex-fast, and held on a beat longer than balance required, and neither of them had mentioned it, and Bram had thought about it for approximately six years afterward.

Eight years of thinking about an afternoon. That was the arithmetic of love, done badly, in secret, at compound interest.

"Bram." Wren's voice, low. "Whatever you're about to give — don't give anything you can't afford."

"That's not how markets work."

"It's how *we* work."

Bram met her eyes. Grey, sharp, worried in a way she'd deny under oath. And Bram thought: *I am walking toward a stone that will ask me for everything. This is practice. Better to lose this gently, by choice, than to have it taken later.*

"This one," they said to the broker.

* * *

The trading was quick and clinical, which Bram resented. The broker held out a small glass phial shaped like a teardrop, and Bram was instructed to hold it against the temple and *let go of the shape of the day*. Which was rather like being told to let go of the shape of one's own hand.

They hesitated only once. Then they thought of the covenant, of Meridian Winterbourne's bone holding up the sky for three hundred years, of the ledger entry that lived behind their ribs now: *one belonging, willingly given.* Practice, they told themselves. Practice.

The memory left like a long exhale.

And then Bram stood in the market with the afternoon gone — truly gone, a hole with edges — and understood, in the specific and merciless way of experience rather than imagination, what they had done. Because the hole had a shape. And the shape of the hole was *Wren*. An absence exactly her-sized, sitting where the plum wine and the rooftop and the caught wrist used to be. Bram knew something had been there; they could read the margins of their own mind the way they read any ledger, and the ledger said: *one summer afternoon, roof of the ward-keeper's station, value incalculable, paid in full.*

But the contents of the entry were smoke.

"What did you give?" Wren asked. She had watched the whole transaction with her jaw set, arms crossed, radiating disapproval like a stove.

"I don't recall," Bram said, aiming for lightness and missing badly.

"That's not funny."

"No," Bram agreed. "It really isn't." They tested the absence the way one tests a missing tooth, probing the gap. There was a feeling attached to the hole, curiously enough — the memory was gone but its residue remained, the way a removed portrait leaves a paler rectangle on the wall. The feeling was warm. It was fond. It ached in a location Bram couldn't name, somewhere near the sternum, slightly left. It felt like missing someone without knowing whom.

They looked at Wren and felt it again and thought, distantly: *oh. Oh, that's who I've lost. I've lost her twice now — once by choice just now, and once years ago by cowardice, and I can't remember the first loss but I can feel it, like a tooth gone, like a portrait gone.*

"Bram." Wren had stepped closer. Her hand hovered near their elbow, not touching. "You've gone white as bone. Sit down."

"I'm fine. Passage acquired. We should — the ferryman—"

"You gave her something about *me*."

It wasn't a question, which meant Bram's face had answered it already. They said nothing, which confirmed everything.

Wren stared at them for a long moment, and something moved across her expression — anger and hurt and something fiercer underneath, all fighting for the door at once. "We'll get it back," she said finally, flatly, in the tone she probably used for declaring walls secure. "After the Stone. However it works, whatever it costs, we buy it back. Put it in your ledger. Write it down. *One memory, owed to Bram Winterbourne, redeemable upon completion of the covenant.*"

"You can't owe someone a feeling they no longer miss."

"Watch me."

Mother Cass appeared at Wren's shoulder with the timing of a woman who had spent thirty years appearing beside people right before they said something unforgivable. "The ferryman's asking for us," she said mildly. "Also, child, you're about to commit violence in a market of the dead, and I'd advise saving it."

* * *

The ferryman was waiting at the market's far edge, where the tents gave out onto a plain of grey grass that chimed faintly in a windless air, like distant bells heard underwater. He was tall and folded-up, his pole of white bone worn glossy at the grip by centuries of other travelers' passages, and when he spoke, his voice came from somewhere around everyone simultaneously rather than from him specifically.

"Five souls," he said. "One paid whole." His gaze — two points of lantern-light where eyes should be — settled on Bram. "The carven one pays dearest. They always do."

"We're ready," Wren said.

"No one is ready," the ferryman said, not unkindly. "Ready is a living-country word." He turned and began to walk, and the grey grass parted before him, chiming.

The crossing was strange and mostly silent. The country thinned further as they went; colors inverted — the grass went black where the sun would be, the sky glowed faintly at the horizon like the inside of a shell — and the dead grew loud. That was the phrase Bram had written in their notes before departure and it proved inadequate. The dead were not loud as crowds are loud. They were loud as meaning is loud: voices arriving inside rather than through the ears, dozens of them, hundreds, murmuring at the edge of comprehension. For most people, Bram gathered, it was like tinnitus of the soul. For Bram, it was like walking into a room where everyone knew their name.

Because the dead knew them. Word traveled here, apparently, at the speed of reputation.

*Winterbourne,* whispered a voice like dry leaves. *The carver's heir.*

*Ledger-keeper,* said another, warmer, with a laugh in it. *You balanced my account in '61. My widow thanks you, though she never knew to.*

*Bonespeaker. Bonespeaker. Little listener.*

Hesk, one of the porters, had gone the color of tallow and walked with his eyes fixed firmly on his boots. Odile had simply stopped speaking altogether. Only Cass seemed comfortable, nodding along to the voices like a woman hearing hymns slightly out of tune.

And then, cutting through the murmur, one voice arrived clear and close and cold, and the chime-grass went silent around them as if listening too.

*The Stone remembers its carver.*

Bram stopped walking.

The voice was old — older than the guild, older perhaps than the wards — and it did not whisper so much as state, the way bedrock states its position. It came from everywhere and nowhere and possibly from below, from the direction they were traveling.

"What does that mean?" Wren asked. She had heard it too; everyone had; Hesk made a small noise and pretended he hadn't.

*I think,* Bram said slowly, *it means the Stone knows who's coming. Has always known. Was waiting.* They looked down at their own hands — carver's hands, scarred, competent, inherited — and felt the full weight of the family business settle onto their shoulders like a yoke fitted three hundred years ago and only now delivered.

"Waiting for what?" Wren's voice had an edge on it now. She had gone very still, in the way she went still before drawing a blade.

"For its heir, presumably." Bram tried to make it sound like a joke. It came out sounding like a eulogy.

The ferryman paused and half-turned, lantern-eyes flickering. "Mind the warning, carven one," he said. "The Stone does not forget. Not the bone that built it. Not the blood that runs in it. Not the debt written into either." He resumed walking. The grey grass chimed shut behind him like a curtain.

They made camp that night — though *night* was a courtesy; the sky merely dimmed from held-breath-grey to held-breath-black — in the lee of a fallen standing stone that hummed faintly when touched. Cass cooked. Hesk recovered the power of speech. Odile asked Bram, in the careful tone of someone approaching a shrine, whether it was true that they could hear the dead speak through relics, and Bram said yes, and she asked what it was like, and Bram considered lying and didn't.

"It's like being the only person in a library who can hear the books."

Odile thought about this. "Do the books mind?"

"Sometimes. Mostly they're grateful someone's reading." Bram wrapped their cloak tighter. "It's the unread ones that grieve."

Across the fire, Wren sat sharpening a blade she did not need to sharpen, watching Bram with an intensity she probably believed was covert. She had not let the matter drop. Of course she hadn't. Somewhere in that stubborn, honorable, mule-headed heart of hers, a new line item had been entered — *one memory, owed* — and Wren Ashvale honored her debts with the relentlessness of water finding stone.

She didn't know, Bram reflected, watching the firelight move on her face, that she was guarding a hole. That the person she was glaring protectively at was missing a piece exactly her size and would go on missing it — cheerfully, functionally, unaware — all the way to the Stone and possibly past it. That Bram had chosen the loss deliberately, as practice, as tuition, and would choose it again, and that this was perhaps the cruelest thing about sacrifice: how much of it you can do to yourself and call it prudence.

The dead murmured around the camp like relatives at a wake, and one of them — the leaf-dry voice, or maybe another, they blurred together — said softly:

*What you sold, little listener, was not yours alone to sell.*

Bram lay awake a long time after that, listening to the grey country breathe, feeling the portrait-shaped absence near their sternum, and composing, in the dark, the first line of a ledger entry they could not yet write:

*Owed: one afternoon, to one Wren Ashvale, whether she knows it or not. Interest accruing. Payable at the Stone.*

Two days later, the grey grass ended at the banks of a river that did not flow with water, and the dead began to sing.
