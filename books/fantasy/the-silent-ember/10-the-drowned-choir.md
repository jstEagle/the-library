## 10. The Drowned Choir

The river appeared on the third morning out from the crossing, and it was the wrongest thing Wren Ashvale had ever seen.

She had grown up on rivers. Real ones — brown, chattering things that carried leaves and occasionally a very indignant duck. This was not that. This was water the color of old pewter, flat as hammered tin, and it did not flow. It *hung*. The surface held still in a way that made her teeth ache, and when she leaned over the bank to look at her reflection, she saw not her own face but a smear of light where a face should be, like someone had pressed a thumb into wet clay.

"Right," said Ferro, the guild's hired muscle, who had thus far survived the Hollow by refusing to think about any of it. "That's a river. Sure. Fine. I've seen weirder. I saw a tree last night that apologized to me."

"It did not apologize," said Maren, the cartographer, flatly.

"It made a sound like 'sorry.' I'm counting it."

Wren let them argue. She was watching Bram, because watching Bram had become, over three days of walking through a country where the sky was the color of a bruise healing, something close to a habit she couldn't break. They stood at the bank with their satchel of relics open, holding one bone carving after another up to the air above the water, listening.

"What are they saying?" Wren asked finally.

Bram tilted their head, which was what they did when the voices came in layers, like an orchestra tuning before anyone had asked for music. "Everything," they said. "All at once. It's—" they paused, hunting for the word, "—it's a choir. A drowned choir. Every voice that ever crossed this river and didn't make it to the other side. They're all still here. Suspended." Their fingers tightened around a small carved sparrow, one of the few relics they hadn't yet returned to its proper resting place. "They're not suffering, exactly. It's more like... waiting. The way notes wait inside a held breath."

"And how do we get across?" Ferro asked. "Because I'll say now, I'm not swimming in that."

"We don't swim," Bram said. "We ask."

* * *

Asking, it turned out, took most of the afternoon.

Bram sat cross-legged at the bank like a merchant negotiating a toll, and slowly, voice by voice, the choir sorted itself. Wren watched them work with the particular admiration of someone who had spent years maintaining wards and knew exactly how much patience invisible labor required. Bram would hold up a relic, listen until one voice rose clear of the mass, speak to it softly — *yes, I hear you; no, I can't carry you home today, but I can carry your name* — and then set the relic down and move to the next. It looked, Wren thought uncharitably, like watching someone answer four hundred letters all marked URGENT, without once losing their temper.

"You're enjoying this," she accused, somewhere around hour two.

"I'm being *useful*," Bram corrected, with the serene smugness of a person who had been told their whole life that hearing the dead was a curse and had recently discovered it could also be a profession. "There's a difference. A large one. You'd know that if you'd ever been useful."

"I kept the border wards standing for six years."

"And then you didn't," Bram said, not cruelly, but not gently either, and Wren shut up, because they were right, and because some wounds you didn't poke just to win an argument on a riverbank in hell.

By dusk, the choir had agreed to terms. The dead would part — actually part, the way curtains part, the suspended voices drawing aside into two slow walls of silvered sound — and the company would walk the riverbed itself, dry-shod, along a path the drowned voices would hold open. In exchange: Bram would carry the names of nine voices who had been waiting longest, speak them aloud on the far shore, and give them "a door's worth of attention," which was apparently a unit of measurement among the dead and which Bram refused to explain further.

"A door's worth," Ferro repeated skeptically. "Of attention."

"Some debts are paid in coin," Bram said mildly, packing up. "Some are paid in being listened to. Guess which kind the dead prefer."

They crossed at dawn. Walking the riverbed was every bit as strange as promised — the parted choir arched overhead like the inside of a cathedral built entirely of sound, each voice a faint glimmering thread, and beneath their feet the riverbed was pale stone worn smooth as glass. Maren mapped it feverishly. Ferro walked with his eyes closed for the first hundred paces, opened them, decided the ceiling of voices was actually quite beautiful, and thereafter walked with his eyes closed anyway, out of habit.

It should have been peaceful. For about an hour, it almost was.

Then the faces started.

* * *

They came out of the walls of sound the way fish rise to a lure — slowly at first, then all at once. Threads of the choir thickened, braided, condensed, and stepped free of the river's architecture wearing shapes. Shapes with faces. Wren saw the first one before anyone else did, because she had spent six years learning to read the boundary between the living world and the Hollow, and she knew the exact quality of wrongness that preceded a hollowing: a shimmer, a hesitation, a thread of song going suddenly, deliberately silent.

"Ferro," she said quietly. "Behind us. Don't look directly at it yet."

Ferro looked directly at it, because he was Ferro.

"That's Guild Master Ondrel," he said. "Or — no. Wait. Ondrel's alive. He was at the hall last month. That's..." He squinted. "That's Ondrel's face on something that is not Ondrel."

"No," Wren said, and her stomach went cold and heavy as a dropped anchor. "It isn't. Look again. Look at the *older* face underneath."

Ferro looked again and made a noise like a man swallowing a whistle.

Because he was right, in the way people are sometimes right about the worst possible thing. The shape wore Ondrel's face — the current guild master, hale and living and safely back in the lit world — but beneath it, translucent as a watermark, was another face entirely: gaunt, bearded, long dead. Aldous Vane. Third master of the Ember Guild. Dead ninety-one years. Wren knew him from the founder portraits in the guild hall, the ones she'd dusted as an apprentice while the older keepers told stories about him that were half legend and half threat.

And there were more coming. Out of the choir's walls, one after another: wraiths wearing the faces of the guild's dead founders, each one layered under the borrowed face of someone still living. Vane under Ondrel. Corvessa Marsh, first mistress of ledgers, under Maren's mentor. Old Tam Hollis, the first ward-keeper, under — Wren realized with a lurch — *her*. Her own face, or near enough, floating on a wraith's shoulders like a mask hung slightly crooked.

"They're wearing us," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle, because there was no other way to say it. "They're wearing our faces over the faces of the founders. Like they're trying to be recognized. Like they're trying to be *claimed*."

"Or they're hunting us," Ferro said, drawing his blade, "and this is the creepiest possible way to do it, which, honestly, tracks."

The company backed together, backs to the center of the riverbed path. There was nowhere to go — forward lay the far shore, hours off; backward, the crossing; overhead, ten thousand suspended voices that had, moments ago, been singing them safe passage. The wraiths drifted closer, unhurried, terrible, their borrowed faces slack and their true faces — the founders' faces — twisted in expressions Wren couldn't parse. Grief? Anger? Hunger?

"Hold the line," she heard herself say, and hated how automatic it sounded, how much it belonged to the keeper she used to be. "Whatever they do, don't break formation. Maren, get behind me. Ferro, left flank. And somebody keep Bram—"

But Bram wasn't behind the line.

Bram had stepped *forward*, out past Wren's shoulder, straight toward the thing wearing Tam Hollis under Wren's own face, and stood there in the open with their head tilted, listening.

"Bram!" Wren's heart did something complicated and painful. "Get back here!"

"In a moment," Bram said calmly, which was the single most infuriating phrase in any language, and raised the carved sparrow like a lantern.

* * *

What happened next, Wren watched from six feet away with a drawn blade and no idea what to do with it, which was, she reflected later, a fairly accurate portrait of her entire relationship with Bram Winterbourne.

The wraiths stopped.

Not all at once — the nearest one froze first, then the next, then the rest, like a wave deciding mid-curl that it would rather be a wall. They hung there at the edge of blade-reach, close enough that Wren could see the seams where living faces had been stretched over dead ones, and they did not attack. They *leaned*. All of them, the whole ragged founding court of the Ember Guild, leaning toward Bram the way plants lean toward a window.

"They're talking," Bram said. Their voice had gone strange — low, careful, the voice they used when the words coming through a relic were too big to rush. "Wren, put the sword down."

"Absolutely not."

"Wren." They turned, and their eyes were wet, which frightened Wren more than the wraiths had. "They're not attacking. They never were. Listen — no, you can't listen, that's the whole problem, so *believe me*. They're begging."

"Begging." Ferro's blade did not lower. "Begging for what?"

"For us to stop." Bram's hands were shaking. Wren had never seen Bram's hands shake, not when appraising a ring full of a murdered woman's last secret, not when the stars went out, not once in all the years she'd known them, and seeing it now undid something in her chest. "Every founder in that choir — Vane, Marsh, Hollis, all of them — they're saying the same thing. They're saying: *we built the covenant. We sealed the first terms. We wrote the sacrifice into the ledger.* And they're saying—" Bram's breath caught. "They're saying they can feel what the guild intends. They felt it the moment we entered the Hollow. They know why we're really here. They know what the ledger says the price is. And they're begging us not to let it happen again."

The riverbed was very quiet. Even the choir overhead had gone still, ten thousand voices holding one enormous breath.

"They're wearing our faces," Wren said slowly, working it through, horror curdling into something else, something worse, "because they're showing us. Showing us what we look like. What the guild looks like. Living faces stacked on top of the dead who paid for them." She looked at the wraith bearing her own features over Tam Hollis's ruined ones, and Tam Hollis's expression, now that she truly looked, was not hunger. It was pity. Pity aimed at her, from ninety years deep, from inside the machine she'd served. "Oh," she said. "*Oh.*"

"They're not monsters," Bram whispered. "They're *warnings*. The founders came back as warnings. The only way they could still speak."

One of the wraiths — Corvessa Marsh, under Maren's mentor's face — extended a hand. Not grasping. Offering. And drifting in the air above its palm, faint as breath on glass, was a shape Wren recognized instantly: a ledger page. Blank. Waiting.

"It wants to tell us something," Bram said. "About the accounts. About what the renewals *cost*, versus what they returned. Wren, I have to take it. I have to touch it. I know what it looks like. I know what you're afraid of. But if I'm right — if these are the founders and not their hollowed shells — then this is the closest thing to help we're going to get in this entire country, and it walked three days to find us."

"That's the problem with you," Wren said, and it came out angrier than she meant it, angrier than she'd meant anything in years, because anger was the only honest language she had left and she'd been saving it up since the oath broke. "That's the *problem* with you, Bram. You always walk toward the thing that frightens everyone else, and you always come back, and I always stand here with a sword pretending I helped."

"Then stand here with a sword," Bram said gently, "and pretend you helped, and I will come back, and we will both keep pretending, and someday maybe one of us will stop." They reached for the offered page. "Watch my back while I do it. That's real. That counts."

So Wren held the line — properly this time, not the reflexive line of a disgraced keeper but a chosen one, blades up, formation tight, guarding Bram's back against wraiths that no longer seemed to want anything but to be heard. And Bram touched the ghost-page, and read it, and what they read changed the color of everything ahead of them.

"The renewals were never balanced," Bram said, reading aloud to the company, to the choir, to the dead themselves. "Every century, the Stone gave less than it was given. The guild recorded the sacrifices as income. Income." Their voice shook. "They booked souls as *revenue*. And the founders — the real founders, underneath — they're telling me the original covenant was never meant to work this way. It was meant to be a door. Someone turned it into a ledger."

Corvessa Marsh's wraith nodded, slow and sorrowful, and dissolved — not into nothing, but back into the choir, her thread rejoining the walls of sound, and as she went, every voice in the river sang one chord, brief and clear, that Wren felt in her sternum like a hand laid flat over her heart.

The remaining wraiths withdrew the same way, one by one, each pausing beside Bram long enough to press something into the air between them — a name, a date, a fragment of the true accounts — before folding back into the silvered dark. Last of all came the one wearing Wren's face over Tam Hollis. It stopped in front of her, specifically her, and looked at her with its own dead eyes through the mask of her living ones, and it said — Bram translated, because of course it spoke in a language only Bram could hear —

"*The keeper who guards the carver's back has already begun to keep again.*"

Wren stood there a long moment with her sword still up and her hands doing something embarrassing, and finally said, "Tell him thank you," in a voice that barely worked, and Tam Hollis smiled — the dead face, under hers — and was gone.

* * *

They made the far shore by late afternoon, dry-shod, carrying nine names and a head full of fragments of the true accounts, and Bram spent the evening speaking each name aloud exactly as promised, giving each voice its door's worth of attention while Ferro cooked something optimistic over a fire that burned blue and gave off no heat and Maren updated the map with a new landmark labeled, in careful script, RIVER OF SUSPENDED VOICES (DO NOT SWIM) (THEY WILL HEAR YOU).

When the ninth name was spoken, Bram sat back down beside Wren on a shelf of pale stone and didn't say anything for a while, and Wren didn't make them.

"You held the line," Bram said eventually.

"I held nothing. They weren't attacking."

"You couldn't have known that. *I* couldn't have known that, not until I stepped out and listened, and stepping out was the dangerous part, and you covered it. That's what holding a line means, Wren. It doesn't mean the danger comes. It means you stand there anyway, so the person walking toward it knows someone's behind them." A pause. "You've been behind me for three days. I've noticed."

Wren looked at the starless sky, at the bruised-dark country, at the road ahead bending toward the Covenant Stone and whatever waited there, and said, "I'm going to keep noticing, then," which was as close to anything tender as she could manage with her whole chest still aching from a dead man's pity, and Bram smiled at her like that was enough, and maybe it was.

Then Bram opened the satchel, and spread the fragments of the true accounts across the stone, and began arranging them the way they arranged everything — edges aligned, chronology strict — and went very still over the last piece.

"Wren," they said quietly. "This fragment. It's dated. It's dated *this year*. These accounts aren't history. Someone in the lit world is still keeping them. Someone is still booking the losses." They looked up, and the firelight caught the fear in their face, plain and unhidden at last. "The guild master didn't just know about the covenant. He's been *auditing* it. Which means somewhere ahead of us—"

"—someone already knows exactly where we're going," Wren finished, "and exactly what we intend to do there."

Far off across the Hollow, in a direction Wren's old instincts recognized with sick certainty as the way they'd come, something that might have been a signal-fire kindled briefly against the dark.
