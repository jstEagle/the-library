## 11. Ancestor's Bone

The shrine found them before they found it.

That was how Bram would later describe it, anyway — the way the path bent when it shouldn't have, doubling back through a stand of petrified trees whose leaves had turned to thin gray glass that chimed in a wind Bram couldn't feel on their own skin. Wren had stopped twice to recheck her bearings and twice declared, with increasing irritation, that they were going in a circle.

"We're not going in a circle," Bram said.

"Then explain the tree."

"The glassy one?"

"The glassy one."

"It's the same tree because it wants us to see it." Bram touched one of the glass leaves and it rang, faintly, like a spoon against a cup. "Things in the Hollow don't get lost, Wren. They *arrive*. There's a difference, and the difference is intention."

"Whose intention?"

"That," said Bram, "is generally the interesting question."

The shrine stood in a clearing where nothing grew, not even the gray moss that carpeted everything else in this part of the Hollow like frost that had given up on ambition. It had been a proper temple once, by the look of it — a ring of pillars around a sunken floor, though most of the pillars had slumped like tired old men leaning on each other, and the floor was choked with ash fine as flour. At its center, on a broken plinth, lay a single object.

A bone. A femur, long and yellowed and carved from end to end with script so dense it looked more like grain than writing.

Bram felt it before they saw it. Every relic Bram had ever touched had a voice, but voices were small things — whispers, murmurs, the last unfinished sentences of ordinary lives. This was not a voice. This was a bell that had been ringing for three hundred years and had learned, in that time, to be very, very quiet about it.

Wren went stiff beside them. "Bram."

"I know."

"What is—"

"*I know.*" Bram's mouth had gone dry. They knew that carving style. They'd grown up tracing it in the guild's oldest ledgers, in the margins of the founder's contracts, in the lintel over the guild hall door where every apprentice rubbed their thumb for luck without ever asking what the words meant. It was Winterbourne work. It was *family* work. It was the hand Bram saw in the mirror every morning when they picked up a chisel.

Meridian Winterbourne. The founder. The first carver. The name in the ledger that started everything.

Bram descended into the shrine alone. Not because Wren didn't offer — she offered twice, once with words and once by simply stepping forward until Bram caught her elbow — but because some doors you have to open yourself or not at all. Wren took it well, which is to say she scowled, posted herself at the top of the steps with her ward-keeper's iron in hand, and announced that if Bram screamed, she was coming down regardless of protocol, propriety, or the fundamental laws of spiritual communion.

"Noted," Bram said. "Try not to stab anything ancestral."

"No promises."

* * *

The bone was warm.

That was the first surprise. Everything else in the Hollow was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature — cold like an unoccupied room, cold like a letter never answered. But the femur under Bram's fingers held heat the way a hearthstone does hours after the fire: banked, patient, remembering.

Bram knelt on the ash, closed their hands around the carved length of it, and did what they had done ten thousand times with lesser relics. They listened.

The silence lasted exactly as long as it takes a person to decide whether or not they like you.

Then Meridian Winterbourne spoke, and her voice was nothing like the dead Bram knew. The dead were echoes; she was an instrument still being played. Her voice came up through the bone itself, through Bram's fingers, through the bones of Bram's wrist and arm and chest, so that Bram didn't so much hear her words as feel them being written somewhere behind their sternum.

*"So. The last of them comes."*

"Grandmother Meridian," Bram managed. It seemed absurd to say aloud, kneeling in ash, addressing a leg bone, but absurdity has never been a barrier to good manners. "I — yes. I suppose I am. The last who carries the blood, anyway."

*"You carry more than blood."* Something moved through the carving under Bram's palms — amusement, maybe, or wind over water. *"You carry the ledgers. I can smell the ink on you even here. Ink and lamp-oil and stubbornness."*

"The guild hall does have a particular smell."

*"I built that hall."* And now there was no mistaking it: pride, old and worn smooth as a river stone. *"I laid its first stone myself, before any of them understood what we were building. Do they still keep the lamps lit in the reading room?"*

"They do. Though mostly because the guild master says replacing the wicks costs more than the apprentices are worth."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Disapproval, Bram thought. Three centuries dead, and Meridian Winterbourne could still disapprove with her whole being.

*"Tell me why you've come, child. And tell me truly. I spent my life among traders of impossible things. Lies arrive pre-wrapped here, and I unwrap them all."*

So Bram told her. About the wards falling, the stars going out, the fracture lines running through the covenant she had sealed with her own death. About the Stone in the Hollow's heart, dark and starving. About the terms — a willing sacrifice, one belonging to both worlds — and about the journey, and the company waiting at the top of the steps, one member of whom was currently pretending very hard not to listen.

Bram did not mention what they intended to do when they reached the Stone. That part they kept folded away, tight and quiet, the way you keep a debt note you haven't dared show anyone.

Meridian let the silence stretch until Bram's knees ached against the stone.

*"You left things out,"* she said at last.

"A few."

*"Traders always do. It's how you know a trader."* The warmth in the bone shifted — not warmer, but closer, like someone drawing near a fire rather than stoking it. *"I know why you've come, Bram Winterbourne. You think I don't? You think I haven't watched my descendants circle this question for three hundred years like moths who've read too much poetry? You've come to ask me if it hurt."*

Bram's breath caught. "Did it?"

*"Yes."* No softness, no comfort, just the plain fact laid down like a coin on a counter. *"It hurt beyond anything your living body has vocabulary for. I will not lie to you about that, and I will not let you lie to yourself. What I can tell you is what the pain bought. I stood inside the covenant as it closed around me, and I saw every year it would hold, every soul on both sides of the border it would shelter, every child born safe under the stars I was about to relight. I held all of it in my hands for the length of one heartbeat. Then I paid. Gladly."*

"Gladly," Bram repeated. The word sat wrong in their mouth, heavy and foreign.

*"Gladly,"* she confirmed. *"And hear me on this, because it is the only thing I have that matters: the covenant cannot be fooled. It reads willingness the way you read ink — directly, completely, down to the last stroke. Willingness cannot be borrowed. Cannot be inherited. Cannot be performed, no matter how good an actor you believe yourself to be, and no Winterbourne has ever yet convinced me they're a good actor. We are many things, we carvers. Honest, mostly. Stubborn, certainly. Actors, never."*

"I wasn't planning to act."

*"No,"* Meridian said gently. *"You were planning to convince yourself first. It's different. It fools everyone, including you — right up until the moment the covenant opens you like a book and finds the fear underneath the resolve, and the love underneath the fear, and the terrible arithmetic underneath the love. I have watched four of my descendants come to this shrine. Two turned back. Two went on and failed, because their willingness had holes in it, and the Stone does not accept damaged goods."*

Bram looked down at their hands, still wrapped around the bone of the woman who had made their whole family possible and impossible in the same stroke. "And me? Can you tell? Am I ready?"

There was a pause. In it, Bram heard — or imagined hearing — the sound of a woman three hundred years dead considering a lie of her own, and deciding against it, because she had promised herself she would never become the kind of ancestor who lied to children at shrines.

*"I can tell,"* she said, *"that you are brave. I can tell that you are tired, and grieving something you haven't named, and carrying more than your share because carrying less never occurred to you. I can tell that someone out there — the one at the top of the steps, the one radiating protective fury like a stove — matters to you far past any sensible measure. And I can tell that whatever answer you gave yourself last night, alone, when you thought no relic was listening—"*

Bram's head snapped up.

*"—child. I am a relic. Everything within a mile of you is a relic. Your belt buckle is a relic. The coins in your pocket are relics. You have been confessing in your sleep since the border fell, and the dead take notes."*

Heat climbed Bram's face. Somewhere above them, Wren called down, "Everything alright? It got very quiet."

"Fine!" Bram called back, voice cracking only slightly. "Just — catching up on family history!"

"You're talking to a *bone*, Bram!"

"Family history *is* bones, Wren!"

A pause. "...Fair," Wren conceded, and resumed her pacing.

Bram turned back to the plinth. When they spoke again, they kept their voice low. "If the dead have been taking notes, then you already know what I mean to do. So tell me honestly, Grandmother. Was it worth it? For you. Knowing everything you know now."

And Meridian Winterbourne — founder, carver, first light of the covenant — answered without hesitation, and that was somehow the worst part, because Bram had hoped for hesitation. Hoped for doubt, for regret, for one seam of sorrow wide enough to climb through.

*"Yes,"* she said. *"Every hour of it. I would pay it again tomorrow. I have had three hundred years to change my mind, and I never once have. That is what true willingness looks like, Bram. Not the white-hot certainty of the night before. The long, quiet yes that doesn't waver when you give it three hundred years to reconsider."* The warmth in the bone pressed closer, almost tender. *"Now. Ask me the real question. The one you actually climbed down here for."*

Bram swallowed. The ash stirred faintly around their knees, though there was no wind.

"Am I ready?"

And Bram Winterbourne — honest, mostly; stubborn, certainly; actor, never, except for once, kneeling in the ruin their family had built — heard themselves say:

"Yes."

The word came out steady. Well-shaped. Correct in every particular, like an entry in a ledger balanced to the last copper.

The bone was silent for a long moment.

*"Ah,"* said Meridian, softly, sadly, and Bram understood that she had unwrapped the lie and found it exactly as pre-wrapped as promised. She did not call it what it was. Perhaps she remembered what it was to need a lie that badly. *"Well,"* she said instead. *"Then you should rest before the road ahead. There is a spring beneath the eastern pillar; the water is clean, or as clean as anything is here. And child — one more thing, freely given, since you gave me a lie freely given."*

"Anything."

*"Whatever you are carrying that you haven't shown her — the one at the top of the steps — set it down soon. Secrets grow heavy in the Hollow. Heavier than bone. Heavier than grief. And secrets carried too long stop being yours, and start deciding things for you."* The warmth began to recede, banking itself back down toward embers. *"Come back to me after, if there is an after. Tell me how the story ends. I have waited three hundred years for a sequel, and I am given to understand I am owed one."*

"Goodnight, Grandmother," Bram whispered.

*"Goodnight, last of them. Carve true."*

The bone cooled under Bram's hands, slowly, like a hand letting go.

* * *

Wren was waiting at the top of the steps with two cups of something hot she'd coaxed out of the camp kettle and a face carefully arranged into unconcern, which fooled neither of them and both of them pretended otherwise.

"So?" she said, handing over a cup. "What did the famous founder have to say?"

Bram drank. The tea tasted of pine and iron and, faintly, of home. "She asked about the guild hall. Whether we still keep the lamps lit."

"And do we?"

"Lamps lit, wicks half-burned, guild master complaining about the cost." Bram smiled into the steam. "She was not impressed."

"She sounds like she'd have fit right in." Wren settled onto the pillar beside them, shoulder almost — not quite — touching theirs. Above the ruined shrine, the starless sky pressed down, vast and blind, and somewhere far to the east the Hollow breathed its slow, tide-less breathing. "Anything useful? Anything about the Stone?"

"She said the covenant reads willingness directly." Bram kept their voice easy, their eyes on the tea. "No faking it. No borrowing it."

"Huh." Wren frowned at the horizon. "Then whoever does this thing had better mean it all the way down."

"Yeah," Bram said. "All the way down."

They sat together while the company slept, saying nothing, shoulders finally touching, and the lie sat between them like a third traveler — quiet, patient, growing heavier by the hour.

In Bram's pack, wrapped in cloth, every relic they carried had begun — very softly, very patiently — to hum.
