## 12. Wren's Confession

The fire was Wren's idea, and she regretted it immediately.

Not because of the light — though in the Hollow, a fire drew things the way an open wound draws flies. She'd built it small and ringed it with salt-ash and three of Bram's relics laid out like sentries: a thimble, a soldier's button, a child's wooden bird. The dead who clustered at the edge of the dark were quiet ones, mostly. Curious ones. They kept their distance the way cats do, and Wren had learned, over two weeks of walking through a world where colors ran backward and shadows fell toward you instead of away, to tell the difference between the dead who watched and the dead who waited.

No. She regretted the fire because fire meant conversation. Fire meant sitting across from Bram Winterbourne with nothing left to audit, no ward-stone to touch, no ledger to consult, and both of them pretending they weren't counting the miles to the Covenant Stone like a condemned prisoner counts stairs.

Bram sat with their knees drawn up, turning the wooden bird over in their long carver's hands. They hadn't spoken in an hour. That was unlike them — or rather, it was unlike the version of them Wren had known before everything broke, the one who filled silence with observations about the grain of things, who talked to relics as comfortably as to people, who had once narrated an entire funeral procession because, they said, somebody ought to say something nice about old Hesper Cobb, and nobody else was going to.

"You're doing your thinking face," Wren said.

"I don't have a thinking face."

"You have four faces. Thinking, appraising, lying, and that one where you're pretending not to be cold." She nodded at Bram's shoulders, which were hunched up around their ears despite the fire. "You're on number four."

"Number three is my professional demeanor."

"Number three *is* lying, Bram."

A ghost of a smile crossed their face and vanished, which was how most of Bram's smiles worked these days. They set the bird down gently among the other relics, the way they handled everything — as if the whole world were something precious that might still be broken. "The dead are restless tonight," they said. "Something's coming. Or we're coming to something."

"The Stone."

"The Stone," Bram agreed, and then, too casually, "We should talk about what happens when we get there."

And there it was. Wren had been waiting for this conversation for days, dreading it, rehearsing her half of it in her head while she took first watch while Bram slept — except Bram didn't really sleep anymore, did they? They drifted, listening. So maybe neither of them had slept properly since the stars went out, and maybe that was why when Wren opened her mouth, what came out wasn't the careful speech she'd drafted.

It was the truth.

"You let me believe you were coming back from this."

Bram went very still. It was a carver's stillness — the kind they must have learned leaning over delicate work, where breathing wrong could ruin a year of effort. "I don't know what you mean."

"Don't." Wren stood, because sitting felt like surrender, and if they were going to have this fight she wanted to be on her feet for it. "Don't you dare do your number-three face at me. I've been keeping ledgers of my own these past weeks, Bram Winterbourne. You gave away your good coat to that widow in Marrowfen. You wrote letters — actual letters, sealed ones — and paid a courier an obscene amount to carry them back across the border. You apologized to Sable. To *Sable*. You've never once apologized to Sable, you told me you'd rather chew gravel."

"Sable deserved an apology. I shorted her on a trade when we were nineteen—"

"And then there's the small matter," Wren pressed on, voice rising, "of you asking me — *me* — to teach you the old ward-cant. The language of binding oaths. Which you already know, by the way, I've heard you muttering it in your sleep, so don't bother denying it. You asked me to teach it to you as if you needed a refresher, and what you actually wanted was to know whether an oath sworn by two people could carry obligations after one of them is *dead*."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, one of the quiet dead made a sound like wind through a keyhole — sympathy, maybe, or just the Hollow breathing.

Bram looked into the flames. When they spoke, their voice had gone flat and careful, which was worse than any lie. "Wren—"

"You're planning to die." It came out steadier than she felt. "At the Stone. Willingly. Because some ledger entry says that's the price, and you've decided that since you're the one with the blood for it, the rest of us just have to live with it. So yes. Let's talk about what happens when we get there. Let's talk about how you've spent three hundred miles arranging your affairs and telling everyone goodbye and looking at me like—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it, angry at herself for the crack in her voice, angrier at Bram for causing it. "Like you're memorizing me. Like I'm a relic you're trying to keep the sound of."

Silence. Long enough that Wren counted her own heartbeats and lost track somewhere past forty.

Then Bram said, quietly: "How long have you known?"

"Since the coat."

"The coat?"

"Nobody gives away their good coat, Bram. Especially not you. You once traded a *tooth* rather than give up that coat." She sat back down, abruptly, all the anger draining out of her and leaving something rawer behind. "I let you keep your secret because I thought — I thought if I confronted you, you'd just get better at hiding it. And I swore I'd find another way before we reached the Stone. I've been racking my brain for two weeks and I've got nothing, Bram. There's no other way. The covenant wants what it wants, and you—" Her voice cracked again, properly this time. "You absolute idiot. You wonderful, stubborn, self-sacrificing idiot."

Bram was staring at her with an expression she'd never seen on them before — not guilt exactly, and not surprise either. Something closer to grief recognizing itself in a mirror.

"That's not fair," they said at last.

"No. It isn't. Death generally isn't."

"That's not — Wren." They dragged a hand down their face, and for a moment they looked every one of their years and then some, a bone-carver grown old in a single season. "You want to talk about secrets? About people deciding things for other people?" Their voice sharpened, and Wren realized, with a lurch, that she'd miscalculated. She'd expected denial, deflection, maybe tears. She hadn't expected counterattack. "Let's talk about the oath, shall we? Let's talk about the night you walked out of the guild hall and unbound yourself without a word of warning. Do you know what that felt like, from my end? Like a door slamming inside my own chest. For six years I carried a hole where you used to be and I didn't even know what shape it was until you showed up at my door asking for a relic, and I thought — oh. *Oh.* That's what's missing. That's what I've been bleeding around all this time."

Wren's mouth went dry. "Bram—"

"You want fury? Fine. I've got some stored up. Six years' worth." Bram was on their feet now too, and the firelight caught the faint luminosity that had been growing under their skin since the shrine — Meridian's bone-light, the ancestors' inheritance, glowing along their forearms like veins of ember. "You let the oath lapse. Our oath. The one we swore when we were young and stupid and so certain the world would make room for us. And you never told me why. Not once. Not in six years. I thought I'd done something. I went through everything — every conversation, every letter, every ledger entry, trying to find the mistake, and there wasn't one, because the mistake was *you*, and you wouldn't even tell me what it was!"

"Because telling you was worse!" It burst out of her louder than she intended, loud enough that the quiet dead at the edge of the dark flinched. Wren pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Because telling you was worse than losing you, and I am apparently a coward, and I have known that about myself for six years, and I hoped — I stupidly hoped — that I could die with you never knowing just how much of a coward."

The words hung there. Bram had stopped moving entirely.

"What," they said carefully, "are you talking about?"

Wren laughed — a wet, awful sound. "You really don't know. All those years, all those ledgers, and you never read it." She dropped her hands and looked at them straight on. "I loved you, Bram. Past tense is a lie; I love you, present tense, continuously, inconveniently, since the winter we were twenty-two and you carved my name into a river stone for no reason at all except that you liked the way the letters fit together. And the oath — the oath we swore bound us to each other, and I thought — I was taught that duty requires distance, that a ward-keeper cannot guard what she cannot bear to lose, that attachment is a flaw in the wards. So I chose duty. I chose it over you. And then I discovered that choosing it over you didn't stop the loving, it just meant I had to do the job with a hole in my chest, which is, incidentally, exactly how you describe it feeling, so congratulations to us both on six years of identical suffering that one honest conversation could have ended."

She was shaking. She hadn't realized until now how much of her life had gone into not saying this — how much energy it took, daily, to stand next to someone and not say the true thing.

Bram hadn't moved. Hadn't blinked. The ember-light under their skin had brightened, flickering with something — pulse, emotion, whatever it was now that their blood carried the covenant's fire.

"You loved me," they repeated, as though testing each word for cracks. "And your solution was to leave."

"My solution was to protect the border with a steady hand instead of a grieving one—"

"That's not how love works, Wren! Love isn't a structural weakness! It's not a hairline fracture in the wards! It's the only thing I've ever held that didn't whisper to me!" Bram's voice broke open, and beneath the fury Wren heard it — the loneliness underneath, years of it, the isolation of a person whose gift made colleagues flinch and whose heart had been quietly broken by its own keeper. "Do you know what it's like to hear the dead all day? Every relic a voice, every appraisal a eulogy? You were the living thing. You were the one person who treated my gift like a gift instead of an affliction, and then you *left*, and I told myself you had reasons, good guild reasons, honorable reasons, and all this time the reason was that you loved me too much to stay?"

"Yes!" Wren shouted. "Yes, that was the reason, and I know how it sounds—"

"It sounds insane!"

"It sounds like *duty!*"

"Well, your duty can go hang itself with its own lanyard, because I have news for you about duty, Wren Ashvale!" Bram snatched up their satchel and pulled out the ledger — the guild ledger, the founder's ledger, the one full of Winterbourne bones and three centuries of quiet theft. They threw it down between them on the bedroll. "Duty is *this*. Duty is a guild master grooming a child for slaughter and calling it accounting. Duty is a covenant that eats one willing soul a century and calls itself peace. Your duty took my family's bones and your career and six years of our lives, and now it wants the rest of me, and I am *done* being dutiful!"

"Then don't die!" The words tore out of Wren with everything behind them — six years, three hundred miles, the memory of holding a chapel door against the Hollow with Bram's blood on her hands. "That's the entire point, Bram! Don't die! Find another way, steal one, carve one, cheat the covenant like the guild cheated everyone else, but don't you dare stand at that Stone and be *brave* at me, because brave doesn't help! Brave just leaves me standing there afterward with nothing but a story about how noble you were, and I have buried enough people I love to know that nobility is cold comfort at a graveside!"

The word was out before she could catch it.

*I love.*

The fire crackled. Bram stared at her, and all the fury drained out of their face, leaving something naked and stunned underneath, and Wren stood there in the ashes of the argument with her chest heaving, unable to take it back, not wanting to take it back, furious at herself and relieved past all measure and terrified, all at once.

"You love me," Bram said. Not a question this time. A man taking inventory. An appraiser confirming provenance.

"Oh, shut up."

"You love me, and your plan to cope with that was distance, and my plan to cope with dying was bookkeeping, and we are—" Bram started to laugh, and it was not a happy laugh, it was the laugh of someone watching their own life from a great height and finding it ridiculous, "we are the two worst people in either realm at this. We are catastrophically bad at this."

"We are," Wren admitted, and then her knees gave out and she sat down hard on the bedroll, and Bram sat down beside her, close, shoulder against hers, warm through two layers of travel wool.

For a while neither of them spoke. Out in the dark, the quiet dead had crept closer — drawn, perhaps, by all that living noise. Wren could feel them at the edge of the firelight like an audience that had paid admission and intended to stay for the second act. One of them, an old woman by the shape of her, was smiling.

"If I'd known," Bram said finally, softly, "all those years ago. If you'd told me. Would it have changed anything?"

"I don't know." Honest, at least. "I'd like to think so. I think I was scared that loving you would make me bad at the job. And then I did the job badly anyway, and lost the license, and lost you, and I still had the loving. Turns out it doesn't go anywhere. It's the one thing I've ever owned that couldn't be confiscated."

Bram turned their head, and their forehead came to rest against Wren's temple — a small gesture, almost shy, from someone who handled the bones of the dead without flinching. "I sold a memory," they said. "In the market. A day with you. The one where we skipped the guild gala and stole apples off Master Corvane's tree and you dared me to eat the sour one and I did, and my face did that thing you laughed at for a month. I sold it for passage, and I've been walking around with a gap ever since. I knew something important had been there. I couldn't remember what."

Wren's breath caught. "Bram..."

"So here's what I propose." Their voice was steadying now, finding its footing the way it always did when they had a plan, even a terrible one. "We argue about who dies at the Stone — properly, exhaustively, all night if we have to. And when we've run out of arguments, we put our heads together and we find the way through that doesn't end with either of us in the ground. Because I'll tell you what I learned from the founders' ledger, Wren. Covenants are contracts, and contracts are written by people, and anything written by people can be rewritten. My ancestor signed ours in blood and bone because she believed there was no other way. But she was alone. She was always alone. And I'm not."

Wren swallowed past the knot in her throat. "You're not," she agreed.

"And you're not."

"No."

"So." Bram lifted their head and looked at her, and the ember-light in their skin caught their eyes and lit them from within, and Wren thought: *there you are.* Six years, three hundred miles, and there you are. "Shall we begin the negotiation? I warn you, I drive a hard bargain. I've been keeping ledgers since I was twelve."

"Apprentice rates," Wren said, and leaned into them, and let herself — for one hour, one stolen hour under a starless sky — simply be a person sitting next to the person she loved, with the dead keeping respectful watch around a small defiant fire.

They argued until the coals burned low. It was, both of them would later agree, the best fight of their lives — fierce and tearful and occasionally, horribly, funny, punctuated by Bram citing contract law and Wren citing military precedent and both of them eventually running aground on the same rock: neither would permit the other to die, and neither would permit themselves to live at the other's expense, and the deadlock was total, and beautiful, and completely unresolvable by argument.

Which was why, near dawn, when Bram suddenly sat bolt upright and said, "Wait. Wait. The oath," in the voice of a carver who has just seen the shape inside the wood, Wren was too tired and too hopeful to ask what they meant before Bram was already digging through the satchel, already pulling out the old oath-token they'd both worn and both kept, all these years, without admitting it.

"Bram," Wren said slowly, watching their face in the dying firelight, "what did you see?"

Bram looked up at her, and they were grinning — wide and wild and slightly unhinged, the grin of someone about to do something either brilliant or unforgivable.

"I saw," they said, "a loophole."

* * *

They broke camp before first gray light, moving fast, because Bram had heard something in the night that they refused to speak of twice — a voice from the east, vast and patient, no longer counting down.

Counting *up*.
