## 13. The Hollow's Heart

**Bram**

The road stopped pretending to be a road three days out from the Stone.

It had begun as honest gravel, then thinned to a suggestion of packed ash, then to a line of pale moss that Wren swore was following them rather than existing underfoot. Now there was nothing at all — just the Hollow's grey light and the sense, persistent as a stone in one's boot, that the land itself was walking alongside them, curious.

Bram had stopped minding. Mostly. The dead were quieter here than they'd been in the living realm, which struck them as the single funniest thing about the entire journey, though they'd kept it to themselves. Telling Wren would have invited commentary, and Wren's commentary on Bram's gift had a way of sounding like affection, and affection was currently a wound neither of them could afford to touch. They'd confessed everything two nights ago — the covenant price, the ledger, the life Bram meant to give — and since then they'd walked in a silence shaped like an argument still smoldering.

*You should have told me,* Wren had said. *I should have told you first,* Bram had answered, and both of them had been right, and both of them had been unbearable, and here they were.

"You're doing the face," Wren said, without turning around.

"What face?"

"The one where you're listening to something and deciding whether it's rude to mention."

Bram considered lying. They'd gotten worse at it lately; the Hollow seemed to thin a person out until only the honest parts remained, like soup reduced to stock. "The dead ahead," they said. "A great many dead ahead. And none of them are afraid of us."

"That's supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be *interesting.* Everything we've met so far has wanted us gone or wanted us eaten. These want—" Bram tilted their head, listening past the wind, past Meridian's faint ancestral hum in the bone shard at their throat. "They want to know if we've brought bread."

Wren stopped. Turned. Her eyebrows performed an entire silent conversation with each other. "Bread."

"Someone always asks about bread. It's how you can tell a crowd is real and not a haunting. Hauntings never ask about bread. Hauntings have better priorities."

"Your gift is a horror, do you know that? You hear the actual voices of the departed and your takeaway is *snack logistics.*"

"My takeaway," Bram said mildly, "is that we are about to walk into the heart of the Hollow, and the people waiting for us there are hungry. I find that considerably more frightening than teeth." They adjusted the pack on their shoulders — lighter now, deliberately, after all those quiet weeks of returning relics and closing accounts — and nodded up the slope. "Come on. We shouldn't keep them waiting. It's rude, and also I think some of them have been waiting rather a long time."

The hill crested, and Bram forgot every clever thing they'd ever thought.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had trained her whole adult life for what waited beyond broken wards. She had diagrams. She had contingency plans. She had, at one low point during her disgraced years, a laminated card.

None of it covered this.

The valley opened below them like a bowl held by careful hands, and in its center stood the Covenant Stone: a monolith taller than any tower she'd ever garrisoned, black as a closed eye, carved over — no. Not carved over. *Fused.* Thousands upon thousands of bones grown together into one seamless mass, edges melted into edges, femurs becoming ribs becoming vertebrae becoming a surface smooth as river stone. It did not gleam. It did not glow. Once, she understood without knowing how she knew, it must have burned like a hearth seen from miles away. Now it was cooling. She could feel the cold coming off it the way you felt cold coming off a body that used to be warm.

And around it, watching, stood the Hollow's inhabitants.

They came in every shape the dead could take. Some were nearly whole — old men with lanterns, women in the fashions of three centuries, children who flickered like candles in drafts. Others were less than that: suggestions, silhouettes, a pair of hands holding nothing, a voice wearing the memory of a coat. They lined the valley walls in their hundreds, maybe thousands, and not one of them moved toward the company. They simply watched, the way starving people watch a table being set.

"They're not attacking," Sera said behind her — the guild clerk who'd joined them at the market, still clutching her ledger satchel like a life vest. "Why aren't they attacking? Everything else attacked."

"Because," Bram said quietly, "they're not monsters."

Wren heard it too, now that she let herself listen past twenty years of ward-keeper training. The Hollow's dead weren't growling or keening. They were murmuring. A vast, patient murmur, like a congregation settling into pews, and threaded through it — she caught it clearly, absurdly — the word *bread*, passed along the crowd like a rumor of rain.

"Bram," she said slowly. "How long has the covenant sealed this place?"

"Three hundred years, near enough. Renewed every century. My family's bone in the seal." Bram was already descending the slope, because of course they were; caution had never once outranked curiosity in that stubborn skull. "Every renewal took a sacrifice and gave back... wards. Borders. Safety for the lit world."

"And what did it give back to *them?*"

Bram didn't answer. That was answer enough.

Wren followed them down, hand on her blade out of habit more than hope, and as she drew closer to the crowd, details resolved that made her stomach turn over. An old woman in a guild apprentice's uniform — the cut was centuries out of date, but Wren knew the stitching. A soldier in the livery of a war Wren had only read about, standing at attention beside a farmer whose boots were caked in mud from fields that hadn't existed in living memory. A little girl holding a wooden bird, worn smooth by small hands long gone.

These weren't Hollow-things. These were *people.* People from the lit world. People who had died and, instead of passing on, had fallen through — slipped between the cracks of a border built to barter rather than breathe, and ended up exiled in the dark with nobody sent after them.

"We abandoned them," Wren said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. "The covenant. It wasn't a wall against monsters. It was—"

"A door we locked," Bram finished softly, "and then stopped checking."

An elder of sorts detached itself from the nearest group — a tall figure, half-translucent, with the bearing of someone who had once given orders and learned, over long years, that orders meant nothing here. It regarded Bram with an expression Wren could only call *careful hope*, the look of a dog that had been kicked often enough to stop running to the gate.

"You're Winterbourne," it said. Its voice sounded like pages turning. "The carver's blood. We smelled the bone before we saw you."

"I am." Bram's chin lifted, and Wren recognized the guild voice, the appraiser's voice, level and courteous over any abyss. "I've come to renew what my ancestor made."

The murmur through the crowd changed. Wren couldn't say how she knew, but she knew: it curdled. Hope going sour in real time.

"Renew," the elder repeated.

"Reforge the covenant. Restore the borders. End the breach."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind moving through the fused bone of the Stone, a low note like breath through a flute. Then the old woman in the guild uniform began to laugh — a small, cracked sound, entirely without meanness, which somehow made it worse.

"Oh, love," she said. "Oh, you sweet ledger-keeper. Come and see what you're offering to fix."

* * *

**Bram**

The elder led them around the base of the Stone, and Bram counted their steps because counting was what they did when the ground gave way beneath their understanding. Forty steps. Fifty. The monolith went on. Sixty. At seventy steps around, the elder stopped and gestured, and Bram looked up at the Stone's flank and saw, for the first time, what the carvings were.

Faces.

Thousands of faces, pressed into the fused bone like flowers pressed in a book. Not decorative. Not symbolic. Each one distinct — an old man's laugh lines, a girl's missing tooth, a soldier's scar — and each one, when Bram reached out with the sense they couldn't switch off, *spoke.*

Not the vast patient countdown from the northern ward-stone. Voices. Individual, exhausted, human voices, murmuring inside the bone the way seeds murmur inside fruit.

"They're *in* it," Bram whispered. "The sacrifices. They never left."

"Never left," the elder agreed. "Each century, your guild brings a soul of both bloods. Each century, the Stone drinks deep and flares bright and the border holds. And the soul stays. Feeding it. Forever." The elder's translucent hands folded. "Meridian was the first. There have been others since. Fewer, lately. Your guild finds it harder to gather willing ones, I think. Harder still to make them willing honestly."

Bram thought of the founder's ledgers. Of the grooming disguised as apprenticeship. Of a career measured out in neat columns, every entry designed to deliver a Winterbourne heir to this exact valley with exactly this expression on their face.

They had known. They had known since chapter and verse of the guild's true accounts. But knowing a betrayal in the abstract and seeing its receipts carved in bone were different hungers entirely.

"How many?" Bram asked. Their voice held steady. They were privately furious at it for doing so.

"Eleven," said the old woman in the guild uniform, shuffling closer. "I was the ninth. 1740. I volunteered, same as you're planning to. I thought I was saving the world." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it was the worst thing Bram had seen in a journey full of terrible things. "The world got saved. I got a wall to hold up for two hundred and eighty years. Nobody ever came to check whether the wall was tired."

The moral vertigo hit Bram then, sudden and total, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there.

They had spent the entire journey carrying their death like a coin in a pocket — heavy, familiar, almost comforting in its certainty. Die willingly. Seal the breach. Save Wren, save the villages, save the lit world, join the honored ranks of the ancestors whose voices had guided them since childhood. A clean transaction. A balanced ledger.

Except the transaction didn't balance. It had *never* balanced. Every renewal had taken a whole soul and returned only a century of borrowed time, and the deficit had been accruing in the dark, unpaid, unacknowledged, until the Stone starved and the stars went out and the bill came due all at once. The covenant hadn't imprisoned the Hollow. It had *abandoned* it — locked the door, pocketed the key, and called the darkness on the other side a monster so no one would ever think to open it and apologize.

Eleven souls. Pressed into bone. Holding up a wall for a world that thanked them once a century with another wall to hold.

"Bram." Wren's hand found their sleeve. When Bram turned, her face was doing something complicated — fury and fear and a fierce, stubborn tenderness all fighting for the wheel. "Tell me you're not still thinking about it. Tell me you can see what I'm seeing."

What Wren was seeing, presumably, was eleven faces in a monolith. What Bram was seeing was slightly worse: themselves, three centuries from now, pressed among them, smiling kindly down at some future heir and saying *oh, love, come and see what you're offering to fix.*

"I see it," Bram said.

"And?"

"And I'm an appraiser, Wren. I've just discovered the item I intended to sell has been misvalued by the entire guild for three hundred years." Bram looked up at the dark, cooling mass of the Stone, at the faces murmuring inside it, at the exiles gathered around it — not monsters, never monsters, just everyone the lit world had decided not to count. "I don't withdraw my offer. But I am officially declaring the terms fraudulent."

Above them, deep inside the fused bone, something stirred. The vast, patient presence from the northern stones — the countdown — rose through the monolith like a bubble through dark water, and for the first time it spoke in words instead of numbers.

"*Then name new terms, carver's child,*" it said, and every face in the Stone went quiet to listen. "*But know this first: the last one who tried to renegotiate with me was your grandmother. And she is still in here, and she is still angry.*"

Behind Bram, Wren made a sound that was half laugh and half prayer. Somewhere in the crowd, the old woman in the guild uniform patted Bram's arm with a hand cool as well-water and said, cheerfully, "That'll be Aunt Vesper. Good luck, love. She ate two negotiators in my century alone."

Bram straightened their collar. Adjusted the pack. Took out, of all things, the appraisal lens they'd carried since apprenticeship, because if the universe insisted on conducting business, then by every saint of every realm, it would be conducted *properly.*

"Well then," they said to the Stone, to the dead, to the grandmother, to the whole starving dark. "Let's talk terms."
