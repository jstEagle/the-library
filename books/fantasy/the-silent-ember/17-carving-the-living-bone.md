## 17. Carving the Living Bone

### Bram

The Hollow's speaker had told them what was needed, and Bram had spent the whole walk back to the Covenant Stone turning it over in their mind like a coin they couldn't quite read.

*A carver of both bloods, working from inside the Stone.* That was the phrase that kept snagging. Inside. Not beside it, not before it, not kneeling at its base with a chisel and a prayer. *Inside.*

Bram had carved bone for twenty years. They knew how bone behaved — how it resisted, how it sang under the blade when the angle was right, how it remembered every cut you gave it. And they knew, with the certainty of a person who had spent their life listening to the dead through their relics, that a dead sacrifice would feed the Stone exactly as long as it took to starve again.

The old covenant had been sealed with Meridian's death, and it had lasted three hundred years on a diet of taking. Every renewal had taken more than it returned. The arithmetic of it was obscene — a ledger written by someone who never intended to balance it.

So the answer wasn't to die for the Stone. The answer was to *teach* it.

"I need your knife," Bram said.

They were standing at the edge of the clearing where the Covenant Stone rose out of the Hollow's dark earth like a tooth from an ancient jaw. Wren was checking the perimeter out of pure habit, her ward-keeper's hands moving through the old signs even though there were no wards left to keep. She looked over her shoulder.

"You have your own knife."

"Not that knife." Bram held out their hand. "Your oath-knife. The one we swore on."

Wren went very still. In the starless dark, with the Hollow's grey light pooling around them, she looked like a woman deciding whether to step off a cliff or push someone else off it first.

"Bram."

"I've been doing the sums," Bram said. "You know I can't not do the sums. A death feeds the Stone once. A death is a payment, and payments run out — that's the whole reason we're standing here. But an oath—" they tapped the faded scar on their own wrist, where the oath-mark still sat beneath the skin like a buried coal, "—an oath isn't a payment. It's a *standing arrangement*. It renews itself as long as both parties keep choosing it. The Stone doesn't need blood. It needs a covenant that actually works the way covenants are supposed to work. Mutual. Living. Renewed by choice, not extracted by grief."

"And you're proposing to carve that out of—"

"Living bone. Mine." Bram swallowed. "And yours. Because I can't hold both sides of a covenant alone, and because the oath we swore already binds us — it's the only living thread between us that predates all of this. We don't build something new. We finish something old."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind them, the Hollow's exiles had begun to gather — the forgotten dead drifting closer, drawn by whatever it is that draws moths and mourners.

"Living bone," Wren said flatly. "Do you have any idea what that *is*? I've seen ward-keepers try to carve living bone. Once. He screamed for six hours, and what he made wouldn't hold a candle-flame."

"Yes," Bram said. "I know exactly what it is. I'm a bone-carver, Wren. It's the only material I've ever worked that's honest. Dead bone lies — it tells you what it used to be. Living bone tells you what it *is*, right now, while it's happening. Which is rather the point, if the covenant we're writing is supposed to be about the present tense."

"That's a very pretty speech for someone describing self-mutilation."

"It's not mutilation if it grows back." Bram paused. "Mostly. Probably. The ancestors will help."

"The ancestors," Wren repeated, "will help."

"They're already singing. Can't you hear them?"

Wren closed her eyes. And Bram watched — watched the exact moment her ward-keeper's training caught up with her gift, the moment she stopped filtering and started listening. Her breath went out of her in a slow, shaking rush.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, they're *loud*."

"They've been loud since the shrine. Meridian started it. I think—" Bram laughed, and it came out wet and unsteady, "I think we've been auditioning for three hundred years' worth of Winterbournes, and apparently we passed."

* * *

### Wren

Later, Wren would try to remember the argument. There wasn't one, really. There was a list of objections she raised — the pain, the risk, the fact that Bram had clearly been planning this since the Drowned Choir and hadn't said a word, which was going to be discussed *at length* if they both survived — and there was Bram answering each one with the patient, maddening logic of a person balancing a ledger.

But underneath the argument, Wren was already saying yes. She had been saying yes since a chapel full of survivors, since a river of voices, since a campfire confession that had ended with both of them furious and holding hands anyway.

The oath they'd sworn at nineteen had been a formal thing: two young fools, one guild ceremony, knives crossed, blood mingled, words spoken in the old cadence. *What is mine is pledged to yours; what breaks in me breaks in thee; while either stands, both stand.* Everyone had assumed it was professional courtesy. Nobody — least of all the two people who'd sworn it — had been brave enough to notice it was a wedding vow wearing work clothes.

Now it was going to be load-bearing architecture.

They prepared at the base of the Stone, because the Stone had to witness, and the Hollow's dead gathered in a wide ring around them — hundreds of them now, the exiled and the forgotten, watching with the wariness of creatures who had been promised things before.

Bram knelt first, sleeves rolled, forearms bare. They laid out their tools on a cloth with the same unhurried care they brought to appraising a ring or closing an account: the fine blades, the bone-needles, a stoppered vial of their own blood for marking, and Wren's oath-knife set apart from all the rest, gleaming dull and honest.

"Rules," Bram said, in the voice they used for apprentices. "One: you hold the line against anything that comes through that clearing, including my screaming, which I'm told is going to be considerable. Two: if I start to fade — truly fade, not just suffer, there's a difference and I'll explain it — you say the renewal words. You'll know them. They're in the oath, the part neither of us ever finished learning. Three—"

"Three," Wren interrupted, "you tell me the truth about how much this is going to hurt."

Bram looked up at her. Their face did something complicated.

"A great deal," they said. "More than dying, possibly. Dying only happens once. This has to keep happening — that's the design. Every year, on the anniversary, the covenant asks us to renew it. Willingly. Together. Forever."

"*Forever*," Wren said.

"I know." Bram's mouth twitched. "I should have courted you more conventionally. Most people just propose with a ring."

Despite everything — the starless sky, the monolith of cooling bone, the crowd of the dead, the certain and intimate agony ahead — Wren laughed. It burst out of her like something escaping a cage, and several of the Hollow's exiles flinched at the sound, having perhaps forgotten what laughter was.

"You absolute ledger-keeper," she said. "You've turned marriage into a subscription service."

"Renewable by mutual consent," Bram agreed solemnly. "No hidden fees. Well. Some hidden fees. There's a pain clause."

"Sign me up," Wren said, and knelt down across from them, and took the oath-knife.

* * *

### Bram

The first cut was the worst thing Bram had ever felt, and Bram had once heard a drowned child's last thought through a locket and carried it for a decade.

The blade went into the meat of their forearm along the old oath-scar, and instead of blood — well, blood too, plenty of blood — but instead of *only* blood, light came. Not the cold grey of the Hollow and not the warm gold of the lit world but something between, an ember-color, the color the guild was named for and had forgotten the meaning of. It ran up the blade into Wren's hand, and Wren gasped, and Bram saw the same light kindle along her wrist where her own oath-scar lay.

"Say it," Bram managed, through teeth clenched hard enough to ache for a week. "The words. Both parts. It needs both."

Wren's voice shook. Then steadied. Ward-keepers learned to speak through worse than fear.

*"What is mine is pledged to yours."*

*"What breaks in me breaks in thee,"* Bram answered, carving as they spoke, each word a cut, each cut a syllable of a language older than the guild. The bone opened under their blade like a book. And inside it — inside themselves — Bram could see the covenant taking shape, not as law, not as chains, but as *structure*. Load-bearing. Breathing room built into every joint.

The Stone noticed.

It noticed the way a starving animal notices warmth — a vast, slow attention turning toward them from the monolith, ancient and hollowed and *hungry*, and for one terrible moment Bram felt it reaching for them the way it had reached for Meridian, the way it had reached for every Winterbourne for three hundred years. Take. Consume. Simplify. One life poured into the stone, clean and final and easy.

"No," Bram said aloud, to a rock, in the voice they used for colleagues trying to lowball an appraisal. "You don't get the whole. You get the *share*. That's the entire innovation. Take less, receive forever. I've done the sums. The sums are *impeccable*."

The Stone hesitated.

And then the ancestors arrived.

They came up through Bram's own opened bone like water finding an old channel — Meridian first, laughing, *actually laughing*, her voice bright as a struck bell — followed by generations of Winterbournes, carvers and ledger-keepers and one memorable aunt who had allegedly traded her shadow for a hat. They didn't push. They *harmonized*. Every cut Bram made, they sang the note beneath it, and the pain didn't lessen, exactly, but it became part of something, the way a single voice becomes part of a choir without disappearing.

Beside them, Wren had opened her own arm — Bram hadn't even seen her take the cut, she was ward-keeper enough to bleed quietly — and their blood met on the surface of the Stone, ember-light leaping between the drops, and the old oath flared to life along both their arms like script being rewritten by an invisible, enthusiastic editor.

*"While either stands,"* Wren said, and her eyes found Bram's, and they were fierce and terrified and absolutely certain, *"both stand."*

"Both stand," Bram echoed.

The Stone drank.

Not greedily — that was the miracle of it, the thing Bram would spend the rest of their life trying to describe. It drank the way dry ground drinks rain, gratefully, and then it *gave back*: a pulse of warmth rolling out through the Hollow, through the ring of watching dead, and the exiles nearest the Stone glowed faintly at the edges, and one of them — a small girl, forgotten two hundred years — laughed, a sound like a spoon dropped in an empty kitchen, startling and ordinary and alive.

The monolith's surface began to change under their hands. Where Bram carved, new growth followed — living lattice threading through dead bone, breathing channels opening where there had only been stone. The covenant was rewriting itself around their shared oath, and it was *warm*, and it was *fair*, and somewhere in its deep structure Bram could feel the fine print assembling itself, and they made sure — half-delirious, singing through gritted teeth along with three hundred years of ancestors — they made absolutely sure to include a clause about guild masters who groomed children for sacrifice.

The dead had insisted on that clause. Bram had agreed enthusiastically.

The light built. It built until the clearing was noon-bright in a world with no sun, until Wren's face across from them was gilded and streaming tears she wasn't bothering to hide, until Bram's own bones hummed like tuning forks and every relic they carried — the ring, the locket, the little carved bird — was singing in their pack, a whole orchestra of the honored dead playing the anthem of a covenant that finally, *finally* worked.

"Almost," Bram gasped. "The keystone. Last cut. Together."

They set the oath-knife between them, both hands on the haft, and cut as one — a shallow line across the Stone's heart, through the newest living layer, signing the work the only way a carver can.

The world went white.

And overhead — impossibly, gloriously, through the endless dark of the Hollow's ceiling — a single point of light kindled, small and stubborn and burning.

A star.

Wren stared up at it, blood-light on her arms, and said, with the wonder of a woman seeing the first sunrise after a lifetime underground:

"Bram. Look. It's *answering*."

And then the Stone pulled them both under, into the test — into visions of everything they stood to lose — and the real trial began.
