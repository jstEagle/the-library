## 22. Ember, Still Burning

### Bram

The bone sang again this morning.

Not the way it used to — that low, grieving hum that had haunted my workbench for thirty years — but something brighter, almost conversational. I set down the chisel and let it speak while my newest apprentice, a round-faced girl named Tamsin with sawdust in her eyebrows, watched me the way she always did when I stopped mid-stroke: as if listening to nothing were a skill one could learn by staring hard enough.

It is, actually. But you can't learn it by staring.

"What's it saying?" she asked. She'd been with me four months now, and she had learned the first lesson thoroughly: never call them *it*, never call them *the relic*. "What are they saying," she corrected herself before I could, and grinned when I raised an eyebrow at her.

"They're telling me about rain," I said. The ring on my bench had belonged to a shepherd who'd died in the spring floods two valleys over, and her voice came through the silver band like water over stones. "She loved the rain. Hated the floods. She wants whoever wears this next to know the difference."

Tamsin frowned. "How do we know who wears it next?"

"We ask." I turned the ring in the light. "That's the whole trade, Tamsin. That's everything I spent twenty years getting wrong and one very long year getting right. We don't take. We don't appraise like the thing is furniture. We ask what they want, and then we find the person who wants to listen."

"And if nobody wants to listen?"

"Then they wait." I smiled. "The dead are very patient. It's the living who are in such a dreadful hurry."

I said it lightly, but Tamsin wrote it down anyway, in the little notebook she kept for my aphorisms, which embarrassed and delighted me in equal measure. Behind her, through the workshop's open shutters, the new guild hall glowed in the afternoon sun — no longer the Ember Guild of ledgers and debts and fine print sharpened into knives, but something smaller, stranger, better. We hadn't settled on a name yet. The apprentices called it the Listening House. I suspected the name would stick, because names have a way of choosing themselves, just like relics do.

My left forearm ached faintly where the carver's mark sat beneath the skin — the seam of living bone I had opened a year ago tonight, with Wren's oath wrapped around mine and every ancestor I'd ever heard singing in my skull. On dry days I forgot it entirely. When rain came, or snow, or the particular cold that rolls off the Hollow border in autumn, it ached like an old break, and I found I didn't mind. A price should be felt, Meridian told me once. Otherwise how would you know it was worth paying?

Meridian visits less often these days. Not gone — never gone; none of them are gone — but quieter, the way a house goes quiet when everyone in it is finally sleeping well. Sometimes I catch her voice at the edge of the workshop, humming an old covenant-song to the bones on my shelf, and I let her. She earned her rest. They all did.

"Master Winterbourne?" Tamsin had her hand on the door. "The evening bell. And Wren says—" she paused, clearly savoring it, "—Wren says if you're late again she's telling the first-years the story about the time you fainted into the Drowned Choir."

"I did not faint. I knelt suddenly."

"That's what she said you'd say."

### Wren

Twelve ward-keepers stood in the training yard beneath the relit stars, and eleven of them thought consent was a formality.

I knew because I'd thought the same, once. You come up through the old guild, through the old academy, and they teach you wards the way you'd teach someone to build a wall: stone by stone, rule by rule, and the people on either side of it are somebody else's problem. It took a collapsing border, an apocalypse of starless dark, and one infuriating bone-carver to teach me that a ward is not a wall. A ward is an agreement. And an agreement nobody agreed to is just a cage with good posture.

"Again," I said. "Sable, your boundary-stone. What did you do wrong?"

Sable, a lanky former caravan guard with a permanent squint, looked at the practice-stone in front of him and sighed. "I didn't ask."

"You didn't ask *what*, specifically?"

He squinted harder. "...I didn't ask the ground?"

"The ground, the river under the ground, the dead in the ground, and the folk on either side of the line you're drawing. A covenant of consent means every party with a stake gets a voice. Yes, even the dead. *Especially* the dead — they'll be living with your ward long after you've stopped maintaining it." I walked the line of students, boots crunching on gravel. "Under the old system, we sealed borders with whatever bone the ledger told us to use, and if that bone happened to belong to someone who never consented to anything, well. Three hundred years later, the stars go out, and everybody acts shocked."

A few of them laughed. Good. Humor keeps the horror at a teachable distance; Bram taught me that too, though he'd deny it.

"Keeper Ashvale." That was Fen, youngest of the twelve, hand half-raised out of habit from some previous life. "Is it true you were stripped of your license?"

"It's true."

"Is it true you got it back?"

"I got something better. A commission signed jointly by both realms." I held up my wrist, where the twin mark sat mirrored against Bram's — his carving, my keeping, one oath renewed in fire and starlight. "But here's what I want you to carry out of tonight: the license wasn't the disgrace. The disgrace was all those years I kept perfect wards and told myself the hollow-eyed people on the far side didn't count. I was good at my job. That was the problem."

Fen wrote that down. They all write things down. Somewhere in a drawer in my quarters there's a growing stack of notebooks full of my worst days turned into other people's lessons, and I've made peace with it. Mostly.

The evening bell rang across the yard, and twelve faces turned toward it with the same hunger I felt in my own chest. One year ago tonight, the sky went black. Tonight the stars burned overhead in their hundreds, thick as sparks off a forge, and the anniversary vigil waited for us at the border — the whole valley turning out, both realms' folk mingling at the gate that breathed instead of barred.

"Class dismissed," I said. "Go home. Put on something warm. And Sable—"

"Yes?"

"If you tell anyone I'm nervous about tonight, extra drills."

"You're nervous about *tonight*? You fought wraiths wearing the faces of dead founders!"

"Wraiths don't invite your family." I slung my cloak over my shoulders. "Bram's cousins are coming. All nine of them."

Sable's laughter followed me out of the yard, and underneath it, faint as a struck bell, I heard the ancestors laughing too. They've taken to eavesdropping on my classes. Bram says it means they approve of me. I say it means I'll never have a private moment again, and honestly, after a lifetime of lonely duty, I wouldn't trade it.

* * *

### Bram

Wren found me at the gate of the Listening House, dressed for the vigil and pretending not to be impatient, which is her second-best disguise after *not nervous*. She looked up at the sign above the door — still unpainted, still just Bram's own carving, a flame and an open ear — and shook her head the way she does when she's fond of something and won't say so.

"You're going to leave it unsigned forever, aren't you."

"A guild without a master's name over the door," I said, locking up. "Scandalous. People might think anyone could walk in and be heard."

"Heaven forbid." She took my arm, right side, the side where the ache lives, and pressed her palm over it briefly — her version of asking how I am, which I answered by leaning into it slightly. Thirty years of words between us, and we've gotten efficient. "Your cousins arrived early. They've already interrogated Tamsin about whether you feed your apprentices properly."

"Tamsin will have told them the truth, which is that I forget lunch and she feeds *me*."

"Mm. And the vigil? Ready?"

"As ready as anyone is." I looked east, toward the border, where lanterns were beginning to bloom along the old ward-road like a constellation laid on the ground to match the one overhead. A year ago I had walked toward that same border carrying a secret shaped like my own death. Tonight I walked it carrying nothing but a flask of mulled cider and Wren's hand in mine, and I want it recorded — in the new ledger, in whatever ledger the dead keep — that the second walk was better.

The crowd at the gate was larger than last year. Word travels, especially through families who got their dead back. I saw a woman embracing a father who'd crossed lawfully through the Hollow gate that morning, gray and luminous and grinning. I saw guild apprentices — mine, and Wren's, and a few defectors from the old academy who'd come to see what consent looked like in practice — clustered around the Covenant Stone's daughter-shard, which we'd planted at the border crossing where it hums contentedly and grants passage to anyone who asks politely. I saw the guild master's replacement, a reformed accountant named Odile who once fined him six years of wages using his own ledger's fine print, holding court near the cider table.

And underneath it all, beneath the music and the voices and the smell of woodsmoke, I heard them. All of them. The ancestors who had ever kept the flame — Meridian and the three hundred years of Winterbournes before her, the ward-keepers, the unnamed dead who had built the first Stone from grief and hope and no small amount of stubbornness — gathered close as moths, singing so softly that only someone with carved ears could catch it.

They were saying thank you.

They had been saying it for a year. I'd stopped being able to hear anything else in it.

"Bram." Wren's voice pulled me back. She was looking up, and I followed her gaze, and there — directly overhead, in the place where the first star had reignited mid-rite while I screamed and she held on — the sky was doing the thing it does every year on this night. Slowly, one by one, in an order no astronomer has ever managed to chart, the brightest stars flickered. Once. Twice. Like a page being turned. Like a ledger being closed. Like a heartbeat.

The crowd hushed. Twelve hundred people, both realms, silent under a sky that winked at them like an old friend sharing a joke across a crowded room.

"Do you feel that?" Wren murmured. Her hand had found mine again without either of us deciding it.

"I feel it every year," I said. "I think it's them. All of them. Everyone who ever carried this."

"Everyone who ever chose to," she corrected gently.

And there it was — the distinction I had crossed a continent and nearly died to learn, delivered in my beloved's dry voice under a mended sky. Meridian had been given no choice; she had made glory of it anyway, and I honor her for it. But the covenant that holds now, the breathing border, the gate that opens for the asking — that one was built differently. It was built by two people who argued about who would die for whom, and then refused, together, to let the argument be settled by anyone's death. It was built from living bone and a broken oath remade. It was built on a Tuesday, actually, though the ballads disagree.

The stars steadied. The crowd exhaled. Somewhere behind us, Sable started a song, and Fen harmonized badly, and Tamsin dragged me toward the cider by the sleeve, and Wren pretended to resist and did not resist at all.

Later — much later, when the lanterns had burned low and the cousins had finally gone home and the border gate sighed itself quiet for the night — Wren and I stood alone on the ward-road with our marks aching in the cold, hearing the last of the ancestors fold themselves away into their long, contented sleep.

"One more year," she said.

"One year down," I said. "However many we get."

"That's the spirit." She bumped her shoulder against mine. Above us the stars burned steady and sure, and below us the earth hummed its consent, and in my chest, in the marrow they'd reshaped, in the ledger I'd rewritten with my own hands, something settled that had been unsettled since the night I was born into a family chosen by other people's choices.

I am a Winterbourne. I was born to bone and debt and a covenant I never signed.

But standing there in the dark with her hand in mine and the dead singing thank you, thank you, thank you — I understood, finally and completely, what I had carved into the world a year ago tonight:

Some inheritances are chosen.
