## 1. The Date That Should Not Exist

There are three rules to guiding smugglers through Thornfell, and I've kept all of them for eleven years. One: never take a road you haven't walked at night before. Two: never let the cargo know it's cargo. Three: never, ever listen to the wolves.

I broke rule three on the last night of autumn, in the black pines above the Vesper Road, and I've regretted it every day since.

Not because listening is dangerous — though it is, gloriously so. Because once you hear what the wolves have to say, you can't unhear it. And what they said that night was this: *the crown is empty, the crown is empty, something sits beneath it, run, little brother, run.*

I crouched in the bracken with my hand pressed flat against cold earth and let their voices pour through my palms like water through fingers. Beast-speech isn't sound, exactly. It's more a pressure behind the eyes, a meaning that arrives already understood. My mother died for it. Burned, when I was nine, while the magistrates read her crime aloud in careful, legal syllables: *consorting with beasts, speaking with unclean mouths.* So I learned to keep my gift folded up small inside me, like a letter I'd never send, and to make my living as nothing more than a very good tracker with very good luck.

"Montrose." Tam Vey's whisper came out of the dark ahead, sharp with nerves. "The pass. Is it clear?"

"The pass is clear," I said, which was true. What I didn't say was that the wolves were clear too — moving east, all of them, whole packs abandoning territories they'd held for generations, streaming toward the capital the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone. Wolves don't migrate toward danger. They flee from it.

Unless, apparently, the danger smells like dinner.

* * *

We made the river crossing by moonrise and camped in the ruin of a chapel that had been old when Thornfell was young. The smugglers liked it — solid walls, one door, no windows left to betray firelight. I liked it for other reasons. Old holy places are quiet in the way animals are quiet around me; whatever gods had been worshipped here had left the building the way a family leaves a house, taking the noise but not the warmth.

Tam set his men to counting crates and I set myself to the far corner, where a heap of fallen masonry half-buried something that caught the light wrong. Not gold. Paper. A book, vellum over oak boards, wedged into a gap in the wall like someone had hidden it in a hurry centuries ago and no one since had been brave enough or bored enough to look.

I should have left it. Rule four, the unwritten one: never touch things that have been hiding.

But the book was warm. Warm as a living thing, in a ruin where nothing had been warm for three hundred years, and my hands — traitorous, curious, my mother's hands — pulled it free.

It fell open across my knees to a page near the end, as if it had been waiting at a marked place. The script was archaic, the ink gone brown, but the words were plain enough:

*In the last autumn of the thirteenth turning, when the wolves forsake the eastern wood and gather at the city's throat, the speaker shall come. He will walk among thieves and wear poverty like a coat, and he will carry in him the voice of every creature, though he has told no soul. His mother burned for it. His father's name he does not know.*

I stopped breathing.

The chapel was suddenly very loud — wind through broken rafters, water ticking somewhere in the dark, Tam's men laughing low over their ledger. All of it distant, underwater. Because whoever had written those words had written *me*. Not a description. A biography. The coat of poverty I wore because fine clothes get remembered. The mother who burned. Even the father-shaped hole I'd spent thirty years not thinking about.

My thumb moved down the page, past the description, to the margin beside it, where a later hand had added a date in smaller, sharper script:

*Tonight.*

No year. No month. Just *tonight*, inked beside a prophecy about a man who could speak with beasts, in a chapel on a smuggling route only a handful of people alive knew existed, on the exact night the wolves had turned from their dens and begun to run.

"No," I said out loud, and my voice cracked on it.

Denial is a comfortable country. I'd lived there most of my life; I knew its roads better than any in Thornfell. But even denial couldn't explain away the date. Someone, a hundred years dead, had known I would be sitting exactly here, exactly now, holding this exact book. And prophecies — I knew this much from the ballads, from the burnings — prophecies don't describe the world. They *make* it. They reach forward and close their hand around you, and everything you do afterward happens inside their grip.

I read on, because of course I did. My hands were shaking and I read on anyway.

*The king is dead. Long has he been dead. That which wears his crown wears his face also, and feeds upon the memory of the realm as a tick feeds upon blood. The kingdom forgets its harvests, its dead, its own name, and loves the thief that steals from it. Only the speaker can hear the true king still, for death does not silence a king who loved his beasts, and his voice lingers in the royal menagerie, in the oldest of them, until the moon completes its thirteenth turn.*

*Hear him. Speak for him. Or Thornfell forgets itself entirely, and becomes a garden where nothing grows but the thing beneath the crown.*

I closed the book. Opened it again. Same words. Closed it, pressed it flat between my palms, felt that impossible warmth seep into my skin like a fever catching hold.

Outside, far off to the east, a wolf began to howl. Then another. Then dozens, a chorus rising out of the dark hills, all of them singing toward the capital, all of them singing the same two notes over and over, and through the ground and up through my knees and into the place where I keep the letter I never sent, I heard them plainly:

*The crown is empty. The crown is empty. Come, little brother. Come and see.*

* * *

Tam found me an hour later, still sitting in the corner with the book. "Found treasure?" he asked, grinning, and I almost lied to him. Almost said *old hymnal, worthless*, and tucked it back into the wall, and walked away at dawn, and spent the rest of my life telling myself the wolves had been singing about weather.

Instead I heard myself say: "Have you ever been to the palace?"

Tam blinked. "The palace? Nobody goes to the palace. King doesn't receive anyone anymore. Hasn't held court in — " He frowned, and I watched the frown deepen, watched him grope for something that should have been easy to find. "Hasn't held court in... a long time. Years, maybe. Why can't I remember the last time? Why can't I remember *any* court?"

His voice had gone strange. Thin. Like a man feeling along a wall for a door that used to be there.

"Because it was taken," I said quietly. "Your memory of it. Something took it."

He laughed, uncertain, and rubbed his arms like the cold had gotten into him. "You're a strange one, Montrose. Good guide, though. Best I've hired." He glanced at the book in my lap. "What is it, really?"

"A problem," I said. "A problem with tonight's date written next to my name."

That wasn't quite true — my name wasn't in it. Just my mother's burning, my father's absence, my secret, my poverty, my exact self laid out in brown ink by a hand a century dead. Close enough to my name to be worse.

Tam went back to his ledgers. I sat in the ruined chapel with a warm book and listened to the wolves sing the kingdom toward its own forgetting, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding all night.

The royal menagerie sat inside the palace walls. The palace sat in the capital, a hard week's ride east. The moon hung overhead, fat and full, beginning its final turn. And the prophecy said the oldest beast in that menagerie still carried a dead king's voice — a voice only I could hear, if I chose to hear it, if I chose to stop being a good tracker with good luck and become instead the thing my mother had burned for.

Every instinct I owned said run west. New routes, new names, new lies. I'd rebuilt myself once from ash; I knew how.

But the book was warm in my hands, and the wolves were calling me little brother, and somewhere in a silent menagerie an old animal was carrying the last true words of a murdered king, alone, in the dark, waiting for the one person in Thornfell who might — *might* — be fool enough to listen.

I looked at the date one more time. *Tonight.* Already bleeding into tomorrow.

"Well," I said to the empty chapel, to the wolves, to the hundred-years-dead hand that had written me into its story without asking. "Damn you all."

And I started packing for the capital.
