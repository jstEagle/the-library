## 2. The Justice Without a Court

I have been arrested exactly once in my life, and I want it on record that it was not my fault. It was the wolves' fault, and the moon's, and mostly the fault of a woman with a broken nose and a warrant she'd written out to herself.

She took me at the ford below Harrowgate, where the smugglers' road dips close to the king's highway — a stupid stretch of trail I'd avoided for six years and chose that morning because the wolves had told me the lowlands stank of iron and forgetting. I should have listened harder. Instead I was three lengths from the water when the net came off the willow branches and dropped me into the mud like a sack of turnips.

"Jonah Montrose," said the woman above me. "Tracker. Guide. Very good at pretending he doesn't hear things."

The mud was cold and smelled like everything I'd ever buried. "You have the wrong man," I said, which is what everyone says, which is why nobody believes anyone anymore.

"Do I." She crouched, elbows on knees, and studied me the way a butcher studies an animal whose weight he's guessing. Her hair was grey at the temples though her face wasn't old enough for it, and her coat had once been royal blue before someone — probably her — stopped caring about such things. "Three nights ago, near Thornfell's western wall, you spoke to a dog. Not *at* a dog. *To* one. And it answered."

"The dog barked."

"It told you the mist had taken its master's name, and you told it you were sorry."

I looked at her for a long moment. Rain dripped off the willow. Somewhere downstream a heron complained about us both.

"You're Wren Ashvale," I said finally.

Something moved behind her eyes — surprise, quickly strangled. "You know me."

"Everyone knows you. The justice who tried to put the crown itself on trial. They stripped your office and let you keep your life as a joke." I spat mud. "What does a disgraced justice want with a smuggler's guide?"

"What does a smuggler's guide want with the dead?" She stood, brushing off her knees. "Because that's what's walking around Thornfell these days, Montrose. The dead, wearing our memories like borrowed coats. And you're the only man in this kingdom who can hear them complain about the fit."

* * *

They did not take me to a jail, because Wren Ashvale no longer commanded jails. She took me to the palace.

That alone should have frightened me more than it did. The royal menagerie sits in the oldest quarter of the palace grounds, past gardens gone wild and fountains full of leaves, behind gates that hadn't been locked since — well. Since, apparently. The guards at the outer wall saluted her out of habit and then looked confused about why they'd done it, their faces going smooth as pond ice when she passed. Forgetting. It was everywhere now, like damp coming through plaster.

Inside the menagerie, the animals were quiet in a way that raised every hair I owned. Beast-speech isn't words, mostly. It's pressure, and scent, and meaning arriving whole in your skull like a stone dropped down a well. What I felt from those cages was not fear. It was *waiting*. Every beast in that place was waiting for something, the way a house waits for its owner to come home.

Wren unlocked a side gate with a key she wore on a cord around her neck — a justice's key, older than her disgrace. "The menagerie keeper died two years ago," she said. "Nobody remembers hiring a replacement. Nobody remembers feeding them. And yet they're alive."

"Something's keeping them," I said quietly. "Something wants them kept."

We walked the rows together. A wolfhound pressed its great head against the bars and breathed my name without sound. A raven watched me with one eye and thought, very clearly, *thirteen*. When we reached the far enclosure — the old lion court, all cracked stone and dry moat — the rain had stopped and the light had turned the color of weak tea, and there he lay: a lion so old his mane had gone the grey of ash, ribs showing under hide like weathered fence rails.

He opened his eyes when I came near.

And Jonah Montrose, who had spent thirty years keeping his mouth shut, heard a voice say:

"*You're late.*"

Not beast-speech. Speech. Human speech, worn and rusted but unmistakably the voice of a king — the cadence of a man used to being obeyed, frayed now to threadbare velvet.

I sat down in the mud again. It seemed to be where I belonged that week.

"That's him," Wren whispered beside me. "That's — Montrose, is that—"

"The king," I managed. "Or what's left of him. He's been living in this lion since—" I listened. Grief has a texture; this grief had been aging like cheese in a cellar. "Since before he died. However long ago that was."

"Four years," Wren said. "Officially, the king has ruled four years. Unofficially, I've stood in audience chamber three times in that span and come away unable to remember his face. Neither could the clerks. Neither could his own sister." Her voice cracked on the last word and she didn't try to hide it. "I built my whole life on the law, Montrose. And the law answers to a throne that eats memory. So either I burn the law or I burn the lie. I've decided which."

The lion's amber eye found mine. "*The girl with the broken law,*" the dead king said, amused, tired. "*She asks good questions. You ask none at all. That must change, beast-speaker, if you mean to save us.*"

"I don't mean to save anyone," I said. "I mean to go back to smuggling and being nobody."

"*You found the prophecy.*"

My blood went cold. "How do you know about—"

"*I wrote half of it.*" The great head shifted, and the smell of dust and myrrh rose off him, incense from a funeral no one remembered holding. "*Before it took my name, I set down what I could. Dates. Instruments. A listener. The rest I hid where only beasts would think to look, because beasts are the one thing it cannot make forget — it has no hooks in them. No names to steal.*" The eye blinked, slow. "*You are the instrument, Jonah Montrose. Your mother's gift, and your mother's courage, if you can find the second part.*"

"My mother was executed for less than what you're asking."

"Yes," said the king gently, in a dead man's voice. "*And the kingdom forgot her too. Shall we compound the crime?*"

* * *

**Wren**

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a palace after dark. I learned it as a child, visiting my father at his clerks' desks; I learned it again the night I was stripped of office, walking out through halls that pretended not to see me. Tonight the silence had teeth.

Montrose slept in the menagerie keeper's cottage, under guard of animals who needed no orders to watch over him. I sat across from the lion court with cold tea and colder thoughts, watching him talk to the beast in low murmurs. He didn't know I could see his mouth move. He didn't know that when the lion answered, something in his face opened like a door in a house long shut up.

I had spent four years collecting evidence no court would hear. I knew the exact hour the king's letters changed hand. I knew the treasurer signed documents in a script two degrees wrong. I knew eleven people who'd walked into the throne room certain and walked out hollow, smiling, empty as eggshells. And every night I lay awake knowing that proof meant nothing, because proof requires someone left with enough mind to weigh it.

But a boy who talks to animals — that wasn't proof. That was heresy. That was the pyre.

And yet.

When he'd spoken to the lion, I had felt something answer in my own chest, some old bell rung once. I am not a mystical woman. I am a woman of writs and witnesses and the lovely clean machinery of due process. But I have also watched a kingdom forget its king while bowing to his shadow, and I will tell you plainly: when the law goes mad, sanity looks like sorcery.

He emerged from the court near midnight, hair full of straw, looking younger than his crimes warranted.

"He says the thing beneath the crown feeds on thirteens," Montrose said. "Thirteenth year, thirteenth moon. In thirteen turns of the moon it finishes digesting the kingdom, and then there won't be enough of us left to even notice we're gone."

"And it needs you alive until then?"

"It needs me *willing*. Apparently the death-song has to be given, not forced." He rubbed his face. "It also says the mists will start taking people I care about to make me willing the other way. To break me instead."

I looked at him — this smuggler, this liar by trade, this man whose mother burned for the gift he'd just bargained away to a stranger with a broken nose. Everything sensible in me said: use him and lose him, before he uses you.

Everything sensible in me had been wrong for four years.

"Then we'd best get you somewhere safe," I said, "and teach you to sing."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, crooked with fear, and I found — to my considerable professional embarrassment — that I wanted to see it again.

"The last person who taught me anything got exiled," he said.

"The last person who trusted me got forgotten." I held out the tea. It had gone cold hours ago. He drank it anyway. "We'll make a fine pair."

Above the lion court, through a tear in the clouds, the moon showed herself — and I would swear before any court, dead or living, that she was thinner than she'd been the night before. Counting down. Thirteen turns, the king had said.

We had twelve left to learn the song.

And somewhere across the sleeping city, in a throne room lit by candles no one remembered lighting, something ancient wearing a dead man's crown turned its head toward the menagerie — and began, softly, to hum.
