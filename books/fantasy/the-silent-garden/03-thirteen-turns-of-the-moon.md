## 3. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

The last moon had begun to wane, and it was eating our friends as it went.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all we had left. It wasn't killing them. Killing would have been a mercy with a shape you could grieve. The mists came at dusk off the lowlands, smelling of rain and old paper, and anyone they touched simply forgot — forgot us, forgot the cause, forgot their own names half the time and stood blinking in the road like men who'd walked into the wrong life.

Old Tammerick, who'd hidden us in his mill for six nights, greeted me on the seventh as a stranger and asked if I'd seen his daughter. His daughter had died forty years ago. He wept when I told him, because to him it was fresh news, and then he thanked me kindly for my trouble and shut the door.

"That's what it does," Wren said beside me on the road, her voice flat the way it goes flat when she's furious enough to file charges against the weather. "It doesn't take lives. It takes *testimony*. Every person who might have stood in a court and said *I saw the king die* — gone. It's building itself an alibi."

"It's building itself a kingdom," I said.

She didn't answer. She'd stopped arguing with me about which of those things was worse, mostly because we'd both realized the answer was *yes*.

I should explain how I knew what the beasts were saying, since apparently that's still a secret I'm keeping from strangers even while committing treason with one. Wren knew, of course. She'd known since the lion — since the night a voice like dry autumn leaves had come out of that old animal's mouth and said my name in a dead king's cadence, and instead of running or arresting me, Wren Ashvale had sat down on the filthy menagerie straw and said, "Well. That changes the evidentiary picture considerably."

That was Wren. The world could end and she'd reach for her notes.

* * *

**Wren**

There is a particular kind of cowardice that disguises itself as procedure, and I spent fifteen years practicing it. A justice of Thornfell learns early that the law is a wall between the powerful and everyone else, and that the wall has doors, and that the doors are sold. I told myself for years that if I just followed the forms faithfully enough, the forms would save somebody someday. Then the king stopped speaking. Then the king stopped signing anything but proclamations of celebration. Then I asked one question too many about why the royal seal had changed its crest by a single leaf, and I found out what happens to justices who notice: disgrace, dismissal, and a quiet suggestion to leave the capital before the mists found something in my memory worth taking.

So yes. I fell in love with a smuggler who talks to animals. You may write it on my headstone. *She finally broke a rule and it worked.*

We had eleven nights left when Tammerick lost himself, and nine by the time we reached the widow Sera's farmstead above the river bend. Sera kept hounds, and Jonah — who pretends he merely understands animals the way good trackers do, a fiction so threadbare I'm amazed he bothers — stood among them each evening with his eyes closed, listening.

"They're frightened," he told me on the eighth night. He looked older than his years when he did that, listening. Older than me, almost, and I've been old since the age of nineteen. "Not of the mist. Of what's *in* the mist. There's something walking it that used to be human. The hounds say it smells like a man wearing another man's face."

"The regent."

"The thing that wears the crown." He rubbed his arms as though cold. "Wren, the wolves in Thornfell say the palace beasts have all gone silent except the lion. It's clearing them out. Everything that might remember the true king's voice." He paused, and I watched him decide whether to tell me the rest, and hated that I loved him enough to wait. "They say it knows about me now. What I am. It's not hunting us generally anymore. It's hunting *me* specifically."

"Then we run," I said. I heard my own voice go hard. "North past the border stones. There are free holds beyond the Greywater. We could be there in four days—"

"And the lion dies on the thirteenth turn, and the true king's spirit unravels, and whatever wears his crown rules here until the sun burns out." He wasn't arguing. That was the worst part. He was stating facts the way he read trails, patiently, sadly, already knowing where they led. "You taught me about testimony, Wren. Somebody has to survive to give it."

"I am not going to let you walk into that throne room as bait."

"You're not *letting* me do anything," he said gently, which was infuriating precisely because it was true. "But I'd like you to come with me anyway."

* * *

**Jonah**

Here's the arithmetic of a doomed plan, laid out plain, because I promised myself no more secrets even the ones I keep from myself:

If we ran, we lived. Probably. The mist-things were slow over open water, and the Greywater ferries owed me favors. We'd wake up free every morning for the rest of our lives, and every morning I'd hear the lion's voice saying *boy, you were the only door left open*, and every morning Wren would watch me flinch and pretend not to, because she loved me and love is mostly pretending not to notice things.

If we stayed, one of us probably died, and it was most likely going to be me, and the best case was that my death bought a kingdom its memory back.

My mother burned for this gift. That's the whole of it, really, under everything else. She burned in the market square when I was seven, and she looked at the crowd the whole time — not at the pyre, not at the priests, at the *people*, as if memorizing them, as if they were the ones being taken somewhere. I built twenty years of hiding on top of that fire. And the prophecy book in the ruined chapel had inked my description beside tonight's date, and I had learned, these eight terrible weeks, that hiding was never actually safety. It was just dying more slowly in a smaller room.

On the ninth night, camped in the hollow beneath the standing stone where the old road crossed the marsh, I told Wren the plan.

"We don't sneak in," I said. "That's what it expects. It's spent months erasing witnesses — it thinks like a magistrate, it guards the record. So we make noise. We walk up the processional road at dusk, in the open, and I let it feel me listening. It'll want me alive long enough to learn who sent me, what I know, who else remembers. Every hour it spends curious about me is an hour it isn't spending on the lion."

"Or an hour it spends learning exactly how to destroy you."

"Also possible," I admitted.

Wren looked at me across the small fire for a long time. She has a way of looking at people like she's cross-examining them, and usually I enjoy it. Tonight I wanted to crawl into the marsh mud and stay there.

"If you die in there," she said, "I will spend the rest of my life prosecuting your ghost. I'll find procedural grounds. I always do."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in all that granite. "Don't give me the speech where you release me from obligation. I sat in a courtroom for fifteen years watching guilty men walk free because everyone sensible kept their heads down and called it wisdom. You are the least sensible thing that ever happened to me, Jonah Montrose, and I have never once regretted it, and if you think I'm letting you face that throne room alone so I can be safe in some grey free hold — you understand animals. Understand this."

Then she kissed me, which was not, strictly speaking, what I'd meant, but I have never once complained about losing an argument that way.

* * *

**Jonah**

The twelfth moon set thin as a paring. We stood at the foot of the processional road with the palace rising black against the pre-dawn, and the mist was already coming down off the hills toward us, patient as paperwork.

At the gates, the sentries' eyes had gone smooth and vacant, and behind them, in the dark of the throne room, something ancient shifted its weight on a stolen throne and turned its attention toward me like a lighthouse beam.

I heard it speak without sound, straight into the place where I listen: *Come in, little speaker. Let's discuss what you remember.*

Beside me, Wren drew a breath, squared her shoulders like a woman entering her own courtroom, and took my hand.

"Together?" I said.

"Together," she said. "Objection noted. Overruled. Walk."

We walked into the mist, and the doors closed behind us like a verdict.
