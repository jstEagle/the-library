## 4. The Death-Song

They say a man's life passes before his eyes at the end. Mine did not. Instead I got the throne room of Thornfell in excruciating detail: the forty-seven banners of the old houses, the gold-leaf peeling off the vaulted ceiling like dead skin, and the thing on the throne wearing the king's face the way a glove wears a hand.

I counted the banners because counting was better than thinking about what I was about to do. Forty-seven. The wolves outside the city had said *wrongness*; the chapel book had said *tonight*; Wren had said, with the particular flatness she reserved for terrible plans she'd already agreed to, "If you die up there, I will be very annoyed, and I am never annoyed."

Chains were the wrong word for what held me. They preferred *escort*. Six guards of the Silent Court — that was what the crown-thing's people called themselves now, though none of them knew why they'd stopped singing in the streets eleven years ago — marched me down the length of the hall while three hundred courtiers watched from the galleries like crows deciding whether something was food or merely interesting.

The king-that-was-not smiled at me with teeth that had been someone else's once.

"Tracker Montrose," it said. Its voice was honey poured over gravel. "You've been difficult to remember."

"That seems to be going around," I said.

Laughter, somewhere high in the galleries — one nervous bark, instantly strangled. Good. Fear was fuel, but so was laughter, if you could get it burning hot enough.

The mist had taken nearly everything on our way here. Old Marta at the crossroads inn, who'd fed me stolen apples for ten years and forgot my name mid-sentence as we rode past. The falconer's boy who'd opened the menagerie gate for us, whose face went smooth and blank before we'd cleared the wall. Every ally the crown-thing ate made its voice stronger and the world quieter, and by the time we reached the palace gates, Wren had to tell me her own name twice before I believed her.

But the lion had waited. Old Ashfall, gray-muzzled, half-blind, pacing his cage for twenty years for reasons no one remembered. When I pressed my hand to the bars, he'd looked at me with the true king's eyes and said, *You came. I was afraid even the beasts had forgotten.*

Beasts don't forget. That's the whole secret of my miserable gift, and the reason my mother died for it. People forget. People are forgetting right now, all across this hall, and they'll forget again tomorrow unless I finish this tonight.

Wren stood among the justices' bench, reinstated an hour ago by royal decree — the crown-thing thought it was clever, putting its most dangerous enemy inside the law where the law could smother her. It hadn't understood her at all. She caught my eye and gave me the smallest nod, the one that meant *the door is open, the plan is real, and also you look terrible.*

"Kneel," said the herald.

I knelt. Not to the throne. To the floor itself, to the stones my mother's blood had soaked into twenty years ago when they burned her in the square beyond these walls. Let the old stone remember what the people couldn't.

"You bring me a gift?" the king asked.

"A song," I said. "An old one. A death-song."

Silence. Three hundred people not breathing. The crown-thing leaned forward, and beneath the borrowed face I saw the hunger move, vast and patient, the thing that had been drinking Thornfell's memories drop by drop for eleven years because a kingdom that remembers nothing cannot miss its king.

"Sing it, then," it said, and there was greed in the honey now, plain as a knife under silk. It wanted the song. It wanted whatever memory lived in it. It had come to the bait like a fish to a lure, and Wren would have hated the fishing metaphor and used it anyway.

I closed my eyes and found the lion.

He was already waiting at the edge of my gift, the way he'd been waiting since the night in the menagerie, patient as only something ancient can be patient. Through the bond I felt his heart slowing, deliberately, the way he'd told me it must: *A death-song is not sung for the living, boy. It is given. My last breath for my king's last truth.*

*I can't,* I'd said. *It'll kill you.*

*Everything kills everything, eventually. The question is only what it buys.*

So I sang. Or rather, Ashfall sang through me, and my throat was only the instrument for a voice that had ruled this kingdom for thirty years before the thing beneath the mountain wore it hollow. The death-song of Aldric the Third: his coronation oath, his daughter's laugh, the plague year he'd spent in the poor quarter carrying water with his own hands, his final night — the true final night — when the crown-thing had come up from beneath the mountain wearing his brother's face and he had fought it alone in this very hall while his court slept and forgot.

Three hundred people heard their own history come home. I heard it break open in them — gasps, then weeping, then names being spoken aloud that hadn't been spoken aloud in years. A woman in the gallery screamed *Marta!* and clutched the rail. An old justice stood up shaking and said, "I filed the report. I filed the report of the king's death and I don't remember filing—"

The crown-thing rose from the throne.

The king's face slid off it like wax. What remained was older than kingdoms — a tall absence, a shape of hunger, the thing the wolves had smelled, the silence at the center of eleven quiet years. It made no sound at all, and its silence was the loudest thing in the world.

*Stop,* it said, without speaking, directly into the forgetting-place behind every eye in the hall. And three hundred people began to forget what they were seeing, forget why they'd wept, forget—

And then, through the high windows, from the direction of the menagerie, a lion roared.

Not a lion's roar. A king's roar. Ashfall, dying on the flagstones of his cage with his last strength spent on one perfect note of command, the same note Aldric had used to still panicked battle-lines, and every beast in Thornfell answered him — dogs in yards, horses in stables, the ravens on the palace roof, all of them crying out at once, and beasts do not forget, and their voices carried the memory back into the hall like a tide coming in.

The absence shrieked. It was a short sound, a door slamming on a house that had finally been named. Then the crown rolled down the steps of the dais, rang once on the stone, and spun to a stop, empty, ordinary, just gold.

What rose from where it fell — thin, translucent, flickering like dawn light through water — was a tired man in a traveling cloak. Aldric the Third looked at his people. He looked at me. He raised one hand, and where it passed, the air warmed, and I swear to you the frost on the windows began, quietly, to melt.

His mouth moved. No sound reached the hall. But I heard it, because I hear everything, and this is what the true king said:

*Namer. You gave me back my name and my people theirs. The garden behind the palace has been silent eleven years because nothing living dared grow under that thing's shadow. Plant it. And know that my choice was made long before tonight.* His fading gaze went past me, to the bench, to Wren Ashvale standing rigid with tears cutting lines through the dust on her face. *Justice does not need a crown. But if she ever wants one—*

He was gone before he finished, the way the dead are allowed to be rude.

Then three hundred people did the thing I had spent my whole life believing impossible. They did not condemn the beast-speaker. They knelt. First the woman who'd cried Marta's name, then the old justice, then the galleries, wave upon wave, until Wren and I stood alone upright in a field of bowed heads and I thought, absurdly, *my mother should see this*, and then thought, *she can*, wherever she is, and had to press my hand over my mouth to keep the sound inside.

"The court," Wren said, into the ringing silence, in her flattest justice voice, which fooled no one because her hands were trembling, "is adjourned."

* * *

Spring came early that year, or perhaps it simply came at all. By the second week of thaw the silent garden behind the palace was silent no longer — green shoots forcing through cracked flagstones, bees arriving from who-knew-where with the shameless confidence of creatures who have never once read a prophecy.

Wren found me there on my knees in the dirt, planting the king's roses, which had survived in seed form in the royal stores for eleven years because seeds, too, do not forget.

"The council wants to offer you a title," she said, sitting down beside me in the mud without hesitation, because dignity was for people with worse secrets than hers. "'Royal Voice,' they're calling it. Beast-speaker to the crown."

"I'm a smuggler."

"You *were*. Now you're a national hero. Try to keep up." She picked up a trowel and started digging beside me, badly. "Also, the council wants to know if the succession question should go to the old houses, or to a vote of the commons. The king's last words were, apparently, ambiguous."

"He wasn't ambiguous. He was interrupted. There's a difference."

"Mm." She glanced at me sideways, and under the mud and the exhaustion and the grief for a lion she'd barely known, there was something warm and terrified and entirely un-judicial in her face. "And what does the Royal Voice advise?"

I looked at her — really looked, the way you're only allowed to after you've walked into a throne room together planning to die — and decided that some prophecies deserve to be amended.

"I advise spring," I said, and put a rose in the ground between us, where anyone walking past could watch it grow.
