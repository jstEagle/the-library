## 1. The Child at the Door

The frost came early that year, the kind that turned the valley's windows into lace and made every breath a small confession. Dominic Brightwater was in his workshop, mending the jawbone of a woman who had died forty years before he was born, when the knocking started.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

He set down his awl and listened. In Hollowmere Valley, nobody knocked past sundoon unless they were dying or delivering something that couldn't wait for morning, and in eleven years of bone-carving here — of listening to the dead murmur through antler and femur and knuckle — he had learned that the two were often the same thing.

The dead, incidentally, had opinions about visitors. They always did.

*Someone small,* sighed the jawbone, which belonged to a chatty miller's wife named Odellia who had never once let him work in peace. *Small feet. Running.*

Dominic opened the door on a child.

She could not have been more than eight winters old, thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a cloak that had been fine once and was now mostly mud and frost. Her lips were blue. Her hands were bare. And clutched against her chest with both fists, held the way other children hold a wounded bird, was a token of carved bone no longer than his thumb.

Dominic knew it the way a man knows his own signature.

He had carved it himself, nine years ago, for Master Aldous Vane on the night the old man announced he was dying and would not be argued with. It was a knotwork of interlocking spirals, a puzzle-piece of stag bone polished smooth as river glass, with a hollow chamber at its heart that Aldous had asked him to seal shut and never ask about. Dominic had asked anyway. Aldous had smiled and said, *"Some doors are better locked from both sides, boy."*

It was supposed to be buried with him. Dominic had attended the funeral. He had watched them lower the box into the churchyard soil on the hillside, twenty paces from where he stood.

"Where did you get that?" he said.

The child looked up at him. She did not answer.

She *spoke* — that much was true — but it was no language Dominic knew, nor any language he'd ever heard described. The words came out low and layered, like wind moving through a cave system, like several voices speaking slightly out of step with one another. And the moment they left her mouth, three things happened in quick succession:

The candle behind him guttered sideways though there was no draft.

Odellia's jawbone, on the bench behind him, rattled once against the wood and went silent for the first time in eleven years.

And somewhere out in the frost-dark of Hollowmere, in the churchyard, beneath the orchards, under every stone that marked a grave — the dead stirred.

Dominic felt it the way you feel a change in weather: a pressure, a turning. His gift had always been quiet here, a gentle thing, a whispering at the edge of hearing. The valley did that. Everyone said so. Magic didn't take root in Hollowmere; charms curdled, hedge-witches' remedies failed, and Dominic's bone-listening came through muted, as if heard through wool. He'd built his whole life inside that softness. He'd told himself he preferred it.

Now the silence shuddered like a struck bell, and every grave in the valley leaned toward this mud-streaked child standing on his step.

"Come inside," he heard himself say, "before you freeze."

He would wonder, later, why he hadn't hesitated. Perhaps because she looked at him the way Aldous used to — as if she already knew what he was going to do next and found it mildly disappointing.

* * *

He fed her stew and sat her by the fire and tried, with increasing desperation, to be sensible about it.

"You can't speak our tongue," he said. She watched him, unblinking, spooning stew with the grim efficiency of someone who had not eaten recently. "I can't speak yours. So we'll do this simply." He pointed at the token, which she had placed on the table between them with great care, exactly centered, as if it were an altar piece. "Who gave you this?"

She touched her own chest. Then she made a gesture — hand flat, sweeping down and outward, the way one describes a hill going to sleep.

"Aldous," Dominic translated. His mouth had gone dry. "Aldous gave you this. Before he died."

She tilted her head. Then she corrected him: she touched the token, then the floor, then pressed her palm flat over her heart and held it there a long time. Beneath. Kept. Hidden.

"He hid you," Dominic said slowly. "He hid *this*. Under the ground."

Her expression flickered — something between frustration and grief — and she spoke again, those layered cave-wind words, and this time the fire bent toward her like a listener leaning in. Outside, Dominic heard dogs begin to howl in the village, all of them at once, and stop all at once too.

He picked up the token with hands that were not quite steady. Nine years sealed. Whatever was in that hollow chamber, Aldous had carried it into the ground and kept it there, and now here was a child who had dug it out — or been given it — and walked, alone, in winter, to the only door in the world where someone might recognize the carver's mark.

Because that was the point, wasn't it. That was the whole horrible, clever point. There was only one door.

"I'm a bone-carver," he told her, absurdly. "I make buttons. Hairpins. Occasionally I talk to dead people who won't let me finish my tea." He rubbed his face. "I am not whatever this is."

She reached across the table and put her small cold hand over his wrist, and said something soft — two syllables, almost tender — and for a heartbeat Dominic heard it not as noise but as meaning, arriving directly behind his eyes: *You are the one he chose.*

Then the dogs started again, and did not stop, and this time they were joined by something else — hooves. Many hooves, far off, coming down along the northern ridge.

* * *

Dominic climbed the track behind his cottage to the old watch-crag, the child wrapped in his second coat and absolutely refusing to stay behind, and looked out over the valley he had never once left in thirty-four years of living.

At the northern lip of Hollowmere, where the road descended between the twin stones everyone called the Gateposts, torchlight was gathering. Riders. A dozen of them at least, armored in a way no border lord of Dominic's acquaintance could afford, their banners too dark to read in the dark. They had drawn up at the valley's edge the way water draws up at a dam.

And they were not crossing.

He watched a rider spur forward through the Gateposts — and slow. Not stopped, exactly. Slowed, the way a man wades into deep water. The horse fought, screamed, backed out. The rider dismounted and stepped through on foot and staggered like a drunkard, and shouted something back in a language that made the child beside Dominic go rigid as a plucked string.

She knew that language too.

"They can't come in," Dominic murmured. "Nobody's ever been able to—" He stopped.

Everyone knew magic didn't work in Hollowmere Valley. It was a fact of the place, like the limestone and the good apples. But watching that rider fight his way back out through the invisible wall, watching the whole company halt and circle like wolves deciding, Dominic understood — with the sick, tilting certainty of a floorboard giving way — that the valley's silence wasn't a fact of nature at all.

Nature didn't build walls. Walls were *made*. And things that were made could be made to keep something *in* — or something *asleep*.

The child tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she was pointing at the riders, then at herself, then making the flat-handed gesture again — the sleeping hill — and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly old.

*They are coming for me,* she meant. *Because of what sleeps here.*

Down in the dark of the churchyard, beneath twenty years of grass, something knocked — once, twice, three times — against the lid of Master Aldous Vane's coffin.

Dominic had spent his whole life listening to the dead. He had never, until that moment, heard one knock.

"Well," he said to the child, whose name he did not know, whose language he could not speak, whom he had known for the length of one bowl of stew. "It seems we're leaving."

She nodded, unsurprised, as if she had already known what he was going to do next.

And far off on the ridge, the first rider raised a horn, and the sound that came out of it slid through the valley's silence like a knife through wool.
