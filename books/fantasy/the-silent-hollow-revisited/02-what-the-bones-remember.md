## 2. What the Bones Remember

The lowlands were mud and old grief, and Dominic had never been so far from either of his fires.

He carried the child on his back through the reed flats because her feet had split from running, and because — though he would have denied it under oath — he could not bear the way she flinched when he reached for her. She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and cold iron. And every few minutes, without seeming to notice she was doing it, she murmured something in that older-than-kingdoms tongue, and somewhere beneath the peat a dead thing rolled over in its sleep.

"Stop that," he said, for the fourth time.

She stopped. Then started again a minute later, quieter, the way a child hums.

The dead here were not like the dead at home. In the valley, they were muffled — whispers behind wool, voices heard through a shut door. Dominic had spent thirty years carving their last words into keepsakes for the grieving, half-deaf work, gentle work. Out here the graves were *loud*. He could feel every bone in the fen muttering at once, like a market square just before dawn, and at the center of all that noise was the token in his pocket, humming against his hip like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

He had carved it himself. Eleven years ago. A crescent moon folded into an open eye, cut from the femur of a horse that had died old and beloved. He remembered the work perfectly — the way the grain had resisted the eye, as if the bone itself were trying to keep a secret. He had carved it for Aldous Wren, his mentor, who had held it up to the light and said, with that dry smile:

*This one's not for any grave, lad. This one's for a door.*

*What door?*

*The one I hope never opens.*

Aldous had been dead six years. His ghost had haunted Dominic's workshop ever since — or rather, had *sat* there, in the good chair by the window, complaining about the quality of Dominic's glue and offering unsolicited opinions on everything from chisel angles to Dominic's love life (or lack thereof). Ghosts faded. That was the rule. But Aldous sat by that window sharp as the day he'd died, and Dominic had been grateful. Grateful, and too fond to ask why.

He was asking now.

"Aldous," he said aloud, to the empty grey afternoon. "I know you're riding along. You always do. So talk."

The child's breath caught against his neck. The reeds bent in a wind that touched nothing else.

And then, sour as vinegar: *"You shouldn't have left the valley."*

Dominic kept walking. "Funny. The riders at the valley's edge couldn't leave it either. Couldn't cross. Care to explain that?"

*"Some borders are kind."*

"Some borders are *built*, Aldous. I felt it. When those riders hit the valley's edge it wasn't like hitting a wall. It was like hitting a wall with a *seam*. Something made that silence." He ducked under a leaning alder, the child's small hands locked at his collarbone. "And I think you know who made it, and I think you know why, and I think — " his voice cracked, and he hated it — "I think you've known since before you gave me that token."

Silence from the ghost. Real silence, which from Aldous was louder than shouting.

The child patted his shoulder once, gravely, as if to say *there, there*, and Dominic almost laughed.

* * *

That night they sheltered in the ruin of a drover's chapel, its roof long gone, its little graveyard huddled around it like sheep. The child slept curled against his coat, one fist still knotted in the fabric. Above them, the thirteenth moon hung where no honest calendar said a moon should be — thin, bruise-colored, wrong.

Dominic took out his knife and went to work.

It was the oldest trick of his gift, and the one he used least: you didn't ask a grave what it knew. You asked its *bones*, and you let your hands do the asking. He chose the nearest marker — a drover, dead forty years, nothing but ribs and regrets — and set the token against a weathered femur, and began to carve. Not the bone. The air above it. Small strokes, the alphabet of listening.

The dead man's voice rose up like water finding a crack.

*...cold... the horses first... and then the year the strangers came down from the Hollow road, a woman with a babe wrapped in—*

The voice *stopped*. Not faded. Stopped, mid-word, as if a hand had clapped over its mouth.

"That's enough of that."

Aldous stood at the edge of the graveyard, solid as memory, arms crossed. Six years dead and still wearing that ridiculous green scarf.

"You're keeping them quiet," Dominic said. He did not stand. His hands stayed flat on the grave-dirt. "You've been keeping them quiet. For how long? Since you died? Or before?"

*"Dominic—"*

"A woman came down from the Hollow road. With a babe." His heart was going hard. "Eleven years ago. About the time you asked me to carve a door-token and told me it was nothing. About the time you started sitting in my chair like your business in this world wasn't finished."

*"If you dig into this, you will bury us all,"* Aldous said, and for the first time since his death his voice shook. *"Boy. Listen to me, because I have loved you since you were nine years old and crying over your first botched joint-cut. There is a name that must never be spoken. There is a war that ended because everyone agreed to forget why it started. The valley's silence is not a curse — it is a* treaty. *And that child is the last living proof that the treaty was signed over a mass grave."*

"Whose mass grave?"

Aldous looked at him, and in the moonlight his face was suddenly, terribly old.

*"Both armies'. At once. Together. They did it to each other, Dominic, in one night, and then both crowns spent a generation pretending the other side did it alone. The founder of the Hollow built the silence to stop the answering massacre. Gave up her own magic to do it. Sealed the whole valley deaf so no ghost, no bone, no whisper could ever testify."* He passed a hand over his eyes — a living gesture, an old habit, and it broke Dominic's heart. *"I was her student. Long before I was yours. And when the last of her bloodline came begging at my door with soldiers behind her, I sent her away and I kept my mouth shut and I let you grow up ordinary because ordinary was SAFE—"*

"And the token?" Dominic lifted it from the grave-dirt. It sang faintly against his palm. "You had me carve this. My own hands. Why would you need me for that unless — "

*"Because a seal needs a key,"* Aldous whispered, *"and a key made by a man who hears the dead can never be stolen. Only given."* His form was fraying at the edges, guilt eating him faster than death ever had. *"I meant to die with the secret. I meant for you to die with it. I never thought the child would find YOU."*

Down the fen, a horn sounded. Once, low, patient. The child woke instantly, silently, already climbing onto Dominic's back — a practiced motion, a child who had done this many times before, and that hurt worse than anything Aldous had confessed.

"They're not stopping at the valley border anymore," Dominic said quietly.

*"No,"* Aldous said. *"Their captain won't. She knew me, Dominic. Before the valley. She'll want to know what I told you."* He was nearly transparent now, only the scarf still solid, absurdly green. *"Whatever you decide — whatever you learn in the Hollow — remember that I lied to you because the truth killed everyone I ever loved, and I could not bear for it to eat you too."*

Then he was gone, and the graveyard's dead all exhaled at once, and the woman-with-a-babe story poured out of the drover's bones in a flood — names, dates, the sigil on the riders' shields, the direction she'd fled.

Toward the Hollow. Always toward the Hollow.

Dominic stood, settled the child higher on his back, and turned north.

"Hold tight," he told her. "We're going to meet your family."

She understood him. He was almost sure she understood him — and she tightened her grip like someone who had finally, after eleven years of running, been believed.
