## 3. The Thirteenth Turn

The Hollow did not want them there.

Dominic felt it in his knees first, the way the ground seemed to lean against each step, and then in his ears, where the dead of this place pressed close like a crowd at a hanging. In the valley, silence had been a blanket. Here, under the ruined arches of the Hollow itself, it was a held breath.

"They're still here," he murmured, mostly to himself.

The child looked up at him — Ash, he'd taken to calling her, because she had arrived out of fire and ash and would not tell him otherwise — and made the small clicking sound at the back of her throat that he had learned, over three weeks of flight, meant *yes but also I am tired of your observations.*

"I know," he said. "I'm tired of mine too."

He was beginning to understand her language. That was the trouble with bone-carvers: they spent their lives listening to things that were not quite words, and eventually the not-quite-words started arranging themselves into sense. Ash's tongue was older than kingdoms, older than the valley, and it lived partly in her hands, partly in the pitch of her voice, and partly — this was the part that frightened him — in the bones around her. When she spoke, the dead leaned in.

She tugged his sleeve and pointed. Through a collapsed colonnade, moonlight fell on a stairway going down into the hill itself, and at the top of the stair stood two pillars carved with figures whose faces someone had chiseled away centuries ago.

*Below,* her hands said. *The First One sleeps below.*

"The founder," Dominic said. "Whose name nobody speaks."

Ash's chin lifted in that particular stubborn way she had, the one that reminded him uncomfortably of himself. She reached into her shirt and drew out the token — his own masterwork, the pendant he had carved for Merrick Vane thirty years ago, a coiled serpent eating its own tail with a face too honest for either end. His mentor had worn it every day of his life and then died wearing it, and now here it was around a child's neck, which meant Merrick had known, had always known, and had lied to Dominic about everything that mattered.

"Come on, then," Dominic said wearily. "Before whatever's chasing us catches up."

*They are already here,* Ash signed, and smiled, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The tomb was not what he expected. He expected grandeur — a tyrant's vanity in marble. Instead he found a small round room, humble as a root cellar, with a single stone bier in the center and no lid upon it. The bones lying there had been arranged with care, hands folded, and they were *singing*.

Not loudly. Not the way the valley dead sometimes moaned through Dominic's gift when storms came. This was a low, patient harmony, like a hymn hummed by people who had all the time in the world, and every bone in Dominic's body wanted to join it.

"Stop that," he told his own skeleton, and Ash laughed — a real laugh, bright and startled out of her, the first one he'd heard.

Then the ghost arrived, and the laughter died.

Merrick Vane had been dead for six years, and in those six years he had haunted Dominic gently, usefully, complaining about the tea and correcting his carving technique. Now he filled the tomb doorway like smoke filling a chimney, and his face was the face of a man about to ask for something unforgivable.

"Dominic."

"You knew." Dominic didn't turn around. He kept his eyes on the singing bones. "You knew what she was. You sent her to me. And you let me believe the riders were conquerors, that the valley's silence was natural, that — " His voice cracked and he hated it. "Thirty years, Merrick. You were my father in every way that counts."

"And you were the only person I could trust to never ask questions," Merrick said softly. "Which is why I could never answer any."

Behind him, he heard Ash shift, and heard her make the low warning sound — *riders, many, close* — and he realized the singing bones had changed key. The hymn had become an alarm.

"Give her to them," Merrick said. "That's all. The Order of the Coil has ridden three hundred years to reclaim their founder's heir. If you hand her over at the tomb, before the thirteenth moon sets, the war stays buried with the rest of it. They take her east. You go home. You carve your little charms and drink your terrible tea and die warm."

"She doesn't want to go with them."

"She's eight. Nobody asks an eight-year-old."

Dominic turned then. "You did. You asked me. I was younger than she is, and you asked me if I wanted to be a carver, and when I said yes you never once decided things *for* me after that. So don't stand in a dead woman's tomb and tell me children don't get a say."

The ghost was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its command and gained something rawer. "If you speak the founder's name before the moon turns, Dominic — if you say it aloud where living ears can hear — the silence breaks. Everything the valley was built to bury comes up. Both armies. Both griefs. Three hundred years of it."

"Maybe it should come up," Dominic said. "Maybe that's what graves are for."

"That's not what graves are for," said Merrick Vane, who had spent his whole life keeping one, and vanished.

* * *

Ash sat on the edge of the stone bier, legs swinging, too short to reach the floor. Above them, through the tomb's mouth, they could hear the riders arriving — hooves, iron, voices in the old tongue that made the walls itch. Dozens of them. Perhaps more.

Dominic sat beside her. It seemed absurd, the two of them perched on a founder's coffin like sparrows on a fence, but there was nowhere else, and sitting down felt honest.

"What do they want?" he asked, in her language, badly — his accent was apparently that of a drunk badger, she'd informed him by gesture.

Ash considered. Her hands moved slowly, choosing words a fool could follow. *They want the war back. Their mothers' mothers promised it. They want to burn the valley because the valley burned us first.*

"Did it?"

Her hands stilled. Then, very carefully, in the smallest signs she knew: *I don't know. I wasn't born yet. But they say I have to hate, and I don't know how, and I'm afraid they'll teach me.*

Something in Dominic's chest went quietly to pieces.

"You listen to me," he said. "You don't have to hate anybody. Not for me, not for them, not for a woman three hundred years dead." He nodded at the singing bones. "Although between us, she seems reasonably pleasant for someone everyone's so frightened of."

Ash almost smiled. Then her hands rose again, urgent now, and her voice came with them — half-spoken, half-signed, the words tumbling out in the broken shape of someone using every tool she had:

*I only need one thing. Believe me. When I show you what's under the valley — believe me. Even if the ghost cries. Even if the riders kneel. Believe me.*

And Dominic Brightwater, who had spent his whole life believing dead people and doubting live ones, took her small hand in his large one.

"Ash," he said, "believing you is the easiest work I've ever done."

Boots rang on the stair. Torchlight climbed the walls. And from the bier beneath them, the founder's bones shifted their song — from a hymn, to a lament, to something that sounded terribly like a warning — as the first armored figure filled the doorway, removed her helm, and looked upon the child with three centuries of hunger in her eyes.

"Moon's nearly set," the captain said. "Decide quickly, carver."
