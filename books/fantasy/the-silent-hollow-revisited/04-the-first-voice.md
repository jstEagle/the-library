## 4. The First Voice

The thirteenth moon rose fat and silver over the ruined Hollow, and it did not look like a moon at all. It looked like an open eye.

Dominic stood at the threshold of the founder's tomb with the child's small hand wrapped in his own, and below them, in the broken amphitheater of the old court, two armies had assembled without a single sword drawn. The riders in their lacquered armor on the eastern slope. The valley folk — farmers, smiths, old Marta with her rolling pin still tucked in her belt as though she'd forgotten it was there — on the western rise. Between them, the dead.

The dead were everywhere tonight. Dominic could feel them the way you feel rain coming: a pressure, a gathering. They pressed against his bones like moths against a lantern glass.

"You don't have to do this," the mentor said beside him, and for once there was no commandment in the ghost's voice, only something worn thin and tired. "There is still time to carry her away. To keep the silence."

"The silence killed people, Aldric." Dominic had not spoken his master's name aloud in twenty years. It felt strange on his tongue now, after everything, like a coat that no longer fit. "It killed *them*." He nodded at the armies. "And it nearly killed this one." The child looked up at him. She had not spoken since the tomb opened. She didn't need to. She squeezed his hand once, hard, the way his mother used to when the boneset hurt but needed doing.

Below, the riders' captain — Serah Vane, who had known Aldric in life, who had wept at the memory of him even while hunting his heir across three provinces — lifted her voice. "Brightwater! You stand between us and what is owed!"

"What's owed," Dominic called back, and his voice carried strangely, the way sound carries over frozen water, "is a truth. And I'm the only man alive — or otherwise—" he glanced at Aldric, who winced, "—qualified to give it."

He turned to the child. "Whatever happens," he said, "you stay right here. And if anyone tries anything, bite them."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile of the whole terrible journey, and Dominic thought: *worth it. All of it, worth it for that.*

Then he stepped down into the amphitheater, alone, into the space between two armies and several thousand dead, and began to speak.

* * *

"Two hundred years ago," Dominic said, "there was no valley silence."

The words came easier than he expected. That was the thing about the dead — they didn't push. They *leaned*. Like children at a storyteller's knee.

"There was a court here, the Court of the Hollow, founded by a woman whose true name has been buried deeper than her body. A name I will give you in a moment, because both your armies have been killing each other for two centuries to possess it, and I think you ought to know what you've been paying for."

Murmurs. Steel whispered half out of sheaths. Above, the thirteenth moon seemed to lean closer.

"But first," Dominic said, "I want you to meet some people."

He raised his hands, and the dead came through him.

It was not like the bone-work he had done all his life, the careful interrogations of knuckle and vertebra. This was a floodgate. He felt them pour through his voice — dozens, then hundreds — and he let them, because they had waited two hundred years and he had only been afraid for one lifetime.

A woman spoke through him first, in a voice like dry leaves: *"I was a weaver of the Hollow. When the riders came — our riders, the ancestors of those on the eastern hill — I hid in the root cellar. They burned the house above me. Not for war. For a name."*

An older voice, cracked and male: *"I rode with the first vengeance host. We were told the Hollow court had murdered our king's children. We burned their granaries in winter. I watched families eat bark before spring. I have been ashamed for two hundred years."*

*"We told ourselves the massacre was theirs alone,"* said another, a chorus forming now, voices braiding through Dominic's throat until it hardly sounded like one man speaking at all. *"But we sealed the valley. We made the silence. We took the magic from this place so that no one could ever learn what we had done — what BOTH sides had done — and we called it peace."*

Serah Vane had gone very pale beneath her helm. On the western rise, old Marta had sat down heavily on a fallen pillar, rolling pin forgotten at last.

"The valley you call home," Dominic said, finding his own voice again, hoarse, "the valley where your farms grow and your children are born and nothing magical can follow — that silence was not a gift. It was a gag. It was placed over this land's mouth so the dead could not testify. Well." He spread his arms. "They're testifying."

And the dead rose.

Not as horrors. As witnesses. They stood in the moonlight among the armies — translucent, patient, terrible in their gentleness — weavers and riders and farmers and soldiers, the massacred and the massacring, standing shoulder to shoulder in death as they never had in life.

"They died together," Dominic said quietly. "They learned together, on the other side, what none of them would admit on this one. There was no righteous side. There was a founder who did a monstrous thing to end a war, and a retaliation that was worse, and two hundred years of descendants pretending the ledger balanced."

He drew a breath. Below him, in the hush, the child had come down the tomb steps on her own — he hadn't told her to; she simply had — and stood beside him, small and straight-backed, watching the armies watch her.

"And so here is the name you've been killing for," Dominic said. "The name meant to start the final war. The founder's true name, which would prove one army's claim and damn the other's forever."

He paused. He looked at Serah Vane. He looked at the valley folk. He looked down at the child, who nodded once — *be believed* — and he gave it to them.

He spoke the name.

It rang off the ruins like a struck bell, and nothing burned, and no army charged. The name simply *was*, hanging in the cold air, belonging to everyone and no one, and Dominic understood in that moment that secrets only have power while they are hoarded. Spoken, they become merely heavy. Heavy things can be set down.

"The war is not yours to restart," he told them. "It never was. It belongs to the dead, and the dead settled it the only way wars truly end — by grieving it together. All that remains for the living is whether you'll be wise enough to do the same."

For a long moment, nobody moved. The thirteenth moon hung full and silent.

Then Serah Vane removed her helmet.

She dropped it — it rang on the stones — and walked down into the amphitheater, past the risen dead, past two hundred years of inherited fury, and stopped before the child. And then the captain of the last army of the Hollow's enemies got down on both knees in the dirt, not as one kneels to an heir or a queen, but as one kneels to a small person who has been running too long.

"I am sorry," she said. "Not as a soldier. As a grown-up."

The child looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched the captain's cheek — the same gesture, Dominic realized, that Aldric had once used on him, that perhaps had been passed down hand to hand through generations of people trying to be gentle in ungentle times.

And the valley folk came down off their western rise, and the riders came off the eastern slope, and somewhere old Marta produced bread from absolutely nowhere, because Marta had opinions about grief and all of them involved feeding people.

* * *

Later — much later, when the fires were lit in the ruin and the dead had thinned back to whispers and the child was asleep against Serah Vane's shoulder with Marta's blanket around them both — Dominic found Aldric standing apart, looking at the moon.

"You lied to me for twenty years," Dominic said.

"Yes."

"I should be angrier than I am."

"You'll get there," Aldric said. "Give it a decade. You always were slow to boil." The ghost smiled, and it was the smile of the man who had taught him to carve, not the keeper of secrets. "You did well tonight, boy. Better than I ever managed. I kept the truth locked up. You gave it away."

"What happens now?" Dominic asked. "To the valley? To the silence?"

Aldric looked out at the fires, at the riders and farmers sharing them, at the sleeping child. "Now the valley learns to speak," he said. "And so do you. First voice of the new accord — that's what they're calling you already. I heard two of Vane's lieutenants arguing about proper etiquette for addressing you. One of them thinks you require a herald."

"A herald," Dominic said flatly. "I hear the dead for a living and they think I need someone to announce me."

"Every madman needs a good staff," Aldric said. His form was going thinner, silvered, the way candle smoke goes when the wick gutters. "I won't be staying, Dominic. The dead who testified — they've moved on. So may I. But I wanted to see it first. The valley, talking. The child, home." He faded a little more, and his voice came softer. "You were the best work I ever did. Don't tell the token. It was proud of itself."

And then he was gone, and for the first time in Dominic's life the silence where his mentor's ghost had been felt not empty but *earned*.

Dominic stood a while longer under the thirteenth moon — the last night it would ever rise over a silent valley — and listened. Not to the dead. To the living: the crackle of shared fires, Marta laughing, the child murmuring in her sleep in a language that was suddenly, everywhere, being learned.

Tomorrow there would be councils, and arguments, and the slow hard work of building an accord out of grief. Tomorrow he would have to decide what a first voice actually does on its second day.

But tonight — tonight, the Hollow spoke, and so did he, and neither of them was silent anymore.

From the fires below, the child's voice rose suddenly, clear and bright, calling his name in her old tongue — and then, carefully, proudly, in his.

"Dominic."

Smiling despite himself, he went down toward the light.
