## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when the knock came.

Sable Ravensworth knew every kind of knock Corvane could produce. The brisk double-rap of a merchant with coin and no patience. The hesitant scratch of someone ashamed to be there. The pounding of drunks. The silence-then-bang of the Wardens, who never knocked so much as announced. This one was soft, spaced too far apart, the knuckles lingering on the wood between strikes — the knock of a person rehearsing courage on a stairwell and losing it twice.

He pinched out the candle before answering. Old habit. A dark room told you more about a visitor than their face did; it made them hesitate in the doorway, and hesitation was information.

The woman on his step was wrapped in a gray traveling cloak gone green at the hem from the city's perpetual damp, hood back, hair the color of wet ash plastered to her cheeks. Snow had begun falling an hour past midnight — the first snow of the season, fat and unhurried, the kind that meant winter intended to stay. It gathered on her shoulders like the beginning of a shroud.

"You're the charm seller," she said. Not a question. Her eyes went past him into the dark of the shop, cataloguing: the shelves of little glass vials, each stoppered with wax and hung with a paper tag written in Sable's careful, unremarkable hand. *Calm for a sleepless night. Courage for a hard conversation. The smell of your mother's kitchen.* Charms. Trinkets. Harmless comforts, everyone in Lowmarsh agreed, worth a few coppers and no more.

"That depends," Sable said, "on what you've been told I sell."

"Memories." She said it plainly, without the flinch most people managed. "Not mine. Someone else's. I want to forget a day. Just one day."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Fifteen years in this city had taught him that people who came at midnight asking to forget were either lying about what they wanted or telling the truth about something unbearable, and it mattered very much which.

"It's cold," he said, "and my kettle isn't."

She stepped inside.

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostreth, widow of a rope-maker, and the day she wanted gone was the ninth of Emberfall, three years past. She didn't say what had happened on the ninth of Emberfall. They never did, at first. Instead she sat at his scarred worktable with both hands wrapped around a clay cup of tea she wasn't drinking, and talked around the wound the way water talks around a stone.

"He was a good man. Everyone says it at funerals, but with Tam it happened to be true. He mended nets for the harbor children free, even after they stopped needing mending, just because they liked watching his hands work." Her thumb traced the cup's rim, around and around. "Three years. You'd think three years would sand it down some."

"Memory doesn't erode," Sable said quietly. "It only settles. And some things settle wrong — sharp edge up, waiting."

"Yes." She looked up at him then, and her eyes were dry in the way that frightened him more than weeping would have. Dry grief was grief that had given up on relief. "That's exactly it. Sharp edge up. Every morning, waking, and for half a heartbeat he's not dead, and then—" Her breath caught. "I can't keep doing the half-heartbeat. I'd rather have nothing of that day than all of it, forever."

Sable rose and went to the shelf where the true stock lived — behind the charm-vials, behind a false panel he'd built himself from reclaimed coffin wood because superstition was cheap insurance. Inside were vials of a different order. Each held a stolen memory, drawn whole from a willing or unwitting donor, distilled to a pale luminescence that moved like smoke under oil. He kept them sorted by weight. Grief weighed heaviest. Joy was nearly weightless; joy sold itself and left no scars.

He found one near the bottom: a memory of loss, ordinary loss, the death of an old hound named Bracken who had belonged to a farmer upcountry. Clean sorrow, well-aged, its edges worn soft by two years of sitting still. It would do. It would fill the shape of her day with something sad but survivable, and when she reached for the ninth of Emberfall she'd find instead a dog dying gently in a barn full of hay-smell, and she would grieve it properly, and be done.

A small betrayal. That's all any of it ever was.

"I should tell you how it works," he said, setting the vial on the table between them. "You drink it. For a moment you'll feel everything in it — the memory wants to be lived before it can lie. Then it settles over the day you're giving me. It won't feel like yours afterward. It'll feel like a story you heard secondhand, and stories fade."

"And my day? Where does it go?"

"Into here." He tapped the vial. "And then nowhere. I destroy what I take. That's the craft — taking is easy, anyone with the knack can take. Knowing how to throw away is the trade."

It was a lie. He kept a hoard. But it was a necessary lie, the kind that kept widows sleeping and kept men like him alive, because no one sold their worst day to a stranger who might sell it back to them, or worse, wear it.

Maren Ostreth turned the vial in her fingers. The light inside stirred, sensing proximity to a mind that grieved.

"How much?"

"Two silvers."

"That's robbery."

"It is," Sable agreed. "Robbery is honest work. Pay me."

Almost, almost, she smiled. Then she counted out the coins, and drank.

* * *

He watched her take it. He always watched. It was part of the discipline — you don't sell a knife without knowing how it cuts.

For three heartbeats her face went slack, then flickered through the memory's weather: the barn, the hay-smell, the old dog's slow breath. Her mouth trembled. A single tear fell and she didn't seem to notice it. Then the new memory settled over the old like snow over a stone, and her shoulders dropped half an inch, and something in her jaw unlocked that had been locked for three years.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, that's — I had a dog like that. When I was a girl." She frowned faintly, reaching for the ninth of Emberfall and finding only fog where the horror had been. "There was something. Wasn't there something I—"

"Grief," Sable said. "You came about grief. It's paid now. Go home and sleep."

She stood, steadier than she'd arrived, and at the door she paused with her hand on the latch.

"They say you can buy happiness in here too. Whole lives of it, some say. Is that true?"

"No," Sable said. "Happiness doesn't keep. Only sorrow keeps. Sorrow's the only thing worth stealing."

She gave him a look he couldn't read — pity, perhaps, or recognition — and went out into the falling snow. He watched her cross the lane until the white swallowed her, and then he stood in his dark shop a long while, listening to the city hold its breath under the first snow.

* * *

Later, alone, he took out the day he'd taken.

This was the part no client ever saw, and no rule required it of him, and he did it anyway because the rules that kept you invisible and the rules that kept you human were not always the same rules. He uncorked a fresh vial, breathed once, and let Maren Ostreth's ninth of Emberfall pour into him — not to keep, but to know what he was carrying. To witness it. Someone had to.

A rope-maker's hands. Tar under the nails. The sound of rigging in wind. And then the accident, quick as a snapped line — a coil underfoot, a fall, the dock-edge, black water closing. Tam Ostreth drowned in ninety seconds and his wife had arrived four minutes too late, and she had knelt on those planks for six hours while the harbor watch waited politely for her to move.

Sable poured it into the destruction-vial, sealed it with black wax, and set it in the iron box where such things went to die. Tomorrow he'd burn the box's contents in the stove, as he did every week. Memory could not be unmade by fire — he was living proof of that — but it could be made unrecoverable, and unrecoverable was the closest thing to mercy his trade allowed.

Then he sat by the dead hearth with his own cold tea and counted his sins the way other men counted coins.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of small betrayals, each one defensible, none of them forgivable. The farmer whose first love he'd lifted for six coppers because the man begged for it and wept after anyway. The apprentice he'd stripped of a humiliation so completely that the boy lost the caution it had taught him and broke his arm within the season. The dozens of small kindnesses that were also thefts, the wounds he'd dressed by amputating. He had told himself for fifteen years that he dealt only in consent and surplus, that he was a surgeon of the inner life, and every night the same voice answered: *you are a fence, Ravensworth. You traffic in stolen goods, and the goods are people.*

He let the voice say its piece. Arguing with it was another discipline, and he'd learned that disciplines were all he had.

Because there were rules. Three of them, carved into him deeper than any creed, and he recited them nightly like a litany:

*Never steal from a Warden.* Their minds were trained labyrinths, warded against intrusion, and one wrong touch would ring like a bell through the whole order. More than one hedge-witch had vanished for trying.

*Never sell a whole life.* A fragment fades. A life taken whole leaves a hollow in the world that other things — hungry things, official things — come to investigate.

*Never be remembered.* No names in ledgers, no debts unpaid long enough to curdle into grudges, no faces held too long in any mind. He took payment and forgot his customers on purpose, scrubbing them from himself the way he scrubbed them from his vials, so that if a Warden ever pulled his memories apart they'd find only a gray corridor of anonymous transactions. Fifteen years, and he could walk through the Marsh Market and meet a hundred people he'd healed or robbed, and know none of them.

Weary discipline. That was the whole architecture of his survival, and he felt its weight tonight especially — the first snow always did this to him, laid the year's account open like a ledger.

Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere north, upriver, bells began tolling in a pattern Sable didn't recognize — not the hour, not a fire, not a festival. An alarm of some older make, the kind the city hadn't rung since before the Purges. He frowned at the window, at the white dark beyond it.

The bells tolled nine times, and stopped.

Sable sat very still, listening to the silence where they'd been, and told himself it was nothing. A mistake. Some Warden rite he'd never been meant to understand.

Then, above the wind, faint and impossible, from somewhere down the lane — a child crying. And beneath the crying, threaded through it like wire through wool, words in a language that had been dead for two hundred years.

A language Sable understood perfectly, and had sworn never to speak again.
