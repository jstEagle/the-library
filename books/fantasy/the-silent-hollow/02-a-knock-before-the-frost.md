## 2. A Knock Before the Frost

The first snow came at dusk, the way bad news always does in Corvane—quietly, from the north, without asking anyone's permission.

Sable Ravensworth watched it through the cracked pane of his shop window, the flakes drifting past the lamplight like ash from a fire no one had bothered to put out. He had seen fifteen winters in this city, and he knew what this one meant. By morning the Wardens would double their patrols. Cold made people desperate, and desperate people remembered things they'd paid good coin to forget. Desperate people came looking for him.

He turned the sign on his door anyway. *Closed for the season* was a lie he could afford until the frost markets opened in three days.

The shop smelled of tallow and dried lavender and something older underneath—the faint mineral tang of memories that had sat too long in glass. Sable moved through the back room with a candle, checking his inventory the way other men counted coins. Vials of gray light on every shelf, each one a stolen hour, a borrowed afternoon: a fisherman's last morning before the sea took him; a bride's laughter, sold twice because grief had made her careless about which version she kept; a child's first steps, which he had taken from a dying woman who wanted them to go somewhere warm instead of into the ground.

Small magics. Harmless ones. That was the whole art of surviving Corvane—never take more than anyone would miss, never give more than anyone could explain away.

Three rules. Fifteen years. Never steal from a Warden. Never sell a whole life. Never be remembered.

He was reciting them to himself, as he did every night, when the knock came.

It was not a loud knock. That was what unsettled him most, later, when he tried to reconstruct it. A soft sound, three taps, patient as snowfall. No one knocked softly at a charm-seller's door unless they were afraid of being heard—or unless they were too small to knock any harder.

Sable stood very still in the dark of the back room, candle cupped behind his hand, and listened.

The knock came again. Three taps. Then, unmistakably, a small voice—but not words. Not words in any tongue spoken south of the mountains, not the guttural market-speech of Corvane's lower quarters or the clipped formal cant of the Wardens' citadel. The sounds were older than that. Rounder. They seemed to come from somewhere beneath the language, as if the child were speaking through the floor of the world.

And Sable understood her.

That was the thing that stopped his heart mid-beat. He understood every syllable, the way you understand the meaning of a bell—not by translation but by recognition, deep in some room of himself he had never found a key to.

*The door is cold,* she said. *Please. The door is so cold.*

For a long moment he considered not answering. Fifteen years of discipline told him that nothing good knocked after dark in the first snow. But the candle trembled in his hand, and the voice came again—*please, please*—and there was something in it that sounded less like a request and more like an echo, as if she had been saying those words for a long time, to doors that had stayed shut.

He opened the door.

The girl couldn't have been more than six years old. She stood ankle-deep in fresh powder, wearing a coat meant for someone twice her size, gray wool gone darker where the wet had soaked through. Her hair was black and cut raggedly, as if someone had done it in haste with a knife. She looked up at him with eyes the color of river ice—that particular pale blue-green that looks almost white until you're drowning in it—and she did not shiver, though the cold coming off the street would have felled a grown man.

Behind her, Ashfall Lane lay empty and blue-white under the falling snow. No footprints led to his door.

Sable noticed that the way he noticed everything: instantly, silently, filed away against future need. No footprints. Either she had been standing there a long time while snow buried her tracks, or—

*You see me now,* she said, in that impossible language. *Good.*

His mouth went dry. "Who sent you?" he asked in Corvanish, because speaking the other tongue felt like opening a door in his own chest.

She only looked at him. Then she raised one small hand, mittened in cloth too big for her, and held it out—not asking for food, not begging shelter the way street children learned to do before they learned anything else. Offering her palm, flat and open, the way you offer your hand to be read.

"I don't know what you—" he began.

She stepped forward, quick as a bird, and pressed her bare fingers against his wrist.

The world broke open.

He was kneeling on his own threshold afterward, and the candle was out, and the girl was inside his shop standing among the shelves of gray vials like a small ghost surveying a graveyard. Snow drifted in over the sill. His hands were shaking. Somewhere far away a dog was barking, and it took him several breaths to understand that time had passed at all.

What he had seen:

Fire. Always fire first—a village burning under a sky the color of a bruise, rooftops collapsing in slow blossoms of orange, and figures moving through the smoke in long gray coats with hoods drawn low, carrying not torches but lanterns of white glass, and where the lantern-light touched, things *went out*. Trees. Chickens. People. Not burned—*erased*, leaving shapes of absence in the world, holes shaped like whatever had stood there.

A mother running. He knew it was the mother even though he never saw her face clearly—she was always half-turned, always looking back, her grip crushing the small bones of the girl's hand. Running along a river. Running through a birch wood where the snow was red. Running, running, and then the memory tore the way wet paper tears, right down the middle—

—and then fragments, scattered like dropped coins. A hayloft. A dead man's boots, too big. A word repeated over and over in a child's voice, a word in Hollowtongue that meant—I understood it and I still cannot render it—something between *shelter* and *remember me*, a word you say to a person, not a place. A word that means *you are where I am supposed to be safe.*

She had walked out of a burned village upriver, alone, following something only she could feel, and every door had stayed shut, and now she was standing in his shop saying that word at him with her whole ruined heart.

"No," Sable said aloud, to himself, to her, to the fire he had just witnessed. "No, no."

The girl tilted her head. *You saw,* she said. It was not a question.

"You can't be here." His voice came out rough, wrong. He crossed the room in four strides, took her by the shoulders—he meant to steer her back out into the snow, that was all, put her on the road to the charity house on Bellwether Street, wash his hands of it, survive another winter. But under his hands she weighed almost nothing, and she was warm, impossibly warm, warm as a hearthstone, and she looked up at him with those ice-colored eyes without fear.

*You are the keeper's son,* she said.

The floor seemed to tilt. "I'm a shopkeeper. I sell charms. I'm nobody's—"

*You are the oath-son.* And she said the word for *oath* in Hollowtongue, and hearing it did something to him he could not describe, some old lock turning somewhere behind his ribs, some door swinging open onto a room he had bricked shut so long ago that he had convinced himself the room had never existed.

He let go of her and stepped back. His heel struck a shelf and three vials chimed softly together, three little hours of other people's lives rattling in the dark.

"Listen to me." He crouched to her level, and made his voice the flat, hard voice he used on customers who asked too many questions. "Whatever you think I am, whoever told you to find me—they were wrong. There are no keepers. There is no sanctuary. There is a city full of men in gray coats whose entire purpose is to burn children like you out of the world, and if they hear one word of that language pass your lips—"

*Hollowtongue,* she said simply, and the word filled the shop like water filling a cellar.

He flinched. Actually flinched, glancing at the shuttered window as though the walls themselves might inform on him.

"Don't," he whispered. "Never say it aloud. Not here. Not anywhere."

She considered him with the grave patience of the very young, and then she did something worse than speak. She reached out and touched the nearest shelf—one fingertip against a vial holding a cooper's memory of his daughter's wedding—and the gray light inside flickered. Brightened. For half a heartbeat the liquid glowed gold, and Sable felt the memory straining toward her like a plant toward sun, the cooper's dead daughter laughing, laughing, leaning out of the glass—

The glow died. The vial settled. But Sable stood in the dark of his own shop with sweat freezing on his spine, because he had spent fifteen years learning exactly how much magic the Wardens could smell across a city block, and he had never once felt it reach for him like that. Like recognition. Like hunger answered.

*They are coming,* the girl said. She said it the way children report weather. *The lantern-men. They are following my footprints backward. They will be here when the moon is highest.*

"There were no footprints."

*There were,* she said. *I unmade them. But they know how to look anyway.*

Outside, the snow fell thicker, erasing the lane, the roofs, the whole gray geometry of Corvane. Somewhere to the north, beyond the river, beyond the hills, a village was smoking. And somewhere closer—close enough that Sable thought he could almost hear it, a sound like white glass ringing—lantern-light was moving through the dark, going from door to door, making the world quiet.

He looked at the child. Six years old. A whole burned life folded up inside her, speaking a language older than kingdoms, standing in his shop as if she had every right, as if she had simply come home.

Every instinct he had honed for fifteen years said *shut the door, bar the door, forget her name.*

But he had already seen her mother's face. He would carry that now whether he wanted to or not—it was in him, lodged under the skin like a splinter, the running, the red snow, the hand crushing hers. Some things, once glimpsed, could not be unsold.

"What's your name?" he heard himself ask.

She smiled then, the first time, and it was such a small, tired, ancient smile that it hurt worse than any vision.

*I had one,* she said. *It burned.*

Sable closed his eyes. When he opened them, the decision had somehow already been made somewhere below his discipline, below his fear, in that locked room behind his ribs where an oath he didn't remember was still keeping its watch.

"All right," he said hoarsely, bolting the door behind her, drawing the shutters, killing even the ember-glow of his lamp. "All right. You'll stay till the moon sets. Then we talk about where we go."

*We,* she repeated, tasting the word.

And far off across the sleeping city, thin as a needle, he heard it: the ringing of white glass, coming nearer.
