## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The village of Ashenford had been burning for three days before the snow finally put it out.

Ilse Varrow stood at the edge of what remained and let the cold work its way through her grey coat, down her collar, into the space between her shoulder blades where no amount of wool ever seemed to reach. The river had frozen in patches along the bank, and the smoke still rose from the ruins in thin grey threads, like the last breaths of something that hadn't quite accepted it was dead.

Fourteen houses. A granary. The little shrine to the River Mother that every upriver village kept, half out of habit and half out of fear that forgetting Her would bring worse luck than remembering. All of it black now, collapsed inward, charred to a uniform grey-white that made the whole settlement look less like a place where people had lived and more like a page burned through.

"Survivors?" she asked.

Sergeant Breck, a broad man with frost in his beard and twenty years of following her without ever quite understanding her, consulted his slate. "Three. A miller's boy with burns on his arms. An old woman, deaf as a post. And a farmhand who hasn't said a word since we found him—just sits by the relief fire and stares."

"The fire started where?"

"That's the strange part, Captain." Breck hesitated, and Ilse turned to look at him properly. He had learned, over the years, that her silences were more dangerous than her questions. "It started in four places at once. Or near enough. We've walked it. Four separate hearths, all flaring within minutes of each other, and no wind to carry it between them."

Ilse looked back at the ruin. Snow was falling again, fine and dry, settling on the ash without melting—the deep cold coming early this year, earlier than anyone could remember.

"Show me the survivors," she said.

* * *

The farmhand sat exactly where Breck had described, wrapped in a Warden-issue blanket he didn't seem to notice, staring at nothing. His hands were folded in his lap like a sleeping man's. When Ilse crouched across from him, his eyes moved to her face and stopped there, and she saw something in them that she had seen only twice before in fifteen years of service: not fear, not grief, but recognition. The look of a man seeing something he already knew.

"You're Varrow," he said. His voice was flat as slate.

"I am."

"They told us you'd come." He swallowed. "They told us before the fire."

Ilse kept her expression still. "Who told you?"

He laughed then—a small, broken sound, like a hinge rusting shut. "Nobody. That's the thing. Nobody told us anything. I just *knew*. Three days ago I woke up knowing the Wardens were coming, and I knew it the way you know your own name, and I couldn't stop knowing it, and then—" his hands began to shake, and he pressed them flat against his knees— "and then the girl came through, and everything got louder."

"What girl?"

"A child. Six years old, maybe seven. Dark hair, thin as a winter sparrow. She came down the river road with a woman—her mother, maybe, though they didn't favor each other much—and they stayed one night in the shrine house, and after that night I couldn't sleep. Every time I closed my eyes I remembered things." His voice dropped, went hoarse. "Things I never did. Things that never happened to *me*. A woman singing over a cradle. A house with a green door. Drowning. I remembered drowning, Captain, and I have never been in deep water in my life."

Ilse felt the cold move through her, but it had nothing to do with the weather now.

"And the fire?"

"After they left. The morning after. It just—it rose. Out of four hearths at once, like the houses themselves had decided to burn. And in the middle of it, standing in the road, the girl turned around and looked back at the village, and I heard her say something. One word. I don't know what language it was. But when she said it, I understood it anyway, the way you understand a blow to the head." His shaking had gotten worse. "It meant *remember*. She looked at Ashenford and told it to remember, and it did, and now it's ash."

He was crying without any change in his face, tears simply sliding down and freezing at his jaw.

"She spoke," he whispered. "But she never opened her mouth. That's the worst of it. The word came from everywhere. From inside my own skull. Like the memory of her voice had always been there, waiting."

Ilse stood. Her knees ached; she noticed distantly that she had been crouching far longer than she'd meant to.

"Describe the mother," she said.

* * *

That night, in the commandeered parlor of the miller's ruined house—half its roof open to the sky, snow drifting down onto the scorched floorboards—Ilse took out the order's own records and read them by lamplight until her eyes stung.

She knew what she was looking for. She had known since the farmhand opened his mouth. There was a signature to these things, a shape left in the aftermath, and the shape of Ashenford matched the oldest entries in the archive the way a wound matches the blade.

**Hollowtongue**, the records called it. Speech older than kingdoms. Words that carried living memory instead of mere meaning, so that to hear them spoken was to *receive* them—to be filled with another mind's past, uninvited, unfiltered. The first Wardens had been founded to hunt it, three hundred years ago, back when the memory-keepers still had sanctuaries in the mountains and the war between those who kept the past and those who devoured it was fought openly, with fire and with worse than fire.

The war was supposed to be over. The keepers were supposed to be extinct. The Hollowtongue was supposed to be a dead language, catalogued and sealed, dangerous only as history.

And yet here was a burned village and a farmhand full of borrowed memories and a child who spoke without opening her mouth, walking upriver toward Corvane like a spark walking toward a powder store.

Ilse turned a page. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above her, the ruined roof let the snow fall through, and it landed on the old records and melted there, blurring ink that was older than her grandmother's grandmother.

There was an account in the margins—one of the last field reports from the purges, written by a Warden whose name had been struck from the roll. *We found the sanctuary empty but for the archives*, he had written, *and the archives were not books. They were people. The keepers did not store the past—they became it. Each one a vessel, a living library, and when we burned them we did not destroy the knowledge. We orphaned it. It is looking for new vessels now. God help whoever it finds.*

Ilse read that passage three times.

Then she sat back, and she allowed herself—for the first time in a long while—to want something honestly.

Because here was the truth she kept locked beneath fifteen years of discipline and promotion and the careful, joyless machinery of her ambition: Ilse Varrow had joined the Wardens at sixteen because she had once touched a dying woman's hand and felt the woman's whole life pour into her—sixty years of it, a harvest and a marriage and a grief—and she had never, in all the years since, managed to feel anything that vivid again. Her own memories were grey. Her own past was a corridor of doors that opened onto nothing. She had spent her life among other people's stolen recollections—confiscated charms, seized relics, the sad gray residue of the trade—and she had wanted them. All of them. Not to destroy them, whatever the oath said. To *have* them.

To be full, the way that dying woman had been full.

And now a child was walking toward her city carrying, if the records were right, the largest archive of human memory in existence. Everything the keepers had saved. Every song and grief and name of every soul the Wardens had erased, held in a six-year-old skull, walking unprotected through the snow.

Breck appeared in the doorway, stamping snow off his boots. "The miller's boy says the pair took the river road south. Toward Corvane. A barge master remembers them—he thinks the mother paid passage as far as the city gates."

"Then they're ahead of us," Ilse said, "but not by much. Not in this weather."

"You think the child set the fire?" Breck asked carefully.

Ilse looked at the snow falling through the broken roof, landing softly on three-hundred-year-old warnings, erasing them one flake at a time.

"I think," she said, "that a child who can make a whole village remember is worth more than every charm in Corvane combined. And I think the Order will want her destroyed, because that is what the Order does." She closed the record. "I intend to reach her first."

Breck shifted his weight. "Captain—if she's what you think she is, the protocols are clear. We report. We request a sanction team from the Citadel—"

"We'll do our duty, Sergeant," Ilse said mildly, and smiled the smile that had unsettled better men than Breck for fifteen years. "Of course we will. First light. Break camp."

When he had gone, she sat alone in the ruined parlor with the lamp burning low, and she thought about the farmhand's drowning that had never happened to him, and the green door, and the woman singing over a cradle.

*A child who remembers too loud.*

Soon, little vessel, she thought. Soon you'll tell me everything. Whether you mean to or not.

South along the frozen river, unseen, the snow went on falling—covering tracks, covering ash, covering the road that led to a charm-seller's door.
