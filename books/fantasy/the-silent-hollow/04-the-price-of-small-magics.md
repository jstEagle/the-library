## 4. The Price of Small Magics

The girl slept in the space behind his stove like something small that had learned to make itself smaller. Three days now. She ate what he gave her, drank the watered wine he left by her pallet, and spoke only when she thought he wasn't listening—soft syllables in a language that had no business living inside a child's mouth.

Hollowtongue. He'd turned the word over all night, the way a man turns over a coin he suspects is cursed.

On the fourth morning he sat across from her with bread and honey and said, in the flattest Corvani he could manage, "You'll need a name."

She looked up at him. Her eyes were the grey of river ice, old eyes in a young face, and she said nothing, because she never said anything—not in any tongue he could pretend not to know.

"Wren," he decided. "Small bird. Nobody looks twice at a small bird." He tore the bread, gave her the larger half. "Wren it is. And Wren—the first lesson, before you learn anything else, is how to be nothing at all."

* * *

He began with the basics, the rules that had kept him breathing through fifteen Corvane winters.

"Magic has a smell," he told her, pacing the narrow room while she sat cross-legged on the floor, watching him with that unnerving patience. "Not to the nose. To the Wardens' instruments—to their tuned glass and their salt-needles. So first: never work a charm near a mirror. Glass remembers resonance. Second: never touch another person's memory without asking your own permission first. Your gift will want to pour into every open hand. Let it starve instead."

Wren nodded solemnly, the way children nod when they intend to do precisely as they please.

"The third rule—" He stopped. He'd been about to say *never be remembered*, and the words curdled in his throat, because this child was already remembered; somewhere upriver there was ash and a burned village and, if the mother's flight had left any trail at all, men in grey coats following it down through the frost.

Instead he knelt in front of her. "When I say the word *still*, you go quiet inside. Not outside—inside. Can you try?"

He reached out slowly, giving her time to pull away, and took her small hand in his.

The world tilted.

It was always like this with the Hollowtouched—or so the fragments in his hoard suggested, the scraps he'd stolen from dead men who'd known too much. Her memory didn't sit still like ordinary memory. It *moved*. He saw snow falling upward, saw a woman's hands pushing him—her—through a window too small for a grown body, saw fire reflected in a hundred windows at once because the whole village had been burning, not one house but all of them, deliberately, methodically, and voices shouting a word that was not a word: *hollow-hollow-hollow.*

He let go. His palm was sweating. Hers was warm and perfectly calm.

"Still," he said.

And she went quiet. Truly quiet—a silence so complete it rang, like the pause between lightning and thunder. For one heartbeat he felt nothing from her at all, no pressure against his own gift, no whisper of borrowed language. Just a small girl holding a piece of bread, chewing.

Then she smiled, satisfied, and the silence broke, and he felt her presence settle back around the room like snowfall—gentle, constant, impossible to shovel away entirely.

"Good," he lied. "That was good."

It was not good. He had spent fifteen years building walls of habit and anonymity, and he understood, kneeling on cold floorboards with honey on his fingers, that no wall he knew how to build would hold against whatever poured off this child. She wasn't leaking magic. She was leaking *memory itself*—the raw substance—and everything she touched would begin to remember.

Including, he realized with a slow drop of his stomach, his charms.

* * *

The first complaint came Tuesday, from the tanner's widow on Coil Street.

Sable met his customers in doorways and back rooms, never the same doorway twice, and Maren the widow had bought a forgetting three weeks past—her husband's last words, which she'd heard wrong and couldn't stop hearing. Standard work. Clean edges. He'd done it a hundred times.

"You sold me rot," she hissed, thrusting a folded kerchief at him in the shadow of the tannery wall. Inside lay his payment and, wrapped beside it, a lock of her husband's hair she must have carried as a token. "I paid to forget. Now I remember *everything*. Not just the words—all of it. Our wedding. The fever. The way he looked at the end." Her voice cracked low and vicious. "Every night since the snow started. Every single night, like it just happened."

"I can re-cut the charm," Sable said carefully. "No charge."

"No charge?" She laughed, wet and bitter. "Do you know what it's worth to me, to have him back? Even like this? Maybe I should thank you." Then her hand shot out and gripped his wrist, hard. "But there are others. The lamplighter on Vell Street—weeps now over a daughter he paid you to unremember. The chandler. Two others I've heard of through the guild wives. Whatever you did, seller, you did it to all of us at once. And people talk."

She shoved past him into the grey morning. Sable stood very still, watching his breath hang in the air.

People talk. People remember.

The charms weren't failing. Charms don't fail—they're cut into the mind like a key into wax, and wax holds its shape. Something had come along afterward and pressed every impression flat again, smoothed the wax smooth, restored every line he'd erased. Something that moved through the city like weather, touching everyone at once.

Something small, and grey-eyed, sleeping behind his stove.

* * *

Odell received him in the counting room above his warehouse, where the ledgers were real and the crates were not, and where a man learned quickly whether Odell's welcome was warm or merely profitable.

Today it was neither. The fence sat behind his desk with his fat hands laced together and did not offer a chair.

"Sit anyway," Odell said, nodding at the stool, which somehow made it worse. "We have a problem, Ravensworth."

"We have many. Pick one."

"The kind that walks on two legs and wears a grey coat." Odell slid a paper across the desk. A list, in a clerk's careful hand: seven names. Maren the widow. The lamplighter. The chandler. Four more. "These are your customers. All of them, in the past fortnight, approached by persons unknown asking about their forgettings—who sold them, where the meeting happened, what was paid. My name came up twice. Do you understand what that means for me? I move goods for forty people in this city, and two of my names are on a Warden clerk's paper."

Sable read the list twice. "Wardens don't investigate charm-sellers. It's beneath them. Petty thaumaturgy, they call it. They fine it or ignore it."

"They ignored it for fifteen years, yes." Odell leaned forward. The lamp caught the sweat on his jowls. "So why now? What changed, Sable? What did you bring into your house with the first snow?"

The room went very quiet. Outside, wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere below, a crate lid creaked under weight it was not designed to hold.

"I don't know what you mean," Sable said.

"See, that's the trouble." Odell sighed, and for a moment the merchant's mask slipped and showed something almost like pity underneath, which frightened Sable more than the grey coats did. "You've always been good at that. Fifteen years, and I've never once seen you flinch. But you're flinching now. You walked in here already flinching." He took the list back, folded it, and burned it in the candle flame without ceremony, letting the ashes fall into his inkwell. "There's a man asking questions I can't answer without dying. He came yesterday. Polite fellow. Grey gloves. He asked me who sells memories that *come back*. Do you know what he called them? Reversions. Like the man was a scholar." Odell's eyes lifted. "Nobody studies petty thaumaturgy, Ravensworth. People study *old* magic. And old magic, in my experience, arrives on your doorstep hungry."

Sable rose. "If anyone asks, I'm a spice factor out of Drossen Hollow."

"If anyone asks, you're a ghost, same as always." Odell waved a hand, dismissive, done. But as Sable reached the door, the fence added, quietly: "Whatever it is you're keeping—keep it better. Because the man in grey gloves left me a token. Said I'd know when to use it."

"A token."

"A whistle. Bone." Odell turned back to his ledgers. "One breath, and the Wardens know everything I know about you. Which is everything, Sable. It always was."

* * *

He walked home the long way, through streets going blue with dusk, and made himself think like a hunter because the hunter was already thinking about him.

Grey gloves. Scholarly questions. *Reversions.* Someone wasn't sweeping up illegal charm-work—that was a constable's labor, tedious and small. Someone was following a thread backward from a handful of broken forgettings toward the thing that broke them. And threads, in Sable's experience, ended in one of two places: a pyre or a bargain.

Fifteen years of discipline, undone by six days of a child's breathing. He had told himself he could contain her. He had actually believed that a man who survived Corvane by being forgettable could shelter a creature made entirely of remembering.

The lamps were being lit as he climbed Gull Street, and he saw the lamplighter pause atop his ladder, staring at nothing, weeping silently over a daughter whose face he'd paid good silver never to see again. Sable kept walking.

At his door, he stopped.

The latch stood a finger's width from closed. He always left it so—Wren couldn't work the bolt—but the gap was wrong. Too wide. And from inside, faintly, he heard her voice. Not the hollow syllables this time. Plain speech, cracked and rusty, a girl learning the shape of words she hadn't used since the fire:

"—and then the grey men came, and Mama said run, and I ran, and I ran—"

She was talking to someone.

Sable pushed the door open. Wren sat on the floor by the stove, and across from her, in the chair he used for mending, sat a figure in a grey coat with grey gloves folded neatly in its lap, its face in shadow, listening to the child speak with the attentive stillness of a man cataloguing every word.

"There you are," the figure said pleasantly. "We were just getting acquainted. Sit down, Master Ravensworth. The child was telling me the most remarkable story."
