## 5. Memories He Should Not Own

The hoard lived beneath the floorboards of the cold room, under a false stone Sable had laid himself the first winter in Corvane, when his hands still shook and the city's silence still felt like a held breath.

He had not opened it in three years.

There were reasons. Good ones. The hoard was not a treasure chest; it was a wound he had learned to walk around. Every memory inside it was stolen—lifted from grieving widows and dying soldiers and one terrified boy whose name he no longer let himself say—and every memory weighed something. Not in the hand. In the chest. He kept them because selling them whole would have made him a monster, and destroying them would have made him a murderer of a gentler kind, and so they sat in the dark beneath his floor like coins minted from other people's grief, accruing interest he never asked for.

Tonight, with Wren asleep on his cot with her small fists curled under her chin and snow still melting in her hair, Sable pried up the stone and reached into the dark.

* * *

The memories did not live as objects, exactly. They lived as pressures—each one a folded thing that pressed against his palms with its own temperature, its own weather. A fisherman's last morning on the water: salt-warm, gull-cry gold. A mother's face at a sickbed: gray-green, damp as river stones. He had catalogued them once by feel, the way a blind man reads a room, and now his fingers moved through them with the grim efficiency of long practice.

He was looking for Hollowtongue. For anything.

Wren had spoken again that evening—not to him, never to him in words he could answer plainly, but in her sleep. She had murmured four syllables against the pillow, and the syllables had done something to the candle flame, bending it toward her like a listener leaning in. Sable had stood in the middle of his own room feeling the language move through the air, and understood two things at once: that he knew what she said—*the doors are sleeping, find the keeper's son*—and that he should not have known it. No book in Corvane contained Hollowtongue. The Wardens had burned the books before they burned the speakers.

So it had come out of him. Out of whatever he'd buried.

He knelt over the open dark beneath the floor and began, memory by memory, to search.

* * *

The third one he touched was an old woman's memory of a funeral, and inside it, half-buried in the crowd of mourners, there was a word scratched on a coffin lid in letters that curved wrong—too many joins, too much intention. He held the memory up to his mind's eye and felt his stomach drop.

He could read it.

Not guess. Not approximate. Read it, the way you read your own name. *Rest in the ground's keeping.* A burial phrase. A keeper's phrase.

His hands went cold around the memory's warmth. He set it back among the others carefully, the way you set down a blade you've realized is sharper than you thought, and kept digging.

* * *

It took him until the bottom of the hoard to admit where the answer had to be.

At the very base, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax he'd pressed his own thumb into fifteen years ago, lay the one memory he had never sold, never examined, never even allowed himself to weigh in his hands longer than necessary. The oldest theft in his collection. The only one where thief and victim were the same man.

The night his family died, Sable Ravensworth had been seventeen years old and already a practiced little criminal of the mind—a boy who could slip into a stranger's head and lift a moment out like picking a pocket. And when he came home to the smoke, to what the Wardens' fire had left of the house on Lantern Row, he had done the only merciful thing his gift knew how to do.

He had stolen the night from himself. The whole of it. The screams, the red light, whatever he had seen or heard or *been told* in those hours—all of it folded up and buried, so that he woke the next morning knowing only that his family was dead and that he could not, no matter how he clawed at the inside of his skull, remember a single detail of how he'd learned it. Grief without a door to knock on. He had built himself a prison of mercy and thrown away the key, and then he had run to Corvane and spent fifteen years telling himself the missing night was simply gone.

Now he sat on the cold-room floor with Wren asleep ten feet away, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

The memory hit him like falling through ice.

Fire. Not the Wardens' clean orange fire but something older, a light that moved *wrong*, crawling along the walls of his childhood home in colors fire has no business knowing—and figures standing in it. Tall. Robed. Faces he almost recognized, faces that turned toward him as he burst through the door, and one of them spoke—

In Hollowtongue.

And seventeen-year-old Sable had answered.

The memory shook him. His own voice, younger, cracking with fury and terror, speaking a language he had no right to know, fluently, pleadingly—the way you speak a mother tongue. *He is not ready. Take me instead.* And the tall figure had bent close, and pressed two fingers to his brow, and spoken words that sank into him like a brand:

*"Keeper's son. Sworn. When the doors wake, you will remember what you swore."*

Then the rest. Then the part he had truly stolen the night away to avoid. His father—quiet, gray-eyed, unremarkable lampmaker's father—standing between the robed figures and the stairs, arms spread, speaking the same impossible language with the calm of a man reciting something he'd said a thousand times. His mother pulling Sable back through the doorway. The robed ones were not the killers. The robed ones had come to *warn* them. What came after the warning wore Warden gray.

He saw his father fall. He heard his mother's last word, and it was not his name—it was the same four syllables Wren had murmured in her sleep. *The doors are sleeping.*

Then nothing. The mercy he'd purchased. The blank.

Sable came back to himself on the floor of his own house, cheek against the boards, throat raw. He had not known he was making noise. Across the room, Wren sat up in the cot, watching him with eyes far too old and far too calm, and she said—awake this time, deliberately, each syllable placed like a stone in a streambed:

"*You swore.*"

"I don't—" His voice failed. He tried again. "I was a child. I didn't understand what I was swearing."

She slid off the cot, crossed the floor barefoot, and took his face in her two small hands the way no six-year-old should touch anyone—gently, gravely, the gesture of a priest or a judge. When she spoke Hollowtongue now, he felt the words land somewhere behind his ribs, in the place the brand had been.

"*An oath does not care about age. It sleeps until the sleeper is needed. You are needed, keeper's son. The doors are waking, and I am their key, and the gray men are coming.*"

"The Wardens," he whispered. "How do you—"

"*Because I remember everything,*" she said simply, and the terrible weight of it settled over him—everything, every burned village, every screaming night, carried in a body that couldn't hold it and a voice that had gone mute from the carrying. "*Everything except how to stop it.*"

Outside, the snow had stopped. The silence that followed a heavy snowfall in Corvane was total, muffled, kind—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Sable had trained himself to hear across fifteen years of listening.

Boots. Many of them. Moving down the street with the unhurried discipline of people who already knew which door they wanted.

Sable pulled Wren behind him and looked down at the open hole in his floor—at the hoard of stolen lives glimmering faintly in the dark like a bed of drowned stars—and understood, with a clarity that felt like the oath itself waking in his blood, that he was going to need every single one of them.
