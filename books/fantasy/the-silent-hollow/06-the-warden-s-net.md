## 6. The Warden's Net

Ilse Varrow had learned long ago that the best traps were the ones that never snapped shut. A cage told its prisoner everything. A corridor that simply grew narrower, room by room, door by door—that taught a man to walk himself into chains and thank you for the escort.

So she did not arrest Odell Fenn.

She bought from him instead.

The charm-seller's stall sat in the crook of Tanner's Row like something grown there rather than built, all sagging canvas and brass hooks strung with little glass vials that caught the market light. Ilse came in plain wool, hood down, no insignia—a woman with coin and a story. She let her gloved hand hover over the vials the way buyers did, hesitant, superstitious.

"Looking for something to take an edge off," she said. "A grief. My sister's. She can't stop seeing the accident."

Odell was a soft man, round-shouldered, with the careful hands of someone who'd spent decades handling things more fragile than glass. He didn't look at her face when he spoke; he looked at her hands, her boots, the cut of her coat. Ilse had watched enough merchants do it to know what he was reading: not whether she could pay, but whether she was safe.

"Grief's a heavy pull," he said. "Most folk want the whole of it gone. I don't sell wholes. Fragments only. Enough to let a body sleep."

"Fragments." She smiled, tired, grateful. "That's honest of you."

"Honesty keeps me alive." He selected a vial with two fingers, held it to the light. Inside, a curl of silver moved like smoke underwater. "Winter market rates. You understand."

She paid too much on purpose. Overpaying made sellers remember you as generous, and generous people got invited back.

The charm worked. That was the obscene thing about them—she'd tested three now, purchased through three different faces, and each one carried a true memory, worn smooth at the edges but true. Somewhere in Corvane there was a man peeling pieces off other people's lives and bottling them for coppers, and doing it well enough that no Warden in fifteen years had caught so much as a scent of him.

*No Warden,* she corrected herself, turning down Ashlane with the vial warm in her pocket. *No Warden but me.*

Because here was what the reports would never say, what she kept folded away behind her teeth like a stolen coin: she wasn't hunting him to destroy him. The order's doctrine said memory-magic was rot, contagion, the old corruption wearing new skin. Ilse had recited the doctrine at a hundred recruitings. But she had held that first vial to the light and felt something move in her chest that had no name in any Warden litany. Want. Plain, animal want. There were lives in those bottles. Whole hours of being someone else, and she had spent thirty years being only herself, and herself had never once been enough.

And then there was the village upriver.

Ashford Hollow. Forty-one dead, burned in a single night, and among the salvage her sweepers had pulled from the ash: a merchant's ledger, half-charred, with one legible line in a shaking hand. *The child remembers too loud.* Ilse had read it forty times. She knew what remembered too loud meant. Every initiate got one lecture on it, delivered like a ghost story—the Hollowtongue, the speech that carried memory in its very grammar, the tongue that had started the war the Wardens were founded to finish. They'd been told it was extinct. Dead language, dead bloodline, dead magic.

But the ledger line hadn't been written by a dead man. It had been written by a terrified one, and terror was always current.

Someone had walked out of that fire. Someone small.

* * *

The net wove itself over eleven days.

Odell was the thread, though he never felt it tighten. Ilse's people didn't follow him—they *shopped* him. A widow with a dead son. A clerk who wanted to forget a humiliation. Each purchase logged, each vial traced, each fragment examined for texture. Memory had grain, Ilse discovered. Stolen memories sat differently in the mind than lived ones; they arrived slightly foreign, like furniture from another house. And every fragment she bought bore the same grain. One thief. One hand.

On the ninth day, a boy in Odell's employ—a runner, fourteen, underfed—took a wrapped parcel across the city and left it in the stone hollow of a wall on Corvane's east slope, where the respectable streets gave up and the rookeries began. On the tenth day, the parcel was gone. On the eleventh, Ilse stood in the cold outside a narrow house with a slate roof and a door painted the blue of old milk, and she understood why fifteen years of Wardens had found nothing.

He lived like a debt. Small rooms, thin walls, nothing worth seizing. A man who had made himself unmemorable in a city that forgot on command. She almost admired it.

She went in alone.

Not because it was wise. Because the bargain she intended to offer required a certain intimacy, and bargains delivered by squads became confessions. She wanted him to feel that this was between the two of them—a conversation, almost. Fear worked better without witnesses.

* * *

Sable Ravensworth opened the door before she knocked.

She noted that. Noted the way his eyes went to her face and then, deliberately, to the absence of anything on her sleeves, and how something behind his expression settled rather than sharpened. He had known. Perhaps not today, perhaps not her name, but some part of him had been waiting fifteen years for a knock like hers.

"Warden-Captain," he said. No tremor. "You're far from the garrison."

"The garrison is wherever I stand." She stepped past him—not forcing, just moving, letting his own courtesy do the work—and the door closed behind her with the soft click of a man accepting his circumstances. The room inside was exactly what she'd imagined: spare, clean, a single lamp. And on a shelf near the window, rows of small glass vials catching the light like a congregation of trapped stars.

His hoard. Or part of it. She made herself look away from it. Hunger showed in the eyes, and hunger was leverage you handed your enemy for free.

"You've been busy," she said. "Eleven purchases in eleven days. Widow Marren. Clerk Doss. A ferryman whose name I won't trouble you with. All fragments. All yours."

"I sell charms. Charms are legal."

"Charms are *tolerated*," Ilse said, "because no one has ever bothered to define them. Do you know why? Because defining them means admitting they work. And if they work—if memory can be lifted, bottled, sold—then the world is full of thieves we cannot catch, and the Wardens' founding purpose becomes a joke told at our expense." She let that sit. "I'm not here to make that argument to a magistrate. Magistrates are slow. I'm here to make it to you."

Sable stood very still. It was a stillness she recognized from interrogations—not innocence, but economy. A man conserving everything.

"There was a fire," she said. "Upstream. Ashford Hollow. Forty-one people. One survivor unaccounted for—a child, six years old, maybe seven. The last man writing in that village wrote four words about her before the flames took the rest." She watched his face with the patience of a fisherman watching still water. "*Remembers too loud.*"

Nothing moved in his features. Nothing moved, and that was the confession. Guilty men flinched. Innocent men asked questions. Sable Ravensworth did neither, because he had already done the arithmetic and knew that every word was a payment.

"I don't have a child," he said.

"No," Ilse agreed pleasantly. "You have a niece visiting from the coast. A neighbor's orphan you took in out of charity. You'll pick whichever story serves, and it doesn't matter, because I haven't come to arrest you." She crossed to the window, turned the lamp's key down until the room went dusk-colored, and only then looked back at him. "I've come to buy."

Something flickered there. She had his attention now—true attention, the kind men paid for with their pulse.

"Here is the shape of it," she said. "Fifteen years of theft. Hundreds of victims. Enough charges to put you in the deep cells until your bones forget their own names. All of it—every crime, every ledger line, every whisper in my files—gone. Forgiven. Unwritten. You walk out of this room a free man with clean hands, and no one in Corvane will ever connect the charm-seller of Tanner's Row to the name Sable Ravensworth again." She paused. "In exchange, you deliver the girl. Alive. Unharmed. To me. Not to the order—to *me*. And no one will ever ask what became of her after."

The lamp hissed. Outside, snow ticked against the glass in fine dry grains, the sound of winter settling in for a long occupation.

"And after," Sable said quietly. "After you have her. What happens to a child who remembers too loud?"

"That's above my—"

"Don't." His voice didn't rise. It *lowered*, which was worse. "Don't insult us both. Your order burns villages. Whatever you tell yourself you are, the ash is the same ash. You want what's in her head. I've seen what wanting looks like on a Warden's face, Captain. I'm looking at it right now."

Ilse held his gaze and did not deny it. Denial was a tool for people who needed something from a room. What she needed, he'd already given her: confirmation that he understood the stakes completely. That made the refusal, when it came, mean something.

"I'm offering you your life," she said.

"You're offering me a smaller cage." He shook his head slowly. "No."

One word. She waited for the rest—the pleading, the bargaining, the desperate counteroffer. It didn't come. He simply stood there in his dim room full of stolen stars and declined, the way a wall declines.

"You understand what happens next," she said. "If I don't get her from you, I get her from the city. And the city gives her up eventually. Everyone does. Children are easy to find and easier to take."

"Then you'll be looking for a while." He moved to the door and opened it, and the cold came in around his ankles like a loyal dog. "Good evening, Captain. Mind the ice on the steps. It's treacherous this time of year."

* * *

She walked down through the rookeries without an escort, boots crunching frost, and let him watch her go. That was deliberate too. Let him see her leave unhurried, unreinforced, alone. Let him lie awake tonight doing the arithmetic she wanted him to do: *she knows where I live, she knows my supplier, she knows my trade, and she let me live.*

Fear was patient. Fear would go to work on him the way water works on mortar, silently, all night, every night. By morning he would start thinking about doors and roads. By the second morning he would start thinking about which road. Men always ran toward the same places—old sanctuaries, old loyalties, the geography of whatever they used to believe. And there were only so many mountains.

At the bottom of the lane she stopped and looked back once. The blue door was closed, but a light still burned in the upstairs window, and behind the frosted glass a shadow moved—quick, purposeful, packing or planning, she couldn't tell which.

It didn't matter. Both led to the same place.

Ilse pulled her collar up against the falling snow and smiled at nothing, at the dark, at the whole white indifferent city. Let him run. A running man chose his own ground, and she had spent eleven days learning to read the grain of another man's memory. Reading a fugitive's fear would be easier.

Somewhere ahead of him, up in the high passes, winter was just beginning to show its teeth.

She hoped he hurried.
