## 7. Flight Across the White Fields

The city gates opened at dawn for the ice-wagons, and Sable went through them inside one, folded beneath cured hides that stank of tallow and cold. Wren fit against his chest in a sling made from his own coat, her breath fogging the hollow of his throat. She had not spoken since the night Ilse let him walk. She didn't need to. Her silence had a weight to it now, like a held note.

Through a knothole in the wagon's slatted side, he watched Corvane shrink behind them: the grey sprawl of roofs furred with snow, the Warden tower standing over it all like a needle driven through the city's spine. Somewhere under that needle was Odell's shop, and Odell himself, released with a broken hand and a warning he'd delivered through a cut lip: *She knows where you'll go, boy. She said it like she was doing you a kindness.*

Of course she knew. He'd told her without meaning to—every flinch, every pause, every question he hadn't asked. A memory-reader learned to read omissions too.

The wagon rolled north along the frozen river road until noon, then turned west where the maps showed nothing but the white teeth of the Vellmark hills. At a waystation whose chimney smoked like a dying signal fire, Sable slipped out from under the hides while the driver argued about tolls, and walked into the open country carrying everything he owned on his back and a child against his heart.

The wind found them within the hour.

It came off the high passes in long white gusts that erased the world twenty paces at a time. The fields out here were fallow and buried, stone fences running under the snow like the bones of something old. Sable kept to the lee of those walls when he could, counting steps, watching the sky for the dark shapes of Warden skiffs that rode the storm-fronts the way gulls rode a plow.

Wren pressed her mouth near his ear.

"*Duvan keleth,*" she said.

And because he could not stop himself—because the words arrived in him already translated, warm as water—he answered in the same tongue. "I know. We're going up. Hollowmere is past the second ridge."

She went quiet again, but her hand crept out of the sling and gripped his collar, and stayed there.

He should not have been able to speak it. That was the thought that kept circling him like the wind. Hollowtongue was dead; the Wardens had spent two centuries killing it, word by word, keeper by keeper, until only scholars' fragments remained, and even those were burned. Yet the syllables lived in him whole, patient, as if they'd been waiting fifteen years in some sealed room of his mind for a door to open. And the door, he understood with a dread that sat cold in his stomach, had opened the night a mute child touched his hand.

* * *

They sheltered the first night in a shepherd's bothy with half a roof, and Sable built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. Wren slept curled against his side with the fierce, boneless trust of the very young, and Sable sat awake listening to the blizzard rearrange the world outside, and did not sleep at all.

Near midnight, hooves.

Not many—three, maybe four—and moving fast for weather like this. He killed the fire with a sweep of his coat, gathered Wren into the dark of the bothy's corner where an old grain chest stood, and eased them both into the space behind it, her back to his chest, his palm hovering an inch from her mouth, ready.

The hoofbeats slowed. Stopped close. Voices, flattened by wind.

"—tracks end here anyway," someone said. "Nothing crosses the open field in this."

"Varrow says he will." The voice was younger, uncertain. "Says men like this always break cover when the child gets cold."

"And if he doesn't?"

A pause. Then, quieter: "Then we keep riding until the mountain does our work for us. Captain's orders are to mark the passes, not die in them. She wants him alive enough to talk. The child—" Another pause. "The child just has to be delivered breathing. Mostly breathing, she said."

Laughter, brief and ugly, swallowed by wind. Hooves turned. Faded.

Sable counted two hundred breaths before he let his hand leave Wren's mouth. When he did, she turned her head and looked up at him in the dark, and her eyes caught what little light the snow gave back, and they were calm. Not the calm of a frightened child. The calm of someone listening to a plan unfold exactly as expected.

"*Shal venne orath?*" she whispered. *How long until the pass?*

"Two days if the storm breaks. Four if it doesn't."

She nodded once, grave as a magistrate, and closed her eyes.

He lay staring at the ceiling beams and admitted, finally, what he had been refusing to admit since the door of his house: she wasn't leading him to Hollowmere. She was following a route she already knew.

* * *

The storm broke at dawn on the second day, and broke hard—clear, savage cold, the kind that made the air itself glitter and the light come down like knives. They climbed. The fields fell away below them into a whiteness without horizon, and ahead the Vellmarks rose in blue-grey ramparts, and somewhere past the second ridge, Wren said, the sanctuary waited.

She walked beside him now, refusing the sling, her small boots punching holes in the crusted snow. Twice he carried her anyway when the drifts came to her waist. Twice she wriggled down again as soon as she could stand.

At midday, on a saddle of bare wind-scoured rock, she stopped walking.

"*Sable.*"

His own name in Hollowtongue stopped him like a hand on his chest. In that language names weren't sounds; they were meanings, worn smooth by use. His name meant something there—*shadow-hoarder*, roughly, or perhaps *the man who keeps what isn't his*. He turned slowly.

"You chose me," he said. It wasn't a question anymore. "Out of every house in Corvane. Out of every smuggler and hedge-witch and kindly fool between here and the sea. You walked to my door in the first snow and you knocked. Why?"

Wren looked up at him, and the wind pulled strands of dark hair across her face, and for a moment he thought she wouldn't answer—that the plain speech would fail her again and she'd retreat into the old tongue where she was safest.

Then she spoke, and her voice was thin and cracked from disuse, a six-year-old's voice pushed through six-year-old lips, saying things no six-year-old should know how to say.

"Because you are the oath-son," she said. "Mother looked for you for three years. Before the fire, after the fire. She said the last keeper died at Hollowmere with one debt unkept, and the debt passed down." She took a breath, and the next words came slower, each one placed like a stone across a stream. "Your mother swore at her hearth. You were there. You don't remember because you cut it out of yourself, but I remember, because my mother remembered, because remembering is what we *are*. The oath was: if the archive ever walks alone, the oath-son carries it home."

The wind screamed across the saddle. Sable stood very still.

"I don't remember," he said, and his voice came out wrong. "Whatever you think you saw—"

"You dreamed it last night." Wren's eyes didn't waver. "You dreamed your mother's kitchen and the smell of bread and a woman with silver thread in her braid, and you woke up crying and you didn't know why. I heard you. Words leak out of people who cut themselves. The hole leaks."

He knelt. He didn't decide to; his legs simply went. The snow soaked through his trouser knees and he barely felt it. Fifteen years of careful, disciplined forgetting—the charms sold, the rules kept, the self amputated piece by piece so he could survive in a city built on amputation—and this child, this scrap of a girl with chapped hands and frost-nipped cheeks, had just told him the shape of the hole.

"What was the keeper's name?" he asked. "The one who died at Hollowmere."

"Maren," Wren said.

Something old and vast shifted in the cellar of him. Maren. He didn't know the name. He knew the name. Both were true, and the distance between them was the width of a stolen memory.

"She said you'd come angry," Wren added, with a child's terrible precision. "She said anger is just grief that hasn't found its door. So we're going to walk, and you're going to be angry, and by Hollowmere you can put it down."

For a long moment Sable could do nothing but breathe. Then he laughed—a short, wrecked sound that the wind tore away—and held out his arms, and she climbed into them without hesitation, as though she'd been waiting the whole journey for permission.

Above them, far up the second ridge, something glinted.

Not ice. Ice didn't hold its shine in one place like that, didn't move, tracking, deliberate. Sable went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather. On the skyline against the burning blue of morning, three dark figures moved along the crest with the unhurried confidence of hunters who had never once doubted their ground—and at their center rode a shape he knew, black-cloaked, still, watching the valley below through something that caught the light and threw it back in a long silver lens.

Ilse Varrow had not followed their trail.

She had guessed the destination, and gone ahead, and now she stood between them and the pass like winter itself given a warrant.

Wren's small hand tightened on his collar.

"She knows another way up," the girl said quietly, in the old tongue, in the language of living memory, and pointed—not at the pass, but at a cleft in the rock below the ridge, half-hidden by hanging ice, a wound in the mountain that breathed faintly warm air into the freezing day. "The keepers dug it. But nobody has gone down there since the burning."

Down, then. Into the dark of the mountain, toward whatever Hollowmere had become.

Behind them, faint across the snow, came the first far-off cry of the hunting horn.
