## 8. Hollowmere's Empty Halls

The mountain showed itself at dusk on the third day, rising out of the white like a drowned ship's mast breaking the surface of a frozen sea. Sable stopped at the tree line, breath ragged, Wren a warm weight against his back where she rode in the sling of his coat. Behind them, the blizzard they'd outrun had spent itself against the foothills; ahead, the sky was bruise-purple and clear, cold enough to crack teeth.

*There,* Wren said—not aloud, not in any tongue that used air. The word arrived behind his eyes like a stone dropped down a well.

"I see it." His voice came out hoarse. Three days of snow and pursuit and he hadn't spoken above a whisper since the river crossing. "Hollowmere."

She slid from his back and stood in the drifts, small boots sinking past her ankles, her face tipped up toward the peak. Snow caught in the dark feathers of her hair. She looked, for a moment, like something carved—a votive figure left at a shrine no one visited anymore.

Then she turned and started walking, and there was nothing votive about her at all. She climbed with the certainty of someone going home.

Sable followed because following was all he had left. Corvane was two days behind him and a lifetime away. Odell was in a Warden cell or a grave. And somewhere on the white fields between here and the city, Ilse Varrow was reading their trail the way Sable read charm-sellers' ledgers—patiently, hungrily, line by line.

The path up was older than the snow. Stone steps surfaced through the drifts at intervals, each one bearing marks that might have been letters once, worn to suggestions. Wren touched each stone as she passed it, and each time, Sable felt a faint pressure against his mind—greetings, he realized. She was greeting the stairs. And the stairs, in whatever way stone could, were answering.

*How long has it been empty?* he asked, and only after the thought left him did he understand he'd asked it in Hollowtongue. The language had been growing in him since the river, uncoiling like a root finding water. He didn't know when he'd learned it. He suspected he'd always known it, the way you know your own heartbeat, and some locked door inside him had finally come open.

Wren considered the question with the grave attention she gave everything.

*Longer than my mother. Longer than the fire.* A pause, snow crunching under her small feet. *But not long enough for the walls to forget.*

He didn't ask what walls remembered. He was afraid of the answer, and anyway the mountain was about to tell him.

They crested the shoulder of the peak as the last light failed, and Hollowmere opened below them in the blue shadow of evening: a sanctuary built into the mountain's hollow crown, terraces of pale stone descending into a central court, arches upon arches, roofs furred with ice. It should have been beautiful. It would have been beautiful, once—Sable could feel the beauty still hanging in the air like perfume in an abandoned room.

What he saw instead were the scorch marks.

They blackened the archways in long licking tongues. Doors hung split from their frames. In the court below, shapes lay half-buried in snow that had drifted and melted and drifted again across years—too many years—and Sable knew without digging what the shapes were. The snow over them had a different texture. Settled. Undisturbed.

No one had come to bury these people. No one had come at all.

Wren made a sound then—the first sound Sable had ever heard her make that wasn't a word. Small. Animal. Her hand found his and gripped hard enough to hurt, and he knelt in the snow and gathered her against his chest and let her shake, because there was nothing else to do for it.

"It's all right," he said, and the lie tasted like ash. "We'll find shelter. We'll—"

*You said there would be keepers.* Her voice in his head was very quiet. *You said someone would know what I am.*

He had said that. He had staked three days of flight, Odell's freedom, both their lives, on the hope that the mountain sanctuary still held its order—that somewhere behind these gates lived men and women who could take the burning archive out of a six-year-old girl and teach her to carry it safely.

He looked out over the ruined terraces, at the settled snow, at the years of silence, and understood that he had brought her to a graveyard.

"Stay close," was all he said.

* * *

They made shelter in what had been a scriptorium—a long hall off the eastern terrace where the roof held and a hearth still drew. Sable got a fire going from broken shelf-wood while Wren explored the room with a child's terrible honesty, touching desks, opening drawers, trailing her fingers along scorched shelves where books had burned down to their boards.

He was watching her rather than the shadows when he saw her stop.

She stood at the far end of the hall, before a low door he'd taken for a cupboard. Her hand was flat against the wood. Her whole body had gone still in that way of hers, the stillness that wasn't emptiness but listening.

*Someone's there,* she signed to him, then corrected herself before her hands finished moving. *Something.*

Sable took the iron poker from the hearth and crossed the room. He put his hand on the door, felt Wren press in against his leg, and pulled it open.

The room beyond was small and clean, swept within the last season. A cot. A stool. A candle stub. And in the cot, propped against the wall, an old woman watching them with eyes like frost on a window—pale, bright, and seeing straight through to the bone.

"You're late," she said. Her voice was dry paper, but steady. "I've been dying for eleven years, boy. I told them someone would come. They laughed at me. Well." She coughed, wet and deep. "Who's laughing now."

Sable lowered the poker. "You're a keeper."

"I'm what's left of one. Name's Maren. Was Maren. Names matter less every year." Her gaze slid past him to Wren, and the old woman's face did something complicated—grief and wonder fighting to a draw. "Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you poor little vessel. Come here, child. Let me look at you."

Wren didn't move. She looked up at Sable, asking permission with her whole body, and the sight of it cracked something in him. He nodded.

Wren crossed the room and took the old woman's hand, and Maren closed her eyes, and for a long moment neither of them moved. When Maren opened her eyes again they were streaming, though her expression hadn't changed.

"The whole archive," she whispered. "Everything we buried in the deep vaults. Everything we scattered into bloodlines hoping time would thin it. It's all in this child. Every word of the Hollowtongue. Every memory of the first days." She looked up at Sable. "Where is her mother?"

"Dead. Village called Ashford, upriver. Burned."

"Ashford." Maren said the name like she was tasting poison. "That was the third one this decade. You understand what that means?"

"That Wardens burn villages."

"No." The old woman's grip tightened on Wren's small hand. "It means the war never ended, boy. That's what I need you to understand before my heart gives out, because everyone else who knew it is under the snow outside. There was no peace. There was a retreat. The keepers lost Corvane, lost the lowlands, lost the cities one by one, and the survivors built sanctuaries like this one and told themselves the war was over because the fighting had stopped." She coughed again, longer this time, and Sable saw the blood-fleck on her lips and understood exactly how much time she had. "The fighting didn't stop. It just learned patience. Your Wardens—your charming gray-cloaked Wardens with their laws against magic—they aren't a response to the old war. They're its inheritance. Founded by the eaters' descendants, staffed by their students, armed with techniques stolen from our dead. Ilse Varrow—" and Sable flinched despite himself, and Maren's frost-pale eyes sharpened, "—ah. You've met her."

"She offered me a bargain."

"And you refused. Good. She'd have taken the child and peeled her like fruit. Varrow's line goes back to Vessic the Eater himself—I've read the genealogies we smuggled out before this place fell. She knows what the Hollowtongue is. She's wanted it since she was younger than that girl. Why do you think she burns villages instead of just taking children? Because a vessel ripped out whole can be traced. But a village burned, a survivor's account filed, a trail followed slowly—" Maren smiled, and it was the worst thing Sable had seen in fifteen years of careful living. "Slowly is how she does everything. Ask your fence. Oh—" a flicker of genuine sympathy, quickly mastered. "Yes. We know about Odell. We know most things, or we did, when there were enough of us to know them."

Sable sat down heavily on the stool. The fire crackled. Outside, wind moved through the broken arches of Hollowmere with a sound like slow breathing.

"Wren," he said carefully. "You said she chose me. On the road. What did you mean?"

Maren answered for her. "She means you're the oath-son. Aren't you? Aldric Galloway's boy."

The name went through him like cold water. His father's name, spoken aloud for the first time in twenty years, in a dying sanctuary at the top of the world.

"My father was a charm-seller."

"My father was a baker," Maren said. "Before. People become what survival requires. Your father was the last keeper sworn to hold the southern archive, and when the purge came to Corvane he hid the archive the only way left—he broke it into pieces and stole it into his own blood. Into you. You were a child; they wiped the oath from your waking mind so you couldn't betray it under torture. But oaths don't die of forgetting. They wait." She gestured at him with a hand like a bundle of sticks. "And a Hollowtongue vessel walked across half a country, through every trap and warding, and knocked on precisely one door. Ask yourself why, boy. Ask yourself what she is. She isn't a girl who carries memories. She is the archive itself, walking around in a child's shape, looking for its keeper. And she found you."

Sable looked at Wren. Wren looked back, unblinking, and did not deny it.

"Why didn't you say?" His voice came out rough. "On the road. Any of it."

Wren's hands moved, small and precise. *You wouldn't have believed words. You had to choose.*

*Choose what?* he almost asked. But he already knew. Choose to run again, alone, the way he'd survived fifteen years. Or choose the thing his father had died refusing to abandon.

"There's no one left," he said to Maren. "You're dying. The order is snow and bones. Even if I took her somewhere—"

"There is nowhere," Maren agreed. "There is only you. Which is why I have one more thing to tell you, and then I think I'll be allowed to sleep." She drew a breath that rattled. "The archive in the child—it can be moved. Not destroyed; the eaters tried destruction for three hundred years and failed. Not hidden; you've seen how hiding ends. Moved. A keeper can take it into himself. All of it. Every word, every year, every grief. Become the archive. Living, breathing, silent. The last vault." Her eyes fixed on his. "Your father swore to do it if it ever came to this. He died before he could. The oath passes to the son, whether the son remembers swearing it or not."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I watched Aldric Galloway prepare for it. I was his teacher." A ghost of a smile. "Which makes me yours."

The fire popped. Somewhere far below them, wind worried at the gates of Hollowmere.

And beneath that—faint, so faint Sable might have imagined it—another sound. Metal. Many boots. Coming up the mountain path through the frozen dark.

Maren heard it too. Her frost-bright eyes went wide, and her hand found Sable's wrist with sudden desperate strength.

"They're here," she whispered. "Boy—the vaults are beneath us, and Varrow wants what's in that child more than she wants her next breath. Whatever you're going to be—" her grip crushed his pulse, "—decide fast."
