## 10. The Last Archive

The gates of Hollowmere stood open behind them, and the cold that came through them was not weather.

Sable felt it the moment Wren's hand slipped from his—the absence of her weight against his side, the sudden lightness of a body that had carried too much for too long and now carried nothing at all. She stood in the snow before him, six years old and older than kingdoms, and she looked up with eyes that held every voice the Wardens had ever silenced.

"You don't have to," he said.

She answered in Hollowtongue. He understood it still—would understand it until the last of it left the world or entered it—and what she said was simple as a knife: *Someone must remember them. You swore.*

"I broke that oath when I was younger than you."

*Then this is how you keep it.*

The sanctuary hall stretched away behind her, roofless in places, its stones furred with frost. The dying keeper's body lay where they had laid it among the others, and Sable could feel the whole mountain listening—the way it had listened for centuries, hollowed and waiting to be filled again. The archive needed a vessel. Wren was the last one made for it, small enough and vast enough both, and if she carried it into the world, the war would find her. It always found her. Ilse Varrow would rise from the ice eventually, or another like her, hungry and patient, and no child should have to run forever.

There was another way. There had only ever been one other way.

"Come here," Sable said, and knelt.

Wren crossed the snow without a sound. When she reached him she put her small hands on either side of his face, the way her mother must once have done, in a village that existed now only in ash and in the memory Sable had glimpsed through her touch—a memory he would lose tonight, along with everything else. Her palms were warm. That undid him more than anything: that after all of it, after the burning and the flight and the drowning of a Warden-Captain in ten thousand stolen lives, the girl's hands were still warm.

He spoke the words the keeper had taught him in the last hour of the old man's life. They came out of his mouth in a language he had never learned, and each one cost him something he could not name yet, because naming was exactly what he was about to give away.

The first thing to go was the smell of his mother's kitchen.

It went quietly, without ceremony—one moment it was there, bread and woodsmoke and the particular iron tang of their dented kettle, and the next moment it was simply a fact about a stranger. He felt it leave like water leaving cupped hands. Then the sound of his father laughing. Then his sister's name, which had been a shape his mouth knew in the dark, and then was nothing, and then was not even a nothing, because you cannot miss what you never had.

He did not fight it. Fighting it would have been keeping it, and keeping it would have broken the transfer, and so Sable Ravensworth sat very still in the snow and let himself be emptied the way he had emptied a hundred strangers' pockets over fifteen years, except that this time he was the mark and the thief and the fence all at once, and the goods were everything he was.

Wren wept while she gave. He felt her grief pour through the joining of their hands—not just hers but the village's, the mother's, the keepers', centuries of mourning compressed into one small body—and he took it all, because taking was the one thing he had ever been good at, and this time it was mercy.

The hoard went next. Every charm he'd ever sold came home to roost: the widow's husband singing off-key in a bathhouse, the soldier's last morning with his brother, the baker's daughter learning to walk. Ten thousand lives he had carried as inventory, as currency, as sin—and they streamed out of him and settled into the deep places of his mind like silt settling in a flooded cellar, becoming foundation, becoming bedrock. The Hollowtongue did not sit in him the way magic sat in Wren, bright and loud. It sank. It became the ground he stood on rather than the fire he carried.

That was the design, he understood dimly, even as understanding itself grew distant. An archive is not a weapon. An archive is quiet. It waits. It holds what must be held until someone comes asking, and if no one ever does, it asks nothing of the world.

His crimes went. His rules went—*never steal from Wardens, never sell a whole life, never be remembered*, three commandments carved by a frightened young man whose reasons he could no longer recall. His oath went, the vow sworn at nineteen beside a pyre, the promise to the keepers he had abandoned. He felt it dissolve like sugar in rain and thought, absurdly, *good riddance*, and then could not think why he'd thought it.

Last of all, the night his family died began to go.

He grabbed for it. Even emptying, even willing, some animal part of him seized that memory and held—his sister's hand going slack in his, the smell of the smoke, the terrible ordinary silence afterward—and for one long heartbeat he was nineteen years old again, kneeling in ash, swearing his oath.

Then he let go. Because that was the price, and because he had spent fifteen years charging other people for forgetting, and it was past time someone paid.

The snow took him gently, the way sleep takes a child.

---

He woke to birdsong.

Not the thin winter piping of Corvane's starlings—real song, layered, insistent, the sound of a world making noise for the joy of it. Sunlight came through a gap in the ruined roof and lay across the stone floor in a warm bar, and dust motes drifted through it like slow golden fish. The air smelled of wet earth and new grass and, faintly, of woodsmoke from somewhere below the mountain.

Spring. The long winter had broken.

He lay still for a long time, taking inventory. He was forty-one years old—or so said the lines on his hands and the ache in his back when he finally sat up—but the number meant nothing to him. Nothing hurt except his body, which was a mercy. His mind was a house with all the furniture gone: rooms and rooms of clean pale space, sunlight moving through them unobstructed. He searched for a family and found a word—*family*—and no faces attached to it. He searched for his crimes and found an odd, pleasant emptiness, like a ledger with every page torn out. Somewhere far down, under everything, there was weight. A vast, settled, patient weight, like a library built into the bones of a hill. He did not examine it. Examining felt wrong, somehow—rude, almost, the way you don't read someone else's letters.

A girl was sitting at the edge of the sunlight, watching him.

She was perhaps six, dark-haired, with smudges of dirt on her cheeks and a stick in her hand with which she had been drawing in the dust. The drawing was of two figures holding hands beneath a mountain, done in the confident strokes of a child who has decided how things are. When she saw him stir, she did not speak. He noticed that immediately: she looked at his mouth with the particular attention of someone who has learned not to expect words from herself.

But when he smiled at her—slowly, uncertainly, the smile of a man greeting the only thing in the world he recognized—her whole face opened like a window.

"You were asleep a long time," she said.

Her voice was rusty, careful, a voice being used for maybe the hundredth time in its life. Ordinary speech, plain Corvane trade-tongue. And yet underneath it, woven through it, he heard the other language—the old one, the deep one—sounding faintly in the rhythm of her breathing, in the pause between her words. Not spoken anymore. Just present, the way a river is present under ice.

He understood it anyway. He understood it the way he understood gravity, or warmth, or the fact that the girl in front of him mattered more than anything else in creation.

"Do I know you?" he asked.

"You know me." She crawled across the stones and took his hand, fitting her fingers into his with the ease of long practice, and the touch rang through him like a bell struck in an empty room—familiar, inevitable, right. "You're Sable."

"Sable," he repeated. It sounded like a word from a story someone had told him once. He turned it over. It fit his mouth well enough. "And you're—"

"Wren."

"Wren." Yes. That was true the way the mountain was true. Something in his chest, some door he hadn't known was closed, swung open onto warmth. "Are we—what are we to each other?"

She considered this with great seriousness, head tipped, stick forgotten in the dust.

"You kept my words safe," she said at last. "So I'm going to keep yours."

"I don't have any words."

"You will." She tugged his hand, imperious, and he let himself be pulled to his feet, swaying, blinking in the enormous spring light. Below the sanctuary, the valley had turned green. Far off, past the white fields and the frozen river now running brown and loud with meltwater, the towers of Corvane caught the sun like knives being slowly sheathed. Someone would be looking for them, probably. Someone with grey eyes and a hunger that had drowned but might not have died. He felt a flicker of unease about that and could not say why, and the feeling passed like a cloud.

They walked down together through the ruins, the child leading, the man following, their shadows joined on the stones. At the gate she stopped, pointed at a patch of moss growing green between the cracks, and said a word—not in trade-tongue, not quite, a word from underneath all languages, soft and round and alive.

The moss shivered. Just barely. Just once.

Sable stared at it. The word echoed in him, and he found—he did not know how—that he could repeat it, perfectly, though he had never learned any such thing in his life. It rose to his lips like water finding its level.

"What language is that?" he asked.

Wren looked up at him with her mother's eyes, in which a whole burned world slept on, safe now, archived, waiting.

"The one we're going to teach you," she said, and led him down the mountain toward the waking world.
