## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

There are three hundred and eleven ways to cross the Thornfell mountains, if you count the ways that get you killed. I count them. It's a professional habit.

The other forty-one are mine.

Not legally, of course. Nothing about me is legal, which is precisely how I like my ledgers: short. In the tax rolls of the Duchy of Vael I am a smudge of ink, an entry so faded the assessor gave up and wrote *unclaimed*. In the garrison lists at Fort Brannoch I appear once, briefly, as *a woman with mules*, which is true in the way that a knife is technically a spoon. And in the great census of the free cities—three volumes, leather-bound, very proud of itself—I do not appear at all.

I paid good money for that last one. Best coin I ever spent.

Tonight I was spending considerably worse coin on eleven mules, four crates of Vel Saren brandy that had no business existing anymore, and one nervous factor named Osk who kept asking questions in the tone of a man who wanted answers but hadn't yet learned what answers cost on a mountain.

"Is it true," Osk said, for the ninth time since dusk, "that there's a pass where you can see yesterday?"

"It's true that there's a pass where you'll wish you hadn't asked." I checked the lead mule's footing where the trail narrowed above the drop. Gravel sang off the edge and took a long time to finish falling. "Walk where I walk. Talk when I talk. Which is mostly never."

"The men in Marrowgate said—"

"The men in Marrowgate say a lot of things over warm beer." I glanced back at him. He was a soft city shape against the stars, all elbows and ledgers, clutching his satchel of contracts like it might nurse him. "Marrowgate also says the Thornfell roads belong to the Duke. The Duke says they belong to the Crown. The Crown says they belong to God. The mountain has never been consulted, and the mountain is the only one of the four holding an opinion worth having."

He shut up. For almost twenty minutes, which for Osk was practically monastic silence.

I let the quiet settle because quiet is a tool, same as a rope or a bribe. On smugglers' roads, noise travels wrong—it bounces off the scree fields and comes back doubled, so a cough sounds like a patrol and a patrol sounds like an army. You learn to move like weather. Slow, inevitable, unremarkable. The trick isn't hiding from people. People are easy; they look at what they expect to see. The trick is hiding from *records*.

Every official crossing through the Thornfells gets written down. Gate tolls at Coldharbour, waystation logs at Nineford, the customs book at the Marrowgate bridge—all of it ink, all of it permanent, all of it a thread someone diligent could follow back to you. So I don't use official crossings. I use goat tracks that predate the duchy, riverbeds that change their minds every spring, and a shepherd's stair cut into the rock by hands that have been dead so long even their gods have forgotten them.

Eleven years I've walked these roads. Three years since I started walking them as no one at all.

Before that—well. Before that is a country that doesn't exist, and I've found it healthiest to keep my geography current.

* * *

We made the high shelf by moonset, where the pines give up and the world goes to stone and lichen. That was when Osk decided he'd recovered enough to be interesting again.

"That coin," he said. "At Coldharbour. The one you paid the ferryman."

"What about it?"

"It was old. Really old. Where does a woman like you get a coin like that?"

"A woman like me gets everything by not answering questions." But I touched my pouch without meaning to, and felt the small weight still left inside—one more of them, worn nearly smooth. "It's just money. Money doesn't have a history. That's its best quality."

Osk made a sound suggesting he did not believe this, which showed a flicker of sense I hadn't credited him with. Then he ruined it. "My grandmother used to sing songs about that seal. The sunburst over the water? She said her grandmother remembered when—"

"Osk."

"—when the kings of—"

"*Osk.*"

He stopped. Ahead of us the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock, and beyond it I could hear water—the Greywash, running high with melt, loud enough to cover conversation. Loud enough to cover other things, too, which is why I liked it.

"Vel Saren fell a hundred years ago," I said, when we were moving again. "Fell, burned, drowned, erased, pick your verb. There are no songs worth singing about dead countries. Sing about beer. Beer is honest."

But my hand was still near the pouch, and I was aware of the coins in it the way you're aware of a splinter—small, foreign, and deeper than it looks.

They'd come to me years ago from a peddler in Nineford, a handful of silver worn faceless, and I'd bought them cheap because old coin spends well with superstitious toll-keepers who think age equals luck. It wasn't until later, turning one under lamplight, that I'd made out the design beneath the wear: a sunburst rising out of stylized waves. I knew the design the way you know a scar—without remembering getting it. Some part of me had flinched before my mind caught up and supplied the name. Vel Saren. The kingdom behind the mountains that the histories insist on calling *vanished*, as though it had wandered off like a dog rather than been put down.

I should have melted them down. Any sensible smuggler would have. Instead I carried six of them in a drawstring pouch against my ribs, and told myself it was because old silver held its weight, and was careful never to examine the sentence too closely, because sentences like that have doors hidden in them.

* * *

The toll came due an hour before dawn, as it always did, at a place that appeared on no map and belonged to no authority I recognized—which is why I paid it gladly.

Hesk's bridge spanned the Greywash at its narrowest point, three pine trunks and rope, rebuilt every spring after the floods took their tithe. Hesk himself sat beside it in a hut made of driftwood and stubbornness, wrapped in a sheepskin that had achieved a texture somewhere between blanket and geology. He was seventy if he was a day, missing two fingers and most of his teeth, and he was the closest thing the high passes had to law.

"Ho, the bridge," I called, the old words, the safe ones.

"Ho, the mules." His eyes found me in the dark with the ease of long practice. "Cold night for contraband."

"Cold nights are for honest cargo. Warm ones are for lies."

He laughed, a sound like a cart going over gravel, and held out his palm. "Toll's doubled. Times being what they are."

"Times being what they always are." I dug in the pouch without looking. This was ritual, and ritual is dangerous precisely because you stop watching it. My fingers sorted by feel—ducal silver here, the old worn pieces there—and I counted out the toll and pressed it into his palm, and it wasn't until his hand closed that I heard the change in his breathing.

He went still. Not startled—still. The stillness of a man listening to something very far away, or very deep.

"Hesk?"

He opened his fingers. Moonlight lay along the coin. The sunburst caught it and threw it back, faintly, like something surfacing in dark water.

"Where did you get this?" His voice had dropped all its performance. What was underneath was older, and quieter, and it frightened me more than any shout could have.

"Peddler. Nineford. Why?"

He looked at the coin. Then he looked at me—at my face, properly, for the first time in eleven years of tolls—and whatever he saw there made him take half a step back, boot scraping on stone, his hand going to the lintel of his hut as though the ground had shifted.

"You have her chin," he said.

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below us the Greywash ran on over its stones, indifferent, carrying snowmelt down toward a world that had never heard of either of us.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, and it came out level, which cost me.

"No," Hesk said slowly. "No, I don't suppose you do." And then, horribly, gently: "She passed this bridge too. Long ago. Carrying you. She paid with the same coin, and she looked at me the same way you're looking at me now, like I'd spoken a word from a language you swore you'd forgotten." He turned the coin in his fingers. "I never told anyone. Not the Duke's men, not the priests, nobody. A toll-keeper forgets things. That's the whole trade. But I remember faces, girl. It's the one ledger I can't help keeping."

My heart was doing something arrhythmic and stupid. Eleven years of careful erasure, three hundred crossings, forty-one roads memorized down to their loose stones—and undone, apparently, by a chin, by an old man's memory, by six pieces of silver I couldn't make myself spend anywhere else.

"You're mistaken," I said. "Old men confuse faces. Take your toll. We're crossing."

"Take the coin back." He pushed it into my hand and folded my fingers over it, and his grip, for all his age, was iron and river-cold. "I won't have it. Not this one. Whatever's written on it—" he stopped himself, jaw working, glancing past me up the dark trail as though the mountains themselves might be listening, "—whatever's owed on it, it isn't mine to collect."

"Nothing's owed. It's *money*."

"Nothing is ever only money." He let go and stepped back into his doorway, and the shadow took him up to the eyes. "Cross, then. Cross quick. And girl—" the pause stretched, and when he spoke again his voice had gone strange, formal, like a man reciting something learned in childhood, "—the door remembers what the door remembers. Mind it doesn't remember you."

I stood there with my fist closed around a piece of a dead kingdom and felt, distinctly, absurdly, the sensation of a page turning somewhere far away.

Behind me, Osk whispered, "What did he mean by—"

"Cross the bridge, Osk."

We crossed. The mules went first, sure-footed, indifferent to prophecy or its absence. Halfway over I made myself open my hand and look at the coin one last time before burying it at the bottom of the pouch: the sunburst over the waves, worn nearly to nothing, holding on anyway.

*Vanished*, the histories said.

Standing on Hesk's bridge in the hour before dawn, with an old man's words stuck in me like a fishhook, I thought: *vanished things don't pay calls. Vanished things don't recognize their own.*

Something had recognized me tonight. Something patient, something buried, something that had waited a hundred years behind a door I didn't know existed—and had just, with a toll-keeper's flinch and a dead woman's chin, begun knocking.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself I'd sell the coins in Marrowgate, melt the doubt down with the silver, and be no one again by week's end.

I was lying. Even then, I think, some part of me knew I was lying. Because the pouch sat against my ribs, warm as a held breath, and all the long miles down to Marrowgate it never once went cold.

*Three days later, in a safehouse above the tanneries, a patron would open a century-old book and show me my own name. But that's another ledger, and it opens on blood.*
