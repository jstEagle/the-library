## 2. A Date With Winter

Marrowgate in the first week of deep winter smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool and the particular desperation of people who had bet the harvest on a mild season. I liked it anyway. There is an honesty in a city that smells bad on purpose.

I came down off the mountain with frost in my eyebrows and three hundred crowns' worth of contraband sleeping in my pack like a baby I intended to sell, and I went straight to the Gilded Scale, because that was where Master Ferren always said to go, and because the alternative was thinking about how tired I was, and I had made a discipline of not doing that.

The Gilded Scale was a counting-house by day and a gambling den by night and neither by the hour between, which was when I arrived, which was when Ferren received visitors who preferred not to be counted among his day associates or his night ones. The doorman — a slab of a man called Ottel who had once tried to rob me and now carried my luggage as a form of apology — let me through without a word. That was the arrangement. Ottel saw nothing, remembered less, and got paid in coin that had never touched my hand where anyone could watch it pass.

Ferren was upstairs in the blue room, which was painted green. He had told me once that he kept it that way so that anyone describing the room afterward would be wrong about something, however small, and small wrongnesses made interrogators doubt everything else. I have stolen a great deal of wisdom from that man over the years, most of it freely given.

"Astrid," he said, without looking up from his ledger. "You're late."

"I'm early. You just moved the appointment."

He smiled at the page. Ferren was seventy, give or take a decade he'd refused to disclose, with a face like a folded map and hands that trembled unless they were holding money or a cup, at which point they went steady as mason's work. He closed the ledger. Then he did something he almost never did.

He locked it.

"You look like a man about to show me something expensive," I said, shrugging out of my coat. "Either that or confess to something. With you those are usually the same evening."

"Sit down, girl." He poured two cups of plum brandy from the bottle he kept hidden inside a hollowed-out treatise on tariffs. "How was the pass?"

"Cold. The toll-keeper at Grey Ford took my coin with both hands and then looked at me like I'd handed him a dead bird. Otherwise uneventful. Cargo's downstairs under salt-hides, manifest says dye-stuffs, nobody will check until after the thaw if I've done my work, and I've done my work."

"Mm." He pushed a cup at me. "Drink."

"Ferren."

"Drink, and then I'm going to show you a book, and then you're going to tell me what you see in it, and I want your honest eyes, not your professional ones. Your professional ones lie beautifully."

That alarmed me more than it should have, and I noted the alarm and set it aside the way I set down heavy things at the top of a climb — carefully, knowing I'd have to pick them up again later. I drank. The brandy tasted like summer, which felt like an accusation in that room.

He brought the book out of a floor safe beneath the rug, which was itself beneath a second rug, because Ferren believed redundancy was the sincerest form of paranoia. It was a fat volume bound in grey leather gone soft as skin — and I use that comparison advisedly, though I didn't know yet how advisedly. No title. No clasp. The spine had been repaired once with thread the color of dried blood.

"Where did this come from?" I asked.

"A dying man in Lowmarsh sold it to me for enough opium to die comfortably. He said it was worth ten times that and he was glad to be rid of it. I've spent four months verifying its age. The paper's a century old, maybe more. The binding is Vel Saren work — see the stitch pattern? Triple-threaded spine. Nobody's done that since the kingdom went under."

Vel Saren. The name landed in me strangely, the way a word in a language you half-remember lands — familiar and foreign at once. Every child in the mountains grew up on Vel Saren the way they grew up on ghosts: a kingdom that had walked behind a door, so to speak, a hundred years ago, leaving roads that went nowhere and waystones no one could read and a hole in every map shaped exactly like itself.

"It's a prophecy book," Ferren said. "They were common enough, back then. Every hedge-wizard with a headache wrote one. Most of them are gibberish with good posture. But this one—" He opened it to a ribbon marker near the middle, and turned it toward me. "Read it."

The page was written in a careful, cramped hand, ink brown with age. The language was formal Old Thornic, the kind used for contracts and coronations. I read it aloud, because Ferren wanted my voice on it, and because reading aloud gives me time to think.

"*In the winter of the long frost,*" I read, "*when the usurper's writ runs to the last free stone, a daughter of the ninth house shall walk the smuggler's road with a name that is no name.*"

I stopped. My mouth had gone dry in the specific way it does before a border crossing, when you're about to hand a stranger a paper that decides whether you hang.

"Keep going," Ferren said softly.

"*Her true name shall be struck from every record, and she shall believe herself forgotten, but the mountains will remember her, and the door opens on her blood.*"

I looked up. "Ferren. This is—"

"Turn the page."

There was a date. Not a year — a day. A specific day, written in the same cramped hand, in a different ink, older-looking, as though the original scribe had come back to the book decades later and added one line:

*She dies on the eve of the solstice.*

And beneath the date, in letters that seemed darker than the rest, pressed harder into the paper, five words:

*Astrid Oyelaran, called Ash.*

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about me: I have been three names in my life and none of them mine. I was born to a name I don't remember being given. I was raised under a name that belonged to a dead cousin. And for three years I have been Ash, which is not a name at all, really — it's a place where a name used to be, swept clean. I paid good money to people in four cities to make it so. I have watched clerks run their fingers down their own ledgers and slide past the space where I should be, their eyes glazing like men looking into fog. Erasure is a craft, and I am very good at it, and I have told myself for three years that I chose it, which is the kind of sentence that only works if you never examine the word *chose* too closely.

So when I saw my name in a century-old book, my first instinct was not fear.

It was professional offense.

"This is a forgery," I said. "A good one. Recent ink aged artificially — you'd know the trick, Ferren, it's in half the archivists' manuals. Iron-gall laid over with a walnut wash, baked slow. Someone with real skill and a grudge put my name in here, and I'd very much like to know who, because I've been careful, and careful people don't get named in books."

Ferren watched me with his steady hands folded. "When was the book bound?"

"A century ago. You said so yourself."

"And when was the page written?"

I hesitated.

"The parchment analysis says the page predates my grandmother," Ferren said. "I checked twice, with two different people, neither of whom knows the other exists, and both of whom I threatened. The ink isn't faked, girl. The name has been sitting in that book longer than you've been alive."

"That's impossible."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "It is. And yet."

I laughed. I'll admit that — I laughed, a short ugly sound, and drained my brandy, and held out the cup for more, and he gave it to me. "All right," I said. "Let's play it your way. Some hedge-prophet a hundred years ago wrote down the name of a child not yet born. Fine. Names repeat. Oyelaran isn't rare in the western valleys. Astrid isn't rare anywhere. A coincidence dressed up as a miracle."

"Read the date again."

"The solstice eve. Which is—" I stopped. Did the arithmetic. Felt the bottom drop out of the evening like a trapdoor. "Nine days from tonight."

"Nine days," Ferren said. "'The door opens on her blood.' Do you bleed easily, Ash?"

"Don't."

"I'm not threatening you. I'm asking whether you believe in doors."

I stood up. Pacing helps; it's the one honest vice I've kept. The green room suddenly felt smaller, the way rooms do when you notice the exits. "What I believe," I said, "is that someone wants me frightened, and this is an expensive way to do it, and expensive means powerful, and powerful means I should leave Marrowgate tonight. If this is a hook, it's baited with the one worm that works on me, which means whoever baited it knows me, which means—"

"Which means," Ferren interrupted, with the mildness of a man laying down a winning card, "that either someone knows you well enough to forge your name in century-old ink across four independent tests — in which case you are already found, and running changes nothing — or the book is what it appears to be. In which case, also, running changes nothing. I'm an old man, Ash. I don't offer comfort. I offer accurate menus of doom."

"You're a ray of winter sun, you know that?"

"I've been told I glow." He tapped the book. "One more thing, and then I'll let you storm out properly. There's a colophon. Back page."

I flipped to the end. The colophon was a single line, in the original hand, the way scribes once signed their sins:

*Set down by order of the Archivium Royal of Vel Saren, in the last year of the kingdom.*

"The royal archive," Ferren said. "Not a hedge-wizard's fever dream. This was an official document of the crown. Kingdoms don't forge prophecies against children unborn — they have no motive. They forge them to legitimize kings already crowned. Which raises the question of why the last legitimate government of Vel Saren spent its final year writing about a smuggler girl and a door."

I had no answer. What I had was a coldness in my chest that I recognized from the far side of certain mountain passes — the cold that means the weather has turned and the smart move is down, now, before the snow closes everything.

"I need to think," I said.

"You need to sleep," Ferren said. "You can have the room above the chandler's, the usual place. Think tomorrow. Fear thinks badly; I've watched it try for sixty years." He paused at the door, and for a moment he looked every one of his undisclosed years. "Ash. Whatever that name in that book costs you — whatever it *meant*, a hundred years ago — you should know that I have known you eight years, and in all that time you have never once lied to me about anything that mattered. Whoever you were before you were Ash, I'd wager she was worth the ink."

He left. I sat alone in the green room that wasn't green, next to a dead kingdom's book, and did not sleep.

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what I thought in those hours, because later — after everything — I was accused of having known, of having suspected and said nothing. Here is the truth: I cycled through disbelief and fury and a black, glittering humor, the kind that shows up at funerals uninvited. I invented eleven separate forgers, each with a motive and a method, and dismantled nine of them before midnight. I considered that Ferren himself had staged it, and rejected it — he'd shown me the verification records, and Ferren's paranoia was load-bearing architecture; you didn't fake that, you lived in it.

Somewhere past the second hour I caught myself whispering the words back to myself. *The door opens on her blood.* Testing them for weight. And underneath the denial, in the cellar of me where I keep the things I don't look at, something old and patient turned over in its sleep, the way the mountains turn over sometimes, and settle a half-inch lower, and every waystone in three valleys hums.

I decided, around the third bell of morning, that I would leave the city at dawn. Not in panic — in *method*. I would go north to Harrow Fen, lose myself in the reed-country where faces drown, and I would send no messages, because messages are how careful people get found.

I never got the chance to be methodical.

I smelled the smoke before I heard anything, curling under the door of the chandler's room, sweet and resinous — not lamp oil. Something hotter. Pitch-fire, the kind that doesn't negotiate with stone. Below me, the chandler's yard erupted into shouting, and through the window slats I saw them coming up the street in file: six men in the grey-and-scarlet of the Usurper's Inquisition, moving with the unhurried precision of people following a map, not hunting a scent. They weren't searching the street. They walked straight to Ferren's door.

Straight to it. As though it were marked.

I was down the back stairs and through the chandler's drying loft before the first torch touched the Gilded Scale's eaves, and I made myself stop in the alley — made myself look — because a part of me insisted on confirming what I already knew. Fire climbed the counting-house windows like ivy in fast motion. Ottel the doorman lay in the yard, very still, in a spreading dark that wasn't shadow. And at the gate, directing his men with two fingers, unhurried as a man arranging flowers, stood an officer I didn't know — tall, greying at the temples, with the calm face of a librarian — and he was holding a book.

Grey leather. Fat. Soft as skin.

Even at that distance, even across firelight and forty feet of freezing air, I knew it. And the officer turned his head, slowly, deliberately, and looked directly into the alley where I stood — into the exact shadow where any ordinary eye would have slid past a woman in a drab coat with a forgettable face — and he smiled.

Then he tucked the book under his arm, gave a small courteous bow, and walked away from the burning building as his men finished their work.

I ran. I'm not ashamed of that; the dead don't grade style. I ran north through the waking city with the smoke chasing me along the rooftops and my heart hammering out a rhythm I couldn't parse, and somewhere between the tannery bridge and the north gate I understood the thing I'd been refusing to understand all night, the thing Ferren had offered me in his menu of dooms:

If the book was forged, they had it, which meant the forger worked for them, which meant I was found.

And if the book was true —

Then the mountains remembered me. And so, apparently, did the Inquisition.

Behind me, Marrowgate's bells began to ring the fire-alarm, nine strokes and silence, nine strokes and silence, like a countdown nobody had asked for. Nine days to the solstice. I went over the north wall in the grey dark with frost forming again in my eyebrows, carrying nothing but the clothes I stood in and a name I was suddenly, desperately unsure had ever really been mine.
