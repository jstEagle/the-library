## 3. The Archivist Who Lied

I left Marrowgate the way smoke leaves a burning house—through gaps nobody had bothered to seal.

The safehouse was still glowing behind me when I slipped into the tannery district, where the air was so thick with rendered fat and lime that even a bloodhound would have wept and given up. I'd used these streets for three years. I knew which doorways remembered you and which ones forgot you by morning. The tannery forgot everything; that was why I loved it.

My plan was simple, the way all my plans were simple: walk north until Marrowgate stopped mattering, then keep walking. The Thornfell passes were three days out if the weather held and six if it didn't, and the weather, in my experience, rarely held for anyone.

What I did not plan on was the man sitting in my escape route like a toll I hadn't agreed to pay.

He was wedged into the alcove beneath the old aqueduct, a lantern turned down to a coal, and he looked less like an ambush than like a man who had been waiting so long he'd begun to fossilize. Grey coat, greyer face, ink stains on his fingers so deep they looked like bruises. A satchel at his feet with the kind of buckles archivists use—the kind that announce *I contain documents and I will die before these buckles open for you*.

"Miss Oyelaran," he said.

Every muscle in my body did the thing muscles do when a stranger says your name out loud in the dark, which is: nothing useful whatsoever. You can't run from a word. It's already inside your ears.

"That's not my name," I said, because it wasn't. Not anymore. Not for three years. "And you're sitting in my road."

"I'm aware of both facts." He raised the lantern a fraction, and his eyes were pale and tired and entirely too awake for this hour. "Tam Vessery. Formerly of the Royal Archive at Corvess. Currently of nowhere, which I find suits me."

"Congratulations. Move."

"You're going to want to hear what I have to say first."

"People who say that are usually wrong," I told him. "People who say it while blocking a smuggler's road at midnight are usually about to be very wrong, briefly."

He didn't move. He did something worse: he opened the satchel and took out a book. Not just any book. *The* book—or its twin, its sister, whatever you called the second copy of a thing that should only exist once. Leather gone soft as skin, pages the color of weak tea, and on the open page, in a hand that had been dead a century:

*Astrid Oyelaran. Born under the eaves of Thornfell. The door opens on her blood.*

"The date's tonight," he said quietly. "Your patron showed you the other copy. And then the safehouse burned, which means someone else read it too, which means—" he closed the book with a small, terrible gentleness, "—you're currently the most valuable person in the free cities, and everyone hunting you knows exactly where you're going."

"And where am I going?"

"Vel Saren," he said. "Or the door to it. Everyone assumes you'll run there. Which is precisely why you shouldn't."

I should have walked away. Any sensible smuggler would have. But he'd said one word that snagged in me like a fishhook through soft cloth, and I heard myself ask before I could stop it:

"What do you mean, *forged*?"

His smile was thin and joyless. "Ah. Now we're having a conversation."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being erased: you spend so much time becoming unreadable that you forget how to be read yourself. Three years of paying in unmarked coin, of answering to *Ash*, of letting innkeepers' eyes slide off me like rain off waxed canvas—and now some ink-stained stranger opens a book and says my mother's name, and I come apart like wet paper.

Because that was the hook. Not the prophecy. Not the fire. The name.

"Sarel Oyelaran," Tam Vessery said, and watched my face do something I couldn't control. "Hedge-witch, herb-trader, resident of the upper Thornfells until nineteen years ago. Disappeared the same winter her daughter did. The usurper's clerks wrote her down as *deceased, cause unknown*, which is clerk-language for *we didn't check*."

"She's dead," I said. My voice came out flat. Good. Flat was professional.

"Probably," he agreed, with the tactlessness of a man who has spent thirty years among records and none among people. "But here's what matters: Sarel Oyelaran appears in four separate tax ledgers between the years 1189 and 1201. Four. And in each one, the entry is written in the same hand—a hand that also appears in the margin of the Vel Saren succession rolls, correcting them. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your mother was literate in archive-cipher. Hedge-witches don't learn archive-cipher. Archivists do. Or their daughters do."

I stared at him. "You're saying my mother worked in the Royal Archive."

"I'm saying she *wrote in it*. What she did there—" he shrugged, palms up, "—the records don't say. Records never say the interesting part. That's rather the point of records."

We had moved by then. He'd insisted on moving—*the aqueduct alcove faces the tannery gate, and the tannery gate faces the inquisitors' postern, and you've been standing in a sightline for ten minutes*—and so we walked, side by side, two shadows arguing along the drainage channel behind the dyers' row, where the water ran green and no one with authority ever came.

He talked. I listened the way I listen to everything: cataloguing exits.

"The prophecy book," he said, "was entered into the Royal Archive's catalogue in the year 1198, accession number 4471, donated by an anonymous benefactor. Anonymous donations to royal archives are like anonymous letters—they're never anonymous, they're just deniable. The book sat uncatalogued in any meaningful way for ninety years, because it's nonsense. It predicts the return of a kingdom that's been gone a century. Then, fourteen months ago, it was pulled from the shelves, recatalogued as *priority*, copied twice, and the copies began to travel south. Toward the free cities. Toward wherever the last loose threads of Vel Saren's bloodline might have washed up."

"So someone wanted it found."

"Somebody wanted it *found by the right person*," he corrected. "Think like a clerk, Miss Oyelaran. If you're the usurper—if you're Corvane Halde's master, or Halde himself—and you believe, or half-believe, that an heir of Vel Saren survived the purges, how do you find her? You can't search the mountains. The mountains don't answer questions. So you write a document that only she can answer. You put a true name in it—you have true names, buried in the old succession rolls—and a date, and a promise, and you let the book drift like chum in water. And then you watch what comes to feed."

"And what came to feed burned my safehouse with my patron's people in it."

"Yes," he said simply. "That would be the trap closing."

We stopped at the mouth of the drainage tunnel where it met the city wall—an old smuggler's postern, barred from the outside since before I was born, except that bars mean nothing to a woman with three years of practice and a good file. I put my shoulder against the stone and considered him.

"Why tell me?" I asked. "If it's a forgery, if it's bait, then the smart play for you is to sell me to Halde. You're a disgraced archivist. Disgrace costs money. I've seen what informants make these days."

Something crossed his face—not guilt exactly, but guilt's older, uglier cousin. Recognition.

"Because I helped make the bait," he said.

* * *

You must understand what the Royal Archive was, before it was Halde's.

It was a cathedral built to memory. Nine floors of shelving sunk into the rock beneath the palace at Corvess, climate-charmed to hold paper steady for centuries, lit by lamps that burned without consuming anything—which should have been our first warning that the whole edifice rested on borrowed magic, but we were archivists, not philosophers. We believed a record kept was a debt honored. We believed the past could be made to hold still.

I was Third Under-Clerk of the Succession Rolls. Vel Saren was my assignment. A dead kingdom, a drawer full of dead names, a career of exquisite irrelevance. I loved it. I want to be honest about that—I loved the dust and the silence and the feeling that nothing I touched could hurt anyone.

Then the purges came, and the new king's men arrived with lists, and I discovered what archives are actually for.

They are for making people stop having existed.

I did it forty-one times. I have counted. Forty-one names struck from the rolls, forty-one lives reclassified as error, rumor, never-was. Some were already dead—that was the official line, that we were merely *correcting the record*. Some, I suspect, were not. There was a girl on the list once, aged nine, marked *deceased, infancy*, and I remember my pen hesitating over the entry, and I remember writing it anyway, because the alternative was to become a name on someone else's list.

That is how corruption works. Not as a choice between good and evil, but as forty-one small decisions to keep your hands.

So when I say I recognized the forgery, understand what I recognized: the *handwriting of fear*. The false entries in the prophecy book's provenance—the donation record, the recataloguing order—were written the way I wrote my forty-one. Too neat. Too obedient. Documents forged by frightened men have a smell, and I know it because I've produced it.

When the copies went south, I stole one and followed it. Because either the book was bait—and I owed the world forty-one corrections—or it was real, and I owed the dead kings of Vel Saren a century of overdue honesty. I genuinely did not know which. I still don't. That is the confession I intended to make to her, eventually, when trust permitted: that I am not a man with answers. I am a man with a debt.

* * *

The file took eleven minutes. Bars, as I've said, mean nothing.

Tam Vessery ducked through the postern after me, complaining under his breath about the state of his coat, and together we dropped into the pine dark on Marrowgate's northern slope. Behind us the city glowed orange in patches—the safehouse fire had spread to the granary street, because fires are loyal to no one—and ahead of us the Thornfells stood black against the stars, wearing their snow like a dare.

I should have lost him at the tree line. Every instinct said lose him. He knew things, and things known are things that can be tortured out of you later, and I had spent three years ensuring there was nothing in anyone's head worth torturing out.

But he'd said *forty-one names*, and he'd said my mother's name, and he'd said *I helped make the bait* with the voice of a man handing you a knife handle-first, and I found I couldn't quite bring myself to leave him standing alone in the dark with a stolen book and no roads.

"You realize," I said, as we climbed, "that I can't verify a single thing you've told me."

"No," he agreed, puffing slightly. Archivists, it turns out, do not climb. "You can't. That's the nature of evidence—it requires institutions, and every institution that could verify my claims is currently owned by the people I'm accusing. Welcome to my life for the past year."

"So why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't." He stopped, braced a hand on a boulder, and looked at me with those pale, exhausted eyes. "Trust is for people with clean hands. What you should do is the arithmetic. Ask yourself: what does each of us gain from the other? If I'm lying, I've spent a year and my last coin tracking a smuggler through the free cities to deliver her to a man who pays informants in silver, not in truth. Possible. But then why warn you off the road to Vel Saren? A delivered fugitive is worth more than a warned one. Whereas if I'm telling the truth, then I need you alive, free, and *suspicious*—because a suspicious woman checks my work, and checked work is the only kind worth having."

"That's a very tidy argument."

"I'm an archivist. Tidiness is my native language." He resumed climbing. "Besides, there's the blackmail."

I nearly missed a foothold. "The *what*?"

"Mutual blackmail," he said, with the mild satisfaction of a man presenting a well-organized filing system. "You know what I did in Corvess—forty-one erasures, if I'm ever fool enough to give you the details, which I won't. And I know what you are. Not the prophecy nonsense—what you actually *are*: a woman who has spent three years making herself unfindable, which means somewhere in your past is a reason, and reasons are leverage. Neither of us can report on the other without destroying ourselves. It's the most stable arrangement in human history. Empires have been founded on less."

I laughed despite myself—a short, ugly bark that startled a roosting bird out of the pines. He was right, and he was horrible, and there was something almost restful about dealing with a man who admitted upfront that the alliance was a pair of knives pointed at each other's throats, held at a friendly angle.

"Fine," I said. "Partners in mutual ruin. But ground rules. One: we don't take the main passes. Two: you carry your own pack. Three: if the book turns out to be real—if there's a door, if there's an heir, if any of it is true—you tell me *before* you write it down anywhere. I've seen what happens to things that get recorded."

He was quiet for a few steps. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—lost the lecture-hall crispness, gained something rawer.

"My wife used to say that about me," he said. "That I trusted paper more than people. She was right. Paper doesn't lie to you, it just gets lied *into*, and I spent thirty years not noticing the difference." He adjusted the satchel strap on his shoulder. "Agreed. All three rules. And a fourth, if you'll allow it: when we reach the old Vel Saren roads—and we will, because the low routes are crawling with Halde's riders by now—you let me see what the mountains do. Whatever they do. I need to witness it myself. Evidence or forgery, I need to stand in front of it."

"The mountains do plenty," I said, thinking of the toll-keeper's flinch, the coin with the seal, the way certain bridges seemed to reform under my boots at dusk like they were remembering their own shapes. "Most of it I'd rather they didn't."

"All the more reason." He glanced back at the burning city, then forward at the dark peaks, and I saw him make a decision the way I'd seen clerks commit an entry—irrevocably, with a small nod. "One more thing, Miss Oyelaran. Before we go further. The prophecy gives eight names before yours. Eight heirs, each dated to die young, each struck from the succession rolls in the same hand as the rest of the forgery work. I've spent a year trying to trace them, and I've found seven graves and one empty coffin, and every grave is in the Thornfells, and every grave is *recently visited*. Someone has been leaving flowers."

The wind came down off the high snowfields and moved through the pines, and for a moment neither of us said anything.

"Flowers," I repeated.

"Fresh ones," said Tam Vessery. "On graves a century old. Whoever tends them knows things the records don't. And tomorrow night, if the weather holds, we pass within a mile of the nearest one."

He started walking. After a moment, hating myself a little, I followed.
