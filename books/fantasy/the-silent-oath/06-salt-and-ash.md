## 6. Salt and Ash

The cottage was smaller than I remembered, which is the way of childhood places. Everything from before you were ten gets built at giant scale in your memory—doors tall as church arches, gardens the size of provinces—and then you go back and find a hovel with a sagging roof and wonder how anything that small could have held all that grief.

It had burned twice already. Once when I was nine, once when I was fourteen, both times courtesy of the Thornfell winters and a stove that hated us. This third burning was newer, and it hadn't been weather.

I stood at the edge of what had been the garden plot and let the wind off the high passes carry the smell to me: char, old rain, and underneath it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, salt. Hedge-salt. Sera Venn had kept her wards the old way, worked into ash and bone-meal and honest sweat, and even ruined they still muttered to themselves. The cottage was dead but not silent.

"You could walk away," I told myself, because talking to yourself on mountainsides is only madness if nobody answers, and I never answered.

I walked in.

* * *

Sera Venn had been my mentor for four years, which in smuggler arithmetic is roughly a lifetime. She'd taught me letters, ledgers, which mountain herbs stopped bleeding and which started it, and—most valuably—how to be forgettable. Not invisible; invisibility costs money. Forgettable is free. You learn to stand where eyes slide off you, to speak in a voice people's ears decline to keep, to be the woman who was maybe there, probably not, ask someone else.

She'd found me half-starved at eleven, after my mother went into the ground without so much as a marker, and she'd asked me exactly one question about her: "Did she love you?"

"Yes."

"Then remember that and nothing else. The rest will get you killed."

I'd spent twenty years being furious about that advice, then twenty more—or felt like twenty more—being grateful for it. Grief keeps better in a sealed jar. Open it too often and it goes to rot.

The interior of the cottage was a black tooth of a room. The roof had mostly fallen inward, laying a skeleton of beams across the floor like ribs. Rain had gotten at everything since the fire, so instead of ash there was a grey sludge that sucked at my boots and smelled of wet cinders. The stove—the murderer of two separate roof fires—had survived, squat and iron and unrepentant in the corner.

I knew where she'd kept things. Everyone has a place, and Sera Venn's was beneath the hearthstone, because if you're going to hide something from inquisitors, hiding it under the thing that burns is either very stupid or very clever, and Sera had never once been stupid.

The hearthstone came up easier than it should have. As if it wanted to move.

Beneath it: an iron box no bigger than a loaf of bread, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle, and resting on top of it a sprig of rowan that should have rotted fifteen years ago and hadn't. Salt crystals clung to the wood like frost. My skin prickled where the air off the hole touched me, the way air does before lightning.

"Hello," I said to the box, because hedge-witchcraft runs on manners and I'd been raised polite.

The box didn't answer, which was fine. I'd had enough of things recognizing me lately. There'd been the toll-keeper at Graywater Stair who'd flinched at my Vel Saren coin like it had bitten him, and there'd been the prophecy book in Marrowgate with my name in it—my true name, the one that existed nowhere else, dated to the winter solstice—and I was handling both of those by the time-honored method of not thinking about them.

Inside the oilcloth, the box opened on a bed of wool gone grey, and in the wool lay a memory-charm.

I'd seen charms like it before, or their corpses. A memory-charm is a hedge-archivist's trick: a stone, usually river-smoothed, worked with a lattice of hair-fine silver wire, holding a voice the way amber holds an insect. Speak your truth into it while the wire's hot and it keeps. They cost more than most people earn in a year, and they're illegal in three jurisdictions, including all three that mattered.

This one was warm. In a cold ruin, in autumn, under a hearthstone. Warm as a hand.

My mother had died when I was eleven. That was the whole of the official story, and the unofficial story was the same story, because I'd made sure there wasn't another one. But here was a charm left in Sera Venn's keeping, salted against rot, waiting under the stone like a letter posted sixteen years late.

Some part of me—the part that had learned to read a cargo manifest upside down across a tavern table—noticed that my hands were shaking. The rest of me was busy picking the charm up.

* * *

You activate a memory-charm with blood, because everything worth having costs, and hedge-craft doesn't pretend otherwise. One drop, on the stone, and the wire remembers its heat.

I pricked my thumb on my knife—I have never in my life carried a needle, needles are evidence—and pressed it to the charm.

The voice that came out was small and clear and it stopped my heart like a fist.

"Astrid."

My mother's voice. Of course it was her voice; whose else would it be? But knowing a thing and having it happen to you are different animals, and I sat down hard in the wet ash of a dead woman's kitchen because my knees had resigned without notice.

"If you're hearing this, then Sera kept her word, which I never doubted, and you've come back, which I also never doubted, though I'm sorry for making you do it alone." A pause. Wind moved through the broken roof, and the voice didn't pause with it—it was a voice out of time, indifferent to weather. "You'll be angry. Be angry. Anger's fuel. Just don't let it drive the cart, or you'll end up in a ditch with excellent posture."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, but it came out.

"I don't have long," the voice said, "so listen. There is a door out of a country that no longer exists. Do you understand me? Not into. Out. Everyone who ever wrote about it wrote about getting in—the heir, the crown, the glory. Nobody asks what the door is *for*. It's an exit, Astrid. It was always an exit. Vel Saren locked itself away to escape a conquest, and locking a country in a box is just a slower way of burying it."

The wind again. Or the recording of wind from some other year, some other night, when my mother had stood somewhere dark saying these words into hot silver wire.

"The door is under water. You'll know it when the stones say our name. Nine names, going back. Count them and count yourself tenth, and then—" the voice wavered, caught itself, "—then decide whether you forgive me. Because I couldn't do what needs doing. I chose you over the kingdom, and the price was that the world would forget you exist. No ledger, no record, no witness. I thought I was saving you. Maybe I was. Maybe I was just a coward with good handwriting."

"No," I said aloud, to a stone, to a dead woman, to nobody. "No, you weren't—"

"There's salt in the box's lining. Take it. Hedge-salt remembers its purpose even after the witch is gone, and you'll need remembering before this is done." Another pause, longer. When the voice resumed it was quieter, the way voices go quiet when they've stopped performing. "I loved you first and last and largest. Whatever the books say about you—and something will say something, eventually, I could never scrub you clean enough to be safe—know that you were never a weapon. You were the point. Everything else was the handle."

The wire cooled. The charm went silent and ordinary in my palm, just a rock with expensive jewelry.

I sat in the ruins for a long time.

* * *

Here is what I had believed, right up until tonight: my mother had been a hedge-witch's daughter from nowhere, married badly, widowed early, dead young. A footnote. I had built my entire self on top of that foundation—smuggler, ghost, the woman with no ledger-line—and it turns out the foundation was a trapdoor.

*Nine names.* Tam Vessery, that dry stick of a disgraced archivist, had spent three days of our acquaintance insisting the prophecy named eight heirs before whoever came ninth, all erased, all dead young. Eight erasures. And my mother, scrubbing my existence out of every record she could reach, salt and ink and bribed priests, building my invisibility by hand like a wall around a well.

She hadn't hidden me from the world. She'd hidden me from the *door*.

And now the door was sending invitations anyway, via a hundred-year-old book with my death-date printed in it, and the usurper's inquisitors were reading the same mail, and somewhere behind all of it a dead country was reaching through a century of silence to grab the hem of the only person left who could hear it.

"Fine," I said to the ruin, to my mother, to the general state of the universe. "Fine. But I'm doing it my way, and my way involves nobody crowning anybody."

The salt in the box's lining was coarse and grey-pink, hedge-salt worked with something that made it glitter wrong in the low light. I filled the little pouch at my belt with it. It weighed nothing and somehow pulled downward, the way important things do.

That was when I heard the horses.

* * *

Three riders, coming up the goat-track from the valley, and I knew before I saw the livery that they weren't smugglers. Smugglers don't ride in threes; threes are for authority, one to talk, one to watch, one to write everything down. I put out the thought of my lantern—hadn't lit it anyway, years of habit—and folded myself into the shadow behind the collapsed chimney stack, and did the other thing, the free thing, the thing Sera Venn had drilled into me until it lived in my spine.

I stopped mattering.

It's hard to explain to people who've never done it. It isn't hiding. Hiding is physical—you behind a wall, breath held, heart loud. Forgetting yourself is stranger. You let your outline soften. You become the kind of presence a searching eye registers as *nothing interesting*, the way you register a fencepost. It costs something. Afterwards I always feel slightly thinner, as if I've paid for the trick out of my own substance. Tonight I paid gladly.

The horses stopped below the ruin. Boots in wet ash. Three men, and the front one carrying a lantern he raised with the unhurried confidence of a person who has never once needed to hurry.

"Inquisitor's office," said a voice, mild and carrying. "Anyone home?"

Nobody answered. Nobody was home. I was a fencepost.

"We know the property," the voice continued conversationally. "Registered to one Sera Venn, hedge-practitioner, deceased sixteen years. The fire that took it was logged as accidental, which interests us, because accidental fires leave patterns, and this one left *salt*. Somebody re-salted a dead ward recently. Within the season." A soft sound, almost a chuckle. "Salt doesn't burn, friends. It waits. Like everything else in these mountains."

They picked through the ruin with the thoroughness of people paid by the hour. I watched the lantern-light swing past my hiding place close enough to warm my knuckles. One of them kicked open the stove. Another pried at the hearthstone—found it loose, found the empty hollow, and called his superior over with a note of triumph.

"Well," said the mild voice, crouching. "Well, well. Someone got here first." He was quiet a moment, and I could feel him thinking the way you can sometimes feel weather coming. "A box. Iron, small, wrapped. And a charm missing from the wool—look at the impression, there, where the dust wasn't disturbed. A memory-charm. In a hedge-cottage." He rose. His boots crunched closer to the chimney stack, and my heart performed some acrobatics that would have impressed a circus. "Gentlemen. Burn it."

"Begging pardon, ser—it's burnt."

"Again. Thoroughly, this time. If we cannot have what was hidden here, we can at least make certain no one else finds comfort in the ashes." And then, softer, almost to himself, in a tone that made the back of my neck crawl: "She was here. Recently. The salt's still settling." He turned in a slow circle, lantern up, and I pressed myself into the fencepost-shape of nothing and felt his gaze pass over me like a hand over water. "Whoever you are, girl," he said to the empty mountainside, "you are running out of places that remember you."

They torched what was left of the roof-beams and rode back down into the dark, and I stayed a fencepost until the last hoofbeat faded, and a while after that.

* * *

I climbed out of the ruin while the new fire was still deciding what it wanted to be. Small flames, hungry ones, licking at wet timber and finding little to eat. The inquisitors had done Sera Venn's cottage the final indignity of burning it badly.

I stood at the garden edge again and watched it, and I won't pretend I was dignified about it. That room had held every Christmas I ever had. Every lesson. The smell of her stew, the scrape of her chair, the sound of her voice saying *again, slower, you're not stupid, you're lazy* —the only person alive who'd ever scolded me like I was worth correcting. Now it was smoke number three, and this time nobody would rebuild.

"They can't burn what's in my head," I said. Which was true, and also exactly what a fool says right before someone finds a way to burn that too.

But grief, my mother had said, is fuel. So I stood there and let the fire have the cottage, and I fed the anger into the furnace-room of my chest where I keep everything that makes me dangerous, and by the time the flames settled into sullen coals I had stopped shaking and started planning.

Under water. Stones that say our name. Nine generations carved, and me the tenth. A door that is an exit, not an entrance—if she was telling the truth, and her voice had the specific tired honesty of a woman past the point of lying—and if the prophecy's date holds, I had until the solstice to reach it before Corvane's hounds did.

There was one man alive who could read a century-old forgery fast enough to tell me what was real in that cursed book, and I had last seen him being blackmailed by me in a ditch outside Marrowgate. He'd hate the mountains. He'd complain the entire way. He wore guilt like cheap cologne and he flinched at loud noises.

He was also, unfortunately, the best I had.

I took one last look at the burning cottage—said goodbye to it, quietly, in the language Sera Venn had taught me, which has no words—and turned east toward the lowlands and the road where a disgraced archivist was presumably still waiting to be useful.

Behind me, sparks went up off the ruin and scattered into the dark, and for one strange moment they hung in the sky like a constellation, like a map, like nine stars and one more rising after them.

Then the wind took them, and I walked.
