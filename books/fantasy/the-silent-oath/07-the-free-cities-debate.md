## 7. The Free Cities Debate

The council chamber of Marrowgate smelled of beeswax and old arguments, which is to say it smelled exactly like every room where important men have ever decided not to do anything.

I had been in the city four days since the burning of the safehouse—four days of sleeping in a different smuggler's cellar each night, like a coin passed hand to hand—and Tam Vessery had spent all four of them insisting, with the stubbornness of a man who has lost his post and gained a cause, that we needed to warn someone. *Someone with power,* he kept saying, as though power were a substance you could find lying around if you knew which gutters to look in. I told him what I tell everyone who uses that phrase: power isn't a thing people have, it's a thing other people agree they have, and the agreement is always more fragile than it looks.

He didn't listen. Archivists never do. They think history is an argument you can win with citations.

So on the morning of the fifth day we stood together in the gallery of the Council of the Free Cities, wedged between a spice merchant's wife who kept checking her reflection in a polished brass plate and a young clerk whose quill scratched faster than anyone was actually talking, and we watched the free cities of the Thornfell marches debate whether they were about to be invaded.

The answer, by lunchtime, was no. It had been decided before we arrived, of course. The debate was theater—the way a funeral is theater, the way a trial is theater. Everyone already knew how it would end. They simply needed to perform the deciding of it so they could sleep afterward.

* * *

Let me describe the chamber, because the chamber is the argument.

It's a converted granary, vaulted, cold enough that the delegates wear fur-lined hoods indoors and consider this a mark of seriousness rather than discomfort. Twelve seats on a raised dais, one for each city, arranged in a shallow arc so that no one faces anyone directly—you can't look an opponent in the eye when the architecture forbids it, and Marrowgate's founders understood that a council where men can glare at each other tends toward knives, whereas a council where men must speak sideways tends toward nothing at all. Nothing at all being, historically, the preferred outcome.

Above the dais hangs the Concord, the founding charter of the free cities, inked on vellum gone the color of weak tea. I could read maybe a third of it from the gallery. The clauses I could make out concerned tariffs, toll exemptions, and the precise weight of a standard loaf. There was a clause about mutual defense somewhere in the middle—I'd heard Tam recite it on the road up from the pass, *an attack upon one city's walls shall be answered as an injury to all*—but from where I sat, the vellum had darkened exactly there, as if even the document had decided to stop looking at that part.

The question before the council, formally entered into the record by the delegate from Vail: *Whether intelligence received concerning the movement of the usurper Corvane Halde's forces warrants extraordinary measures of preparation.*

*Extraordinary measures.* In council language that means money, and money means every delegate suddenly remembering his constituents' feelings with the tenderness of new fathers.

I want to be fair here. I've spent three years learning how institutions fail from the outside—smuggling is, among other things, a graduate seminar in the gaps between law and reality—but watching it happen from inside the gallery, watching it happen slowly, in order, with minutes taken and quorum confirmed and procedural courtesies observed, was something else. It was like watching a drowning performed in slow motion by people who insisted, throughout, that the water was fine.

* * *

The delegate from Vail spoke first. His name was Odger Brant, and he had the voice of a man who had made a career of sounding reasonable, which is the most dangerous voice there is. Reasonable is what cowardice wears when it can afford tailoring.

"We do not dispute," Brant said, "that the usurper's armies exist. We do not dispute that they are numerous. What we dispute—" and here he smiled slightly, the smile of a man about to be generous with other people's lives, "—is arithmetic."

Arithmetic. He had charts. Of course he had charts; the man had commissioned charts. He unrolled them across the table and pointed out that the passes through the Thornfells closed with snow by midwinter, that a supply train moving through high country in deep winter loses a third of its strength to frost alone, that no army in recorded history had crossed the central massif between solstice and thaw. He cited two wars and a failed expedition by some long-dead duke whose name I've deliberately forgotten. The numbers were good numbers. That's the thing about Brant—they were *good numbers*. An army crossing in winter would be madness.

What the numbers did not account for, because numbers never do, was a man for whom madness is a tool he keeps sharpened on his belt next to the ordinary ones.

Corvane Halde does not move armies the way armies are supposed to move. I know this because I've moved things through those mountains for three years, things that weren't supposed to move, along roads that aren't supposed to exist, and I can tell you that the Thornfells close only for people who believe they're closed. There are ways through. There are always ways through—that's practically the definition of mountains, that they dare you. Halde has been buying smugglers' knowledge for two years. I know because some of my colleagues sold it to him, and I know because the ones who sold it stopped turning up afterward, which tells you both that he bought it and what he thought of the merchandise.

I couldn't say any of this, obviously. A woman who doesn't officially exist doesn't get the floor. So I sat in the gallery with my hands folded like a well-brought-up ghost and watched a man explain winter to a room full of people who lived below the snowline.

Tam, at least, could speak. He'd requested standing as a witness—disgraced archivists travel on their credentials the way drowned men float, briefly and against their nature—and the council granted him five minutes, which in council time is roughly the length of a sneeze.

He used them well. I'll give him that. He stood at the witness table in his shabby coat with its frayed archivist's braid still visible at the cuff like a scar, and he laid out what he'd found: supply requisitions from the usurper's southern armories dated for midwinter consumption, mountain guides retained under sealed contracts, grain purchases in the foothill towns at three times the usual rate. "They are not preparing to garrison the lowlands through winter," he said. "They are preparing to *march*. The requisitions don't lie. I forged enough documents in my life to know the difference between paper written to deceive and paper written to provision."

That last line got a murmur from the gallery—a genuine murmur, the sound of people recognizing honesty even when it comes wrapped in confession. The delegates on the dais heard it differently. To them, Tam was a disgraced man admitting to forgery, and everything he said thereafter was contaminated by the admission. One of them—Hessa Kole, from Dunmark, a woman with a face like a closed ledger—actually said, out loud, "We are asked to take the word of a confessed forger regarding documents." And the chamber chuckled, and Tam stood there taking it, jaw tight, and I wanted to stand up and say: yes. Take his word. He's the only man in this building who knows what forgery looks like from the inside, which makes him the only expert you have.

But that's not how councils work. Councils don't ask who's right. They ask who's clean.

* * *

There was a recess at midday. Recesses are where the real council happens; the dais is just where the minutes get read back.

I followed the crowd down into the concourse, keeping to the edges the way I do, and listened. This is my actual craft—not the passes, not the cargo, but the listening. Three years of erasing myself taught me that people talk differently when they think no one significant is present. They talk *true*.

What I heard in the concourse, over meat pies and watered wine:

A wool factor from Vail telling his junior partner that whatever happened, the contracts had to hold, because if the cities panicked the markets would follow and panic was worse than soldiers. Soldiers, he said, you can price. Panic reprices everything daily.

Two clerks arguing about whether the usurper would even *want* the free cities—too poor to bother with, too scattered to govern—and the older one saying, with the weary certainty of a man who has read dispatches, that Halde didn't want to govern anything, he wanted to *close accounts*, and the free cities were an open account.

And near the fountain, half-heard, a woman in guild colors—smuggler-guild colors, the grey-and-rust of the Thornfell Road Brotherhood—telling a man I didn't catch that her chapter had already voted. Already voted. Days ago. Before the council ever convened. Moving grain into the high caches, sealing the tunnel mouths they meant to keep, mapping which cellars connected to which streets.

The law was debating. The underworld had already decided.

I found Tam afterward, sitting alone on a bench by the concourse doors, eating nothing, staring at the middle distance with the expression of a man doing sums that keep coming out wrong. I put a meat pie in his hand because he forgets to eat when he's frightened, and he forgets he's frightened when he eats, and either way it helps.

"They're going to vote it down," he said. Not asking. Telling me, so that later, when it happened, he could say he'd known.

"Probably."

"The requisitions are *dated*, Astrid. Midwinter. Solstice week. I copied them myself off the merchant-post manifests."

"I believe you."

"Then help me understand why nobody—" He stopped himself, which was growth; three days earlier he'd have finished the sentence with *listens to me*. He took a bite of the pie instead and chewed like it owed him money.

"Because believing you costs money and disbelieving you costs nothing today," I said. "Today is the only day councils live in. You keep trying to hand them a bill due at solstice and wondering why they won't open it."

"That's cynical."

"That's *arithmetic*," I said, and enjoyed, briefly, viciously, throwing Brant's word back through the doors.

* * *

Afternoon session. The speeches blurred together the way afternoon speeches do—each delegate performing his city's virtue for the record, each carefully not saying anything that could be quoted against him in spring if spring went badly. Hessa Kole of Dunmark gave what the gallery agreed was the finest speech of the day, which should tell you everything: it was a speech about prudence. Prudence, she said, was the free cities' founding virtue. The Concord itself was an act of prudence. To arm, to fortify, to spend against a threat that might not come—that would be to become the thing the free cities were founded to escape. Kingdoms prepare. Free cities endure.

Endure. As if endurance were passive. As if walls hold themselves.

I sat there and thought about my mother, whom I barely remember, and about the hedge-cottage I'd grown up in, and about every village in those mountains where someone had once decided that preparing was alarmism and enduring was a plan. Enduring is what you call it afterward, if you survive. If you don't, they call it something shorter.

Brant called the question late in the afternoon, when the light through the granary windows had gone amber and everyone was tired and tired rooms vote for comfort. The motion: that the intelligence be referred to a joint commission for further study, with findings due at the spring session, and that in the interim each city attend to its own affairs as it saw fit.

Further study. Spring findings. Interim discretion. Every clause of it a delay wearing the costume of diligence. The commission would meet twice, quarrel about jurisdiction, request documents from the usurper's chancery—which would decline—and dissolve quietly in April having produced eleven pages of careful nothing. I have seen this exact mechanism work before, in smaller rooms, over smaller graves.

The vote went nine to three. Nine cities for waiting. Three for preparing: Dunmark, against Kole's own speech, because Dunmark sits closest to the passes and its delegate had seen the requisition manifests herself and apparently valued her eyes over her eloquence. And the third—

The third wasn't a city at all. That was the thing. That was the thing I keep turning over.

The twelfth seat on the dais belongs, by ancient and much-resented custom, to the Smuggler-Guilds' Compact—one seat, one voice, no veto, granted a century ago when the cities discovered that their roads through the mountains ran through territory only the guilds controlled, and that you cannot tax what you cannot see. The guilds' delegate that day was a broad, grey-bearded woman named Sera Ilva, whom I knew by reputation and she knew me by reputation, neither of us acknowledging the acquaintance, because that's how reputation works between professionals.

Ilva rose after the vote was announced, and the chamber braced for a protest, and instead she simply said: "Noted. The Compact will prepare regardless. The passes are ours to keep or close, and we will keep them open for refugees and closed for soldiers, and we will do it without your funding, your sanction, or your permission, because permission is a thing kingdoms require and we are not a kingdom." She gathered her papers. "You have voted to wait until spring. The snows will not wait, and neither will Halde. When your commission meets in April, I suggest you invite survivors."

And she walked out, and the chamber murmured its disapproval of her tone, and the clerk recorded the dissent, and the session adjourned on schedule, because schedules matter, because procedure matters, because when a ship is sinking the officers still log the hour.

* * *

Tam found me in the colonnade afterward. Or I let him find me; with Tam it's hard to distinguish pursuit from coincidence, since he moves through crowds like a man reading a document—head down, oblivious, arriving places by accident.

"Well," he said. "Democracy."

"Don't blame democracy," I said. "Blame the voters' imaginations. Democracy is just the machinery. Feed it fear and it arms; feed it comfort and it sleeps. Today they were fed comfort. Brant's charts were comfortable. Your requisitions were uncomfortable. Rooms choose comfort at three in the afternoon."

"You sound like you expected this."

"I expected this the way you expect weather. I grew up under a kingdom, Tam. I've lived under no government at all for three years, which sounds like freedom until you need a doctor. But I have never once lived under a government that prepared for trouble early. Preparation requires imagining yourself as the victim, and institutions can't do it. Institutions imagine themselves as the survivor. Always. The archive survives, the treasury survives, the council survives—it's the *people* who are expendable, and the people aren't seated on the dais."

He flinched at that, because Tam is an archivist and I had just described archives as things that outlive their contents, and there is no crueller sentence you can say to a man who has spent his life believing records are a form of rescue.

"So what do we do?" he asked. "If the law won't act—"

"The law will act," I said. "In April. It will act decisively, at length, with full quorum, over the ruins. Law is very brave retroactively. It's the bravest thing about it."

We stood in the colonnade while the delegates streamed past us into the evening, hoods up, conversations already pivoting to dinner and tariffs and a disputed bridge toll, the invasion filed away under *spring concerns*. Somewhere behind us the clerk was blowing sand across wet ink, preserving the decision perfectly for historians, which is the other thing law is good at: the record. The record will show the free cities deliberated. The record will show nine votes and three dissents and a guild delegate's sharp words. The record will be impeccable.

The record will not save anyone.

I know that better than almost anyone alive. I *am* a gap in the record. I have spent three years becoming one—no name in any ledger, no face in any register, no debts, no taxes, no trail—and I can tell you what the record's absence gets you: survival, of a kind. Cold camps. False names. A self worn smooth like river stone, all the rough particularity rubbed away. The record doesn't save people, but its absence doesn't save them either. Nothing saves people except people, acting early, without permission, on imperfect information, at cost to themselves.

Which is, I realized, standing there watching the council disperse into its warm evening, exactly what the smuggler-guilds had just voted to do.

* * *

Sera Ilva found me at dusk. Or sent for me—there was a boy, and a token, and the token was a lead seal stamped with the Road Brotherhood's mark, and the message was one line: *The gallery ghost should come drink with the guild.*

Gallery ghost. She'd marked me even up there, wedged between the spice merchant's wife and the clerk. Of course she had. You don't run a smuggling compact for thirty years without learning to spot the person in a room who is working very hard to be unremarkable, because that person is either a spy or a colleague, and either way you want to buy them a drink before someone else does.

I went. Tam came too, trailing me like a guilty conscience, and Ilva looked him over once and said, "The forger. Good. Forgers are useful," which Tam later admitted was the kindest professional assessment he'd received in six years.

Her chapter-house was above a rope-maker's shop, and it smelled of hemp and tar and strong tea, and it was full—not with dignitaries but with people: road-masters, cache-keepers, a woman who ran the night ferries, two brothers who between them knew every goat track from Dunmark to the salt flats. Maps on the tables. Grain tallies. Lists of cellar connections in Marrowgate's old quarter, annotated in three hands.

They were doing, that evening, in a ropewalk above a shop, with tea and chalk, everything the council had declined to do that afternoon in its granary with its charter and its quorum.

"Why?" Tam asked Ilva, genuinely wanting to know. "Why spend your own coin? If the cities fall, the guilds lose their markets same as everyone. If the cities hold, they'll never thank you. Where's the return?"

Ilva poured him more tea before answering, which I recognized as her version of contempt.

"The return," she said, "is that in spring, if it goes badly, there are ten thousand people between the passes and these walls who will need roads out, and roads out are our trade. We're not preparing for war, archivist. War is the cities' business. We're preparing for *refugees*. Refugees pay in labor and loyalty, and unlike cities, they remember who carried them." She glanced at me then, briefly, appraisingly, the look of a woman confirming a suspicion she'd been holding since morning. "Besides. Someone in this room owes the mountains a debt. Don't they, gallery ghost?"

I hadn't told her anything. I hadn't spoken above a whisper in her presence in my life.

But I'd paid a toll once, months back, with a coin bearing Vel Saren's seal, and the toll-keeper had flinched at me like a man recognizing a ghost, and Ilva's chapters control every toll-house on the western roads, and toll-keepers talk to their guild-mistresses the way priests talk to their confessors.

She knew something. She wasn't going to say what. Guild discipline. But she'd voted to prepare, and she'd summoned me, and she'd said *owes the mountains a debt* while looking straight at me, and I sat there with my tea going cold and felt the net drawing tighter—not Corvane's net, not yet, something older and stranger, woven from recognition, the thing I had spent three years fleeing finally catching up with me one knowing glance at a time.

Then a runner came in off the night road, snow in his collar, mud to the knee, and handed Ilva a strip of oilcloth, and she read it twice and put it face-down on the table and said, without inflection, the way bad news gets said in rooms like this:

"Foothill garrison at Karst Hollow doubled its grain draw yesterday. Third such report this month." She turned the oilcloth over, showed us the tally marks, the dates. "Your requisitions, archivist. Confirmed on the ground. They're provisioning to march."

Tam exhaled like a man surfacing. "When? Can you tell—"

"Winter," Ilva said. "Solstice week, if the weather holds cold. Which means," and here she looked at me again, harder now, and said the thing that ended the evening and started whatever comes next, "someone in this room has until solstice week to decide what she's carrying through those passes. Because the roads will close for everyone else, gallery ghost. Yours won't."

I didn't ask what she meant. She didn't offer. That's guild manners—we both pretended the sentence hadn't been said, and we both knew it had, and I walked home through the frozen streets of Marrowgate with a memory-charm in my pocket that I hadn't yet dared play, and a date in my head that wasn't mine, and the growing, sickening understanding that the law had finished with me hours ago, and everything that happened next would happen in the country without one.
