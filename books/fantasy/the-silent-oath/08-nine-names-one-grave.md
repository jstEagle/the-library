## 8. Nine Names, One Grave

I want to say, in my own defense, that I noticed the discrepancy at three o'clock in the morning, which is when all honest work happens and all dishonest confessions become inevitable.

We were camped in a shepherd's bothy two days north of Marrowgate, the kind of stone hut that smells permanently of sheep and old smoke, with a fire barely larger than a cupped hand because Astrid Oyelaran had opinions about smoke. She was asleep—or performing sleep, which is a distinct skill among people who have spent years being hunted—and I had the prophecy book open on my knees by firelight, doing what archivists do, which is read the same passage eleven times hoping it will apologize.

It didn't. It got worse.

Here is what I had missed, and I will tell you exactly how I missed it, because the manner of missing things is the only honest autobiography an archivist can write. The book—the *Ledger of the Hidden Year*, as its spine announced, in script a century dead—was organized not by prophecy but by succession. Each entry followed the same structure: a name, a birth-sign, a line of verse, and a date. I had read the entry for Astrid perhaps forty times since Marrowgate burned. Her name in the old Vel Saren orthography, her mother's name beneath it, the verse about the door opening on her blood, and the date: the winter solstice, this coming one, sixty-odd days hence.

What I had never done was count backward.

Archivists count. It is the first thing they beat into you at the Royal Archive in Corvane's capital, before they teach you anything else: *count everything, trust nothing that arrives pre-counted.* And I had been so busy being terrified of Astrid's entry—so busy constructing elaborate scholarly arguments for why a century-old book could not possibly name a living woman—that I had treated her page as an ending rather than what it plainly was.

The ninth entry.

I turned back a leaf. The eighth entry: a name I did not know, a birth-sign in the year of the Salt Frost, a verse about *the river remembering*, and a death-date. Age nineteen. Drowned, according to the marginal gloss in a later hand, though the gloss itself was in ink that didn't match the page, which is the sort of detail that keeps archivists warm at night.

The seventh entry: another name, another sign, a verse about *the mountain keeping its counsel*. Death-date. Age twenty-three.

The sixth. Death-date. Seventeen.

I counted backward through the whole book with my thumb moving down the margin like a man counting his own heartbeats while deciding whether to be alarmed. Eight names before Astrid's. Eight heirs, if the word *heir* meant what the structure said it meant. Eight birth-dates spaced roughly twenty-five years apart, like a kingdom taking a breath between attempts. And eight death-dates, every single one young, every single one preceded by erasure marks in the margins—those little scraped patches where a scribe had begun writing something and thought better of it.

Someone had tried to record these eight lives and someone else—or the same someone, later, frightened—had scratched them out again.

"Tam." Astrid's voice, from across the dead fire. Not asleep after all, then. Or woken by the sound of a grown man breathing like a bellows over a book. "You've found something."

"I've found arithmetic," I said. "Which is worse."

She sat up. In the firelight she looked the way she always looked in bad light: like nobody. That was her gift, her recorded absence, her talent for sliding off the eye. Three years of erasing herself from ledgers, and she could apparently do it to firelight too. "Arithmetic," she repeated.

"There are nine entries in the succession," I said. "Yours is the last. The other eight are all—" I stopped, because there was no gentle way to finish the sentence, so I finished it the way an archivist finishes sentences, flatly, letting the fact do the bleeding. "Dead. Young. Every one of them erased from the historical record. I've checked against everything I know of Vel Saren genealogy, and I can't match a single name. Not one. Eight heirs of the hidden kingdom, Astrid, and history doesn't remember that any of them existed."

The silence went on long enough for me to hear the wind rearranging itself around the bothy.

"You're saying the prophecy has killed eight people," she said at last.

"I'm saying something has killed eight people who were each, in turn, named in this book as the one who would open the door. And then erased their names so thoroughly that a royal archivist with access to the full Corvane archives couldn't find a trace." I closed the book, then opened it again immediately, because closing it felt like agreeing with it. "And your entry is dated sixty days from now."

* * *

I should explain how I know so much about forgery, since it becomes relevant, and since I am apparently going to spend this chapter confessing things, I may as well do it properly, in order, the way documents are supposed to be destroyed: oldest first.

Ten years ago I was a rising junior archivist in the Office of Provenance, which is the office that decides which old documents are real. It is, when you think about it, an enormous amount of power concentrated in people who are paid almost nothing and praised even less, and it attracts exactly the sort of person—young, clever, hungry to matter—who can be talked into using that power badly.

A man came to me. He was courteous, well-dressed, and introduced himself as an agent of the Crown's historical commission, which did not exist, though I did not know that yet, and he brought me a document and asked me a simple question: *could this have been written when it claims?*

I examined it. The parchment was right, the ink was period-correct, the hand was competent. But the scribe had made a mistake—a small one, a ligature style that fell out of use thirty years after the document's claimed date—and I told him so. I expected him to thank me and leave.

Instead he thanked me and asked if I could fix it.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because I have spent ten years telling myself a version of events with the guilt filed under *coercion*, and tonight, holding a book that kills people, coercion felt like too generous a filing system. He offered me money first. I refused the money—I remember being proud of that, God help me—and he smiled as if refusal were the answer he'd hoped for, and he explained that the document concerned a land claim, and that the rightful holder was a widow with three children who would lose her farm without it, and that the current claimant was a man with friends in the provincial court. He let me imagine I was choosing justice. He may even have believed it himself; liars always construct a version they can live inside.

I fixed the ligature. It took me an afternoon. It was the finest piece of work I have ever done, and I have hated my own hands ever since.

There were more documents after that. There are always more documents. Each one urgent, each one justified, each one smaller than the last until I couldn't find the size below which I'd refuse. I told myself I was a scalpel in the hands of surgeons. Then, when the commissions stopped pretending to be benign, I told myself I had no choice. Then, when I finally grew a spine and refused, I discovered that a forger's confession is worth more than his service, and that the men I had worked for kept excellent records—which is the one thing I can credit them for, professionally speaking.

That is why I was disgraced. That is why I was in Marrowgate, selling authentication services to smugglers who needed bills of lading aged convincingly, drinking in the evenings and telling myself that at least forged shipping documents moved grain rather than armies. And that is why, when I saw the *Ledger of the Hidden Year* for the first time in a patron's safehouse, I recognized—with the sick instant certainty of a man recognizing his own handwriting in a stranger's suicide note—the marginal erasures.

Not the ones around the eight names. Those were older, a different hand entirely.

The ones around *Astrid's* entry. Fresh scrapings. Recent. Done within the last few years, by someone working quickly, with a blade and bad light.

I knew the technique. Because I invented half of it.

* * *

"So let me hear it," Astrid said.

We had walked out onto the shelf of rock above the bothy, because she didn't like confessing indoors, or being confessed to indoors, or possibly because she didn't trust me not to burn the building down out of professional embarrassment. Dawn was still an hour off. Below us the valley held its snow like a held breath.

"The erasures around your entry," I said. "They're recent. Within the decade. Someone altered this book recently—not the prophecy itself, the margins. They scraped away whatever was written there."

"What was written there?"

"If I had to guess? Instructions. A provenance note. Where the book came from, how it was authenticated." I wrapped my coat tighter. My hands wanted to shake and I declined to permit it, mostly. "Astrid, a genuine century-old prophecy doesn't get its margins cleaned. Only a forgery needs tidying."

"You think it's fake."

"I think—" I stopped. This was the moment I had been circling for two days, the confession underneath the confession, and I found that my voice wanted very much to fail me, and I made it wait. "I think whoever made this book used techniques I helped develop. Techniques I taught, indirectly, through documents that circulated in certain offices. The erasure-and-reinscribing method. The ink-matching. Even the verse meter—it's modeled on the *Canticles of the Saren Court*, but the scansion is wrong in a way that a forger trained on the Canticles would get wrong, because the original meter depends on a pronunciation shift nobody teaches anymore."

She watched me for a long moment. Snow ticked down somewhere in the dark.

"And how would you know," she said, very quietly, "what forgers are trained on?"

Because she was not a fool. She had been reading me since Marrowgate, the way she read everything—toll-books, faces, weather, exits—and she had already assembled most of the truth and was now simply requiring me to say it aloud, which is the cruelest and most necessary thing an interrogator can do.

"Because ten years ago," I said, "I forged documents for the men who put the usurper's faction in power. Land claims. Succession papers. Two royal warrants. I was good at it. Some of the methods in circulation now are methods I originated." The wind took my breath and I spoke into the gap. "So when I tell you the prophecy might be forged, you are entitled to ask whether I am an expert or an accomplice, and the honest answer is that I don't know anymore, and that I have not known for ten years, and that this book might be bait set to flush out Vel Saren's heir using skills I personally put into the world."

I waited for her to walk away. I had rehearsed her walking away. It seemed the most likely outcome, and I had prepared a dignified speech for the occasion involving my intention to continue alone toward the lake regardless, which is easy to feel noble about in rehearsal.

Instead she said: "The toll-keeper."

"...What?"

"In the pass. When I paid with the coin. He flinched at me—looked at me like he'd seen me before, or seen someone who looked like I was going to look." Her breath fogged and shredded. "If your forgers planted this book to flush out the heir, they'd need people watching. Toll points. Crossings. Someone pays with Vel Saren silver, someone flinches, word goes up the chain." She turned to face me fully. "You're telling me the trap exists. I'm telling you I've already stepped in it once and walked out. So the question isn't whether the book is bait. It's whether the fish cares."

"That is not how traps work."

"No," she agreed. "Traps work on animals that have somewhere better to be. I don't." She said it without self-pity, the way you'd report a road closure. "Corvane's inquisitors burned my safehouse and my patron's people. Whatever waits behind that door, it's the only future on the table that isn't a pyre. If the prophecy is forged, then the door might not exist, fine—but then neither does my death-date, and I'd rather bet on a door that might be fake than a date somebody wrote in blood and believed."

"And if the book is genuine?" I heard my own voice go thin. "Eight heirs, Astrid. Genuine or forged, eight people stood where you're standing, read their own names in this succession, and died young enough that someone needed to erase them. Whether the prophecy *predicted* their deaths or *caused* them, the result is identical, and you are entry number nine."

For a moment she was silent, and I watched her do what she does, which is take a fact like a knife and decide where to keep it.

"Then we stop being entry numbers," she said. "All nine of them let the book define the terms. Died young, died erased, died *on schedule*. Fine. You're the archivist." She held out her hand for the book, and I gave it to her, and she held it up between us in the grey pre-dawn like evidence at a trial—which, I suppose, is exactly what it was becoming. "So find out what happened to the other eight. Not the verses. The *records*. Births, deaths, the erasure marks—who scraped them, and when, and why. You said someone tried to write their histories down before someone else scratched them out."

"Yes."

"Then somebody, a century ago, thought they mattered. Find that somebody's notes." She handed the book back. "And Tam—"

"I know."

"You don't. Listen anyway." Her voice dropped into something that wasn't anger. Anger would have been easier to bear. "I don't forgive you. I've known you nine days; forgiveness isn't on the menu. But I've spent three years making myself unrecordable, and I know exactly what it costs a person to put their name back on paper. So: you confessed. That buys you a hearing, not absolution. Don't confuse the two, and don't expect me to."

"I wouldn't dream of it," I said, and discovered that I meant it gratefully. Absolution is a debt; a hearing is a job. I have always preferred jobs.

* * *

We worked through the morning, and I will record what we found, because it is the kind of finding that changes the shape of a war.

The erasure marks on the eight earlier entries were not uniform. Under magnification—by which I mean holding pages at arm's length toward the failing firelight and squinting like a fraud, our lamp-oil being rationed—three distinct hands emerged. The earliest erasures were rough, hurried, done with a knife-edge: someone destroying information in panic. The middle set was cleaner, professional, the work of a trained scribe following a policy. But the last set, the ones around the eighth heir, were the strangest: the text hadn't been scraped away so much as *transferred*—faint ghosting on the facing leaf where wet ink had been blotted off one page and pressed, deliberately, onto another. Whoever did that wasn't destroying the record.

They were hiding it *inside the same book*.

I turned to the facing leaves and read the ghost-text by tilting the pages against the light, and there it was, in a century-dead hand pressed thin as breath:

*A list. Eight names—the true names, the scraped ones—each followed by a place and a phrase. The first seven places I did not recognize. The eighth I did: a village name that appears on no modern map, in the high valley above the drowned lake, where the memory-charm's directions begin.*

And beside each name, the same four words, written eight times, growing fainter with each repetition until the last was barely a shadow:

*They chose to be forgotten.*

I sat with that for a long time. Astrid watched me, and did not rush me, which I have come to understand is her particular mercy.

"They weren't murdered," I said finally. "Or—not only. The erasures weren't done *to* them. At least some were done *by* them, or by those who loved them. Eight heirs who reached for the door and then... withdrew. Erased themselves. The way you did, Astrid. The way your mother arranged." I looked up. "This isn't a succession of victims. It's a succession of refusals. And every refusal bought the kingdom another twenty-five years of hiding."

"Until the book says the ninth doesn't refuse."

"Until the book says the ninth dies instead," I corrected, because I owed her accuracy even now. Especially now. "Which is either a prophecy or a threat, and I no longer believe I can tell the difference from the outside. What I can tell you is that the last entry in the ghost-hand isn't yours. There's a tenth note, undated, unfinished. It reads: *If the ninth also refuses, the door will—*" I stopped.

"Will what?"

"It stops there. Ink trail-off. The writer was interrupted, or died, or ran out of certainty." I closed the book gently, the way you close something that has started breathing. "Someone a century ago believed there could be a ninth refusal, Astrid. Believed it hard enough to hide the evidence of eight. And they never learned how the story ends."

Below us, far down the valley on the south road, dust moved where no cart had any business being—riders, six or eight of them, moving at the steady unhurried pace of soldiers who are searching rather than traveling. Astrid saw them a heartbeat after I did. She didn't reach for a weapon. She reached for the book, and tucked it inside her coat, against her ribs, where a heartbeat could vouch for it.

"Well," she said, "let's go make sure the tenth note stays unfinished a while longer. And Tam—if those riders have a ledger with my face in it—"

"They won't," I said. "No ledger has your face in it. That's rather the point of you."

"Mm." She was already moving toward the back of the bothy, where the shepherd's track fell away toward the dusk-roads. "Keep telling yourself that. One of us should believe it."

Behind us, the sun cleared the ridge and lit the snow, and ahead of us the road bent down toward the drowned lake, where a door was waiting to learn whether I had spent ten years forging chains for a kingdom—or the key out of one.
