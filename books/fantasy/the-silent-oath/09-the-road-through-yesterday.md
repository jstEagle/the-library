## 9. The Road Through Yesterday

The trouble with being hunted by professionals is that they don't panic. Amateurs chase you until they're tired, then go home and drink about it. Professionals set a pace you can live with—just slow enough to seem survivable—and walk you straight into the net.

We'd been living at Corvane's pace for two days.

"They're behind us again," Tam said, for the eleventh time since dawn. He said it the way other men announce weather. "Three riders on the ridge. Maybe four."

"Then stop counting them. Counting them just means you keep turning around, and turning around slows us down."

"I'm an archivist. Enumeration is my entire personality."

"That explains so much about you."

He shot me a look over his shoulder—the look of a man who had spent three weeks discovering that his scholarly contempt did not, in fact, function as armor in the physical world—but he kept walking. That was the thing about Tam Vessery. Under all the huffing, he moved like a man who understood exactly how much he was worth alive to our enemies, which is to say: like a man who intended to stay worth nothing to anyone but me.

Behind us, somewhere past the tree line, the riders held their distance. Three of them, maybe four. Corvane's men didn't ride like soldiers. Soldiers gallop when they see you and curse when they lose you. These ones drifted, patient as weather, always visible, never closing—a message written in hoofprints: *we know where you are. We will always know where you are.*

I hated it more than I could say. Not the danger—I'd been dangerous-adjacent my whole adult life—but the *being seen*. Three years I'd spent teaching the Thornfells to forget me. I paid tolls in coins nobody traced. I slept in caves that weren't on maps because the maps had been burned by people who owed me favors. I had become, through patience and small betrayals of my own history, a rumor with legs. And now some parchment-pusher in a clean coat had written me back into existence with one forged prophecy, and every eye in the mountains had been given permission to find me again.

"You have that face," Tam observed.

"What face?"

"The one where you're composing insults you'll never deliver because they'd require conversation."

"I was thinking," I said, "about doors."

"Doors."

"A road I've never told anyone about." I looked up at the sky doing its late-afternoon performance, all bruised gold and gathering cloud. "There's a pass ahead called the Shepherd's Regret. On any map you care to buy, it's a dead end. A shelf of rock with a shrine at the top and nowhere to go."

"But."

"But Vel Saren built roads that only exist when they want to. You told me yourself—you've seen them appear at dusk. Waystones warming. Bridges remembering themselves." I shrugged. "I grew up running cargo for people who knew which of those roads still worked. The Shepherd's Regret has one. It's not on any map, because maps are promises made to everyone, and this road keeps itself for very few."

"And where does it lead?"

"Nowhere," I said cheerfully. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the road through the Shepherd's Regret, and I knew it the way you know a scar—by touch, by ache, by the story somebody told you once and never explained:

Vel Saren had been afraid. Not of armies—armies were weather to them, come and gone—but of being *found*. Of the wrong person reading the right name. So their road-wrights built defenses into the land itself, and the finest of these was a joke with teeth. A road that led nowhere. A road that, if you rode it without knowing its secret, would simply refuse to arrive anywhere at all. It would give you back your day. Again and again. Sunrise, noon, dusk, sunrise—until your horses died under you or your mind gave up trying to count.

My mentor, old Wren, who taught me half my trade and most of my cynicism, had walked it twice in her smuggling days. She said the trick was simple and horrible: the road repeats the day, but it doesn't repeat *you*. It remembers what you carry. If you carry grief, it feeds it back to you. If you carry guilt—" she used to tap her own chest here, meaningfully, while I pretended not to understand "—it sets a place for it at every meal.

To leave, she said, you had to want to be somewhere the road couldn't follow. You had to mean it. Not wish it. *Mean* it.

I'd never walked it myself. But I'd carried the knowledge of it for fifteen years like a coin sewn into a hem, waiting for a day worth spending it on.

Watching those patient riders drift along our back trail, I decided today had earned it.

"There's a stream crossing ahead," I told Tam. "We go up the north bank, into the scree. Don't ask questions for the next hour. Can you manage that?"

"I once catalogued eleven thousand tax receipts in silence," Tam said. "Questions are a luxury I abandoned professionally years ago."

* * *

The Shepherd's Regret earned its name honestly. The climb was loose shale and old prayers—little stone cairns left by shepherds who'd lost flocks to the drop, each one a small argument with God. At the top stood the shrine: a ring of standing stones worn smooth as knuckles, a roofless circle open to a sky going the color of a healing bruise.

And beyond it, where every map promised a sheer face of granite, there was a road.

It appeared the way Tam had described the dusk roads appearing—not rising out of the ground but *agreeing* to be there, as if the mountain had been holding the thought of it for a century and finally let itself think it aloud. Pale flagstones. Waystones flanking it at intervals, each carved with the coiled serpent-and-star of Vel Saren, each faintly warm when I laid my palm against it. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm.

Tam made a sound I'd previously only heard from him upon finding an intact first edition in a flooded cellar.

"It's real," he breathed. "Astrid. Astrid, it's *real*, do you understand what—"

"Yes. Very exciting. Walk faster."

Below us, faint on the wind, came the jingle of tack and the sure, unhurried rhythm of hooves finding the same path we'd climbed. They'd seen the road too. Of course they had. Corvane's men saw everything; that was the whole horror of them.

"Will it work on them?" Tam asked as we stepped onto the flagstones. "The repetition? Or does it need—" he gestured vaguely at all of me, "—whatever you are?"

"It needs nothing except ignorance," I said. "You have to not know the trick. And you have to be riding it for a reason the road disagrees with." I glanced back once, down the pale length of it, at three dark riders cresting the shrine's ring. Four. It had been four all day; I'd miscounted on purpose, a habit. "They're hunting me. The road was built to protect someone like me. I suspect it's about to be very disagreeable."

The waystones warmed under my hand as we walked, one after another, like a pulse quickening.

* * *

The sun set.

I knew it would. That was part of the design, presumably—some road-wright's flourish, the curtain falling on act one. What I hadn't fully prepared for was the specific quality of the light coming back.

It rose the way light rises when you've already watched it rise: slightly stale. Familiar in the way a word repeated forty times becomes strange. The same cloud sat in the same quarter of the sky, wearing the same shape. The same hawk crossed overhead on the same line, and when I watched for it, it banked at precisely the moment it had banked before.

"Oh no," Tam said softly. He had stopped walking. He was looking at the waystone beside him, at a bead of dew sitting on its carved serpent, and the dew was sitting in exactly the place it had sat at yesterday's dusk. "Oh, this is—do you feel that? We haven't moved. I *know* we haven't moved. My feet say we walked six miles."

"We walked six miles yesterday," I said. "Today we'll walk them again."

"How long does it last?"

"Until the horses give out," I said, "or the riders do. Whichever comes first. Old Wren said her party lasted four days. The men chasing them lasted five." I smiled, and it wasn't entirely a nice smile. "They went mad on the sixth morning, apparently. Started fighting each other over which direction was forward."

Tam sat down heavily on a waystone. Then he got up immediately, because the stone was warm and he hadn't asked its permission, and archivists are polite to the point of pathology.

"This is impossible," he said. "I want that noted. Formally noted, somewhere, by someone. This is impossible and I am witnessing it."

"Noted," I said. "By me. Which is worse for you, really, because my records are all verbal and I exaggerate."

He laughed—one short, startled bark—and then pressed his hands over his face, and when he took them away he looked older and younger simultaneously, a man watching his entire worldview get politely shown the door.

"The prophecies," he said quietly. "The dusk roads. The waystones warming when you touch them. This road answering to you." He looked at me with an expression I liked considerably less than wonder: pity. "Astrid, I told you it was forged. I staked my professional reputation on it being forged. I'm beginning to worry I staked it on a lie I wanted to believe."

"Eat something," I advised. "Repetition is easier on a full stomach."

* * *

Here is what the road does, and I will write it plainly because Tam certainly wrote it plainly in that journal he thought I didn't know he kept:

The second day was the first day. Exactly. The same hawk. The same cloud. The same stiffness in my left knee announcing itself at the same hour. Even Tam's jokes arrived on schedule—he complained about the quality of trail rations at roughly midday both times, using nearly identical words, and I caught him doing it and said nothing, because saying it might break something fragile.

But the riders felt it.

From the ridgeline behind us—they never closed the gap; the road wouldn't let them—we could see the change. On the first pass through the day, they'd ridden like professionals: spaced, silent, economical. By the third loop, they rode like men arguing with arithmetic. One of them kept stopping to look at the sun. Another had begun talking to his horse in a low continuous mutter, the sound carrying down the valley in fragments: *—same stone, it's the same stone, I marked it, look, I marked it—*

"He marked the waystone," Tam murmured, watching through a spyglass he absolutely should not have owned. "Yesterday. He scratched a mark into the serpent's coil. And today the mark is—" he lowered the glass. His voice had gone thin. "The mark isn't there. The stone healed. Astrid, the stone *healed*."

"Vel Saren didn't build roads," I said. "It built arguments. And the road always wins."

On the fourth loop, I started hearing things.

Not voices, exactly. More like the memory of voices—the way a room holds a conversation after everyone's left, if rooms could be sentimental. Walking the flagstones at dusk, I passed the shrine and heard, beneath the wind, a woman singing a lullaby in Old Sarenic. I didn't know the words. I knew the tune the way you know a tune you heard before you could remember hearing it.

Tam, walking beside me, had gone rigid.

"You hear it too," I said.

"I hear an entire *choir*," he whispered. "Vespers. The evening office of some rite I've read descriptions of and never—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's coming from the stones. From all of them. They're not waystones, Astrid. They're *congregations*. Every one of these markers is a grave-marker wearing a road's uniform."

That night—or the memory of night, the road's courteous imitation of darkness—we camped in the lee of the shrine, and neither of us slept well, and in the morning it was dusk again, and the hawk flew its line, and somewhere behind us a man begged his horse to be real.

* * *

The fifth loop was when it turned on me.

I should have expected it. Wren warned me: *the road remembers what you carry.* I'd filed that away as smuggler poetry, the kind of thing old women say to make their hard lives sound mystical. But walking the same mile for the fifth time, with my body keeping perfect time to a day I'd lived four times, my mind began handing things over.

First, small things. The smell of tallow candles—that was Marrowgate burning, three years ago, the warehouse fire I'd started myself to erase a ledger with my name in it. The taste of copper—that was simpler, that was just fear, fear tastes of copper, everyone knows that. Then larger things, arriving with the punctuality of the hawk: my mother's hands, which I had not pictured clearly in years, suddenly vivid, scarred across both palms in a pattern I now recognized with a sick lurch as the pattern carved on the waystone serpents.

And then, at the hour the lullaby came drifting down from the shrine, the road played me the worst thing it had.

A kitchen. Low ceiling, herb bundles drying, a woman humming the same tune the stones sang. She was packing a bag for me—small things wrapped in cloth, a knife, bread, a charm on a cord—and she was crying without any of the noise of crying, tears just falling steadily while she hummed, because the humming was for me, so I wouldn't be scared, and the crying was hers alone. She knelt. She put the cord around my neck. She said—

She said, and the road gave it to me whole, in her voice, in her accent, in the exact cadence I'd spent fifteen years failing to forget:

*"If anyone ever asks who you are, tell them nothing. Not a false name. Nothing. A lie can be caught. Silence can't. Be forgotten, little bird. Being forgotten is the safest country there is."*

I stopped walking.

The road waited. It was very good at waiting.

"Astrid?" Tam's voice, from somewhere behind me, arriving late, as if from across water. "Astrid, you've stopped. Your eyes are—" Footsteps. His hand on my arm, light, uncertain, a man who had read about grief and never had to carry any. "What did you see?"

"Nothing," I said, which was a lie, and lies can be caught, and I watched him decide not to catch it, which was its own small kindness and therefore suspicious, because Tam was many things but generous wasn't among them unless generosity served Tam.

"We're close," I said instead. "They'll break soon. Horses first, then men. When they break, the road lets us out. There's a descent on the far side, down to the Vail watershed, two days from Saltmere."

"And until then?"

"Until then," I said, "we walk the same day until it wears out."

I didn't tell him about the kitchen. Some cargoes you don't declare.

* * *

On the seventh loop, the horses quit.

We heard it before we saw it: a scream from the rear column, high and equine and done with pretending. One of the riders' mounts simply lay down in the middle of the road, the way animals do when a body has been lied to too long—every instinct screaming that it had already walked this ground, had already drunk at this stream, had already slept this sleep, until the animal concluded it must already be dead and acted accordingly.

The rider dismounted. Stood beside his horse. Did not try to make it rise.

Through Tam's glass we watched the rest of it unfold with the terrible tidiness of a proof completing itself. The muttering man stopped muttering and sat down in the road. The one who'd scratched marks on everything—there were dozens of scratches now, on trees, on stones, on his own forearm, a whole private archive of evidence the road erased nightly—began to laugh. Quietly at first. Then with commitment.

The fourth rider—the tall one, the one who never spoke, who rode always a half-length ahead of the others like a man following instructions only he could read—did none of these things. He dismounted, stood very still for a long moment, and then did something that raised the hair on my arms.

He bowed. Toward us. Toward me, specifically; there was no ambiguity in the angle. A deep, formal, unhurried bow, the bow of a man acknowledging a superior, and then he turned, mounted his comrade's still-living horse, and rode back down the road the way he'd come—out, somehow, though the road had no exit that direction, unless you knew the trick, and the road parted for him the way a crowd parts for a man with the right letters.

"He knows," Tam said hollowly. "He knows the trick. He *chose* to withdraw."

"No," I said slowly, watching the dust of him fade. "He chose to report. There's a difference, and it's going to matter."

Because here was the thing I'd been chewing on since the bow: that man hadn't broken. Seven loops of the same day and he'd stood in it like a man reading a familiar book. Either he'd walked a Vel Saren road before—which no one outside a handful of smugglers and a handful of scholars had—or someone had told him what it was before he entered it.

Someone like a man who studied prophecy books for a living. Someone like a gentle, methodical inquisitor-general who never sent his people anywhere unprepared.

"He knew," I said aloud. "Corvane knew what this road was, and he sent four men into it anyway. Not to catch us. To measure it. To measure *me*." My hands had curled into fists without consulting me. "We weren't the quarry, Tam. We were the experiment."

The road sighed—or I imagined it sighed, a long settling exhalation through the waystones, the warmth fading from them one by one like lamps being shuttered—and ahead of us, for the first time in seven days, the flagstones ran on past the horizon instead of folding back into the shrine. Mist stood at the road's end, and through the mist, far below, glinted the grey thread of the Vail river.

Saltmere. Two days. And somewhere between here and there, a man who planned for roads that repeat time, waiting to learn what his experiment had cost and what it had proven.

Tam was scribbling in his journal, fast, hunched over it like a man sheltering a flame.

"What are you writing?" I asked.

He hesitated. Then, instead of hiding the page, he turned it toward me.

It was a list. Nine entries. Eight of them crossed out, names I almost recognized—names I'd glimpsed in stolen documents, in the margins of the prophecy book, children's names struck from parish rolls. And at the bottom, uncrossed, freshly inked, the ninth entry.

He hadn't written my name.

He'd written: *the one who walks the repeating road — confirmed.*

"Tam," I said, "who else knows that list exists?"

He looked up at me, and for once the archivist had no footnote, no qualification, no clever hedge. Just a man watching his own handwriting turn into a death warrant.

"Before yesterday," he said, "no one. But Astrid—the fourth rider. When he bowed." He swallowed. "I think he was looking past you. At me. I think he recognized me."

The mist at the road's end stirred, though there was no wind, and somewhere far off—too far to be possible, close enough to be meant—a church bell rang once, in a city we hadn't reached yet, in a day that hadn't started.

"Then we'd better get to Saltmere first," I said, and stepped off the road, into tomorrow.
