## 13. The Burning of Marrowgate

The council was still arguing about whether to send a rider to the mountain garrisons when the first smoke went up over the Salt Quarter.

I want that recorded somewhere, in Tam's honest history if he ever writes it: they were arguing about *quorum*. A wool merchant named Aldous Vey had objected that emergency powers required a two-thirds vote, and a two-thirds vote required all nine delegates present, and the delegate from Ferrow's Landing was at his daughter's wedding. So the free city of Marrowgate—the last great ungoverned thing between the usurper and the sea—spent its final afternoon of freedom debating attendance policy.

Tam and I had come back against my better judgment. I'd wanted to keep moving, put the lake behind us, find the door before Corvane did. But Tam said the prophecy gave us until solstice, and solstice was eleven days off, and Marrowgate was where the smuggler-guilds kept their maps, their caches, their people. "You can't open a door," he'd said, "if the men who know the roads are dead."

He wasn't wrong. He just wasn't right fast enough.

We were in the guildhall cellar when the bells started—wrong bells, the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one—and every smuggler in the room went still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"That's not a drill," said Orla Fen, guild-mistress of the River Roads, who had once thrown a customs officer into her own canal for smiling at her wrong. She was already moving. "That's the north road. That's *inside* the north road."

"They can't be," said someone. "The passes—"

"The passes I bought us were a season," Orla snapped. "Someone sold them early. Or someone walked around them. It doesn't matter now." She turned and looked at me, and what I saw in her face wasn't blame. It was worse. It was *request*. "Ash. The tunnels under the Salt Quarter. You cut half of them yourself."

I had. Three years of moving contraband teaches you a city's bones better than any architect learns them. The Salt Quarter sat on old Vel Saren foundations—of course it did, everything here did, the free cities were built on the drowned kingdom like barnacles on a hull—and beneath the warehouses ran drainage galleries and collapsed cellars and one long smuggled artery we called the Undervein, running from the tannery district clear to the river gate.

"How many people can it hold?" Tam asked.

"It's not about holding," I said. "It's about how many we can move before the fires reach the grates."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the end of a city: it is mostly noise, and then it is mostly light, and then it is mostly quiet, and the quiet is the part that stays with you.

The noise came first. Screaming, yes, but also the ordinary sounds refusing to stop—a dog barking, a cart wheel squeaking, some madman in the Fishmarket still calling prices as though the world were functioning—as if the city itself couldn't believe what was happening and kept reciting its own daily routine like a prayer. Then the light: the usurper's army fired the granaries first, which told me everything about their orders. Not a raid. Not a punishment. An erasure. You don't burn food you intend to occupy with. You burn food when the plan is that no one comes back.

Corvane Halde's work, or done in his style. Methodical gentleness, Orla called it once, and she'd been right: the heralds rode ahead of the flames announcing that any civilian who surrendered peacefully would be fed and registered. Fed and registered. As if starvation and paperwork were mercy. Some people believed it. I watched a family walk out of their bakery with their hands up, walking toward the soldiers the way you'd walk toward a doctor with bad news, hoping the diagnosis could be argued with.

I couldn't save everyone. Say that plainly, because I've spent three years learning to say things plainly: I could not save everyone. There were forty thousand people in Marrowgate and there were perhaps sixty of us who knew the tunnels, and the arithmetic of that is not arithmetic at all. It is a knife you carry in your chest for the rest of your life, and I am carrying it now, and this chapter will not resolve it because nothing resolves it.

But the Undervein could take hundreds. Maybe a thousand, if we moved fast and didn't stop to argue.

Orla organized the warehouse crews. Tam—I don't know what I expected of Tam, the soft-handed archivist, the man who flinched at loud voices—but he planted himself at the tunnel mouth with an ink brush and a scrap of vellum, writing names. "If we're going to pull people out of a burning city," he said, "someone should write down who came out." It was the most useful act of scholarship I have ever witnessed, and I include the deciphering of nine dead heirs in that judgment.

I went into the dark.

* * *

The tunnels in fire-time are a different animal than tunnels in smuggling-time. Smuggling is patient. You wait for the patrol to pass, you count your breaths, you let the cargo cool. But when the city above you is burning, the tunnels fill with smoke through every grate and drain, and the air goes bad from the river-end first, and you learn very quickly that the difference between a route and a trap is about twenty minutes.

I ran the Undervein from the tannery end, kicking open hatches I'd sealed years ago, shouting down into cellars. *Undervein! Follow the rope! Hands on the rope, eyes down, breathe shallow!* We'd strung guide-ropes through the whole system back when the guilds voted to prepare—thank the saints for the smugglers' stubbornness, thank Orla for outvoting the wool merchants of the world—and those ropes were the only reason anyone found the way at all, coughing, blind, children strapped to backs, an old man carrying a birdcage with the bird still in it, which struck me then, and strikes me now, as exactly the kind of thing humans do that no prophecy ever accounts for.

At the third hatch I found the baker's family. The ones who'd surrendered. No—their neighbors. The ones who'd watched them go. A woman with flour still on her apron holding a baby and a boy of about eight, standing in a cellar that was starting to rain plaster.

"Tunnel's that way," I said. "Rope. Go."

She looked at me the way people look at rescuers, which is to say she didn't look at me at all, she looked past me at the darkness, judging whether the dark was safer than the fire. It was. Barely. They went.

It was at the fifth hatch, down by the old tollhouse quarter near the river gate, that I heard the voice I'd been half-dreading and half-hoping for.

"Ash? Ash Oyelaran?"

Bren. The toll-keeper from the high pass, the one who'd flinched at my coin a lifetime ago—three months ago, actually, but time had gone strange since the safehouse burned. He'd come down to Marrowgate after Corvane's men visited him, after our conversation in the snow where I'd told him to forget my face and he'd promised he couldn't. His brother kept a chandlery on the river. He'd brought his family for the winter festival.

His family. Which meant—

"Sefa," he said, grabbing my sleeve, and his hand was shaking so hard I could hear the tremor in it. "My daughter. She was at the festival grounds with her cousins when the horns sounded. The soldiers are sweeping the waterfront, they're—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They're taking the young ones. Registering them. She's fourteen, Ash. Fourteen and she looks like her mother, and her mother was Vel Saren-born, and I've seen what registration means for girls with old blood in their faces."

The festival grounds. The waterfront. Between me and her: six blocks of burning city and a cordon of inquisitors doing Corvane's gentle, methodical work.

"I'll get her," I said.

"You don't even know—"

"Bren." I took his face in both hands, the way you do with panicking mules and panicking fathers, because the technique transfers. "You carried my secret through a winter and never sold it, not even when they offered you amnesty. So I'm telling you the truth: I have spent three years becoming impossible to catch, and tonight I am going to be impossible to catch *in the direction of your daughter*. Get your people to the rope. Count them out loud. I'll bring her to the river gate grate—you know the one, the loose one, third past the fish stairs."

He nodded. Then he did something I didn't expect. He pressed something into my palm: a toll-token, iron, stamped with the pass-mark. "Roads remember debts," he said. "Bring her home and we're even."

I almost laughed. Almost. There was no room in my chest for laughing; it was full of smoke already.

* * *

Six blocks through a burning city. Let me tell you how it's done, since apparently I'm the expert.

You don't run. Running marks you—soldiers chase runners the way hawks chase anything that moves, on instinct, without orders. You walk. You walk like you belong, which means you walk like you're terrified in the ordinary way, like everyone else, clutching something worthless—a blanket, a pot, a child's shoe—and you keep your head down and your pace steady and you let the chaos be your cloak. Three years of being forgotten taught me that being forgotten is easiest in a crowd that wants to forget everything.

The fires helped and hindered. They lit the streets well enough to see, which was bad, but they made shadows jump and shift, which was good, and they covered sound completely, which was best of all. I moved block by block, alley to alley, using routes I'd mapped in my head during a hundred boring nights of waiting out patrols. The city I'd learned as a hiding place became, tonight, a weapon.

The festival grounds were ash and abandoned shoes. The waterfront was worse. The inquisitors had formed the crowd into lines—orderly, documented, each person's name taken down by a clerk with neat handwriting—and the lines led onto barges. I stood in the shadow of a burning warehouse and watched a girl of maybe ten ask a soldier when she'd see her mother again, and watch the soldier consult his ledger as if the answer might be written there, and watch his face do the small terrible thing that faces do when they realize they're the answer.

Sefa wasn't in the lines. Of course she wasn't. Fourteen-year-old girls with any sense and most without don't stand in lines when soldiers arrive. She'd run. The question was where.

I thought like a fourteen-year-old. Where do you hide in a burning waterfront when you can't go home and can't surrender? The salt cellars. The old Vel Saren vaults under the customs house—every local kid knew them, knew the gaps in the foundations, knew the places the grown-ups had forgotten. I'd hidden in them myself at fifteen, running from the first people who ever tried to erase me.

The customs house hadn't burned yet. Its cellars were dark and cold and smelled of brine and old empire, and in the second vault, behind a false wall I'd have missed myself if I hadn't built half these hiding places' grandparents, I found her: curled small, knees to chest, in the dark, humming to keep the fear company.

"Sefa?" I said softly, before I stepped in, because surprising a frightened girl in a black hole is how you get bitten.

She uncurled enough to look at me. "Are you real?"

"As real as anything tonight."

"Mama says the soldiers take girls like me and make them sign things. Signatures are chains. She said never sign anything." Her mother had died two winters back; I knew that from Bren, from the long conversations toll-keepers and smugglers have across tollbooths while wagons are checked. The girl was quoting a dead woman's law to herself in the dark, and holding to it, and I have rarely been so proud of a stranger.

"No signatures," I agreed. "No chains. Come with me and we'll break every chain we meet. But you have to be brave for six blocks, and brave looks like boring. Can you do boring?"

"I live with Papa. Boring is my whole life."

Then come, I said, and she came.

* * *

We nearly didn't make the river gate. Two blocks from the grate, a patrol swept the cross-street ahead of us—not soldiers, inquisitors, gray cloaks and white staves, Corvane's own—and I pulled Sefa into a doorway that was mostly fire and partly shelter, and we stood in the heat pressing our backs to stone while they passed, close enough that I could smell the lamp-oil on their torches and hear one of them complaining about his boots.

And here is the thing I need to write down, because it matters, because it's the hinge this whole book turns on:

One of them looked at me. Straight at me, in the firelight, from four feet away. And his eyes slid off. Not quickly, not suspiciously—*smoothly*, the way water slides off glass. He saw a woman in a doorway and his mind declined to hold the image. Three years of erasure, three years of paying clerks and bribing record-keepers and teaching my own face to go unfelt, and it worked. It worked perfectly, at the exact moment when everything else was failing, and I stood in a burning doorway holding a trembling child and felt—nothing. No triumph. Just the cold mechanical fact of it, like watching a lock turn. My invisibility saved us, and it felt like being already dead, like the thing they were trying to make of me had gotten there first and was doing their work for them.

Sefa felt me flinch. Kids always feel it. She squeezed my hand and didn't ask, and I loved her a little for that, and I hated the world a lot more.

The grate was where I'd said. Bren was there, or rather Bren's rope was, and beyond it the Undervein breathed its bad slow air full of refugees. I pushed Sefa through and heard her father's voice crack on the other side saying her name, saying it over and over like a man counting himself back into existence.

I went back for more. Of course I went back. That's not heroism; that's just the shape the night had. I made four more runs before the smoke beat me—before the air in the Undervein's eastern arm went truly foul and Orla's people sealed the grates behind the last string of refugees, sealing it with people still knocking on the far side, and I stood at the grate with my palms flat against the bars while fists hammered the other side, and Orla put her hand on my shoulder and said, "Ash," just my name, just that, because there was nothing else in any language.

The hammering stopped sometime before dawn. I counted the knocks. I want you to know that I counted them. Forty-one.

* * *

By morning, Marrowgate was a rumor of itself. The council building had burned with the quorum question unanswered. Aldous Vey the wool merchant was pulled alive from a cellar, reportedly still insisting that the occupation lacked proper legal notice. The free cities' grand experiment in talking instead of preparing lasted exactly as long as it took an army to walk faster than a committee.

We gathered in the tannery yard—survivors, guild-folk, the tunnel-people, maybe six hundred souls smelling of smoke and river. Orla stood on a salt barrel and told them the truth, because that was the currency left: "Marrowgate has fallen. The other free cities will bargain or burn, each in its turn. The mountains are closed. There is no law coming to save us, because the law is what just burned down."

A silence followed, the big kind, the kind with weight.

Then a voice from the back—Bren's voice, rough with smoke and grief and something harder underneath: "Then what's left?"

And I stood up. I hadn't planned to. My legs decided before I did, which is how you know a thing is true.

"What's left is what was always left," I said. "People. Six hundred people who know roads the armies don't, who keep records no clerk can seize, who owe each other favors instead of oaths. The council wouldn't believe the war was coming, so the war came. Fine. Then we stop asking permission from institutions. The guilds held a court last month to decide whether to give me up—they'll tell you about it, some of them wanted to. No writs. No oaths. Just truth, weighed by people with nothing left to lose. That's what justice looks like when the law dies. It looks like this yard."

Tam, beside me, went pale, and I understood why: I'd just announced, in front of six hundred witnesses, the tribunal that would decide my fate. If the guilds voted to trade me to Corvane for peace—if such a thing could even be bought—it would happen here, fairly, by the rules I'd just praised.

Orla caught my eye and smiled like a knife being drawn slowly. "The smuggler-guilds accept the motion," she said. "Court convenes tomorrow, sundown, the salt yard. All claims heard. Including the claim," she added, looking directly at me, "that the woman called Ash Oyelaran is worth more to the living than the peace she might buy."

I looked out at the six hundred. At Sefa, asleep against her father's chest. At the old man with the empty birdcage. At Tam, already reaching for fresh vellum, because whatever happened tomorrow, someone was going to write it down honestly, and that job had finally found its man.

Forty-one knocks. Tomorrow they would weigh my life against a city's survival, and I would have to argue my own case with the whole truth, prophecy included, in front of the only court left in the world that couldn't be bribed with a crown.

I'd faced inquisitors, loops of repeating days, doors under lakes. None of it scared me like a fair trial.
