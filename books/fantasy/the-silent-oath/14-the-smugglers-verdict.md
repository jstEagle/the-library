## 14. The Smugglers' Verdict

The smuggler's court convened in the old tannery beneath Marrowgate's fish market, which was fitting, because everything about it smelled like a lie that had been left out in the sun too long to be fixed and might as well be useful.

No writs hung on the walls—smugglers don't recognize writs. No oaths were sworn—oaths, we'd all learned lately, are just chains with better tailoring. Instead there were thirteen chairs arranged in a crescent, one for each guild that had survived the burning of Marrowgate's upper districts: salt-runners, night-carters, the Ferryman's Kin, the Widow's Ledger, and so on down through a list of names that sounded less like businesses and more like apologies.

I stood in the center of the crescent on a floor of old oak stained dark by a century of honest work and dishonest cargo, and I waited for someone to tell me the rules.

Nobody did. That *was* the rules.

"Astrid Oyelaran." The speaker was Marda Quill of the Salt-Runners, a woman built like a doorstop and about as easy to move once she'd decided something. She held the prophecy book—the actual book, Tam's copy of it, because the original was ash with the safehouse—and she read from it like a woman reading a bill of lading, checking for discrepancies. "'Born under Thornfell, named by her mother, marked for the solstice. The door opens on her blood.' That's you?"

"That's what it says."

"Is it true?"

And there it was. The whole trap of a smuggler's court in one question. In a royal court I could have lied; in an inquisitorial court I'd have been flayed before I finished lying; in a council chamber I could have hired someone to lie beautifully on my behalf. But thirteen guildmasters who'd spent their lives detecting a false bottom in a crate were not going to be fooled by anything I said, only by anything I *was*.

"It's true," I said. "Mostly. The book leaves things out."

Marda grunted, which from her was a standing ovation. "Corvane Halde has offered terms. Full amnesty for Marrowgate. Rebuilding funds. The usurper's own seal on it. All he wants is you." She looked up. "Thirteen guilds. Eleven have lost people in the last month. One lost a warehouse, which for the Ferriers counts double. We're going to vote whether to hand you over."

"I know. I was at the meeting where your messenger told me. It was very well organized."

A few people laughed. Not many. Sefa's father wasn't among them—he sat in the gallery with his daughter asleep against his chest again, watching me with the particular expression of a man who owed me his child's life and was praying he wouldn't have to pay for it with his principles.

"You can argue your case," Marda said. "Whole truth, they're calling it. Start whenever you like. End when we stop listening."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about telling the whole truth: it takes forever, and halfway through you start to hate yourself for the parts you're tempted to trim.

I started with the coin. The toll at the high pass, three years ago, when a toll-keeper named Brannic had flinched at the Vel Saren seal as if the metal had bitten him. I laid the coin itself on the floor between us—I'd carried it since, because I'm sentimental about the moments my life stops making sense—and let them pass it around.

Then the ledger of small names. I explained how I'd erased myself: bribes structured to look like clerical errors, witnesses moved to towns with similar-sounding names, a funeral registered in my honor in a village I'd never visited, complete with a gravestone I'd carved myself because if you want a thing done convincingly, do it tired and grieving. Laughter at that one, thin but real. Smugglers respect good paperwork. It's the only kind of magic they trust.

Then Tam came forward, because I'd asked him to, and because a disgraced archivist testifying voluntarily carries a weight no defendant can fake. He gave them the forgery theory first—the planted book, the baited prophecy, eight dead heirs before me, all erased—and then, visibly hating every word, gave them the counter-evidence. Waystones warming under my hands. Bridges reassembling themselves at dusk like patient corpses. A road that swallowed Corvane's riders into a single repeating Tuesday until their horses lay down and refused to participate further.

"The evidence," Tam concluded, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant, "supports the prophecy being genuine. I was wrong. I would very much like to still be right, but I am not, and an honest archive does not get to choose its contents."

He sat down. Thirteen faces turned back to me.

"So that's the shape of it," I said. "A century-old book says I die on the solstice. A general who believes it wants me delivered alive—which should worry you more than if he wanted me dead, because men kill for lots of reasons, but they only keep things alive when the thing is *useful*. And a kingdom under a lake wants me to open its door, take its crown, and forget why I ever wanted to be free." I spread my hands. "That's the whole truth. Now let me tell you why it doesn't matter."

Marda raised an eyebrow. Bold strategy, telling a court the evidence was irrelevant. But I'd watched the free cities' council fail, and I knew exactly how this room differed from that one, and I intended to use every inch of the difference.

"Corvane offered you amnesty," I said. "Read the terms again. Read them *closely*, the way you'd read a contract from a merchant you didn't trust, because that's what he is. Amnesty for Marrowgate. What about Thornfell? What about the passes? What about every village that sheltered a smuggler, fed a fugitive, looked the other way?" I let that sit. "Amnesties are ledgers too. And Corvane Halde keeps excellent ones. He'll write down every name in this room, every debt forgiven, and the day the usurper needs a favor—a levy, a conscription, a few dozen 'temporary' garrisons—you'll find the amnesty was a loan, not a gift, and the interest is due."

"That's speculation," said one of the Ferriers, a narrow man whose grief for his warehouse had apparently calcified into policy.

"So is the amnesty," I said. "At least mine comes with a track record. Ask the mountain villages what Corvane's kindness looks like. He brought them grain. He healed their sick. He learned their children's names. And then he took those names to his ledger, and the villages that helped him got bread, and the villages that didn't got visits. Gentleness, applied systematically, is just a softer word for siege."

Silence. Somewhere above us, the fish market was opening for the day, and the ordinary sounds of ordinary commerce filtered down—haggling, cursing, the slap of fresh catch on wet stone. A world that didn't know it was on trial too.

"Here's what I actually know," I went on, "and I want you to weigh it against eleven votes of grief, because grief deserves its vote, but it shouldn't get the whole ballot. I know that Vel Saren bound itself with oath-magic to escape a conquest, and it has been dying of that choice for two hundred years. I know the door opens on my blood, and that inside it is the accumulated memory of every king who chose safety over freedom, waiting for someone to inherit the mistake. And I know—" and here I had to stop, because the next part was the true part, the expensive part, and expensive truths need a breath before you spend them—

"I know that I can break it. Not take it. Break it. Unbind the oath-magic, free the people Vel Saren chained to its dead kings, and end the thing Corvane is hunting before he can put it on like a weapon." I looked around the crescent. "But the backlash erases my name. Every record. Every ledger. The prophecy itself goes blank. Your amnesty wouldn't have anyone to deliver even if you voted for it, because the woman you'd be selling won't exist anywhere except standing right here, arguing with you, and after the solstice, not even here."

"Why tell us that?" Marda said quietly. "You could have argued self-interest. You could have argued the amnesty's a trap and stopped there. Why hand us the one fact that makes surrendering you *pointless*?"

"Because a court that can't be bribed with a crown also can't be managed with omissions," I said. "That's the whole point of you. You're the only institution left in these mountains that runs on truth instead of authority. The council dithered. The law burned. The kings under the lake forgot how to be human. Somebody has to keep justice alive the way smugglers keep anything alive—illegally, personally, and one delivery at a time." I shrugged. "So. Whole truth. Delivered. Check the manifest."

* * *

They deliberated for two hours, which everyone agreed was fast for a body containing thirteen guildmasters, and slow for a room full of people deciding whether to feed a woman to a wolf to save the flock.

I spent it in the yard behind the tannery, sitting on a salt barrel with Tam, watching gulls conduct their own ruthless tribunal over the fish refuse.

"You lied," Tam said.

"I told the whole truth."

"You told the whole truth *arranged like a lie*. You saved the name-erasure for last, after they'd already heard the case against surrender. Classic advocate's sequencing. I've read enough forged court transcripts to know the technique." He picked at the barrel's edge. "Also, 'break it, not take it'—you said that with enormous confidence for someone who learned it from a dead king's begging two days ago."

"The dead kings have been wrong about everything for two centuries. They were due to be right about something."

Tam smiled despite himself, then sobered. "If they vote you over, I want you to run. Don't argue, don't wait, don't be noble. Run. I'll burn the archives myself so they can't sell your location, and I'll go to the lake alone, and I'll figure out the oath-breaking from the texts, and if it kills me it will at least make an interesting footnote."

"No footnotes. You're writing the honest history, remember. Honest histories don't have martyrs in them unless the martyr was actually useful."

"I'm extremely useful."

"Noted for the record."

The door opened. Sefa's father stepped out—not the guildmaster, just him, the man whose daughter I'd carried through smoke—and crossed the yard and stood in front of me and didn't say anything for a long moment.

"They're calling the vote," he finally said. Then, quieter: "I can't tell you what I hope for. If giving you up saves six hundred people, I should hope for it. My daughter slept through the burning of our street because you kept running. I don't know how a man holds both of those." His jaw worked. "But I wanted you to hear it from somebody. Whatever they decide, somebody in that room owes you more than a verdict."

He went back inside. Tam exhaled slowly.

"Well," he said. "That was either the most comforting or most horrifying speech I've ever witnessed."

"Yes," I said, and followed him in.

* * *

Thirteen stones. That was the method—one stone per guildmaster, white for surrender, black for release, dropped into a tanner's vat of clear water where everyone could watch each one land. No writs. No oaths. Just rocks and water and whatever justice looks like when you strip away everything except people paying attention.

Marda dropped first. White.

My stomach did something professional and detached, like a sailor watching a storm come over the horizon and deciding to have opinions about it later.

Second stone: white. Third: black—the Widow's Ledger, an old woman who met my eyes as she dropped it and gave me nothing, no apology, no absolution. Fourth: white. Fifth: white. Six hundred lives against one, arithmetic doing what arithmetic always does in frightened rooms.

Sixth stone: black. Seventh: black. Eighth: white. Nine, ten—white, black. Eleven: white. Eight whites, four blacks, and the twelfth guildmaster stood holding his stone, and I realized I'd stopped breathing somewhere around stone nine, and that Sefa's father was watching me instead of the vat, and that Tam had his eyes closed like a man praying to a god he'd spent thirty years professionally disbelieving in.

Twelve: black.

Twelve stones. Six whites, six blacks. Deadlocked, and every head turned to the thirteenth chair, where the Ferryman's narrow man sat with his stone unthrown, his warehouse-grief on his face like a mask that had slipped and shown the man underneath: tired, scared, and honest enough to know that fear makes terrible bookkeeping.

"I lost a building," he said, to no one and everyone. "Buildings get rebuilt. I've rebuilt four." He looked at me. "You said the amnesty was a loan. I read the terms again, like you said, the way I'd read a bad contract. There's a clause. Subsection nine. 'Recipients shall render assistance as required to maintain regional order.'" His mouth twisted. "*As required.* I've been a ferryman forty years. Nobody writes 'as required' into a gift."

The stone dropped. Black.

Seven to six. The water settled, and Marda Quill looked around at the result the way a captain looks at weather—no joy in it, just the facts of the sky—and said the words formally, for the record Tam was already scratching onto vellum:

"The guilds will not surrender Astrid Oyelaran. The guilds declare her passage free on every road, river, tunnel, and rooftop we control. The guilds warn all members: any hand raised to deliver her answers to us, not to the usurper." She paused. "And the guilds note, for the honest history somebody's apparently writing, that justice survived today in a tannery, with rocks, and it worked better than it did in any marble hall I've ever seen."

The gallery made a sound—not a cheer, nothing so clean. More like the noise a city makes when it decides, collectively and all at once, not to die quietly.

I stood there while it washed over me, seven-to-six, one stone of margin, the whole weight of my continued existence resting on subsection nine and a ferryman's forty years of reading contracts. I thought about the free cities' council, with its marble and its procedure and its beautiful, useless majority. I thought about a drowned kingdom that had voted, once, for safety, and had been dying of the result ever since.

"Oyelaran." Marda's voice cut through. "Don't stand there looking grateful, it's unsettling. Solstice is tomorrow. Corvane's vanguard hit the lower pass this morning—our runners confirm it. You've got maybe a day, less if the weather turns." She jerked her chin toward the door. "Whatever you're going to do at that lake, the guilds are going to buy you the time to do it. Starting now. Go."

I went. Tam fell into step beside me, vellum under his arm, ink on his fingers, wearing the expression of a man who had just watched ordinary people accomplish the impossible and fully intended to hold them accountable for repeating the performance.

Behind us, the smugglers of Marrowgate began, without ceremony and without orders, to prepare for war.

Above us, through the tannery vents, snow was starting to fall on the solstice eve—earlier than any winter in living memory, as if the year itself had read the prophecy and decided to keep the appointment.
