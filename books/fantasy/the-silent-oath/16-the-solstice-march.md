## 16. The Solstice March

The winter solstice came down off the peaks like a debt collector.

I'd spent three years learning to read the mountains the way other people read faces—the way a snowfield lies about its depth, the way a pass holds its breath before a storm—and what they told me that morning was that we were out of time. The wind had turned south, warm and wrong for Thornfell in deepwinter, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and horses from the low country. Armies smell like that. Like every hearth between here and the sea, burned at once.

"They're through the Serpent's Tooth," Sefa said. She was the guild's best scout and the worst liar I'd ever met, which is why they made her one—she couldn't soften bad news even to save her life. "Vanguard, maybe four hundred riders. Corvane's banner. They'll reach the lake by tomorrow's dusk."

"Solstice eve," Tam said quietly, from his corner of the waystation where he'd been annotating the prophecy book until the margins looked like a spider had died there. "As written."

"Thank you, archivist. Your contributions to morale are noted and treasured."

He didn't look up. "You should sleep."

"I'll sleep when the door's open or I'm dead. Whichever comes with better weather."

Sefa almost smiled. Almost. She'd lost two cousins when Marrowgate burned, and since then she'd been spending herself like coin she didn't expect to get change for. The whole guild was like that now. Forty smugglers camped in a hollow below the high passes, sharpening knives by firelight, and every one of them knew what forty against four hundred meant.

They came anyway. That's the thing about the guilds nobody in the free cities ever understood. We have no writs, no oaths, no crowns. What we have is each other, and a shared conviction that no one gets to own the roads.

"You don't have to do this," I told Sefa, for the third time. It was ritual by then. She gave me the same answer she always did.

"The toll-keeper's daughter says thank you, by the way. She's in Lowmark now, apprenticed to a chandler." She tested her blade's edge with her thumb. "You pulled her out of a burning city through a tunnel that flooded behind you. You think any of us are walking away from this?"

"I think four hundred riders are walking toward this, and you're standing in front of them."

"That's the general idea."

* * *

Tam Vessery kept a journal the way penitents keep prayers: constantly, compulsively, as though the writing itself might eventually balance some ancient account. He wrote while Astrid argued logistics with the smugglers. He wrote while the fires were banked. He wrote now, by the last guttering candle, because if he stopped writing he would have to admit how afraid he was.

*The evidence is conclusive,* he wrote. *The prophecy is genuine, which means my life's work is wrong, which means everything I built my disgrace upon was true and everything I called forgery was real, and I find I would rather be wrong than right about this, and I do not know what that says about me.*

He put down the pen. Picked it up again.

*Correction: I know exactly what it says about me.*

Across the hollow, Astrid Oyelaran stood over a map drawn in charcoal on a stretched hide, moving pebbles with the confidence of someone who had personally bribed, robbed, or befriended every mile of these mountains. Her hands were steady. He had watched them shake once—only once, the night she'd listened to the memory-charm, her mother's voice describing a door out of a country that no longer exists—and he understood that the steadiness was not calm. It was discipline laid over terror like mortar over brick.

She caught him looking. "You're writing about me again."

"I'm writing about the campaign."

"You're writing about me. Your left ear goes red when you lie."

"My left ear—" He touched it reflexively, and her grin was quick and crooked and gone, and he thought, not for the first time, that history would remember this woman however he chose to write her, and he had never in his life held so much power over anything, and it terrified him more than Corvane Halde did.

"There's something I should tell you," he said instead, because dawn was coming and cowards confess at night but he'd always been a slow learner. "If tomorrow goes badly. If you reach the door and I don't—"

"Don't."

"—the archive under Marrowgate has a false bottom in the cartography room. Third shelf, the maps of Vel Saren's southern coast. Everything I took from the usurper's scribes. Proof of the forgeries, proof of the nine heirs, proof of—"

"Tam." She crossed the hollow and closed the journal over his ink-stained fingers. "You're going to be there when I open it. Someone has to check my citations."

He laughed. It came out broken, but it was a laugh. In twenty years of royal service he could not recall a kinder order than *check my citations*, and he resolved, then and there, to survive long enough to obey it.

* * *

Corvane Halde rode at the head of his vanguard the way a surgeon walks into an operating theater: unhurried, gloved, certain of the outcome and attentive to the details.

He had stopped at dawn to let the horses drink from a frozen stream, and while they drank he had reviewed the ledger. Not the army's ledger—the other one. The one no one else had ever seen, written in his own careful hand across thirty years: names, dates, fulfillments. Every prophecy concerning himself, annotated. *Instrument of the Usurper, page 214. The mountain road will open before him, page 219. On the solstice eve the heir will be within his reach, page 231.* It had all come true, exactly as written, and he had long ago stopped asking whether that was because the prophecies were true or because he had made them true by obeying them. The question had teeth either way.

"Report," he said.

Captain Dreth reined up beside him, breath steaming. "Scouts found the pass blocked. Rockfall at the narrows—deliberate. And there are men on the ridgelines. Smuggler banners."

"How many?"

"Perhaps fifty."

Fifty. Corvane consulted the ledger in his mind. Page 228: *The guilds will bar the road, and their blood will buy the heir her hour.* So it was all proceeding. All according to words set down a century before he was born, by people who had never met him, who had decided what he would do and whom he would kill and how it would feel—he suspected it would feel like nothing; it usually did—and he had simply... agreed. For thirty years. With excellent posture.

"Take the pass," he said. "But offer terms first. Amnesty for any who stand down. Full pardon."

Dreth hesitated. "They're outlaws, General."

"They're people who have chosen to die on a mountainside for a woman most of them barely know. That deserves an honest offer, Captain. Kindness costs me nothing, and it unsettles the enemy far more than arrows do." He smiled the smile his interrogators called the lantern-light, the one that made hardened toll-keepers weep with relief and confess things they hadn't done. "Besides. If even one of them accepts, the others will fight worse afterward, wondering why they didn't."

*Page 229,* he thought, as the trumpets sounded. *One will accept. It will not be enough.*

He wondered, sometimes—not often, only at dawn and dusk and other liminal hours—what page 240 said. Whether the ledger ended with him standing in some warm room full of honors, or whether the hand that wrote him had simply stopped mid-sentence, leaving him a man forever approaching a door that would never open.

He put the thought away, as he always did, in the locked drawer where he kept everything sharp.

* * *

They held the narrows for six hours.

I wasn't there—I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in whatever history Tam manages to write. I was already running for the lake when the fighting started, and everything I know about those six hours came to me afterward in pieces, in Sefa's flat voice, in the way the survivors wouldn't talk about it in front of the ones who hadn't.

But I know this much. They rolled the first rockfall onto the vanguard's lead riders, and when the riders climbed back up on foot, the smugglers fought them hand to hand in a defile so narrow that numbers meant nothing, which was the entire plan and also the entire hope. Fifty against four hundred only works if the four hundred have to arrive four at a time.

And I know that when Corvane offered amnesty—a real one, sealed and shouted up the ridge, the methodical gentleness of the man deployed like a weapon—one of ours took it. A boy named Wren, nineteen years old, who'd joined the guild eight months ago because the alternative was starving. He climbed down with his hands up and Corvane kept his word to the letter, and the rest of the line fought worse after that, just like the ledger promised, wondering, all day, why they hadn't.

I know Sefa held the center of the line until the third assault broke over her, and I know she was still holding it when the retreat signal came, because she sent the retreat signal herself and stayed to make sure it was heard.

And I know that at dusk, with the pass finally cleared, Corvane's riders came down onto the lake road at a canter, six hours late and furious about it, and found the shore empty except for waystones glowing warm as hearth-bricks along a path that led straight out across the frozen water.

To me.

The mountains remembered whose blood ran in the ninth generation. The least I could do was run fast enough to deserve it.

* * *

Tam had never run so far in his life. He was fifty-three years old, archivists were not built for this, and he informed Astrid of all of it in gasps as they raced across the lake ice with the sound of hooves growing behind them.

"Noted," Astrid said, not slowing. "Complaint filed. Denied."

"The ice—" His boot skidded; her hand shot out and hauled him upright without breaking stride, an act of strength he would examine later and find alarming. "Astrid, the ice is singing."

It was. Under their feet, a low harmonic hum, rising and falling, and Tam realized with a lurch that it wasn't the ice at all—it was the water beneath it, the drowned door answering her approach the way a struck bell answers its twin. The waystones along the shore had lit for her. The road through yesterday had bent its loops around her pursuers. And now the lake itself sang under the boots of a disgraced archivist and an unrecorded smuggler running toward a kingdom that had been waiting a hundred years to die.

Behind them, torchlight spilled onto the southern shore. Horns. The vanguard had arrived.

"They're on the ice," Tam panted. "Astrid, they're—"

"I know." She skidded to a stop at the center of the lake, where the memory-charm had led them, where her family name was carved in stone nine generations deep beneath their feet, and she pulled off her glove and drew her knife.

He saw what she meant to do and his stomach dropped. "You don't know what the oath will take. We researched this—you of all people know what it costs—"

"I know exactly what it costs." She looked at him then, and her eyes in the torchlight were calm in the way of someone standing at the edge of a very tall place on purpose. "It takes everything I remember being. That's the price of admission. That's what Mother refused, and why I grew up a ghost." The knife hovered over her palm. "So I'm changing the terms."

"You can't alter an oath a hundred years old—"

"Watch me." Above the wind, above the horns, above the singing ice, she smiled that crooked smile. "Check my citations later, Tam. Right now—"

The first crossbow bolt hit the ice ten feet away and shattered like glass.

"—right now, hold my coat."

She cut her palm, pressed her blood to the black water showing through the ice, and spoke the opening words of a kingdom that no longer existed—

—and changed them.

* * *

The ice went white.

Not cracked—lit. Light bloomed outward from her hand in rings, and the humming became a chord, and Tam Vessery, royal archivist, keeper of the largest collection of forbidden documents in the free cities, fell to his knees on a frozen lake and watched the impossible happen anyway.

The water opened. Not violently—politely, the way a library door opens, folding aside to reveal stairs descending into dry golden air where a lake should have been. Stone carved with nine generations of names. Warm light rising from a depth no map had ever charted.

And Astrid Oyelaran, bleeding, laughing, crying, stood at the top of those stairs with her ruined palm raised like a torch.

"It opened," she said, to Tam, to the lake, to the riders frozen at the lake's edge, to no one. "It opened anyway. I told it to remember instead of forget, and it—" She turned to him, and the light was in her face, and she was the most alive person Tam had ever seen. "Come on. Someone has to check my citations."

Below them, in the dark of the descending stair, something old stirred. Something that had been waiting a hundred years to make an offer.

Something with a crown.

Hoofbeats thundered onto the ice behind them. There was no time left, and there never had been, and Astrid took the first step down.
