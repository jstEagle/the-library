## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The salt taught Freya patience before anything else did.

It had its own schedule, the salt. It rose in the pans overnight whether you watched it or not, silvering the shallow water like frost that had lost its way, and it could not be hurried—not by wishing, not by cursing, and certainly not by a woman with a rake standing hip-deep in a brine pond at dawn, trying to will the harvest along. Nine years in the Salt Valley had taught her this: you scraped when the salt was ready, and not one moment sooner, and if you tried to rush it you got bitter crystals full of mud and regret.

"Patience," she told the pan, because talking to it was cheaper than talking to herself, and she'd long since stopped being embarrassed about either. "You're worse than my mother ever was."

Her mother would have laughed at that. Her mother was four hundred miles and nine years away, which was roughly the same thing as dead, as far as letters went. The valley sat at the bottom of the world like a coin dropped into a beggar's cup—everyone knew it was there, no one wanted to reach in after it.

That was the point.

Freya dragged her rake through the crystallizing pan in slow, even strokes, and the morning sun caught the ridges of salt and threw them back at her in a thousand small glares. Behind her, the terraced pans stepped down the valley floor in pale squares, forty-two of them, each one numbered on the wooden stakes in her own careful hand. Beyond them, the village of Brackish Hollow huddled under its chimney smoke—a dozen houses, a meeting hall with a crooked roof, a shrine to no particular god that everyone visited anyway out of politeness.

And beyond all of that, rising on every side like the cupped hands of something enormous and sleeping, the valley walls. Grey stone streaked white with mineral bleed, so tall that from the floor of the valley you could only see sky directly overhead, a ribbon of blue that changed with the hours and never with the weather. Storms went around the Salt Valley the way water goes around a stone in a stream. She had watched lightning split the ridgeline a hand's width from the boundary and felt nothing—not a whisper of pressure, not the prickle at the base of her spine that used to precede every storm for fifty miles around.

Nothing. For nine years, blessedly, achingly nothing.

She had come here at nineteen with a false name, a false history, and a talent she'd sworn into silence, and the valley had received all three lies without comment. It was the only place in the realm where magic simply *wasn't*—where a storm-caller couldn't call, where a fire-toucher's palms stayed cool, where gifts lay dormant as stones at the bottom of a well. People came here to forget what they were. Some of them were running from debts. Some were running from grief. And some—the quiet ones, the ones who flinched at raised voices and kept their sleeves pulled down over their wrists—were running from exactly what Freya was running from.

Nobody asked. That was the covenant of the valley, unwritten but iron-clad: you arrived, you took up a trade, you let people be who they said they were. Old Wenna sold bread and had once, rumor said, been a lady-in-waiting; now she was just a baker with strong opinions about rye. Tam Voss mended roofs and fished the brine ponds and had a laugh that could be heard three terraces away, and if there had ever been anything else in him, it had never woken, and he was grateful for it daily.

Freya scraped salt. Freya carried salt. Freya sold salt to the caravans that came down the pass twice a season, traded for cloth and lamp oil and the small luxuries that made exile livable. And every morning she woke before dawn out of old habit, listened hard to the silence where a storm should have been singing in her blood, and found nothing, and called that peace.

Most mornings, anyway.

This morning she was halfway down terrace thirty-one when she heard the goats.

Not the goats making noise—that was constant, a background bleating as reliable as birdsong—but the goats going suddenly, completely silent. All of them at once, up on the eastern slope, the way animals go quiet when a predator is near or when the ground remembers an earthquake.

Freya straightened, rake in hand, and looked east.

The morning light came over the ridge in a long gold sheet, and for a heartbeat everything was ordinary—scrub grass, grey rock, the goat track winding up toward the high pasture where Tam's daughter Pell kept the herd. Then the light shifted, and she saw them.

Horses. Four of them, maybe five, moving along the ridgeline in single file, dark against the bright sky. They moved wrong for traders—too disciplined, too spaced, like beads on a wire—and they were coming down the switchback trail that no one used anymore because the valley had no use for visitors and visitors had no use for the valley.

Freya stood very still. A drop of brine water slid off her rake handle and fell into the pan with a sound like a held breath finally released.

*They're lost,* she told herself. *Caravan got turned around. Surveyors, maybe. Tax collectors.* Even tax collectors made sense; even tax collectors were survivable. The crown had forgotten the Salt Valley existed for nine years, and forgetting was the whole architecture of her life. She had built it brick by brick: the false name, the flat voice, the woman who complained about weather like everyone else instead of listening to it like a lover.

But the riders didn't look lost. Lost riders wandered. These ones descended the switchbacks with the unhurried certainty of men following a map they trusted, and when they reached the valley floor and turned onto the road toward Brackish Hollow, Freya saw the lead rider's cloak catch the wind.

Grey. Crown-grey, dyed with the expensive ash-madder that only government contracts bought, hemmed in black.

Witch-hunters wore ash-grey. Everyone knew that. Everyone in the valley had always known it, the way you know the shape of the thing under your bed—you just agreed never to look at it directly.

Freya's hands went cold around the rake. Not her magic—her magic was a locked room in a house she'd abandoned, and besides, the valley ate such things for breakfast. This was older and simpler than magic. This was her body remembering what fear was for.

*Nine years,* she thought. *Nine years of salt and silence and nobody's business but my own.*

She set down the rake with exaggerated care, the way you do when you want to throw it, and started walking toward the village. Then she thought better of it, turned back, and did the day's work first—or half of it, mechanically, scraping salt that no longer needed scraping, because if she arrived panicked then whatever happened next would happen to a panicked woman, and panicked women remembered their real names too easily.

By midmorning the riders had reached the outskirts, and the whole village had developed the strained cheerfulness of a household tidying up before inspection. Wenna was suddenly sweeping a path that had been clean since dawn. Tam came down from the roofs with sawdust in his hair and put his arm around his daughter like it was a casual thing, though his knuckles were white.

There were five of them. Five hunters in ash-grey, mounted, with the lean, well-fed look of men who served power rather than land. Their leader was a broad man with a face like a closed ledger—nothing cruel in it, nothing kind either, just entries waiting to be recorded. He dismounted in the square by the shrine, and his boots hit the packed earth with a finality that made the whole valley feel smaller.

"Good morning," he said, to no one and everyone, in the tone of a man reading a proclamation. He produced a scroll sealed with black wax. "By order of the Crown, I am instructed to conduct a census."

"A census," Wenna repeated, in the voice she used for customers who tried to return week-old bread.

"The realm has grown careless of its people." The hunter smiled. It was the worst smile Freya had ever seen, because it was patient. "The Crown wishes to correct that. Names, trades, family. Nothing more." His eyes moved across the gathered villagers, unhurried, cataloguing. "For now."

*For now.* Two words, dropped into the square like stones into still water.

Freya stood at the edge of the crowd with her salt-stained apron and her borrowed name, and watched the ledger-faced man take the village apart with questions. Name. Trade. How long resident. Where from, originally? And the answers came—nervous, rehearsed-for-years answers—and the hunters wrote everything down in a book bound in grey leather, and Freya understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the book was the point. That somewhere in a capital she'd only seen in nightmares, names were being sorted into piles, and somewhere in those piles was a pile for people like her, and the valley—the valley that forgot, the valley that let you forget—had just been remembered.

*Invisibility was always rented,* she thought. *I just never read the lease.*

The interview line moved slowly. Tam went, joked nervously about the price of fish, came back looking like he'd aged a year. Wenna went, lied fluently about her past with the skill of a born baker—kneading falsehoods smooth—and came back greyer. And then the ledger-faced hunter looked up from his book, and his eyes found Freya across the square, and he gestured with two fingers. *You.*

She walked over. Her legs worked. She was almost proud of them.

"Name?"

"Freya Marsh." The lie came out smooth as poured brine. Nine years of practice.

"Trade?"

"Salt-harvester. Terraces thirty-one through forty."

He wrote it down. Then he paused, quill hovering, and looked up at her with those entry-way eyes, and asked the question she'd been dreading since the riders appeared on the ridge:

"And where are you from, Freya Marsh? Originally."

"South," she said. "Coastal towns. Moved around."

"Mm." He studied her the way she'd study a pan with a suspicious discoloration—not alarmed yet, merely interested. "You have the look of someone from further north."

"It's the wind chapping," Freya said. "We get odd winds here."

Something flickered across his face—amusement, maybe, or appetite—and he wrote something else in the book, something short, and closed it. "Thank you for your cooperation, mistress Marsh. The Crown thanks you." He remounted, and the five riders wheeled toward the eastern farms, where the interviews would continue, and Freya stood in the square with the sun warm on her shoulders and cold settling into her bones beneath it.

Tam found her there, staring at nothing. He was a big man, Tam, with a beard full of sawdust and a heart full of daughters—one daughter, Pell, twelve years old and currently pretending very hard to feed chickens within earshot.

"Well," he said. "That was friendly."

"Mm."

"Census." He spat, delicately, away from Wenna's clean path. "Crown never counted us before. Crown didn't know we existed. That was rather the arrangement."

"That was rather the arrangement," Freya agreed.

They stood together in the kind of silence that has too much in it. Down the road, the hunters' voices drifted faintly from farm to farm, pleasant and terrible, questions like soft rain.

"My gran used to say," Tam offered at last, "that the valley keeps what you give it. You give it your secrets, it keeps 'em safe. But—" he scratched under his beard, uncomfortable, "—she also said nothing stays buried forever. Just sleeps. And things that sleep can be woken."

Freya looked at him sharply, but his face was open, guileless, worried only in the ordinary way of a man worried about taxes and soldiers and the price of lamp oil. He didn't know. Of course he didn't know. In nine years, no one in the valley had ever known, and that was the miracle she had lived inside.

"They'll be gone in a few days," she said. "Censuses end. Counters ride home."

"Sure." Tam nodded, agreeing with her the way you agree with a child about monsters. Then he squeezed her shoulder, rough and warm, and went to check on Pell, who was overfeeding the chickens with tremendous dedication.

Freya went back to the pans.

She worked until the light went gold and then copper, scraping salt that was already perfect, because her hands needed doing and her mind needed emptying, and neither was getting what it wanted. The valley held its evening quiet around her—the good quiet, the deep quiet she'd built her life on—and she tried to draw comfort from it the way she had nine years running.

But tonight the quiet had seams in it. Tonight she could see the stitching.

Because here was the truth she'd known and refused to know since the riders crested the ridge: the silence of the Salt Valley wasn't hers. It had never been hers. She hadn't earned this peace; she'd been sheltering under it, the way a traveler shelters under another man's roof, and roofs belong to whoever built them, and whoever built this one had apparently decided, after nine years, to send someone round to collect the rent.

She was kneeling at the edge of terrace thirty-one, packing the last of the day's harvest into her basket, when the arrow came.

She heard it first—a thin whistle cutting the dusk, wrong somehow, too straight a line for any bird—and then it struck the earth a foot from her knee, burying itself in the salt crust with a soft *thock*, upright, quivering.

Freya went absolutely still. Out on the flats, the last light lay like spilled honey, and the valley walls were turning violet, and the arrow stood in the ground between her knees and the village, feathered in grey and white.

Grey and white. Ash-grey, with a crest stamped into the fletching's binding wax.

She knew that sigil. Every child in the realm knew it, drawn in schoolroom ink: the crowned tower, the king's mark, the mark that meant the law had arrived and the law was not asking.

A scrap of paper was tied below the fletchings. Her fingers, when she reached for it, were steadier than she deserved. She unrolled it and read by the dying light:

*The census continues. One resident of this valley has failed to appear for counting. All residents will assemble tomorrow at dawn in the square. Attendance is mandatory.*

And beneath it, smaller, in a different hand—a clerk's precise script, added like a footnote:

*Freya Marsh, terraces 31–40, was observed at her pans at midday and again at dusk. Why does she avoid the assembly?*

Freya read it twice. Then a third time, though the words didn't change, and neither did the arithmetic underneath them: someone had been watching her. All day. While she scraped salt and lied smoothly and walked home with her legs working properly, someone with a grey cloak and a clerk's patience had been writing her name in a book.

She sat back on her heels in the cooling salt-pan, the paper trembling slightly in her hand, and above her the first stars were coming out in the ribbon of sky between the valley walls—stars she had wished on for nine years, stars that had never once answered, and she had loved them for that, for their beautiful incurious silence.

Tomorrow at dawn, she thought. Tomorrow at dawn the whole valley would stand in the square and be counted, and somewhere among the counted faces would be a woman whose name was a coat she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it was a coat, and the man with the ledger-face would look at her again with those entry-way eyes, and this time—

This time the valley might not forget fast enough to save her.

Far off, beyond the eastern wall, beyond the boundary where storms went around the valley like water around a stone, thunder muttered. Faint. Distant. Outside.

And for the first time in nine years, Freya Almeida—salt-harvester, liar, ghost—felt something answer it, deep in the locked room of her chest. Just a stir. Just a knock.

As if the storm had always known she'd come back to the door eventually.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat by her cold hearth with the arrow laid across her knees like a question, turning it in the lamplight, learning its weight. Crown-work, the shaft. Hunter-fletched, the feathers. Expensive, all of it—expensive the way a leash is expensive, crafted by someone skilled to hold something alive.

Outside, the valley slept the sleep of people who believed they were safe. Somewhere down the lane, Tam snored behind walls that wouldn't matter. Somewhere in the meeting hall, the census book waited with all their names in it, sorted and shelved, seed-grain for whatever the crown intended to grow from them.

Freya looked at the fire she hadn't lit, and at her hands, and made herself say the true thing, quietly, to the dark, because saying it aloud was the only way to make it real enough to act on:

"I was never invisible. I was unread."

And unread pages, she thought, setting the arrow aside at last, were exactly the sort the crown had just begun collecting.

Dawn was four hours away. The square was a mile down the road.

She had four hours to decide who was going to walk into it.
