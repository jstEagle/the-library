## 2. Ash on the Wind

The smoke woke Freya before the bells did.

She had learned, over nine years, to read the valley's mornings the way other people read weather. The salt-pans told her things—when rain was coming by the way the crust went soft and grey, when the wind would shift by how the brine pools shivered. This morning they told her nothing at all, because this morning she was already running, barefoot through the shallows with her skirts hitched up and her heart going like a drum in a festival parade.

Smoke from the east. From the farms. From Tam's farm.

"Please be the bread oven," she said to herself, because Tam's bread oven smoked like a dragon with a grudge on the best of days, and because saying *please be anything else* out loud felt like inviting it. "Be the bread oven. Be the bread oven."

It was not the bread oven.

From the rise above the eastern fields she could see it all laid out below her like a painting nobody should ever have made: three farms burning, the thatch catching light like it had been waiting years for permission. Figures moved between the buildings in matching dark coats, moving with a terrible tidiness, the way men move when they've done a thing often enough that it has stopped being interesting. Horses stamped in the lane. A wagon stood waiting with bars on its sides, which was somehow worse than the fire—the fire was chaos and hunger, but the wagon was *planned*.

And there, kneeling in the mud of his own dooryard with two hunters' hands on his shoulders, was Tam.

Tam, who had pulled her cart out of the ditch twice in her first winter and never once asked why a woman would come to a valley where magic couldn't follow and stay for nine years without explaining herself. Tam, whose gift had never woken—who had been tested as a boy, tested again at his wedding, tested so many times he'd started making jokes about it, telling Freya once, laughing over mugs of cider, *"I'm the least magical man in the realm, Frey. The witches themselves gave up on me."*

They were putting him in the wagon anyway.

Freya stood on the rise with the wind pulling at her hair, and every instinct she had honed for nine years screamed the same lesson: *be small, be salt, be nothing.* Stay on the hill. Grieve later. Invisible people survived; visible people burned or rode away in wagons with bars.

Then she heard the screaming.

Not Tam's. Tam was being terribly quiet, the particular quiet of a man conserving himself for something. It was a smaller voice, high and furious and terrified, coming from the barn—and Freya knew that voice, had taught that voice to whistle through a grass blade just last summer, had watched that girl fall into the brine pool and come up spitting and laughing and demanding nobody tell her father.

Wren. Tam's daughter. Eight years old and hiding in a barn while her world burned.

Two hunters were walking toward the barn door. One of them carried a coil of rope and the bored expression of a man doing inventory.

Later—much later, on roads far from the valley—Freya would try to find the moment the decision happened, and she never could. There was no decision. There was only the fact that Wren was eight years old, and hiding, and that the men with the rope thought a child's fear was an item on a list.

Freya went down the hill like the storm she used to be.

* *

The valley pushed back against her before she'd taken ten steps.

That was the thing about Salt Valley that no one who hadn't lived inside its silence could understand: it wasn't empty of magic. It was *full* of the absence of it, the way a held breath is full of air that isn't moving. For nine years Freya had lived inside that held breath, and she'd told herself the quiet was a mercy—a place where the thing inside her could sleep. But running down that hill, reaching for the first thread of storm she'd touched since the worst night of her life, she discovered the truth: the silence wasn't passive. It pressed. It leaned on her gift like a hand on a lid, like deep water on a drowned bell, and when she clawed at the power coiled in her chest it came up slow and thick and *angry*, dragging resistance behind it the way a plough drags clay.

Her skin prickled. Her teeth ached. Somewhere behind her eyes, pressure built like the sky before hail.

*Come on,* she begged it, stumbling through the wet grass. *Just a little. Just enough. I'm not asking for a storm, I'm asking for a gust, a shadow, a trick—anything.*

The valley squeezed harder. She could almost hear it—not words, nothing so kind as words, but a feeling like the whole landscape inhaling and refusing to let go. *You don't get to be that here,* the silence said. *That's what you came here to stop being.*

"I know," she gasped, aloud, to a valley that didn't answer. "I know what I did. Just this once. Just—"

The barn door opened ahead of her. The hunter with the rope stepped inside.

And something in Freya tore loose.

It wasn't much. That was the miracle and the horror of it—it wasn't much at all. A flicker. A single strand of storm-light pulled up out of her marrow like a splinter drawn from a wound, thin as wire, hot as summer lightning. The valley clamped down on it instantly, and she felt the whole covenant-silence shudder around her like a struck drumhead, felt every salt-pool for a mile go still and attentive—

—but for one heartbeat, one, the flicker slipped free.

She threw it without aiming, the way you'd throw a stone at a dog worrying a lamb. It hit the lantern hanging by the barn's hay-loft—the one some careful farmer had left lit against the early dark—and the flame leapt, guttered, and died. In the same instant every loose straw in the loft shifted with a dry whisper, sliding down across the barn's back wall, burying the gap where a small frightened girl was hidden under a blanket of ordinary, unmagical hay.

The hunter in the doorway frowned at the dead lantern. Grumbled something about wind. Struck a match to relight it—and paused, sniffing, head cocking like a bird's.

Because there it was. The smell of it. Ozone, faint and wrong in a valley where no storm had broken in nine years. The scent of magic, dying even as it faded, a fingerprint left in the air.

The hunter turned slowly, scanning the fields.

Freya dropped flat behind the low stone wall at the field's edge, her heart slamming, the spent flicker leaving her shaking and hollowed and—gods help her—*tingling*. Nine years since she'd felt her power move. Nine years of numbness, of deliberate deafness to the thing she was. And now, having spent barely a candleflame's worth, her whole body sang with it like a struck fence-wire, and underneath the terror was something warm and awful and familiar:

It had felt *good*.

* *

They took Tam at midmorning.

Freya watched from the loft of her own cottage, half a mile off, through a gap in the thatch, hating herself for watching and unable to stop. They walked him past his burning fields. They let him stop at the well, which she understood—they always let them do one human thing, she remembered, the hunters, because it made the rest go smoother—and Tam filled a cup from the bucket and drank it slowly and set the cup upside-down on the well's rim, which meant nothing to the hunters and everything to anyone raised in the valley: *the water stays. Come back.*

He looked at the barn as they led him past. He didn't look long—one breath, maybe two—and he didn't call out, and he didn't beg, and Freya realized with a lurch that he knew. He knew Wren was under the hay in the loft, safe, breathing slow the way he'd taught her to breathe during the summer storms, when thunder rolled over the valley and Wren got frightened and Tam would sit with her and say, *breathe like the tide, little fish, in slow and out slower.* He knew, and he was choosing not to look, because looking would tell the hunters where to search next.

Tam, the least magical man in the realm, performed more magic in that one not-look than Freya had managed in nine years of hiding hers.

The wagon rolled west toward the valley's edge, taking him with it. The fires burned down to smoking bones. And Wren stayed hidden until full dark, exactly as instructed, exactly as trained, before she crept out of the hay and ran the ditches and drainage cuts to Freya's door, arriving with hay in her hair and ash on her cheeks and her chin up in the fierce way that meant she was one word away from crying and would bite anyone who noticed.

"They took Da," she said.

"I know, love."

"He's not even gifted. He's *not*. They tested him. Everybody knows they tested him."

"I know." Freya knelt and picked hay out of the girl's hair, gently, piece by piece, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. "Sit down. I'll make tea."

"We're not supposed to use tea leaves on days like this," Wren said, with the hollow precision of someone reciting rules because the rules are all that's holding the roof up. "Aunt Bess says waste on grief days insults the leaf."

"Aunt Bess," Freya said, setting the kettle on, "can insult me any day she likes, and today I'm insulting the leaf right back."

Something cracked in the girl then—not tears, quite, but the sound of a child letting herself be eight years old for exactly ten seconds. Freya held her while it passed, and stared over her head at her own dark window, and thought about the lantern, and the ozone, and the hunter turning his head like a bird dog catching a scent.

"You saved me," Wren said into her shoulder. "In the barn. The light went out and the hay fell and I thought—I thought Da sent somebody. Did Da send you?"

"No one sent me."

"But the light—" The girl pulled back, frowning, working it through with the relentless honesty of children. "There wasn't any wind, Freya. I checked. Before, I mean. There wasn't any wind at all."

Outside, the night lay utterly still. Not one branch moved. The kettle began, very softly, to sing.

Freya met the girl's eyes and lied to her, smoothly and kindly and hating every syllable: "Old houses do strange things when there's fire nearby. Drafts in the walls. Go on, drink your tea."

Wren drank her tea. But she kept glancing at the still, silent windows, and once, when the fire popped, she flinched toward the door instead of away from it—and Freya understood that the lie had bought less than she'd hoped. Children believed what fit. And somewhere in Wren's sharp little mind, a story was already assembling itself: the story of the woman on the hill, the one who never talked about before, the one who'd appeared out of nowhere the exact moment the light went out.

The story was true. That was the problem with it.

* *

Wren slept at last, curled in Freya's bed among the quilts, one fist closed around a salt-crystal she'd brought from home like a talisman. Freya sat by the cold hearth and finally let herself feel it—all of it, in order.

Guilt first, because guilt was oldest and loudest. The lantern. The flicker. The ozone hanging in the air like a signature on a confession. Nine years of discipline, spent in ten seconds, and for what—for one child saved out of a valley full of them, while the wagons kept rolling. She had sworn she'd never touch it again. She'd told herself the vow was penance, but sitting there in the dark she finally admitted what it really was: fear wearing penance's coat. She hadn't renounced her power because it was evil. She'd renounced it because the last time she'd held it fully, someone had died, and she had never wanted to know whether the killing had been the storm's fault or hers.

Tonight, though. Tonight it had been a lantern and a drift of hay. Tonight it had been a child breathing under straw while men with ropes walked past.

And it had felt like *mercy*.

That was the thought that undid her. Nine years she'd known her power only as the thing that killed Elder Voss, the crack of it, the smell of burnt air, the weight of a body. Tonight, for the first time, the storm in her had been a small warm hand cupped over a candleflame. The same power. Exactly the same power. Mercy and murder, and nothing to choose between them except what she did with it—and if that was true, then the vow hadn't been protecting the valley from her.

It had been protecting her from choosing.

The silence of the valley pressed in around the cottage, that great held breath, and Freya could have sworn it felt different tonight. Thinner. Strained. Like a lid rattling on a pot. When she reached, experimentally, toward the place where her storm slept, the silence shoved back hard—but the shove had a new quality to it now, something almost like alarm. Like a thing that had been holding its breath for a very long time and had just felt, deep in its lungs, the first tremor of a sneeze.

She sat up straight.

Because there it was, written plain in the pressure and the stillness: the silence wasn't just pushing back against *her*. It was failing. Failing the way a dam fails—slowly, invisibly, and then all at once. If her single spent flicker had left ozone a hunter could smell, then the valley's great covenant of quiet was leaking everywhere, in ways she'd never been able to feel before tonight, because she'd been too busy not-feeling anything at all.

The eastern horizon was still faintly stained with the glow of burned farms. Somewhere west, beyond the salt flats, a wagon with bars was carrying the least magical man in the realm to whatever the crown did with people it stole. And somewhere in the dark between, the held breath of the whole valley was starting, very slowly, to slip.

Wren stirred in the bed. "Freya?" she mumbled, half-asleep. "Why did they take Da if he's not gifted?"

And Freya, staring into the dark with her gift humming beneath her skin like a wire before lightning, heard herself give the honest answer at last:

"Because, love, I don't think it was ever about who's gifted."

Far off, past the salt flats, past the boundary she'd never once crossed in nine years, thunder rolled—or something very like thunder, though the sky overhead stayed clear and starry and utterly still.

The valley held its breath. But Freya had begun to suspect it couldn't hold it much longer.
