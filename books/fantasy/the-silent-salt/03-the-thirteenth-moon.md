## 3. The Thirteenth Moon

The night after the eastern farms burned, Freya Almeida did the only sensible thing a frightened woman could do: she salted her door.

Not for preservation—though the winter mutton could use it—but for warding. Salt was the one language the valley understood. She'd learned it in her first year here, from old Widow Brack, who had pressed a handful of coarse grey crystals into her palm and said, *Sprinkle it at the threshold and nothing with teeth can cross.* Freya had asked what counted as teeth. The widow had looked at her a long moment, the way people in the valley sometimes looked at newcomers who asked too many questions, and said only, "You'll know."

Nine years of sprinkling salt at thresholds. Nine years of tending the pans out on the flats, raking brine into glittering white fields that caught the sun like fallen stars. Nine years of being nobody, which after everything was the finest thing Freya had ever been.

But tonight, as she laid the line of crystals along her doorframe with the care of a woman threading a needle, her hands would not stop shaking.

*They took Tam.*

Tam, who couldn't so much as light a candle with his mind if his life depended on it—which, apparently, it now did. Tam, who once spent an entire harvest festival trying to convince anyone who'd listen that he'd felt "a sort of tingling" behind his ear, and whose wife had laughed until she cried and told him it was probably the ale.

They had taken him anyway. And they had burned his fields while his daughter Wren stood in the road and watched.

Freya sat back on her heels and looked at the little heap of salt on her doorstep, absurdly small against the dark. Somewhere beyond the hills, the fires were still burning. She could smell them on the wind—not woodsmoke, but something richer and fouler, like the valley itself had been set alight.

"You'll catch your death sitting there."

She hadn't heard the gate open. That alone should have frightened her; Maren moved like weather, all around you before you noticed. But then, Freya reflected, everything about tonight was wrong. Why not this too?

The old woman stood in her garden path wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, a lantern hanging from one knuckled hand. She was small and bent as a shepherd's crook, with a face like a walnut left out in the rain, and she had been ancient when Freya arrived nine years ago and appeared determined to remain exactly as ancient forever, out of what Freya suspected was pure stubbornness.

"Come inside," Freya said, standing. "It's freezing."

"It is." Maren didn't move. "And yet I thought we might stand out here a moment longer, you and I, and look at your very fine pile of salt."

Freya felt her cheeks warm. "Widow Brack's trick."

"Mm." Maren's eyes—pale as skimmed milk, sharp as awls—moved to the doorframe, then to the sky, then, unbearably, to Freya herself. "Did it work? The salt?"

"For mice," Freya admitted. "Mostly."

A sound escaped the old woman that might have been a laugh in a former life. Then she lifted the lantern higher, and the light fell across her face, and Freya saw that whatever humor lived in her had gone down deep beneath something else. Something grave.

"The hunters crossed the western rim three days ago," Maren said. "You knew that?"

"I saw their fires from the ridge."

"And yesterday they took Tam Vessel, whose gift never woke, and whose grandmother once dreamed of drowning, which in some courts is apparently enough." Maren's voice stayed level, but there was iron under it. "Tomorrow they reach the central farms. The day after, the village. You have perhaps two nights, girl, before someone remembers that the quiet woman on the salt-flats never once got sick in nine winters, never once lost a roof in a storm, and always, *always* knew when rain was coming two days early."

Freya's mouth went dry. "I rake salt. It's not suspicious to know weather. It's practically required."

"Nothing about this valley is required, Freya." Maren stepped closer, lantern swinging. "That is precisely what I've come to tell you."

* * *

Maren's cottage sat at the edge of the village, half-swallowed by briar roses, and smelled permanently of dried herbs and lamp oil and, inexplicably, boiled sweets—a smell that had confused Freya for years until she discovered the old woman genuinely kept a dish of them on the mantel and offered them to visitors the way other people offered tea. Tonight the dish sat untouched between them, which Freya took as a very bad sign indeed.

Maren built up the fire without speaking. Then she sat across from Freya, folded her hands atop her cane, and looked at her for a long, unhurried moment, the way one looks at a knot before deciding which thread to pull.

"Do you remember," she said, "when you first came here? What you noticed?"

Freya blinked. "The silence."

"The silence." Maren nodded slowly. "Say more."

"I—" Freya frowned, reaching back. Nine years, and the memory was soft-edged now, worn like a river stone. "I'd been walking for days. Running, really. Half-mad with grief and fear. And then I crested the ridge above the valley and it was like..." She searched. "Like stepping into a room where someone had just stopped shouting. Everything went *quiet*. Not my ears—the rest of me. The part of me that had been screaming since the fire." Her hands curled in her lap. "I thought I'd died. Or that I'd finally broken completely, and this was what broken felt like. Peaceful."

"That was the first thing every gift-born exile notices," Maren said. "The silence. They come over the ridge carrying storms and griefs and gifts they'd give their right arms to be rid of, and the valley takes them away like a fever breaking." She leaned forward. "And do you know why, child?"

"Geography?" Freya tried. "Widow Brack said the salt. Something in the ground. Iron deposits, maybe. I never—I assumed there was a reason. There's usually a reason."

"There is a reason." Maren reached beneath her shawl and drew out something on a cord: a stone. Not round, not smooth—an ugly, jagged thing the size of a fist, shot through with veins of dull silver, and cracked straight through its middle like an egg struck by a knife. Even as Maren held it up, Freya felt the hairs on her arms rise.

"What is that?"

"This," Maren said quietly, "is an oath-stone. One third of the seal that keeps magic out of this valley. And it is dying."

The fire popped. Outside, wind worried at the eaves.

"When I say *seal*, I don't mean a charm or a hedge-witch's knot," Maren went on. "I mean a covenant. An agreement, sworn between three realms, older than any kingdom you could name. Long ago, magic ran wild across every border—it drowned one world, scorched another, and nearly unmade this one. So the realms' keepers met and made a bargain. They bound great seams between the worlds, and into those seams they wove oaths, and into the oaths they set stones such as this one. Where a stone lies, magic cannot pass. Not cannot—*will not*. It is law written deeper than law." She turned the cracked stone in the lamplight. "This valley is not naturally dead, Freya. It is *sealed*. Deliberately. Lovingly. By people who wanted somewhere in all the worlds where the gift-born could lay down their burdens and simply live."

Freya stared at her. "Then why does everyone here think it's just—geology?"

"Because that is what a good seal looks like from the inside. Invisible. Inevitable. Like the sky." Maren's jaw tightened. "But seals are not stones only. They are maintained. Fed, in a manner of speaking, by the turning of the moons—each full moon renews the weave, twelve turnings to the cycle, and at the twelfth the covenant rests, whole, before beginning again." She paused. "There has not been a twelfth moon this cycle, Freya. There will not be."

The words took a moment to find their meaning. When they did, Freya felt the floor tilt gently beneath her chair.

"I don't understand. The moon doesn't skip."

"No. It doesn't." Maren set the cracked stone on the table between them, and Freya saw now, looking properly, how the silver veins within it had dimmed—like embers going grey. "Something at court has found a way to break the count. To steal a moon from the cycle and bend its power elsewhere. Each turning since, the seal has weakened. First the edges—you may have noticed the brambles by the west road growing strange this summer, fruiting twice. Then the animals. Then—" her pale eyes fixed on Freya, merciless and kind at once, "—then the dreams. Tell me you haven't had them."

Freya's throat worked. Because she had. For weeks now: dreams of standing on the salt-flats under a green-black sky, lightning crawling along her arms like silver serpents, and her own voice crying out in a tongue she didn't know but somehow understood. She'd woken each morning with her pillow damp and her heart hammering and told herself it was the mutton.

"They're getting louder," she whispered.

"Because the seal is thinning, and what's inside you is pressing against it." Maren tapped the cracked stone, once, gently, the way one taps a grave. "And here is the arithmetic, child, plain as bread: the thirteenth moon rises in twenty-six nights. When it crests, the covenant fails entirely. Every seam opens. Magic will flood back into this valley—and every other sealed place in the realm—all at once, all ungoverned, all *starving*. And the crown's hunters will not need to drag the gift-born out anymore, because the gift-born will blaze like bonfires. Every hidden child, every sleeping elder, every woman who ever salted her door against the dark—they will all be lit up for hunting, visible from horizon to horizon."

The fire crackled. Freya became aware, distantly, that she was gripping the arms of her chair hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

"Who?" she managed. "Who would break it? Who *could*?"

"Someone high. Someone with access to the old rites and no love for the old bargains." Maren's face went grim. "Someone feeding on what they drain. The hunters aren't collecting the gifted for prisons, Freya. They're collecting them the way a farmer collects milk. Something drinks what they take. I don't know what. But I know the covenant doesn't break itself, and it isn't breaking by accident."

Silence stretched. Freya looked down at her hands—the same hands that had called the storm nine years ago, that had raked salt and kneaded bread and buried Widow Brack last spring, ordinary hands, quiet hands—and felt, for the first time since she'd crossed the ridge, that the quiet had been a loan. Borrowed time, accruing interest. Now the debt had come due, and the collector wore the crown's sigil.

"So that's it," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Twenty-six days, and then everyone I—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There are children in this valley, Maren. Wren Vessel is seven years old and she thinks her father was taken because he stole a chicken."

"I know."

"And you're telling me the sky itself is going to mark them."

"I'm telling you worse than that." Maren leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the silver in her hair and the terrible steadiness in her eyes. "I'm telling you the seal can be reforged. Three stones. Three keepers—one for each realm that swore the oath. This one was mine, and I am too old and too tired to carry it further, which is why it cracked when the weakening began. A keeper must carry it to the seam and renew the oath in person. All three. Before the thirteenth moon." She slid the stone across the table toward Freya.

Freya looked at it as though it were a snake.

"No," she said. "No. Absolutely not. I'm a salt-raker. I'm a *nobody*. That's the entire point of the last nine years—that's the whole blessed arrangement. I don't leave the valley. Leaving the valley is the one thing I swore I would never—"

"Your storm comes back the moment you cross the boundary," Maren said simply. "All of it. Every hour of it you've suppressed, returned at once. It will hurt. It may well level the first hill you stand on. And yes, the hunters will feel it, and they will come for you, and you will spend the rest of this journey being exactly what they hunt." She shrugged, thin shoulders moving under the slate shawl. "I won't pretend otherwise. But I will tell you what I've learned in ninety-three years, Freya Almeida: dread is a heavy load when you're running from it, and almost weightless when you're walking toward it. Same weight. Different shoulders."

Freya laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it at all. "Is that meant to help?"

"It's meant to be true. Help is extra." Maren pushed the stone closer still, until it rested against Freya's folded hands, cold as a riverbed. "The second keeper is in Vess, the drowned city. The third, further yet, in Ondu. You have time enough to reach them if you leave before the week turns. You do not have time enough to sit here salting your door."

Freya stared at the cracked stone. Silver veins, banked like sleeping coals. Somewhere beneath her breastbone, something that had lain quiet for nine years shifted in its sleep, as if it had heard its name called from very far away.

"If I go," she said slowly, "and I fail—"

"Then the thirteenth moon rises over an open valley, and we meet it together, and we fight." Maren smiled then, small and crooked and unexpectedly young. "But if you succeed—if you walk out of here carrying a piece of the oldest law in creation—then you will not be running anymore, child. You will be *aimed*. There is a difference. Ask any arrow."

Outside, the wind rose, worrying at the eaves again, and far off to the east the burn-smell drifted faint and foul. Freya picked up the oath-stone. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"Twenty-six nights," she said.

"Twenty-five, by dawn." Maren rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and fetched her shawl close. At the door she paused, and glanced back with those pale, awl-sharp eyes. "Boiled sweet? For the road. You'll want your strength, and I refuse to let history record that I sent you off empty-handed."

Despite everything—despite the cracking seal and the stolen moon and the hunters closing like a hand—Freya found herself laughing, wet-eyed and helpless, as she took one.

* * *

She did not sleep. Instead she sat at her table by candlelight and wrote letters she would never send: to Tam, wherever he was; to Wren; to the valley itself, which had given her nine years of peace and deserved better thanks than this. When the candles guttered she gave up and packed instead—bread, cheese, the good knife, Widow Brack's spare cloak. The oath-stone she wrapped in flannel and tucked against her heart, where she could feel it, faint as a second pulse.

An hour before dawn, she stood in her doorway and looked back at the small house, the salt-pans silvering grey under the fading stars, the whole sleeping valley she had loved precisely because it demanded nothing of her.

"Dread walking forward," she murmured to herself. "Weightless. Right."

Her voice shook. Her feet, remarkably, did not.

Behind her, in the east, the sky had begun to pale—and Freya could not shake the feeling that somewhere beyond the hills, something vast and patient had already marked the place where the storm would return, and was waiting, with the crown's patience, for the thunder.
