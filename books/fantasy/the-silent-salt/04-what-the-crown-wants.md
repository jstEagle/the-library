## 4. What the Crown Wants

Corvus Hale woke before dawn, as he always did, and made tea for two.

He drank both cups. The second was habit, nothing more—a ritual inherited from a dead man, performed so faithfully that some mornings he almost forgot it was empty. He set the second cup upside down on the shelf beside the window, where the steam ghost of it had long since faded, and pulled on his boots.

The boots were good boots. Crown-issue leather, resoled twice a year by a cobbler in the palace district who charged him half price because Corvus had once, quietly, arranged for the man's son to be struck from the census of gifted children. The boy had shown a small talent—a warmth in his hands, nothing more, enough to keep bread rising on cold mornings—and such talents were exactly the kind that got families dragged into wagons when the quotas came due. Corvus had erased the boy with three lines of ink and never mentioned it to anyone, least of all the cobbler, who wept over his discount as if gratitude were a debt he could repay in leather.

Corvus accepted the discount. He did not accept the tears. Tears implied the arrangement required forgiveness, and Corvus did not believe his work required forgiveness. It required precision. Everything else was sentiment, and sentiment was how villages burned.

He checked the ledgers first. He always checked the ledgers first.

The morning reports lay stacked on his desk in the hunter's quarters above the White Stables—so named because no white horse had ever been stabled there, the name being one of the crown's smaller jokes, the sort of joke that grows like moss over an atrocity until everyone forgets there is stone beneath it. Twelve reports from twelve districts. Nine clean. Three with names.

He read them the way a surgeon reads: no flinching, no lingering, attention distributed exactly where it was owed.

The first name was a ferryman on the Greywash, sixty years old, whose gift had surfaced late—a knack for knowing which crossing would hold and which would drown him. Harmless. Useful, even. Corvus marked it *defer* with a note: *observe through spring thaw.* A man like that could be left alone for years if the harvest numbers held.

The second name was a child in Ondu, seven years old, who sang notes that made glass hum. Corvus read the description twice—the informant's breathless account of chandeliers ringing without being touched, of wine glasses shivering themselves to shards—and felt the familiar cold arithmetic begin its work behind his eyes. Pitch-sensitives were rare. Pitch-sensitives were *valuable*. Which meant the child would go on the priority list, and the priority list went to the king's chamberlain, and the chamberlain's men came with soft hoods and gentle voices, and somewhere below the palace the child's gift would be drawn out of her like thread from a spindle, and she would live the rest of her life as a quiet, hollowed, well-fed ward of the crown, unable to say what she had lost because she would no longer remember it clearly herself.

The draining did not kill. That was the official comfort, repeated in every proclamation, every pulpit, every nervous conversation between parents of strange children across the realm. *No one dies.* It was even true, in the way that saying a house does not burn down when you have stripped out its beams is true.

Corvus marked the child's entry and did not let himself think about chandeliers.

The third name stopped his pen mid-stroke.

Salt Valley. Eastern farms. An arrow recovered at the boundary marker, fletched with the crown's grey-and-gold, loosed by one of his own outriders—a warning shot, per protocol, fired wide of a woman tending the salt-pans. The report described her only briefly: dark hair going iron at the temples, hands scarred from brine work, posture too still for a farmwife. The outrider had written, in the careful understatement drilled into every hunter, *"Subject displayed unusual composure. Recommend continued observation."*

Below it, someone else had written something else. The handwriting belonged to Veyra Doss, his second, and it said: *"Storm-signature possible. Request authority to enter."*

Corvus sat back and looked at the words for a long moment while the tea went cold in his cup.

Storm-signature. In Salt Valley, where no magic had crossed the boundary in nine years—not because the valley's people were clean, but because the valley itself was *sealed*, warded at its edges by a covenant older than the kingdom, older than the crown, older than anything the histories were permitted to mention. Salt Valley was the one place in the realm where the crown's writ ended not by law but by physics. Nothing woke there. Nothing burned there. For nine years it had been the single blank page in Corvus's ledger, and he had come, over those years, to regard that blank page with something he refused to call relief.

Now there was writing on it.

He should have been pleased. A storm-caller surfacing inside the valley meant the seal was thinning, which meant access, which meant the last untouchable reserve of hidden gifts was opening to him like a fist unclenching. His superiors would rejoice. The quotas would be met a season early. There were men in the palace who would toast him for it.

Corvus found, examining himself honestly as he always did—honesty being the one luxury he allowed—he was not pleased. He was unsettled. And he distrusted his own unsettledness more than he trusted anyone else's grief.

Because if the seal was thinning, then it was thinning for a reason, and Corvus knew the reason, because Corvus knew everything, because knowing everything was his function the way a knife's function was its edge.

* * *

He walked to the palace through the waking city, alone, as always. Hunters traveled in pairs for safety; Corvus traveled alone because pairs talked, and talking was where competence went to die. Besides, the streets were safe for him in a way they would never be safe for the people he served. No one threw stones at the chief hunter. They simply went quiet as he passed, a silence rolling ahead of him like water parting, mothers drawing children close not out of fear of violence but fear of *notice*. Corvus had learned to hear that silence the way a sailor hears weather. It told him everything about the health of the realm, and lately it had been telling him the realm had a fever.

The guards at the Ivory Gate saluted. He returned each salute precisely, meeting each pair of eyes—young men, mostly, boys in polished mail who had joined the hunt out of patriotism or hunger or the simple human appetite for belonging to something fierce—and he gave them what dignity offered back: acknowledgment without warmth. He had stopped trying to save any of them years ago. You could not save a man from the shape of his own obedience.

Beneath the palace, the corridors changed.

This was the part of the palace no map showed, though Corvus had memorized it anyway, because a chief hunter who did not know every corridor of his own territory was a man waiting to be surprised, and Corvus Hale had not been surprised in nineteen years of service. The stair descended past the wine vaults, past the old treasury, past the sealed doors of the plague crypts, and then kept descending into stone that predated the palace altogether—black basalt, veined with something pale that might have been quartz and was not. The air grew warm. Not comfortably warm. Warm the way a sickroom is warm, warm with a borrowed heat, and along the walls the veins in the stone glowed faintly, pulsing with a rhythm just slightly too slow to be a heartbeat, which was somehow worse than if it had matched.

Two doorwardens stood at the final arch. They did not salute; the doorwardens were chosen from men who had been drained so thoroughly that ceremony had gone out of them like heat out of cooling bread. One of them opened the way. Neither spoke. Speaking was discouraged this deep. Not forbidden—the king's decrees rarely forbade anything outright, preferring instead to make certain behaviors quietly unthinkable—but discouraged, the way sleep is discouraged in a house with a crying infant.

The chamber at the bottom was vast and round and wrong. Corvus had never gotten used to it, and he had decided long ago that never getting used to it was a professional discipline, like checking the ledgers first.

It was not a dungeon. Dungeons held prisoners, and prisoners had rights, of a kind—rights to trial, to confession, to a name spoken aloud at their end. What the chamber held had no name, no trial, no end. It filled the far half of the space the way a shadow fills a corner: a mass of darkness given just enough form to suggest depth, layered and folding, threaded through with those pale veins that pulsed in the slow, patient rhythm Corvus had come to think of, privately, in the privacy of his own relentless honesty, as *digestion*.

Above it, suspended in cages of black iron that floated without chains, hung the lights.

That was the only word for them. Lights—dozens of them, hundreds, small soft glimmers the color of every gift the realm had ever produced: green-gold for the growth-touched, silver-blue for the tide-speakers, a restless violet flicker that Corvus knew was storm-light, gathered from a girl taken out of the hill country two winters ago. Each light had been a person's whole strangeness once. Their laughter at their own small miracles. Now they hung here, dimming by fractions, and the great dark thing below breathed them in the way a field breathes rain.

Feeding time was noon. Corvus arrived early, as always, because he preferred to see the thing hungry rather than fed. Hunger, at least, was honest.

"Numbers," he said to the under-steward of the vaults, a grey man named Pellin who kept the intake records.

Pellin read aloud. Corvus listened, and the arithmetic assembled itself in his head with the smooth, terrible ease of long practice. Intake up fourteen percent over the autumn. Retention down six. The thing below was eating faster than the culls supplied, which meant either the quotas rose—which meant more wagons, more soft hoods, more chandeliers ringing in empty houses—or the intervals shortened, which meant the thing below was growing toward something, and the king's astrologers had begun using a phrase in their private memos that Corvus was not supposed to have read: *thirteenth moon.*

"You're frowning, Chief Hunter," said a voice from the shadows near the feeding dais.

Corvus turned. The king's chamberlain stood there, or rather the man who served as chamberlain, though Aldous Wren had not signed a document or managed a household in six years. He lived down here now. His skin had taken on the pale veins' faint luminescence, and his smile had the particular serenity of a man who has stopped needing to persuade anyone of anything.

"I don't frown," Corvus said. "I allocate attention."

"Of course." Wren drifted closer, robes whispering. "You've seen the Salt Valley report."

"I've seen all the reports. That's my function."

"And?"

And. Corvus considered the man, and beyond the man the great patient dark, and beyond the dark the hundreds of dying lights, and he performed the exercise he performed every time the question came—the exercise that let him climb these stairs again and keep doing the work, because the alternative to the work was not innocence, it was only someone else doing it worse.

"It means the seal is failing," Corvus said. "Which means whatever covenant built it is failing. And if the old wards are coming apart on their own schedule, then the gifts trapped inside that valley will come loose whether or not we are standing there with nets." He nodded at the suspended lights, at the dark below them. "A wild surge of unbound talent, released all at once, with no order and no hand guiding it—that is how the old tales say the Sundering began. Villages waking full of power they couldn't hold. Fires that burned cities. The crown's culling exists so that never happens again. I would rather gather the storm than stand under it."

Wren smiled his serene smile. "Spoken like a true servant of the throne."

"I serve the realm," Corvus said. "The throne is the instrument currently available."

It was the truest sentence he had spoken in months, and he watched it land without effect, because truth was another currency Wren had stopped needing. The chamberlain turned back toward the feeding dais, where attendants were beginning the noon rite, lifting the first of the day's lights down from its cage with long silver tongs, cradling it like an egg, carrying it to the edge of the dark.

The light sank in. Somewhere in the mass of shadow, something that was not quite a voice made a sound that was not quite pleasure, and Corvus stood very straight and allocated his attention elsewhere, to the stone floor, to the grain of the basalt, to anything at all, and waited until it was done.

He had told himself the story so many times it had worn smooth as river glass: *the culling prevents catastrophe; better a thousand quiet hollowings than one burning century; I am the scalpel that keeps the realm from the axe.* He believed it the way a man believes in the load-bearing wall of his own house—completely, constantly, and with a small, buried awareness of what would happen if he ever actually tested it.

Today, watching the storm-violet light disappear into the dark, the wall seemed to shift under his palm. Just slightly. Just enough.

* * *

Back in the daylight, he summoned Veyra Doss.

She came to the White Stables within the hour, helmet under her arm, frost still on her cloak from the boundary roads. She was the best of his hunters and she knew it, which made her useful, and she was ambitious, which made her dangerous, and she was cruel in the casual way of people who had never examined a single one of their own beliefs, which made her, in Corvus's private taxonomy, *typical*.

"The valley," he said, without greeting. "Your request for entry."

"Granted?" Her eyes lit. She had wanted Salt Valley since her first year of service; every young hunter wanted it, the blank page, the legend, the place where the crown's reach visibly failed.

"Not granted. Amended." He slid the amended orders across the desk. "You will take four riders. You will establish observation posts at the eastern boundary. You will *not* cross the line. You will document every anomaly—weather, wildlife, dreams reported by border settlements, anything. If the woman at the salt-pans leaves the valley, you follow her. Quietly. At distance."

Doss frowned at the page. "If the seal's failing, Chief, why wait? Every week we sit, the quarry—"

"Is not a quarry yet. She is a woman raking salt." He looked up and let her see, briefly, the flat gray patience that had ended the careers of louder officers than she. "Nine years ago we lost an entire operation in that region because an eager commander crossed a boundary he didn't understand, and the crown spent a decade pretending the resulting silence was a victory. We do not repeat that. If a storm-caller is waking in Salt Valley, she has spent nine years learning to hide from us, and hiding is a skill that improves with practice. We will be thorough before we are fast. Thorough wins."

"Yes, Chief." She took the orders. At the door she paused, and Corvus knew what was coming before she said it, because ambition always asked the same question eventually. "The records from nine years ago—the massacre at Fenmere. The storm-caller responsible was never identified. You worked that file yourself, didn't you? Before your promotion."

"I worked every file," Corvus said.

"There's a theory in the barracks that the Fenmere caller fled into the valley. That the crown let the silence stand because the silence was easier than the chase." Her smile showed the cruelty underneath it, bright and unexamined. "And now the valley's opening. Wouldn't it be something if she'd been sitting there the whole time? Raking salt. Growing old. Thinking herself safe."

Corvus looked at her for a moment longer than the remark deserved.

"Wouldn't it," he said.

She left, boots ringing on the stair, already composing the glory of it. Corvus remained at his desk with the cold tea and the ledgers and the third report, the one with the salt-raker's careful, understated description, and he did something he almost never did: he opened the old file.

Fenmere, nine years past. He remembered it perfectly—perfect recall being one of his few acknowledged vanities—and perfect memory was a burden on days like this, because it gave him the village elder's face, the scorched well, the child's shoe in the doorway of a house that had no roof anymore, and above it all the sky torn open and *pouring*, lightning falling like judgment, like grief, like something that had been held too long and too gently and had finally, catastrophically, broken.

The file's conclusion had never satisfied him. *Perpetrator deceased in event of record.* Neat. Final. Written by a younger Corvus who had needed it to be true, or at least had needed the realm to need it to be true.

He looked at the salt-raker's description again. Dark hair going iron. Hands like a working woman's. Unusual composure.

Composure. He turned the word over. Most people who saw a crown arrow land at their feet ran, or screamed, or froze. Composure was what you got from someone who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to them.

Corvus took out a fresh sheet and began, in his precise, unhurried hand, a new file. At its head he wrote the only designation the old records offered for the Fenmere storm, the phrase the survivors had used, the phrase that had haunted him through nine quiet years of blank-page valleys:

*The woman who called the rain.*

Outside, the afternoon light was going gold over the palace roofs, and beneath his feet, faint and patient, the slow pulse of the thing below kept its terrible time. Somewhere east, past three hundred miles of road and one impossible boundary, a woman was raking salt under a sky that had stayed silent for nine years.

Corvus Hale capped his ink, and gave the silence, for the first time, a deadline.
