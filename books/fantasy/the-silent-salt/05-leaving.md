## 5. Leaving

The last morning in Salt Valley smelled the same as every other morning for nine years: salt, woodsmoke, and Maren's bitter tea, which she claimed was medicinal and which everyone else claimed was a war crime.

"You're sulking at your porridge," Maren said.

"I'm not sulking."

"You've been staring into that bowl like it owes you an apology for ten minutes. Either you're sulking or the porridge has said something unforgivable."

Freya pushed the bowl away. "It's just porridge."

"Nothing is *just* anything anymore, girl." Maren set down her cup with the particular clack she used when a conversation was about to become serious whether anyone wanted it to be or not. "The thirteenth moon turns in eleven days. The hunters will be back—there were four of them on the ridge last night, I counted their fires. And you are still here, drinking my terrible tea, pretending that if you sit very still the world will forget your name."

"It worked for nine years."

"And how did that end?" Maren gestured broadly out the window, where the eastern sky still carried a smudge of smoke from the burned farms. Tam's farm. Tam's daughter, who was sleeping in Maren's back room because there was nowhere else for her, and who had asked twice this morning when her father would come home, and twice Freya had lied to her with a smile that felt like swallowing glass.

"All right," Freya said quietly.

Maren's voice softened by exactly one degree, which for Maren was practically a lullaby. "All right. Then we do this properly. Eat your porridge. A woman should walk to her own execution on a full stomach."

"It's not an execution."

"Mm," said Maren, in the tone of someone who had buried enough friends to know the difference between leaving and dying only by a hair's breadth, and sometimes not even then.

*

They walked north through the salt-pans while the light was still gray and forgiving, so that Freya would not have to see the valley too clearly on her way out of it. That was Maren's kindness, though she'd never have admitted it. She simply announced, "We go early, before the sun makes everything sentimental," as if sentiment were a weather condition one could avoid by scheduling around it.

The pans stretched out flat and blinding-white even in half-light, shallow pools holding the sky upside down. Nine years of them. Nine years of raking salt at dawn, of hauling brine, of learning the exact angle of wind that meant rain—which never mattered, because nothing ever came over the valley edge but more silence. Nine years of being nobody, which had hurt less than being somebody, right up until it hadn't.

Tam's daughter had pressed something into Freya's hand at the door this morning without a word: a smooth white pebble from the pans, worn round. Children gave strange gifts. Freya held it now in her pocket like a coin she couldn't spend, turning it over and over with her thumb.

"The boundary's past the white rise," Maren said. "You've never been past it?"

"Nobody goes past it."

"Nobody *can*. That's rather the point of a prison, even a comfortable one." Maren walked with a stick she didn't need and a scowl she did. She was small and bent and brown as old rope, and Freya had known her for nine years without knowing until three nights ago that those hands had once signed a covenant between realms. Some people kept their histories folded up small, like letters they'd read too many times. "When we cross, it will hurt. I won't lie about that."

"How much?"

"You remember the first time you called a storm? What did it feel like?"

"Like—" Freya searched. It had been so long. "Like the sky got inside me. Like I was full of weather."

"Yes. Now imagine nine years of weather arriving all at once, with interest." Maren glanced sideways at her. "I've seen folk come out of sealed places before. One man bit through his own lip. A woman screamed for an hour and then laughed for two, which frightened me considerably more."

"Wonderful. Anything else? Should I write a will?"

"If you have anything worth leaving, leave it to me. I want your rake."

"My rake."

"It's a good rake."

Freya laughed—a short, startled sound—and then found she couldn't stop entirely, and then found her eyes stinging, and understood that Maren had done it on purpose. The old woman looked insufferably pleased with herself and pretended not to.

They crested the white rise as the sun cleared the eastern hills, and the valley lay below them one last time: the pans like scattered silver coins, the crooked roofs, the thread of chimney smoke, the whole small quiet world that had held her like a hand closed gently around something breakable.

She made herself look at all of it. If she was going to lose it, she'd lose it honestly.

"Well," she said. Her voice came out rough. "Goodbye, valley."

"Don't say goodbye to geography, it can't hear you," Maren said. But she stood a moment longer than she needed to, looking down too.

Then they walked down the far side, and the world ended.

*

There was no wall. There was no line drawn anywhere that Freya could see—one step was salt-flat scrub and gray rock, ordinary as bread—and the next step was the boundary itself, and it took her like deep water takes a drowning woman.

It began in her teeth. A hum, then a ringing, then a soundless pressure behind her eyes, and then the storms came home.

Every storm she had ever swallowed rose up at once.

The first was small—the little thunder she'd pulled out of the air at fourteen, giddy with power, before she knew what she was. Then the ones she'd hidden during her early years in the valley, instinctive swallows whenever the silence had pressed against her gift too hard: dozens of them, dozens, each one a knot of lightning and pressure stuffed down somewhere beneath her ribs, and all of them now tearing loose together.

Freya went to her knees. The ground under her hands buzzed. Static crawled up her arms and lifted the fine hairs along them, and her heartbeat stuttered into arrhythmia, and somewhere very far away Maren was saying her name in a voice stripped of all its dryness.

The biggest storm was the oldest one. It wasn't a storm she'd hidden; it was the storm she *was*, the one that had torn apart a village square and killed an old man, the one she'd carried across the valley line nine years ago and let the silence take from her like a surgeon lifting out a tumor. She had believed it gone. It was not gone. It had been waiting, patient as salt, and now it poured back into the hollow space it had left behind, filling her to the seams.

She screamed. Or thought she did. Light was coming out of her skin—that much she could see, white-blue threads crawling over her wrists—and the air above the flats had gone green-black in a ring around her, clouds assembling out of clear morning with terrible eagerness, as if they'd only been waiting for permission.

"*Let it pass through you!*" Maren's voice, close now, her grip iron on Freya's shoulder. "Don't hold it, don't fight it—weather isn't meant to be kept, girl, it's meant to *move*—"

Freya stopped fighting.

It is difficult to describe what happened next, partly because she was somewhat occupied, and partly because what happened next was not really an event so much as a change of state, the way ice becoming water is not an event to the ice. The storm passed through her. All of them did. Nine years of suppressed thunder moved through her body like a river resuming its course after a dam breaks, and for one endless moment Freya Almeida was not a woman kneeling on a hillside but a column of open sky, and it was—

It was *magnificent*.

That was the horrifying part. Even terrified, even hurting, some deep animal part of her sang. This was what she was. This vastness, this voltage, this sense that the horizon was not a boundary but a suggestion. For nine years she had lived in a whisper, and now she remembered that she had once been a shout.

Then it was over, and she was a woman on her hands and knees in the dirt, shaking, soaked with sweat, with a scorch-mark fanned out around her in the scrub grass and a ragged little cloud overhead dissolving sheepishly back into blue.

Maren sat down beside her, heavily, breathing hard as though she'd run a mile instead of standing still.

"Well," Freya managed. "That was."

"Take your time."

"That was *awful*."

"Yes."

"And also—" Freya sat up. Her whole body rang like a struck bell that hadn't finished deciding to be quiet. Under her skin, everywhere, was the hum. Not pain now. Presence. She could feel the wind's intentions three valleys off. She could feel water in the ground below her, patient and cold. "Also I think I understand why people burn for this."

"There it is," Maren said grimly. "The exhilaration. Give it an hour before you trust it. Power always flatters you at first. So did my second husband."

Freya laughed, wet-eyed and shaky, and let Maren haul her upright.

*

They walked another hour, putting distance between Freya and the border, until Maren stopped at a stream in the shadow of the first real hills—hills, actual hills, land that rose and fell and had opinions—and built a small fire, which seemed reckless and which Maren defended with "we've hours before any hunter picks up a scent that strong, and you need tea."

The tea was, if possible, worse outdoors.

While it brewed, Maren reached into the folds of her coat and brought out something wrapped in oilcloth, which she unwrapped layer by layer with the ceremony of a woman who had rehearsed this moment for decades and intended to enjoy it.

Inside was a stone the size of a fist, and it was broken.

Not chipped—*broken*, sheared clean into three pieces long ago, though the pieces sat nested together as if reluctant to admit it. The stone was gray-green, veined with lines that caught the firelight and moved in it, faintly, like slow thoughts. When Freya leaned close, the hum under her skin answered it. She actually felt the pull, a lean in her chest toward the broken thing, the way a compass needle leans north.

"What is it?"

"One third of the covenant key. The oath-stone of the keepers of this realm." Maren turned it in the light. "There were three stones, one for each realm—earth, sea, sky. Three keepers, three stones, one binding. When the covenant was forged, each keeper broke their stone and scattered the pieces, so that no one person could unmake what all three had sworn. Mine I kept. The others went to Vess, in the drowned country, and to Ondu, beyond the mountains."

"And now the covenant's failing."

"Now the covenant's failing, and these—" she tapped the seam where the stone had broken, and the veins flickered angrily, like a sleeper disturbed, "—are waking up. Feel it?"

"I feel it."

"That's the key wanting to be whole again. It knows what it's for. Stones are better at remembering their purpose than people are." Maren wrapped it back up, all but a corner of it, and held it out. "Take it."

Freya didn't move. "Why me? You're the keeper. You signed it—you told me. You should carry it to Vess yourself."

Maren looked at her over the fire for a long moment.

"Girl, I am seventy-one years old, my knees sound like someone stepping on gravel, and I haven't left this valley in forty years because I *can't*—the seal holds me tighter than it holds you, since my name is written into it. If I cross that line I'll die before I reach the stream on the other side. I've known that for forty years. It's why I stayed. It's why I taught myself to be content with salt." She said it plainly, without self-pity, and somehow that was worse than weeping would have been. "You are young, you are stubborn, and as of this morning you are the strongest storm-caller in the living memory of this realm, whether you like it or not. The stone wants a keeper who can survive the road. It chose you. I'm only the messenger."

"The stone chose me."

"Stones have opinions. You'll learn." Maren pressed the bundle into her hands. It was warm. Warmer than it should have been, warm like a living thing, and the hum of it climbed up Freya's arms and settled somewhere behind her sternum, next to the storms, as if finding its place among them.

"So I find Sable in Vess," Freya said slowly, working it through. "And the third keeper in Ondu. And we bring the three stones together and reforge the covenant before the thirteenth moon."

"Before the thirteenth moon. Eleven days. And you'll do it while the crown hunts you, because as of this morning—" Maren nodded back toward the valley, toward the scorched circle in the grass, toward the whole loud fact of her, "—you are no longer hiding from them. You are what they hunt. Whatever you were running from for nine years, you're carrying it openly now, on your sleeve, humming like a struck pot."

Freya looked down at her own hands. They looked the same as always—salt-cracked knuckles, a scar on the left thumb from a rake handle. But they weren't the same. Nothing under the skin was the same. She could feel the weather gathering in the west, a fat low pressure rolling in off the far sea, and she could feel the oath-stone's warmth, and underneath both, quieter, the thing she refused to name yet: the terrifying, disgraceful, unmistakable feeling of *relief*.

Nine years of holding her breath. She hadn't realized how tired she was until she finally exhaled.

"I'm scared," she said.

"Good. Fear keeps the exhilaration honest." Maren sipped her terrible tea. "Now. Practical matters. In Vess you'll want to find the choir steps at low tide—the city's half-drowned, and the tide-speakers gather where the bells used to hang. Look for water moving against its nature. That's how they talk."

"Sable," Freya said. "What's she like?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. "She was the kindest of us," she said finally. "And the covenant will have cost her the most. Go gently with her." Then, briskly, before the softness could settle anywhere and become embarrassing: "And when you reach Ondu, don't sell any memories. I know how that city feeds travelers. A day seems cheap until it's gone."

"I wouldn't."

"Hm," said Maren, who had apparently decided that skepticism was a form of love.

*

They parted at the fork where the stream road met the mountain track, because Maren would go no farther—because going farther was dying, and because she had forty years of practice at stopping exactly where stopping was required.

Freya stood with her pack on her shoulders, the oath-stone heavy against her hip, the wind already combing through her hair with what felt like recognition, like the weather introducing itself to an old friend.

"Eleven days," Maren said. "Walk fast. Trust the stone. And Freya—" she paused, and for once all the dryness fell away from her voice entirely, and she sounded simply like an old woman watching a younger one walk into danger she could not share. "Whatever you find at the end of this road. Whoever you have to be to get there. Come back and tell me. I'll want to know how it ends, even if I have to haunt you to hear it."

"I'll tell you," Freya said. "Even if I have to haunt you first."

Maren snorted, which was her version of weeping, and turned back down the road toward the valley without looking again, because looking again was not something either of them could have survived.

Freya walked the other way.

North, toward the hills, toward Vess, toward a drowned city and a stranger's grief and eleven days on the world's shortest clock. Behind her the valley sank into its silver silence, and ahead the wind rose to meet her, testing her, tugging at her sleeves like a child wanting to show her something.

Under her ribs, the storms stirred—not hungry, not angry. Awake. Patient as salt.

Somewhere far to the east, she felt it before she saw it: a wrongness in the air, a thread of cold pulling against the natural grain of the world, like water flowing uphill. Faint. Deliberate. Moving toward her.

The hunters had picked up her scent.
