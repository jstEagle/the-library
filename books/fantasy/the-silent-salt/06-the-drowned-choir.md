## 6. The Drowned Choir

The road down to Vess smelled of rot and roses.

Freya noticed the contradiction before she understood it—sweetness layered over decay, like perfume dabbed on a corpse to fool the mourners. The cause announced itself an hour later, when the road crested a low hill and she saw the city for the first time: a crescent of pale towers rising straight out of green water, their lower stories drowned, their upper windows hung with laundry and lanterns and strings of drying fish that caught the light like coins.

"Half of Vess is under the sea," Maren had told her, back at the valley's edge, when Freya had asked what lay along the coast road. "And half of what's above water belongs to the drowned anyway. You'll understand when you see it."

Freya understood now. The flood was old—generations old, if the water-stains on the tower walls meant anything—and the people of Vess had simply... adapted. Boats replaced carts. Bridges spanned streets that had become canals. Children dove from second-floor balconies into brown-green water and surfaced laughing, hair slicked back, hauling up crabs for supper. Nobody seemed to grieve the drowned half of their city. They treated it like a relative who'd moved downstairs.

She stood on the hill a moment longer than she needed to, letting her legs remember they were tired instead of terrified.

Nine days out of the valley. Nine days of walking with a shattered oath-stone wrapped in wool at the bottom of her pack, feeling it hum against her spine whenever she slept. Nine days of magic living in her skin again—real magic, unfiltered by the valley's silence—and she still hadn't gotten used to it. It was like wearing a coat made of weather. Some hours the coat fit. Other hours it crackled and pulled and reminded her that storms didn't care about her comfort.

A cart driver heading down the hill gave her a look—the look travelers give a woman alone with a pack and a sword she clearly doesn't know how to use—and called out, "Ferry's at the bottom, miss! Two coppers to the Spire Quarter!"

"Thank you," Freya said, and then, because the question had been building since the hilltop: "Is there trouble in the city? I heard—" She'd heard nothing, actually. Maren had given her a name and a direction and very little else. *Sable. Tide-speaker. Find her before the crown does.*

"Trouble?" The driver laughed, missing teeth and all. "Only the usual. King's men took the harbor speaker last spring, so the tides don't get warned about anymore, and twice a season somebody drowns who shouldn't have. But that's not trouble, is it? That's just Tuesday." He clicked his tongue at his mule and rolled on.

Freya stood very still.

*Took the harbor speaker.*

Maren had said Sable still lived. Maren had said the stones would answer to three keepers, and one of them waited in Vess. But Maren had left the valley perhaps once in forty years, and her information might be as stale as the bread Freya carried.

*Find her before the crown does,* Maren had said. Perhaps Maren already knew she was too late.

Freya adjusted her pack and started downhill.

* * *

Vess by boat was a different city than Vess by road.

The ferryman was a broad woman with salt-cracked hands and a name she offered freely—Bett—and opinions she offered more freely still. She poled them through the canal where Market Street used to run, past shopfronts with their doors open underwater, kelp swaying in doorways like shopkeepers waving hello.

"First time in Vess?" Bett asked, reading Freya's face.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You're looking *down* at the water. Locals look across. Down means you're seeing what was lost. Across means you're seeing what's here." She shrugged. "Lost things make bad neighbors. Here things are better."

"What's here?"

Bett grinned and nodded toward a doorway below the waterline, where a school of silver fish poured through a ruined archway like liquid light. "That," she said, "is the Drowned Choir."

"The what?"

"Old cathedral, two streets down, all underwater now. And every evening at slack tide—" Bett tapped her pole against the hull twice, reverently, "—the fish sing. Not fish-singing. *Choir*-singing. Hymns, some say. Songs nobody's written. The priests used to call it the voice of the drowned congregation. Now we just call it the best free music in Vess."

"Fish don't sing hymns," Freya said, then winced, because nine years among salt-pans had apparently stripped her of imagination.

Bett only looked at her with the mild pity locals reserve for tourists. "No," she agreed. "Fish don't."

They passed under a bridge and out onto the open lagoon, and Freya saw the Spire Quarter: the highest ground, the richest towers, and—moored offshore, black hulls gleaming—a pair of crown ships flying the king's hunting standard. Her stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat.

"Tax collectors?" she asked, aiming for casual.

Bett spat over the side with tremendous precision. "Witch-hunters. Been moored there two months. Everyone thought they'd come for the choir—crown's been muttering about the singing being 'anomalous'—but mostly they take folk from the low quarters. Ones with gifts that never got declared. Or ones whose neighbors wanted their fishing spot." She said it lightly, but her knuckles were white around the pole. "You want the Salt Lantern inn, miss? That's where I'm headed."

"That's fine," Freya said, and spent the rest of the crossing watching the black ships and trying to breathe like someone who wasn't hunted.

* * *

The Salt Lantern was built on pilings over the shallows, its common room a wide deck open to the evening air, and it was full of exactly the kind of people Freya had hoped to find and dreaded needing: smugglers, salvagers, tide-readers, and at least two women whose eyes held the flat watchfulness of others hiding from the crown.

She paid for a room, bought a bowl of eel stew, and listened.

Listening, she was learning, was a skill the valley had taught her without her noticing. Nine years of keeping her head down and her gift buried had made her excellent at hearing what wasn't said aloud. So she sat in a corner with her stew and let the room talk to her.

The hunters had taken eleven people from the low quarters this month. They were paying salvage crews triple rate for "deep recovery work" near the old cathedral. There was a rumor—half price, because rumors about the crown always ran cheap—that whatever they were dredging for, it wasn't treasure.

And there was another rumor, whispered between a one-eyed salvage captain and a boy too young to be drinking:

"—the mute woman, out past the bell tower. The one who talks to the water. They tried to take her in spring, and the whole bay stood up and told them no."

"Told them *no*," the boy repeated skeptically.

"A wave the size of a church, boy. Came right up the channel and set their longboat down on the market roof like a mother putting a child to bed. Gently! That's the part that gets me. It could've smashed them. It *placed* them." The captain drank. "Then the water went flat as glass, and the mute woman went back to her nets, and the hunters decided they had urgent business elsewhere."

Freya's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

*The bay stood up.* She knew that feeling from the inside—the power moving through you, vast and careful at once, wanting to smash and choosing not to. It was the hardest thing magic ever asked of you. Harder than the killing. Harder even than the holding-back.

She found the salvage captain before he could drink himself under.

"The mute woman," she said. "Past the bell tower."

His one eye traveled over her—pack, sword, storm-grey eyes—and something in his face shifted from drunk to shrewd. "Friend of hers?"

"I need to be."

"Mm." He turned his cup slowly. "People who need to find Sable usually owe her or fear her. Which are you?"

"Neither," Freya said honestly. "I think I'm the third thing. I think I'm what she's been waiting for."

The captain studied her a long moment. Then he fished a bent copper nail from his pocket, dropped it into his ale, and watched it sink. "Bell tower's flooded to the second story. You go at slack tide, tonight, after the choir sings. Walk in dry. Ask the water politely—it likes manners. And tell her Odo still owes her for the winter of the red tide." He pushed the soggy nail aside and looked up. "One more thing, girl. If you're what I think you are—don't sing along with the choir. Whatever you hear down there. However much it feels like yours." His eye held hers, steady and sad. "It isn't. Not yet."

* * *

Slack tide came at moonrise, and the choir sang first.

Freya stood on the rim of the flooded cathedral—an enormous broken ring of stone rising out of dark water, its spire snapped off centuries ago, its windows empty sockets filled with moonlight—and she heard it begin.

It rose out of the water the way heat rises off summer stone. Not one song but many voices braided together, high and low, human-shaped and not quite human, harmonies that moved the way tides move, inevitable and patient. The silver fish she'd seen earlier gathered beneath the surface in spiraling columns, thousands of them, turning in patterns too precise to be accidental—as if the music were writing itself in fish.

It was beautiful. It was also, unmistakably, grief.

Not performed grief. Real grief—the kind that has nowhere to go, so it goes everywhere; the kind that fills a space the way water fills a cellar, quietly, completely, without asking permission. Freya stood at the edge of the flooded nave with her hand pressed over her mouth and felt nine years rise in her throat. The elder's face. Tam's daughter's small hand in hers. Her mother's kitchen. Everything she'd drowned in herself, everything the valley's silence had pressed flat, and now here it was again, sung back to her by a drowned church.

*Don't sing along,* Odo had said. *It isn't yours. Not yet.*

"Not yet," she whispered, and understood him suddenly. The choir was grief waiting to be claimed. One day—if she survived, if the covenant was reforged, if the stolen gifts rained back down on the world—maybe those songs would belong to the singers again. Tonight they were just an echo of everything taken.

When the last note settled into the water, the silence afterward was enormous.

Freya stepped down into the cathedral.

The water was ankle-deep inside—slack tide had drained the nave to a shining mirror that reflected the moon through the broken roof. She walked the drowned aisle carefully, past stone pillars furred with weed, past pews drifting loose like sleeping animals. Moonlight fell in silver bars. Somewhere ahead, in the apse where an altar must once have stood, a single lamp burned behind sea-glass.

"You walk well for someone carrying a storm," said a voice from the water itself.

Freya froze.

The voice hadn't come from a mouth. It had risen out of the flooded aisle beside her—a ripple speaking, a small standing wave shaping itself into words, soft and strange and slightly blurred, the way sound is blurred underwater.

"I was told to ask the water politely," Freya managed.

"And did you?"

"...I'm asking now. Please. I'm looking for Sable."

The ripple folded, unfolded. A pause, long enough that Freya wondered whether she'd failed some test. Then, from the shadow of the apse, a figure rose—tall, spare, grey-haired, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin gone stiff with salt. A woman with a face weathered as driftwood and eyes the exact green of deep water. She did not speak with her mouth. Her mouth stayed closed, calm, almost serene, while beside her a basin of seawater—carried somehow, held somehow—rippled upward into words.

"You're the one from the valley." Not a question. "Maren's letter arrived a month ago. Slowest post in the realm." The water-voice was dry as beach sand, and Freya realized, absurdly, that Sable was making a joke.

"She sent a letter?"

"Scribes cost money. Letters float." Sable tilted her head, studying Freya the way the tide studies a shoreline—patiently, taking measurements. "You have her stone. I can feel it humming. Third pocket on the left, wrapped in wool, because you're afraid of it."

Freya's hand twitched toward the pack before she could stop it.

"Don't apologize," the water said. "Fear of the thing you carry is the only sensible fear there is." Sable lifted the basin from its stand and walked slowly toward her, barefoot on the flooded stone, and up close Freya saw what the lamplight had hidden: a scar at the woman's throat, surgical and precise, running from jaw to collarbone. The mark the crown's drainings left when they took a gift out through the voice.

"They cut it out of me in the palace cellars eleven years ago," Sable said, and the basin rippled gently, gently, as though the words themselves needed soothing. "Tide-speaking lives in the throat, you see. They didn't know that. They thought any gift could be drawn out through any door. They learned otherwise on me—I nearly drowned the whole cellar block before they got the knife in. So they fixed their mistake." Her mouth smiled. Her eyes did not. "They kept the voice. It's in a jar somewhere under the palace, in the dark, with hundreds of others. I hear it sometimes. In dreams, the sea tells me these things."

Freya couldn't speak. Her throat had closed around something large and sharp.

This was the future Maren hadn't warned her about. Not death—the hunts didn't need to kill anymore, not when gifts could be *harvested*. This: walking around afterward, alive, complete except for the piece of yourself they kept. Speaking through a basin of water for the rest of your life because your own voice was in a jar in the dark, feeding—

*Feeding something,* Corvus Hale would have said. *Feeding the throne.*

"I saw the ships in the harbor," Freya said, when she could.

"Mm. They'll try for me again eventually. Waves only discourage a man so long; greed outlasts fear." Sable set the basin down on the altar stone and looked at Freya directly, and the water in the whole flooded nave went perfectly, unnaturally still, mirror-flat, attentive. "But you didn't cross the valley line and walk nine days to warn an old woman about ships. Show me the stone."

Freya unwrapped it with numb fingers. The shattered oath-stone sat in her palm, humming, its broken facets drinking the moonlight.

Sable reached out—not touching it, hovering close—and closed her eyes. The basin beside her began, very softly, to sing. The same harmony as the choir. The same grief. And Freya understood, with a jolt that went through her like lightning finding ground, that the Drowned Choir wasn't fish at all.

It was the stolen gifts.

Every voice the crown had drained and bottled under the palace leaked, somehow, into the sea, and the sea carried the leakage home to Vess, and the drowned cathedral sang it back every night at slack tide—a requiem sung by the taken, for the taken, heard by almost no one who understood it.

"You hear them too," Sable said quietly, opening her eyes. "Good. Then you already know why the covenant matters. It isn't about sealing realms apart, girl. It never was. It's about keeping magic *free*—belonging to the land and the blood that carries it, not to kings with jars in their cellars." The water-voice hardened, waves gathering at the edges of each word. "My voice is in one of those jars. Three hundred more are stacked beside it. And every lunar turn, the seal weakens, and the thing they feed grows stronger, and the jars fill faster."

"So you'll help me." Freya held out the stone. "The covenant needs three keepers. You, me, and a mountain-wright named Ivo, somewhere east. Reforge it before the thirteenth moon, and—"

"And the payment," Sable said.

Freya's arm stopped mid-offer. "...What payment?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. The basin sang its sad chord. When she answered, the water had gone gentle again, the gentleness of someone delivering news they've had eleven years to make peace with.

"Maren didn't tell you? No. Of course she didn't. She never could say it aloud either." The tide-speaker looked up at the broken roof, at the moon pouring through. "Covenants aren't sealed with good intentions, child. They're sealed with weight. Something of equal measure to what binds. The old oath-keepers paid in years of life. The one before them paid in names." Her green eyes came back down and rested on Freya, kind and terrible.

"When you reforge it, the covenant will ask you for a memory."

The flooded nave was silent. Even the water in the basin had stopped singing.

"It will weigh everything you carry," Sable said, "and it will take the heaviest thing. So walk the road knowing that. Whatever you love most when you reach the rift—that's what it costs."

Freya stood in the drowned church with a shattered stone in her hand and the moonlight cold on her shoulders, and she thought of the valley—Tam's laugh, the salt wind, nine years of quiet mornings—and felt the first true terror of the journey settle over her, heavier than any hunter's arrow.

Behind Sable, out in the dark lagoon, a lantern flared on the water.

Then a second. A third. A slow procession of lights, moving toward the cathedral in formation—and from the direction of the harbor, faint but unmistakable, the creak of oars from black-hulled ships getting underway.

Sable's basin flared upright, every ripple sharpened to a blade-edge.

"Well," said the water. "They've stopped being discouraged."
