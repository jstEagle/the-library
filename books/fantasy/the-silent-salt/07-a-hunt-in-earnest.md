## 7. A Hunt in Earnest

The report from Vess arrived on Corvus Hale's desk at dawn, smelling of brine and bureaucratic panic.

He read it twice, standing, because his clerk had left the candle stubs untrimmed again and the light was too poor to read sitting down. *Unregistered surge of elemental force, district of the Drowned Choir. Three fishing skiffs lifted by an unnatural swell and set down unharmed. One warehouse wall collapsed outward, as if pushed by wind from within. Witnesses describe thunder with no cloud.*

Corvus set the paper down and looked out the window at the capital waking below—the smoke of ten thousand hearths rising straight up into a windless sky.

Thunder with no cloud. In Vess, where no storm had broken in living memory, because Vess sat under a sky that had forgotten how.

"Well," he said to the empty office, "that's inconvenient."

His clerk, Pell, poked his head in. Pell was nineteen, earnest, and had the singular gift of appearing exactly when Corvus was about to say something he'd regret being overheard saying. "Sir?"

"Pack for travel. We're going to the coast."

Pell's face did the thing it always did when travel was mentioned—a flicker of dread quickly smothered by duty. "The whole coast, sir? Or—"

"Vess."

"Ah." Pell swallowed. "The one that smells like fish forever, then."

"The very one. Pack the good boots. And Pell—if anyone asks where we've gone, we're inspecting lighthouse maintenance."

"We don't maintain lighthouses, sir."

"No," Corvus agreed, shrugging into his coat. "But nobody knows that, which is rather the point of a lie."

* * *

Vess greeted him with its usual charm: streets half-drowned in green water, bridges where roads should be, and citizens who traveled by gondola and gossiped about everything and told the crown precisely nothing. The city had been flooded two generations ago when the river changed its mind, and instead of leaving, the people of Vess had simply... adapted, as if drowning were a minor administrative inconvenience.

Corvus stood on the quay outside the collapsed warehouse and let the evidence speak.

It spoke clearly, if you knew how to listen. He crouched at the base of the broken wall and pressed his palm flat against the stone. Residue clung to it—not dust, not rot, but something finer. A charge. It prickled faintly against his skin, like the air before a summer storm, except this was weeks old and should have faded.

Someone had called power here. Real power, raw and enormous, and then—this was the part that interested him—they had *stopped*. Pulled the blow. A wall shoved outward, three boats set down gently, not a single body among the casualties. Whoever this was had enough force to level a city block and had chosen, deliberately, to break as little as possible.

That was either mercy or discipline, and in Corvus's experience both were rarer than the power itself.

"Terrible business, sir," said the harbormaster, a round man whose beard appeared to have small fishhooks woven into it as decoration or possibly habitat. "Never seen anything like it. Sky went strange, water stood up on end, and then—" he made a gesture suggesting the sea had politely apologized, "—all calm again. Like it never happened."

"Like it never happened," Corvus repeated. "Did anything else happen that also never happened? A stranger passing through, perhaps. Someone asking questions. Someone who didn't quite belong."

The harbormaster's eyes slid toward the water, which was the Vessian equivalent of a shrug. "Lots of strangers come through Vess, m'lord. City of travelers, see. Canals go everywhere."

"Someone traveling toward Ondu, then. Or away from the valley road."

Something moved behind the harbormaster's eyes—quick, there and gone—and Corvus had spent twenty years learning to catch exactly that movement. People always looked toward the thing they were protecting. It was a law of nature, like water running downhill.

"You saw someone," Corvus said, not unkindly. "And you liked them."

"I never said—"

"You didn't have to. You looked at the canal, and your face went soft. That's not nothing, friend. That's memory doing what memory does." He let the silence sit a moment. Then, gently: "I'm not going to drag them back in chains. I need to find them before others do—others who aren't me. You understand what I mean."

The harbormaster did understand. Everyone in the realm understood what Corvus meant, these days; it was why they lied to him so poorly and so constantly. He was the reasonable hunter, the fair one, the one who gave warnings instead of ropes. The king's men had learned to use that reputation the way sailors used a lighthouse—to steer around it.

After a long moment, the harbormaster sighed the sigh of a man betraying someone he'd known for less than a day. "Woman. Dark-haired. Kept to herself. Bought passage east on a memory-barge—no papers, paid in coin she shouldn't have had. And—" he hesitated, "—there was another one with her. Older woman. Gray. They argued about which barge to take, quiet-like, but I've got ears, haven't I."

"Gray-haired older woman," Corvus said slowly. "Traveling with a stranger who can call storms out of clear skies."

"Aye."

Corvus turned that over. A witch alone was a fugitive. A witch with a guide—an escort, a teacher—was something else entirely. Someone was moving her along a route, deliberately, toward Ondu. Toward the memory market. Which meant whoever planned her journey knew things about her that even she might not know.

"Which barge?" he asked. "And how many days ahead?"

* * *

That night, in a rented room above a tavern that smelled overwhelmingly of fried eel, Corvus opened the case he'd carried across the realm and hadn't opened in nine years.

It wasn't sentiment. He told himself that every time, and every time the telling was a little less convincing. It was reference material. A professional kept his archives.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the records of the Almeida Massacre—or the Elder's Death, or the Storm of Hollowmere, depending on which document you consulted, because bureaucracies loved a tragedy best when they could argue about what to call it. Nine years ago, in a village on the edge of the Salt Valley, an elder named Brannoc had died torn apart by lightning in front of thirty witnesses. The girl responsible—sixteen years old, a farm laborer's daughter—had fled into the Valley before the king's riders reached the village, and the Valley, being what it was, had closed behind her like water over a stone.

Corvus had been a junior hunter then, twenty-eight and hungry, assigned to carry the file and ask questions. His superior had closed the case within a month. *Fugitive beyond reach. Valley is magic-dead. Nothing to do but wait for her to die of old age among the salt.* Case stamped, shelved, forgotten by everyone except the family of the dead elder and, apparently, Corvus.

He laid the pages out on the tavern table, weighted at the corners with his cup, his knife, a candlestick, and Pell's abandoned dinner roll.

There were witness statements. There was a sketch of the girl—bad, all chin and shadow, drawn from frightened memory. But most valuable, to Corvus's mind, was the eighth page: a weather-mage's assessment, commissioned after the fact, describing the *signature* of the killing bolt.

Every worker of storms had a signature. It was like handwriting, like a heartbeat. The way power gathered, the shape of its release, the residue it left in stone and air. Most signatures were common—rain-callers and wind-whisperers blended together like drops in a bucket. But some were distinct. Some were singular.

And the mage's description of the Hollowmere bolt had made Corvus uneasy even at twenty-eight, though he hadn't had the words for it then. *A gathering pattern unlike any catalogued form,* the old man had written. *Triple-spiral intake, discharge in a single vertical column, followed by complete atmospheric silence within moments. I have seen nothing like it in forty years of study. I hope never to see it again.*

Corvus took out the residue-rubbing he'd taken from the warehouse wall that afternoon—pressed onto treated paper, the way he'd been taught—and held it beside the mage's description.

Triple-spiral intake. Discharge in a column. And afterward, that uncanny stillness, the air scrubbed clean, the storm swallowed whole.

The same. After nine years, after the Valley, after everything—the same hand. The same heart, if a storm could be said to have one.

"Pell," he said.

From the next bed, where Pell had been pretending to sleep with the dedication of a much younger boy, came a muffled "Sir?"

"What do you know about the Salt Valley?"

A pause. "It's flat, sir. And full of salt. And nothing happens there, ever, which sounds lovely compared to Vess."

"Nothing happened there," Corvus corrected. "Past tense matters, Pell. Remember that—it'll save your career someday." He began packing the file back into its case, page by page, with the care other men reserved for holy relics. "Nine years ago a girl killed a man with lightning and vanished into that valley. Today a woman in Vess threw a storm with the exact same fingerprint. Do you follow me?"

Another pause, longer. When Pell spoke again, the sleepiness was gone. "You think it's her. The one from the file."

"I don't think. Thinking is for philosophers and men with leisure." Corvus buckled the case shut. "I *know*, the way you know rain's coming by the smell of it. She's left the valley. She's moving east with a guide who knows the roads, and she's headed for Ondu, and she has no idea—" he stopped himself, reconsidered, amended, "—or perhaps she has every idea, which would be worse, that the thirteenth moon is coming."

"The moon, sir?"

"There are thirteen turns in the cycle this season, Pell. Hasn't happened in a hundred years. And when the thirteenth moon crests—" he waved a hand vaguely eastward, encompassing everything he suspected and none of what he could prove, "—things change. Old things wake. The king is... attentive to it. Let's leave it there."

Pell was quiet for a moment. Then, in the careful tone of a young man approaching thin ice: "Sir, can I ask something? About the girl. In the file. If it's her—she was sixteen, wasn't she? When it happened?"

"She was sixteen."

"And the man she—" Pell stopped, started again. "I read the statements while you were at the harbor. All thirty witnesses. They all agree on one thing, sir. Nobody says she looked angry. They say she looked *scared*. Like the lightning came out of her the way a sneeze comes out of you. Unwanted."

Corvus paused in the doorway, his travel cloak already over his shoulders, the case in his hand. Outside the shuttered window, Vess glittered—lamplight doubled in black water, the whole drowned city burning like a reflection of somewhere better.

"That's the trouble with this work, Pell," he said finally. "Everyone you hunt has a reason. The guilty ones have the best reasons of all. Read the statements again tomorrow and tell me what Brannoc was doing in that square with thirty witnesses and a girl he'd summoned specially. Tell me what kind of elder calls a public gathering and doesn't tell anyone why." He pulled the door open. "Then ask yourself why my superiors closed the case so fast, and why the file's been locked in a drawer for nine years, and why nobody at court wanted me looking at it."

"But—you're looking at it now, sir."

"Yes," said Corvus Hale, chief hunter of the realm, stepping out into the salt-smelling dark. "I am. And I'd very much like to know why that feels less like duty and more like digging up something somebody buried."

* * *

They rode east through the gray hours before dawn, hooves muffled in fog, following a canal road that ran arrow-straight toward Ondu. At the first toll bridge, Corvus paused and asked the night-keeper a single question: had a dark-haired woman passed, traveling with an older woman, gray?

She had. Four days ago. Paid double for speed and asked which inns along the route kept their mouths shut.

Four days. Corvus did the arithmetic against the pace of a memory-barge and the whims of canal traffic, and came out with a gap that was closing but not yet closed. Two days, perhaps three, if the weather held—and it would hold, he thought grimly, because the only person in the realm who might have broken it was the person he was chasing, and she seemed determined to leave the sky undisturbed.

At the far end of the bridge, he reined in and looked back once at Vess, sinking into the mist behind them. Somewhere in his chest, beneath twenty years of professional calm, something stirred that he refused to name. It felt uncomfortably like anticipation, and anticipation was a luxury for men who hadn't read the eighth page of a massacre file as carefully as he had.

Because here was the truth he kept folded away with the oilcloth: he had never believed the official story. Not at twenty-eight, not now. A girl doesn't summon lightning out of a clear heart. Something had happened in that square at Hollowmere—something the witnesses had seen and the record had sanded smooth—and the girl had run not from justice but from *questions*, and the kingdom had preferred her gone to her answering them.

Now the storm-hand had surfaced again, nine years later, breaking walls with restraint and sparing every life it touched. That wasn't the behavior of a murderer settling into her nature.

That was the behavior of someone running out of road.

"Hold up, sir?" Pell called from behind. "Or push through the night?"

Corvus looked east. Beyond the fog, beyond the drowned country and the salt flats, the road ran on toward Ondu and whatever waited there—and beyond Ondu, if the trail bent that way, the pale line of the Salt Valley, where a name had been sleeping for nine years.

"Push through," he said. "We've caught a scent, Pell, and I mean to run it to ground. By the time the thirteenth moon rises, I'll have a face to put to the storm. Her name."

He said it aloud for the first time, tasting it, and the fog seemed to lean closer to listen.

"Freya Almeida."
