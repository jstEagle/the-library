## 8. The Memory Market

Ondu smelled of cinnamon and old paper, which Freya would later think was a strange thing for a city to smell like when its chief export was people's lives.

She came down off the caravan road at dusk, her boots worn through at the heel from three weeks of walking, the shattered oath-stone wrapped in wool against her breastbone. Maren's instructions had been simple enough: *Go to Ondu. Find the House of Amber Glass. Do not sell anything you cannot bear to lose.* Maren had said it lightly, the way she said most things, but then she'd held Freya's wrist a moment too long before letting go.

The bazaar-city did not sleep so much as hum. Lanterns hung on every wire like low-hanging fruit, and beneath them the stalls sold everything a person could carry out of their own head: first kisses in little corked vials, a soldier's last morning at home decanted into a stoppered jar that glowed faintly gold, a woman's memory of her daughter learning to walk, priced high and haggled over by a childless couple who kept touching the glass.

Freya stopped in front of that stall longer than she meant to.

"You want it?" the vendor asked. He had kind eyes and the flat voice of someone who had watched ten thousand people decide what they could live without. "It's genuine. The girl's grown now, sells fish two streets over. She'll never remember her mother teaching her."

"That's—" Freya swallowed. "That's terrible."

"It's commerce." He shrugged. "Everything true is terrible if you stare at it long enough. You selling or buying?"

"Passing through." Which was becoming less and less true by the hour, because passage out of Ondu toward the eastern passes—the only road to Ivo, the third keeper—cost more than Freya had. She had exactly one coin, half a loaf of bread gone stale in her pack, and a storm coiled under her ribs that she dared not spend where Corvus Hale might be listening for it.

*Do not sell anything you cannot bear to lose.*

She found a bench by the fountain in the Amber Quarter and ate her stale bread and made a list, because lists were how Freya Almeida survived things. Nine years of exile had taught her that grief was bearable in columns.

What she owned, in memories:

- The valley. Every salt pan, every dawn, Tam's terrible singing, the taste of the bread at the common table. *Not for sale.* That was nine years of quiet, the only home she'd been allowed to keep.
- The killing. The elder's face. The child screaming behind him. *Not for sale,* though some mornings she'd have paid good money to be rid of it.
- Her mother.

Here the list went soft at the edges.

Her mother had died the winter before the massacre, before the exile, before everything. Freya had been nineteen and furious at the world in general and her mother in particular, because her mother had spent that last year trying to marry her off to a cooper's son with kind hands and no imagination, and Freya had wanted storms, wanted the sea, wanted anything but the small gray town where the sky never did anything interesting.

Then there had been one afternoon—one golden, unremarkable afternoon—that Freya had carried with her through nine years of silence like an ember cupped in both hands.

It had started badly. Her mother had caught her practicing small weather behind the smokehouse—just a breeze, just a shiver of raincloud no bigger than a table—and instead of the sermon Freya expected, her mother had sat down on the chopping block, patted the space beside her, and said, "Show me again. Slower this time. I want to see how you do it."

And they had stayed there until the light went amber. Her mother asking questions. *Does it feel like pulling? Like breathing? Does it hurt?* And Freya, who had never once been asked a gentle question about the thing that lived in her, answering all of them, and laughing, actually laughing, when her mother tried to make a cloud herself by waving her arms and got nothing but a face full of her own hair.

"I always knew," her mother had said near the end, quietly, watching the little cloud drift and dissolve. "Since you were small. I prayed I was wrong, and then I prayed I was right, and I don't know what that says about me." She'd taken Freya's hand, flour still on her knuckles. "Whatever comes of it—and something will come of it, girls like you don't get to stay small—you remember this afternoon. Not the sermons. Not the fear. This."

Freya remembered it every day. Some days it was the only thing that felt like proof she had ever been loved without conditions.

She looked down at her list. The ink had blurred slightly where a drop of water—fountain spray, she told herself firmly, fountain spray—had fallen on the page.

*Do not sell anything you cannot bear to lose.*

"Well," Freya said aloud, to no one, in the way of people who have talked to themselves for three weeks on the road. "I can't lose the valley. It's the only thing keeping me sane." A pause. "I can't sell the bad day. Nobody would buy it anyway."

She folded the list away and did not let herself think the third sentence, because thinking it would make it real, and not thinking it gave her one more hour of pretending there was another way.

There wasn't another way. There was a caravan leaving at dawn for the eastern passes, and a caravan master who took payment in memory, and a gate tax, and a guide's fee, and Freya had a coin and half a loaf.

* * *

The House of Amber Glass sat at the end of a lane so narrow the lanterns overhead nearly touched, and it was neither a house nor made of amber nor, strictly speaking, glass. It was a long low building of dark wood with windows of honey-colored resin, and inside it smelled the way the whole city wished it smelled: warm, sweet, safe, like every kitchen you'd ever been happy in, concentrated.

The broker was a small round man with spectacles and silver hair and the demeanor of a favorite uncle, which Freya suspected was a professional asset rather than an accident of personality.

"Traveler," he said warmly. "You have the look of a woman who needs to leave Ondu and cannot afford to. Am I close?"

"You're close."

"They always have the look." He gestured her into a chair across a counter of polished amber wood. "Now. Before we begin, the rules, which I will say quickly because everyone pretends to listen and nobody does. One: you choose what to sell. I do not choose, I do not suggest, and if you ask me what I'd sell, I will tell you my mother's funeral, which is already gone and which I regret daily. Two: extraction is painless. Removal is permanent. Three—" and here his voice lost a degree of its warmth, not cruelly, just honestly, "—you will not know what you paid until it is gone. Memory is the only currency whose value is assessed after spending. Choose carefully."

Freya's hands were steady. She noted this with distant surprise. Somewhere far away, a version of her was screaming, but the version sitting in the chair had learned, over nine years, how to put the screaming in a room and shut the door.

"I want to sell one day," she said. "A single day."

"A day is a large denomination. Days are expensive to us—we pay well for them. What day?"

And here was the trap she'd built for herself on the fountain bench, laid out plain. Because she had told herself, all evening, a story: *sell a bad day.* Sell the day of the killing, or the day the hunters' arrow landed in the salt, or the gray Tuesday her mother lectured her about duty. Sell something heavy and be lighter for it. Everyone in this city did it; surely it was survivable; surely—

But the oath-stone pulsed faintly against her chest, and Maren's voice was in her ear—*the covenant runs on memory, child, everything true does*—and Freya understood, with the awful clarity that visits right before you do the wrong thing, that the stone would know. That whatever she fed this transaction should be worth what she bought. That selling her worst day to buy her escape was a kind of cheating, and covenants and cheats did not mix.

Or perhaps that was just the story she told afterward. Freya would wonder about this for years, standing outside a valley she couldn't name.

"One day," she said again. "An afternoon, really. In late summer. My mother's garden. We were making a cloud together."

The broker's pen paused. He looked up, and for a moment the uncle-warmth fell away entirely, and what was underneath was older and sadder and much, much better at his job than he pretended.

"Traveler," he said gently, "that is the day you keep. People come here to sell the days they keep. It's why they're worth so much."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because in thirty years I have watched perhaps four hundred people sit in that chair and say those words, and I can count on one hand the ones who walked out glad."

"And the rest?"

"The rest come back." He set down his pen. "They come back years later, having sold other things around the hole, and they stand in my doorway looking at me like I stole something, and they say, *what did I sell you*, and I tell them, and they say *no, that can't be right, I wouldn't have*, and I show them the ledger, and they cry in my shop, and I pour them tea, because that is also part of the job." He folded his hands. "So I am going to ask you one time, and then I will do whatever you say, because it is your head and your coin and none of my business. Are you certain?"

The screaming in the locked room got louder.

Freya thought about the eastern passes, and Ivo in his mountain cellars, and the thirteenth moon climbing its slow ladder toward fullness, and Sable waiting in drowned Vess with her water-words, and a king feeding something under his palace with the souls of the gifted. She thought about the covenant. About weight. About what things cost.

Some prices, Maren hadn't warned her, are only understood afterward. But some you understand exactly, fully, in the moment—and pay anyway, with your eyes open, because the alternative is worse. That was the part nobody sang songs about. The brave payments were the ones you made knowing.

"Yes," Freya said. "One afternoon. Late summer. A cloud the size of a table."

* * *

The extraction chair was upholstered in green velvet gone bald at the arms. The broker fitted a circlet of copper and amber around her brow, warm as bathwater, and told her to think of the day—not to hold it, just to look at it, the way you'd look at a bird you intended to release.

It was easy. It was always easy to look at that day.

Amber light. Flour on her mother's knuckles. *Show me again. Slower this time.* The smell of cut grass and woodsmoke. Her mother waving her arms at the sky like a scarecrow dancing, getting nothing but a mouthful of her own hair, and Freya laughing so hard her ribs hurt, and the small cloud drifting between them like a secret, and her mother's hand closing over hers, warm, warm—

"There it is," the broker murmured. "Beautiful piece. Full sensory. Those carry best." A soft chime. "Payment rendered to the caravan office under the name you gave. Gate fees waived. Guide arranged for dawn. Traveler? Traveler, we're finished. You can open your eyes."

Freya opened her eyes.

The shop was the same. The broker was the same, counting coins into a leather purse with the brisk contentment of a man concluding honest business. The circlet was being lifted from her brow. Everything was exactly where it had been.

Except.

Except there was a shape in her chest where something used to be, and the shape was shaped like nothing, which was the cruelest trick of it, because you cannot grieve a nothing. You can only feel the draft blowing through.

"Is it—" Her voice came out wrong. Smaller. "Did it work?"

"Perfectly. You sold one afternoon, late summer, a garden." He slid the purse across. "You'll feel hollowed for a few hours. Eat something sweet. Sleep if you can. And traveler—" he hesitated, pen hovering over his ledger, and for one unguarded second he looked like a man who poured a great deal of tea, "—do you want to write it down? Before it fades? Just a note. *I sold a day with my mother. It was worth it.* People who write it down fare better."

Freya stared at her own hands.

Somewhere in her there had been a garden. She knew this the way you know a word in a language you've forgotten—knowing the shape of the knowledge without the knowledge. Her mother had died the winter before the exile. Of that she was certain. And there had been a day, one day, that she had carried through everything since, and it was gone, and she could not even mourn it properly because mourning requires remembering, and what remained was only the fact of loss itself, clean and total, like a tooth-socket.

"No," she heard herself say. "No note."

"Traveler—"

"If I write it down, I'll read it every day." She stood. Her legs worked. That seemed obscene somehow, that her legs simply worked. "If I read it every day, it'll be a wound instead of a price. Let it be a price."

The broker wrote something in his ledger—she never learned what—and showed her out, and the lane with its touching lanterns received her back into the humming crowd, and Ondu went on smelling of cinnamon and old paper, and somewhere a woman was haggling over a vial of somebody's first kiss, and none of it, none of it, had ever mattered less or more.

* * *

She didn't go straight to the caravan office. She walked. Past the fountain where she'd made her list—she checked the page and found the column labeled *my mother* and felt nothing where the ember had been, and had to grip the bench to stay upright. Past the stall with the memory of the daughter learning to walk. Past a hundred lanterns hanging like fruit she could no longer pick.

At some point she realized she was crying without any feeling attached to the tears, which was somehow worse than sobbing, and she found a wall and leaned against it and let the tears happen the way weather happens, without her, while she stood apart and watched.

*A day is a large denomination. Days are expensive.*

She thought: *I'll buy it back someday. When the covenant is reforged. I'll find the ledger, I'll pay whatever—*

But even as she thought it she heard Maren's voice: *the seal takes a payment of equal weight, child. Equal. Nothing borrowed, nothing returned.* And beneath that, colder, the truth she was only beginning to see the edges of: if the covenant demanded one afternoon now, what would it demand at the end? What was a whole valley worth?

Freya wiped her face, squared her pack, and went to find the caravan master's office, because dawn comes whether you're ready or not, and the thirteenth moon was turning.

She was three steps from the office door when a boy peeled off a wall opposite—a thin, quick boy with the bored eyes of a professional—and fell into step beside her.

"You're the storm-lady," he said, too quietly for anyone else to hear. "From the salt valley. Someone pays good silver to know where you sleep tonight."
