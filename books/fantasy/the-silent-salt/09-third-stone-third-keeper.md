## 9. Third Stone, Third Keeper

Ondu smelled of cinnamon and hot iron and, underneath it all, of other people's lives. That was the memory trade for you. A city built on the buying and selling of yesterday ran a little sweet and a little scorched, like bread left too long in an oven by someone who'd sold the memory of how to bake.

Freya had been there four days and she still flinched every time a vendor called out *fresh recollections, warm from the skull!* She kept her hood up and her coin pouch inside her coat and her face angled away from the memory-brokers' stalls, because brokers could taste what you were carrying if you let them look at you too long. Her remaining memories felt lighter now. Thinner. Like a purse with one coin missing—you noticed the absence every time you reached for it.

She tried not to reach for it.

The afternoon her mother had died was gone. Not softened, not dimmed—*gone*, scooped out clean as a pit from a plum, and in its place was a smooth blank wall that her mind kept bumping into like a drunk in a dark hallway. She knew the day existed. She knew its shape from the outside: her mother had been shelling peas on the step, and there had been rain coming in off the hills, and Freya had been seventeen and impatient about something so small she couldn't even remember what the small thing *was*. That was the cruelty of it. The broker hadn't taken the grief. He'd taken the *afternoon*—the light, the smell of the pea shells, whatever her mother's last words to her had been—and left Freya holding a hole with edges sharp enough to cut.

"Never sell a day you love," Maren had told her once, back when Freya still thought the old woman's warnings were just the mutterings of age. "Sell a Tuesday. Sell a toothache. Never sell anything with a person in it."

Freya had sold a day with her mother in it because it had seemed safe. Because she'd thought, *that day is already painful, what worse can losing it do?*

She knew better now. Some prices are only legible after they're paid.

* * *

The prison of Ondu was not called a prison. It was called the House of Honest Exchange, which was the sort of name that made Freya want to set something on fire on principle. It sat on the city's north rim where the terraces climbed toward the mountain passes, a squat granite block with windows like coin slots and a courtyard where, three times a day, the wardens walked the inmates in circles so the memory-brokers' appraisers could look them over through the fence.

Ivo Brannick was in the third circle.

Freya had spent two days confirming it, and the confirmation had cost her almost nothing, which was itself suspicious. Everyone in Ondu knew about the mountain-wright who wouldn't sell. He was a minor legend in the bazaars—the wright whose gift could read stone the way sailors read weather, who could tell you where a mountain would crack fifty years before it cracked, and who had stood in front of the appraisers and said no. Not angrily, by all accounts. The story everyone told was that he'd thanked them politely for their offer and then asked whether they'd be willing to buy his hands instead, since those were attached to the gift and he'd hate to separate them.

They'd given him ten years for contempt of commerce.

"Contempt of commerce," Sable would have signed, if Sable had been there, and then made the gesture that meant something between *absurd* and *typical*. But Sable was a league behind them in Vess, resting, guarding the second oath-stone wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, and so Freya stood alone at a fruit stall across the road from the House of Honest Exchange, buying apples she didn't want, and watching the third circle go around.

He was easy to pick out even at distance. The other prisoners shuffled. Ivo walked like a man inspecting a foundation—slow, deliberate, eyes down but not defeated, cataloguing. When he passed a crack in the courtyard flagstones he stopped, crouched, pressed one broad thumb into it, and nodded as though the crack had confirmed something he'd already suspected. The warden prodded him along. Ivo went, but Freya noticed he tapped the flagstone twice with his knuckle first, some mason's goodbye or some mason's promise.

*That,* she thought, *is the man who holds the third stone.*

Maren's letter—or rather, the letter Maren had arranged to have waiting in Vess, because Maren never did anything so traceable as writing—had described him precisely: *The third keeper is a wright of the Karst Mountains. Stubborn as bedrock and half as talkative. His stone is set in his left forearm; he broke his own bone to hide it when the crown's men came. If he trusts you, it will take time. You do not have time.*

No, she didn't. Eleven nights until the thirteenth moon. Two stones gathered—one shattered in Maren's keeping, one whole and humming faintly under Sable's oilcloth. One to go, and it was locked inside a man inside a fortress inside a city that ran on selling pieces of yourself.

Freya paid for her unwanted apples and went to find out how much a prison cost to open.

* * *

The answer was: less than she expected, and more than she could afford honestly.

Bribing a warden was simple. Bribing a warden in a city full of memory-brokers was complicated, because the natural currency was memory, and Freya had sworn on Maren's grave-dirt—Maren wasn't dead, but the phrase had felt appropriately solemn—that she would not sell another day with a person in it. Which left coin, and she didn't have enough coin to make a warden risk his post.

But she didn't need a warden to risk his post. She needed him to *leave* it.

The plan, such as it was, assembled itself over two evenings of sitting in cheap taverns listening to guards complain, which Freya had decided was the most underrated form of intelligence-gathering in the world. The House of Honest Exchange had one weakness and it was not a wall or a lock. It was the cistern. Ondu's north rim had no spring; water came up the cliffside in an aqueduct older than the crown, and the prison's cistern was fed by a wooden sluice-gate that jammed in dry weather. Every guard complained about it. The sluice-gate was on the aqueduct side of the wall, accessible from the terrace path, and when it jammed, someone had to climb out and unjam it, and the someone was always the youngest warden, and the youngest warden hated it more than he hated anything except being spoken to before noon.

All of which meant that on the third evening, when the sky over the Karst foothills went the particular bruised grey that Freya had known her whole life the way other people knew their own pulse, the youngest warden looked at the clouds, looked at the sluice-gate, and sighed.

Freya had made those clouds. It had taken her most of the afternoon, coaxing moisture up the valley air with fingers of pressure so gentle they barely qualified as magic—a whisper of a storm, a suggestion of rain, nothing any hunter could read as a signature. Just weather. Weather happened. Even in a world going wrong, weather was allowed.

The warden climbed the ladder to the sluice-gate. The gate, which Freya had ensured was jammed with a branch she'd wedged there herself that morning, required both his hands and all his attention. The yard gate that admitted workers to the cistern channel was latched but not locked, because who would be fool enough to break *into* a prison?

Freya, apparently. She'd been rehearsing that thought all day to keep her hands steady.

She went over the wall in the gap between patrol rounds, dropped into the channel, and came up in the shadow of the cistern outflow with her heart going like a drum in a parade. Above her, the warden cursed at the branch. Somewhere below, in the cells, the third circle would be settling into its evening count.

Now the hard part. Now the part where she needed a storm after all.

Because the cell locks of the House of Honest Exchange weren't locks, exactly. They were bindings—commerce-magic, contract-iron, the same school of craft that powered the memory-trade. You couldn't pick them. You couldn't bribe them. They answered only to paid debts, and Ivo Brannick owed the House ten years.

But iron answered to storms too. Old law, older than contracts. Lightning was the sky collecting what it was owed, and every smith and wright knew you didn't work cold iron in thunder weather unless you wanted the metal remembering things.

Freya stood in the cistern shadow, closed her eyes, and reached for the smallest storm she had ever tried to build.

It was like threading a needle with lightning. She gathered charge the way you'd gather breath to blow out a single candle—not the great rolling pressure of the storm that had killed Elder Voss, not the screaming front she'd loosed over the pass at Corvus Hale's hunters, but a thread. A filament of voltage, thin as spider silk, aimed down through the prison's iron roof-beam, into the lock-works of one specific cell, third row, second door.

She let it go.

The thunder, when it came, was a *click*. A sound like a very large, very dignified key turning. Rain began to fall on the terrace, soft and businesslike, covering everything.

In the dark of the cell block, a door swung open, and a voice that sounded like gravel being polite said, "Well. Either I've finally lost my mind, or the weather's come to call."

* * *

Getting Ivo Brannick out of the cell turned out to be the easiest part of the night, mostly because Ivo Brannick had been planning his own escape for nine months and simply lacked one impossible ingredient, which was a door that opened.

"The lock's contract-iron," he'd said, appearing in the corridor with the unhurried air of a man checking on a damp cellar rather than escaping a prison. "Can't be picked, can't be forced. Been telling them for months they ought to worry about lightning. Nobody listens to the stonemason about metal." He'd looked at her then—really looked, the way he'd looked at the crack in the flagstones—and said, "You're the storm-caller. From the Salt Valley."

"I need your stone," Freya had whispered. "And then I need you to run, and I'm sorry, and there's no time to explain—"

"There's always time to explain badly," Ivo had said. "Come on. Count happens in six minutes and the night warden takes his tea at the east window like clockwork, because he's a creature of habit and habits are load-bearing walls, girl. This way."

So they went his way, which was faster than hers, because he'd spent nine months memorizing the building the way he memorized everything: cracks, loads, weaknesses, the exact spot on the second-floor landing where the flagstone rang hollow and could be pried with a spoon. They went out through the laundry chute, which undignified them both, and over the wall during the change of watch, and down the terrace path into the olive groves while the rain fell gently and the youngest warden, far above, finally freed the sluice-gate and never knew what he'd unlatched along with it.

Only when they were two miles into the foothills, in the lee of a shepherd's hut with its roof fallen in, did Ivo stop walking and turn to face her fully. Up close he was younger than his legend—forty, maybe, with forearms like bridge cables and a beard going grey at the corners of his mouth, which gave him the permanent appearance of suppressed amusement.

"Now," he said. "Explain badly."

So Freya explained badly. The covenant, the valley, the seal failing, the thirteenth moon, the thing beneath the palace drinking the harvested gifts, Maren, Sable, the stones. She talked fast and low while the rain ticked on the ruined roof, and Ivo listened without interrupting, which she was learning was his way—he took information the way he took measurements, completely, once.

When she finished, he was quiet for a long moment. Then he rolled up his left sleeve.

His forearm was a map of old scars, and there, sunk into the muscle below the elbow, was a sliver of stone no bigger than a knife-blade. It should have looked wrong, flesh grown over mineral. Instead it looked *settled*, the way a keystone looks settled, like the arm had been built around it from the start.

"They offered me a fortune for this," Ivo said. "More than a wright earns in thirty years. All I had to do was let them draw the gift out through it—there's a way, did you know? They don't need the stone. They need the *agreement*. The gift has to be given, or at least given up." He turned his arm in the failing light, and the stone caught a dull gleam. "I said no. My wife asked me why, after. We had nothing, Freya Almeida. Two children in one room, a workshop mortgaged twice. I said no and she didn't speak to me for a month." He pulled the sleeve down. "Then the hunters came for the valley folk anyway, and she stopped asking why and started helping me hide it. People are like stone that way. They hold until they don't, and then they hold forever."

"You could have bought them free," Freya said quietly. "With the gift. I understand why—"

"No," Ivo said, and it wasn't harsh, it was structural, the word of a man who had checked the calculation a thousand times and gotten the same answer. "You don't understand yet, so listen. What they're feeding under that palace isn't hungry the way a wolf is hungry. It's hungry the way a mine is hungry. You feed it one vein, it wants the mountain. My gift would have bought my family a year, maybe two, and then it would have come for their gifts, and their children's. There's no selling enough. There's only breaking the thing that makes us currency at all." He looked at her, and for the first time she saw the weight in him, the thing under the dry humor. "You're here to reforge the covenant. So ask me properly."

Freya blinked. "Ask you—"

"Maren taught you the words?"

"She taught me... fragments. She said the asking would know itself when—" Freya stopped. Because her hand was already moving. Because the shattered oath-stone fragment Maren had given her, the one she'd carried against her sternum since the valley, was warm through her shirt, and pulling, the way a compass pulls, the way a held breath releases.

She held it out on her palm.

Ivo looked at it for a long moment. Then he pressed his scarred forearm against it, stone to stone, and spoke—not the formal words of any rite, just his own, rough and plain:

"Ivo Brannick, wright of the Karst. I keep what is built. I hold what is broken. Third of the three, last of the oath. Put me where the wall needs bearing."

The fragment flared white. Freya felt it sing up her arm and into her teeth, and somewhere impossibly far away she heard—she would swear ever after she *heard*—the sound of two other stones answering, one tide-deep and one earth-old, a chord completing itself in a key she had no name for.

Then it was done, and the rain was just rain, and Ivo Brannick sat down heavily on a fallen roof-beam and laughed, a short bark of genuine astonishment.

"Well," he said. "Nine months in that cell and it turns out all I needed was bad weather and a girl with good timing." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. "My wife is going to kill me. Assuming the crown doesn't get there first."

The words landed in Freya's stomach like dropped stones.

"Your wife," she said slowly. "Your children. Ivo—if they took you for refusing to sell, they'll—"

"They'll hold them," Ivo said. His jaw worked. "Hostage-writ. Standard practice for a convicted refuser. They'll be moved to the capital within the week, where they're worth more as leverage than as drained gifts, because a live family keeps a fugitive predictable." He met her eyes. "Which means, storm-caller, that you and I have a decision to make, and we have to make it fast, and either way we make it, there's no road back from it."

Freya looked out at the rain falling soft and steady on the foothills—the little storm she'd built, her careful, invisible, deniable whisper of weather—and understood, with the clarity of a crack running through stone, that invisibility was over. Whatever else happened tonight, somewhere in a tower in Ondu or a vault beneath a palace, a man named Corvus Hale was feeling this storm the way a hound feels a scent on the wind. Small as it was, it was *hers*. It carried her signature the way everything she did now carried it. She had crossed the line from hunted rumor to hunted fact, and she had done it gladly, and the price was already assembling itself in the dark.

"Where are they held?" she asked.

Ivo smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man looking at a wall he intended to bring down.

"House garrison, north quarter. Twelve guards, two wards, one gate." He cracked his knuckles, one hand against the other, like a mason testing mortar. "I've been drawing the layout in my head for nine months, girl. I only ever lacked the weather."

Far to the south, past the mountains, past the drowned city, past the whole breathing length of the realm, Freya felt the thirteenth moon rising toward its turn—eleven nights now, ten, and counting—and she stood up out of the ruins and said the words that closed every door behind her:

"Then let's go collect your family before the crown remembers they exist."

Above the northern passes, unnoticed by anyone but her, the wind changed direction.

It was blowing toward the palace. And it was carrying her name.
