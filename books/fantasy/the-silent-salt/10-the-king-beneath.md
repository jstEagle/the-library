## 10. The King Beneath

Corvus Hale had been chief witch-hunter of the realm for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once been invited below.

He had been *permitted* below, twice, to deliver captives whose gifts were judged too valuable for the ordinary cull. Both times he had been escorted by silent men in grey who walked him down a stair so long it seemed less like architecture than a punishment, and both times he had waited in a stone antechamber while others took custody of his prisoners. He had heard things through that door. A sound like breathing, if breathing could be bored. A voice that was not a voice, more a pressure behind the ears, the way thunder is felt in the teeth before it is heard.

He had told himself, both times, that whatever lay beyond that door was the crown's business, and that his business was the road above—the ledgers, the warrants, the long cold rides to villages where people whispered too loudly about things they shouldn't have.

Tonight there was no escort. Tonight there was a summons written in the king's own hand, which was strange enough, and a single line of instruction, which was stranger still:

*Come alone. Come hungry.*

Corvus read the last two words four times before he understood they were literal. The king wanted him to skip supper. He almost laughed. In thirty years of service to the crown—first as a soldier, then as a hunter—he had received many peculiar orders. March here. Burn this. Look away from that. But no one had ever before concerned themselves with the state of his stomach.

He skipped supper. It seemed unwise to disobey an instruction so small; small instructions were how kings tested whether large ones would be followed.

The stair began behind a tapestry in the east wing, one depicting the founding of the realm—a king, a river, three kneeling figures who might have been saints or might have been hostages, depending on how you looked at them. Corvus had walked past it a thousand times. He had never once wondered what it hid. That, he would reflect later, was perhaps the most damning thing about him.

The steps went down farther than the palace had height. He counted out of habit, the way he counted everything—paces, prisoners, winters—and lost the count somewhere past four hundred when the air changed. It grew warm. Then it grew *thick*, the way air gets before a summer storm, except that no storm had ever smelled like this: hot metal and old honey and something underneath both, faint and wrong, like a room where a candle has just gone out.

His witch-sense—the hunters' crude name for it, though what he actually had was less a sense than a tooth that ached when magic was near—began to ache. Not the ordinary ache of a hedge-witch's charm or a smuggled amulet. This was the ache of standing too close to a bonfire. Every gift he had ever dragged out of some terrified farmer's attic, every spark he had ever chained and delivered down these stairs, all of it was *here*, pooled in the dark below him like rainwater in a cellar.

And most of it was gone. Used. Spent. What remained had the feel of a larder picked nearly clean.

Corvus stopped on the stairs and made himself think the thought he had spent eleven years not thinking.

*What eats this much?*

* * *

The vault at the bottom was not a vault. Vaults held treasure, and treasure was small. This was a cavern that the palace had merely been built on top of, the way a hat sits on a head without knowing anything about the skull beneath. Its ceiling vanished upward into darkness shot through with veins of pale light—captured gifts, suspended in the stone like flies in amber, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a stolen flicker of someone's life. He recognized the colors. Storm-blue, for the callers. Green-gold for the growers. A soft silver he'd only seen once before, in a tide-speaker from Vess who had wept the entire way down the mountain and thanked him at the end, which had been worse than the weeping.

They hung there, dimming. And beneath them, filling the floor of the cavern the way a shadow fills a doorway—

It was bound. He saw the chains first because chains were honest work and his eye went to them gratefully. Seven of them, thick as masts, anchored into the rock at seven points around the cavern, each one humming faintly, each one strung with charms older than the dynasty. They converged on the center, where the thing lay coiled.

Coiled was the best word. It did not have a shape so much as a suggestion of one, the way heat has a shape over a summer road. It was vast and it was thin. That was the detail that undid him—not its size, not the slow churn of it, but the *thinness*. He had starved animals before, in sieges, in hard winters. He knew what hunger did to a body. It drew it in on itself. It made the bones prominent. Whatever this was, it had been magnificent once, and now it was a great slack emptiness wearing the memory of magnificence, and it was *hungry*, and every gift Corvus had ever delivered had gone into that hunger the way a cup of water goes into a field.

"Ah," said the king, from somewhere near the nearest chain. "You came hungry. Good."

Corvus hadn't seen him. His Majesty stood in a plain travelling cloak, no crown, holding a lantern like any man checking on his cattle. Up close, without the throne and the distance and the gold, the king looked tired in a way that Corvus found deeply unsettling, because tired was a human complaint and he had never permitted himself to think of the king as human. Kings were weather. You dressed for them. You did not notice when they looked unwell.

"Your Majesty." He knelt, because the knee knew what to do even when the rest of him didn't.

"Get up, Corvus. I didn't bring you down here to kneel. I brought you down here so you'd stop asking questions."

"I haven't asked any questions, sire."

"No," the king agreed pleasantly. "That's what makes you my best hunter. Eleven years, and you've never asked. Do you know how rare that is? Vasken asked. You remember what happened to Vasken." He said it the way another man might say *you remember what happened to the biscuits.* "I've decided you've earned the truth. Partly because you've earned it. Mostly because I need you to understand what's coming, and you won't move fast enough unless you're frightened."

"I'm rarely frightened, sire."

"I know," said the king, with something almost like affection. "It's one of the reasons I chose you. Watch."

He raised the lantern.

* * *

What happened next, Corvus would spend the rest of his life failing to describe properly.

The king spoke a word—not in any language Corvus knew, or perhaps in all of them at once, compressed—and the nearest chain flared white, and the thing below stirred. Not much. A few feet of it lifted toward the light, and where the light touched it, Corvus saw what it was made of, and his mind simply declined the information and offered him, instead, the image of a well seen from the top, depth without bottom.

Then it noticed him.

There was no other way to put it. Something in the vast dim bulk of it turned its attention upon Corvus Hale, chief witch-hunter of the realm, and he felt that attention the way a rabbit feels the shadow of the hawk: entirely, and uselessly, and with the sudden vivid understanding of every mistake that had led to this moment.

And it spoke to him. Not aloud. The pressure-behind-the-teeth voice, the one he'd heard through the antechamber door years ago, except now it was inside his skull, arranging itself comfortably among his own thoughts like a guest who intended to stay the winter.

***Hunter.***

"I don't talk to prisoners," Corvus said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, and he decided to be proud of that later if there was a later.

***Not a prisoner. A keeper. You keep me. You feed me. Eleven years, hunter. Your hands have carried more of me than any man alive.***

"That's delivery work," Corvus said. "I don't pretend to know what's in the crates."

A sound moved through the cavern. It took him a moment to recognize it as laughter, and another moment to wish he hadn't.

***You know. You have always known. You tell yourself stories—you are very good at stories, hunter, better than your king, who tells himself only one story and believes it completely—but you know. You have known since the first winter, since the girl with the green-gold light, since she looked at you and said* please *and you carried her down anyway.***

Corvus did not answer. There was nothing to say that the thing couldn't reach into his chest and read off his ribs.

***I could make it right,*** it said, and its voice changed, softened, became the voice of a reasonable friend at the end of a long evening. ***That is what I do, hunter. That is all I have ever done. I make things right. Ask your king what he wanted, all those years ago, when he first came down these stairs. A dying father? A contested throne? I gave him what he needed. I ask so little in return. A little light. A little warmth. And I could give you—***

"Don't," Corvus said.

***—your mother's garden,*** it finished gently. ***The year the blight came. I remember it. You remember it. I could grow it again for you, every rose, every summer, and this time nothing would die.***

The lantern light seemed very far away. Corvus stood in the dark of a cavern beneath the palace he had served for thirty years, being offered his mother's garden by a starving god, and the truly obscene part—the part that made him want to be sick—was that for one full breath, one entire heartbeat, he *wanted it*. He wanted it with a longing so clean and total that it felt less like temptation than like homesickness.

He thought about the girl with the green-gold light. He made himself think about her, deliberately, the way a man presses on a bruise to prove he can still feel pain. She had said *please*. She had been nineteen. Her gift had been so small it wouldn't have lit a candle—she'd made beans sprout a day early, that was all, beans, and some neighbor had envied her and reported her, and Corvus had put her in a cart because the warrant was signed and the law was the law, and the law, he had told himself for eleven years, was the wall between the realm and chaos.

The law was a delivery route. That was all it had ever been.

"Your Majesty," he said, and his voice was not quite steady anymore, and he found he didn't care. "What happens when the thirteenth moon crests?"

The king lowered the lantern. In its light his face was calm and pleasant and utterly empty, the face of a man reading numbers off a ledger.

"The seal breaks," the king said. "All of it. The valley, the borders, the old covenant—every wall between us and the wild magic comes down at once. And when it does, everything loose in the world comes through. Unless he—" a nod at the dark, casual as introducing a business partner, "—is strong enough to hold the door. He needs to be fed before then. Fully fed. Which means the culling accelerates. Every dormant gift in the realm, Corvus. Every child who hasn't sparked yet. We take them all before the moon turns."

Something in Corvus's chest went very quiet, the way air goes quiet before lightning.

"And the Salt Valley?" he heard himself ask. "The ones hiding there?"

"The valley's protection dies with the covenant. You'll go in yourself. There's a storm-caller there—we believe the same one from nine years ago, the massacre at Aldenmoor. The records match. Find her. Bring her down these stairs personally. He's developed a taste for storms." The king smiled. "Consider it a promotion."

* * *

Corvus climbed the four hundred and some stairs without counting them, which had never once happened in his life.

At the top, beside the tapestry of the founding king and the three kneeling figures, he stopped. He looked at the kneeling figures properly for the first time. Oath-keepers, the old stories called them, though the church had renamed them saints and the schoolbooks had renamed them founders' companions. Three figures. Three stones, if the fragmentary accounts were true. A covenant made to bind something enormous and hungry beneath the world, paid for across generations, maintained by silence.

Someone had forged that silence. Someone had built the valley, and the borders, and the long patient walls. And the king meant to spend all of it—every seal, every hidden life, every child—to feed a debt that had never been his to incur, to a creditor who whispered in gardens.

Corvus leaned against the cold stone of the corridor and laughed, quietly, because the alternative was screaming and he had a reputation to maintain.

Eleven years he had told himself he was the wall against chaos. He was not the wall. He was the man who brought the wolf its dinner and called the wolf a fence.

He thought about the storm-caller. Aldenmoor. He had read those records a hundred times—an elder dead, a village scattered, a girl of sixteen gone into the salt country and swallowed by its silence. He had always filed her under *loose end*. Now the king had handed him her name like a bone to a dog, and expected him to fetch.

Instead, Corvus Hale stood in the corridor above the starving god and did the most dangerous thing he had done in thirty years of service.

He began, carefully, to plan how to fail.

The thirteenth moon was turning. Somewhere out beyond the salt flats, a storm-caller was running, and for the first time in his long obedient life, Corvus Hale hoped she ran fast.
