## 11. Campfire Confessions

The high passes did not care that they were fugitives. That was, Freya was learning, one of the few honest things about mountains: they were indifferent with a completeness no person could manage. The wind up here had no opinions about witch-hunters. The stone had never heard of the crown. It was almost restful.

Almost.

They had found shelter in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, three days out from Ondu and its memory-markets, two days ahead of any pursuit Ivo's betrayal might have bought them—though Ivo insisted, for the hundredth time, that it hadn't been a betrayal, and for the hundredth time nobody answered him, which was somehow worse than an argument would have been.

Freya crouched at the mouth of the hollow, feeding twigs to a fire no bigger than a cupped pair of hands. Above them, the thirteenth moon was four turns from full—a sliver like a paring of fingernail, pale against a sky salted with stars. Four turns. She tried not to do the arithmetic too often, because every time she did, the numbers seemed to have gotten worse without her permission.

Behind her, Sable sat wrapped in two blankets and Ivo's spare coat, propped against the stone with a bowl of melted snow warming near the fire. She could not speak—the crown's drainings had taken her voice years ago, along with most of her tide-gift—but water still spoke for her when it chose to. Right now a shallow puddle of meltwater beside her knee shivered into a shape: a long wavy line, then three small bumps.

*Mountains are terrible,* the water said. Or that was how Freya translated it. Water had a limited vocabulary, but it managed a great deal with tone.

"I know," Freya murmured. "We'll be down to the lowlands in five days."

The puddle flattened itself pointedly, which in Sable's dialect meant *I'll believe it when my feet touch grass.*

Ivo came back from checking the horses with frost in his beard and an expression like a man who had lost an argument with the weather. He was a mountain-wright, broad as a door and roughly as talkative, and he carried stones the way other men carried grievances—constantly, and close to the chest. He dropped an armful of dead juniper by the fire.

"Wolves," he announced.

"Wolves?"

"Maybe wolves." He settled down with a groan that suggested his knees had their own opinion about the altitude. "Something was moving on the ridge. Could've been goats."

"Could've been Corvus Hale," Freya said quietly.

Ivo's hands went still over the wood he was stacking. He didn't look at her. "Hale doesn't move alone," he said finally. "You'd hear horses. You'd hear *men*." A pause. "It was probably goats."

Sable's puddle rippled into something that looked suspiciously like laughter, and even Ivo's mouth twitched, once, before he got it under control.

They ate in the way people eat when food is scarce and hunger is not a novelty but a colleague: slowly, carefully, making each bite last. Ivo had produced a heel of black bread from somewhere in his coat and divided it into three pieces with the gravity of a man splitting a kingdom. Sable soaked hers in the warm snowmelt until it softened enough for her to manage. Freya chewed hers and watched the moon-sliver climb and thought about the arithmetic again, and stopped, and started again.

It was Sable who broke the silence. She always broke silences; it was one of the ironies of her condition that a woman without a voice could not bear quiet. The puddle beside her stirred, gathered itself, and formed a shape Freya hadn't seen before: a circle, with lines radiating outward, like a child's drawing of a sun.

Then the shape collapsed and reformed: the same circle, cracked down the middle.

Freya stared at it. "What is that?"

Sable's dark eyes were steady on her. Patient. Waiting.

"The oath-stone?" Freya touched her pocket, where Maren's shattered third of the covenant key rode against her hip like a second heartbeat. "That's the covenant?"

Sable nodded. Then the water made the circle again—and this time, instead of cracking it, it drew a small figure beside it. A stick-figure. With what appeared to be hair.

"That's me?"

Another nod. The stick-figure stood beside the intact circle for a moment. Then Sable drew the circle cracked, and drew the stick-figure standing apart from it, off at the edge, alone.

Freya felt something tighten behind her ribs. "I don't understand what you're asking."

Sable exhaled through her nose—an old habit, speaking breath where speech should be—and reached across and took Freya's hand. Her grip was cool and light. Then she turned Freya's hand palm-up, and traced one finger across it, slow and deliberate, the way you'd write a word for someone who couldn't read quickly.

*Tell.*

One word. But Freya understood all of it.

She pulled her hand back—not unkindly, but the way you pull a hand back from a stove—and looked into the fire for a long time.

"You both think you're traveling with someone who killed an elder because she was angry," Freya said at last. "Or frightened. Or careless. You've been polite about it for weeks. Ivo, you haven't asked me a single question since Ondu, and Ivo, you ask questions the way rivers ask questions of everything they meet."

"Rivers erode things," Ivo said.

"Exactly." She laughed, but it came out thin. She wrapped her arms around her knees and watched the flames bend. "So. Before we walk into whatever's waiting under the palace. Before you stake your lives—what's left of them—on my storm-heart. You should know what kind of person carries it."

The wind moved through the juniper above the hollow. Somewhere far below, a rock clattered down a scree slope—Ivo's maybe-wolves, or goats, or nothing.

"Her name was Aldenmoor," Freya began. "The valley I come from. Before Salt Valley. You'd know it as the massacre place. Everyone does. They tell it different ways depending on how much they want to scare their children. In some versions I'm a demon. In some versions I'm a victim. In the crown's version I'm proof that magic must be culled before it culls you." She turned a twig between her fingers. "None of those versions are true. Here's the real one, and it's worse, because the real one is boring right up until the moment it isn't."

Sable leaned forward. Even Ivo, who claimed indifference the way other men claimed titles, had gone still.

"When I was sixteen, Elder Brannoc ruled Aldenmoor. He'd ruled it my whole life. He was the kind of man who speaks slowly so you mistake it for wisdom, who decides things in advance and calls the deciding council. And he had—" she searched for the word— "*hungers*. Not for money. For importance. For being the man who mattered, the man the realm noticed. And our valley was poor and far and the realm never noticed us at all."

She fed the twig to the fire. It caught, flared, and died down.

"My gift woke late. Storm-calling—it runs in my mother's line, though we didn't speak of it. The first time I called lightning I was fourteen and I cried for a day afterward because it felt like holding a god's wrist. Too much power. All the time. Like being a cup that's always overflowing." Her voice steadied; this part she could say easily now. "Brannoc knew. He knew what I was. And instead of fearing me, I think—" she hesitated— "I think he saw a use for me. A tool. Something rare enough that the court might finally look at our valley."

"What happened?" Ivo asked. Quietly. His first question in weeks, spent exactly where it mattered.

"There was a child," Freya said. "Toma. The cooper's boy. Eight years old. Sweet little thing, always muddy, always laughing. And in the autumn of my sixteenth year, Brannoc began to prepare for a rite. He told the village it was a blessing ceremony—for the harvest, for protection. He dressed it in all the old words. He built a stone basin up on the tor. And he chose Toma to be blessed."

The fire popped. Nobody moved.

"I didn't understand," Freya went on, and her voice had gone flat now, the flatness of ground walked many times. "That's the part I can't forgive, that I *didn't understand*, even though everything was wrong. The basin was wrong—deep, carved with channels. The timing was wrong—the night of the new moon, when no blessing wants darkness. He kept Toma away from his parents. He told them the boy needed to fast and keep vigil alone. And I helped. Gods help me, I *helped*. I carried stones for him. I was so pleased to be chosen, to be useful, to be the girl the elder trusted." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "He'd learned something. I understand that now, after everything Maren showed us, after the vaults, after all of it. There are whispers that run through the world like root-rot. Old hungers. Teachings that promise power to the desperate. Brannoc had found one. And what those teachings want—what the thing under the palace wants—isn't worship. It isn't faith. It's *gift*. Raw, living magic, drained from a vessel while the vessel is still alive. And a child's gift is—"

She stopped. Sable's hand found her shoulder. Freya breathed until she could go on.

"A child's gift is bright," she finished. "Unspent. Unspoiled. The sweetest meat there is, if you're the kind of thing that eats."

Ivo had closed his eyes. His big hands rested on his knees, knotted together.

"The night of the rite, I followed them up the tor. I wasn't supposed to be there. Brannoc had told me to stay below and call the storm when he raised his staff—storm-signs were part of the ceremony, he said. Thunder to announce the blessing. He wanted my lightning for theater. He wanted the village watching the sky while he opened the boy's throat in the basin." The words came out of her steady and cold, and she hated how steady they were, hated that she'd rehearsed this confession in the privacy of her skull for nine years until it had worn smooth as river stone. "And I climbed up instead. And I saw the basin. And I saw Toma tied into it, and I saw the channels carved in the stone, and I finally—*finally*—understood what they were for."

"What did you do?" Sable's eyes asked it, though the water beside her only trembled.

"I called the storm." Freya looked up. "No warning. No negotiation. I was sixteen years old and I had a god's wrist in my hand and I was terrified and furious, and I brought the lightning down on that tor like judgment. It struck the basin. It struck Brannoc. It killed him instantly—he never even screamed. And it split the basin open and scattered the rite to the four winds, and Toma lived. Toma *lived*." Her laugh was awful. "Do you see? Do you see the joke of it? I saved him. I saved him and the whole realm has spent nine years telling the story of the monster who murdered a holy elder and destroyed a village. Because that's the version that survived. Brannoc's friends told it. The frightened village told it. The crown polished it and put it in its records and sent hunters after the loose end."

Silence. The fire burned. High above, the moon-sliver climbed another degree toward its fatal fullness.

"And Toma?" Ivo asked at last.

"They got him out. His parents took him south. I never saw him again. I hope—" She shook her head. "I hope he grew up remembering a blessing that went strangely, and nothing more. Children forget. It's the mercy of children." She drew a breath. "But here's the part that matters. Here's why I'm telling you this tonight, badly, around a goat-fire, instead of keeping it where I've kept it for nine years."

She pulled the oath-stone fragment from her pocket and held it up so the firelight moved in its broken facets.

"Maren told us the covenant binds the realms apart so that nothing crosses between them. So that the hungers on the other side can't reach in, and ours can't reach out. And I've spent nine years thinking my crime was the worst thing I ever did. Murder. Rage. A girl who held too much power and used it to kill." Her hand tightened on the stone. "But that's not what it was. Brannoc was trying to feed a child to the same hunger that sits under the palace right now, fat and starving on stolen gifts. My crime—my stupid, violent, unforgivable crime—was a *warning*. The rot was already in our valley, in our holy man, in the ceremony everyone applauded. It was already everywhere. And I didn't hear the warning. I didn't understand what I'd seen until it was nine years too late and the crown was harvesting children by the cartload."

She looked at each of them in turn. Ivo, whose family was in a hunter's cell because he'd tried to buy their safety with betrayal and bought nothing. Sable, whose voice had been poured out of her into the king's vaults.

"I'm not telling you so you'll forgive me," Freya said. "I don't know if it's forgivable. Maybe killing is killing even when the man deserves it, even when a child lives because of it. Maybe that's a debt I carry past the end of this." The stone caught the light. "I'm telling you so you know what you're walking with. Not a girl who lost control. A girl who saw the truth of the world one night on a hilltop and was too young, too scared, and too proud to understand it. And who has spent nine years learning. And who will not—"

Her voice cracked. She let it crack. There was no one left to perform strength for.

"—who will *not* fail to hear it twice."

The wind came down the pass and bent the flames sideways. Sable moved first. She always moved first; grief had taught her that hesitation was just pain with better manners. She crossed the space between them and took Freya's face in both cool hands, and pressed her forehead to Freya's, and held there, breathing, the oldest comfort there is.

When she pulled back, the puddle at her knee had risen—drawn up into a shape that hovered trembling in the cold air. A circle, whole. And inside it, three small figures, side by side.

Freya laughed, wet-eyed. "That's us?"

Sable nodded once, firmly, the way she nodded about things that were not open to discussion.

Ivo cleared his throat. It was a sound like a quarry settling. "For what it's worth," he rumbled, staring hard at the fire, "the version they tell in the mines is that you struck down a holy man in his moment of glory. The miners drink to you. They think you killed a hypocrite." A pause. "Miners are usually right about hypocrites."

"Ivo."

"It's true. Best judges in the realm." He poked the fire with unnecessary violence. "My family sold me for amnesty and got chains. You killed a man and saved a child and got exile. If the realm keeps score, it keeps it badly." He glanced up at her, and for once his stony face was simply, plainly kind. "You heard the warning now. That's worth more than innocence. Innocence never built anything."

Freya wiped her face with her sleeve and laughed—really laughed, rough and surprised. "Look at us. Three criminals and a puddle, rewriting the realm's conscience."

"The puddle objects to 'criminal,'" Sable's water shaped, with great dignity.

"The puddle drowned a tax collector in Vess."

"He was *asking* for it."

And then—

The fire guttered. Not from wind. The flames leaned all at once toward the mouth of the hollow, as if pulled, and the warmth went out of the air like a snuffed candle, and Sable's hovering water-shape collapsed into the puddle with a splash.

Freya was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, storm gathering in her palms, silver-blue light crawling under her skin.

On the ridge above the hollow, where the maybe-wolves had moved hours ago, something stood against the stars.

Not a wolf. A man. Alone, unhorsed, unarmed—hands open and empty at his sides, the way you stand when you know every gesture might be your last.

Even in the dark, even at forty paces, Freya knew the silhouette. She had seen it in nightmares for nine years, wearing different faces, and in the crown's records wearing this one.

"Storm-caller." Corvus Hale's voice carried down the cold air, level and quiet, a man speaking carefully across a chasm. "I'm not here to take you. I need to show you what's under the palace." A pause, and then, quieter:

"And I think you already know its name."
