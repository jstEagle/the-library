## 12. Betrayal at the Pass

Ivo Stonehand had carved his name into mountain rock exactly once in his life, and he had done it badly, and the mountain had laughed at him.

That was the thing about mountains people didn't understand. They weren't silent. They were patient. A mountain would hold a grudge for ten thousand years and never once raise its voice about it, which Ivo had always thought was the most dignified way to go about anything. He tried to live the same way. He was a wright of the Grey Teeth, third-generation, trained to listen to stone the way other men listened to weather, and for forty-one years the stone had told him only useful things: *there's a seam here, don't cut there, this bridge will hold if you mind the western footing.*

Then the crown's men came asking questions, and the stone started telling him things like *they're lying* and *run*, and it turned out some knowledge was worse than ignorance.

He sat now in the hunters' camp at the mouth of Wolfjaw Pass, on a stool that creaked under him like a guilty conscience, across a folding table from Corvus Hale, chief witch-hunter of the realm, who had just finished laying out a map of the high passes with the neatness of a man setting a table for dinner.

"Tea?" Corvus asked.

There was tea. There was, absurdly, honey for the tea. There was even a small tin of dried apricots, which Ivo recognized because his daughter Pia loved them and he'd carried them over the passes himself not six weeks ago, bought from a trader in Ondu with money he'd earned mending a shrine wall. The sight of them did something to his chest that he chose not to examine.

"I'm fine," Ivo said, which was a lie so large it barely fit in the tent.

"You've been walking three days," Corvus said. "My scouts picked you up at the ford. You looked like a man carrying something heavy."

"A man my age looks like that standing still."

Corvus smiled. It was a real smile, or close enough to one that Ivo suspected the man practiced it in front of polished armor until it stopped being false. That was somehow more frightening than cruelty. Cruelty you could spit at. Kindness that wasn't kind left you nowhere to put your anger.

"Master Ivo," Corvus said, "I want to be honest with you, because I think you're an honest man, and honest men deserve honesty."

*Here it comes,* said the stone in Ivo's bones.

"You're a mountain-wright. Your gift is structural — you hear load-bearing truth. The crown has records of your assessment work on the Ondu aqueduct, the Vess seawall repairs, eleven bridges. Excellent work, all of it. No one disputes you're a craftsman of the first order." Corvus folded his hands. "And no one wants to punish you for it. What we want is the storm-caller. Freya Almeida. Where she's going. Which pass she means to take."

"She's a stranger to me," Ivo said. "A woman I met on the road."

"Yes," Corvus agreed pleasantly. "A woman you met on the road, whose escape route happens to run directly through territory only a mountain-wright can read safely. Who travels with a tide-speaker and carries oath-stones." He tapped the map. "You see how a suspicious man might find that interesting. Fortunately for you—" and here his voice warmed by a full degree, which from Corvus Hale felt like a hearth-fire, "—I am not a suspicious man. I'm a practical one."

He turned the map around and laid something beside it: a paper bearing the crown's seal, and beneath the paper, three names written in a clerk's tidy hand.

*Iva Stonehand. Pia Stonehand, age nine. Dagny Stonehand, age sixty-seven.*

His mother. His daughter. His wife.

"They're in Ondu," Ivo said. His voice came out flat as slate. "In my sister's house."

"They were in Ondu," Corvus corrected gently. "They're currently guests of the Crown Detention House on Candler Street. Comfortable quarters, as such places go. My orders regarding them depend entirely," he said, "on your cooperation."

The stone in Ivo's chest — the one that had been holding since they took him at the ford, since before that, maybe since the day years ago when a crown clerk had come to the quarry and offered him a salary for his gift and he'd refused, politely, the way you refuse a snake — that stone finally cracked.

Not all at once. Stones don't break all at once. They break along their weakest line, and Ivo's weakest line had always been Pia, who had her mother's laugh and her grandmother's stubbornness and who had asked him last spring, solemn as a magistrate, whether rocks dreamed. He'd told her yes, but very slowly, and she'd nodded like this confirmed everything.

"What do you want," he heard himself say, "to know."

* * *

Afterward, he told himself he hadn't decided. That was the trick of it, the mercy of it, the cowardice of it — the whole thing happened in the space between one breath and the next, and he never once said *yes*. He simply stopped saying *no*, and the world treated that as agreement, the way the world does.

He showed them on the map. Not all of it. Not the shepherd's track over Kettlebone that bypassed the main pass entirely — that much he kept, a pebble of resistance hidden in his boot, though he already knew, with a wright's terrible certainty, that it wouldn't matter. He gave them the main route. The high col below the Old Sentinel. The place where the trail narrowed between cliff faces that he, personally, knew were rotten to the core, riddled with frost-cracks, waiting for nothing but a loud noise and bad luck.

"The storm-caller will use thunder," Corvus said, reading the terrain the same way Ivo did, and hating that they read it the same way. "She'll have to, if pressed. And a thunderclap in that narrows—"

"Buries the trail under forty feet of mountain," Ivo said. "Or buries whoever's standing in it. Depends on her aim." He stared at the map. "You'd lose men."

"We might," Corvus said. "Which is why I'd rather take her at the campsite tonight, asleep, before the narrows. The information matters less than the timing. But I wanted to understand what you know, Master Ivo. A man should understand what his words buy."

*A man should understand what his words buy.* Ivo would carry that sentence for the rest of his life, however long the rest of it turned out to be, and it would sit in him like cold iron.

"And my family?"

"Will be released within the day. Escorted home. Full amnesty — the crown values craftsmanship, and it does not punish a man for the company he keeps on the road." Corvus stood and extended his hand. "You have my word."

Ivo shook it. The hunter's grip was dry, firm, unhurried. Somewhere underneath the leather glove, Ivo thought he felt calluses — a swordsman's, or maybe a mason's, though that was probably just his own gift reaching for comfort and finding none.

He walked out of the tent into the grey afternoon, and the mountains around Wolfjaw Pass looked down at him, and said nothing at all, which was the loudest thing they'd ever said to him.

* * *

They let him go. That was part of the design, he understood later — a released man runs to warn his friends, and a followed man leads you to them. But they needn't have bothered following him. He walked straight up the trail toward the keepers' camp because there was nowhere else in the world for his feet to go, and every step was a conversation between two voices in his head.

*You can still turn back,* said one voice. *Tell them you talked. Tell them to run.*

*They'll move slower with warning than without it,* said the other voice, smooth as water finding a crack. *And if you warn them and they scatter, the deal sours. Pia stays in Candler Street. If you stay quiet, they take Freya at dawn and Pia comes home by week's end.*

*Freya Almeida killed an elder,* said the second voice, warming to its work. *Whatever she's doing with those stones, whatever song the tide-speaker sings about covenants, the crown says she's dangerous, and you've seen her storms, haven't you? Felt them through the rock? Maybe it's even true.*

That was the poison in it. It was even true. He had felt her magic through fifty feet of granite and it had frightened him — that vast rolling pressure, like the mountain itself deciding to get up and walk. A person could carry that much sky inside them and still be kind; he'd watched her mend Sable's bandages with hands that shook. But a person could also be kind and still be a landslide. The mountain taught you that early.

By the time he reached the treeline below the camp, the sun was going down and the two voices had merged into one, which sounded remarkably like his mother saying, *Well, Ivo, you've made your bed.*

He smelled woodsmoke. He saw Sable sitting apart from the fire, her back against a boulder, moving her fingers through a basin of water so that shapes rose in it — her voice, speaking in currents. He saw Maren's absence like a wound; the old keeper had fallen behind two days past, her lungs bad in the thin air. And he saw Freya Almeida turning something over in her palms — an oath-stone, one of the shattered thirds — while she listened to the water talk.

Pia would be eating supper right now. Rice, probably, with too much pepper because Dagny salted nothing and overspiced everything to compensate. Pia hated pepper. She'd eat it anyway and complain the entire time and Dagny would pretend not to hear, and Ivo had walked away from a thousand evenings exactly like that one without ever once understanding they were the whole point of everything.

"Stonehand." Freya had seen him. Of course she had. "You're supposed to be scouting ahead. You were gone longer than—"

"I need to talk to you," Ivo said.

And then — gods forgive him, gods remember it against him forever — he said, "Not here. Walk with me a ways."

Because if she walked with him, away from the fire, away from Sable, he could tell her to run and no one would overhear, and if no one overheard, the hunters' plan might fail on its own, through no fault of his, and the deal would hold, and Pia would come home, and no one would ever have to know what he'd done except the mountain, and the mountain kept secrets for ten thousand years.

That was the plan. That was the whole shabby architecture of it. He led her thirty yards down the slope, into the blue dusk, and opened his mouth to say *they know, run, go over Kettlebone, go now* —

— and the first arrow took him in the shoulder.

* * *

Later — much later, when he had time to lie in the dirt and review the evening the way a wright reviews a collapsed bridge — he understood the arithmetic of his error. Corvus Hale had never needed Ivo to lure the storm-caller anywhere. He'd needed Ivo to separate her from her companions. One word from Ivo, *walk with me*, and the trap sprang itself. He hadn't sold them the route. He'd sold them the moment. He'd been the bait on his own hook and had swallowed it with his eyes open.

"Down!" Freya screamed, and the world broke open.

Thunder without a cloud. It came up out of her like something that had been held underwater for nine years, and the pressure of it flattened Ivo against the slope and rang in his broken shoulder like a bell being murdered. Arrows snapped overhead — the hunters had loosed into the dark where she'd been a heartbeat before, and where she'd been was now simply somewhere else, wrapped in wind, hair lifting off her neck, eyes lit faintly from inside like lamps behind stained glass.

Beautiful. Horrifying. Both, in the same breath, the way a mountain is both.

"Hold fire!" Corvus's voice, cutting through the gale, calm as a clerk. "Hold! Take the wright!"

Hands seized Ivo's collar. Through the smoke and the screaming wind he saw Sable rise from her basin with water sheeting off her arms like armor, saw her fling both hands outward, and the stream beside the trail stood up — stood *up*, a wall of white water taller than a man — and crashed downslope into the hunters' line, taking two of them off their feet, spinning their bows away into the dark.

And he saw Freya look at him. Across twenty feet of storm, with lightning crawling on her shoulders like a living thing, she looked at Ivo Stonehand and her face did something he would see every time he closed his eyes for the rest of his life.

It wasn't rage. That was the unbearable part. It was grief — quick, sharp, almost polite, the face of a woman seeing a debt confirmed that she'd half hoped didn't exist. Then Sable grabbed her arm and pointed upslope, toward Kettlebone, and the two of them went up into the dark like sparks off a grinder, and the storm went with them.

The hands on Ivo's collar dragged him around, and there was Corvus Hale, kneeling in the mud, unbothered, checking Ivo's wound with two efficient fingers.

"Shoulder. Through and through, probably. You'll live." He said it the way he'd said *tea?* — the same mild competence. Then he looked up the slope after the vanished women, and for just a moment something crossed his face that wasn't in any manual Ivo had ever imagined for chief witch-hunters. It might have been respect. It might have been envy. It might have been the beginning of a question he'd spend chapters answering.

"Kettlebone," Ivo rasped, blood in his teeth. "They'll go over Kettlebone. The shepherd's track. I didn't tell you that one. I kept that one back."

"I know," Corvus said. "Your sister's house was searched the day we took you. Pia draws maps. Quite detailed ones, for a child. She's proud of her papa's work." He stood, signaling to his men, and the column began to move — upslope, toward the pass, toward the narrows, toward the rotten cliffs Ivo knew were waiting. "Your family will be released tomorrow, Master Ivo, as promised."

Something in the sentence's architecture made Ivo's stomach drop before his mind caught up. *As promised.* Not *this morning*. Not *already done*.

"Where are they now," he said. "Where are they *now*, Hale."

"Gone ahead," Corvus said, and there it was again, that terrible gentleness. "With the advance party. Standard practice for collateral detainees in field operations. They'll meet us at the pass." He paused, and for one strange moment the mask slipped far enough that Ivo saw the man underneath was tired — bone-tired, in a way that had nothing to do with marching. "I give you my word they'll be released. I keep my word, wright. It's one of the few things I have left."

He turned and strode up into the dark after his men, and Ivo lay in the mud with an arrow-hole in his shoulder and understood, with a clarity that felt like frost cracking stone, exactly what he'd bought.

Nothing. He'd bought nothing. The amnesty had been real the way the apricots had been real — a kindness placed precisely where it would hurt most, worth exactly as much as the man offering it decided it was worth, revocable at any moment, contingent on a cooperation that could never end, because a man who'd betrayed once could always be made to betray again. Pia and Dagny would travel with the hunters until the hunt was over, and then — if the hunt succeeded — perhaps home, perhaps not. And if the hunt failed, if the storm-caller buried the pass and half the column with it—

"No," Ivo said, out loud, to nobody, to the mountain. "No, no, no."

He got his feet under him. The world tilted and righted. His shoulder screamed. Somewhere above him, torches crawled up the trail toward the narrows like a lit fuse burning toward powder, and somewhere beyond them, two women climbed for Kettlebone with a dying covenant in their pockets.

The mountain looked down at him, patient as ever.

"All right," Ivo Stonehand said, spitting blood, hauling himself upright against a pine trunk, one-armed, furious, forty-one years of listening to stone finally paying off in the only coin that mattered. "All right, old girl. You've been telling me for three days they're lying. So tell me this instead."

He pressed his good palm flat against the rock, and closed his eyes, and listened.

Far above, deep in the bones of the pass, the mountain answered — and what it told him made him start climbing.
