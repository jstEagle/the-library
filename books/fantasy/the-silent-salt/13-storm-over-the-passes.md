## 13. Storm Over the Passes

The high passes had a way of making you feel both enormous and insignificant at once, and Freya had come to appreciate the paradox. From the saddle of the ridge they'd camped on, she could see three valleys stitched together with snowmelt streams, the morning fog pooling in them like spilled milk. Somewhere down there were roads, and on those roads was Corvus Hale.

"Your tea is terrible," Sable announced, in her particular way—which was to say, not aloud at all.

Freya looked up from the fire. Water from their cooking pot had risen into the air beside her, twisting itself into shapes: first a cup, then a face with an exaggerated frown, then a cup again with a small wave crashing out of it.

"It's chamomile," Freya said. "It's supposed to taste like that."

*It tastes like punishment,* the water said, and sloshed itself back into the pot with a dignity that only Sable could give to water. Nine days of traveling with a woman who spoke through it, and Freya still hadn't gotten used to the fact that rivers could gossip.

Ivo snorted from where he sat sharpening his climbing axes against a flat stone. The mountain-wright was a slab of a man, gray-bearded and gray-tempered, with hands so large he could have palmed Freya's head like an apple. He'd barely spoken since the pass below Ondu, since the betrayal that had bought nothing, since his family had been taken anyway. But he sharpened his axes every morning like clockwork, and Freya had decided this was its own kind of conversation. *I am preparing,* the scraping said. *I am not done.*

"We should reach the rift road by tomorrow night," Ivo said now, testing an edge with his thumb. "If your hunter friend's maps are true."

"He's not my hunter friend."

"You let him live."

"I let him *walk away*. There's a difference." Freya poked the fire. "And his knowledge is real. The ward-labyrinth under the palace—he designed half of it. Without him we're walking into a locked house blindfolded."

"Without him," Ivo said, "we're walking into a locked house without the man who built the locks."

"That's what I said."

"That's what *he'd* say. That's what worries me."

Sable made a sound—not with her throat but by flicking a stream of meltwater against the stones, a bright plink-plink-plink that meant, in her vocabulary, *stop bickering, both of you, the tea is getting cold and I refuse to drink it twice.*

Freya smiled despite herself. This was the strange arithmetic of the last nine days: terror braided through with ordinary mornings. They were fugitives carrying two-thirds of a broken covenant toward the heart of a kingdom that wanted them dead, and still there was tea, and still there was bickering, and still Sable found ways to make jokes out of puddles. Freya held onto these moments the way a climber holds rock—knowing the hold would eventually end, gripping anyway.

She didn't know yet how soon this one would end.

* * *

The attack came at dusk, when the light went copper and long shadows turned every boulder into a potential ambush.

Later, Freya would try to reconstruct how it happened—the sequence, the signals—but memory refused to be orderly about it. What she remembered was the birds. A whole slope of them going up at once, a dark sheet torn off the mountainside, and Ivo saying one word—"Down"—in a voice like a door slamming.

Then the arrows came.

They hissed out of the rocks to the east, three volleys in the time it took Freya to throw herself behind a granite shelf. The first buried themselves in the earth where they'd been sitting. The second found their packs, their bedrolls, the poor innocent teapot. One skipped off the stone inches from her cheek, close enough that she felt the wind of it, and something in her chest went very cold and very quiet.

"Hunters," Ivo growled, pressing himself flat beside her. "A full company. How—"

"The pass trail," Freya breathed. "Ivo. The only route they could know is the one *you* sold them."

His face went gray. "That was before—they took my family regardless—I told myself it was finished, paid for—"

"They kept the receipt." She hadn't meant it as cruelty; it simply came out true. "We'll grieve later. Right now we move or we die."

But moving was the problem. Below them, the trail switchbacked down through open scree—a killing field. Above, the rocks climbed steeply toward a col that might, if the fog held, offer escape. And between those options, hunters were already flowing out of the shadows like ink poured uphill, thirty of them at least, in the gray-green leathers of the crown, crossbows leveled, spreading into a line with the smooth unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and never once lost sleep over it.

A voice carried up the slope, clear and cold. Not Corvus—she'd know his voice—but one of his captains, a woman with a scarred jaw and a voice like a whetstone.

"Freya Almeida. By order of the crown, surrender the oath-stones and yourselves. Your companions will be treated fairly. You have sixty counts."

"She's lying," Sable signed—or rather, the thin ribbon of water she'd drawn from their skins wrote it in the air, letters dripping: *THEY NEVER TREAT ANYONE FAIRLY.*

"No argument," Freya murmured. Her heart was slamming. Her palms were sweating. And underneath all of it—underneath the fear, beneath even the guilt of having led Sable and Ivo into this—something else was waking up.

It had been asleep for nine years. Mostly.

It had flickered awake in the valley when she'd saved Tam's daughter, a candle-flame of storm that the valley's silence had smothered almost instantly. It had come back to her fully at the boundary crossing, a flood that had left her screaming in Maren's arms while every suppressed thunderhead of nearly a decade poured back into her bones. Since then she'd used it carefully, sparingly—a small storm to break Ivo free, a whisper of wind to confuse trackers. Careful. Measured. A person who owned her power instead of—

"Forty counts," called the captain.

"—instead of being owned by it," she finished aloud, not realizing she'd spoken until Ivo looked at her strangely.

"There's another way up," Ivo said urgently, pointing east along the shelf. "Narrow. We could climb it in the dark, lose them in the fog—"

"With wounded?" Freya shook her head. "They'd pick us off the cliff face like ticks. No." She looked at the line of hunters below, at the crossbows glinting copper in the dying light, at the certainty in their formation—and felt the old familiar terror rise to meet the other thing rising in her chest.

Because here was the truth she'd been circling for nine years, the truth the valley's silence had let her avoid: the power wasn't a tool. It wasn't even a gift. It was a *weather system*, and she was the sky it lived in, and right now the pressure was building toward something she wasn't sure she could aim.

"Freya." Sable's water-letters trembled slightly. *Whatever you're thinking—*

"Get behind me," Freya said. "Both of you. And whatever happens—whatever you see—don't try to stop me. Please. Just don't get in my way."

Ivo grabbed her arm. His grip could have bent iron. "Girl. What did you do at that village, nine years ago? Truly?"

She met his eyes. "You already know. Everyone does. I killed an elder with lightning."

"That's the story. I'm asking what happened inside you when it did."

Freya opened her mouth to say *nothing good*, and instead heard herself say: "It felt like coming home."

The admission hung between them, awful and honest. Ivo's grip loosened—not in fear, she realized, but in grief. He released her, stepped back, and pulled Sable behind the great granite shelf.

"Thirty counts," the captain called, bored now.

"Ten will do," Freya said, and stepped out onto the open rock, and let the sky in.

* * *

There were no words for it, afterward, that satisfied anyone—including her. But she tried, later, around a different fire, and this was the account she gave:

It began in her lungs. A pressure, like the moment before the first drop of a summer cloudburst, when the whole world holds its breath. It spread down her arms and up through her skull and out through the soles of her feet into the mountain itself, and the mountain—she swore this, though no one quite believed her—*leaned into her*, the way a horse leans into a rider it trusts.

Above the passes, the clouds answered.

They came from every horizon at once, boiling inward, stacking themselves into a rotating cathedral of slate and violet that swallowed the last copper light whole. Wind arrived ahead of the rain, a hot dry gust that flattened the grasses and sent the hunters' line rippling like wheat. Someone shouted an order. Crossbows rose.

Freya raised one hand.

The lightning that answered was not the single white bolt she remembered from nine years ago—that terrible clean line, over in a heartbeat. This was a *storm's worth* of lightning, forked and branching, dancing between the cloud layers like veins of fire in dark glass, illuminating the whole amphitheater of peaks. For one suspended moment everything was lit: the hunters' upturned faces, the scree fields, Sable's wide eyes peeking past the stone, Ivo's hand pressed flat against the granite as if bracing for the world to tip.

Then Freya brought her hand down—not a fist, just fingers opening, releasing—and the sky discharged everywhere except where her friends stood.

Thunder hit like a physical blow. The sound alone knocked a dozen hunters off their feet. Bolts walked along the ridgeline in deliberate, precise strides—each one striking exactly where she willed it, splintering crossbows, splitting the rocks between squads, herding thirty trained killers into a shrieking, scrambling retreat down the far side of the pass. It was magnificent. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever done.

And some deep, calm, horrifying part of her noticed how easy it was. How *natural*. How the power fit her like the valley's silence never had—as if nine years of exile had been the interruption, and this, the storm, the command, the sky bending to her shoulder like an old dance partner, was the life she'd been made for.

*Come on,* the storm seemed to purr through her. *More. You've been rationing yourself your whole life. Look what you actually are.*

"Stop," she whispered—to the storm, to herself, she wasn't sure which.

The rain came then, a deluge, hammering down and washing the scent of ozone across the slopes. Through it she saw the captain regrouping her survivors below, saw them fall back in good order, saw one man look back up at the roiling clouds with his face nakedly awed—and she understood, with a lurch, that she had just shown the entire crown exactly what it was hunting. Not a rumor. Not a signature. A *storm with a name*.

Corvus Hale wouldn't need records anymore. He'd only need to follow the weather.

She let go.

The clouds unraveled reluctantly, like a child asked to leave a party. The wind died. Rain thinned to a drizzle, then to mist, and the first stars punched through overhead, indifferent and clean. Freya stood on the wet rock with her arm still half-raised, shaking, feeling the power settle back down into her bones like a sated animal curling up to sleep.

*Ivo,* she thought distantly. *Sable.* She turned.

And stopped.

Sable wasn't behind the granite shelf.

She was ten paces away, at the edge of the shelf, standing in the open where she had no business being—standing where she must have moved during the storm, though Freya couldn't imagine why—and there was a crossbow bolt in her, high on the left side, just below the collarbone. One of the hunters, braver or more desperate than the rest, had loosed into the lightning rather than run. And Sable—quiet Sable, tide-speaker, the woman who had lost her voice to the crown and learned to speak through every river in the realm—had stepped out of cover anyway.

Later, the water told Freya why. Later, when there was time, Sable drew it slowly in a ribbon of meltwater: *YOU STOOD IN THE OPEN. I WOULDN'T LET YOU DO IT ALONE.*

But in that first moment there was no explanation at all. There was only Sable folding to her knees on the wet stone, and Freya running, sliding, catching her before her head hit the rock, and the blood—so much blood, welling fast and dark between Freya's fingers, too fast.

"No. No no no. Stay with me. IVO!"

He came at a run, and then they were both of them pressing cloth to the wound while Sable's breath went ragged and shallow, and all around them the pass dripped and steamed and said nothing, because storms are generous with thunder and stingy with mercy.

* * *

They made camp in the mouth of a shallow cave a quarter mile further up, because staying where the blood had fallen felt unbearable to all of them. Ivo carried Sable the whole way without speaking, his jaw set like quarried stone, and Freya walked beside them holding a makeshift bandage in place, hating herself with a thoroughness that surprised even her.

Inside the cave, Ivo built a fire with hands that shook only a little, which Freya suspected cost him more effort than lifting Sable had. Freya knelt and did what little she knew of mending flesh—boiled water, cleaned the wound, packed it with the yarrow Ivo produced from a pouch with the grim competence of a man whose family had been miners and whose mining towns had always needed someone who knew wounds. The bolt had missed the lung. Barely. By a finger's width of luck that Freya knew, with sickening clarity, she had not earned.

Sable lay propped against Ivo's rolled cloak, gray-faced, fever already beginning to glitter in her eyes. But when Freya settled beside her, a thread of water rose from the basin they'd brought, wobbling with exhaustion, and shaped itself into words:

*STOP THAT.*

"Stop what?"

*THE FACE. THE ONE WHERE YOU'RE DECIDING IT'S ALL YOUR FAULT AND YOU SHOULD WALK INTO THE SNOW AND SPARE US.* The letters sagged, reformed. *I MOVED. MY CHOICE. YOU DIDN'T PUT THE BOLT IN ME—A MAN WITH A CROSSBOW DID, AND IF YOUR STORM HADN'T SCARED THE REST OF THEM OFF, THERE'D BE THIRTY BOLTS IN BOTH OF US. SO STOP.*

"You could die," Freya said. It came out smaller than she intended. "Because of me. Because I stood in the open like a—" like a woman who had forgotten, for one glorious minute, that she was made of lightning and consequences.

*IF I DIE,* the water wrote, slower now, the letters trembling at the edges, *IT WILL BE AS A WOMAN WHO CHOSE HER PEOPLE OVER COVER. THERE ARE WORSE DEATHS. I'VE SEEN THE CROWN'S FAVORITES.*

Then, because she was Sable, and because apparently even mortal wounds were no excuse for sobriety, the thread of water added: *ALSO YOUR STORM WAS SHOWING OFF. VERY DRAMATIC. THE LIGHTNING WALKING ALONG THE RIDGE. WHO TAUGHT YOU TO POSE LIKE THAT.*

Despite everything—despite the blood on her hands and the fear sitting on her chest like a stone—Freya laughed, a cracked, wet sound that was halfway to a sob. "Nobody. Apparently it's native to me."

*MENACE,* the water agreed, and sank back into the basin, spent.

Ivo crouched at the cave mouth, watching the passes. After a long silence he said, without turning: "What you did tonight. Could you have done it smaller?"

"Yes." The honesty cost her, but she owed him that much. "Some of it. The rest—" She looked at her own hands in the firelight. "The rest wanted to be big. That's the part that frightens me, Ivo. Not the hunters. Not the bolts. The part of me that *enjoyed* it. Nine years I told myself the valley was keeping me safe from the crown. Some nights lately I wonder if I was keeping the world safe from me."

Ivo was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was gravel-soft. "My wife says—I suppose I should say *said*—" his jaw worked, "—my wife used to say that a strong back isn't dangerous. It's a strong back that won't admit what it's carrying. You admitted it tonight. Out loud. To my face." He finally looked at her. "The ones who frighten me are the ones who've never once wondered."

Outside, the stars wheeled slowly over the black teeth of the passes. Somewhere below, campfires dotted the darkness—the hunters, regrouping, waiting for reinforcements. Waiting, perhaps, for word to travel to a certain chief hunter with gray eyes and a ledger for a soul.

Freya sat between her sleeping friends and watched the moonrise. It was fuller than it had been last night. She counted without wanting to.

Twelve turns gone. The thirteenth moon was swelling toward its crest, and beneath it, in the vaults of a palace she could not yet see, hundreds of stolen gifts hung trapped in the air like weather that had nowhere to fall.

Two-thirds of a covenant slept beside a fire in a cave. The third stone waited somewhere ahead, past the ward-labyrinth, past the king, past the thing the king fed.

And Corvus Hale—who no longer needed records, who needed only to look up at the sky—was coming.

Freya pulled her cloak tighter and kept watch until dawn.
