## 14. The Hollow Court

Corvus Hale had attended the king's court for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth knowing.

First: never stand downwind of Lord Chancellor Vess's perfumed robes. Second: the wine served at evening audiences was watered precisely to the point where a man could drink all night and remember nothing, which was not an accident. Third—and this was the lesson that had taken him the longest, because he had resisted it like a man resisting a diagnosis—the crown did not lie to its servants.

It simply never told them anything at all.

He stood now in the antechamber beneath the throne room, where the candles burned blue-white and gave off no heat, and waited to be summoned. The summons had come at midnight, which was itself unusual. Midnight summons were for emergencies, and emergencies, in Corvus's long experience, were almost always manufactured by someone who wanted something.

"You look cheerful," said Wren.

Wren was his second, twenty-three years old, sharp as a paper cut, and the only person in the entire hunting corps whom Corvus trusted further than he could throw her—which, admittedly, was not far; she was built like a keg of nails. She had joined the hunters at seventeen because, she said, it was either that or marry a fishmonger, and she'd smelled both futures and preferred the one with horses.

"I'm always cheerful," Corvus said.

"You're always *competent*. It's different. Cheerful involves the mouth doing something other than judging."

"The mouth is resting."

"Uh-huh." She fell into step beside him as a liveried page appeared and beckoned without a word. "Do you know why we're here? Because I don't know why we're here, and I've asked everyone, and everyone has done that thing where they look at my collar instead of my eyes."

"That's because you're being considered for promotion."

"I'm being considered for *something*. Everyone keeps looking at my collar." She tugged at it. "Corvus. Why are we here?"

And the honest answer—the answer he did not give her, because he was still assembling it himself in the back of his mind like a man fitting together shards of a broken mirror—was: *because nine years ago I hunted a storm-caller out of a valley, and last week I matched her signature to the old records, and when I reported it, the king's steward went pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear of her.*

*Because I have been asking quiet questions ever since, and tonight someone decided it was time I stopped asking and started seeing.*

The page led them down. Not up—down. Past the state rooms, past the guard barracks, past the wine cellars where the good vintages were kept for ambassadors and the bad ones for the king's brothers. Down stairs that were older than the palace above them, hewn from the living rock the palace had been built on top of, the way a man builds a fine house over a grave he prefers not to discuss.

The torches down here burned ordinary orange, and somehow that was worse. Blue-white flame was strange. Orange flame was honest, and it showed him everything: the damp stone, the iron doors, the chains bolted into walls with no cells attached to them, as though the builders had once intended prisoners and then decided the whole corridor was the prison.

"Wren," Corvus said quietly. "If I tell you to run, run."

"If you tell me to run, I'm going to assume it's a test and stay."

"That is exactly the sort of loyalty that gets people killed."

"It's also why you promoted me," Wren said, "so let's not pretend you're complaining."

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was round, and it should not have existed.

Corvus knew the palace's foundations. He had helped design the ward-labyrinth that laced through them—he had drawn half those lines himself, eleven years ago, a young hunter with a steady hand and a head full of doctrine about protection and service. There was no round chamber in the plans. There was a cistern in approximately this location, disused, sealed.

Someone had unsealed it. Someone had hollowed out the rock beneath the heart of the kingdom, and no one had told the man whose job it was to know such things.

In the center of the chamber stood the king.

Not on a throne—there was no throne down here, and that absence struck Corvus harder than any presence could have. King Aldric IV stood in plain boots on bare stone, his crown left upstairs, his hands folded before him with the patience of a man waiting for bread to rise. Around him stood six court mages in grey, their faces slack in a way Corvus recognized from his work: the emptiness of people who had been drained a little, regularly, for years. Batteries that had learned to stand upright.

And beyond the king, filling the far half of the chamber like smoke given architecture—

Corvus had hunted magic his entire adult life. He had seen gifts tear men apart. He had seen a storm-caller's grief level a village square. He had catalogued, in careful ink, forty-one varieties of the uncanny, and he had believed, right up until this moment, that he had a complete taxonomy.

The thing beneath the palace did not fit in any column. It was vast and it was thin, the way a starved dog is thin—great frame, nothing on it. It hung against the ceiling and pooled along the floor and did both at once, which hurt to look at, and it was whispering. Corvus could hear it from across the room, a sound like wind through a keyhole, like a hundred voices agreeing about something just below the threshold of understanding.

"*Hungry,*" it whispered. "*So long. So hungry. But faithful Aldric feeds me. Faithful Aldric will be rewarded.*"

"There he is," the king said warmly, turning. "My chief hunter."

Corvus bowed, because his body had eleven years of habit and habits hold the line even when the mind has begun to desert it. "Your Majesty. You summoned me at midnight."

"I did." The king smiled. He was not, Corvus reflected, an evil-looking man. That was perhaps the most horrifying discovery of the night: that King Aldric IV had a pleasant face, laugh lines, the easy charm of a man who remembered names and birthdays. Evil, in Corvus's experience, rarely looked like anything at all. It looked like a neighbor. It looked like a man waiting for bread to rise. "You've been asking questions, Master Hale."

"I investigate for a living, Your Majesty. Questions are the tools of the trade."

"Mm." The king gestured, and one of the grey-faced mages shuffled aside, revealing what lay behind him.

A cage. Small—child-sized. Iron, but the iron was wrong, threaded through with hair-fine silver wire in patterns Corvus knew intimately, because he had helped refine those patterns. Ward-work. Suppression work. The kind used on adults who fought.

Inside the cage sat a girl of perhaps eight years, with mud-brown hair and a smudge of dirt on her cheek, watching everything with the terrible attentiveness of children who have learned that the world contains things worth watching for.

"She was taken outside Ondu," the king said, conversational, as if discussing a shipment of wool. "A little ember-gift. Nothing much. Lights candles, warms soup. Her village called it a blessing and her mother called it a nuisance." He smiled again. "But small gifts are easier to move, and the thing below has been... particular lately. Fussy. It wants fresh. Do you understand, Master Hale? It wants *fresh*."

Corvus understood that his career, his doctrine, his eleven years of service—all of it had been standing on a floor that was not a floor.

"The culling protects the realm," he heard himself say. His voice came out flat and professional, and he hated how steady it was. "That is the mandate. Uncontrolled gifts are a danger. We contain them. We—"

"We harvest them," the king said pleasantly. "Let us not use small words at this hour; we're all tired. Yes, Master Hale. The witch-hunts are a harvest. They have always been a harvest. Your hunters are reapers with good posture, and your wards—" he tapped the wall affectionately, "—are a fence around a pantry. You built a very fine fence."

The whispering thing stirred overhead, and Corvus felt it notice him—a pressure like deep water, like a thumb testing fruit.

"*This one,*" it breathed. "*This one thinks. This one doubts. Doubt sours the gift. Feed me the child first, and let the doubter watch. Watching ripens them.*"

"Patience," the king murmured to it, the way a man soothes a horse. Then, to Corvus, briskly: "You may observe. Consider it part of your education. You've earned transparency, Master Hale—you're the best servant I have. And afterward, you'll understand why the work must continue. Order requires cost. Someone must be willing to pay it so that thousands needn't."

*There it is,* Corvus thought, with a strange, distant clarity. *There's the sentence. Every atrocity in history fits inside it like a letter in an envelope.*

The grey mages moved. The cage opened. And the little girl—mud-smudged, watchful, eight years old—did not cry. That was the detail that would follow Corvus into every sleepless night for the rest of his life: she didn't cry. She looked at the king, and then at the thing above him, and then, briefly, at Corvus—looked at him the way one looks at the last door in a room full of locked doors—and then she closed her eyes and held out her small hands, palms up, the way children are taught to offer things.

Her gift rose out of her like a candle-flame leaving its wick. A faint gold warmth, trembling, the size of a sparrow's heart. The smallest magic Corvus had ever seen taken.

The thing beneath the palace drank it in one long pull, and shuddered, and sighed with a hundred satisfied mouths, and grew by nothing anyone could measure, and whispered, "*More. Soon. More.*"

The girl slumped in the cage like laundry off a line. Breathing. Empty. Alive, technically, in the way that a field after harvest is alive.

Corvus did not move. He noted, from very far away, that Wren had gone white beside him and that her hand had found his sleeve and gripped it hard enough to bruise, and that she was shaking—not with fear, he realized. With the specific fury of a young woman discovering that the institution she'd chosen over the fishmonger had been rotten from the cellar up.

"Come," the king said cheerfully, clapping Corvus on the shoulder as he passed toward the stairs. "Walk with me a moment. There's a matter in the Salt Valley I want your opinion on."

* * *

They walked up the old stairs side by side, king and hunter, torchlight sliding along the walls. Behind them, the grey mages began the business of moving the cage, and the thing below settled into its whispering patience, and none of it—not one stone of it—would ever be unseen by Corvus Hale again.

"The storm-caller," the king said. "Almeida. You've found her trail, yes? Vess, then Ondu. My stewards tell me you're close."

"Close," Corvus said.

"When she's taken, bring her to me alive. Her gift is large enough to feed the below for a season, perhaps two. And there's a symmetry to it, don't you think? Nine years ago she killed for her power. Now her power will keep the realm safe. There's a justice in that." The king smiled his pleasant smile. "You appreciate justice, Master Hale. It's why you're good at your work."

"Yes," Corvus said. "I appreciate justice."

At the top of the stairs, the king paused. "You saw what must be seen tonight. Most servants go their whole lives without seeing, and they're happier for it. But you—I think you'll serve better for knowing. Knowing makes the choice real." He squeezed Corvus's arm, man to man, comrade to comrade. "Order requires cost. Good night."

He ascended toward his chambers, humming.

Corvus stood in the corridor a long moment. Somewhere above, dawn was still hours away. Somewhere east, a woman with a storm in her blood was running from him, and he had spent weeks telling himself that catching her was protection—that the net was mercy, that the cage was care, that the leash was love of country.

*She killed a man trying to sacrifice a child,* some buried record in his memory whispered, a report he'd skimmed nine years ago and filed under *justification confirmed.* *And I spent nine years working for a man who does it every month.*

"Well," Corvus said aloud, to no one. His voice sounded odd in the empty corridor—rusty, like a gate opening after years. "That's that, then."

"What's that?" Wren stood behind him, arms crossed, eyes red-rimmed and hard. "What do we do? Tell me what we do, because if you say *we report through channels*, I'm going to put you in a cell myself."

Corvus reached into his coat and drew out his commission—the heavy parchment, the seal of black wax, the elegant script naming him Chief Hunter of the Realm, protector of the peace, sword against the uncanny. Eleven years of identity, folded twice over his heart.

He had no flint. He never carried one; a chief hunter had no need of fire when he commanded fire's suppression.

Wren watched him pat his pockets, and understood, because she always understood, and she struck a light off her belt-knife without a word and held it out.

The parchment caught slowly, the way old things burn, reluctant and then all at once. Corvus held it until the flames licked his gloves, until the black wax ran like a wound, until the words *protector* and *realm* and *service* curled into ash and drifted down the corridor toward the stairs that led back down to the thing below.

"Desertion," Wren said. It wasn't a question or an accusation. It was a word being tested, weighed, tried on.

"Treason, probably," Corvus said. "Desertion implies I'm abandoning something worth staying for." He flexed his scorched fingers. "I spent eleven years building the fence around the pantry, Wren. I drew half the lines myself. Which means I know every gap in it."

She stared at him. Then, slowly, the fierce grim thing that was her version of a smile arrived on her face.

"The storm-caller," she said. "You're going to find her first."

"I'm going to find her," Corvus agreed, already walking, fast, toward the stables and the dark and the road east, "and I'm going to do the one thing I swore I never would."

"What's that?"

"Help."
