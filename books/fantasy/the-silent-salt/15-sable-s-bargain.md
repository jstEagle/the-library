## 15. Sable's Bargain

The cave above the tree line had been a shrine once, or a shepherd's shelter, or both—Freya couldn't tell, and neither could the mountain, which seemed to have opinions about the matter and expressed them in drafts that found every gap in the wool blankets.

Sable lay against the far wall where Ivo had propped her, wrapped in his spare coat because her own had been soaked through with blood and tide and could not be saved. Her breathing came in long, careful pulls, like someone counting the steps of a staircase she wasn't sure would hold.

Freya sat beside her and did what she had been doing for two days: she held Sable's hand, and she talked, because Sable could not.

"You know," Freya said, "I've been thinking about what you'd say if you could. Which is a terrible habit, because I keep making you say things I agree with. You'd tell me my stew is an abomination. You'd tell me Ivo snores like a rockslide. You'd tell me—"

Sable's fingers twitched against hers. A small, deliberate pressure. Then, from the puddle of meltwater that had seeped into a hollow of the cave floor—water that had no business moving, this high and this cold, and moved anyway—a shape rose. A ribbon of liquid, thin as a finger, curling upward into letters that hung in the air and then collapsed back into the puddle.

*Stop narrating me. I am not a eulogy yet.*

Freya laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, because that was the thing about Sable: even dying, even voiceless, she was funny in a dry, tidal way, like something the sea had worn smooth and left on the shore for you to find.

"I'm sorry," Freya said. "It's a coping mechanism. I have exactly two of them and the other one is storms."

*The other one is why I'm dying,* the water wrote, and then, quickly, before Freya could flinch: *Joke. That was a joke. Ivo said you'd take everything literally. He was right. He is always right and it is exhausting.*

Ivo, across the cave, was pretending to sharpen an axe that did not need sharpening. He had been sharpening it for two days. The blade had achieved an edge that could probably split moonlight. "I never said exhausting," he rumbled. "I said *trying*."

*You said it about him too,* Sable's water wrote, the ribbon flicking toward Freya. *You are both trying. I am surrounded by trying people. It is the worst deathbed in the history of deathbeds and I want it noted.*

"Noted," Freya said softly. "It's noted."

And then, because the joke had done what jokes do—held the roof up a little longer—the quiet came back, and with it the truth, sitting in the corner of the cave like a third patient.

Sable was dying. Not quickly, not loudly, but certainly. The wound in her side had gone the color of deep water, and no gift Freya had, no storm she might call, could pull the cold out of it. Tide-speakers knew the sea's rhythms, and Sable, Freya realized, knew this rhythm too. She was counting it. She had been counting it since the pass.

*Freya.* The water-ribbon had grown shorter these last hours. The letters came smaller, the way handwriting does when the hand is tired. *Come here. Properly here. Not talking-at me. Here.*

Freya leaned close. Sable's skin was cool in the way of the sea in winter, and her eyes—grey-green, the color of the flooded city of Vess where Freya had first seen a woman speak through water and thought: *there but for the salt go I*—those eyes were still clear. That was the cruelest part. Everything else was failing, and her eyes were still clear.

*The stones,* Sable wrote. The puddle had nearly frozen; Ivo got up without being asked and built up the fire, and the ribbon trembled back to life. *You have yours. Shattered, cracked, held together with stubbornness and string, but yours. Ivo has his. Mine—*

She stopped. The water hung there, unfinished, and Freya watched the shape of a sentence die and be reborn three times before Sable let it stand.

*Mine is not a stone anymore. It was taken with my voice, the day the crown's drainers came to Vess. They took both together. They didn't know the difference. They thought they were taking a gift. They were taking a self.*

"I know," Freya said. "I saw what was left."

*What is left is here.* Sable pressed Freya's hand flat against her own sternum, over the heart. Her skin was cold but underneath, faint as a fish glimpsed in deep water, something moved. A pull. A rhythm. *The tide they could not drain. It is small now. It is the last of me. And I am going to give it to you, and you are going to say no, so let me say the rest before you start.*

Freya had, in fact, already opened her mouth to say no. She closed it.

*I have thought about this for two days, which is more thought than I have given anything since the choir drowned, so do not insult me by thinking you have thought about it more.* The letters came steadier now, the ribbon rising taller, as if the giving of the words fed the water and the water fed Sable and the whole small system of them was running on something stronger than blood. *The covenant needs three. Storm, stone, and tide. You have a storm-heart. Ivo is a mountain with legs and a bad temper. The tide—*

She paused. And when the water wrote again, it wrote slowly, each letter placed like a stone in a harbor wall.

*The tide is me. What remains of me. It can finish my stone. It can bind the third key. But a gift given is not a gift spent—it is a gift *carried*. And I have one thing I need carried.*

"Anything," Freya said, and meant it so completely that it frightened her.

*The choir.*

The word sat in the cave. Ivo stopped sharpening the axe.

*You saw Vess,* Sable wrote. *You saw the flooded streets, the bells under the water. But you did not hear it. Nobody hears it anymore, because the thing that drowned the city took the sound too, and the crown keeps it, down in whatever vault they keep everything, in a jar or a well or a throat. The drowned choir. Two hundred voices. My mother's voice. My teacher's. The boy who sold mussels on the corner and sang off-key on purpose because he liked watching people wince.*

The ribbon wavered. For the first time, the letters lost their precision, blurring like writing seen through moving water.

*I was not in the city when it drowned. That is the whole of my tragedy and the whole of my shame. I was up the coast. I felt the tide turn wrong and I ran and I was too slow and I have spent eleven years speaking through puddles because the sea took my voice as payment for the ones it kept. Do you understand? It was a trade. The sea does not steal. It trades. It kept two hundred voices and it took mine so that someone, someday, would come looking for them with the authority of the water itself.*

Freya's throat ached. "Sable—"

*Let me finish. I am dying. I get to finish.* But the ribbon curled, briefly, around Freya's wrist—a touch, the only touch Sable had left—and the touch said what the letters didn't: *gently, love, gently.*

*When you reforge the covenant, it will demand a memory. Maren told you that. A payment of equal weight to the binding. And you are going to pay, because you are the sort of person who pays, which is why I am not giving this to Ivo, no offense, Ivo.*

"None taken," said Ivo, who had definitely been offended.

*But a covenant with only a payment is a contract. A covenant with a *gift inside it* is a promise. So I am asking you this: when the covenant takes its price from you, give it mine too. Not as payment. As cargo. Carry the drowned choir into the new binding. Fold it into the walls of the world. Let two hundred voices become part of the seal that keeps the realms apart and free. Let my mother sing at the edge of everything, forever, where the thing under the palace can never reach her again.*

Freya could not speak. She looked at Ivo, and Ivo, the mountain, had turned his face to the cave wall, and his shoulders were doing something that a mountain's shoulders should not do.

"I don't know if it's possible," Freya said finally. "I don't know the covenant will take a gift that isn't mine to pay."

*Then it will refuse, and I will have died having asked for something impossible, which is how I have lived, so it is all of a piece.* But the water-ribbon rose higher, and there was something in its movement now that was not Sable being wry. It was Sable being *certain*. *Freya. You called a storm to save a child who was not yours, in a valley where magic is death, knowing what it would cost. You are the only person I have ever met who treats power like a debt instead of a prize. If anyone can smuggle a choir into the bones of the world, it is you.*

She stopped. The letters hung. Then, smaller:

*And I do not want it to end with me. That is the whole of it. That is the small, selfish, human bottom of it. Eleven years I have been a silence with a woman attached. I do not want the silence to be the last thing. I want the singing to be.*

The fire popped. Outside, the wind moved through the pass with a sound like a held breath.

Freya looked down at their joined hands—at the cold skin and the faint, stubborn pull beneath it, the last tide of a drowned city, beating like a second heart.

"If I do this," Freya said, "you have to tell me what your mother's voice sounded like. First. Before. So I know what I'm carrying."

Something happened to Sable's face that had not happened in two days. It was not quite a smile. It was the thing smiles are made from.

*She sounded like the moment before rain,* the water wrote. *Like a door opening in a warm house when you have been out in the cold too long. She sang the morning songs off-key too, but only on purpose, only the high notes, because my father hated it and she loved him and loving people means knowing exactly which wrong notes to sing.*

"Off-key on the high notes," Freya repeated. "I'll remember that."

*You had better.* The ribbon rose one last time, full height now, and the puddle beneath it had begun to glow—faintly, sea-green, the color of phosphor on a night wake. *Now. Take my hand. Both hands. And Ivo, put down the axe, because what I am about to give her is going to look like drowning, and I know how you get.*

"I do not *get*—" Ivo began.

*You get. You get like a landslide gets.*

Freya took Sable's hands. They were cold. The pull beneath the sternum was not.

"Tell me what to do," Freya whispered.

*Nothing. That is the whole instruction. The tide does the work. You only have to not fight it, which I know, for you, is the hardest task in the three realms.*

And the tide rose.

It came up through Sable's hands and into Freya's arms like cold water filling a hold—gentle, irresistible, ancient. Freya gasped. Every instinct she had, every scar from nine years of silence, screamed at her to push back, to wall it out, to be stone. Instead she thought of a door opening in a warm house, and she let go, and she did not fight it.

The tide-gift poured into her and it was *vast*. It was the memory of every shore. It was the patience of water that has been working on the same cliff for ten thousand years and is not finished and is not worried about it. It was grief, yes—grief in layers, strata of it, eleven years of speaking through puddles—but underneath the grief, holding it up like a seabed holds an ocean, was something else. Something that felt, unmistakably, like *love that had decided to become useful*.

Somewhere in the pouring, the cave filled with sound.

It was not loud. It was the opposite of loud. It was the sound of a choir heard from very far away, through very deep water—two hundred voices, blurred and beautiful, singing something that had no words Freya knew and needed none. The high notes were off-key. All of them. On purpose. Freya understood, suddenly and completely, that the whole drowned choir had learned to sing the way Sable's mother sang, because that is what choirs are: people agreeing, note by note, on how to be wrong together.

She was crying. She couldn't say when that had started.

The glow in the puddle faded. The ribbon of water settled back into the hollow of the floor, and did not move again, and Freya knew—with a certainty that lived in her bones now, in the tide-bones Sable had just given her—what that stillness meant.

But Sable's eyes were still open. Still clear. And her mouth—her mouth was *moving*, and though no sound came, Freya found she could read it now, the way you read water, the way you read tide, the way you read someone you have carried inside you:

*There. Now I am not a silence. Now I am cargo.*

"Sable—" Freya's voice broke. "Sable, I've got them. I've got all of them. Every voice. I can hear them. I will carry them to the edge of everything, I swear it, I swear it on the storm and the stone and the—"

*Stop narrating,* Sable mouthed, and her eyes were laughing. *You are so bad at this. You are the worst deathbed companion in the history of deathbeds.*

"Then die slower," Freya begged, and heard how small it sounded, and did not take it back.

Sable's hand found hers one more time and squeezed—once, twice, three times. A rhythm. A tide-pull. *In. Out. In. Out.* The rhythm of a shore. The rhythm of a door opening in a warm house.

*Go,* her lips said. *The moon is nearly full. Go and build the walls of the world, and put my mother in them, high notes and all.*

*And Freya—*

The last of the glow gathered in Sable's eyes, sea-green and clear, and her mouth shaped the final words slowly, so there could be no mistake:

*When the covenant asks for its price—and it will ask, it always asks—do not flinch. Pay it. Whatever it asks. Because I have learned the last great secret of the sea, in this cold cave, at the end of everything:*

*The water does not keep what it takes. It only makes it part of the shore.*

Her hand went still in Freya's, still as deep water, still as a harbor at dawn.

Ivo made a sound like a mountain deciding not to fall. Freya bowed her head over Sable's hands and let the tide inside her do what tides do, which is carry things.

* * *

They buried her at dawn, high on the pass, in ground Ivo broke open with his bare hands because, he said, "stone should do this, not iron." There was no marker. Sable would have hated a marker. Instead Ivo set a small hollow of rock at the head of the grave, and Freya filled it with meltwater, and they watched, all three of them—the two living and the one newly everywhere—while the water caught the sunrise and turned it sea-green.

Freya stood a long time. The tide-gift sat in her chest like a second heartbeat, patient and vast, and inside it, faint and constant, the choir sang. Off-key on the high notes.

"We should move," Ivo said at last, gruff and gentle at once. "The moon turns in four nights. And the road to the rift runs through the palace wards, and I do not know the way, and neither do you."

"No," Freya said. "But I know someone who does."

She had been feeling it since the burial began—a signature on the edge of her new senses, storm-adjacent and wrong, familiar the way a scar is familiar. Someone had been shadowing the pass for two days. Someone who moved like a hunter and hesitated like a man reading a letter he wished he hadn't opened.

Ivo followed her gaze to the ridgeline, where the morning mist was parting around a figure in a travel-stained coat, standing very still, making no effort at all to hide.

"Is that," Ivo said slowly, "the chief witch-hunter of the realm?"

"Yes," Freya said. The choir sang. The tide pulled. The thirteenth moon hung, three-quarters full, over the palace far to the south. "And I think he's come to surrender."

Corvus Hale raised one hand—not a threat, not quite a greeting—and began to walk down toward them through the snow.
