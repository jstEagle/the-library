## 16. The Road to the Rift

He came down the mountain the way a man walks to his own trial: steady, unhurried, and entirely aware that everyone watching him had excellent reasons to kill him.

Freya counted her assets while he descended. One oath-stone in her pocket, warm as a sleeping bird. Ivo beside her, who could raise a wall of living granite before you finished insulting him. Sable's tide-gift coiled around her wrist beneath the bandage—a cold, patient pressure, like the sea waiting for permission. And herself, which was either an asset or the entire problem, depending on the hour.

Corvus Hale stopped ten paces away. Up close he looked worse than his reputation: gray at the temples, a healing cut along his jaw, and the particular hollow-eyed look of a man who had recently seen something that rearranged his understanding of his own life.

"Freya Almeida," he said.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Freya said. "I know exactly who you are."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, perhaps, come to pay its respects.

"I burned my commission four days ago," he said. "I'm told that makes me either a traitor or the only honest man left in the crown's service. I've stopped trying to decide which."

"And we should trust you because?"

"We shouldn't." He said it plainly, without flinching, and somehow that was more disarming than any plea would have been. "Trust is for people who haven't read each other's files. You should *use* me. There's a difference. Trust can betray you. Usefulness is verifiable."

Ivo shifted his weight, and the ground under Corvus's boots settled half an inch deeper—just enough to be noticed. A mountain-wright's way of clearing his throat.

"He knows the palace wards," Freya said to Ivo, not taking her eyes off the hunter.

"I heard," Ivo said. "I also remember whose signature was on the orders for Ondu prison."

"My hand drew them," Corvus said. "My seal approved them. And every night since, I've been drawing them again from memory—in the dirt, in ash, on my own arm when there was nothing else—because I intend to walk us through them and then watch them fail." He looked at Freya directly. "I helped design a cage, Mistress Almeida. Let me be the one to open it."

The choir-memory stirred under Freya's skin, Sable's drowned voices humming their low warning note. *Careful*, they seemed to say. *But also: listen.*

"Walk with us, then," Freya said. "Behind us. Ten paces. If your hands move toward a weapon, Ivo will introduce you to the mountain."

"I'd deserve it," Corvus said, and fell into step.

* * *

They traveled south through the high passes, three days from the rift, and it was the strangest company Freya had ever kept—which was saying something, given that one of her companions was technically deceased and spoke through her veins.

Corvus, it turned out, was not what nine years of nightmares had built him. The nightmare version was tall and pitiless and smelled of smoke. The real version argued with Ivo about trail rations, lost gracefully, and had a habit of naming things.

"That's a peregrine," he said the first morning, pointing at a speck wheeling over the col. "There's a pair nesting on the south face. They'll take rabbits mostly, but they'll dive at anything shiny, so keep the stone covered."

"How do you know birds?" Freya asked, before she could stop herself.

"Hunters learn everything about a landscape except how to belong to it." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "Birds are easier than people. They only lie with their feathers."

Ivo snorted. "That's the most honest thing a crown's man has ever said."

"I was very good at my work," Corvus said. "You should all be much more frightened of me than you are."

"We're working on it," Freya said, and was alarmed to find she almost meant it warmly.

* * *

On the second evening, camped in the shelter of a leaning granite spine, Corvus unrolled his maps by firelight and became, briefly, terrifying.

"The palace has three rings of wards," he said, tapping the parchment. "The outer ring is old—the covenant-era work, actually. Your predecessors built it to keep things *in* as much as out. It reads intent. You cannot bluff it. But—" he glanced up at Freya, and there was something almost apologetic in it— "it was keyed to the crown's authority. Which I held until four days ago. My seal still opens the outer gates. They haven't revoked it because revoking a seal of that rank requires the king himself, and the king hasn't been... available."

"Available," Ivo repeated flatly.

"He spends most of his time below now," Corvus said. "With it." He let that sit in the firelight a moment. "The middle ring is mine. Twelve years ago I rebuilt it after the last breach. It reads magic itself—not intent, not allegiance, just the raw fact of power. Any gifted person crossing it lights up like a beacon." His eyes found Freya's wrist, where the tide-gift slept under its wrappings. "Which is a problem for you specifically. You're carrying enough stored magic to light up the whole ring at once."

"So how do we cross?" Freya asked.

"There are two ways through the middle ring. The gates, which require my seal and put us in front of forty guards and whatever the thing below sends up to look through their eyes. Or the cisterns." He traced a line under the palace's foundations on the map. "The middle ring dips under the waterways—it had to; you can't ward running water cleanly. Tide-work especially confuses it. Water remembers where it's been. A warded boundary hates that."

Freya felt the cold coil at her wrist shift, attentive. Sable's gift, listening to talk of water the way a dog listens for its name.

"Sable would have loved that," Freya said quietly. "A door made of the one thing the crown couldn't hold."

For the first time, Corvus looked genuinely uncomfortable. "I knew her file," he said. "Tide-speaker, Vess. Voice taken in the fourth draining. I signed the requisition order for the vessel they used." He met Freya's eyes. "I won't ask forgiveness. I'm telling you so you know I understand exactly what walks beside you, and why it might want my blood, and that I'll stand still if it comes for it."

The choir hummed under Freya's skin—Sable's memory, weighing him. It did not feel like vengeance. It felt like sorrow with a long view.

"She says," Freya translated, though no words were needed, "that the water forgives faster than people do, and that this is not a compliment to either."

Corvus bowed his head slightly. "Fair."

* * *

The third night, Freya woke to find Corvus sitting watch alone, feeding the fire stick by stick, and instead of going back to sleep she sat down across from him.

"You knew my file too," she said.

"Yes."

"Then say it. Whatever's in it. I'd rather hear it from you than carry it around as a mystery."

He considered her for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, the peregrines called once in the dark.

"Storm-caller, Salt Valley," he recited softly. "Nine years ago. Village elder Aldous Crane, killed by lightning strike during a confrontation. No witnesses willing to speak. Subject fled to the valley and could not be pursued." He closed the recitation like a book. "It was the strangest file I ever read. Every other gifted person we hunted, the record showed escalation—small acts growing large. Yours began at the top. Full storm, first recorded use. I never believed it."

"Why not?"

"Because storms don't come from nowhere." He poked the fire. "Power like that builds. Pressure, years of it. A girl doesn't summon a killing storm out of a clear sky unless the sky inside her was already breaking." He looked up. "What did Crane do?"

Freya's throat tightened. In the high passes, around another fire, she had confessed it once—to Ivo, to Sable's fading warmth—and it had not killed her, which had surprised her. Saying it a second time was easier, the way a road gets easier the more feet travel it.

"He was feeding a child to something," she said. "The same thing that's under the palace. I didn't know what. I only knew he had the child and there were knives and he was singing in a language I didn't recognize, and my whole life came out of my hands before I decided anything." She watched the fire. "Your records say murder. The truth is I don't know what word fits. I've had nine years to choose one and I still haven't."

Corvus nodded slowly. "In my trade we had a rule. Never write down the part of the story you can't verify, because someday someone will read only the page you wrote." He was quiet a moment. "I wrote yours. I'm sorry for it."

"Will you unwrite it? When this is done?"

"If there's anyone left to read it," he said, "yes."

Above the southern horizon, past the shoulders of the mountains, the thirteenth moon climbed—three-quarters full now and swelling, hung low and wrong-colored, bruised gold at the rim. Even from here Freya could feel it pulling, a tide in her chest answering a tide in the sky. Under her wrist-wrappings, the choir sang a single sustained note that had been rising for three days.

"Three days," Ivo said from his bedroll, without opening his eyes. He was always awake when it mattered; it was deeply annoying. "Two, if the weather holds ugly."

"The weather will hold ugly," Freya said. "I'll see to it personally."

* * *

They came down out of the passes on the second day, and the world changed the way it does near the capital: roads widened, then filled, then crowded. Refugees, mostly, flowing the wrong way—away from the palace, away from the rift that yawned invisibly above it. Freya walked among them hooded, and learned quickly that she needn't have bothered. Nobody was looking for a storm-caller anymore. Everyone was looking at the sky.

The moon was nearly full. At midday. Visible. That was new.

"It started showing at dawn two days ago," a carter told them at a watering stop, in the tone of a man discussing crop blight. "And the drained ones—you've seen the drained ones walking about? They've stopped being empty. They stand in fields at night with their hands up, catching. Like they're full of weather looking for a sky." He shook his head and clucked his mule onward.

Corvus went very still. "The drainings," he said. "When the covenant breaks fully, everything they took—"

"Goes home," Freya finished. "Or goes anywhere. All at once." She thought of the vaults beneath the palace, of hundreds of stolen gifts hanging in the dark like trapped lightning, and of the thing that had eaten them for years suddenly finding its meal walking off its plate. "We're not just reforging a seal, are we. We're setting off a reservoir."

"Now you understand the timeline," Corvus said grimly. "It isn't just that the moon shatters the old covenant. It's that when the gifts release, the thing below starves in an instant—after years of feast. Starving things do not go gently." He looked at each of them in turn. "We reforge the covenant before the crest, or we fight what comes up out of those vaults with nothing sealed and nothing spared."

"Wonderful," said Ivo. "I'll bring extra rock."

* * *

They saw the palace at dusk on the third day, from the ridge above the river road—and Freya, who had prepared herself for dread, was instead struck dumb by grief.

The rift hung over it like a wound in the world's skin. Not a crack, not a tear—an *opening*, wide as a cathedral, edges shimmering with the bruised gold of the moon, and through it, faintly, a light that was not light and a sound that was not sound. Below it the palace glittered, serene and lit, ordinary people moving through ordinary evenings under a hole in the sky.

And threaded between the palace and the rift—visible now even to ungifted eyes, visible to the refugees pointing from the road—hung strands. Hundreds of them. Gossamer-thin, drifting like silk in wind, each one glimmering faintly with captured color. Each one a gift. Someone's grandmother's green thumb. Someone's son's way with horses. Someone's little daughter's song, stretched taut between a vault and a starving god.

"Oh," Ivo said softly. "Oh, that's *ugly*."

"They can see them now," Corvus murmured. "Everyone can. The closer to full, the thinner the veil." His jaw worked. "I spent twelve years telling myself I was building walls to protect people. From up here it looks like scaffolding around a theft."

Freya stood with the wind coming off the river, the choir singing high and urgent under her skin, the tide-gift pulling southward like a compass needle, her own storm-heart rolling restlessly against her ribs. Three pieces of a broken promise, walking toward the place it broke.

"Well," she said. "We know the way in. We know what waits. We know the deadline." She pulled her hood back and let the wind have her hair, and let the last of the hiding go. "Outer gate at midnight. Your seal, Corvus. Then the cisterns."

"And if your seal's been revoked?" Ivo asked.

"Then," Corvus said, "we improvise. I'm told that's what you people do best."

They started down the ridge together—three oath-keepers and a deserter, walking into the teeth of the brightest-lit darkness in the realm—and behind them, above them, the thirteenth moon swelled another fraction fuller, and deep beneath the palace, something that had fed for years lifted its head and tasted the air.

It had begun, Freya realized, to sing back.
