## 17. Thirteenth Turn Rising

Corvus Hale had designed the palace wards the way a man designs his own coffin: carefully, and with the hope he'd never have to lie in it.

He stood now at the edge of the Rift Quarter, three nights before the thirteenth moon would crest, and looked up at what he had helped make possible. The rift hung above the palace like a wound in the sky—a seam of pale light where the air simply stopped agreeing to be air. It had been a hairline crack when he'd first seen it, five years ago, thin as a scratch on glass. Now it yawned wide enough that he could see movement inside it: shapes turning slowly, like fish under ice.

And around it, hanging in the night air for half a mile in every direction, was the weather of stolen gifts.

That was the only way Corvus could think to describe it. The drained magic of hundreds of captured men, women, and children did not vanish when the king's machines pulled it out of them. It rose. It pooled above the palace in slow, luminous banks—green-gold wisps of tide-gift from Vess, deep amber threads of wright-craft from Ondu, silver flickers of storm-sense from the northern coasts—all of it trapped beneath the rift like fog under a lid, pressing down on the city with a weight no one else seemed to feel.

The people of the capital called it *the glow* and thought it beautiful. They held festivals beneath it. Children caught stray motes of it in jars and sold them at market.

Corvus knew better. It wasn't decoration. It was inventory.

"You're doing the face again," said Freya Almeida beside him.

He glanced down at her. She was wrapped in a plain traveler's cloak, hood back, salt-white hair catching the rift-light, looking entirely too calm for a woman standing in the heart of the kingdom that wanted her head. "What face?"

"The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic and the numbers keep coming out wrong."

"That is an accurate description of my entire career," Corvus said. "Come. We lose the dark if we stand here admiring my failures."

Behind them, Ivo shifted the pack on his broad shoulders—the one holding the two whole oath-stones and the fragments of the third—and muttered something in mountain dialect that Corvus chose not to translate. Sable's gift rode with them too, folded into a vial of seawater Freya carried against her heart. Four keepers' worth of covenant, walking into the trap's mouth on purpose.

Corvus still hadn't entirely decided whether they were brave or insane. He suspected the distinction didn't matter anymore.

* * *

The ward-labyrinth began at the old granary gate, and Corvus felt it the moment they crossed its outermost thread—a faint pressure behind the eyes, like a hand testing a door handle from the other side.

"Stop," he murmured, and raised one fist. The others halted. "From here, we walk exactly where I walk. Not a step ahead, not a step behind. The wards don't read faces. They read intention."

"Meaning what?" Ivo rumbled.

"Meaning if you think about attacking a guard, the ward knows before your boots do. If you think about running, same. For the next hour, all four of you are going to be the most boring people in this city. Think about bread. Think about laundry. Think about absolutely nothing."

"Nothing," Freya repeated. "You want me to think about nothing while walking into the place that's been hunting me for nine years."

"I want you to think about nothing *quietly*," Corvus said. "It's a skill. I've had practice." He paused, then added, because it seemed only fair to warn them: "There's also a verse. The wards respond to a countersign—it's a nursery rhyme, actually. The king's grandfather set it so his own household could move freely. It's been changed twice since, but the melody stays; wards are sentimental that way."

"A nursery rhyme," Ivo said flatly.

"'The miller's daughter counts her sheep, / one for waking, two for sleep—'" Corvus began, and Ivo made a sound like a landslide deciding against happening.

"We're going to reforge the covenant of the three realms," Freya said, "humming a children's song about sheep."

"Would you prefer the alternative? Because the alternative is that the wards peel your intentions open like fruit and every hunter in the palace knows precisely where we are within ten breaths." Corvus started forward. "Sheep, Almeida. Count sheep."

To her credit, she fell into step behind him without further argument. But he could feel her mind behind him like heat from a forge—not empty, not boring, but *disciplined*, which was somehow worse and better at once. She wasn't thinking of nothing. She was thinking of one thing, very hard, and keeping everything else behind a wall of salt and storm.

He didn't ask what the one thing was. He suspected it was the thirteenth moon, or the vault below, or possibly the exact dimensions of his throat. All reasonable options.

* * *

They moved through the Rift Quarter in the dead hours, when even the festival-drunk city slept off its glow. Corvus led them down a service stair, across a cistern yard, through a tunnel beneath the old aviary where the ward-threads ran thick as spider silk overhead—invisible, but he could map them by memory, and by the way the air tasted faintly of ozone where they crossed.

At the third checkpoint, a pair of junior hunters stood drowsing over a brazier. Corvus walked straight toward them, unhurried, wearing the gray commission coat he'd kept for exactly this purpose, and the others melted into the shadow of the archway behind him.

"Hale," said the younger one, straightening. "Thought you'd resigned."

"Sick leave," Corvus said. "The healers say sea air. I'm getting it secondhand." He held up a sealed ledger. "Ward audit. Chief Warden's orders. You'll want to log me through."

The older hunter squinted at the ledger, then at him. "Audit? Night before the turn?"

"Best time for it," Corvus said pleasantly. "Everyone's watching the sky. Nobody's watching the walls. That's rather the point of an audit, isn't it?"

It took eleven seconds. He counted them. Then the man waved him through, and Corvus walked past the brazier with his spine straight and his heartbeat perfectly, professionally steady, and did not exhale until the archway had swallowed him and the shadows closed around the four of them again.

"Sea air," Freya whispered.

"It was the first lie I thought of."

"You're getting worse at these."

"I'm getting *tired* at these," Corvus corrected. "There's a difference. Come on. The worst part's ahead."

* * *

The worst part was the Gallery of Names.

He'd named it that himself, years ago, in a report nobody read: the long corridor outside the vault antechambers where the drained gifts passed closest to the surface, close enough that the trapped weather pressed down through the stone like a thumb on wet clay. The walls there were lined with the registry of the taken—every captured gift-worker, every child, catalogued in the crown's neat hand. Corvus had written a third of those entries himself.

Now he stood at its entrance with three oath-keepers and a vial of drowned song, and the weight above them was so heavy that Ivo staggered, bracing a hand against the wall.

"What *is* that?" the mountain-wright gasped. His voice came out cracked. "It's like—like standing under a river that won't fall."

"The harvest," Corvus said quietly. "Every gift the king has taken since the draining began. It doesn't dissipate. It waits. He means to feed it through the rift when the moon crests—all of it at once."

Freya had gone very still. In the green-gold light seeping between the flagstones, her face looked carved from something older than grief. "How many?"

Corvus could have lied. A month ago he would have; the number was a weapon either way. Instead he said, "Six hundred and forty-one registered. Perhaps eighty more unregistered. Children included."

The silence that followed had texture. Sable's vial, against Freya's chest, gave off a faint pulse of blue, like a heartbeat remembering itself.

"And you fed it," Freya said. Not accusing. Just asking, the way you ask a question whose answer you already carry.

"For nine years I told myself I was keeping order," Corvus said. "That a controlled drain prevented uncontrolled catastrophe. That the alternative was panic, and panic kills more people than patience does." He looked up the corridor, at the neat rows of names marching away into the dark. "I was very good at telling myself things. It's the one craft I've truly mastered."

Ivo grunted. "Man who builds the cage always thinks he's the zookeeper."

"Yes," Corvus said. "Something like that." He squared his shoulders. "The antechamber is thirty paces ahead. After that, my maps end and my guesses begin. So—if anyone wants to count sheep, do it now."

* * *

The antechamber doors were warded with the full lattice—the one he'd designed after the incident with the Vess delegation, triple-threaded, keyed to royal blood and hunter's oath both. There was no rhyme for this one. There was no trick.

There was only the fact that Corvus Hale's oath, burned into the ward-stone nine years ago, still recognized its maker.

"It will know I've broken faith," he said, palm hovering over the lock-plate. "The moment I touch it, every alarm in the palace will sing. We'll have perhaps four minutes before the guard responds—less if the king is awake, and he'll be awake. He hasn't slept properly since the rift widened past a handspan."

"So we run," Freya said.

"So we run." He almost smiled. "I spent my whole career making sure no one could ever run in this building. It seems fitting that I spend my last night in it proving myself wrong."

Freya looked at him then—really looked, the way she'd mostly refused to since the passes, since the day he'd appeared on their road with his commission burned and his reasons unspoken. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something, because she nodded once, sharp, and turned to the others.

"Ivo. Stones ready. When we reach the vault floor, you start the circle and don't stop for anything, including me screaming. Sable—" her hand went to the vial, and her voice gentled in a way Corvus had never heard from her, "—we'll carry you the rest of the way. I promise."

The water pulsed blue. Twice. Which, Corvus had learned, meant *yes*, and also *hurry*.

Corvus set his palm flat against the lock-plate.

The ward recognized him the way an old hound recognizes a master who has come home smelling of another pack—a moment of joyful recognition, then confusion, then a rising note of betrayal that climbed through the stone and up through the floors and out into the night, ringing bell upon bell upon bell.

"There it is," Corvus said, as the doors ground open onto darkness and cold air breathed out from below, air that smelled of starlight going stale. "Four minutes. Possibly three."

Above them, through the distant eye of the corridor, the thirteenth moon slid free of the clouds—swollen, silver, and nearly full—and the rift above the palace answered with a long, low shudder, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep.

And from far below, up through the vaults, through six hundred and forty-one names and the weather of their stolen selves, something whispered a single word that all four of them heard at once:

*"Finally."*

Corvus Hale, who had spent nine years believing himself incapable of fear, discovered he'd merely been saving it up.

"After you," he said, and stepped into the dark.
