## 18. The Vault of Stolen Sky

The stairs went down farther than the palace had any right to be deep.

Freya counted the steps at first, because counting was something to do with her hands when her hands were shaking—except her hands weren't shaking, actually, they were perfectly steady, and it was everything *else* that trembled: the air, the torchlight, Corvus's jaw where he ground his teeth like a millstone.

"Four hundred and twelve," she said, mostly to hear her own voice. "Four hundred and thirteen. Does anyone else feel like we're walking down the throat of something?"

"Yes," said Ivo, without breaking stride.

"No," said Corvus, which from Corvus meant *yes*.

Sable didn't answer. Sable never answered anymore; she spoke through water, and there was no water here except what Freya carried for her in a stoppered flask against her hip. But Freya felt the flask go warm, and felt the meaning arrive anyway, wordless and cold as a hand on the back of her neck:

*It knows we're coming.*

"Well," Freya said. "Good. Saves us knocking."

The last hundred steps were stone that had never known sunlight, and then the stairs simply stopped, opening out into a vault so vast that Freya's eyes refused, for several long moments, to believe in it.

She had expected a cellar. A dungeon. A room with chains and a thing in them.

What she got was sky.

That was the only word for it. The ceiling of the vault arched up and away into darkness shot through with drifting lights—not stars, she realized, not quite, but *weather*. Ribbons of color turned slowly overhead like aurora caught in amber, and threaded through them, dimmer than the rest but unmistakable, hung hundreds of small bright knots of light, each one pulsing gently, each one breathing.

Each one, she understood with a sick lurch, was a gift. A stolen self. The tide-speaker's voice. The ember-wright's warmth. Six hundred and forty-one names, Corvus had said, and here they all were, hung up like lanterns in a borrowed heaven while the thing below drank them slow.

"Oh," Freya whispered. "Oh, that's *cruel*. It made itself a sky."

"It made itself a *larder*," Ivo growled. His hands were already moving over the nearest wall, reading the mountain-stone the way other men read letters. "This whole vault sits on bedrock older than the kingdom. The binding chains run through it—here, here, and here." He tapped three points. "The stones go at the joints. That part is simple."

"And the part that isn't simple?" Corvus asked.

Ivo looked at him. "The part where we're still standing in its mouth."

* * *

They crossed the vault floor in silence after that, because some silences are chosen and some are simply the correct response to being small. The floor was salt-white stone—salt, Freya thought bitterly, even here, even under the palace, the world couldn't stop reminding her—and at its center rose the thing the whole architecture existed to serve.

A well, or a pit, or a throat. And rising out of it—

Not a body, exactly. The entity had no body the way a fire has no shape; it took the outline of whatever would hold it longest. Right now it wore darkness stitched with light, and the light it wore was *stolen*, threads pulled from the hanging gifts above until each knot flickered a little weaker, and Freya felt her stomach turn because she recognized the trick. She'd seen it before. In a mirror. In herself, nine years ago, in the moment her grief had reached for the storm and the storm had reached back.

The thing beneath the palace was hungry the way she had been hungry. That was the horror of it. Not monstrous. *Familiar.*

*"Storm-caller."*

The voice arrived inside her skull rather than her ears, vast and unhurried, the way weather arrives. It did not boom. That was somehow worse—it spoke the way an old friend speaks across a quiet kitchen.

*"You came all this way. Through my moon, past my hunter—"*

"He's not your hunter," Freya said aloud, and heard Corvus shift behind her. "*Was.* Past *my* hunter."

*"Mm,"* the entity said, in the tone of someone declining to argue about furniture. *"And yet here you stand, in my house, on my stone, above my hunger. Do you know how long I have waited for someone who could actually finish this? Nine years ago you killed an old man with a bolt out of a clear sky. Do you know what that was?"*

"A murder," Freya said flatly.

*"A signature,"* the entity said. *"A knock at my door. Every covenant has a seam, little storm, and every seam needs a hand strong enough to tear it. I have been pulling at mine for three hundred years. You nearly tore it for me by accident, in one afternoon, over one foolish old man."*

Freya's mouth went dry. Behind her, she heard Ivo mutter, "Stones. Now. While it talks, it isn't eating."

But the entity wasn't finished. And this was the moment Freya would think about later, in the years afterward, when she tried to explain to people who hadn't been there why she had almost said yes:

Because the light overhead shifted. And in the shifting, the vault changed.

* * *

It happened gently. That was the unforgivable part.

One moment she stood in a vault of stolen sky; the next, the salt-white floor was warm earth under bare feet, and the air smelled of brine and cut hay, and somewhere ahead of her the salt-pans glittered gold in late-afternoon sun—the eastern pans, her pans, the ones she'd tended every morning for nine years until her shoulders knew the rake better than her own name.

Tam was there, laughing at something, his sleeves rolled up. His daughter sat on the dike wall swinging her legs. Old Marta waved from the drying-sheds. And the valley rose green and complete around it all, every hill, every fence, every crooked gate she'd ever leaned on, rendered so perfectly that her chest ached with the accuracy of it.

Her mother stood by the gate.

Not the memory of her mother—the real one, or something wearing the real one's face, flour on her forearms, that particular half-smile she saved for when Freya came home muddy and pretending she hadn't been crying.

"You sold a day," her mother said. "In Ondu. Our last afternoon together. I remember it, love. You don't. Doesn't that seem backwards? Let me give it back."

"Don't," Freya said. Her voice cracked straight down the middle.

"The elder," her mother went on, gently, terribly, walking closer across ground that didn't exist. "The man you killed. Undo it. Not forgiven—*unmade*. He lives, you never lifted your hand, you never left home at all. Nine years of exile, erased like chalk in rain. And the valley—" she spread her arms, and the whole valley spread with them, hills and salt-pans and smoke off evening chimneys— "the valley stays exactly as it is. Quiet forever. Safe forever. No hunters. No thirteenth moon. All you have to do is put these stones down and walk away and let me keep drinking."

"That's not my mother."

"Isn't she?" Her mother tilted her head, and for one horrible instant Freya saw through the mask—saw the vast patient hunger underneath, wearing a dead woman's smile like a borrowed coat. "I've eaten six hundred and forty-one selves, storm-caller. I know how memory tastes. I'm not offering you a lie. I'm offering you the truth, arranged kindly. There is no version of your future that contains both the covenant and the valley. Choose which you'd rather mourn."

Behind her, faintly, she heard Ivo swearing at a joint that wouldn't seat. Heard Sable's flask rattling against her hip like a warning bell. Heard Corvus say, low and steady, "Freya. It lies by telling truths. That's the oldest trick in the vault."

And the mother-that-was-not stepped close enough to touch, and held out a hand full of afternoon light.

"Come home," it said, in her mother's voice, with her mother's exact warmth, and that was the knife of it—that the warmth was *real*, harvested, kept fresh in a jar under the world for who knew how long. Somewhere in those hanging lights, or in this creature's endless gullet, was the true taste of that afternoon, and it was being offered back to her like a bone tossed to a dog.

Freya looked at the offered hand for a long time.

Then she started, quietly, to laugh.

* * *

"What," said the entity, and for the first time its voice lost a fraction of its weather-calm.

"I'm sorry." Freya wiped her eyes. "It's just—you've eaten six hundred souls and three centuries of scheming, and your best offer is *the life I already ran away from?*" She shook her head. "You've been down here so long you've forgotten what people are. My mother used to say I had my grandmother's stubbornness and my grandfather's sense of when to leave a bad bargain. You're a very bad bargain."

*"I am offering you everything you've mourned for nine years—"*

"You're offering me a *jar*," Freya said. "A beautiful jar with my valley pickled inside it. And here's what you don't understand, because nothing down here has ever loved anything it didn't intend to swallow—" She stepped forward, right up to the edge of the false afternoon, into the smell of hay and brine, and let her storm rise through her like dawn. "My mother wouldn't want to be your bait. Tam wouldn't. Marta wouldn't. They'd be furious. And if there's one thing I learned in nine years of tending salt, it's this—"

Lightning woke along her arms, gentle as lamplight.

"—the valley was never the place. It was the people. And the people are *upstairs*, and *out there*, and they are going to be free of you if I have to reforge the sky to do it."

The illusion shattered like frost under a boot.

The vault snapped back around them—white stone, hanging lights, the pit, the dark—and the entity's voice, when it came again, had stopped pretending to be kind.

*"Then bleed for it,"* it said. *"As I bled. As they all bled."*

Above them, the six hundred and forty-one lights flared at once, screaming silently, and the rift far overhead tore open another handspan wider, and the thirteenth moon poured itself down through the gap like silver oil, and Freya Almeida turned to her companions—her impossible, exhausted, magnificent companions—with the first oath-stone already burning in her fist.

"Ivo. Joints. Now. Sable—give me the tide."

And the pit began, at long last, to fight back.
