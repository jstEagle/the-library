## 20. The Price of Seals

The light did not fade so much as settle.

When the thirteenth moon crested and the last thread of storm left Freya's hands—woven through Ivo's stone, carried on Sable's tide-gift, anchored by the stubborn weight of her own heart—the vault of stolen sky went quiet in a way that vaults are not supposed to go quiet. The bound thing beneath the palace stopped whispering. The rift above yawned once more, a vast indrawn breath, and then closed like a mouth deciding, at last, to say nothing.

Freya knelt on the cracked flagstones with her palms flat against the floor and felt the covenant complete itself through her bones. It was not loud. That was the strangest part. Nine years of dread, three realms of road, a dying friend's gift, a traitor's confession, a hunter turned deserter standing at her shoulder with his burned commission still smelling of ash—and it all ended in a silence as small and total as the one she had lived inside at Salt Valley.

"It's done," Ivo said behind her. His voice was rough. He had said little during the forging; mountain-wrights spoke best with their hands, and his hands had trembled against the third stone until the very last moment, when they had gone steady as bedrock.

"I know," Freya said.

"You're crying."

"I'm aware of that too."

Corvus crouched beside her—not touching, never quite touching, but near enough that she could feel the warmth of him, which after everything was its own kind of miracle. "Is it supposed to feel like that?" he asked. "Like the whole world just exhaled?"

"No," Freya said, and then, honestly: "I don't know. Nobody alive has reforged a covenant before. We're writing the manual as we bleed."

That earned a sound from Corvus that might have been a laugh in a man who remembered how. Ivo actually did laugh, short and startled out of him, and for one suspended heartbeat the three of them were just people kneeling in a ruined vault under an ordinary moon, alive, absurdly, against every reasonable expectation.

Then the covenant asked for its payment.

* * *

It came not as a voice but as a knowing, the way you know you have left the stove lit or forgotten to say goodbye. A pressure behind Freya's eyes. An accounting.

*A binding of this weight,* the covenant said, in the language beneath language, the one Sable had spoken through water and Ivo through stone and Freya through storm, *requires a memory of equal weight. Something held as long. Something loved as deep.*

Freya's stomach dropped. She thought—I will be honest, she thought it in a single white-hot flash of panic—that it meant Sable. That it would take Sable from her, the memory of drowned choir-song and webbed fingers signing patience, the way Sable had pressed her tide-gift into Freya's hands and smiled with her eyes because her voice was long gone. She thought it meant Ivo, his terrible honesty, his family lost and rebuilt in grief. She thought it might mean the storm itself, and she braced against that too, because the storm was hers, had always been hers, even when it killed, even when it saved.

But the covenant was precise. Covenants always are. That is their whole nature.

*The valley,* it said. *The valley, and all it holds.*

And Freya, who had spent nine years learning what things cost, understood immediately that this was fair.

"Oh," she said aloud. Her voice came out very small in the great hush of the vault. "Oh, that's—yes. All right."

"Freya." Corvus's hand hovered over her shoulder. "What does it want?"

"The valley," she said. "All of it. The salt-pans and the wind off them and—" Her throat closed. She made herself keep going, because if she stopped she would never start again. "Maren. Tam. Tam's daughter, Wren, who used to steal honeycakes and blame gulls. The smell of the pans at dawn. Nine years. It wants nine years of my life back."

"You can't," Ivo said. Not cruelly. As a fact, the way he'd say *that beam won't hold*. "A person can't just—give away years."

"A person can't reforge a covenant either." Freya wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Here we are."

She looked at them. She wanted, desperately, to look longer—to fix Corvus's crooked-burned commission and Ivo's dust-gray beard and the exact quality of light coming through the closing rift, because some part of her knew that looking was already losing. Everything she looked at tonight, she was looking at for the last time or the first time, and she could not yet tell which.

"How does it work?" Corvus asked. He was doing the thing he did—turning horror into procedure, grief into logistics. It should have chilled her. Instead it steadied her. "Do you choose what remains? Is there a shape to it?"

"I don't think there's a shape," Freya said. "I think it takes the whole cloth. You can't cut nine years out of a life and leave the seams neat." She laughed, wet and unsteady. "Which means tomorrow morning I'll wake up and none of you will mean anything to me. I'll know your names because you'll tell me. I'll trust you because—" she gestured vaguely at the sealed vault, the quieted dark, the whole mended architecture of three realms—"evidence, apparently. But the woman who walked into this vault with you is going to be someone I've only heard about. Like a story."

"That's barbaric," Ivo said.

"It's arithmetic," Freya said. "Equal weight. The binding holds three realms apart forever. What else weighs that much? My mother's afternoon? I already sold that. Sable's song? That belongs to the covenant now, carried inside it, safe. No. It wants the thing I hid inside longest. It wants the place I went to disappear." She took a breath. "And honestly? Of everything it could ask for—it's asking for the one thing I can survive losing. Because the valley taught me how to hide. And I'm done hiding."

Nobody argued. That was its own mercy.

* * *

They climbed out of the vaults together, because Freya refused to let the payment take her somewhere alone. If she was going to lose nine years, she would lose them in company, walking up the winding stairs of the palace with a hunter's lamp ahead of her and a wright's heavy tread behind.

The palace above was strange now. The drained gifts of hundreds—hung in the air like trapped weather for so many months—were dispersing, settling across the city and the countryside beyond like warm rain. Freya could feel them going, little flickers of returned magic finding their owners or drifting loose to seed new ones. Somewhere a baker was about to discover his bread rose impossibly well. Somewhere a child was about to make her hair float. The world was going to be softer-edged and stranger, and most people would never know why.

"Will you feel it happen?" Corvus asked quietly as they crossed the moonlit courtyard. "The taking?"

"I think it'll be like falling asleep mid-sentence," Freya said. "One moment mid-thought. Then waking somewhere new, mid-something else." She paused. "There's a version of me, in a few hours, who's going to be extremely confused about why a witch-hunter is being nice to her."

"I'll show her my commission," Corvus said. "What's left of it."

"She'll probably try to storm you."

"Probably." Something moved through his face that was almost tenderness and mostly fear. "Freya. Before—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't give me a speech. Speeches are for people who get to keep their memories. Just—" She thought about it. She wanted to be careful with this last hour the way you're careful with the last of anything precious. "Tell me one thing instead. One true thing about us. So that later, when you tell me who I was, you'll have already practiced saying it."

Corvus considered. This was a man who had spent his life measuring words before spending them, and she watched him measure now.

"You laughed at my ledgers," he said finally. "First night we traveled together. I showed you the crown's records of every storm-signature I'd ever hunted, decades of meticulous work, and you read three pages and said, 'You alphabetized your obsession.' And then you laughed until you cried, and I realized I'd never once been laughed at before, and that I didn't mind it." His jaw worked. "That's the true thing. You made me laughable to myself, and it saved my life."

Freya's eyes filled again. "Good," she managed. "That's a good one. Keep that."

Ivo cleared his throat. "My turn, since we're doing this." He put one enormous hand briefly on her head, like blessing a beam into place. "You told me once that stone doesn't remember so it can grieve. It remembers so it can hold. I've been thinking about that for weeks." He squeezed her shoulder once, gently for a man whose hands had broken prison doors. "Hold well, storm-child."

And then, because the covenant is patient but not infinitely so, the pressure behind her eyes bloomed into something like dawn.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest kindness of it.

It felt like the end of a fever, when the heat slides out of you and you surface into cool sheets without any sense of the days that passed. Freya stood in the courtyard under the ordinary moon and felt the valley begin to lift out of her—gently, gently, the way you lift a sleeping child.

She fought it anyway. Not to stop it—she had chosen, truly chosen, and her choice held—but because some animal part of her wanted to say goodbye properly, and the covenant was not a thing you said goodbye to. It simply collected.

The salt-pans went first, or perhaps she noticed them first: the endless shallow mirrors of water and crystal, pink at dawn, gold at dusk, the rake-marks like handwriting. She felt them smooth over in her mind like water settling. Then the wind off them, that particular clean bite of salt that got into everything, into bread and bedding and the corners of your grief. Gone, and she watched it go.

Faces. Maren's, sharp-eyed and patient, the old oath-keeper who had named her exile a mission before Freya knew it was one. Tam's, kind and worried, a man whose gift never woke and who paid for everyone else's anyway. Wren, honeycake thief, gull-blamer. The neighbors whose names she suddenly clutched at—*there was a woman who mended nets, there was a boy who raced the tide-line*—and found the names dissolving even as the shapes lingered, faces becoming silhouettes becoming the idea of faces.

Nine years. Nine years of quiet, folding up like a map.

She had time for exactly one coherent thought at the center of it, and she chose it deliberately, spending her last seconds of remembering the way you spend your last coin:

*I chose this. Whatever I am when I wake—I chose this, and it was worth it.*

Then the valley was gone.

Not hidden. Not suppressed. *Gone*, the way the covenant had gone from the world—sealed, whole, free. There was a hole where it had been, but it was a clean hole, a healed wound, and around it stood everything else: the road to Vess, the flooded choir-lofts where a silent woman had spoken volumes; the bazaars of Ondu and the terrible bright stall where she had sold a day; a prison cell and a mountain-wright's steady hands; a pass, a betrayal, a storm magnificent and horrifying; a campfire and a confession that had finally set down a nine-year weight; a vault, two stones, a dead friend's tide-gift singing in her blood; a burned commission; a ledger joke.

She knew those things. She knew them the way you know a story someone told you—one you believe completely, one that has your name in it, one you cannot quite feel from the inside.

Freya opened her eyes.

She was standing in a moonlit courtyard with salt-stained hands and a stranger's warmth still fading from her shoulder where someone had touched her. Two men were watching her with expressions she could only describe as *braced*.

"Freya?" said the tall one in the scorched coat. His voice was careful, the voice of a man handling something breakable. "Do you know your name?"

"Freya Almeida," she said. It came easily. Most things were coming easily, which was itself unsettling. "Storm-caller. Oath-keeper." She flexed her fingers; the two stones in her palm were warm as living things, and a third warmth—older, wetter, sung rather than spoken—moved underneath them. "And you are... giving me a look like I owe you money."

Something in the tall man's face cracked open with relief so naked it embarrassed them both. "Corvus Hale," he said. "Formerly chief hunter of the crown. Currently unemployed."

"And I'm Ivo," said the large one, "and you called me a granite-headed boulder-worshiper the day we met, and I've decided to forgive you for it in advance, since apparently you can't remember doing it."

"I would never," Freya said, and then, catching something in both their faces—the ghost of a laugh, shared, just out of her reach—amended: "I absolutely did, didn't I."

"It was very funny," Ivo said solemnly.

She looked past them, at the palace, at the sealed dark beneath it, at the sky where no thirteenth moon hung anymore—just thirteen ordinary moons' worth of stars, all of them allowed. She felt the covenant humming in the stones, whole and permanent, and felt Sable inside it somehow, her drowned choir finally given walls to echo in. Grief was there. But it was grief with somewhere to go, which was the only useful kind.

"So," Freya said. She turned the warm stones over in her palm, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. "Someone want to tell me what happens next? Because whoever I was before tonight seems to have been the responsible one, and frankly I have questions."

Corvus and Ivo exchanged a glance over her head—the particular glance of men who had loved someone and were meeting her again—and Corvus began, carefully, at the beginning.

Far to the north, under a rising sun, a girl stood at the edge of a valley full of salt, holding a name she couldn't yet explain to anyone.
