## 21. After the Storm

The rain began at dawn over the capital, and it was not ordinary rain.

Corvus Hale stood in the wreckage of the palace ward-labyrinth—half of it his own design, all of it now useless—and watched the freed gifts settle across the city like weather returning to a country that had forgotten its own seasons. They came down in slow bright drifts, threads of borrowed power finding their way home or finding new homes, he could not tell which. A lamplighter in the street below suddenly glowed faintly blue and dropped his ladder. A baker's oven roared hot enough to singe her eyebrows, and she laughed about it for the rest of the morning. Somewhere a child made the fountain water dance.

Nobody screamed. That was the part Corvus had expected and did not get.

He had spent eleven years telling himself that the culling was a dam against catastrophe—that untrained gifts left loose would drown the realm in accidents and atrocities. He had built lists, procedures, quiet wagons with quiet locks. He had been very good at it. He had told himself that competence was a kind of mercy.

The rain drifted down through a hole in the vault ceiling where something ancient and half-starved had finally stopped whispering, and nobody drowned, and nobody burned, and a baker laughed at her own scorched eyebrows.

"Well," said the young hunter beside him, whose name Corvus had never bothered to learn until three days ago. "What do we do now, sir?"

"Now," Corvus said, "you learn my name is not 'sir,' because as of an hour ago I hold no rank." He set his commission papers—the ones he had already burned, these being the second copy, because apparently one needed paperwork even to resign from atrocity—on the broken stone. "And now you go home. And I go to the council."

"The council's a mob, sir. Half of them want your head."

"Yes," Corvus agreed. "That seems fair. I'd like to be present when they discuss it."

* * *

They gave him a cell with a window, which he suspected was someone's idea of either kindness or cruelty, and he honestly could not have said which he deserved. Through the window he could see the rift above the old palace grounds closing by degrees, like a wound remembering how to be skin. The thirteenth moon had waned. The world had not ended. It was, instead, extremely inconvenienced, which the world seemed to prefer.

The reformed council took its time with him. There were seven members now instead of one king, on the theory—newly fashionable—that seven people were harder to corrupt than one, though Corvus privately felt this underestimated seven people. Three of them had lost family to the hunts. One of them, a broad-shouldered woman named Aldith who had once run grain ledgers for the crown, looked at him across the table with the expression of someone reading a ledger full of numbers she did not like but could not dispute.

"You designed the wards," she said. "You built the wagons. You trained forty hunters."

"I did."

"And then you burned your commission and walked a storm-caller through those same wards."

"Her name was Freya Almeida."

"I know her name, Master Hale. Everyone knows her name. The ballads are unbearable." Aldith folded her hands. "Here is what puzzles me. A man who repents at the end still chose every step before it. Why should we believe the last step cancels the rest?"

"It shouldn't," Corvus said. "It doesn't. Nothing cancels anything. I'm not asking you to cancel me. I'm asking you to use me." He laid out what he knew—not as defense but as inventory: names of informants, routes of the remaining hunt-bands, the ledger of everyone taken and where they'd been held, which gifts had been drained past recovering and which might still come back. "I spent eleven years building a machine for hurting people. I am the only person alive who knows where all its parts are buried."

Silence. Then the youngest councilor, who had so far only frowned, asked quietly, "Do you sleep?"

"No," Corvus admitted.

"At least you're honest about that much."

In the end they did not give him his head. They gave him something worse, or better—he went back and forth on this daily—a desk. A room in the records hall. The task of finding every stolen gift that could still be found, and writing letters to families whose names appeared in his own neat handwriting on intake forms nine years old.

He wrote the first letter the same night. It took him four hours, because he kept starting over, and because his hand shook, and because there was no sentence in any language that could carry what needed carrying. He settled eventually on the truth, plainly put, and signed his full name, and sat for a long while afterward looking at it.

His name had been on documents that ruined hundreds of lives. It seemed right that it should appear on paper trying, too late, to mend a few.

Outside his narrow window, a thread of freed light drifted past like a lazy firefly, paused, considered the glass, and moved on. Even the magic, Corvus thought, wanted nothing to do with him yet.

Fair enough. He could wait. Waiting was the one skill his old life had taught him that was worth keeping.

* * *

Ivo Stone-Wright rebuilt the mountain road first, because it was there and because his hands needed work more than his heart needed rest, though he'd have denied having a heart if you asked. Stonewrights didn't cry. Stonewrights grumbled, and lifted things that ought not be liftable, and occasionally wept into their beer when they thought no one was counting, which no one ever was, except everyone was.

The pass where the ambush had happened was scarred still—black scorch on grey rock where Freya's storm had torn the sky open. Travelers gave that stretch of trail a wide berth for the first season, and told stories about it, each story worse than the last, until the story featured a dragon, three ghosts, and a woman made of lightning, none of which was accurate except the last one, sort of.

Ivo worked through it stone by stone. His gift came back slowly, the way feeling returns to a frostbitten limb—painful, patchy, welcome. When he laid his palms against broken masonry now, the stone remembered itself. Walls stood up straighter when he passed. Once, a whole collapsed bridge sighed upright while he was merely leaning on it, and a passing merchant demanded to know if he'd charged anyone for that, and Ivo said the bridge owed *him*, actually, and they argued pleasantly for a mile.

His family came home in the spring. That was the hard part, harder than the road. His wife Marta looked at him a long time when she arrived, and he looked at the ground, and neither of them said the thing that needed saying, which was: *you sold us.* She knew. He knew she knew. The hunters had taken them anyway, and amnesty had bought nothing, and somehow that made it both unforgivable and almost forgivable, because the crime had failed even at being a crime.

"What are you going to do?" Marta asked finally.

"Rebuild the road," Ivo said.

"That's not what I mean."

"I know it's not." He turned a stone over in his hands, feeling it warm to him the way stones did now, the way they always had before he'd sold his silence for his family's safety and gotten robbed on both ends. "I don't know what to do about the other thing. I thought maybe if I fixed enough roads, eventually I'd fix into somebody you could stand to look at again."

Marta considered this with the flat patience of a woman who had married a stonewright and therefore understood that some materials took years to set properly. "The kitchen wall leans," she said. "It has leaned since before you were taken. Fix that first."

He fixed the kitchen wall. It was not forgiveness. But she watched him do it, and afterward she made dinner, and they ate at the same table, and the wall stood straight, and Ivo decided—for now, this season, this stone at a time—that this was how rebuilding started. Not with grand gestures. With a level wall and two people choosing, daily, awkwardly, to keep laying courses.

His daughter, who was eight and had recently developed the ability to make small rocks float when she sneezed, thought the whole thing was tremendously funny. "Papa sold us and got *nothing*," she announced to visitors, with the ruthless clarity of children, "and now he does all the dishes."

"I do the dishes because Marta's cooking could strip mortar," Ivo said.

"And the dishes?"

"And the dishes," Ivo admitted, "are penance. Go float something."

* * *

Freya came to the valley in autumn, because she did not remember why, only that some compass in her chest pointed there and would not be argued with.

She had tried arguing with it. In Vess she'd stood at the harbor and told herself firmly that the drowned choir had nothing to do with her, that she was a stranger here, that grief without memory was just weather. In Ondu she'd walked past the memory market stalls—rebuilt now, reformed, vendors selling honestly labeled days under new council regulation—and felt something twist behind her ribs like a key turning in a lock that opened onto nothing.

The oath-stone rode in her pocket everywhere she went. It was warm. It had never once gone cold since the night the covenant sealed, and Maren—if Maren was even real; Freya had proof of exactly one letter in a handwriting that made her chest ache without explanation—Maren had written to say that the warmth meant it remembered for her. *Stones hold what people cannot,* the letter said. *Yours holds a valley. Visit it sometime. It will not recognize you. Recognize it anyway.*

So she walked the salt flats in October, when the pans glittered white under a low sun, and she felt like a tourist in her own bones.

A woman was working the eastern pans—raking brine, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with the practiced economy of someone who had done ten thousand mornings exactly like this one. She looked up as Freya approached, and her face did something complicated: recognition, and loss, and a decision to smile anyway, all within the space of a breath.

"You're her," the woman said. "The storm-caller."

"So they say." Freya spread her hands. "Honestly, I mostly feel like a woman who walked a very long way for no reason she can name."

"There's usually a reason." The woman leaned on her rake and studied her. "I'm Odile. This is my pan, my father's before me. We—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "You lived here. Nine years. Third house past the well. You made terrible bread and excellent storms, though we only got the bread, mostly."

"I don't remember any of it." Saying it aloud, to a stranger, in a place that was supposedly hers, was like pressing on a bruise to confirm it was still there. It was. "The covenant took it. As payment. I'm told I agreed."

"You'd have agreed," Odile said. "Knowing you. Knowing what little I—" She caught herself again. Freya realized she was going to spend the rest of her life watching people catch themselves around her, editing out the shared past like a splinter. "There's bread," Odile said instead. "It's good bread. I learned from watching you fail, so. Your legacy."

Freya laughed, and it surprised her, coming out wet at the edges. They sat on the low wall by the pans, and ate bread, and the salt wind came off the flats the way wind comes off nowhere else, sharp and clean and smelling faintly of the sea it had left behind centuries ago.

"Tell me something," Freya said. "Anything. About her. About—me. Small things. I can't have the years back, but I could have... secondhand ones."

Odile thought about it. "You hummed when you raked. Badly. Off-key in a way that suggested you'd invented your own scale out of stubbornness." She smiled. "You talked to the salt. Not magic-talk. Just—complained to it. Told it it was behaving badly when the crystals clumped. And once—" her voice softened, "—once, when Tam's daughter fell in the brine channel and nearly drowned, you pulled her out before anyone else had even shouted. Nobody saw you move. Tam swore for years that you'd always been standing closest, that you were just quick. He never guessed."

"He never guessed," Freya repeated. Something in her chest hurt in a way that also felt like being given a gift.

"He named his daughter after you, after you left. Well. After you were taken. Freya. She's twelve now and insufferable about it."

The sun slid lower. The salt pans caught fire with evening light, every pan a sheet of hammered gold, and Freya sat in the middle of a life she could not remember and found—to her astonishment—that she was not entirely unhappy. Grief was there. So was something else. The stone in her pocket pulsed gently, like a sleeping animal, like a held breath finally let halfway out.

"I keep thinking I'll find myself somewhere," she said. "Some town where I'll walk in and everything will fit. But it doesn't work that way, does it. The valley's gone whether or not I remember it. What's left is—" she gestured at the bread, the rake, the gold-lit flats, Odile's careful face, "—this. Strangers deciding to be kind to me."

"That's not nothing," Odile said. "That's how most belonging is built, if you ask me. Memory just gets the credit because it's lazier."

They sat until the light went violet. When Freya rose to go—south, she supposed, toward whatever the compass in her chest pointed at next—Odile pressed a small sack into her hands. Salt from the eastern pan. "For the road. You used to swear by it. For cooking, I mean. Probably."

Freya was halfway down the track toward the valley's edge when she heard footsteps behind her—quick, light, determined. She turned.

A girl stood on the path in the last light. Twelve, maybe. Salt-stained boots, a rake callus on one hand, and an expression of ferocious purpose that Freya recognized without knowing why, the way you recognize a shape in a fire.

"You're leaving," the girl said. Accusingly.

"I am."

"They all say you're leaving. My mother says the covenant took your memories and you're a stranger now and I shouldn't—" The girl faltered, rallied. "But you're *Freya*. You're my namesake. And you pulled me out of a brine channel when I was three, and I don't care if you don't remember it, because *I* remember it, and somebody in this arrangement ought to."

The wind came off the flats. The oath-stone pulsed warm in Freya's pocket, patient as stone, holding everything she had paid away.

"I don't remember you," Freya said gently. "I want to be honest about that."

"Good," said the girl. "Honesty's a start. Where are you going? Because wherever it is, I've decided I'm coming, and I should warn you—I'm very annoying when I've decided things. Ask anyone."

Freya looked south, where the road ran out of the valley toward a world full of strangers and secondhand memories and bread she hadn't yet learned to ruin. Then she looked at the girl, standing her ground on the salt-white path like something planted.

"Well," Freya said, and found she was smiling. "Then I suppose you'd best tell me your name."

"You already know it," the girl said. "That's the whole point."

And Freya laughed—off-key, inventing her own scale out of sheer stubbornness—and together they walked out of the valley she could not remember, into the evening, toward whatever came next.
