## 22. A Door Left Open

The town was called Millbrook, which was a lie, because there was no mill and barely a brook. There was a baker who sang off-key at dawn, a ferryman who charged double when it rained and half when it didn't—"weather tax," he called it, with a straight face—and a narrow house at the end of Lantern Row with blue shutters that Freya had painted herself in a fit of optimism she couldn't entirely explain.

She had lived there four months.

Four months since the vault beneath the palace, since the thirteenth moon crested and the covenant sealed like a wound closing over her heart. Four months since she had traded away nine years of salt wind and quiet mornings for the freedom of every gifted soul in three realms. She did not regret it. She wanted to be very clear about that, even in the privacy of her own skull, where no one could hear her being clear about anything.

But some mornings she woke with the ghost of a feeling she could not name—the way you wake from a dream of falling and your body still remembers the drop though your mind holds nothing. A smell like brine. A weight like stillness. And once, terribly, the word *Tam*, arriving whole and meaningless as a coin fished out of a riverbed.

She kept those fragments in a little tin box on her mantel. Not memories—echoes of memories, the shells left behind after the tide went out. The oath-stone sat beside them, warm to the touch no matter the weather, humming its patient hum.

Today the stone was warmer than usual.

Freya noticed it while kneading bread, which she did badly. Her dough always came out dense enough to stop an arrow, and the baker next door had taken to leaving "charity loaves" on her windowsill with a note that read, in looping script, *You are a lovely woman and a menace to flour.* She was reaching for the salt—salt, always salt; she used too much of it and never knew why—when the warmth against her hip made her pause.

The stone had never done that before.

She wiped her hands and took it down. It pulsed gently, like something sleeping and dreaming well. Outside, the autumn light lay gold across the street, and somewhere the off-key baker was massacring a ballad about a fisherman's lost love, and everything was ordinary, and everything was fine.

Then someone knocked on her door.

* * *

The girl on her doorstep could not have been more than fourteen. She was travel-worn in the particular way of someone who had walked a long way without knowing how long it would be—dust in the seams of her boots, a pack that hung off one shoulder like it had been carried past the point of comfort and then some further distance after that. Her hair was dark and cut short in a way that suggested scissors wielded by an amateur with more resolve than skill.

"Hello," the girl said. Then, squaring her shoulders as though stepping up to a firing line: "You're Freya Almeida."

It wasn't a question, and it landed strangely anyway. People in Millbrook knew her name, of course. But they knew it the way neighbors know names—as furniture. This girl said it like it meant something old and heavy, and Freya felt the word settle over her like a coat tailored for someone else.

"I am," Freya said carefully. "Do I know you?"

Something flickered across the girl's face—not hurt exactly, but its cousin. She'd expected this, Freya realized. She'd rehearsed for it. Knowing didn't make it easier.

"No," the girl said. "Not anymore." She stuck out a hand, formal as a magistrate. "I'm Wren. Wren Ashby. I'm from Salt Valley."

Salt Valley.

The words went through Freya like cold water through cloth. They meant nothing. That was the horror of it—they were supposed to mean nothing, that had been the price, the whole design—and yet her hands went cold and her heart began to pound as if her body remembered what her mind had surrendered.

"You'll have to come in," Freya heard herself say, "because I appear to be having some kind of episode, and I'd rather not have it in the street."

Wren almost smiled. "I've been told you're dramatic."

"By whom?"

"That's rather the thing I've come to talk about."

* * *

They sat at her kitchen table with tea neither of them drank, and Wren talked.

She talked about the valley—the name alone raised gooseflesh on Freya's arms—and about the farms, the salt-pans glittering white under the sun, the boundary stones that marked the edge of a silence so complete that children born there grew up thinking magic was a story told by travelers. She talked about the night the witch-hunters came, and the fire on the eastern ridge, and a man named Tam who had no gift at all and was taken anyway.

"Tam," Freya repeated. The coin surfaced again from the riverbed. She turned it over. Nothing. Just the shape of a name, and the strange certainty that she had once known the shape of his laugh.

"He had a daughter," Wren said. "Nessa. You pulled her out of a burning barn with a storm small enough to fit inside a heartbeat. I saw it. I was hiding behind the rain barrel, because I was eleven and stupid, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought you were the bravest person I'd ever seen. And then you left, and everyone said you were dead or gone or worse, and I spent years not knowing which."

"And now?"

"Now the hunts are over. Now there's a council, and reparations, and people arguing about proper compensation forms, which somehow feels worse than the hunting, if you want my opinion." Wren wrapped both hands around her cup. "And now the valley is just a valley again. Magic comes and goes across the boundary like weather. Some folk stayed. Some folk left. Nessa left. She's training as a healer in Vess—she says the drowned city sings to her, whatever that means."

"It means she has tide-gift," Freya said quietly. "Someone should teach her gently."

"Nessa says the same about you."

"About me?"

"She says—" Wren hesitated, and for the first time since arriving she looked young, and tired, and uncertain. "She says you paid for all of us. Nobody knows the details. But the council records say the covenant required payment of equal weight, and everyone who knew you says the valley was the weight. So." She gestured vaguely at Freya, at the table, at the dense bread and the charity loaves and the blue shutters and the whole improbable life assembled here at the end of Lantern Row. "Here you are. Starting from nothing. On purpose."

"Not on purpose," Freya said. "Under duress. It's different. It comes with better excuses."

That got a real smile out of the girl, quick and crooked, and something in it snagged at Freya like a hook—a familiarity with no memory behind it, a shape she recognized without knowing why.

"Why did you come?" Freya asked.

Wren looked down into her cup for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had changed—less recitation, more truth.

"Because everyone in the valley talks about you. You're a story there. *The Stormcaller of the Salt Pans.* Half of them think you're ten feet tall. And I got so tired of hearing about you from people who barely knew you, and I thought—that's not fair. Somebody ought to know you who actually does. Somebody ought to get to start fresh instead of secondhand." She lifted her chin. "So I walked here. It took five weeks. Your bread smells terrible from the road, by the way. We smelled it from the top of the hill and Tam's wife said, *well, either she's baking or the house is dying.*"

"My bread is a work in progress."

"It's a work in progress the way a landslide is a landscaping project."

Freya laughed—really laughed, the first time in weeks—and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were stinging. "You're insufferable. Did I know you? In the valley?"

"I delivered eggs to your house sometimes. You gave me honey cake and told me the clouds' moods. You said cirrus meant the sky was thinking about something and wouldn't tell anyone what." Wren shrugged, aiming for casual and missing by a mile. "I remembered that. All these years. Cirrus means the sky is keeping a secret. I don't know why I kept it. But I did."

Freya pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the ache that had no address. She had given away the valley and everyone in it, willingly, gladly, and would do it again tomorrow—and yet here was a stranger across the table carrying pieces of her she hadn't known were missing, offering them back like eggs in a basket.

"I don't remember you," Freya said gently. "I want to be honest about that. I can't give you back what I paid. If you came here hoping—"

"I didn't." Wren set down the cup with a decisive click. "Well. I did, a little. On the road I did. But I've been sitting outside your house since dawn working up the nerve, and I watched you fight a loaf of bread and lose, and I thought—no. I'm not here for the old Freya. I'm here for the new one. Whoever she turns out to be." A beat. "Though I hope she keeps the cloud thing. The cloud thing was good."

"The cloud thing stays," Freya said solemnly. "Some things survive any price."

* * *

Wren stayed the night in the spare room, which had been a storage room until approximately that afternoon and was now a bedroom, thanks to a furious hour of sweeping and the strategic relocation of three chairs and a suspicious quantity of yarn.

In the morning, Freya walked her to the edge of town, meaning to send her off properly with provisions and directions, and instead found herself standing at the crossroads with her hands full of cheese and no idea what she intended to say.

"So," Wren said.

"So."

"I'm going back the way I came. Eventually. There's people waiting—Nessa writes me letters, and Tam wants news, and there's a boy in the village who thinks I'm going to marry him, which is hilarious, but I digress." She shifted her pack. "But I'm in no hurry. And you're clearly going to starve to death or be arrested for crimes against flour if nobody checks on you."

"Wren."

"I'm just saying. I could stay a week. Two weeks. Long enough to teach you to bake, which will take two weeks minimum, possibly a season."

"That's not—"

"Three weeks. I'm flexible."

Freya looked at her—at the dust in her boot seams and the crooked haircut and the stubborn set of her jaw, a jaw that some buried part of Freya's heart insisted it had seen set exactly that way a hundred times, over honey cake, under cirrus skies—and felt the strangest sensation, like a door standing open in a wall she'd thought was solid.

She could not walk back through what she had given away. That door was sealed, and rightly, and she would guard the seal with her last breath. But doors opened in other walls. New ones. Doors you built yourself, hinge by hinge, out of choice instead of memory.

"A month," Freya said. "And you'll help paint the shutters on the back. They're still primer-gray and it haunts me."

"A month," Wren agreed. Then, grinning: "Can I ask you something weird first?"

"You may ask me many weird things. It's practically the terms of our arrangement."

"What was the sky doing this morning? Before you came out. You were looking at it like it owed you money."

Freya tipped her head back. The clouds overhead were high and thin and bright-edged, combing slowly eastward, keeping their secrets the way they always had.

"Cirrus," she said. "Which means the sky is thinking about something and won't tell anyone what."

Wren laughed, loud and delighted, and the sound rang against the hills like a bell that had been waiting years to be rung. "You *do* still have it. Oh, Nessa is going to lose her *mind.*"

They turned back toward the house together, side by side on the road, two strangers walking at an easy pace—one step, then another, then another, each one chosen freely and belonging to no one but themselves.

Behind them, far to the north, a storm was gathering over mountains that Freya did not know she loved. It rolled southward all that day and all the next, patient and vast, trailing a scent of rain and brine—and when it finally broke over Millbrook in the small hours, washing the dust from the shutters and filling the dry brook to singing, Freya stood barefoot in her doorway with her face turned up to it, laughing and weeping and unable to say why, while inside the house an oath-stone hummed warm against the dark, and a girl slept soundly, dreaming of home.
