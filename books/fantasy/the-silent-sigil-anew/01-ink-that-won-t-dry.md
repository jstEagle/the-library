## 1. Ink That Won't Dry

There are three hundred and eleven days left on my family's debt to the Temple of Quiet Hours. I know this the way other people know their own birthdays: precisely, obsessively, and with a small cold stone of arithmetic sitting in the pit of my stomach where a warmer feeling might have gone.

Three hundred and eleven days of copying ledgers in the scriptorium beneath the bell tower that never rings. Three hundred and eleven days of ink-stained fingers and the particular ache behind the eyes that comes from squinting at a prior's cramped accounting hand by tallow-light. And then I walk out through the valley gate a free woman, assuming the gate is open, which it always is, because Vell Hollow has exactly one road out and nothing worth leaving for on the other side of it.

That was the theory, anyway. The theory held up beautifully right up until the morning Prior Aldous set a page in front of me that refused to stay written.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. A scribe should have some respect for chronology. It's the one virtue our profession actually requires.

* * *

The scriptorium of the Temple of Quiet Hours smells of beeswax, dust, and the vinegar-and-iron tang of oak gall ink. It is a long low room with windows set high, so that the light comes down in slanted columns and you spend your working life chasing a bright rectangle across your desk like a cat chasing a sunspot. There are fourteen desks. On most days, nine of them are occupied. Vell Hollow does not generate an enormous volume of documents requiring professional attention. We are a valley of turnip farmers and sheep-herders and one man who insists he makes cheese but whom everyone suspects of merely curdling milk with spite.

I had been at my desk since the sixth bell, as always, copying the harvest-tithe rolls into the fair hand required by the temple archive. This is work so tedious it achieves a kind of holiness. You stop being a person. You become a mechanism for moving numbers from one page to another, and the numbers are things like *fourteen bushels, barley, spoiled at margin, adjusted* and *one ewe, name withheld, assessed value disputed*, and if you do it long enough your mind detaches from your body entirely and floats somewhere near the rafters while your hand performs its duty below.

I had reached ewe number forty when Brother Cassian cleared his throat beside me.

Cassian is the under-librarian, a narrow man built like a quill himself, all shaft and nib, with a voice pitched permanently at the volume of someone trying not to wake a sleeping infant. He is also the closest thing the temple has to a friend of mine, in the sense that he sometimes brings me tea and once defended me to the prior over a matter of marginalia, which in this valley constitutes an act of heroic loyalty.

"Mara." He set something down on my desk. Not tea. A folio, wrapped in grey linen, tied with plain string. "A commission."

I looked at it without touching it. Commissions do not come wrapped in linen. Tithes come in ledgers. Correspondence comes loose and smudged. Wrapped linen means either a funeral or money, and we don't do funerals in linen here.

"From whom?"

"The house at Coldbrook Bend." He hesitated, and Cassian hesitating is like a clock pausing between ticks — noticeable, and slightly wrong. "Old Mistress Halloway's household. She died last month, as you'll know. Her nephew is settling the estate, and there's a document among her papers that needs copying before it can be read into probate."

"Needs copying," I said. "Before reading. Why? If it's legible, read it. If it isn't, what good is my copy?"

"That's not for me to say." He untied the string with two careful pulls. "The nephew pays double rate. Triple, if it's done quickly and quietly. And Mara—" his voice dropped even further, which I hadn't thought anatomically possible, "—he asked for you specifically. Said he'd heard you were the best hand in the valley."

"Everyone in this valley knows four people who write," I said. "That's a small pond to be the best fish in."

"He said it anyway." Cassian lifted back the linen.

The page underneath was ordinary in every respect I could see at first glance: vellum, well-cured, trimmed square, perhaps a hand's width larger than a standard letter sheet. There was writing on it in brown-black ink, a fine spidery hand, dense and unhurried. I leaned closer, expecting a will, or an inventory, or some old woman's devotional ramblings.

It was none of those things. It appeared to be a letter. Addressed to no one. Beginning mid-thought, as though the first line had been cut away:

*—and so the question is not whether the valley holds, but how long the holding costs, and who pays.*

I sat back. "What is this?"

"I don't know," Cassian said, which I believed, because Cassian is incapable of lying about anything more interesting than whether he's eaten the last of the honey cakes. "The nephew wouldn't say. Only that his aunt instructed it be copied by a temple scribe, in full, before her effects were distributed, and that the copy be made within three days." He paused again. "She also instructed that whoever copies it should not read it aloud. Any part of it."

"Whyever not?"

"She didn't say." He rewrapped nothing, because the page lay bare between us, and instead straightened the linen around it with fussy little motions. "Double rate, Mara. That's nearly two weeks off your count."

Two weeks. Two weeks is fourteen mornings I could sleep past the fifth bell. Fourteen fewer evenings of calculating bushels of barley against years of my life. My mother had signed us into debt when I was six, after my father died and the tithe collectors came with their polite, merciless arithmetic, and I had been paying it since I was old enough to hold a pen. I would have copied a laundry list in blood for double rate.

"When do you need it started?" I asked.

"You'd start tonight. It goes back tomorrow evening." Cassian was already moving away. Then he stopped, turned half around, and said, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he didn't want to carry any farther, "One more thing. The nephew mentioned — only in passing — that the aunt made him promise the same copyist take it both nights. In case the first night's work was... unsatisfactory."

"Both nights," I repeated. "Why two nights for one page?"

Cassian looked at me for a moment with an expression I couldn't read, and then he said, "Because she said so," and left me alone with the page and the smell of ink and the growing, unreasonable conviction that I had just agreed to something I would regret.

* * *

I want to be clear about what Vell Hollow is, because it matters, though I didn't yet understand how much.

Vell Hollow is a valley like any other valley, which is to say it is a bowl of fields and orchards ringed with grey hills, with a river running through the middle of it that behaves itself, a market square that floods every third spring, and a population whose chief exports are wool, turnips, and a deep suspicion of anything unusual. We are not a superstitious people, exactly. We are a *practical* people. When Old Tam Wicker swore he saw lights dancing on the fells, the practical response was to conclude he'd been drinking. When the miller's daughter claimed her bread rose better than anyone else's, the practical response was envy, followed by imitation, followed by grudging acceptance that some people just have luck with yeast.

We did not believe in magic. Not the way you disbelieve in something you've argued about. We disbelieved the way you disbelieve in dragons — easily, completely, without ever once feeling the lack. Magic was a thing from stories, from far-off places, from the old ballads the temple allowed only in expurgated versions. Here, in Vell Hollow, things were what they were. Bread was bread. Lights on the fells were drink. And a strange commission from a dead woman's estate was just a strange commission, worth two weeks off a debt, and nothing in the world more than that.

I told myself all of this very firmly that evening, as I carried the linen-wrapped folio back to my room above the cooper's shop, and I almost believed it.

My room is small and clean and mine, rented at a rate that the temple kindly deducts from wages it already deducts the debt from, a piece of accounting so elegantly circular that I've sometimes admired it purely as craftsmanship. I have a bed, a stool, a shelf of seven books I have read until they're soft as cloth, and a window that looks out on the cooper's yard and, beyond it, the dark shoulder of the western hills.

I lit my candle. I unwrapped the linen. I sharpened my best quill — the goose-feather one, the one with the spring in it — and I mixed fresh ink, and I laid the page flat, and I weighted its corners, and I began.

The first lines were easy. Strange, but easy. The hand was beautiful in a way that made my fingers itch, all long descenders and patient curves, the writing of someone who loved the act of writing itself. The words were stranger. Mistress Halloway, who in life had been famous for prize-winning onions and an unshakeable belief in the moral superiority of early bedtimes, had apparently spent her final years composing a letter about wellsprings. About covenants. About a boundary drawn across a valley like a bandage over a wound.

*The founders did not build Vell Hollow to keep the world out,* she wrote. *They built it to keep something in. Or rather, they built it to keep something quiet. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

I copied it faithfully. That was the job. Whatever I felt about onion-women writing theology, my task was to reproduce the words in a fair hand, and I did, line after line, the candle burning down, the cooper's dog sighing in the yard below, my wrist aching in the familiar way.

Somewhere around the middle of the page, the letter changed. The handwriting shifted — subtly, the way a voice shifts when a person stops performing and starts confessing. And the subject changed too. From abstractions about valleys and boundaries to something smaller, sharper, personal:

*They brought a child through the border in the winter of the bad fever. Carried her, wrapped in a shepherd's cloak, past the standing stones while the snow came down like a curtain. The child slept through the crossing. They told her parents it was mercy, that the valley would keep her safe. What they meant was that the valley would keep her hidden. Safe and hidden are not the same word, whatever the founders' descendants like to pretend.*

I stopped. My quill hovered. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as still as if the air itself were listening.

A child. A fever year. A shepherd's cloak. I was born in a fever year — my mother never spoke of those months, only that we'd come to Vell Hollow when I was small, after my father died, that the temple took pity on us. Taken pity on us. Or taken us. I had never asked which. Children don't ask. By the time I was old enough to ask, the habit of not asking had grown roots.

I am not a fanciful woman. I want that on the record. I have spent my entire adult life among numbers and margins and the honest drudgery of fair copies, and I do not see portents in candle flames or hear voices in the wind. But sitting there with that page, my heart was going hard and quick, and my skin had prickled into gooseflesh despite the warmth of the candle, and I had the distinct, humiliating sensation of being *described* — of the dead woman's spidery hand reaching up out of the vellum and pointing directly at me.

I finished the copy. Of course I finished it. Double rate. Two weeks. I wrote the last line, blew on the ink, sand-dried it, and set my pages beneath the original on the corner of my desk, weighted under my spare inkstone.

Then I went to bed and lay awake for two hours listening to the silence of Vell Hollow, which is normally the most comforting sound in the world, and which that night felt less like peace and more like a held breath.

* * *

Morning came grey and ordinary. The cooper's boys hammered barrels in the yard. Someone's rooster committed its crimes. I dressed, ate yesterday's bread, and went down to my desk to wrap the commission for return to the temple, already rehearsing my report to Cassian: *Done. Fair copy attached. Nothing remarkable.* 

Except.

I stopped at the top of the stairs, because something was wrong with the light in my room, or the air, or — no. Something was wrong with the desk.

The original page lay where I had left it. My fair copy lay beneath it, weighted by the inkstone. Both exactly as arranged. Except that the original — the dead woman's letter, the one I had spent three hours reproducing stroke for stroke — was no longer the letter I had copied.

Oh, it was the same page. Same vellum, same creases, same faint stain near the lower corner shaped like nothing in particular. But the writing had moved. Had *changed*. Where Mistress Halloway's confession of wellsprings and children had been, there was now different text in a different hand — and I knew the hand. That was the thing. That was the thing that dropped the floor out from under me. The new writing was in a hand I knew the way you know your own face in a mirror: instantly, intimately, with a lurch of recognition that bypasses thought altogether.

It was my handwriting. Not my current hand — not the trained, disciplined, temple-perfect script I produce forty hours a week. It was my childhood hand, the round uncertain letters I had written at six, at seven, the letters I had used before the temple scribes beat the individuality out of my pen strokes. Letters I had not written in fifteen years, on a page I had never written them on, in a room locked against the world.

I did not touch it. I remember being proud of that later — I did not touch it. I stood at the top of the stairs with the morning noise rising from the street below, the barrels and the rooster and the ordinary blessed racket of a world where pages do not rewrite themselves, and I read what my own vanished childhood hand had written overnight:

*Mara. Stop copying. Start remembering.*

Just that. Seven words, in the handwriting of a six-year-old girl who had been carried through a border in a shepherd's cloak and had never once been told why.

I should have taken it to Prior Aldous. That was procedure; that was sense; that was what the sensible woman I had spent twenty-two years constructing would have done. Instead I heard myself thinking, with a clarity that frightened me more than the page did: *they burn things here*. I thought of the prior's little iron brazier, the one he kept in his study, the one I'd seen him feed with papers more than once when he thought the corridor empty. I thought of the word *hidden*, and the word *quiet*, carved over the temple door — Quiet Hours — and how I had never wondered what, precisely, we were being quiet about.

And underneath all of that, underneath the fear and the confusion and the cold crawling certainty that my life had just tilted on its axis, there was something else. Something waking. It had no shape I could name. It felt like the moment before a sneeze, stretched out; like a word on the tip of the tongue that has been sitting there for twenty years; like a muscle remembered from a body I'd never inhabited, flexing experimentally in the dark.

It wanted the page. I understood that much. Whatever it was, curled up in me beneath the ledgers and the debt and the dutiful fair hand, it looked at that impossible page the way a starving man looks at bread.

So I did the unsensible thing. I folded the page small — my childhood hand and all its seven impossible words facing inward — and I hid it inside the spine of my oldest book, the Psalter of Hours with its broken binding and its hollowed-back boards, where I kept the only other secret of my life: the tally of days remaining on my debt, scratched in pencil, three hundred and eleven of them as of that morning.

Then I wrapped the blank space where the commission should have been in its grey linen, tied the string with two careful pulls, and carried it to the temple to tell Cassian the copy was finished.

Which it was. Technically. Just not on paper.

I walked through the waking streets of Vell Hollow with my heartbeat loud in my ears and the ghost of my own small handwriting burning against my ribs, and I told myself the only story I had left: *double rate. Two weeks. One strange page, and then back to turnips and tithes and the honest arithmetic of an ordinary life.*

The valley held its breath around me, patient as snowfall.

And three hundred and ten days suddenly felt like a very long time indeed.
