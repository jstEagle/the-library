## 2. The Crown's Long Shadow

There is a particular sound a valley makes when it decides to be afraid. It isn't dramatic. No horns, no screams. It's quieter than that — doors latching a half-beat sooner, boots pausing at corners, the market going polite in the way that politeness is the loudest thing a frightened person can say.

I heard it on Thirdday morning, standing at my copying desk with ink to my second knuckle and forty pages of grain tariffs between me and supper.

The crier came up the temple road just after Prime, his voice carrying that flat, official cheerfulness criers are taught so they can deliver terrible things without their faces getting involved.

"By decree of His Majesty's Grace, all persons harboring knowledge of unlawful arts are bound by honor and law to declare them within thirty days. The Crown extends mercy to those who come forward." A pause, professionally timed. "The Crown extends nothing to those who don't."

*Unlawful arts.* In Vell Hollow, where magic went to die before I was born, where the most supernatural thing that had happened in living memory was the year the miller's wife grew a pumpkin the size of a church pew. The proclamation might as well have outlawed dragons. We hadn't seen one. None of us had. That was the entire point of this place.

Except.

I kept my eyes on my tariff ledger and did not look toward the little cupboard behind my cot, where a page of parchment sat folded in oilcloth, rewriting itself every night in handwriting I could not explain. The page I was supposed to have delivered to the prior two weeks ago. The page that had made something wake up in my chest the first time I touched it, like a cat stretching after a long nap.

*Declare them within thirty days,* said the crier.

Twenty-eight days left, if I'd been counting. I had been counting. I counted everything; it was the occupational disease of scribes.

* * *

"You're grinding your quill into splinters," said Brother Anselm from the next desk. He didn't look up from his own work. Anselm was seventy if he was a day and had spent so long copying scripture that he'd begun to resemble it — thin, creased, faintly scented of vellum. "Whatever you're angry at, it can't read."

"I'm not angry."

"You've ruined four pens this month. Prior Odell thinks you're eating them."

Prior Odell thought a great many things about me, none of them accurate, which suited me fine. My family owed the Temple of Quiet Hours more money than my mother's death had managed to settle, and so I copied ledgers six days out of seven until the arithmetic of my life came out even. Debt was simple. Debt was honest. You knew exactly what it would take from you and exactly when.

It was everything else lately that had gotten vague.

At midday bell I carried the finished folios down to the prior's study, and that was where I saw the smoke.

Not hearth-smoke. Hearth-smoke smells like bread and comfort. This came from the little iron brazier Prior Odell kept for melting sealing wax, except he wasn't sealing anything. He stood over it feeding papers into the flame, one at a time, unhurried, the way a man feeds a fire who has done this before and expects to do it again. His face wore the expression of someone balancing a column of numbers that refuses to sum.

He saw me in the doorway and didn't stop.

"Sister Quintana." He never got my title right. I was no sister; I was hired labor with a prayer book. "The folios?"

"On your desk, Prior." I set them down and made the mistake of glancing at the brazier. "That looks like correspondence."

"That looks like *old* correspondence," he said mildly. "Records past retention. The crown's new proclamations ask temples to review their holdings. Some things are better ash than evidence."

"Evidence of what? This is Vell Hollow. Our most dangerous relic is Father Merrin's dancing shoes, and those only because he still wears them."

Odell smiled the way stone smiles — technically, with effort. "Indeed. And long may it remain so. Which is why we will be especially diligent in demonstrating that our records contain nothing of interest. Nothing at all." He fed another page to the flames. For half a heartbeat before it curled, I saw writing on it — not the temple's neat ecclesiastical hand but something slanted, urgent, crowded to the margins like the writer had too much to say and too little paper.

Something about that handwriting itched at the back of my skull.

"Was there anything else?" Odell asked.

"No, Prior."

"Then close the door on your way out. And Sister—" I paused with my hand on the latch. "The valley keeps its promises. Keep yours to it. Quiet hours, quiet hands, quiet hearts. There is no fourth quiet, but if there were, it would be *quiet thoughts*, and I'd recommend those above all."

I closed the door and stood in the corridor doing sums again, because sums were safe. *Old correspondence. Past retention.* Except the temple burned its true dead records at the equinox, in a proper ceremony, with Anselm weeping over every page because he'd copied half of them himself. This was different. This was a man destroying paper *before* anyone asked to see it, which meant he already knew what questions were coming.

And somewhere beneath the itch of recognition in my head, a colder thought: how much did the prior know about what had arrived in his scriptorium three weeks ago? About the commission that had come with no sender named and payment in coin minted nowhere I recognized?

About the page that wouldn't stop writing?

* * *

I told myself I would burn it that evening.

I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this account, assuming anyone does: I stood in my little room with the oilcloth packet in my hands and I genuinely intended to feed it to my candle. It was the sensible act. The safe act. The act of a woman whose ambitions extended exactly as far as the next meal and the eventual settling of her debt.

Then I unfolded it, because apparently I am the kind of fool who reads the letter she means to burn.

By daylight it looked ordinary enough — good rag paper, no watermark, edges trimmed clean. But the text had changed since yesterday. It changed every night, always while I slept, always in the same hand: a slanted, margin-crowded script that pressed hard enough to emboss the page from behind. I'd held it to candlelight a dozen times hoping to find a palimpsest beneath, some original text it was merely covering. There was nothing underneath. The words weren't written *on* the page. As far as I could tell, they were grown there, the way frost grows on glass.

Last week it had been a list of names. Places. Words I didn't know: *Wellspire. Meridian. The salt-roads under the river.* I'd assumed madness, mine or the page's, whichever of us was more convenient.

Tonight, the text was shorter.

*Mara.*

Just that, alone on the first line, and beneath it:

*You copied me once before. You'll remember when it matters. Twenty-seven days. Don't let them take the well.*

My name. Not the name I use here — not *Sister Quintana, debtor, scribe, nobody* — but *Mara*, plain, intimate, the way my mother used to say it when she called me in from the rain. And the handwriting—

I turned it sideways. Held it near the light. Tilted it the way I tilt my own drafts when I'm checking for forgery, which is a skill they teach you in a debt-scriptorium right alongside penmanship, because people will forge anything, including forgiveness.

The slant matched. The pressure matched. The way the descender of the *r* hooked left instead of right, an idiosyncrasy I had never seen in another hand in my life because — because it was a flaw *I* had, one I corrected whenever I noticed it, one that appeared only when I wrote fast and tired and honest.

It was almost my handwriting. Almost. Close enough that if you showed me a sample and lied about whose it was, I'd swear on any altar available that it was mine. But older somehow. Rougher. Like my hand learned by someone who'd been writing longer than I'd been alive.

*I copied it once before.*

That sentence was the one that finally made me fold the packet shut and put it back in the cupboard, because the alternative was sitting with it, and sitting with it meant admitting that some part of me wanted the sentence to be true. That some traitorous corner of my heart had spent three weeks feeling less like a woman who'd found a cursed page and more like a woman who'd found a letter she'd mailed to herself and forgotten the address.

I blew out my candle. I lay down. I did not sleep.

* * *

Thirdday became Fifthday, and the valley's fear stopped being ambient and started having names.

Old Wenna at the bakery refused flour to the Corrin family because their cousin had once, twenty years ago, married a woman from outside the valley, and who knew what came in with outside blood these days. Tam Corrin — nine years old, gap-toothed, the boy who could open any lock in the village with a hairpin and a hum — came to the temple steps at dusk with a split lip and asked me, very seriously, whether being able to do a thing you couldn't explain counted as an art. Unlawful or otherwise.

"It counts as practice," I told him, cleaning the cut. "Everything else counts as talent. Talent's legal."

"What if the thing is locks?"

"Especially locks. Locks are practically scripture. All those psalms about doors and keys."

He laughed, and then stopped laughing, because his mother was calling him from across the square in the sharp voice mothers use when fear is wearing their mouths, and he ran off, and I sat on the temple steps feeling the dread tighten around the ordinary like a band around a barrel stave. One turn each day. One notch. You didn't notice it until the hoops started to sing.

Because here was the thing I couldn't stop noticing: the crown's hunt was aimed at the gifted, and Vell Hollow had none. Everyone knew that. It was the founding boast of the place, carved over the gate in letters the weather had been politely erasing for two centuries: *Here, Quiet. Here, Safe.* The hunters should have ridden past us entirely, the way you ride past a field that grows nothing.

Unless they'd received word that something in Vell Hollow had recently begun, against all local tradition, to grow.

* * *

On Sixthday, the strangers came through the gate.

Two of them, riding crown-gray horses with the royal badge on their saddlecloths — a sun split by a sword, which is the sort of emblem you design when subtlety died young. They wore no livery otherwise, which was worse; livery says *we belong to an army*, while plain gray wool says *we belong to ourselves, and we have authority, and we would prefer you work out which of those is scarier*.

They stopped at the temple first. Of course they did. Where else does officialdom go in a small town but the biggest building with the best chairs?

I was in the scriptorium when Prior Odell went down to meet them, and I confess — freely, without shame — that I listened at the stairwell door. Anselm pretended not to notice. Anselm had been pretending not to notice my various sins for weeks now, and had developed the technique to an art form: he simply copied louder.

The tall stranger's voice carried up the stairwell, smooth and dry as sand. "A routine survey, Prior. The crown asks each district to confirm its holdings. Records of unusual provenance. Correspondence from beyond the border. Gifts received from unnamed parties."

"We are a temple of quiet hours," Odell said. "We receive nothing from anyone. It's rather our specialty."

"And yet," the smooth voice said, and I heard paper — the rustle of a list, or maybe just the sound of a man enjoying his own pause — "our office has record of a commission sent to this valley. Three weeks past. A single page, dispatched to a house of learning, recipient unspecified. Payment enclosed in old coin."

Silence. Long enough that I could hear the brazier in Odell's study ticking as it cooled, three rooms away, or believed I could.

"There must be some error," Odell said at last. "Nothing of the kind arrived here."

*It arrived,* I thought, pressing my back flat against the cold stone. *It arrived, and I stole it, and I have been hiding it behind my spare shift for nineteen days like the world's worst criminal.*

"Errors happen," the smooth voice agreed pleasantly. "Paper travels strange roads. But errors have a way of correcting themselves, Prior, and correction is rather my life's work. Captain Reyes, of the Office of Mercy." A pause. "Yes, I know. The name is aspirational."

They left an hour later. I watched from the scriptorium window as the two riders walked their horses slowly down the main street, slower than travel required, turning their heads side to side like men taking inventory. At the well, the tall one — Reyes, presumably, though I'd have bet the short one was the actual danger — reined in, dismounted, and drank from the common cup. He looked at the water a long moment afterward. Looked at the valley walls. Looked up, at the sky over Vell Hollow, with the expression of a man testing a lock by rattling the handle gently, once, to hear what sound it makes.

Then he mounted and rode out, and the gates closed behind him, and the whole valley exhaled like a patient after a long examination.

I did not exhale. Because as they'd passed beneath my window, the shorter stranger had been holding a piece of paper against his knee, studying it, and even at that distance and angle I knew the shape of a page that had been folded in quarters many times, and I knew — the way you know your own reflection in a dark shopfront, wrongly and completely — what was written on it.

Not my name. Not yet. Just a list of names and places in a slanted, margin-crowded hand.

But pages change. Mine changed nightly. And whatever hand had written the original commission — the hand that was almost mine, that might have been mine in some life I couldn't remember living — had evidently not sent just one copy into the world.

That night I took the page from the cupboard and set it on my desk and sat watching it until midnight, waiting for the words to shift, dreading it, needing it. When the change finally came, it came all at once, the way frost climbs a window when the temperature turns — silently, completely, everywhere at once.

Three lines now.

*Mara. Twenty-six days.*

*He is coming for the well, and you are the last person alive who knows where it is. You erased yourself to prove it. Undo it.*

I read it eleven times. Then I did the only reasonable thing a sane woman could do.

I began, in my own careful, corrected, unremarkable hand, to copy it — every version I'd ever seen, every night's text, dated and cross-referenced like the ledger entries I understood far better than prophecy. If my life was becoming a document, then it would at least be a *maintained* document. A scribe's rebellion: accuracy.

Somewhere below my window, out in the dark valley, a dog began to bark at nothing. Then another. Then all of them, the whole scattered chorus of Vell Hollow, barking at a nothing that was moving through the fields toward the border stones.

I dipped my pen, and my hand, entirely without my permission, was shaking.
