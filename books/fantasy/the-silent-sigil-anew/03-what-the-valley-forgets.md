## 3. What the Valley Forgets

The page had been rewriting itself for six days, and I had run out of rational explanations somewhere around day four.

I want to be clear about this: I am a woman who has explained away a great many things. The temple's cold spots were drafts. The prior's sleepwalking was piety. The way ink sometimes pooled on my page in shapes that looked almost like letters was, I told myself, a quirk of cheap pigment and tired eyes. Vell Hollow is a valley built on the principle that nothing strange can survive here, and I had made myself into the same kind of ground—flat, quiet, inhospitable to anything that might grow.

But the page kept writing.

Every morning I lifted the oilcloth from the drawer where I'd hidden it, and every morning the commission I'd copied out—the dull inventory of temple stores, forty lines of barley counts and candle weights—had rearranged itself into something else. Not words, at first. Shapes. A looping sigil like a knot pulled tight. Then, by the third night, words. And by the sixth, the words had arranged themselves into a single sentence that sat in the middle of the page in handwriting I knew better than my own face:

*They are coming for you, little scribe.*

The handwriting was mine. That was the part I couldn't explain away. Not similar to mine—mine, down to the way my 'r' always leaned too far east, the little hitch where my pen caught on the downstroke because I'd never learned to hold it properly and no one at the Temple of Quiet Hours had ever bothered to correct me. Debtors' children don't get calligraphy lessons. They get ledgers.

I sat on the floor of my cell with the page in my lap and the door bolted, though a bolted door means nothing against whatever put those words there, and I did what any sensible person does when confronted with the impossible.

I went to find someone older and stranger than myself.

* * *

Sefa lived at the far end of Hollow Lane, past the tannery, in a house that leaned against its neighbor the way old friends lean together at a funeral. Everyone in Vell Hollow knew Sefa was odd. She grew herbs nobody bought, kept a goat with a milky eye she claimed could see weather three days off, and paid her temple tithe in dried nettle and honey, which Prior Aldous accepted with the expression of a man swallowing a stone. She was the closest thing our valley had to a hedge-witch, though saying so aloud would have been like saying the word *magic*, and we didn't say that word here. We said *nothing*. We said *surely not*. We said *what a strange season*.

She answered the door before I knocked.

"I wondered when you'd come," she said. "You've got your mother's face when she's frightened. Which is to say, you've got a face pretending very hard it isn't."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"No. It's supposed to be true." She stepped aside. "Come in. Wipe your feet. The floor remembers everything and I haven't the strength to argue with it."

Her house smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and something underneath both, something mineral and old, like the inside of a cave. I set the folded page on her table between a chipped bowl and a jar of dead bees, and I watched her look at it, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"You copied this," she said.

"The temple commissioned it. A transcription job. It came through the postern with no name attached." I hesitated. "It rewrites itself. Every night. And this morning—" My voice did something embarrassing. "This morning it wrote this. In my handwriting."

Sefa didn't touch the page. She circled it the way you'd circle a sleeping animal, reading it from four directions, and then she sat down across from me and folded her hands and looked at me for a long time with her pale, watery eyes.

"How much do you know about why this valley exists?" she asked.

"It's good farmland. The temple holds the debt-titles. There's a salt spring." I shrugged. "That's the whole of what they teach in the schoolroom."

"That's the whole of what they're *allowed* to teach." She leaned forward. "Vell Hollow wasn't founded, girl. It was *built*. Stone by stone, ward by ward, by people who wanted one thing above all else: a place where the gift cannot wake. There's salt in the foundations of every building in this town. Salt in the well-water. Salt in the boundary stones ringing the valley rim, laid out in a pattern older than the crown, older than the temple, older than anyone's memory of laying them. Magic doesn't fail to happen here. Magic is *refused* here. Every hour of every day, by ground that was taught how."

My hands had gone cold around my cup. "Why would anyone build that?"

"Because once, everything burned." Her voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone reciting something learned by heart so as not to feel it. "There was a war—not the kind with armies, or not only. A war of gifts. People who could unmake things with a thought, and people who could remake them, and the rest of us caught between like wheat between millstones. The founders of this valley were refugees who decided they'd rather be safe than gifted. They cut a hole in the world and lined it with salt and called it peace."

"And the temple knows?"

"The temple *is* the mechanism, child. Why do you think we're called the Temple of Quiet Hours? Quiet isn't a virtue here. It's maintenance." She tapped the table beside the page. "And now something in their own storehouse is writing itself awake, and it's writing in your hand, and you came to an old woman's door at dusk looking like a girl who already knows the answer and can't bear it."

"I don't know the answer," I said. "I promise you, I don't know anything. I copy ledgers. I owe the temple eleven years of labor for my father's debts. That's the entire shape of my life."

"Eleven years." Something moved behind her eyes—pity, or grief, or arithmetic. "You came to the valley when you were five. Do you remember arriving?"

"There's nothing to remember. I was born here."

"You weren't." She said it gently, the way you tell someone their house has burned while they slept. "You were carried through the western gate at night, twenty years ago this autumn, wrapped in a grey cloak, asleep in the arms of a man who paid the gatekeeper in coin he shouldn't have had and begged him never to speak of it. You were marked, Mara. Marked before you ever crossed the border. Whatever sleeps in you, the valley has been sitting on it your whole life like a stone on a seed. And stones, given enough years and enough rain, crack."

The room tilted. Or I did. I gripped the edge of the table and stared at the page, at my own handwriting staring back at me, and felt the ground under twenty years of my life shift and prove itself hollow.

"My parents," I said. "Who carried me?"

"A scribe. A covenant-scribe, if the stories I heard secondhand hold any truth—and I'll tell you now, most of what I know is secondhand, gathered over sixty years from travelers who passed through and forgot more than they remembered, because that's what this valley does. It forgets for you. It's kinder that way." She looked at the page again. "Your mother died young. Your father drank himself into debt and then into the ground. Neither of them ever spoke of where you came from, and I never asked, because asking questions in Vell Hollow is how you end up digging your own garden at midnight."

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because the page found you, and pages like that don't make mistakes." She stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and went to the window. "Because yesterday a royal courier rode through the gates wearing colors I haven't seen since I was younger than you, and this morning there were criers at the market square reading a writ about the hunting of the gifted, and because the gatekeeper who took your father's coin twenty years ago hanged himself three days ago and left a note that said only *she's still here*."

The floor seemed to drop away beneath my chair. "*She's still here.*"

"He knew what you were. He kept the secret for twenty years because your father made him swear it on something that scared him more than the crown does. And then the writ came, and his nerve broke, and rather than choose between his oath and his fear, he chose the rope." Sefa turned back to me. "So. Hunters are coming up the pass—I give it two days, three at the outside. They'll have papers. They'll have instruments. They'll knock on every door in this valley, and when they reach yours, they will find a girl whose handwriting writes itself, and they will take you apart to see what makes you work. Do you understand me?"

I understood her. Understanding arrived all at once, whole and terrible, the way ice goes out of a river—not melting but breaking.

"I have nowhere to go," I said. It came out small. "This is my home. It's a prison and a debt, but it's mine. Eleven years of copying ledgers, and I hate every hour of it, and it's still the only thing I—"

"Stop." Sefa crossed the room and took my face in her hands, and her palms smelled of rosemary and something sharper, something that stung faintly like lightning about to happen. "Listen to me, child, because I'm only going to say this once and I'm too old to say it twice. This valley was never your home. It was your *hiding place*. There's a difference, and the difference is that hiding places are meant to be left."

* * *

We left at moonset, when the dark is at its deepest and even the temple's night-bells seem to hesitate between strikes.

Sefa dressed me in her dead husband's coat, which hung off me like a sail, and packed a bundle with bread, cheese, a flask of something that smelled strong enough to strip paint, and the self-writing page, folded into oilcloth and bound with twine. She wouldn't let me carry it against my skin. "It's drawn to you," was all she said. "Don't help it along."

We went out the back of her house, through the herb garden gone wild and silver in the moonlight, past the goat—who watched us go with her good eye, and I swear on every ledger I've ever copied that the animal bowed her head—and down a track behind the tannery that I had walked a hundred times without ever noticing where it went. That, I was learning, was the valley's whole trick. It didn't hide things. It just made sure you never looked.

The track became a path. The path became a gully walled with blackthorn. And the gully became a road—a real road, old ones, paved with flat stones furred with moss, running straight as a rule toward the western rim.

"This is the salt-road," Sefa said. "The founders' back door. Every ward in this valley has a seam, and this is the seam. The gatekeepers used it for smuggling; the temple pretends it doesn't exist; and tonight, we use it for you."

"Why aren't you coming?"

"Because I'm the last living person who knows how this valley breathes, and if I leave, it stops breathing." She said it matter-of-factly, but her hand shook as she adjusted the bundle on my shoulder. "Someone has to stay behind and be slow, and deaf, and boring, and answer the hunters' questions with the muddleheaded nonsense old women are expected to produce. By the time they've finished searching my herb jars, you'll be over the rim."

"Sefa—"

"Don't thank me yet. Thanking me implies this ends well, and I'm a hedge-witch, not a prophet." But her voice cracked on the last word, and she turned away quickly and pretended to check the sky. "Cloud cover's thinning. You have perhaps two hours of dark. Follow the road to the third boundary stone—it'll be the one with the split top, shaped like a broken tooth. The wards are thinnest there. Walk through slow. It'll feel like walking through cold water, or grief, whichever you know better. Don't stop halfway. Halfway is where the valley keeps what it catches."

"What does that mean?"

"It means don't stop halfway." She pressed something into my hand—a letter, sealed with wax, heavy as a brick. "For later. When you're somewhere safe, and only then. And Mara—" She stopped. For a moment the old woman looked older than the valley itself, older than the stones, older than the war that had built them. "Whatever you find out there, whatever you learn about who you were before—you were loved. Whoever carried you through that gate at night was doing the bravest and most foolish thing a person can do, which is hoping. Remember that, even if you remember nothing else."

I hugged her. She let me, briefly, the way you let a bird land on your hand—carefully, already braced for it to go.

Then I walked.

The salt-road ran through country I'd never seen, though I'd lived my whole life within sight of these hills. The valley had kept me the way a jar keeps a moth—kindly, completely, and without my consent. With every step westward I catalogued the theft: the orchards I hadn't picked, the roads I hadn't taken, the twenty years of a life spent copying other people's numbers into other people's books. Grief, I discovered, is not a big dramatic thing. It's an inventory. You walk down the rows and count what's missing, and the count takes longer than you have.

The third boundary stone rose out of the dark exactly as promised, split-topped, jagged as a broken tooth against the stars.

Behind me, far below, a bell began to ring. Not the temple's night-bells—something faster, harsher, an alarm. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried up the valley on the cold air: hooves. Many hooves. Coming up the pass.

Two days, Sefa had guessed. She'd been wrong by two days.

I put my hand against the stone. It hummed under my palm, faint and constant, like a hive thinking. Beyond it the air looked different—thicker, darker, waiting. I thought of everything Sefa had said. Cold water, or grief. Don't stop halfway.

I thought of the page in my bundle, and the handwriting that was mine, and the sentence I hadn't dared read twice: *they are coming for you.*

The hoofbeats were closer now. Somewhere behind me, torchlight bloomed against the hillsides like a wound opening.

I stepped through.

The crossing was exactly as she'd described—cold water, or grief, both at once, a pressure that slid over my skin and through it, peeling something away that I hadn't known I was wearing. For one heartbeat I stood in two places at once: the valley behind me, silent and salted and safe as a grave, and the world ahead, which breathed.

And oh, it *breathed*. The night air on the far side of the stone tasted of rain and iron and living things. The stars overhead were brighter, nearer, wrong in a way that made my teeth ache. Something deep in my chest, something that had been sitting quiet for twenty years under a stone of salt and silence, rolled over—

—and opened its eyes.

Light spilled from my fingertips. Just a thread of it, gold-white, curling off my skin like ink rising through water, and I stood in the dark beyond the border watching my own hands glow, and I laughed, once, high and unhinged, because it didn't hurt. After twenty years of numbness it was the opposite of hurt. It was waking up.

Then I saw the torches cresting the western pass behind me, and heard the dogs, and understood that the hunters hadn't come for the valley at all.

They'd come for me.

I ran—east, north, anywhere the road went, coat flying, bundle banging against my spine, light still leaking from my hands like a lantern I couldn't shutter—down off the rim and into a world I knew nothing about, pursued by everything, carrying nothing but a dead woman's letter and a page that wrote itself.

Somewhere behind me, Vell Hollow closed its gates and forgot my name.

I intended to forget it right back.

I failed.
