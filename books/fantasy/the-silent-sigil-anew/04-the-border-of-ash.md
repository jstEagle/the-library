## 4. The Border of Ash

I have read, in the course of my scribal career, some four hundred accounts of travel through wild country, and not one of them mentions the blisters.

Poets speak of wind on the heath and stars like scattered salt. Chroniclers speak of distances in leagues and perils in tidy numbered lists. Nobody—nobody—writes: *and then your boots, which fit you perfectly for eleven years, become instruments of torture by the second hour, and you understand that all human suffering is really just friction applied patiently.*

So let the record show that I fled Vell Hollow at a dead run, that I ran until my lungs filed a formal complaint, and that by the time I stopped to look behind me, I had traveled perhaps four miles and learned nothing except that terror is an excellent short-term fuel and a catastrophic long-term strategy.

The valley was gone. That was the first thing.

I stood on a ridge with my hands on my knees, gasping, and looked back at a wall of grey hills where Vell Hollow should have been—the crooked roofs, the temple bell, Sefa's cottage leaning against the slope like an old man against a fence post. None of it. Just hills, folded over each other like a fist closing, and a haze sitting in the hollow where my whole life used to be.

It wasn't fog. I want to be clear about that, because I spent a good minute telling myself it was fog, the way you tell yourself a lot of things when the alternative requires new furniture in your head. Fog moves. This sat. It had the patient, deliberate quality of something doing a job. The valley wasn't hidden from me; it was *closed*, shuttered like the temple's reliquary after evening bells, and I was on the wrong side of the door.

"Fine," I said, to nobody. My voice came out scraped raw. "Fine. Good riddance."

My hands were still glowing.

I held them up in the grey morning light and watched the light move under my skin—not on top of it, not reflected off it, but *in* it, the way lamplight lives inside horn lanterns. It pulsed when my heart did. It brightened when I was afraid, which meant it was very bright indeed, because by then I had compiled quite a comprehensive list of things to be afraid of and was working through it methodically.

Item one: the hunters behind me.

Item two: whatever this was in front of me.

Item three: the letter in my coat pocket, written in Sefa's cramped hand, which I had not yet been brave enough to read past the first line. *If you are reading this, then the valley has failed, and I am sorry, and you must not stop walking until you cross the border.*

*What border?* I had wanted to shout at her. There were no borders. Vell Hollow was a bowl of sheep fields and debt-ledgers, the most boring place ever constructed by mortal hands. The only thing we exported was wool and quiet despair.

Except that wasn't true either, was it. Because valleys don't need to be *built*. Valleys happen. And everything about Vell Hollow—the way weather never quite arrived, the way the temple prior burned papers at midnight, the way no one ever left and no one ever asked why—everything about it now looked less like home and more like a cage someone had built very carefully around a sleeping animal.

Around me.

I walked. Running felt like an invitation to fall down a ravine, so I walked fast, east along the ridgeline, following what might generously have been called a path and more accurately called a rumor of a path. The light in my hands dimmed as I calmed, or as I told myself I was calming, and by midday I could almost pretend it was gone if I kept my fists closed and didn't look directly at them. Like a bad tooth. Like a debt.

Always debts. Even this.

The land changed as I went. I noticed it before I understood it, the way you notice a room has gone quiet without noticing any particular sound stopping. The grass grew longer, then shorter, then strange—stalks with a faint blue sheen that bent toward me as I passed, which I chose not to think about. A stream crossed the trail and its water ran with a sound like whispering, actual whispering, syllable-shaped, and I stepped across it quickly and did not apologize to it, because apologizing to water is how you end up being the sort of person who gets found dead in a ditch with a look of profound embarrassment.

Then the birds stopped.

Not gradually. One moment there was lark-song somewhere above me, thin and ordinary, and the next there was nothing—a silence so complete I could hear my own blood moving, my own coat rustling, the small wet sounds of my own fear. Ahead of me the trail crested a low rise, and beyond the rise the world simply *ended*: a line across the earth, visible, unmistakable, running north to south as far as I could see in both directions.

On my side of the line: brown grass, blue-sheened stalks, grey sky.

On the other side: green. Impossible, saturated, greedy green, the green of a place that has never heard the word rationing. And light. Not sunlight exactly—sunlight plus something, sunlight with an undertone, the way wine has an undertone of the barrel it slept in.

I knew what it was. That was the horrible part. Some knowledge I had never earned rose up in me like water finding its level, calm and certain: *that is the boundary. That is the edge of the dead country. Everything you have ever been taught to be afraid of is on the other side of that line, and also, apparently, everything you are.*

Sefa's letter said don't stop walking until I crossed it.

The hunters said don't cross it, implicitly, by existing behind me with dogs and torches and royal writs. I glanced back. Far to the west, small as beetles, riders moved along the valley rim, fanning out in the patient pattern of people who know their quarry is tired. They hadn't seen me yet. The haze that had swallowed Vell Hollow seemed to be swallowing my trail too, which was either luck, mercy, or the valley's last gift, mailed late and postage due.

I stood at the edge of the boundary for a long time.

I am not going to pretend I was brave. I stood there because standing there was the last moment of my old life, and even a life as gray and ledger-bound as mine looks golden when you're about to trade it for the unknown. I thought about the Temple of Quiet Hours, about Prior Aldous burning papers by candlelight with his sleeves rolled up like a man doing dishes. I thought about my mother, whose voice I could suddenly not remember, which frightened me more than the riders did—I reached for it and found a hole shaped exactly like her, smooth-sided, polished, as though the absence had been there for years and I'd only just put my hand in it.

Then I thought about the page in my bundle, the one that rewrote itself, the one that had written my name in handwriting I almost recognized.

"Right," I told the boundary. "Well. One of us has to blink."

And I stepped across.

*

Pain is not an adequate word. Pain implies a location, a source, a thing that hurts. What happened to me at the border was a total event, a change of state, the way ice doesn't hurt when it melts—it just stops being itself.

Light went through me. Not from my hands—from everywhere, from the ground and the air and the green impossible grass, all of it pouring into me at once as though I were a dry channel and this was the first rain in a decade. Every nerve I owned lit up and reported simultaneously. I heard colors. I tasted sound. Somewhere very far away a voice that was mine was screaming, and closer than that, my knees hit the soft green turf, and closer still, inside my own skull, something *woke*.

I saw—flashes, fragments, none of them mine and all of them mine: a tower of white stone wrapped in ivy that glowed. A hall of shelves taller than cathedrals, breathing. Ink rising off a page and hanging in the air like a flock of black starlings. A woman's hand—my hand, younger, unscarred—pressing a seal into wax that was not wax but memory itself, molten and silver—

A voice, vast and layered, speaking a word I understood completely and could not afterward recall:

*"You came back."*

Then darkness, briefly, mercifully. Then grass against my cheek and my own ragged breathing and the taste of copper and ozone, which I am given to understand is not a normal flavor for a Tuesday.

I lay there for what might have been minutes or might have been the better part of an hour. When I finally managed to sit up, the light in my hands had settled—not gone, but banked, quieter, like coals raked over. My skin hummed. My teeth hummed. Somewhere in my chest, behind the breastbone, a door that had been locked my entire life stood open, and things were moving through it, and they were *home*.

"Well," I croaked. "That explains the valley."

Because it did, didn't it. You don't build a cage unless something needs caging. You don't smother a fire unless something burns. Vell Hollow hadn't been protecting the world from magic.

It had been protecting magic from the world. Or—worse, colder—it had been protecting *me*, and I had spent twenty years resenting every grey, safe, suffocating day of it.

I got up. I swayed. I got up again, because behind me, faint but no longer hypothetical, I could hear dogs.

*

They found me at dusk. Or rather—they found my smoke, because I had made the tactical error of building a fire, and the additional tactical error of being alive and visible next to it.

I smelled them first: horse and leather and woodsmoke that wasn't mine. Then voices, low and rough, coming up the darkening hillside, and I did the arithmetic every fugitive does—run and be chased in the open, or stay and be caught by the fire—and chose neither, because I was busy noticing something else.

There were five of them, and they weren't hunters. They were too ragged, for one thing. Hunters wore the crown's grey-and-gold; these wore patchwork, road-stained, mended with thread that didn't match. They had carts—one cart, actually, drawn by a mule with the expression of a retired philosopher—and the cart was full of bundles and birdcages and one enormous brass kettle. And the woman leading them had her hand raised, palm out, and the fire I had built was bending toward her like a dog greeting its mistress, and the sparks were spelling something in the air.

Sparks do not spell things. I had a whole education confirming that sparks do not spell things.

"Hoy," said the woman, spotting me in the shadows. Her voice carried the flat authority of someone who has talked many people down from many ledges. "You going to sit there glowing in the dark all night, or are you coming where it's warm?"

I looked down at myself. I was, in fact, glowing. Dimly, through my coat seams, through my collar, a faint gold seepage like a badly shuttered lamp. So much for keeping my fists closed.

"I'm not—" I started, and stopped, because the lie wouldn't finish assembling itself. *I'm not gifted.* Twenty-two years of practice at that sentence, and it died on my lips the moment I crossed a line in the grass.

"She's fresh," said one of the men, a wiry fellow missing two fingers. "Crossed today, by the look. Look at her eyes."

"Don't look at her eyes," the woman said briskly. "Look at the hill. Riders on the hill, if I'm any judge of torchlight, and we've maybe a quarter hour. Girl." She turned to me fully, and in the firelight I saw she was older than I'd guessed, sixty at least, with a face like a well-used cutting board. "Are they yours?"

"Are they my—"

"The ones chasing you. Are they yours, or are they somebody else's problem?"

"They're mine," I said. "I think. I'm not entirely sure what I did."

That got a laugh out of them—all of them, a low collective chuckle, the laugh of people who had each said some version of that exact sentence at some point on this same road. The woman jerked her thumb at the cart.

"Doss," she said. "Ferryman, mostly retired, currently a smuggler of persons. That's Brannic"—the wiry man—"that's Yeva, who'll want to talk to you about your light later because it's giving her a headache, and the rest you can meet when we're not about to die. Can you walk?"

"Yes," I said.

"Can you run?"

"...Yes?"

"Wonderful. Get in the cart."

Getting in the cart turned out to mean wedging myself between a birdcage full of what I sincerely hoped were pigeons and the enormous kettle, while Doss took the mule's halter and Yeva did something with her fingers and the fire—which went out all at once, every spark, swallowed whole, leaving us in perfect darkness. Behind us, on the hillside, torches swung and shouted at each other in confusion, hunting a glow that had just politely ceased to exist.

We rolled. The cart's axle complained in a dialect I came to know intimately over the following hours. And in the dark, pressed against warm brass and indignant pigeons, I heard Doss say, conversationally, to no one in particular:

"Another one out of the Hollow, then. That's three this year. Old hedge-witch must finally be dead."

My whole body went still.

"You knew Sefa," I said.

Doss was quiet long enough for the axle to complain twice. "Knew her?" he said at last. "Girl, half the gifted in the western reaches owe their skins to that woman. She held the border shut for forty years so the crown would forget there was anything worth hunting in that valley." A pause. "Guess she couldn't hold it no more."

I looked at my hands, glowing softly in the dark like two dying embers, and thought about a lean little cottage against a hillside, and a woman who had fed me soup and lied to me kindly for twenty years, and a letter in my pocket I still hadn't finished reading.

Behind us, the torches on the hill regrouped, and began, with terrible patience, to follow the cart tracks south.

* * *

Ivo Reyes counted his hounds' frustration the way other men counted money, and found himself, unusually, short.

"Lost it," said Sergeant Hale, reining up beside him on the ridge. The night below was empty where the glow had been. "Or it was never there. Could've been marsh-light, Captain. Could've been—"

"It was her." Ivo kept his voice level. He had learned, in fifteen years of service to the Office of Inquiry, that a captain's certainty was worth more than a captain's accuracy. Certainty moved men. Accuracy only moved paperwork. "The Quintana anomaly. She crossed the boundary at approximately the fourth bell—that flare would have registered on a deaf man's teeth—and she's traveling east with no gear, no guide, and no idea what she's carrying." He turned his horse south, toward the wheel-ruts stitched across the dark hillside. "Which means she'll find the caravans within two days. They always do. The gifted smell each other out like cats."

"And if she's joined a caravan?"

"Then we stop being subtle." Ivo studied the ruts a moment longer. Something nagged at him—the tracks, the depth of them, the turn of the wheels. He had grown up around carts. He had grown up around... 

He frowned. The thought slid away, the way thoughts did sometimes, like water through a cracked cup, and he found himself looking at his own hand on the rein and wondering, briefly, why he'd been looking at it.

"Captain?" Hale said.

"South pass," Ivo said. "Full company. And Hale—if we take her alive, she goes to Meridian intact. The writ says *intact*." He said it firmly, the way a man repeats a thing he has told himself before. "Whatever the handler's people suggest otherwise."

He spurred his horse down toward the road, and did not notice that he was humming a tune he had no memory of learning.

* * *

Two days in the cart taught me several things.

First: pigeon cages are not load-bearing, and neither, morally speaking, is a man named Brannic.

Second: the gifted do not introduce themselves the way normal people do. Normal people say their names and their trades. These people showed you. The boy who spoke to locks—he was maybe ten, all elbows, named Tam—picked the padlock off the kettle's lid just by humming at it, then looked at me with the defiant pride of a child showing a scar. Yeva braided the wind above the cart into shapes: a fish, a hand, a rude gesture aimed back down the road at our pursuit, which she assured me had morale value. Brannic mended a cracked wagon-wheel by holding it and *apologizing to it*, and I swear on the Temple's ledgers, the wheel forgave him.

Third: they were afraid. All of them, constantly, the way I had been afraid in Vell Hollow—quietly, habitually, the fear worn smooth as a prayer bead. They were running from the same crown that had sent riders to my valley, and they ran the way you breathe, without thinking about it, because stopping meant dying.

And fourth—this one took both days and cost me the most sleep—they wanted nothing from me.

I kept waiting for it. In Vell Hollow, kindness always came with a ledger attached. The Temple fed you and wrote it down. Neighbors helped you and remembered it at tithe season. But Doss fed me stew from the enormous kettle and asked for nothing. Yeva taught me how to bank the light in my hands—*think of it as breath, girl, you're holding it like a scream*—and asked for nothing. Tam scooted over on the cart bench to make room for me and asked for stories instead, and even those he paid for, trading me one of his own, lock by lock, gate by gate, a whole secret geography of doors.

"What's your gift, then?" he asked me on the second evening, legs swinging, as the cart crawled through country so beautiful it felt like showing off.

"I don't know," I admitted. It was easier to say aloud now. Almost everything was easier to say aloud now, which was its own kind of terrifying. "Something with writing. With pages. Things I write come... true. Or change. Or remember."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "That's the best one," he announced.

"It's the worst one," Doss said, from the mule's head, not turning around. "Gifts that touch paper touch ledgers, girl, and ledgers touch the crown, and the crown touches back." He spat, expertly, off the moving cart. "But we'll make something of it. We make something of everybody."

Ahead of us, past a shoulder of dark hills, the land dropped away into a plain—and on the plain, far off, a city glowed. Not with fire. With light the way my hands glowed, contained and deliberate, a whole skyline of it, towers crowned with lamps that burned without flame. Roads converged on it from every direction like threads pulled toward a needle's eye, and on those roads, even at this distance, I could see movement: carts, caravans, processions, all flowing in.

"What is that?" I asked.

"Meridian," Yeva said, and her voice did something complicated—want and grief in the same syllable. "City of Counted Years. Where memories are money and the gifted pay rent in their own heads." She smiled, without any joy in it whatsoever. "Every road leads there eventually, girl. Even ours. *Especially* ours."

I looked at the glowing city, and at the letter in my coat that I still had not finished reading, and at my own hands, banked coals beneath my skin.

Somewhere behind us, patient as ink drying, the hunters followed.

And ahead, in a city built on remembering, waited whatever it was that had already known my name.
