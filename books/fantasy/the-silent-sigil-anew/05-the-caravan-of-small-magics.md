## 5. The Caravan of Small Magics

I've hauled a lot of cargo across the borderlands in my years—grain, salt, contraband hymnals, one very angry goat—but I'd never hauled anything as fragile as the girl Sefa's letter vouched for.

Mara Quintana came to us at dusk on the third day past the pass of ash, walking like someone who expected the ground to bill her for it. She had a satchel clutched to her chest the way other folk clutch children or coin, and she looked at our fires the way a stray looks at a lit window: wanting in, certain it wasn't allowed.

"Name's Doss," I told her. "Ferryman by trade, which out here means I decide who crosses and who drowns, and mostly I mean that about rivers."

She blinked at me. "That's supposed to be reassuring?"

"No, it's supposed to be true. Reassurance is extra." I jerked my chin toward the wagons strung along the creek like beads on a wire. "Supper's at the blue wagon. Don't touch Yeva's kettle unless you want your eyebrows weathered off."

She didn't laugh. She did something better—she filed it away, like ink on a page. A collector, then. Good. Collectors live longest.

* * *

You want to know what a caravan of the gifted looks like? Disappointment, mostly. That's the honest answer. You're imagining towers and thunderbolts, and instead you get forty-odd souls in patched canvas, mules with opinions, and a cookfire that smokes because Brann the mender keeps fixing the flue wrong on purpose so he has something to fix again tomorrow.

We had menders, three of them, whose gift is coaxing broken things back toward whole—kettles, bones, axles, occasionally marriages if you pay double and don't expect miracles. We had Yeva, who sings to weather the way a mother scolds a child she loves anyway. We had the Corven sisters, who can taste lies on the air, which makes card night an exercise in theology. And we had Tam.

Tam was eleven when he came to us, all elbows and secrets, found locked inside a granary he couldn't have gotten into and couldn't be gotten out of until he asked the lock nicely. Speaks to locks, our boy. Not opens them—speaks to them. Says they're lonely. Says most locks only ever get one conversation their whole lives, the click of being made, and then nobody talks to them again except thieves, and thieves are rude.

The crown calls what we have a curse. The temples call it a stain. The criers call it grounds for the pyre. Out here, over forty years and three hundred miles of road, we learned the truth, which is smaller and harder and better:

Gift is craft. It's a trade, like coopering or midwifery. It wants apprenticeship, practice, failure, sore hands. Nobody expects a farrier to shoe his first horse blindfolded in a thunderstorm, but that's exactly what the world demands of the gifted—master your gift alone, in terror, or die of it. So we built a school disguised as a refugee train, and we charged no tuition but work.

Mara watched us for two days before she said a word beyond yes, thank you, and where do you keep the ink.

It was the ink question that gave her away, if I'm honest. Nobody asks where we keep the ink.

* * *

On the fourth morning I found her sitting cross-legged outside Brann's wagon, watching him mend a shattered milk jug, her fingers twitching like she was copying every motion into some ledger behind her eyes.

"He'll fail twice before noon," I said, sitting down beside her with the deliberate groan of a man whose knees file complaints. "Then he'll get it."

"Why would he fail on purpose?"

"Because the gift isn't in the success, girl. It's in the listening. First time he fixed that jug, it held water for a day and burst. Second time, a week. He's up to a season now. He says the jug tells him where it broke." I shrugged. "Maybe it does. Maybe he tells himself. Either way, that's craft. You listen, you learn the grain of the thing, you work with it, not over it."

Mara turned her head slowly. "And yours? What do you listen to?"

"Ferryman's gift. Water." I picked up a pebble and tossed it in the creek. "Every crossing has a temper. Some rivers want to drown you and some just want to be asked properly. I ask properly." I glanced sideways at her. "Your turn."

"I don't have—" She stopped. Her hand went to her satchel, that reflexive clutch. "There was a page," she said carefully. "In Vell Hollow. It rewrote itself overnight. In handwriting that—I think it was mine. I don't remember writing it."

Ah. There it was. I'd seen that look before, usually on the young ones we took in—the look of a person standing at the edge of a well, hearing their own echo come back wrong.

"Vell Hollow smothers magic," I said. "Been that way longer than anyone remembers. But smothering isn't killing, girl. It's holding under the water. You were carried through that border once already, by the smell of you. Whatever woke when you crossed back over—it didn't arrive. It surfaced."

"And what if it drowns me?"

"Then you learn to swim," I said, "and we'll teach you. That's the whole arrangement. That's the entire covenant between us and anybody who walks into this camp: we teach each other to swim."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "Sefa wrote that you'd say something like that."

"Sefa," I said, "owes me eleven silver and a good knife, and now I'll never collect, so tell me everything she taught you and we'll call it even."

For the first time since she'd walked into camp, Mara Quintana almost smiled. Almost. It was more of a crack in the ice. But cracks are how rivers start.

* * *

She began transcribing on the fifth day.

It started small. She sat with Brann while he worked and wrote down not just the motions but the words he muttered—not spells exactly, more like a carpenter talking to a stubborn board—and she noticed things none of us had words for. She noticed that his failures clustered near the new moon. She noticed that Yeva's storm-songs changed key depending on whether she wanted rain to fall or merely to wait. She sat with the Corven sisters and asked them to describe the taste of a lie, and when they said copper-and-honey, she wrote *copper-and-honey*, and then asked, in a voice gone very quiet, whether different liars tasted different.

They do. We'd never thought to say it aloud. It was like watching someone invent notation for music everyone could hum but nobody could write.

"That's what you were," Yeva said to her one evening, watching Mara's pen fly across a cheap pulp page by firelight. "Before the valley. Before whatever they did to you. You were a keeper."

"I was a debt-bound copyist," Mara said, without looking up.

"You were a keeper," Yeva repeated, in the tone she used on weather fronts that thought about misbehaving. "There's a difference between writing down a thing and keeping a thing alive on paper. Most scribes do the first. The Archive used to do the second, back when there was an Archive worth the name." She spat into the fire, which is Yeva's way of punctuating. "Crown burned the branches one by one. Now gifts die with their owners, unwritten, and each generation starts poorer than the last. That's not accident, girl. That's policy."

Mara's pen had gone still. I watched her absorb it—the shape of the trap, the slow erasure dressed up as law and order—and I watched her make a decision. I've ferried enough souls to know the moment a person stops drifting and starts rowing. Her shoulders set. She turned to a fresh page and wrote at the top, in a hand that suddenly looked older than she was:

*Caravan of Small Magics — Being a True and Living Record.*

"There's no such book," I said.

"There is now."

Well. What do you say to that? I said nothing. I went and got her more ink from my private stock, the good iron-gall, the stuff I save for contracts, because if a girl's going to write a living book, she ought to write it in ink that outlives her.

* * *

Tam adopted her around the same time, in the way of boys, which is to say he appeared beside her constantly, offered unsolicited commentary, and pretended this was her privilege.

"You hold your pen like you're afraid of it," he informed her on the sixth evening.

"It's a quill, not a snake."

"All quills are snakes. They bite when you least expect it. Splinters." He peered at her pages. "You wrote down what Brann says to the jug. Why?"

"So it doesn't die with him."

Tam considered this with the gravity of an emperor reviewing troops. "Locks talk too," he offered. "If you want. For your book. Most people don't want. They just want me to open things."

"What do locks say?" Mara asked—and here's the thing, she asked like she actually wanted the answer, leaned in and everything, and I saw Tam's ears go pink and knew the boy was lost to her forever.

"They say what they're guarding," Tam said. "Did you know that? Every lock describes its treasure. The granary talked about wheat for hours. The strongbox on the toll-road wouldn't shut up about deeds and letters. Locks are proud. Somebody should write them down." He looked up at her, suddenly fierce. "Somebody should write everything down. Before the riders come."

The fires crackled. Somewhere a mule complained philosophically. And Mara put her hand on the boy's shoulder and said, "Then teach me what they say, and I'll write every word."

I turned away so neither of them would see the ferryman's face doing something unprofessional. Found family forms fast out here. Fast and fragile, like ice on a spring creek—you learn to love it knowing the thaw is coming, or you learn nothing at all.

* * *

I want to record, in my own hand as it were, that we knew the riders were coming. We weren't fools. A caravan of forty gifted moving east after a royal hunt gets announced is a candle in a barn full of hay; the only questions were when, and whether we'd smell the smoke in time.

Yeva smelled it first, naturally. Ninth morning, she stood up from breakfast with her head cocked like a bird's and said, "South wind's carrying horse. A lot of horse. And iron." Then she looked at me and said, "Half a day. Maybe less if they push."

We struck camp the way we'd drilled it—a drill Mara had watched with wide eyes and transcribed, God help me, even then, even while we packed. Wagons into column. Non-combatants forward. Menders scatter to pre-weaken the ford downstream so any pursuit has to slow. Corven sisters upwind as pickets, tasting the air for lies, because soldiers lie to themselves loudest of all right before a charge.

But half a day is a wish, not a guarantee, and wishes run out.

They hit us at the camp's edge while the last wagon was still being hitched—not the main company, just six riders, outriders, testing us like a tongue tests a sore tooth. I'll give the crown this much: their cavalry picks brave men, or men too stupid to know better. Six against forty, even forty running, takes either courage or orders written by someone else's cowardice.

The first arrow took the tarp off Brann's wagon. The second took old Piet in the shoulder, and I heard Brann scream—not at the wound, at the *mending*, because Piet's heart was stuttering and Brann had to listen through the blood to find the rhythm of the break, hands shaking, lips moving, telling the wound where it lived until it agreed to stop.

Yeva sang.

I've heard her sing storms down out of a clear sky and droughts out of a drowned field, but I'd never heard her sing *angry*. The south wind turned. Dust rose up in a wall and blinded the riders' horses, and their formation—that neat, brutal wedge—shredded into six separate men suddenly negotiating with animals that wanted no part of it.

And in the middle of it, Mara Quintana stood in the open with her book held over her head like a shield, screaming at Tam to get under the wagon, getting under nothing herself, because her pages were loose in the wind and she was diving for every single sheet like a woman rescuing drowning children.

I grabbed her by the collar and hauled her behind the wheel of my wagon as a rider thundered past close enough to count his teeth. "The book can be rewritten!" I shouted at her.

"It *can't*—" and then she stopped, and something crossed her face like weather, and she said, quieter, terrible: "Unless the writer survives. Unless somebody remembers."

"That's the lesson," I said. "First one and last one. Everything else is commentary."

The dust settled. The riders wheeled off south—one lay groaning with a broken leg his own horse had given him, and Brann, curse his soft heart, splinted it before we let him go. We lost the tarp, some stores, Piet nearly lost his life and kept his arm. And we gained a piece of knowledge we hadn't wanted: they knew where we were, and next time it wouldn't be six.

That night we moved by moonlight, dark-camped, cold food, no fires. Mara sat beside me on the wagon bench, holding her rescued pages, smoothing them flat one by one like a woman counting her children.

"Doss," she said. "The page from Vell Hollow. The one that rewrites itself. When the wind scattered everything, it flew highest. Like it *wanted* to be found."

"Pages don't want."

"This one does." She looked east, toward Meridian, though in the dark I doubt she could see anything but her own thoughts. "And I think I need to find out why. I think it's the reason Sefa died getting me out."

I flicked the reins and the mule grumbled forward into the dark, hauling forty lives and one impossible book toward the City of Counted Years, where memories mint into coin and every one of us was spending faster than we earned.

Behind us, faint on the south wind, I heard horses again. More than six this time.

Much more.
