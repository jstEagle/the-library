## 7. The City of Counted Years

The first thing I saw of Meridian was the coin-tower, and the second thing I saw was that the tower was breathing.

I want to be clear about this, because Doss would later insist I was imagining it. The tower rose out of the city's heart like a needle made of pale glass, and along its length ran veins of gold light that pulsed—slowly, patiently, the way a sleeping thing breathes. Every pulse sent a shiver down through the streets below, and every shiver was answered by a sound like a thousand small bells being struck at once, somewhere too far away to find.

"That's the Mint," Doss said, and spat, which seemed to be his opinion of most things. "Where they make the money."

"Money comes from mines," I said. "Ore, and fire, and men with hammers."

"Not here." He reined the cart horse around a rut deep enough to drown a boot in. "Here, money comes from heads."

I looked at him. He did not look back.

"You're joking," I said.

"I've never joked in my life. Ask anyone." He nodded toward the tower. "Memories, girl. They take them out of people and they press them into coin. A first kiss'll buy you bread for a month. Your mother's face might buy a house, if it's a good one. Whole childhoods go for fortunes." He paused, and his voice went flat as slate. "The rich buy other people's lives because their own aren't enough. Been that way since before my grandfather's grandfather."

I sat with that for the rest of the road into the city, which took another hour, and by the end of the hour I had stopped being able to tell whether the cold in my chest was the wind off the lowlands or something else.

Because I knew about paying with memories now. Chapter six of my life, if I were crude enough to number it: the checkpoint ledger, the forged courier licenses, and afterward the discovery that my gift took its fee not in blood or sweat but in recollection. I had paid without meaning to. The sound of my mother's voice—gone. Not dimmed. *Gone*, the way a page is gone when you burn it, leaving only the shape of where it used to sit in the stack.

So I understood, in the small way a man who has lost a finger understands a country where fingers are currency. What I did not understand, until we came over the last rise and the whole gray sprawl of Meridian opened beneath us, was what it meant for a city to be built on the practice.

* * *

Meridian smelled like rain on hot stone and, underneath that, like nothing at all. That was the strangest part. Every city I'd ever heard described had a smell—fish, smoke, tanneries, sewage, incense. Meridian's smell was absence, a scrubbed blankness, as though the city itself had been paid to forget what it was.

The caravan came in through the Weft Gate with the sun going down behind us, so our shadows stretched out ahead like long dark heralds announcing our arrival, and nobody in the streets so much as glanced up. That told me something too. In Vell Hollow, strangers were an event. Here, we were weather.

Doss led us into a quarter where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators, and there he stopped the carts and stood up on the bench and addressed us all in the voice he probably used on rivers.

"All right. Listen, because I'll say this once. Meridian's got rules, and the rules are simple. One: don't sell anything you can't afford to lose. Two: don't sign anything you can't read. Three—" and here he looked directly at me, "—don't go looking for trouble in tall buildings."

"What tall building?" asked Tam, who was fourteen and had the gift of making every adult within earshot feel ancient.

"The one you're both already planning to visit," Doss said. "Goodnight."

He was right, of course. That was the humiliating part. Yeva went off with the menders to find lodging among the rememberers' quarter—more on that phrase shortly—and Tam and I stood in the lamplight outside a chandler's shop, and I said, "We should find the Covenant Archive," and Tam said, "Obviously," in the tone of someone agreeing the sky is up.

Here is what I knew, or thought I knew. Somewhere in Meridian there was an archive—not a library exactly, but something older, something that predated the Mint and possibly the city—that held the record of the covenant between the two realms. The old pact. The reason the valley existed. The reason magic frayed at the edges of the world like old cloth. Sefa had died holding a border closed and had left me a letter I hadn't finished reading, because reading it felt like standing at the edge of a cliff in the dark, and I wanted daylight first. But the letter had named the Archive, and names are invitations.

What I did not know—what I could not have known—was waiting for me inside.

* * *

We found it the way you find anything important in a strange city: by asking the wrong people first. A fishwife directed us to a countinghouse. A drunk directed us to a brothel he spoke of with genuine affection. Finally a boy selling roasted chestnuts pointed his poker at a narrow street climbing the hill and said, "The Quiet House? Up there, past the place with no windows," and Tam said, "The place with no windows?" and the boy shrugged and said, "It doesn't need any," which was somehow worse than an answer.

The Covenant Archive did not look like much from outside. A door of black wood, iron-banded, set into a wall of the same gray stone as everything else, with a lintel carved in letters worn nearly smooth. I could just make them out: *WHAT IS GIVEN CANNOT BE UNGIVEN.*

Cheerful place.

Tam knocked. Nothing happened. He knocked again, harder, and the door opened on its own—not swung, exactly, more like it decided we had knocked enough—and we stepped into a hall that was larger than the building containing it, which I have since learned is a thing archives do when they're showing off.

Candles burned in niches with no visible source of wax. Shelves climbed past the reach of sight. And everywhere, everywhere, *books*—but not books the way I knew them. These breathed. Their covers rose and fell faintly, like sleeping animals. Some murmured. One, near the door, appeared to be snoring.

A figure glided toward us out of the stacks. I say glided because I never saw its feet; its robe simply continued down into shadow. It had a face like a mask carved by someone who had been told about faces secondhand, and when it spoke, its voice came from slightly to the left of where it stood.

"Visitors," it said. "How rare. How expensive."

"We want information," I said. "About the covenant. Between the realms."

The mask-face tilted. Something behind it, whatever actually lived in this place, considered me, and I felt the consideration physically, like a hand pressed flat against my sternum.

"Do you have payment?"

"I have—" I patted my pockets with the dignity of the destitute. "Coin. A little."

"Coin." The word came out flavored with contempt, or perhaps pity. "The Mint's coin. No. This house trades in the true currency. Memory, freely given, or memory taken as toll. What do you know of the price?"

More than I wanted to, as it happened. "I know gifts cost memories," I said carefully. "Mine does. I paid once. I know how it works."

"You know how *your* mechanism works," the archivist said. "You do not know how *this* house works. Here, memory is not spent. It is *filed*. Given memories are not destroyed—they are kept. Read. Sometimes returned, if the borrower proves interesting." It drifted closer, and the candle flames bent toward it like grass toward wind. "You wish to know of the covenant. Very well. The shallow answer costs little. The covenant between the realm of mortals and the realm of the sigil-born was sealed long ago, and broken long ago, and everything since—the valleys that smother, the hunts, the Mint, the fraying boundary your caravan crossed to get here—is the slow unwinding of that break. Two realms shared one wellspring. The pact governed how it flowed. Someone cut the pact. Now the wellspring runs wild on one side and dry on the other, and both sides are dying of it at different speeds."

"And the deep answer?" Tam asked, because Tam had no survival instincts whatsoever.

"The deep answer," the archivist said, "requires that I show you the breaking itself. The record of it. The hand that made it." Its mask turned to me with the slow inevitability of a door closing. "And that record, scribe, is filed under your name."

The hall went very quiet. Even the snoring book stopped.

"I think," I said, "you've mistaken me for someone else."

"I have never mistaken anyone for anything," said the archivist. "It is the one skill this house possesses without flaw. Mara Quintana. Of Vell Hollow. Temple-trained. Debt-bound. Carried across a border as a child by a woman who was not her mother, and marked before she could walk." It raised one hand, and a book came down off a shelf three stories up, drifting through the candlelight into its palm like a tired bird. "Shall I continue? There is so much. You were here before, you know. In this city. In this house. You knew these shelves. You shelved some of them yourself."

"No," I said.

"You signed the ledger of acquisitions in a hand the house still keeps samples of," the archivist went on, merciless, gentle, terrible. "Would you like to see it? The comparison costs nothing. The house considers it a courtesy."

And the book in its hands opened, and turned, and floated to me, and I looked down at two pages side by side.

On the left, an old entry in faded brown ink—a signature, a date in a calendar I didn't recognize, a line of formal script beneath it. On the right, a fresh page in my own handwriting, the transcription of Yeva's rain-song I'd made on the road, the ink barely a week dry.

They matched. Stroke for stroke. The same slanted capitals. The same peculiar way I crossed my t's with a single impatient flick. The same small habit of pressing too hard on descenders, as if I didn't quite trust ink to stay where I put it.

"That's impossible," I whispered. "I've never been here. I grew up in the valley. I've never left it. I would *remember*—"

"Would you?" said the archivist.

And the bottom dropped out of the world.

Because I wouldn't, would I. I knew exactly how memories left a person—I had watched one leave *me*, recently, completely, without even the courtesy of a goodbye. I knew a person could lose a thing so thoroughly she didn't miss it, didn't ache for it, didn't even know there was a hole, because the hole closed over like water. My mother's voice was gone and I had grieved it only after I learned it was gone. What else had closed over? What else had I paid, or been made to pay, or chosen to pay in a life I had no access to?

"How much of me is missing?" I asked. My voice sounded like someone else's. "How much did I give? Or—was it taken?"

"That," said the archivist, "is the deep answer. And the deep answer has a price."

"Of course it does." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Everything has a price. Fine. Name it."

"The memory of standing in this room tonight," the archivist said. "Freely given, freely kept. You will leave knowing less than you know now—but the knowledge will be yours again whenever you return to claim it. The house does not destroy what it holds, scribe. It waits. The question is whether you can afford to wait with it."

Tam's hand found my sleeve. "Mara. Don't."

But I was already looking at the two pages. At my own handwriting staring back at me across a gap of years I couldn't see into. A scribe broke the covenant, the archivist had said—or would say, or had implied, the words rearranging themselves in my head even as I tried to hold them. A scribe trained in a temple. A scribe who wrote the way I write. A scribe who erased herself, maybe—who paid, or was made to pay, or chose to pay everything, and walked into a valley built to keep her empty, and grew up copying ledgers for a debt that was never really about money.

The vertigo hit then, properly, the floor tilting under feet that were standing perfectly still. Because if the woman in the old ledger was me—if the hand that broke the covenant was my hand—then I hadn't come to Meridian to learn about the pact. I'd come back. Whatever I'd been running from, or running *as*, had brought me here the way water finds the sea, and everyone who had helped me along the road—Sefa dying at the border, Doss and his river voice, Yeva and Tam and the boy who spoke to locks—everyone had been carrying me toward a door I had apparently walked through once before and paid a fortune to forget.

"Mara," Tam said again, sharper. "Whatever it's asking, we can find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't always another way," I said. "Sometimes there's just the way, and the price, and the walking."

I looked at the archivist. The archivist looked at me, if a mask can be said to look, and waited with the patience of a thing that has waited longer than cities.

"One night's memory," I said. "That's all?"

"For the deep answer. Yes."

"And after I pay it—I won't know what I learned here. I'll walk out thinking we came for nothing."

"You will walk out," the archivist agreed, "with a hole shaped exactly like the truth. Most people live their whole lives that way. You will merely know where the hole is. That is more than most are given."

I thought of my mother's voice. I thought of how I hadn't known it was gone until someone told me, and how the telling had hurt worse than the losing, and how I would do it anyway, again, knowingly, because the alternative was to stand in front of the answer to everything I was and turn around and go back to copying ledgers.

"Show me the breaking first," I said. "Before I decide. Show me the hand."

Something moved behind the mask that might have been a smile.

"As you wish," said the archivist, and opened the old book wide, and the candles in a thousand niches guttered all at once, and the shelves began, softly, to breathe.
