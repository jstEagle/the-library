## 9. Lockspeak

The first thing you should know about locks is that they're liars.

Every lock in Meridian tells you the same story: *I am strong, I am closed, I am not for you.* And every lock in Meridian is wrong about all three, if you know how to listen. A lock is just a door's way of saying please. It says it in tumblers and wards and little brass teeth, and most people never hear the words because they're too busy trying to break its heart with a crowbar.

I don't use a crowbar. Crowbars are rude.

I was eleven years old when I learned I could hear them — crouched in a counting-house cellar where my mother had hidden me during a raid on the rememberers' quarter, pressing my ear to an iron-banded door while boots thundered overhead. The lock said, very quietly, *I'm so tired of holding this shut. Nobody ever asks if I want to hold things shut.* And I asked. That was all it took. That was always all it took. Locks are so lonely they'll open for anyone who bothers to ask what they want.

My mother sold her memories of me two winters later to pay our indenture fees. I know because I watched her do it — watched the clerk draw the sound of my name out of her like thread from a spool. Afterward she looked at me the way you look at furniture in a room you've just moved into. She still fed me. She still signed my work-chits. But somewhere under the city there's a coin with my whole childhood pressed into one side of it, and some merchant probably spends it on wine without ever knowing he's drinking a boy's first snowfall.

That's Meridian for you. Everything here can be counted, and everything that counts can be taken.

Except stories. Stories nobody wants. Too bulky, too cheap, no resale value. You can't mint a memory of a bad joke told twice. So when I met the scribe woman from the caravan — Mara, with her ink-stained fingers and her face like someone had handed her a bill she couldn't read — I didn't offer her a trade. Trades are how this city eats people. I offered her a story instead, free of charge, and watched her flinch like I'd thrown something at her.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong," I told her, three evenings after the caravan limped through the Counted Gate.

She looked up from her ledger. We'd claimed a corner of the Lamplighters' Rest, which wasn't really a rest at all but a squat behind a tannery where the smell kept the census-takers away. Doss had gone off to argue with a ferryman about tolls, Yeva was teaching two of the menders to braid fog, and Mara sat hunched over her transcription work like the pages might bite.

"Doing what wrong?"

"That." I pointed at the page. She was copying out one of Yeva's weather-songs, the ones that coaxed rain off rooftops before it rotted the thatch. "You're writing it down like it's dead."

Mara frowned. Up close she had ink on her chin and a smudge on her cheekbone and eyes that did arithmetic even when she was resting. Temple-trained. You could always tell — temple scribes held their pens like the pen might be audited later.

"It's a preservation," she said. "If I copy the spell accurately, it survives even if—" She stopped. Even if the singer got blanked. Even if the crown's men came with their writs and their silver needles. She didn't say it. Nobody in the caravan said it out loud; we just all heard it anyway, the way you hear a lock ticking over in its sleep.

"Accurate," I said. "Right. Here's what accurate gets you." I pulled a scrap of paper from my coat — actual paper, not bark-pulp, stolen fair and square from a merchant's bin — and wrote: *Rain comes when called.* "There. Accurate. Now make it rain."

"I can't make it rain."

"No. Because it's dead. You copied the corpse of the song." I tapped her ledger. "Yeva doesn't sing *rain comes when called.* She sings about the river missing the sea, about clouds being heavy with everything they've carried, about how the roof is tired too and would like to lie down wet. The rain isn't commanded. It's *invited.* Your valley never taught you that? Magic isn't a ledger line. It's a guest."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a pickpocket — hunger and grief holding hands. "The valley didn't teach me anything," she said quietly. "The valley taught me that magic doesn't exist."

"And yet here you sit, rewriting a checkpoint ledger with your mind and losing your mother's voice to pay for it, in a city where memories are money." I shrugged. "Sounds like magic exists pretty hard."

Her hand went to her throat, the way it did whenever anyone mentioned the price she'd paid at the checkpoint. I'd noticed that. People carry their lost memories like phantom limbs. Doss rubs his left shoulder where his brother's laugh used to live. Yeva hums around holes in her own songs and pretends the tune was always meant to skip there.

"Fine," Mara said at last. "Teach me the living version."

So I did. Not the weather-song — that was Yeva's to give, and Yeva gave it two nights later, sitting cross-legged on the tannery roof with the whole ragged caravan gathered round while the clouds came down the valley like homesick dogs. What I taught Mara was smaller. I taught her to ask before she wrote. To put her ear against the page, figuratively speaking, and hear what the spell wanted to be instead of deciding for it.

"You talk to locks," she said, the fourth evening. Not a question. Word travels fast in a caravan of twenty-three people sharing three pots.

"Locks, hinges, padlocks, hasps. Anything with a mechanism and an opinion."

"What do they say?"

"Mostly complaints. Mostly loneliness." I picked the squat's front door open one-handed to demonstrate — it sighed open like an old man settling into a bath. "See? It just wanted someone to knock properly. Doors spend their whole lives being slammed. You'd be bitter too."

And Mara Quintana laughed. Actually laughed — short and rusty, like a hinge herself, like something that hadn't been oiled in years — and I decided right then that I liked her, in the specific way you like a person who has clearly been hurt recently by the world and keeps showing up to it anyway with clean hands and straight posture. Temple scribes. Give them a debt and they'll pay it to the last copper, even when the debt is their entire self.

* * *

Here's the thing about trading stories instead of memories: nobody warns you how fast it goes.

A memory costs you forever. You pay it and it's gone, and some clerk in a counted house owns the shape of your tenth birthday until the coin wears smooth. But a story costs nothing and gives nothing up. You tell it and keep it. Tell it again tomorrow and it's changed a little, grown a new elbow, learned a better ending. Stories are the only currency in Meridian that appreciates.

So we traded. I gave her the tale of how Doss won the caravan its mule — which involves a rigged dice game, a furious widow, and the mule itself eating the evidence — and she gave me Vell Hollow, the valley where nothing magical could breathe. She described it the way you describe a parent you have complicated feelings about: the chalk-white ledgers, the prior with his soft voice and burning hands, the way the air felt thick and dumb, like wading through porridge.

"It wasn't peaceful," she said. "Everyone says valleys like that must be peaceful. It was more like... held breath. Twenty years of held breath."

"Were you happy?"

The question hung there. I watched her reach for the answer and find the shelf empty — because of course, she'd already spent the sound of her mother's voice, and maybe happiness lived next door to it, in rooms that had collapsed when that wall came out.

"I was safe," she said finally. "Safe and small. There's a difference between those and happy, and I'm only learning the vocabulary now."

Then she asked about my mother. I told her about the clerk's needle and the thread and the look on my mother's face afterward, and I told it funny, because that's how I tell everything, and Mara didn't laugh. She put her pen down and looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, and said, "Tam. That isn't a funny story."

"It's funnier if you tell it fast."

"It isn't funny at all." And she reached across the table — careful, slow, the way you reach toward a cat that's been kicked — and squeezed my wrist once, and let go, and picked her pen back up like nothing happened.

I went to bed that night behind the tannery and lay awake listening to the building's locks murmur their insomnia, and thought: *huh.* Nobody had done that. Said *that isn't funny* and meant it. In Meridian everyone knows sad stories; sad stories are inventory. But knowing a sad story and refusing to laugh at it are different trades entirely.

* * *

On the sixth day I showed her the children.

Not because she asked. Because she'd been asking without asking — her eyes tracking the small figures in the rememberers' quarter whenever we passed through, the kids in grey smocks with numbered chits stitched at the collar, hauling memory-crates twice their weight or sitting in the counting-houses' windows like display goods. Indentured minors. The crown calls them *apprentices.* The clerks call them *stock.*

"There are four hundred and six," I told her, walking the long gallery above the Tallymarket, where you could see down into the minting floors through grated iron. Below us, children fed memories into the presses — other people's memories, mostly, sorted and graded like fruit. "I've counted. I count them every week. Four hundred and six as of Tuesday. Two were freed in spring. One ran and got caught and they blanked him as an example, which — you can't blank an example of running away, that's the whole point of running away, but logic isn't big in the counting-houses."

Mara stood very still at the rail. Below, a girl of maybe eight fed a strip of pale memory-glass into the press, and the machine drank it, and stamped out coins.

"How old is she?"

"Seven when they took her in. She's nine now. She remembers being seven and nothing since, because they take the years as she earns them. They call it *drawing down the account.*" My voice came out flat. I'd learned to say it flat. Flat kept it bearable. "They'll release her at sixteen with whatever balance remains, which will be nothing, because it's designed to be nothing. The indenture is a machine for turning children into empty adults who are grateful for the emptiness because at least they don't remember what was taken."

"Why do you know all their numbers?"

"Because I promised myself somebody would." I turned to look at her straight on. This was the part that mattered. This was the part I'd been walking toward for six days, and my stomach was doing the thing it does before a hard lock, the fluttery part, the part where you ask nicely and hope the mechanism is feeling generous. "Nobody counts them, Mara. The census counts them as property. Their families forget them — that's half the business model, sell the parents' memories of the kid first, so there's no one left to come looking. Somebody has to remember four hundred and six children exist, or they stop existing anywhere except a ledger."

The market noise rose around us — hawkers, coin-clerk bells, the groan of the presses below — and Mara Quintana stood at the rail with her knuckles white on the iron and her jaw set in that temple-scribe line, and I watched her do the thing she apparently did, which was turn grief into resolve the way Yeva turned fog into rain.

"Promise me," I said.

"Promise you what?"

"That they won't be forgotten. All of them. Whatever you're here to do — the Archive, the covenant, the whole sigil-born business, whatever it turns out to be — promise me the children go in the ledger of things that matter. Write it down if you have to. You're good at writing things down."

She was quiet for a long moment. Down below, the press stamped, stamped, stamped, patient as a heartbeat.

"I can't promise I'll succeed," she said at last. "I've seen enough ledgers to know promises cost more than people think."

"I'm not asking for success. I'm asking for remembering. Those are different jobs."

"Then yes." She turned and faced me fully, and there was something in her face that hadn't been there when she came off the salt-road — some load-bearing piece clicking into place. "I promise, Tam. The four hundred and six. None of them forgotten. Not while I hold a pen."

We shook on it, which is a temple thing, apparently, and felt strange and formal and exactly right.

* * *

I knew trouble had come to town before I saw a single grey cloak, because the doors started gossiping.

Locks feel footsteps differently depending on who's wearing the boots. Tax collectors make locks nervous. Thieves make locks excited. But soldiers — crown soldiers, inquisition soldiers — make locks go quiet, the way birds go quiet when a hawk crosses the yard. All through the Counted Gate district that evening, every lock I passed pulled its wards in tight and pretended to be asleep, and I walked home through a street full of doors playing dead.

"They're here," I told Mara at the squat, low, while Doss argued tariffs with a fence in the back room. "Crown party. Came in through the Ambassador's Gate with diplomatic seals — I heard the gate complaining about forged paperwork, which means the paperwork's real and the diplomacy isn't."

"How many?"

"A dozen riders. And a clerk." I let that land. Caravans understand clerks. "They're asking after a scribe. Quietly, at first. The gate-lock heard the name." I made myself say it. "Quintana. They're calling you the Quintana anomaly."

Mara's hand went to her throat again — the phantom limb, the mother's-voice place — and then, slowly, deliberately, she lowered it. She looked at her ledger, at the living spells she'd been transcribing for six days, at the four hundred and six names I'd started dictating to her that afternoon, and when she raised her eyes they weren't frightened anymore. They were doing arithmetic.

"An anomaly," she repeated. "In their books, I'm an error."

"Errors get corrected."

"Errors get *audited,*" she said, and almost smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "Then we finish the transcriptions tonight, and tomorrow we move. And Tam—" she caught my sleeve as I turned to warn the others, and her grip was ink-stained and steady, "—the promise stands. Whatever they take, whoever they blank, the four hundred and six stay written. Start giving me the names again. All of them."

Outside, past the tannery stink and the fog Yeva had braided along the eaves, the city doors held their breath, and somewhere across the rooftops a dozen riders settled into a diplomatic inn with a writ folded in a clerk's case, patient as a press.

I started reciting names.
