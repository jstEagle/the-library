## 10. The Inquisitor's Bargain

I have read a great many confessions in my career. Most of them lie in the same way: not with their words, but with their margins. A confession written in fear has cramped edges. One written in hope leaves room at the bottom of the page for a future the writer doesn't yet believe in.

Mara Quintana's ledger-work had generous margins.

That was what told me, before I ever saw her face again, that she would come to the meeting. Not because she trusted me—I could see from across the candle-lit table that trust and she had parted ways some miles back, probably around the time my riders burned her caravan—but because a scribe reads documents the way other people breathe, involuntarily and completely, and I had made certain the writ I carried was legible enough to be tempting.

The rememberers' quarter of Meridian smelled of tallow, ink, and old paper left too long in damp rooms. My escort waited outside the tenement where she'd been hiding; I dismissed them at the door. An inquisitor arrives alone or he arrives as an army, and only one of those gets an honest answer.

She opened the door herself, which surprised me. I'd expected the lock-boy, or a barricade, or a crossbow bolt through the gap. Instead: a small woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that catalogued me the way I catalogued her—the calluses on her right hand, the way she stood slightly behind the doorframe as though the door were a shield she hadn't decided to raise.

"Inquisitor Reyes," she said.

"Miss Quintana." I inclined my head. "You know my name."

"The whole valley knew your name by the end. Criers are thorough." She stepped back—not welcome, exactly, but not refusal either. "You're alone. That's either courtesy or a trap."

"It's usually both," I said, "but tonight it's mostly courtesy."

Inside, the room was narrow and cold. A single candle. The boy—Tam, the lockspeaker, the one whose file I'd annotated *low threat, high nuisance*—was nowhere visible, though I noted the way her glance flicked to a wardrobe and I chose, generously, to pretend I hadn't. There were papers on the table. Scribe's papers, covered in that hand I'd spent four months chasing across three provinces: precise, economical, with a strange sigil-like flourish where most people crossed their letters. The Archive's archivists called it *sigil-hand*. They said only one living person wrote that way.

They'd been right. That was the problem.

"You've been reading," I said, nodding at the papers.

"I've been working." She sat down across from me, spine straight, hands folded like a woman settling in for a negotiation she intended to win. "Copying spells before they fade out of the people who carry them. It's honest work. Better than ledgers."

"Is it? In Meridian, honest work for the gifted tends to be indentured work. You've seen the quarter. Rememberers selling their childhoods by the ounce so merchants can bottle the feeling of being loved." I let that sit between us. "The crown calls this mercy, you know. Better to sell memories than burn witches. Progress."

"And you?" she asked. "What do you call it?"

I had prepared for many questions that night. That one wasn't among them, and I felt it land somewhere I preferred not to be landed upon.

"I call it the law," I said. "Which is why I'm here. Because there is a version of the law that doesn't have to end the way these things usually end."

* * *

Here is what I told her, and here is the thing you must understand about me, whoever you are reading this: every word of it was true. That's what makes the profession filthy. Lies can be spotted. Truth arranged in the wrong order is undetectable.

I told her about the hunt. Its true scope—the crown wanted her specifically, the Quintana anomaly, the scribe whose handwriting appeared in documents predating her own birth. I told her that the writ on her head was signed by the Lord Chancellor himself, and that writs of that magnitude did not get rescinded, only redirected. I told her that I had been hunting gifted folk for eleven years, that I had learned precisely how such hunts ended when left to run their course: bonfires, mass renderings, provinces gone quiet in a way no province should be quiet.

And then I offered her the bargain.

"The Archive holds a key," I said. "The covenant-key. The old pact between realms—the one your valley was built to bury. The crown believes that if they hold the key, they can control the boundary instead of merely policing its wreckage. I believe that's madness. But madness with resources outranks sanity without them, so my options are limited." I set the writ on the table, face down, my hand flat atop it. "Bring me the key. Surrender yourself into my custody—not my handlers', mine—and I will enter you into the rolls as a cooperative asset under diplomatic review. No rendering. No blanking. The caravan survivors already flagged in my reports get the same protection. The hunt ends humanely, or as close to humane as this crown permits."

"You'd falsify Crown records," she said.

"I'd interpret them," I corrected. "Interpretation is the inquisitor's entire art. We are, all of us, scribes of a sort—we just write in other people's lives instead of ink." I tapped the writ once. "This is the instrument of your clemency. Read it if you like. I have nothing to hide."

That last sentence was the closest I came to a lie all evening, and even then, God help me, I believed it when I said it. I had read that writ forty times. I knew its clauses the way a priest knows his liturgy. I knew what it promised and I knew what it required, and I had convinced myself—with the particular sleight-of-hand that eleven years of service teaches you—that the second served the first.

She didn't reach for it. Not at first.

"Why me?" she asked instead. "There are hundreds of gifted in this city. Thousands, if you count the indentured. Why does the crown chase one scribe across half a kingdom?"

Because you broke the world, I did not say, because I did not yet know it. Because your handwriting is on the founding documents of the realm and the Chancellor lies awake wondering if the woman who sealed the covenant can unseal it. Because you are not an anomaly, Miss Quintana; you are the author, and we are living in your marginalia.

"Because you're dangerous," I said, which was true, and simpler. "Not the fire-and-brimstone kind. The kind that matters. You rewrite things. Ledgers. Checkpoints. Writs, given time. The crown fears a person who can edit reality's paperwork, and honestly—" I smiled, and it was almost genuine, "—so do I."

Something moved behind her eyes at that. Not fear. Recognition, maybe. Or grief. She looked down at her own hands, those careful scribe's hands, and for a long moment the candle hissed and neither of us spoke.

"My mother's voice," she said finally. "Do you know what happened to it?"

I did. The checkpoint at Marrow Ford—a trap I had designed myself, though I'd been a province away when it sprang. The cost extracted by the crossing-spell. I had read the incident report with a strange tightness in my chest I'd chosen not to examine.

"Yes," I said.

"She paid it to save people my writ would destroy." Her voice stayed level. Scribes' voices always stay level; it's the hands that tell the truth, and hers had gone very still on the table, still the way water goes still before it freezes. "And now you're offering to protect them. If I give you the key."

"If you give me the key, I can protect them. Protection requires standing, Miss Quintana. Standing requires the crown believing I've delivered. I cannot shield your friends with good intentions. Good intentions burn faster than kindling."

"And if I refuse?"

I gave her the honest answer, because she'd earned it. "Then the raid comes. Tomorrow, likely. The Chancellor's patience is a fixed quantity and I have spent nearly all of it getting us this meeting instead of a squad. If you refuse, I will do my duty, and I am very good at my duty, and you will lose everything except the parts that run."

Tam's wardrobe creaked, very softly. She didn't look at it. I didn't either. Professional courtesy.

"And you," she said quietly. "What do you lose?"

"No one asks the hound that question."

"I'm asking."

I looked at her then—really looked, past the file, past the anomaly, past the sigil-hand that haunted three centuries of archives—and I saw something I have tried since to unsee: she looked at me the way I imagine a man looks at a locked door he suspects he built himself and forgot. Like I was a question she'd answered once, long ago, and lost the answer to.

"What I lose is irrelevant," I said, and stood, and turned the writ over, and pushed it across the table toward her. "Read it. All of it. Every clause, every subclause, the endorsements at the back. I mean that sincerely—read all of it. Then decide."

* * *

I want to record, in whatever ledger this account eventually becomes, that I watched her read it. That was my mistake, or my penance—I've never settled which. I stood by the window with my back half-turned like a courteous man giving a lady privacy, and I listened to pages turn, and I let myself feel the thing I had been refusing to feel for eleven years, which was hope.

Clause by clause. I heard them turn.

The writ was a handsome document. Vellum, triple-sealed, the Chancellor's cipher in gold leaf. Its opening declared the purpose in language any reasonable person could love: *the peaceful resolution of the Quintana matter, the preservation of life where practicable, the ending of the general persecution through targeted resolution.* Clemency. Cooperation. Diplomatic review. Everything I had promised her, in the Chancellor's own gilded hand.

Then the fine print. There is always fine print. In eleven years I have never once encountered a Crown document whose meaning lived in its headlines.

Subclause fourteen, tucked beneath the endorsement seals where even diligent readers grow careless: *cooperative assets shall be subject to standard remediation protocols.* Remediation protocols. The Crown's term for blanking. And subclause fifteen, worse, the one I had read forty times and somehow never truly read: *upon conclusion of the Quintana matter, all persons designated gifted within Crown territories shall be processed per Schedule B.*

Schedule B was an appendix. Eleven pages. A list. Names, capacities, locations—including, I realized with a slow horror that began in my stomach and worked outward, names I had entered myself. Doss the ferryman. Yeva the weather-singer. Children. The lock-boy, Tam, filed under *minor, remediable.*

All gifted. All rendered blank. Not as punishment—as *housekeeping.* The end of the hunt, achieved by ending everyone the hunt existed to catch. Peace, of a kind. A kingdom gone quiet in a way no kingdom should be quiet.

I had carried that document into her room and called it mercy.

Behind me, the page-turning stopped.

"Inquisitor." Her voice was still level. Scribes. God, the discipline of them. "What is Schedule B?"

I did not answer quickly enough, and my silence answered for me.

I turned. She was holding the writ at arm's length, the way one holds a dead animal, and she had reached the appendix, and her eyes were moving down the list of names, and I watched her find Doss's name, and Yeva's, and then—smaller, further down, in a section marked *pending acquisition*—the boy's.

"You said read all of it," she whispered.

"Mara—"

"*You said read all of it.*"

There are perhaps three times in a man's life when he hears his own words come back to him wearing their true face, and this was mine. I had handed her the truth myself. Arranged truth in the wrong order is undetectable—but arrange it rightly, every clause in sequence, and it becomes a blade. She had done with my writ what I had refused to do for eleven years: she had read it honestly.

"Listen to me," I said, and crossed the room, and she stood and backed away with the writ clutched to her chest like armor or evidence, and I stopped, because advancing on her would confirm everything, and everything was already confirmed. "Fourteen days ago I would have defended that document clause by clause. Seven days ago I would have defended it badly. Today—" I spread my empty hands. "Today I am standing in a rented room in the rememberers' quarter telling a fugitive that the writ I brought her as salvation is a slaughter-list, and I don't fully know when I became a man who does that. But I am one. I think maybe I always was, and the uniform kept me from checking."

"You expect me to believe that."

"No," I said. "I expect you to be smarter than belief. Belief is for people with time. You have until dawn, when the raid comes whether you sign anything or not." I pulled the secondary seal from my coat—the diplomatic pass, my own authority, the one piece of paper in the kingdom with my name above the Chancellor's—and laid it on the table between us. "Schedule B executes at week's end. The raid tomorrow is mine, and I can shape what my raid finds. Empty rooms. Scattered birds. Nothing else. Take the boy, take your people, take the pass, and be gone before first bell."

"And you'll report—what?"

"That the Quintana anomaly evaded capture through means requiring further investigation." I permitted myself the ghost of a smile. "Investigation is wonderfully elastic. It can stretch for years."

She stared at me. The candle guttered, and in the flicker her face went young and old at once, a woman deciding the fate of more people than she knew.

"Why?" she asked. It was the same question as before, but it had changed shape. Before it had been a scribe's question—why me, what's the mechanism. This one was a person's question. Why are you doing this.

And because I am, in the end, a coward of long standing, I gave her the answer I could survive rather than the one that was true.

"Because someone has to end the hunt," I said, "and it clearly isn't going to be the crown."

She looked at the diplomatic pass for a long moment. Then at me. Then—this is the part I keep returning to, the splinter in the mind—her expression did something I couldn't read at all. Not gratitude. Not suspicion. Something older and stranger, as if she were recognizing not my offer but some previous version of this exact conversation, held in another century, in another voice.

"I've read this before," she said softly. Almost to herself. "Not this writ. This *moment.* Someone offering mercy with a knife in the other hand." Her thumb moved over the appendix, over the names. "Last time, I took the deal. I think—God, I think last time, taking the deal is how we got here."

"Miss Quintana—"

"Get out," she said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse than fury. "Before I change my mind about letting you leave. And Inquisitor—if your raid comes at dawn, you'd better pray it finds nothing. Because if it finds my people, I won't rewrite a checkpoint next time." She lifted the writ. "I'll rewrite *you.* And I'm told I'm very good."

I went. Of course I went. What else does the hound do, when the door closes?

Outside, the night over Meridian was wrong—stars bleeding through the daylight hours, the boundary fraying visibly now, wisps of the other realm hanging over the city like torn curtains. The end of things, coming on schedule. I stood in the street and realized two things with perfect clarity.

First: I could not go back. Whatever I had been when I walked up those stairs, the man walking down them had read Schedule B aloud in his own conscience, and the Chancellor did not forgive readers.

Second: the raid was coming at dawn regardless. My handlers had never trusted me with the timing alone—and while I'd been offering clemency by candlelight, the strike squads had already been moving into position around the rememberers' quarter.

I had come dressed as mercy. Mercy, it turned out, was the distraction.

I started running.
