## 11. Blank Pages

The bell in the Rememberers' Quarter rang wrong.

I'd been in Meridian long enough to learn its bells — the mint's chime at dawn, the Archive's low thrum at noon, the small cracked thing above Tam's tenement that always sounded like it was apologizing. This one wasn't any of them. It was a single flat note, struck once and left to die, and every gifted soul in the quarter stopped mid-stride the way a field stops when a hawk's shadow crosses it.

I was in the counting-house loft with Doss, transcribing his road-songs before they faded from him entirely. He'd been losing them for weeks — not to the blankers, just to time and distance, the way a ferryman loses rivers he no longer runs. His voice had gone rough around each verse, like stones dragged through shallows.

"That one," he said, tapping the page where I'd written *the Vellwater crossing song* in my careful scribe's hand. "Sing it back. If you get it wrong, the river gets it wrong, and then we're all swimming."

"I don't sing."

"Everyone sings. Some of you just do it badly on purpose." He leaned over my shoulder, and I caught the smell of tar and old rope that clung to him like a second coat. "Huh. Your hand's changed."

"It hasn't changed. It's just — settling." That was the only word I had for it. Since the checkpoint ledger, since the sound of my mother's voice had poured out of me onto paper and become a courier's license, my handwriting had been doing things handwriting shouldn't. Curling. Listening. Sometimes, late at night, I'd catch a page breathing under my palm, faint as a sleeping animal.

The flat bell died. In the silence after it, Doss went very still.

"That's the ward-bell," he said. "That's the *old* ward-bell. They haven't rung that since—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. We both heard what filled the gap: boots. Not many yet. But organized, which is worse. Chaos you can run from. Organization comes for you.

"Doss—"

"Ledger," he said sharply. "Where is it?"

"The checkpoint ledger? I burned it—"

"Not that one. The *quarter* ledger. Every rememberer registered in this district, every indenture contract, every name." He was already moving, hauling open the floor hatch, and I understood with a cold drop in my stomach why he wanted it. Because if the riders came with writs, the writs would have names. And names written by my hand could be *rewritten* by my hand.

"They'll know it's forged," I said, even as I dug it out of my satchel. "They'll check against the originals—"

"By then you're three districts gone." He took the book from me and pressed it back into my hands, closing my fingers over it hard enough to hurt. "Listen to me, girl. You're the only one here who can make paper lie convincingly. That makes you the most dangerous person in this room, and I've met weather-witches."

"Flatterer."

"Yeva could drown a cavalry troop. You could erase the reason they ever rode out." He almost smiled. Then the smile went, because the boots had reached the mouth of the street below, and there were more of them now, and someone was calling orders in the clipped cadence of the crown's inquisitorial guard.

Doss looked at me for a long moment. Ferryman's eyes, gray as river fog, taking my measure one last time.

"Back stairs," he said. "Down past the dye-works. Don't stop for anyone. Don't be clever. Be gone."

"You're coming with me."

"No." He said it simply, the way he said everything about rivers. "Someone has to stand at the landing and look like the cargo matters, or they'll search every loft on this street. I'm old, Mara. My memories are mostly rope burns and bad whiskey. There's nothing down in me worth their ink." He turned me toward the stairs with two big hands. "But there's something up in you worth the whole world, and I'll be damned if I let them find it while I'm standing here being interesting."

"Doss—"

"*Go.*"

I went. I want to tell you I argued longer, fought harder, did something other than obey the first adult who'd ever put himself between me and danger. But the boots were on the stairs behind us, and obedience was faster than love, and love was going to have to wait its turn.

I took the back stairs two at a time, the quarter-ledger clutched to my chest, and burst out into the dye-works alley reeking of mordant and indigo. Above the rooftops I could see smoke — not burning smoke, but the pale, deliberate smudge of the crown's censer-wagons, the ones they rolled ahead of raids to fog the air with consecrated ash so no gift could spark. Somewhere a woman screamed, and then, horribly, she didn't. The silence where her scream should have continued was worse than the scream.

And I ran.

* * *

I found Tam at the well, of all places, hauling water like it was an ordinary morning, because for him — for twenty minutes ago — it had been.

"Mara!" His face did the thing it always did, that whole-hearted lit-up thing that made him look twelve even though he swore he was fourteen. Then he read my face and the light went out. "They're here."

"They're here."

"My sisters—" He dropped the buckets. Water spread across the cobbles between us, and I watched it move, watched it hesitate at a crack in the stone and pool there like it was thinking, and I realized distantly that even the water in this city knew something was wrong.

"Which house?"

"Candlemaker's Row. But Mara, the guards are at the end of the street already, I saw the ash-wagons, I can't—"

"Then we go the way locks can't follow." I grabbed his wrist. "Tam. Look at me. Can you get us into the underways from here?"

His eyes went wide, then narrow, then focused in a way I'd never seen on him — the lock-speaker looking at the whole city as one vast mechanism. "The tannery drain. Old grate, older lock. Nobody's opened it since the plague years."

"Can you?"

"I can try."

He could do better than try. I stood watch at the alley mouth while he knelt at the grate, laid his palm flat on the iron, and murmured to it — not words, exactly, or not words I understood. A coaxing. A *convincing*. The lock considered its long retirement, sighed through some internal rusted throat, and gave.

Inside smelled like every bad century Meridian had ever had. We went down into the dark with the screaming muffled above us, and Tam held my hand, and I held the ledger, and somewhere in the wet dark between heartbeats I thought: *this is what it costs to be the dangerous one. Everyone you love stands at the landing so you can escape.*

* * *

We surfaced near the tannery ponds, half a district east and a world away from Candlemaker's Row, where — we learned by dusk, from a ragged boy who'd hidden in a rain barrel — the raid had done its work. Forty rememberers taken. Six resisted. One of the six was Doss.

They didn't hang him. Hanging would have been mercy, and the inquisitor had come to Meridian under diplomatic cover, which meant he needed demonstrations, not corpses.

They did it in the mint square at noon, publicly, with the censer-wagons drawn in a ring and the city's licensed rememberers marched in to watch. Tam got us close — closer than was safe, closer than was sane — because I couldn't not see it, and because some part of me, the part that kept ledgers, insisted that a debt witnessed is a debt recorded.

They sat Doss in a chair in the middle of the square, chained at wrist and ankle, and he looked — gods, he looked *bored*. He found our faces in the crowd, mine and Tam's, and he didn't plead. He tipped his head slightly, the way he did when a customer haggled too long at the ferry-ramp, and I understood: *don't be clever. Be gone.*

The blanker was a machine I hadn't seen before — brass and glass and a core of something that glowed the color of a dead star. They fixed a circlet to his head, and the inquisitor's clerk read the writ aloud: *for the crime of unlicensed conveyance of the gifted, sentence of cognitive excision, full measure.*

Full measure. Not a fine. Not a levy. Everything.

Doss sang, at the end. One line of the Vellwater crossing song, rough as gravel, defiant as sunrise — *"and the far bank keeps what the near bank loses"* — before the machine drank the rest.

What was left when they unbuckled the circlet wasn't Doss. It wore Doss's body, blinked with Doss's eyes, but when the guards asked him his name, he looked at them with polite, bottomless emptiness and said, "Should I know it?" And the crowd — the crowd *cheered*, because most of them weren't gifted, and to them it looked like justice, clean and bright as minted coin.

I broke, quietly, in a doorway, with Tam's arm around my shoulders and my own hand jammed against my mouth so the sound stayed inside. Because I had sung that song back to him wrong. I'd gotten one verse crooked, and he'd laughed and said the river forgives, and now the river had lost him, and the far bank kept what the near bank loses, and the near bank was *me*, and I had lost him.

Rage is a strange thing. It doesn't feel like fire, not really. It feels like a page that refuses ink. Like a hand that won't hold the pen the way it's told. I stood in that doorway with tears drying salt on my face and felt something settle in me that I suspect will never unsettle again: not grief. Grief asks *why*. This asked *what are you going to do about it.*

* * *

That night, in the cellar where Tam hid me beneath a floor of loose slate, I finally opened Sefa's letter.

I'd carried it since the valley. I'd carried it through the border of ash and the caravan roads and the gates of Meridian, and I had not read past the first paragraph, because the first paragraph was Sefa's voice saying goodbye and I am, at bottom, a coward about certain kinds of pain. But Doss had paid full measure today so that a girl he called *cargo worth the whole world* could live to be dangerous, and cowards don't deserve the freight we carry.

Sefa wrote:

*Mara. If you are reading this, then I am dust and the valley is breached, and I am sorry for both, but mostly for the lying. So here is the truth, all of it, plain as I can set it down.*

*You were taught the valley keeps magic out. That is the story we tell children and tax collectors. The truth is that Vell Hollow does not keep magic out. It keeps magic* in *— sealed, held, dammed at the border like a river behind a weir. The valley isn't a refuge from the gift. It is a cork in a wound.*

*Two realms share one wellspring. The one you were born into and the one the sigil-born come from — they are not neighbors. They are one body, folded, the way a page folds. What flows in one flows in both. Long ago there was a covenant, a living seal, a person bound willingly to hold the fold open so power could pass without drowning either side. The covenant broke. Not by accident. By choice. By a scribe who saw what the crown's founder meant to do with that power and chose to starve him rather than feed him — knowing it would starve everyone, slowly, for generations.*

*I don't know if that scribe was right. I know she was desperate, and young, and alone, and that she erased herself so thoroughly that not even the crown's hunters could find what she'd been. But erasure leaves a seam, child. And seams show.*

*You were carried through the border as a babe already marked. The valley didn't hide you FROM the magic. It hid the magic IN you — pressed flat, folded small, the way you fold a letter so it fits in a pocket. Every year you grew, the seal in you grew with you. And every year I held the valley's borders closed with my own small craft, buying time, hoping you'd never need to know.*

*The hunters have breached the border. Which means the time I bought is spent. There is a place called the Wellspire, where the wellspring touches the world. Only a willing seal can hold the covenant open — willing, do you hear me, that word is load-bearing. A forced seal becomes a leash. A bought seal becomes a chain. It must be given, freely, by someone who remembers why it matters.*

*I know what I am asking. I have watched you copy ledgers for eleven years, Mara Quintana, and I know your heart better than you think: you count everything, you spend nothing, you hoard yourself like a miser hoards coin. Do not hoard yourself. You were never meant to be a closed book. You were meant to be the binding.*

*Go to the Wellspire. And when you get there, remember: the price is not your life. The price is your memory. The wellspring feeds on what we treasure. That is the whole horror and the whole mercy of it — it takes only what is truly given.*

*Burn this letter. Or don't. You were always going to keep it. You keep everything.*

*— Sefa, who was proud of you before you ever did anything, and prouder after*

I read it twice. Then I sat in the dark of Tam's cellar with the paper trembling in my hands, and I understood, at last, the shape of the cage I'd been living in — and the shape of the key.

Two realms. One wellspring. A broken covenant starving both worlds by inches — and the crown's answer was to blank the gifted, to drain the wellspring's vessels dry, to mint human memory into coin until nothing living remained to hold anything open. Ivo Reyes wasn't hunting criminals. He was harvesting the last seal-material in existence, one mind at a time, starting with ferrymen who sang on the way to the chair.

And the only thing that could stop it was me. Not my gift, exactly. Not my blood. My *willingness* — that word, load-bearing, the one word in Sefa's letter I already dreaded.

"Mara?" Tam's whisper came down through the slate. "You've been quiet a long time. Quiet like planning. I know quiet like planning."

I looked at Sefa's letter, and at the ledger on my knees, and at my own hand — my scribe's hand, the one that could make paper lie, the one that had rewritten a checkpoint ledger and cost me my mother's voice, the one that had copied Doss's songs crooked and would never get the chance to copy them right.

"Tam," I said, and my voice came out level, which surprised me, because underneath the level there was a furnace. "How much do you know about the Wellspire?"

Above us, faint through the slate and the earth, the city bells began to ring curfew — and beneath the curfew bells, barely, another sound. Boots. Organized. Coming down the tannery lane.

Not for the quarter this time.

For us.
