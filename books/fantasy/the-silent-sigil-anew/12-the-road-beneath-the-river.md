## 12. The Road Beneath the River

**Mara**

The drowned underways of Meridian smelled like every bad decision I'd ever made, steeped together in standing water.

"Keep your hand on my shoulder," Tam whispered. "And don't step where I don't step."

"I'm a scribe, not a foal," I whispered back. "I can walk."

"You can walk *upstairs*. Down here the floor has opinions." He said it cheerfully, which I was learning was Tam's way of being terrified. The louder the joke, the worse the fear underneath. By that arithmetic, the boy who'd joked about the hounds at the rememberers' quarter — *good news, they're only the size of ponies* — had been one breath from screaming.

Behind us, above us, through forty feet of brick and silt and drowned history, the city was tearing itself apart looking for us. Ivo Reyes's hounds didn't bark. That was the worst part. They made a sound like a page being torn, slowly, over and over, and every time I heard it I felt it somewhere behind my sternum, as if the sound knew what I was and what I'd spent and was taking notes.

We moved by Tam's counting. He had the underways mapped in his head the way I had ledgers mapped in mine — not written, *known*, in that stubborn place where knowledge becomes part of the body. Left at the third arch. Wader's Row. The old sluice where the river had been invited in and then never asked to leave.

"Here." He stopped at a door that was less a door than a suggestion — a slab of green oak swollen in its frame, locked with a padlock the size of my fist, furred with rust.

"Tell me you have a key."

"I *am* the key." He put both hands flat on the lock and closed his eyes, and I felt it — not saw, *felt* — the mechanism inside shift and sigh and unclench, like an old man deciding, finally, to forgive somebody. The shackle popped open with a click that echoed too far.

"How long have you been able to do that?" I asked. It was the wrong question and I knew it. There was no *how long* for any of us. There was only *before the hunt* and *after*.

"Since I was six," he said, pocketing the lock like it was a pet. "First thing I ever opened was my mother's strongbox. She cried. I thought she was proud." He shrugged, and the shrug carried twenty years of the wrong kind of crying in it, though he was maybe fourteen. "It's not locked anymore. Doors, I mean. Nothing's ever really locked to me. Sometimes I wish it were."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I gave him the only honest currency I had left. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me when we're not fish food."

* * *

**Tam**

Here's what nobody tells you about being the useful one: useful gets used.

Doss used to say I was the most valuable thing on four legs in the whole caravan, and he'd say it loud, and I'd go to sleep warm with it. Then the riders came and Doss went blank-eyed in a cage and I was running through a flooded cellar with a scribe I'd known for three days, and *valuable* turned out to mean *carriable*. You carry a valuable thing. You don't ask it where it wants to go.

Except Mara kept asking. That was the strange part. Every fork in the tunnel, every locked grate, every moment where a smarter fugitive would've just shoved me forward and said *go*, she'd stop and say, "Which way do you think?" like my thinking was worth the asking.

She was a mess, too. That helped. People who are put-together make you feel like luggage. Mara had ink on her fingers that never fully came off — I think it was under the skin by now — and her braid was coming apart, and she had a way of talking to herself while she worked, muttering numbers, that made her seem less like a legendary covenant-seal and more like a woman doing arithmetic under a very bad roof.

Because that was the other thing. The Archive had called her the living seal. Sefa's letter called her the only door. Ivo's writ, the bit I'd read over her shoulder before we burned our way out, called her *the Quintana anomaly, priority retrieval, condition: intact*.

I called her Mara. Somebody had to.

"Tam." Her voice came low through the dark. "The water's moving wrong."

I looked. She was right — the black water ahead of us was sliding *toward* us, against the slope, smooth as poured glass. My stomach did the thing it did when locks started talking to me in my sleep.

"That's not current," she said. "That's attention."

"Okay," I said, "new plan, we go back—"

"There is no back. You said the sluice is the only route past the cistern."

I had said that. I had also said, an hour ago, that the hounds were only the size of ponies, so my credibility was not at an all-time high.

The water rose. Not fast — that was the horrible elegance of it, the way it rose *politely*, like a tide that had been asked nicely. And in the surface of it, reflections that weren't ours. Stars. A whole sky of them, wheeling slowly under the city, under the river, in water that had no business holding a sky.

"The boundary," Mara breathed. "It's fraying. Even down here."

The water touched my boots and I heard — felt — a lock turning somewhere enormous. The oldest lock I'd ever felt. And it wasn't locked *against* us.

It was locked against *everything*, and it was failing, and through the gap the other realm was leaning in to look at us.

"Mara," I said. "I think it knows you."

* * *

**Mara**

I want it on the record that I did not want to be known by an underground sky.

The star-water lapped at our knees and the reflections wheeled, and every star in that false firmament seemed to tilt, minutely, toward me — the way flowers lean at a window, the way dogs turn their heads when their name is said in another room. I felt it like a hand on the back of my neck. Not cruel. Curious. Worse, in a way. I'd been *hunted* for weeks; I'd learned the shape of cruelty. Curiosity was new, and new things in my experience were always expensive.

"It's the wellspring," I said, mostly to myself. "The letter said the realms share it. If the boundary's failing—"

"Then the other side is leaking through," Tam said. "Into the drains. That's so much worse than rats."

And then the hounds found us.

I'll give Ivo Reyes this: his beasts are quiet right up until they aren't. The tearing-page sound came down the tunnel behind us, three of them, four, their eyes catching the starlight and giving back nothing. Tam grabbed my wrist and we ran, splashing, the water fighting us now — or the *attention* in the water was, dragging at our ankles like a child who doesn't want a guest to leave.

There was a gate at the end of the sluice. Iron, ancient, chained, padlocked with something that had been put there by people who understood locks and feared them.

"Tam—"

"I see it." He had his hands on the iron and his eyes squeezed shut and I could see him *straining*, the way you strain to hear a whisper in a loud room, and the lock wasn't answering. "It's old. It's *scared*. It's been locked so long it doesn't remember being a door—"

The tearing sound was close now. Close enough that I could smell the hounds, wet iron and blank paper.

So I did the thing I do.

I didn't have a ledger. I had Tam's arm, and my own memory, and the desperate arithmetic of a woman who has already paid a mother's voice and a friend's face and is beginning to understand she is a purse that people — and gates, and realms — keep reaching into. I put my free hand flat on the padlock and I *wrote*. Not on it. Into it. I gave it a line of entry, the way you'd open a ledger's first page: *Gate, iron, drowned, forgotten — opened this hour by two travelers who mean no harm and can pay nothing.*

The lock thought about it.

The lock, I swear on every debt I've ever copied, *read*.

And it opened.

We went through and Tam hauled it shut behind us and the hounds hit the far side with a sound like a book slammed in a library, and then silence, except the water, and our breathing, and somewhere above us, impossibly, birdsong.

"Daylight," Tam said. "There's daylight. Mara. There's *daylight*."

* * *

**Tam**

We came up out of a storm grate in a hillside pasture, and I have never in my life been so happy to see grass, which is a sentence I did not expect to think, ever, being a city boy born and bred.

Mara came out behind me and just — stopped. Stood there in the wet grass with her ruined braid and her ink-stained hands and looked at the sky.

I looked too. That's when I saw what she was seeing.

It was mid-afternoon. The sun was up. And the stars were up with it — not faint, not shy, *there*, scattered across the blue like someone had spilled a second sky and hadn't cleaned it up. And between the stars, in patches, you could see through to something else. Not darkness. A *greenness*. A vast slow turning green, like looking through a rip in cloth at a garden the size of everything.

"It's beautiful," I said, because it was, and because I'm an idiot who says things.

"It's the end," Mara said. "Or the beginning. With boundaries it's always one or the other, and nobody tells you which until it's too late."

We walked. Not because we had a destination — Sefa's letter said the Wellspire, and the Wellspire was wherever it was, west, maybe, or north — but because standing still felt like drowning and walking felt like choosing. The pasture gave way to gorse and a broken stone wall, and we followed the wall because walls go somewhere, eventually, even broken ones. Especially broken ones.

She stumbled at dusk. I caught her elbow and she laughed — a cracked, exhausted laugh — and said, "I've been awake for two days and I've spent more of myself than I can count. I'm going to sit down now, and if the crown finds me, they find me sitting."

So we sat, in the lee of the wall, and shared the last of the bread I'd stolen from the rememberers' kitchen, which was stale and tasted like freedom anyway.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

"You can ask. I reserve the right to bill you."

"Your memory. The one you paid at the checkpoint. Your mother's voice. Do you miss it? If you don't remember it, can you miss it?"

She was quiet a long time. A star moved overhead, deliberate as a ship.

"Yes," she said finally. "It's like — you know when a room's been rearranged? You can't say what's gone but you keep turning to look at where it isn't. I turn, and there's nothing there, and the turning *hurts*, and the hurt is the only proof I have that there was ever anything to turn toward." She looked at me. "Why do you ask, Tam?"

"Because when we get to the Wellspire," I said, "and they tell you the price — and they will, everything costs, that's the one law that's never broken — I don't want you to think you'd be paying into nothing. You'd be paying into a hurt. And a hurt means it was real."

She stared at me. Then she did something I didn't expect, which was reach over and put her ink-stained hand on top of my grubby one, right there on the cold stone.

"When did you get wise?" she said. "You're fourteen."

"Thirteen and a half. And I'm not wise, I'm just small enough to see under things."

She laughed again, and this time it didn't crack, and I thought: *I would follow this woman into the Wellspire itself. I would follow her into the well.*

I didn't know yet that I'd have to.

* * *

**Mara**

We slept in shifts, which is to say Tam slept and I watched the fraying sky and did the thing I always do when I can't stop the world: I counted.

Stars visible at midday: too many. Patches of the other realm: seven, by my last count, and growing. Hours since the hounds: nine. Hours since I'd last heard Doss's voice before they took it: I couldn't count that one. Some numbers you keep in a different ledger, the kind with no bottom line.

Tam woke near dawn — the real dawn, the sun doing its honest work, though the stars were still loitering — and found me writing in the mud with a stick.

"What are you doing?"

"Practicing," I said. "Braid-work. Yeva showed me before the raid — memory into spellwork, small stitches instead of whole years. If I'm going to pay at the Wellspire, I want to pay in coins, not in limbs."

He crouched and read my mud-scribbles upside down, which is a very Tam thing to do. "That one's a name. That's mine."

"It's a stitch," I said, embarrassed. "A small one. The memory of you teaching me the underways. If I ever need one small spell, I want it to be that one."

He was quiet. Then: "You know what's funny? Everyone in the caravan paid things too. Doss paid his whole name once, to get us across a border. Yeva paid the memory of her wedding. And we all just — kept going. Like it was rent." He picked up a pebble and threw it at nothing. "I used to think that was sad. Now I think it's the bravest thing I ever saw. Paying and *staying*."

The sun cleared the hills. And in its light I saw what we'd been walking beside all night: the broken wall wasn't a wall. It was a road — an old one, sunk to its shoulders in the earth, paved with stones each carved with a single mark.

A sigil. The same sigil, over and over, marching west.

Tam stood up slowly. "Mara. Is that—"

"The seal-road," I said, and my voice came out strange, half mine and half the voice of a woman I used to be and couldn't remember being. "It leads to the Wellspire. It's been here the whole time. It's been *waiting*."

The sigil-stones caught the sunrise, and every one of them, as far as I could see, began — softly, like breath on a window — to glow.

"Well," said Tam, shouldering our nothing-bag. "I guess we're expected."

We started walking, side by side, down a road that remembered me better than I remembered myself. And behind us, far off across the hills, thin and clear and tearing like a page, something began to howl.

Ivo had found the road too.

But for the first time since the valley — since the page that rewrote itself, since Sefa's salt-road, since everything — I wasn't running from the sound.

I was walking toward what came after it. And I wasn't walking alone.
