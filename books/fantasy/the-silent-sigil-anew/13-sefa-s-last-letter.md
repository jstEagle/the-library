## 13. Sefa's Last Letter

We made camp that night in the shell of a waystation that had been a waystation in some other century and was now mostly an argument between stone and ivy. Tam built a fire the size of his ambitions — small, stubborn, likely to go out if nobody believed in it — and I sat with Sefa's letter on my knees for a long time before I opened it.

I'd been carrying it since the salt-road. Since she pressed it into my hands at the border and said, *not yet.* Since then it had ridden through checkpoints and raids and one very undignified swim beneath Meridian, wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs like a second heartbeat. There is a particular kind of cowardice in carrying a letter you're afraid to read. I had become fluent in it.

"What is it?" Tam asked, feeding a twig to the fire.

"A letter."

"You've mentioned. Several times. Usually followed by not reading it." He didn't say it unkindly. That was Tam's whole trick, I'd learned: he could point a knife at your worst habits and make it feel like a compliment.

"It's from a dead woman," I said. "There's no hurry. She'll wait."

"That's exactly the sort of thing dead women hate," Tam said, "being kept waiting," and then had the grace to look embarrassed about the joke, which is more than most living men manage.

I broke the seal.

The wax had been stamped with nothing — just the flat press of her thumb, because Sefa had never owned a sigil and had considered owning one a form of vanity. The paper inside smelled of her still: woodsmoke and bitterroot and the particular dry sweetness of herbs kept too long in a warm cupboard. For a moment that smell undid me more thoroughly than anything the crown had managed. Inquisitors could burn a quarter of a city. A cupboard-smell could level me.

Her handwriting started neat and got less so, the way her patience always did.

---

*Mara.*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead, or you have finally developed the sense I spent twenty years trying to beat into you and opened this before it was too late. Given the odds as I know my girl, I will assume I am dead. Don't waste good grief on me. Grief is heavy and you'll need both hands free.*

*I am going to tell you what Vell Hollow is, and I am going to tell it plainly, because you were raised by scribes and plainness is the only language you trust.*

*You think the valley keeps magic out. Everyone thinks that. It's the story we let the Temple tell because it was easier than the truth and cheaper than the fighting. But valleys don't have opinions, child. Borders don't care about people generally. Somebody builds a wall around something specific, or there's no wall worth building.*

*Vell Hollow was built around one thing. Around you.*

*You were two years old when they carried you through. I know because I helped carry you, though you won't remember me from those days and I'm glad of it; I looked worse then than I do now, which is saying something. Your mother came to us out of the east with a baby who glowed in the dark. That's not poetry. I mean it the way I mean rain is wet. You lit up rooms. Sigils woke when you cried. The old covenant was already fraying by then — the crown's founder had begun cutting pieces out of the wellspring to feed his ambition, and everything loose in the world was looking for somewhere to land, and half of it wanted to land in you.*

*A child like that has maybe three futures. Weapon. Fuel. Or grave. Your mother chose a fourth one, which was no future at all, and she paid the toll for it, and I have never told anyone what that toll was because it wasn't mine to spend. Ask me nothing about her voice. I can't give you back what I never had.*

Hmm. Wait. Let me read that again.

*Ask me nothing about her voice. I gave what I could of it to the road, and the rest went into the ground with her, and even a hedge-witch doesn't dig twice.*

My hands had gone cold. I read it again anyway, a third time, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something survivable. They did not. Somewhere past the checkpoint at Grey Ford, paying for the caravan's passage, I had lost the sound of my mother's voice — traded it away without ever having heard it, apparently, because Sefa had already buried it years before I knew it existed. The ledger of my losses had a column I hadn't known I was keeping.

Tam watched me across the fire and, to his enormous credit, said nothing. He just nudged the bag of dried plums toward me, because Tam's love language was dried fruit and proximity.

I read on.

*The Temple of Quiet Hours isn't a temple, whatever the prior's robes say. It's a keystone. The debt-ledgers, the copying, the endless ink — all of it is ritual, though nobody left alive remembers it as such. Every page copied in quiet hours presses the valley's silence down another layer. Your family's debt was real debt, mind, but the reason they put a scribe's daughter on ledger duty and kept her there was never arithmetic. Ink is a leash when you write it right. We kept you writing silence over yourself for nineteen years.*

*It worked. Mostly. Magic is patient and children grow, and lately the seams have been showing — the page that rewrote itself, if that found its way to you, was your own hand coming home. You wrote that page once, long ago, or a version of you did, the version the valley was built to bury. When it surfaced, I knew we had perhaps a season.*

*Then the crown's hunters crossed where they should not have been able to cross, and I understood that the founder's heirs had stopped hunting gifted folk generally and started hunting you specifically. The Quintana anomaly. As if you were a misfiled invoice.*

*So here is the truth, and then I will let you get on with the business of being furious at me, which you are entitled to, and at the world, which is also entitled, though the world rarely notices.*

*Two realms drink from one wellspring. The covenant is the cup that keeps the sharing gentle. Your older self — and yes, I know how that sounds, and yes, it's true, and no, I don't understand all of it either, I am a hedge-witch, not a philosopher — broke that covenant once to starve a tyrant, and the breaking is why magic is dying on one side of the border and running wild and cruel on the other. Only a willing seal can hold it open again. Only you. Not because you're special in the way crowns mean special. Because you're the only one who remembers why it matters, even the parts of you that forgot.*

*Which brings me to the last thing.*

*When I smuggled you out over the salt-road, I stayed behind. Someone had to hold the gate shut behind you, and the gate only opens outward now, and holding it costs everything a body has. I want you to understand that I did not die of old age in my bed surrounded by cats, which was the retirement plan, and I am genuinely annoyed about it. I died standing up, doing the one useful thing I was ever any good at, which was being stubborn in a doorway.*

*I held the border closed so they couldn't follow you. It took everything. I would pay it again. If you feel you owe me — and you don't, but you're your mother's daughter and you will feel it anyway — then here is the payment: stop running. Running is what the valley taught you and the crown wants from you and fear insists upon. Go to the Wellspire. Walk in the front door. And when they ask what you've brought to seal a covenant with, tell them a hedge-witch sent you, and that she paid full price, and that she expects change.*

*Burn this letter after. Or don't. You were always terrible at following instructions.*

*— Sefa*

*P.S. The plums in your pack are Tam's. He pretends otherwise. Share them anyway. That's how families start.*

---

I read it twice more. Then a third time, slower, the way I used to copy ledgers, letting each line settle into me like sediment.

The fire had burned down to a red argument among coals. Tam had lain back against his pack with his hands laced behind his head, eyes closed in the elaborate fiction of sleep that boys perform when they're giving you privacy without leaving you alone. Above the broken roof, stars bled through daylight hours ago and now simply hung there, wrong and beautiful, the boundary fraying visibly — Sefa's death buying us time, and time running out anyway.

She had died in a doorway. I turned the phrase over and over. Twenty years of knowing her — the sharp tongue, the bitterroot tea, the way she'd smack your knuckles with a spoon for slouching over your copies and then secretly leave honey-cakes where you'd find them — and I had never once wondered why a hedge-witch lived alone in a valley that hated what she was. I'd thought she was hiding. She hadn't been hiding. She'd been *posted*. Standing guard over a child who thought her own life was a debt she owed to a temple.

All those years of copying ledgers. All those pages of silence, written in my own hand, laid over myself like snow. And underneath, the whole time, a girl who glowed in the dark.

"I'm not angry at her," I heard myself say.

Tam opened one eye. "Wasn't sure you were talking to me."

"I keep waiting to be. It seems like the correct response. She lied to me for twenty years. She let me think I was nothing special, chained to a debt—" I stopped. Started again. "But she also fed me and taught me letters and stood in a doorway until it killed her. Both things are true and I don't know where to put the second one except here." I pressed the letter flat against my sternum. "Here."

"That's generally where I keep things too," Tam said. "Under the ribs. Next to the grudge."

That startled a laugh out of me, wet and unexpected, and once it started it took a while to stop, and Tam sat up and put his arm around my shoulders and let it happen, which is possibly the single bravest thing anyone did in this entire story.

When I could speak again, I said, "She says the Wellspire. That I have to go there and seal the covenant myself."

"Is that far?"

"It's the anchor of everything. It's where the wellspring comes up out of the world. So yes. Far." I looked at him. "And Ivo is on the road behind us, and the Archive wants payment I'm not sure I have, and the last person who tried to fix the covenant was me and I broke it instead. Those are the facts as compiled."

"Terrible prospectus," Tam agreed solemnly. "I've read better investment proposals off the back of lock-picks." Then, gentler: "But you're going."

"She died holding a door shut so I could walk through it. The least I can do is walk to the next one." I folded the letter along Sefa's creases and tucked it back into the oilcloth — I was not burning it; she'd known I wouldn't — and felt something shift in my chest as I did, a weight changing shape. Mourning, I realized, doesn't have to be a stone you carry. Sometimes, if you hold it right, it's a compass.

Because here was the thing I understood now, sitting in a ruin under bleeding stars with a boy who spoke to locks and a letter from a dead woman who expected change: I had spent my whole life believing I was small. A debtor. A copyist. A mistake hidden in a valley of forgetting. Sefa had spent her whole life making sure I'd believe it, because a hidden thing is a safe thing, and she'd known the world would come for me the moment I stopped believing it.

Well. The world had come. The valley was ash and the temple was compromised and the woman who guarded me had paid her final invoice, and I was done being filed away.

"They think I'm an anomaly," I said. "A misfiled page." I fed the last of Tam's plums to the fire's edge, warming them, and handed him half, because families start somehow and might as well start with stolen fruit. "Let's go be the correction."

Tam chewed. Swallowed. "That's the most scribe-terrifying battle cry I've ever heard."

"It's what I have."

"It'll do," he said, and grinned, and for a moment the ruin wasn't a ruin and the stars weren't wrong and the road ahead wasn't the longest and worst road in two realms. "Wellspire, then. How far?"

I reached into the nothing-bag for the map Yeva had sketched for us, and unrolled it across my knees — and stopped.

At the bottom edge, in handwriting I had not seen her add, in ink that was still, impossibly, drying, a line had appeared that neither of us had written:

*She knows you're coming. Bring the boy. Bring the letter. Do not bring mercy — you will need the room.*

Below it, drawn small and precise, a sigil I recognized from a page that had once rewritten itself every night in a temple office:

The mark of the living seal.

Mine.
