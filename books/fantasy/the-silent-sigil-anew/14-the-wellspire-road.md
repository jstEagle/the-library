## 14. The Wellspire Road

### DOSS

I've hauled a lot of cargo in my years on the road—salt, tin, fugitives, grief—but I never thought the heaviest load I'd carry would be hope.

We found her at the crossroads shrine outside Ferrow's Bend, three days after the raid on Meridian's rememberers' quarter burned half the district and taught me that the crown's idea of "demonstration" involves a man who used to sing harvest-songs sitting blank-eyed in a cage, drooling at nothing. That was Rennick. He'd mended our wagon axles with a touch and a hum. Now he couldn't tell an axle from his own hand.

So when I saw a thin figure in a scribe's coat standing at the shrine with a boy at her side, I did what any sensible ferryman of lost causes does.

I checked the treeline for riders first.

"Doss," Mara said, like we'd parted over breakfast instead of across a burning city with hounds on our heels.

"Mara." I looked at Tam, who was grinning like he'd personally invented survival. "You kept the boy alive. That's one more miracle than I expected from you."

"You're welcome," she said, dry as the salt-road. And there it was—the thing about Mara Quintana. Most people who've been hunted through a drowned underworld come out the other side either screaming or praying. She came out making jokes with a straight face and ink stains on her fingers. I decided right then I'd follow her to the Wellspire, which was fortunate, because she was going anyway and I'd have hated to be left behind.

The remnant of us numbered eleven. Eleven out of forty. I won't do that arithmetic out loud anymore; it has teeth.

Yeva came down from the rocks where she'd been keeping watch, and when she saw Mara, she made a sound I'd only ever heard from her once before—when her sister's name was read off a seizure list in Meridian. Then she crossed the distance and held Mara's face in her weather-browned hands like she was checking for rain.

"You look terrible," Yeva announced.

"She always looks terrible," Tam said. "It's part of her charm."

"Charm," Mara muttered, "is for people whose faces aren't on wanted broadsheets."

"Then you're safe," I said. "Nobody draws that face that ugly."

And just like that, we had a camp again. Not a caravan—we had two wagons and no mule—but a camp, a fire, a circle of people too stubborn to be dead. That night nobody talked about ledgers or covenants or the fraying sky. We talked about Rennick, and we sang the harvest-song he couldn't remember, badly, until the boy who speaks to locks cried himself out against his sister's shoulder, and then we slept.

In the morning, the road to the Wellspire waited, and so did the work.

### YEVA

People think weather-singing is shouting at clouds. My grandmother corrected that notion with a willow switch, and I will correct it now with words, which hurt less but linger longer.

Weather is memory. The wind remembers every valley it has crossed; the rain remembers the sea it rose from. A weather-singer doesn't command anything. She *reminds*, and the sky, being old and sentimental, agrees to oblige.

Mara came to me on the third morning of the Wellspire road, while the others broke camp, and stood there holding her own hands like they belonged to a debt collector.

"Teach me," she said. "To braid. Without losing whole years. You said it could be done."

I said many things under stress. But I looked at her—this woman who had paid a mother's voice out of her own skull to save strangers at a checkpoint, who had walked past a boy who loved her because the Archive charged admission to her own heart—and I said, "Sit. And don't interrupt. Grandmother hated interruptions and I inherited everything of hers except patience."

Here is what I taught her, and I set it down here in my mind's ledger because someone should keep the record even if the record-keeper forgets herself someday:

A whole memory is a storm. It has weight, motion, appetite. Pour one into a working and the working will drink it down and ask for more, the way a flood drinks a village. That's what Mara had been doing—paying in storms.

But a memory can be *skimmed*. Take not the year your mother died but the ten seconds of light through a window on the day she buried her own mother. Take not the friendship entire, but the smell of woodsmoke on the evening Tam first laughed at your joke. Small weights. Splinters of gold, not ingots. The spell barely notices. The self barely bleeds.

"The trick," I told her, "is that you have to go looking for the small thing on purpose. Grief hands you the big memories first because they're heavy and near the top of the sack. You have to dig past them."

"And if I dig past them," Mara said quietly, "and find the big ones anyway?"

"Then you feel them," I said. "Fully, once, without spending them. Feeling isn't paying. Nobody charges you for grieving, girl, whatever else this world has told you."

She sat with that for a long moment. Wind came up the valley, smelling of pine and far-off snow, and I felt it asking, the way wind does, whether anyone wanted to be reminded of anything.

"Show me," she said.

I took her hands—I'm not sentimental, but braiding needs contact, skin remembering skin—and I walked her into a small working. Nothing grand. We called a mist off the stream, just enough to blur our camp from the road, and I guided her to pay for it with something small.

She reached, I felt her reach—and she flinched, because the first thing her gift offered up was enormous. I felt the shape of it through her palms. A valley. Salt walls. A woman's voice saying *stay low, little bird*. Sefa, though I didn't know the name yet.

"No," I said. "Dig. Past it. What's underneath?"

Her brow furrowed. Her lips moved, counting, the way scribes count. Then her whole face changed—not the flinch of loss but the soft stupid surprise of finding a coin in an old coat. 

"Bread," she whispered. "There was bread on the windowsill that day. Burnt on one edge. She gave me the good half."

"That," I said, "will buy you a fog."

She paid it. The mist came up the bank like it had been waiting all its life for permission, curling around the wagons, softening our outlines against the world. Mara stared at it with wet eyes and didn't spill a single tear, which is its own kind of discipline.

"I didn't lose it," she said. "The bread. I still have the burnt edge."

"You never lose what you skim, if you do it right. You just spend the *extra* of it. There's always extra. Living makes more than dying spends." I let go of her hands. "That's the covenant in miniature, if you want the truth of it. Two realms, one wellspring, and enough to spare if nobody gets greedy."

She looked at me sharply. "How do you know about the covenant?"

"I'm old, I travel, and I listen," I said. "Also, Sefa wrote to me twice a year for thirty years. You were a frequent topic."

Whatever she was going to say to that got interrupted by Tam yelling that riders were coming, which is how most conversations end on this road.

### TAM

Okay so. Riders. Everyone panic, but like, organized panic.

Doss got us into the mist Mara had just made—it was still hanging around, all polite, like it wanted to help—which, credit where due, was the first time I ever saw a spell get used for something practical instead of tragic. Mara's spells are always tragic. This one just hid us. Progress.

Six riders came down the road. Not crown colors. Mercenary gray, the kind that means somebody's paying in real coin, which in this country means somebody's paying in minted memory, which means the crown even when it isn't wearing the crown.

They stopped at the shrine. One dismounted and read the marker stone. Another kicked the cold ashes of some pilgrim's fire. And then the leader took off his helmet and I grabbed Doss's sleeve hard enough to bruise, because I knew that face.

"That's him," I whispered. "The inquisitor. Reyes. He was in Meridian, he—he talked to me, he pretended—" My throat did a thing. "He asked me questions about Mara like he was friendly."

Doss's hand closed over mine, steady as a mooring line. "Is he alone?"

"He's got five men. And he's looking at the mist."

And here's the part I keep turning over, because locks tell me things and lately people are starting to seem like locks to me—all their tumblers showing if you know how to listen. Ivo Reyes stood at the edge of Mara's mist for a long time. Long enough that his sergeant got restless. And then he turned his horse around and rode back the way he came, and his sergeant said something sharp, and Reyes said something I couldn't hear but could *read*, because his shoulders said it:

*Not yet.*

He knows we're here. He could take eleven tired refugees with six hardened riders and maybe lose two men doing it. Instead he turned around.

Either he's setting a trap deeper down the road—or something in that man's ledger is starting not to add up.

I told Mara that night by the fire. Told her all of it. She listened the way she listens, which is like she's transcribing you onto the inside of her own skull, and when I finished she was quiet for a while.

"When I rewrote the checkpoint ledger," she finally said, "he was there. At the checkpoint. He read my forgery and he *let it pass*." She fed the fire a stick. "He had the writ. He had the authority. He watched a forged courier-license walk forty gifted people past his own soldiers, and he drank his tea."

"Maybe he's bad at his job."

"Maybe." She didn't believe it. Neither did I, honestly. People who are bad at their job don't become royal inquisitors; they become prior of a quiet little temple and burn suspicious records on schedule. "Or maybe he's reading the fine print. Late, but reading it."

I thought about the indentured kids in Meridian, the ones I'd promised wouldn't be forgotten. I thought about Rennick in his cage. I thought about Ivo Reyes turning his horse around at the edge of a mist that smelled faintly, if you knew to notice, of burnt bread.

"Fine print sinks everybody eventually," I said. "Even inquisitors."

Mara almost smiled. "Was that philosophy or lockspeak?"

"It's both. I contain multitudes. Ask any door."

For a second—one whole second—she laughed. And I know she doesn't remember teaching me to pick my first lock, doesn't remember the drowned tunnels, doesn't remember any of it. But the laugh was the same laugh. Some things aren't stored in memory. Some things are just load-bearing.

### IVO REYES

A man in my profession learns to distrust three things above all others: a clean trail, a cooperative witness, and his own certainties.

The trail to the Wellspire was clean. Too clean. Mara Quintana is many things—heretic, forger, anomaly of the first order—but sloppy has never been among them, and a party of eleven moving with wagons does not leave no sign unless someone is erasing the signs. Weather-craft. I logged it in my report to Handler Vess: *subject displays defensive meteorological capability, source unknown, estimated cost minimal.*

Estimated cost minimal. I wrote that with a steady hand, and then I sat with it after, the way you sit with a document whose margins have started whispering.

Because here is what I did not write in any report: I have begun auditing myself.

It started small, as rot does. A clerk in Vell Hollow mentioned the hunt had been funded by a bond issue—"memory-backed," he said, proud of knowing the term—and I signed his requisition and walked away and stopped walking. Memory-backed. Of course. Everything is, in Meridian. The crown sold bonds backed by forfeited memories to fund the witch-hunts. Elegant. Self-funding righteousness.

But that night I dreamed of a kitchen I did not recognize, and a woman's hands kneading dough, and a word I woke with in my mouth like a stone: *Ivolet.* Someone had called me that. Once. Someone small enough that the word fit entirely in her mouth.

I have no memory of any kitchen. I have no memory of anyone called Ivolet. I checked—quietly, through channels I'll deny if asked—whether I had ever sold memories. The answer came back sealed, which is itself an answer. Men with complete ledgers are not issued seals.

So: I am hunting a woman whose crime, per my writ, is existing with a gift intact. And I may be a man whose gift was taken to fund the taking. If that sentence is true, then my writ is not law. It is a recipe, and I am both the meal and the cook, and somewhere upstairs a handler is eating.

My sergeant thinks I'm being cautious, circling the refugee camp instead of striking it. Let him think it. The truth is I sat at the edge of that mist and felt it—felt the working in it, felt the craft in it, felt something in the deep cellar of myself turn over like a sleeper remembering a name—and I could not make my hand draw steel against it.

Vess sends orders weekly now. *Press the subject. Deny her the spire. Render all contacts blank.* Each order arrives with a stipend attached, drawn on the same bonded accounts.

Last night I took the newest order and, instead of filing it, I copied it out by hand. All of it. Every clause, including clause nine, which is never read aloud: *all persons of demonstrated gift, upon processing, to be rendered permanently insensate to the wellspring, retention of personhood non-guaranteed.*

Non-guaranteed. As in: sometimes we take all of you and give back none.

I sat with my copy until the candle drowned. Then I did the thing I have been not-doing for weeks.

I opened my personal ledger—the one with my own expenditures, my own payments, my own life itemized in the crown's neat script—and I began, line by line, to audit.

Somewhere south of here, Mara Quintana is walking toward the Wellspire to unmake the covenant-break. And somewhere north of here, in a locked drawer, sits a sealed file with my childhood in it.

One of those doors, I am going to open.

I find I no longer care which one opens me first.

* * *

### TAM

Fifth night on the Wellspire road. Mist practice went weird tonight—good-weird. Mara braided fog AND warmth into the same working, skimming two small things instead of one, and Yeva actually clapped, which for Yeva is basically a parade.

But here's why I'm writing this down in my head where nobody can confiscate it.

After the others slept, Mara stayed up feeding the fire, and she said my name. Just my name, quiet, like testing whether it still worked.

"Tam," she said. "I don't remember meeting you. I know that. I know what I paid, and I know what it bought, and I'd pay it again." Firelight did a thing with her face. "But tonight, braiding, I needed something small to warm the fog. And my gift went digging on its own. And it brought back—" she laughed, embarrassed, which she never is, "—a splinter. Ten seconds, maybe. Somebody laughing at my joke in a tunnel that smelled like river water. I don't know who. I don't know when. I just know that whoever it was, they laughed like my joke was the best thing that happened to them all year."

"That was me," I said. "The tunnel. The joke was about the eel."

"Eel?"

"You'll get it back someday. Or I'll just tell you the story until it sticks. Either way." I shrugged, casual, while my chest did acrobatics. "Point is, you spent the big version to save everyone, and the small version survived. Yeva says living makes more than dying spends. Turns out love works the same. You can't fully delete it. There's always extra."

She looked at me for a long time. And then she said, "When this is done—if I'm still me, or some new me—tell me the eel joke first. Before anything else. Promise."

"Promise," I said. "Right after 'they came back for me.'"

She frowned. "Why that first?"

"No reason," I said, and poked the fire, and did not tell her that I don't know why either, only that some door in me rattles when I say it, like something on the other side is already reaching for the handle.

Far off to the north, thunder rolled where there were no clouds. Yeva lifted her head, listened, and smiled a small dangerous smile.

"Not our weather," she said. "Somebody else is braiding."

And the road to the Wellspire went on ahead of us, dark and patient, like a page waiting for ink.
