## 15. Ivo's Empty Ledger

There is a particular kind of silence a ledger makes when it is lying to you.

I have spent twenty-two years listening to ledgers, so believe me when I say I know the sound. A clean ledger hums. Columns sit where columns should sit; sums close like well-made doors. A lying ledger doesn't shout. It just goes quiet in one place, the way a house goes quiet around the room nobody talks about.

The Crown Requisition Ledger for the Meridian Hunt, year of the Founder's Peace 312, went quiet on folio forty-one.

I sat in my quarters at the Gilded Quill — the crown keeps its inquisitors in comfort, comfort being cheaper than gratitude — and read the entry for the ninth time.

*Item: One (1) childhood memory-bundle, subject: REYES, IVO, age six through twelve. Quality: high-fidelity, emotionally dense. Sold by: guardian of record. Proceeds: 4,000 crowns, disbursed to founding of the Office of Anomalous Persons.*

The ink was old. The hand was clerical, tidy, unremarkable. It did not know what it had done. That's the thing about clerks; they think they're moving numbers. Nobody tells them the numbers are people.

I put the ledger down. My hands were steady, which surprised me. I'd have expected them to shake. Instead there was this terrible calm, like the moment before a door opens and you already know who's standing behind it.

You should understand something about me before you judge what I did next. I did not join the hunt out of hatred. Hatred is expensive and I've never been a rich man. I joined because I believed the hunt was mercy — that taking the gift from people who couldn't control it was kinder than letting it burn their villages down around them. I told myself stories. Inquisitors are professional story-tellers; it's the whole trade. We tell ourselves a story every morning so we can sleep at night, and if we're good enough at the telling, sometimes we even sleep.

But here is what no one tells you about being blanked. You don't miss what's gone. You can't. Missing requires a shape, and the shape has been cut out of you. What you get instead is a kind of weather. A grief with no object. I have spent my whole life feeling like a man walking through a house he used to own, touching walls and knowing, without evidence, that pictures used to hang there. I called it discipline. I called it dedication. I built a career on the empty rooms and gave the emptiness a uniform.

Folio forty-one gave the emptiness an address.

* * *

The Office of Anomalous Persons kept its records three floors below street level, behind a lock Tam Vess could have opened in his sleep and which took me, a grown man with a writ from the crown itself, eleven minutes and a bribed under-clerk named Pell. Pell took my coin without looking at my face, which I appreciated. There are mercies in this city and most of them are small and cost a silver mark.

The archive of disbursements smelled of cold stone and older paper. I found the original requisition in a box labeled with a date I knew by heart, because it was the date I had been given as my mother's death.

Except she hadn't died.

That was the first lie to come apart. She had signed. Her name was there, at the bottom of the sale document, in a hand that shook only slightly — a woman selling her son's childhood for four thousand crowns because my father's debts had come due and the crown's buyers paid better than the moneylenders. And beneath her signature, a second line, added later, in the founder's own administrative style:

*Subject retained for training. Blanking deferred to age fourteen per standard protocol. Note: residual aptitude detected. Monitor.*

Residual aptitude. Monitor.

They hadn't just taken it. They had watched me for it. Every commendation I ever earned — every time an instructor said Reyes has a nose for the gifted, finds them where hounds fail — I had been sniffing out my own kind with a gift they left in me like a splinter too deep to dig out. The blanking was never total. It couldn't be. You cannot fully remove magic from a person any more than you can remove salt from the sea; you can only boil off enough water that what remains looks like something else.

I sat on the archive floor among boxes of other people's sold years and laughed until it turned into something else, and then I stopped, because there was work to do, and Ivo Reyes has always been good at doing his work with whatever is left of him.

Here is the arithmetic I did, sitting in the dark. The Office of Anomalous Persons — my office, my hunt, my life's labor — had been founded with the proceeds of my own stolen childhood. Four thousand crowns. I had been the seed capital of my own cage. Somewhere a clerk had balanced those books and gone home to supper.

I want to be honest, since honesty has become a rare currency in my possession: my first thought was not justice. It was not rage, or pity, or any of the noble things a better man would claim. My first thought was *give it back*. Give me back the summers. Give me back whatever my mother's voice sounded like before she signed. I would have traded everything — rank, writ, the whole rotten apparatus — for one afternoon of being eight years old again.

That is what the gift does, I understand now. That wanting. That reach backward through time with both hands. They cut it out of us and we spend our lives leaning against the wound without knowing why it hurts.

* * *

Handler Vane received me in his office above the Office proper, a room designed to make visitors feel like petitions rather than people. Low ceiling. High desk. Vane himself behind it, small and dry and precise, a man assembled entirely from margins.

"Inquisitor." He didn't look up from his papers. "Meridian report?"

"I've come about a requisition," I said. "Year 305. A childhood bundle. Subject Reyes."

The quill stopped.

It was a small thing, the stopping. Half a heartbeat. But I've interrogated two hundred people, and I will tell you the secret of the trade: nobody confesses with words. They confess with rhythm. And Vane's rhythm had just broken like a plate.

"Administrative matter," he said. "Handled at the clerk level. If you have concerns about your compensation—"

"My compensation was four thousand crowns," I said. "Paid into the founding of this office. I was the endowment, Vane. I'm standing inside myself."

He looked up then. And here is the thing I will remember until they blank me properly or put me in the ground, whichever comes first: he wasn't ashamed. He wasn't even sorry. He looked at me the way a steward looks at a horse that has learned to open its own stall — not with cruelty, but with the weary irritation of a man whose inventory is misbehaving.

"You were a liability," Vane said. "A gifted child of no family and less coin. The alternatives were the pits or the pyre. The office chose education." He folded his hands. "Every year of your life since has been purchased. Be accurate about the accounting, at least."

"Purchased," I repeated. The word tasted like the salt-road, like ash. "By whom?"

"The crown serves continuity, Inquisitor. The Founder understood that magic is a debt the realm cannot service. Your writ — *all persons of anomalous aptitude to be rendered blank, by consent where practicable*" — he recited it with the smoothness of long rehearsal — "is not cruelty. It is amortization."

"Consent where practicable." I set the original requisition on his desk. Folio forty-one's little sibling. "Show me where I consented. Show me where Doss the ferryman consented, the one your riders took last week and blanked in the square as a demonstration. Show me where any of the children in the rememberers' quarter—"

"The demonstration achieved results," Vane said. "Compliance in the quarter is up thirty percent."

"Doss is a man who carried refugees across a border for eleven years and asked nothing but toll money and someone to argue with," I said. "And you erased him in front of a crowd and called the screaming *compliance*."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes. Not shame. Calculation. I watched him do the sum — the value of an inquisitor versus the cost of an inconvenient question — and I watched the sum come out wrong for me.

"Inquisitor Reyes," he said gently, which was how I knew. "You look tired. The Meridian assignment has been long. Perhaps you should surrender your writ for review. Rest. There are physicians who specialize in... occupational fatigue."

Occupational fatigue. It had a clinical ring to it, the way they'd phrase it afterward. *Inquisitor Reyes, burned out by years of diligent service, voluntarily submitted for restorative treatment.* And some other blank-eyed man would sign the order, and I would walk into the treatment room believing — because they always made you believe, that was the craft of it — that it was my own idea.

"You're going to blank me," I said.

"I am going to *rest* you," Vane said. "Unless you'd prefer the word."

I'll give him this: he didn't flinch when I came over the desk. Most men would have. But Vane had been handling dangerous documents his whole life and I suppose I was just another one — something to be stamped, filed, rendered inert. Two of his clerks pulled me off him, and I let them, because fighting in that room was exactly the story he wanted written about me. *Inquisitor Reyes, unstable. Inquisitor Reyes, anomalous.* I had spent twenty years writing that sentence about other people. I knew how it ended.

So instead I did the only thing left to a man whose whole self has just been revealed as somebody else's property.

I quoted scripture at him. Our scripture. The hunt's own catechism, the one they beat into us at the academy: *An anomaly identified must be documented; an anomaly documented must be contained; containment begins with the writ.* I stood between the clerks' hands, blood on my lip, and I said, "Then draw the writ, Vane. Brand me. Because I promise you — I *promise* you — the moment you render me blank is the moment the last person who believed in your mercy stops believing in it. I was your best argument. I was the proof it could be done kindly. Look at me now and tell me what I'm proof of."

Vane straightened his collar. He took up his pen. And he wrote.

I heard the brand before I felt the meaning of it — the scratch of the pen, the wax, the seal pressed down with his thumb. When they turned me around to read it aloud, per procedure, per the little theater of law they loved so much, the under-clerk's voice cracked on the word:

"*Traitor.*"

There it was. Twenty-two years of service, and the ledger finally closed. Funny thing — after all that, after the requisition and the archive and the arithmetic of my own erasure — the word landed lighter than I expected. Traitor implies you betrayed something that deserved keeping. What I felt, standing there with my wrists bound, was closer to relief. A door had shut. Doors are honest. They tell you plainly which side of them you're on.

They took my sword, my seal, my quarters at the Gilded Quill. They did not take my boots, because procedure does not concern itself with boots, and I want it recorded that I walked out of the Office of Anomalous Persons on my own feet, past Pell the under-clerk, who looked at my bound wrists and then very carefully looked at the floor, and who later — though he couldn't have known it — would leave a certain gate unlocked at a certain hour.

Small mercies. Silver marks. The economy of a dying regime.

* * *

They held me overnight in the Office cells, pending transport for treatment. Standard protocol. I know, because I helped write it.

I lay on the cot and took stock, the way you do. Inventory of Ivo Reyes, age thirty-four: one coat, one pair of boots, one writ revoked, one childhood sold for four thousand crowns and unrecoverable. Liabilities: everything else. Assets: —

And then I found them. Not memories. I don't have those; folio forty-one saw to that. Something smaller. Splinters. The residual aptitude Vane's predecessors had noted and monitored and never quite managed to file away. All my life it had been a tool — the nose for the gifted, the sense that found Quintana's flare across half a valley. I had aimed it outward, always, a hunting instrument.

Lying in the dark, I aimed it inward for the first time.

And the empty rooms lit up.

Not with what was lost — that was gone, truly gone, and no amount of wanting would boil the water back into the sea. But with the *shapes* of it. Where the pictures had hung. Which doors had led to summer. I felt, unmistakably, the outline of a woman's voice I could not hear, a kitchen I could not see, a boy — myself — laughing at something in a garden that no longer existed anywhere but in the negative space of my own chest. The blanking had been thorough. It had not been perfect. Nothing these people build is perfect; that's their tragedy, and tonight, God forgive me, it was mine to use.

Because shapes are enough. Shapes tell you what mattered. And what mattered, I realized, staring at the ceiling of my cell, was never the crown. Never Vane, never the writ, never the neat amortized mercy of a realm that sold its children to balance its books. The shape of my life, traced backward through all that absence, pointed one direction only — toward the border, toward the pass, toward the ragged caravan and the scribe with the ledger-tricks whose trail I had followed across half the map like a man walking in his sleep.

Mara Quintana. The anomaly. The debt-bound girl from the dead valley who rewrote reality with a clerk's pen and paid for it in pieces of herself.

I had hunted her with everything I had. And everything I had, it turned out, had been stolen from the same place her gifts were taken from. We were the same ledger, she and I. Debit and debit. I just hadn't known which column I was in.

Near dawn, boots in the corridor. Keys. My heart did the arithmetic of escape routes and came up short — one door, two guards, no coat, no blade.

The door opened, and Pell the under-clerk stood there, alone, holding a ring of keys and not meeting my eyes.

"Transport detail's been delayed," he said to the wall. "Clerical error. Could be an hour. Could be a day. Terrible thing, the state of the records lately." He set a bundle on the floor inside the cell — a plain coat, a knife, bread wrapped in cloth — and then he did look at me, once, quickly, and what was in his face wasn't courage. It was the look of a man setting down something heavy he'd carried too long. "My sister's in the rememberers' quarter," he said. "She sings weather. They haven't taken her yet. Make sure they never do."

Then he was gone, keys jingling down the corridor like a man whistling past a graveyard, and I was alone with a coat, a knife, four thousand crowns of stolen childhood, and a debt of my own for the first time in my life.

I dressed. I ate. I sharpened nothing, because the knife was sharp, and I thought about the road east of the city, where the sky has started bleeding stars into the daylight — I'd seen the reports and filed them as hysteria, and God help me, I'd been the hysteria all along.

Somewhere out there, Mara Quintana was walking toward the Wellspire with a found family and a plan to remake the world. Somewhere out there, Vane was drafting my treatment order. And somewhere between the two, a branded traitor with empty rooms where his life used to be was going to have to decide what, exactly, a man purchases when he buys back his own soul.

I blew out the cell's candle out of habit, which was absurd, since I was leaving.

Then I walked out of the Office of Anomalous Persons for the last time — past the unlocked gate, past Pell's empty desk, past twenty-two years of paperwork describing a mercy that had never once existed — and turned east, toward the fraying edge of the world, to find the woman I had hunted and ask her the only question left in my ledger:

How much of a man survives the blanking — and is it enough to fight?
