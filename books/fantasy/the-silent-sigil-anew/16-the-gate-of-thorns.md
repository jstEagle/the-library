## 16. The Gate of Thorns

The Wellspire announced itself the way a debt collector announces himself: politely, from a distance, so you had time to dread it.

We'd been walking three days since the drowned underways spat us out into a country where the sky couldn't decide what it was doing. Stars bled through the afternoon light like ink through wet paper — pretty, if you didn't know what it meant. I knew what it meant. Everything between the realms was fraying, and I was the loose thread holding both fabrics together, which was a lot of responsibility to place on a woman who still occasionally flinched at her own handwriting.

Tam walked beside me the whole way. He talked the whole way, too, because that was Tam — filling silence the way other people filled pantries, hoarding stories against famine. He told me about his mother's house in Meridian's lock-quarter, where every door sang a different note when it opened. He told me about the first time he'd spoken to a lock, aged six, and how he'd cried because he thought he'd broken it when it opened for him without being asked. He told me about Doss, about Yeva, about the night the riders came to the caravan camp, about all of it, twice.

"You're cataloguing," I said, on the third morning.

"What?"

"Me. You're telling me everything twice because you're afraid I'll need it later." I kept my eyes on the road. "I'm not going anywhere, Tam."

He was quiet for maybe forty paces. For Tam, that was practically a novena.

"It's not that," he said finally. "It's just — things are better said twice. First time you hear them. Second time you keep them."

"Wise."

"Sefa told me that."

The name landed in my chest the way Sefa's name always landed now: heavy, warm, and slightly accusatory. My old teacher who had died holding a border closed for a girl she'd smuggled out in the dark. A girl who turned out to be me. I was still learning how to carry that.

Ahead of us, Yeva stopped walking. When a weather-singer stops walking, everyone stops walking; it's an occupational courtesy, like getting out of the way of a falling tree.

"There," she said.

* * *

The Gate of Thorns was not made of thorns. That would have been honest, at least. It was made of light — or rather, of the absence of light, a doorway-shaped hole cut out of a cliff face that had no business having a door in it. Around its edges grew pale, translucent vines that moved without wind, curling and uncurling like fingers counting something. Counting us, probably.

The ground before it was covered in objects. Left behind things. A child's shoe, laced and waiting. A wedding ring sitting in the dust as though set down carefully by someone meaning to come back. A bundle of letters tied with string, addressed in seven different hands to the same name.

"People paid," Yeva said quietly. "And then they turned around."

"Or they went through and left these behind," Tam said. "Maybe the gate keeps the change."

"Nobody comes back through, boy. That's what the songs say."

"The songs also say weather-singers can't make it rain indoors, and I've seen you flood a tavern."

Yeva smiled despite herself, which I suspect was Tam's actual gift, the one nobody had bothered to write down.

The Archive's voice came out of the gate itself, and I use the word *voice* loosely, because what I heard wasn't sound so much as recognition — that feeling of being read. Every scribe knows it: the moment a superior takes your page and their eyes move across your work and you feel simultaneously exposed and erased. The gate did that. To all of us.

**THE SEAL RETURNS,** it said. **THE GATE REMEMBERS YOUR HAND, MARA QUINTANA.**

"Then you know why I'm here."

**WE KNOW WHY ALL WHO COME ARE HERE. THEY WANT. THE GATE WEIGHS WANT.** The vines curled inward, and the darkness inside them deepened until looking at it felt like looking down a well with no bottom. **PASSAGE REQUIRES PAYMENT OF EQUAL WEIGHT. YOU SEEK THE WELLSPRING. THE WELLSPRING IS EVERYTHING. WHAT WILL YOU BRING THAT IS EVERYTHING?**

"I don't have everything," I said. "Everything is rather the problem."

Behind me, Yeva murmured, "Mara—"

But I was already understanding it, the way you understand a ledger entry just before you write it — the whole shape of the transaction arriving at once, cold and complete. Memory. Of course memory. In Meridian, memories were currency; here, at the covenant's own door, they were the only coin that rang true. And I knew what the Archive wanted, because I'd spent weeks now piecing together what I'd once been: its living seal, its scribe, the one who'd written herself out of her own life to hide the covenant from a king who wanted to eat it.

The Archive didn't want my mother's voice. It already had that; I'd paid it at the checkpoint, and I'd felt the hole ever since, smooth-edged and terrible, like touching a tooth that isn't there. It didn't want Sefa, or the valley, or the road songs. Those were payments along the way. This was the toll at the door, and doors to places like this don't ask for pocket change.

They ask for the thing you'd least survive losing.

And there was only one answer to that question, and it was walking beside me, telling a story for the second time because second times were how you kept things.

"No," Tam said. He'd followed my eyes. Tam always followed my eyes; I hadn't noticed until that moment how often he read me, how fluently, the way you read a lock before you speak to it. "No. Whatever you're thinking — no."

"Tam—"

"Don't." His voice cracked clean down the middle. "Don't you dare stand there and do arithmetic with me. I've seen what you lose when you pay. I held your hand after the checkpoint. You asked me who I was and I watched you decide to trust me anyway, and it was the bravest thing I ever saw, but I am not — I will not be a—" He ran out of words, which for Tam was like a river running out of water.

"A price?" I offered gently.

"A person!" he shouted, and the vines flinched, and somewhere in the cliff face something ancient shifted its weight. "You're not allowed to pay with people. People aren't coin. That's the whole point of everything we've been doing since Meridian, that's the whole point of *you* —"

"Tam." I took his hands. They were shaking. Sixteen years old and he'd taught half a city's indentured children to pick locks so they could run, and he'd made me promise the ones we lost wouldn't be forgotten, and he'd never once asked me for anything except that promise. "Look at me. What happens if I turn around? What happens to Doss, blanked in some crown cell? What happens to Yeva and the children and every rememberer in Meridian when the boundary finishes collapsing? What happens to you?"

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "None of this has been fair since chapter one. But the gate doesn't take fairness. It takes equal weight. And the only thing I carry that weighs as much as the wellspring is—" My voice did something humiliating. I let it. "Is you. Is this. Is three days of you telling me everything twice because second times are how you keep things."

"Mara—"

"Which means it works," I said, and laughed, horribly, wetly. "Do you see? It works *because* it matters. If I could pay with something cheap, the gate would laugh at me. The fact that this is going to gut me is the entire point. It's proof of worth."

**THE SEAL UNDERSTANDS,** said the gate, with what I could have sworn was satisfaction.

"The seal wishes the gate would shut up."

* * *

Yeva braided a small wind while I prepared — nothing grand, just moving air, enough to keep the dust off the letters on the ground, because even now I couldn't bear the thought of someone's last payment getting rained on. She did it without being asked. Weather-singers grieve in weather, she'd told me once. This was hers: a small kindness aimed at strangers' abandoned shoes.

She didn't argue with me. I loved her for that and hated her for it in equal measure.

"Rules first," she said instead, practical to the marrow. "When it takes it, you'll still be able to walk. Probably. Sit down after anyway. And Mara — the memory doesn't vanish. Nothing vanishes. It goes into the gate, into the Archive. Which means someday, if the covenant holds and we win this, it can be given back." Her eyes were bright and fierce. "So this isn't goodbye. It's a deposit."

"You're lying to make me feel better."

"I'm a weather-singer. Lying to the sky is literally my job." She gripped my shoulders. "But I'm not lying about this one."

Tam stood apart. He'd been standing apart since the argument, arms crossed, jaw working, and when I finally turned to him he spoke first, fast, before I could.

"Okay. Okay. Conditions."

"Conditions aren't really—"

"Conditions," he repeated, with the iron I'd first heard in his voice in Meridian's underways, talking back to hounds in the dark. "One: you listen to my whole story first. All of it. The version I tell at the end, the good one, with the jokes. Because if you're going to forget me, you're going to forget the best version, not some sad summary."

"That's — fine. That's actually fine."

"Two." His voice wobbled and he bullied it straight. "You leave something. Before you go through. Write it down — you're the scribe, it's what you do. Your handwriting, on paper, something true about us. So that even if I'm a stranger to you, there'll be a page that says otherwise. Pages outlast people. You taught me that."

My chest did something structural. "Tam, I don't remember teaching you that."

"You will have," he said. "That's the point. Go on. I'll wait."

So I sat in the shadow of the Gate of Thorns, with stars bleeding through the daylight above me, and I wrote. My hand shook; the letters came out ugly, and I hated them, and I wrote them anyway:

*Tam Vess speaks to locks, and they speak back, and none of them — not one, in all the city — ever opened for him faster than he opened for us. He is sixteen and braver than any soldier the crown ever minted. He made me promise the forgotten children would be remembered. I am keeping that promise right now, on this page, in ink that will not rewrite itself no matter what the Archive does, because some things are written to stay.*

*I don't know yet what he'll be to me tomorrow. I know what he is today: family. Chosen, argued over, stubbornly maintained. Family.*

I signed it. Then, because it seemed important, I added: *Second times are how you keep things. Come find me for the second time.*

Tam read it without touching it — reading over shoulders was apparently a lock-speaker habit, doors and boundaries alike — and when he finished, he nodded once, sharp, like a man signing a contract he hated every word of.

"Three," he said. "When you walk past me and don't know my name — don't apologize. Strangers don't owe each other apologies. Just look confused. Look confused and let me fix it."

"I'm going to walk past you," I said, "and I'm not going to know that I'm supposed to be heartbroken. Do you understand how cruel that is? To you? You'll be carrying all of this and I'll just — blink at you."

"Yeah." He wiped his face with his sleeve, briskly, like drying hands after dishes. "Yeah, that's the worst part. I thought about it all night. But you know what? Doss got blanked and we didn't give up on him either. Nobody in this ridiculous caravan gets left as a stranger if we can help it. Those are the rules. I didn't make them." A wet, furious grin. "I just enforce them."

* * *

The gate opened its attention fully when I stepped forward, and the vines reached toward my temples like a scribe reaching for a fresh quill.

**NAME THE PAYMENT.**

I looked at Tam one more time. He stood straight, chin up, hands in his pockets like a boy waiting out a rainstorm. Behind him, Yeva's small wind stirred the dust around the abandoned ring, the shoe, the letters.

"His friendship," I said aloud. "Three days on the road beneath the river. The underways. The locks opening like flowers. The stories told twice. All of it — the whole shape of what we are to each other. That's the weight. Weigh it."

**IT IS WEIGHED,** said the gate, and paused, and I swear on every ledger I've ever copied, it hesitated. **IT IS... SUFFICIENT.**

"Funny," I whispered. "Everyone says that like they're surprised."

**THE SEAL BUILT US TO BE SURPRISED BY WORTH. IT WAS HER BEST INSTRUCTION.**

And then it took it.

There was no pain, which was somehow worse. There was just a gentle unhooking, one link at a time, the way you'd remove charms from a bracelet — and I felt each link go and could not stop it: the dark of the underways and Tam's voice making echoes funny; the hounds behind us and his hand pulling mine through a door that sang; the promise about the children; the joke about flooding taverns; the way he said my name like it was a place he was glad to be. Each one lifted away bright and whole, and the space it left behind didn't hurt, it simply *wasn't*, a room I couldn't remember having stood in.

The last thing to go was small. His voice, saying, *Things are better said twice. First time you hear them. Second time you keep them.*

I felt myself reach for it even as it left. I heard myself say, "Wait—"

The vines withdrew. The darkness ahead of me was just darkness now, deep and patient, leading down toward the wellspring and the echo of a woman I used to be.

I walked forward. My feet knew the way even though I didn't know why I was walking into a hole in a cliff, only that I had paid something enormous for the privilege and could no longer say what. Behind me, someone was crying — a boy, young, calling out a name that might have been mine, or might have been a prayer, or might have been nothing to do with me at all.

Strangers don't owe each other apologies.

I blinked at him, confused, and kept walking.

The dark took me gently, like a page turning, and far below, in the roots of the world, something that wore my face lifted its head and said: *You came back. Now let me show you what you broke.*
