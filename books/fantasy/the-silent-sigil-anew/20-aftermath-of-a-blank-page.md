## 20. Aftermath of a Blank Page

I've picked a lot of locks in my life. Cheap ones, clever ones, ones that were supposed to be impossible—locks that whispered their secrets to me the way other people's mothers whispered theirs to them. I know what an opening sounds like. The little sigh a bolt makes when it gives up.

Mara made no such sound when I found her at the foot of the wellspring.

She was just lying there on the pale stone like a coat somebody had dropped and forgotten to come back for. Alive—I could see her breathing, shallow but steady, the rise of her ribs counting out some slow arithmetic—but she didn't move when I called her name. Didn't move when I slid down the last stretch of spire-stair on my backside because my legs had stopped negotiating with my brain somewhere around the fourth hour of running. Didn't move until I put my hand on her shoulder, and even then all she did was open her eyes.

They were brown. Same as always. That much hadn't been taken.

"Tam," I said. "It's me. It's Tam. You're okay. Well—you're not okay, nobody who looks like that is okay, but you're alive, which is the bar we're clearing today."

She looked at me the way you look at a door you're pretty sure you've never opened. Polite. Slightly wary. The look of someone doing arithmetic about how fast they could stand up.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

Her voice worked fine. That was somehow worse. If her voice had been broken, if the words had come out wrong or empty, I could have told myself something had simply *broken* and things that break can be mended. But nothing was broken. Everything was just—absent. A room swept clean so thoroughly that even the dust remembered being furniture.

"You knew me," I said. "You knew me really well, actually. You owe me a promise about locks, and half a story about a ferryman, and—" My voice did a thing I didn't authorize. Cracked, folded, whatever. "You're Mara Quintana."

"Mara," she repeated, tasting it. Not recognizing it. Tasting it the way you taste a coin to check it's real. "Is that—is that mine?"

"Yeah," I said. "It's yours. You wrote it yourself. On a fresh page. First thing you did after saving both realms, actually, was sign your own name. Very dramatic. There was blood involved. Yours, mostly."

"I don't remember saving anything." She sat up slowly, wincing, pressing a hand to her sternum like something in there ached in a way ribs couldn't explain. "I don't remember anything. There's—there's a hole where everything should be, and the hole has edges, and the edges hurt." She looked up at me, and here's the part I'll tell my grandchildren, if I ever get any, if anyone ever wants to hear stories from a lock-speaking boy who grew up indentured in Meridian: she looked at me, a stranger, and said, "Did I lose someone?"

"Not lost," I said, because Doss would have wanted me to say that, and Yeva, and honestly because I'd promised myself on the road beneath the river that I'd never lie to her. "Spent. You spent us. On purpose. To seal the covenant. And it worked, Mara. The boundary's holding. I saw it from outside—stars went back where stars belong. You did it."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not memory. Memory was gone. More like the shape memory leaves behind, the way a boot leaves a print in mud after the foot's walked on.

"That's why it hurts," she said quietly. "Not what I lost. That I can tell I lost something. Like carrying a ledger with pages torn out and knowing—*knowing*—the entries mattered, without being able to read a single one." She laughed, and it was a terrible small sound. "I'm a scribe. Do you understand? I don't even have my own book anymore."

"You've got a blank one," I said. "Blank's not nothing. Blank is where I started too, remember? Before anybody taught me locks had names." Then I remembered she didn't remember, and shut up, and helped her stand.

* * *

We came down out of the Wellspire into daylight that felt wrong only because for two days the sky had been bleeding starlight into noon, and now it wasn't. Ordinary blue. Ordinary sun. The caravan remnant had camped at the spire's base among the thorn-gates, which had gone quiet and almost gentle, the way a guard dog sleeps after a long night. Doss was sitting by the fire with a blanket around his shoulders looking like a ship that had survived a storm by refusing, on principle, to sink.

He saw Mara and got up fast—too fast, he still moved like a man whose mind had been recently emptied and imperfectly refilled—and then stopped himself, halfway, arms half-open, because Yeva had briefed everyone on the walk down: *she won't know us. Don't crowd her.*

So he just stood there, this huge gruff ferryman who'd once threatened to throw me off his wagon for picking his strongbox lock (a fair response), and said, gruffly, gently, "Miss Quintana. You'll want soup. Everyone wants soup after the end of the world."

"Thank you," Mara said politely, and took the bowl, and drank it like a guest at a stranger's fire.

Yeva cried. Quietly, into her sleeve, braiding the air over the fire so the smoke rose in neat spirals instead of drifting into people's faces—a habit of hers, tidying grief into craft. Ivo stood apart, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who'd spent months hunting a person and had arrived just in time to watch her erase herself, and couldn't decide whether that counted as justice or as the worst thing he'd ever witnessed. Given that Ivo had recently discovered his own childhood had been sold out from under him to fund the very hunt he ran, I gave him room. We all did.

And Mara sat among us like a coin from another country. Right size, right weight, wrong face on it.

"The Archive," she said suddenly, halfway through the soup. "You mentioned—the Archive. It took things from me before. Can it give them back?"

Everyone looked at each other. Nobody wanted to answer. So I answered, because apparently that's my job now.

"It demanded payment, last time. Passage cost a memory of equal weight. It doesn't do refunds."

"But it's weakened," she said. She'd gone scribe-brained; I could see it, the same focused stillness she used to get transcribing Doss's fading spells by firelight. "The covenant's sealed through it now—or through me, or through whatever I am now. It needs me functional. A broken seal is a bad seal." She set down the bowl. "Take me to it."

* * *

The Archive received us in its oldest chamber, the one where the shelves grew out of the walls like teeth. It had always spoken in that voice like paper settling—dry, patient, faintly smug. Now it sounded like paper that had gotten wet and dried again. Warped. Humble, if paper can be humble.

**LIVING SEAL,** it said. **YOU ARE INTACT. THE BOUNDARY HOLDS. THIS PLEASES THE COLLECTION.**

"Cut the cataloguing," I said. It didn't scare me anymore. Nothing scared me anymore, or rather, everything already had, all at once, and I'd come out the far side of frightened into something flatter and more useful. "Give her back what's hers."

**WHAT IS HERS WAS SPENT. SPENDING IS NOT REVERSIBLE.**

"She spent it buying *you* a functioning covenant," I shot back. "Sounds like a purchase to me. And purchases come with warranties."

A pause. Somewhere in the dark between shelves, pages turned without hands.

**THE ARCHIVE IS WEAKENED,** it admitted at last, and the admission seemed to cost it, the way confession costs proud old men. **THE OLD COVENANT MADE US STRONG. MEMORIES FLOWED THROUGH US LIKE RIVER WATER THROUGH A MILL. NOW THE MILL TURNS SLOWLY, AND WHAT WE HELD IN TRUST FOR HER—"**

It stopped. Started again. I swear the whole chamber creaked, like a ship changing its mind.

**WE HELD FRAGMENTS IN ESCROW. SCRAPS THAT CLUNG TO THE SEAL WHEN SHE PAID. SHE GAVE WHOLE YEARS. WHAT REMAINED WERE SHAVINGS. EDGES. IT IS NOT RESTORATION. IT IS MERCY OF A SMALL AND INSUFFICIENT KIND.**

"Give them anyway," Mara said.

**THE PRICE—**

"Was paid," she said, and her voice did something I recognized even though she wouldn't: it went flat and certain, the voice of a woman reading the fine print of someone else's writ. "By me. In full. Check your ledgers, Archivist. I know you keep them."

Another silence. Longer. Then, from the shelves, a sound like snow sliding off a roof, and light gathered—small lights, motes, dozens of them, drifting down toward her like dust deciding to become fireflies.

**TAKE THEM,** the Archive said. **AND KNOW THAT THE DEBT RUNS THE OTHER WAY NOW. THE ARCHIVE REMEMBERS WHAT YOU DID. WHEN OTHERS COME SEEKING WHAT WAS TAKEN FROM THEM BY CROWN OR COIN, THE ARCHIVE WILL NOT CHARGE THEM. THIS IS THE BEGINNING OF REPAYMENT, NOT THE END OF IT.**

The lights touched her forehead, her hands, her closed eyes, and sank in.

* * *

What came back weren't memories. Not really. They were more like—splinters. Echoes. The Archive hadn't lied: shavings, edges, the sawdust of a life.

Mara sat very still afterward, palms up in her lap like she was waiting for rain. Her face kept changing. Grief, wonder, grief again, moving across it like weather.

"I have a feeling," she said slowly. "About a song. Road song. Someone with a terrible voice sang it and I mocked him for it and he kept singing anyway." Doss made a noise that might have been a laugh or might have been a man swallowing something large. "I have a feeling about salt roads, and night travel, and being afraid, and someone holding my hand—not romantically, just—steadying. Was that you?"

"Sefa," I said. "On the way out of the valley. Yeah, that was her."

"Sefa." She said the name carefully, like placing a valuable object on a high shelf. "I know I loved her. I can't remember loving her. But I know it. The way you know a load-bearing wall is there even after the house is built around it." She turned to Yeva. "Weather-braids. I learned weather-braids. I remember *that I know them* and not how to do them."

"Fragments," Yeva said softly. "That's what he said. Shavings."

"And you." Mara looked at me, and I braced myself, the way you brace for a wave you know is coming. "There's a boy. Younger than me. Speaks to locks. He made me promise something about children in Meridian—that they wouldn't be forgotten. I don't remember making the promise. But I feel it. It's in me like a splinter that won't work its way out. Did I keep it? Whatever it was—did I keep it?"

"You're keeping it right now," I said. "The hunt-ledgers are getting burned. Ivo's doing it himself. Every name the crown collected on gifted kids—it's going up in smoke. So yeah. Kept."

She nodded. Then, quieter: "And you. What am I supposed to feel about you? Because there's something. Something warm and specific, shaped like a person, and I reach for it and get nothing but the warmth. It's driving me mad. Tell me what we were, so at least the shape has edges."

I looked at her for a long moment. Around the fire, everybody else had developed an intense interest in the soup, the stars, the distant thorn-gate, anything. Even Ivo.

"We were friends," I said. "Good ones. The kind that trade stories instead of memories, because stories you can give away for free and they multiply. That's what we were."

"Friends," she repeated. And then, with a small, fierce frustration that was the most *her* thing I'd seen since the wellspring: "Why does that word feel like it's holding a door shut on something bigger?"

Because friendship was the door, Mara. Family was the room. But I didn't say it. Some things you don't hand a person. You build them a place to find it themselves.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. Neither did I, much. We ended up side by side near the dying fire, wrapped in blankets, watching sparks climb.

"I keep thinking," she said eventually, "about the torn-out pages. How I know the entries mattered. Everyone keeps telling me what I did, what I sacrificed, how brave. And I believe them. But belief isn't memory. It's like being handed someone else's diary about my life and being told, *trust us, this is you.*" She hugged her knees. "Do you know what the worst part is? Not the mother's voice, though I'm told that was first, and gods, that hurts even blank. Not Sefa, though I ache. The worst part is that I can't grieve properly. Grief needs a shape. Mine's just—an absence with edges."

"You said that before," I said. "Edges. You like that word."

"It's accurate." A ghost of a smile. "Scribes like accuracy."

"Well then, here's something accurate," I said. "Everything they took from you—every year, every voice, every road—we know it. All of it. Doss knows the songs. Yeva knows the braids. I know the promises. You're not the only one carrying those memories, Mara. You never were. That was kind of the whole point of us." I shrugged, embarrassed by my own honesty. "So the pages aren't destroyed. They're just... filed elsewhere. Badly organized, admittedly, in a ferryman and a weather-singer and a lock-boy and a reformed inquisitor. But filed."

She was quiet a long time.

"That's either the kindest thing anyone's ever said to me," she said finally, "or a very elaborate way of saying I should trust four strangers with my entire past."

"They're not strangers," I said. "That's the thing. *You're* the stranger. To us. And we're going to keep not being strangers at you until it takes."

She laughed—really laughed, short and startled, the first one since I'd found her—and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

"I lost everything," she said into her palms. "And you're all sitting here acting like that's fixable."

"It's not fixable," I agreed. "Nothing's fixable. Things are rebuildable. Different words. Better ones, sometimes."

She lowered her hands and looked at me across the embers, and there it was again, that stubborn set to her jaw I'd have known anywhere, even blank, even new-minted. The jaw of a woman who'd copied ledgers for years to pay a debt she never owed, and who'd just paid a debt she chose, and who was clearly already drafting terms for whatever came next.

"All right," she said. "Then start at the beginning. Whoever you are—Tam. Start at the beginning and don't skip anything. If I have to be a blank page, then fill me in. Slowly. Accurately. And Tam?"

"Yeah?"

"When you tell it—tell me the parts where I was wrong, too. The parts where I was afraid, and mean, and stupid. I don't want a legend. Legends are useless for living in."

I grinned, despite everything. Especially because of everything.

"Oh, you're going to love chapter six," I said. "The checkpoint ledger. Absolute crimes against bureaucratic integrity. Doss still gets misty-eyed about it."

But before I could start—before the first word of the first story, the first brick of the rebuild—Doss cleared his throat from across the fire, and beside him Yeva had gone still, head tilted, listening to something none of the rest of us could hear yet. Weather-singers hear weather coming days off, and what was coming wasn't weather.

"Riders," Yeva said. "Many. Flying no colors I know." She looked at Ivo, and Ivo's face did something complicated and grim. "And they're not crown. Crown hunts with writs and warrants. These ride like folk following a map."

Mara straightened, blank page and all, splinters of memory flickering behind her eyes like a storm deciding.

"A map to what?" she asked.

"To you," I said. "Whoever they are—they're riding for you."

And the fire cracked, and the dark leaned in, and the blank page waited for whatever would be written next.
