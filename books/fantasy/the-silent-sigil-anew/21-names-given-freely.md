## 21. Names Given Freely

**Doss**

I've taught the road songs to forty-one people in my life. Forty of them could carry a tune. The forty-first was a scribe from a valley that hated music on principle, and she sang like a door hinge that had been asked nicely to stop complaining.

It didn't matter. That's the thing about road songs nobody tells you before you learn them: they aren't songs, not really. They're knots. You tie them around a name so the name doesn't blow away.

Mara Quintana came back to us empty. I want to be clear about what "empty" means, because poets get it wrong and so do priests. Empty isn't sad. Empty is a wagon with no cargo rattling over stones — everything still there, still rolling, just nothing *in* it. She looked at me and saw a large weathered man with a bad knee and a ferryman's squint. Which is accurate. But she didn't see Doss-of-the-Caravan, who once talked her out of selling her boots for bread by pointing out that her feet were the only part of her that had never lied to anyone.

So I did what I do. I started at the beginning.

"The first song," I told her, sitting on an upturned crate outside the rebuilt rememberers' quarter, "is called *The Ferryman's Regret*. It has four verses and every one of them is a lie, which is why it works."

She raised an eyebrow at me like I'd handed her a fish and asked her to explain theology. Good. That was the old Mara too. She never took anything on faith, including things she herself had said.

"Why does it work if it lies?"

"Because the lie is shaped like the truth." I spat, because a ferryman must, professionally. "Verse one says I left my home because the road called me. Truth is the tax collectors called me. Verse two says I love the open sky. Truth is I couldn't afford a roof. Verse three says I've never turned back. Verse four says I regret nothing." I let that sit. "You sing it enough times and the lies wear smooth, and underneath there's this hard little stone of fact that you can hold in your mouth when everything else is gone."

"Everything else being gone," she said carefully, "is something I know about."

"I know." And here's where I'll tell you the thing I'm proudest of, more proud than any crossing I ever made with a full hold in flood season. I didn't tell her what she'd lost. Not one word of it. Yeva wanted to. Tam wanted to — God help us all, Tam wanted to most of all, and it was the hardest thing I ever did to put my hand flat on that boy's chest and say *not yet, and maybe not ever, and that has to be okay.*

Because grief given without consent is just another kind of theft. The crown stole her memories once. We weren't going to steal them back and call it rescue.

Instead I taught her the songs as if she'd never heard them. Which she hadn't. Same thing, almost.

She was terrible for six days. On the seventh day she got verse two right, and something in her face went strange and soft, like she'd walked into a room she'd never seen and known where the chair was. She didn't say anything. Neither did I. A ferryman knows when the current is doing the work.

By the end of the month she could sing all four verses, plus the rude fifth verse we don't teach children, plus the harmony line that only the person sitting next to the fire-keeper is supposed to know. She asked me why the harmony line was secret.

"It isn't secret," I said. "It's *given*. There's a difference. A secret keeps itself. A gift needs somebody to hand it over."

She thought about that for a long time. Then she said, "Sing it with me tomorrow?"

And I did. Badly, both of us, hinge and gravel, under a sky that had finally stopped bleeding stars through its seams.

**Yeva**

Weather-braiding cannot be taught to someone who wants to control the weather. This is the first lesson and the last lesson and every lesson between. The sky is not a horse. You do not break it. You ask it very politely to dance, and sometimes it says yes, and mostly it says *who are you again*, and once in a great while it says *oh, YOU* — and that last one is what happened with Mara, and I am getting ahead of myself.

When she came back to us blank, everyone assumed the gift went with the memories. Reasonable assumption. Wrong. The gift lives deeper than memory, down where the body keeps its own ledger — the way your hands remember rope-work after your mind forgets the knot's name. Mara's hands remembered ink. Oh, how they remembered ink.

But braiding is not writing, and this frustrated her beautifully. She kept trying to *compose* the wind. She'd stand on the ridge above Meridian with her arms out like a woman dictating a letter, and the wind would look at her the way a cat looks at a closed door.

"You're instructing," I told her. "Stop instructing. Braid."

"Braid what? There's nothing in my hands."

"There's always something in your hands. Right now there's a Tuesday. Give me the Tuesday."

She looked at me like I was mad. Fair. I have been called worse by better skies.

Here is what I mean, and I will write it plainly because Mara-the-scribe trusts plainness: a braid is three strands. Strand one is a memory — small, small, *small*, a crumb, never a loaf. Strand two is a need, spoken aloud or not. Strand three is permission — yours, given freely, that the sky may take what it takes. Wind costs. Rain costs. If you braid big memories you come back hollow, and hollow is how the crown liked its gifted, once. So: crumbs. A smell. A color. The way bread sounded when your mother tore it.

I did not tell her about her mother. See, Doss thinks I agreed with his silence out of principle. Partly. Also partly because I watched her flinch at nothing, three times a day, reaching for something that wasn't there, and I decided the reaching was hers to end or keep.

We started with crumbs. She gave the sky the memory of wet slate after rain — a nothing-memory, a scribe's memory, the smell of a clean page — and asked for a breeze to dry the washing lines. The breeze came. Small, obedient, perfect.

Then she gave it the Tuesday. Just some Tuesday, unimportant, she said, though her voice cracked on the word *unimportant* in a way that made me turn away and fuss with my braid-cords. And the sky said *oh, YOU.*

What came off that ridge was not a breeze. It was a long slow spiral of cloud, gold-edged, turning over the city like a hand turning a page, and every soul in the rememberers' quarter stopped and looked up, and the indentured children — our children now, freed by writ and fire both — started laughing for no reason anybody could name, which is the oldest sign of weather done right.

Mara stood in the middle of it with tears running down her face and no idea why she was crying.

"That's normal," I told her, and put my arm around her shoulders, and lied the way Doss taught me lies should be shaped: like the truth. "Everyone cries at their first storm. It's the sky saying thank you."

She believed me. Or she chose to. With gifts like hers, I suspect those are the same act.

Later — much later, when the light was going amber — she asked me, quietly, whether she'd ever braided before. Whether the sky had known her.

"The sky forgets faster than people do," I said. "That's its mercy. But it remembers *how* you touch it. And it touched you back today like an old friend."

She nodded slowly. Then: "Teach me the rain-braids next. The ones for drought."

"Drought-braids cost more than Tuesdays."

"Then I'll pay in colors." She smiled, and it was a new smile, built fresh, no history in it, and it was already becoming one of my favorite smiles in a life that has held perhaps nine favorites total. "I have a lot of colors. Nobody's using them."

**Tam**

Okay so. Here's the thing about telling someone their own story.

I practiced it for eleven days. Eleven days I stood outside the room where Mara was mending ledgers for the new archive — she does it free now, can you believe that, the girl who was debt-bound since birth volunteers at a library — and I rehearsed. *Hi, I'm Tam. We met on the road beneath the river. You paid a memory of me to get through the Wellspire gate. It was worth it. Please don't feel bad.* Eleven days of that, in a mirror, in a stairwell, to a lock (the lock said I sounded insane, and locks know).

And then the day came and I threw all of it away and just knocked.

"Come in," she said, and looked up, and waited, because she's patient with strangers in a way she probably learned twice.

"Hi. I'm Tam. I speak to locks." I stuck out my hand. "We were friends. Before. You don't remember, and that's fine, and I'm not here to make it fine, I'm here because—" and then my rehearsed speech fell out of my head entirely and what came out instead was: "—because you promised me something once and I want to give you the chance to promise it again."

Her eyebrows went up. "What did I promise?"

"You promised the kids wouldn't be forgotten." My throat did a thing. "The indentured ones. In the counting-houses. You said — you said *a debt paid in names stays paid*. You wrote it into a checkpoint ledger and it saved forty lives and then you forgot you wrote it, but the ledger didn't. Ledgers don't forget. That's the whole point of you."

She was quiet for a second. Then she said, "Show me the ledger."

So I did. We walked down to the archive together and she pulled the old checkpoint book — Ivo brought it back when he burned the hunt-records, kept this one page out of the fire like a man saving a single plank from a shipwreck — and there it was. Her handwriting. Her sigil-hand, the one the Archive used to whisper about, looping and sure. Forty names entered as licensed couriers, each one real, each one alive because of it.

She read every name aloud. Slowly. Like she was minting them.

Then she looked at me and said, "Tell me the rest. From the beginning. All of it. Even the parts that hurt."

"Especially those?" I asked, because I'm brave about locks and cowardly about everything else.

"*Especially* those," she said. "A story with the hurt cut out is just a rumor."

So I told her. All of it. The salt-road and Sefa. The caravan and Doss's terrible singing. The checkpoint and the price. Meridian, the Archive, the drowned underways where she held my hand not because she was scared but because I was. The Wellspire gate, where she paid me away and walked past me and I let her, because she asked me to, and keeping a promise matters more than keeping a friend — she taught me that, the version of her I lost, and apparently the lesson stuck in me even when it left her.

I told her the parts that hurt. She cried at the right places, which felt like a miracle, because how did she know they were the right places? How do you cry at a stranger's grief?

"You're crying," I said, stupidly, at the part about the gate.

"I know." She wiped her face and laughed, wet and helpless. "I don't know why. It's like — it's like my body read the ledger before I did."

After that we made a rule, her and me. Every week she tells me something true about herself that nobody taught her. Every week I tell her something true about us. No obligation. No expectation. She can stop any time. That's what makes it a gift instead of a debt — Doss's law, and it's a good law.

Last week she told me she dreams about a river with stars in it, and wakes up happy, and doesn't know why.

I didn't tell her. I just wrote it down, in my own hand, in my own book, under a heading that says *things she'll figure out eventually.*

There's room in that book for years.

**Ivo Reyes**

They gave me the hunt-ledgers because nobody else would touch them. Thirty-one years of the crown's accounting of souls — names, prices, blankings, the whole filthy arithmetic — and the council of the rebuilt quarter looked at the crates and looked at me and arrived, unanimously, at the obvious candidate. The inquisitor. The hound. Let him burn what he fetched.

I expected to hate the work. I did not expect to be good at it.

There is a discipline to destruction, it turns out. You cannot burn a ledger carelessly; the ink resists, the pages gutter and fly, and half-burned names are worse than whole ones. So you do it properly. Page by page. You read each name — you *must* read it, that's the discipline, that's the penance — and then you feed it to the fire and watch it go all the way to ash, and you say nothing, because nothing you could say would be adequate.

Four hundred and six names so far. I have read every one.

Some of them I knew. Some of them I signed for. One of them — page two hundred and twelve, a boy of nine, sold by his father's creditors, rendered blank in the autumn of a year I remember mainly for the smell of the processing hall — was mine.

I have written about this elsewhere and I will not relive it fully here. Suffice to say: I found my own name in the crown's arithmetic, priced at eleven marks, and I burned it last of all, and I stood at the brazier until dawn, and when Mara Quintana found me there she didn't offer comfort. She offered work.

"You're reading them wrong," she said.

"They're names. There's no wrong way to—"

"You're reading them as debts. Read them as entries in a covenant." She sat down beside me, cross-legged, in the ash-smell, unbothered. "Every name in there was a promise somebody broke. Burn them as broken promises, not as prices. It goes easier."

It does not go easier. But it goes *truer*, and I have decided truer is the currency I deal in now. The writ that made me is ash. The handler who made the writ is awaiting trial, and I have requested — formally, in writing, with the terrible patience of a man who once believed in procedure — to testify. They think I want vengeance. Let them. What I want is the witness box, and the whole court listening, and one sentence spoken clearly:

*I was on the list too.*

The caravan has not forgiven me. I haven't asked them to. Doss speaks to me in short sentences and Yeva doesn't speak to me at all, and both of those are fair tariffs. But Tam sits with me sometimes while I burn, and reads the names along with me in a whisper, learning them, and last week he said the thing I keep in my chest now like a coal:

"You're not burning them to erase them. You're burning them so they stop being *entries* and go back to being people."

Yes. Exactly that. A boy who talks to locks understood in one sentence what took me thirty-one years and a war's worth of ruin.

Mara comes most evenings. She watches the fire with her hands around her knees and sometimes she hums one of Doss's road songs, badly, and the fire burns straighter for it — weather-singer's trick, or maybe just company. Last night she asked me, without looking over, "Do you remember anything? From before?"

"A smell," I said. "Bread. And someone laughing. That's all the crown left me."

"That's not nothing."

"No," I agreed. "It's not nothing. It's a start. Apparently starts are the family business now."

She laughed at that — laughed, at *me*, the hound of Vell Hollow — and I found, to my considerable surprise, that being laughed at by Mara Quintana was a thing I could build a life around.

**Mara**

They are teaching me myself, piece by piece, and they refuse to hurry.

This is what I understand now, in the way you understand a load-bearing wall — not by studying it but by leaning on it: I lost a memory and gained a family, and the family was there both times. Before, when I couldn't see it. After, when I couldn't recall it. Twice given. Twice chosen. Doss says that makes it stronger, not weaker — a knot tied twice holds in flood season. He would know. He ties everything twice.

Tonight we're all at the fire behind the rebuilt archive. Doss is singing *The Ferryman's Regret* and making me do the harmony line, and I'm getting it wrong, and he says getting it wrong is part of the song, which I suspect is also a lie shaped like the truth. Yeva is showing Tam how to braid a candle-flame steady against the wind, using a crumb of memory — Tam is giving it the sound of a lock opening, which is apparently the happiest sound he owns. Ivo is at the edge of the circle, reading names into the fire, and every few pages he looks up to check we're still here, and every time we are.

I don't remember any of them. I want that written plainly somewhere, because everyone keeps being gentle about it and gentleness can curdle into pretending. I do not remember these people. I know them the way you know a house you've lived in for a month — where the chairs are, where the warmth pools, which floorboard bites. I don't know why the river-with-stars dream makes me happy. I don't know why verse two of a ferryman's lie makes my chest ache. I don't know why, when Tam laughs, some deep cellar-room in me lights up like someone struck flint down there.

But I know this: they came back for me. Not for the seal, not for the scribe, not for the woman who reforged the covenant. For me — the blank page, the stranger, the woman who sings like a complaining door hinge. They looked at everything I wasn't anymore and stayed anyway.

Earlier tonight, before the fire, I sat at my desk in the archive and opened a fresh book. Clean paper. No debt in it, no prior, no covenant, no handwriting but mine. At the top of the first page I wrote my name — slowly, the way Yeva taught me to braid, giving each letter permission.

And beneath it, before I could think better of it, one line. I don't remember living it. I believe it completely. It came out of my hand the way the gold-edged cloud came off the ridge — unbidden, certain, mine:

*They came back for me.*

Tam says I should write down everything true about myself that nobody taught me. So tomorrow I'll start a second list. Things I don't remember but know. I already have three entries.

One: the road songs are lies shaped like truths, and the truth underneath is that I was loved on that road.

Two: the sky knows how I touch it, and touches back.

Three: whatever I paid at the gate, it was worth it — and the people on the other side of that gate have spent a season proving the payment wasn't final.

The fire is low now. Doss has started the rude fifth verse and Yeva is threatening him with a Tuesday. Tam is asleep against my shoulder, and I've decided not to move, because moving would end this moment and this moment appears to be the whole point of everything.

Tomorrow, Ivo burns the last crate. Tomorrow, the council asks me to formalize the new covenant's terms — my terms, written in my hand, the living seal consulted at last. Tomorrow there will be ledgers again, because there are always ledgers.

But tonight there is a fire, and a family I'm meeting for the first time, and a page in a clean book with my name on it and one line of absolute certainty underneath.

Whatever comes next — whatever the council asks, whatever the Archive whispers from its weakened shelves, whatever new debt or old magic comes knocking — I know how this story goes now.

Someone tells me the truth. I write it down. And they come back for me.

Every time.
