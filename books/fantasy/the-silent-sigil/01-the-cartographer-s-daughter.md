## 1. The Cartographer's Daughter

The fingerbone was the last honest thing about Maren Ostreva, and I said as much to her while I worked.

"Your husband lied on his deathbed," I told the bone, turning it under my lens. "Told the priest he'd made peace. Your sister stole your good shawl before you were cold. And this—" I tapped the little joint with my awl, gentle as a knock on a door I didn't want opened, "—this is the only part of you that never learned to lie."

The bone didn't answer yet. That was the trick of it, and the curse, and the trade: bones only spoke once you gave them shape. A raw bone was just a bone. But carve it—give the dead a form to pour themselves into—and they'd whisper up through the grain like water through chalk.

My mother taught me that. My mother, who was the cartographer's daughter, who married down into a family of carvers and never let anyone forget she'd married *down*, who could draw you a map of Vessarene from memory with her eyes closed and her hands shaking with drink. She's dead eleven years now, in a kingdom that's been dead six, and I still hear her voice every time I pick up a blade. *Carve true or don't carve at all, Quentin.* She meant the lines. She also, I suspect, meant everything else.

I meant to carve true tonight. It was what I owed Maren Ostreva, whose daughter had pressed three coppers and a knucklebone into my palm behind the tannery in Lower Sallow and asked, in the flat voice people use when they've cried themselves empty, if I could make something so her mother wouldn't be entirely gone.

I could. That was the whole rotten miracle of me.

* * *

You want to know how it works? Everyone asks eventually, usually right before they decide they're sorry they did.

It isn't magic the way the Concord means magic—the burning kind, the kind they drown villages for. It's older and smaller than that. Every life leaves a mark on the body that carried it, the way water leaves a mark on stone. Joy wears the grain smooth in places. Grief cuts deep channels. A life of lying leaves a bone cloudy, full of seams. A life of love leaves it dense, heavy in the hand past all reason. The body is a ledger, and death doesn't close the book; it just stops anyone from reading it.

Except carvers. Except us.

There were nine families of bone-carvers in Vessarene when I was born. There are two now, maybe three, and one of those is a rumor I started myself to keep hunters looking elsewhere. We are what the Ashen Concord calls *resonant abominations*, which is a long way to say: people who listen. They burned the shrines first, then the priests, then the carvers, because a carver can prove the dead still exist somewhere, and an empire built on ash needs its subjects to believe the past is ash too. Nothing before the Concord. Nothing beneath the Concord. The old gods sealed away, the old prayers outlawed, and everyone encouraged to get very busy not remembering.

I remembered for a living. Quietly. For coppers.

So: I set Maren Ostreva's knucklebone on my cloth, took up my finest blade—a sliver of Vessarene steel no longer than my smallest finger, wrapped in leather worn soft as skin—and began.

A keepsake takes an hour if the bone is willing. This one was willing. Some bones fight you, dense with grievance, and you come out of the carving wrung out and hating someone you never met. But Maren's bone had a clean grain, patient, almost relieved. As though it had been waiting eleven days in a grave for exactly this.

I carved small, because small is safe. A bead, mostly, with a spiral running around it—not any sigil, nothing that would light up an inquisitor's senses, just a pattern like fern-fronds, like frost, like the whorl of an ear. Something a granddaughter could wear on a cord and touch without knowing why it comforted her.

And then, as always happens, as I have never once managed to prevent, the bone began to speak.

* * *

It comes up through the fingers first. Not sound, exactly. More like cold water finding a crack in a wall—you feel it arrive before you understand what it is. The workshop went quiet in that particular way it does, the way where the silence itself has weight and texture, and Maren Ostreva poured herself into the spiral I'd given her and looked out through sixty years of memory at whatever she looked at last.

She was praying.

That shouldn't have surprised me. Most people's last words are prayers, or curses, or names—usually a child's name, sometimes a debt-collector's. But there are prayers and there are prayers, and Maren's was the kind I hadn't heard since the fall. Not the Concord's sanctioned petitions, those gray little transactions—*Ashen Father, keep me dutiful, keep me useful, keep me from wanting*. No. Maren was praying the old way, the way my grandmother prayed, face open, voice unguarded, addressing the gods by their secret names the way you'd address old friends.

*"Lirien of the threshold, keeper of doors, I am coming to yours. Let me through clean. Watch my girl—watch Annetta, she forgets to eat when she grieves, watch her—"*

The prayer broke off there. Last words do. And here is the thing I need you to understand, the thing that makes this trade unbearable rather than merely strange: the prayer kept going. In the bone. In the grain. Maren had died mid-plea, and the plea had nowhere to go, and so it had curled up inside her knuckle like a snail in its shell and waited eleven days for someone to carve a door in the doorless dark and let it finish.

*"—watch her, Lirien, watch her, watch her—"*

Over and over. Not words anymore by the end, just the shape of them, the desperate repetition wearing a groove into the bone like a river wears a canyon. *Watch her, watch her, watch her,* addressed to a goddess who had been silent for twenty years, sealed behind a door that no longer existed, in a heaven that might have emptied out entire for all anyone could tell.

I sat with it. That's the discipline. You sit with the dead's last moments until they've finished saying them, because cutting the connection early is theft—it's robbing a person of their final sentence, and I refuse. My rule has three parts and I recite them the way other people recite the Concord catechism, out of spite as much as habit:

Carve. Listen. Forget.

Three words. Simple. Sustainable. The alternative is what happened to old Master Corvane, who listened and listened and never forgot, and who ended his days talking to a drawer full of relics because the silence between voices felt like drowning. The alternative is carrying every grief in the province inside your chest until your ribs crack under the weight of other people's love.

So I sat with Maren until the *watch her* faded to an echo, and the echo faded to grain, and the grain was just grain again, and then I wrapped the bead in wool and blew out my candle and performed the third word of my rule with the practiced ease of a woman who has done it four hundred times.

Forget.

Mostly it works.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about forgetting as a discipline: it's not erasure. Erasure would be mercy. What it actually is, is stacking. You put Maren Ostreva on top of the tanner's son who begged his brother not to sell the boat, and him on top of the Concord soldier who wept for his mother in a language from the southern coast, and her on top of the refugee child whose last word was *cold*, and you press the stack down flat and you tell yourself it's discipline, it's professionalism, it's the only sane way to do this work—

—and some nights the stack shifts.

Some nights you're walking a smuggler's road with a family of strangers and a child looks up at you with eyes the exact color of a dead woman's memory, and the whole pile comes loose at once, and you have to stop and pretend to check the sky for patrol-lanterns so nobody sees what's happening to your face.

Tonight was a stacking night. I finished the bead, and I cleaned my blades, and I ate bread that tasted of nothing, and I lay on my cot in the loft above the chandler's shop—which pays me no rent and asks no questions, which in occupied Vessarene is the warmest hospitality available—and I stared at the rafters and heard, faint and patient and utterly ignored, *watch her, watch her, watch her.*

Annetta would come for the bead tomorrow. She'd tie the cord around her neck and feel her mother close and never know the half of it. She'd never know that her mother's last thought had been so fierce and specific and hopeless. She'd never know that the prayer had landed nowhere—that it had been shouted into a sealed room for twenty years, that every prayer in Vessarene for a generation had been shouted into that same sealed room, and the room had never once answered.

Or.

I turned the thought over the way I turn a doubtful bone under the lens. Because there was a story the old carvers told, back when telling it wasn't a hanging offense. About the Silent Sigil. About a door between worlds, sealed from both sides at the end of the godswar, sealed so thoroughly that even the gods' own names stopped working—and about the wardens who held the seal, the bloodline charged with keeping it shut, generation after generation, until Vessarene fell and the Concord hunted that bloodline into the ground along with everything else old.

Door-wardens, the story called them. Royal blood, twisted into lock-smiths. Extinct, presumably. The Concord liked its monsters extinct.

I'd grown up on that story. I'd grown up on it the way you grow up on a fairy tale—fond of it, dismissive of it, never once wondering whether it might be about you.

I rolled onto my side and told the rafters, in the voice I use for clients who haggle, "If you're listening, Lirien of the threshold, I hope you're having a better century than we are."

Nothing answered.

Of course nothing answered. Nothing ever answered. That was the whole architecture of the world now: prayers going up, silence coming down, and a bone-carver in a chandler's loft holding the stack of everyone's unfinished sentences against her chest and calling it discipline.

I closed my eyes. I began, with professional thoroughness, to forget.

And that was when the pack in the corner—the plain, scarred, travel-pack that goes everywhere I go, that has crossed the Thornfell passes forty times under patrol lanterns without so much as warming—began, very softly, to glow.

Not firelight-glow. Not lantern-glow. A warmth from inside the leather, amber-colored, pulsing slow as a sleeper's heart, throwing the shadow of my own buckles across the floorboards in shapes I didn't recognize and couldn't look away from.

I knew what was in there. I knew exactly which relic sat in the inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth: an amulet I'd carved three years ago for a dying pilgrim woman on the Salt Road, a simple thing, a name-bead, already paid for, already delivered to a family that should have buried it with her by now.

It had never glowed before. Nothing had ever glowed before. In six years of hearing the dead, in a lifetime of listening, in all the stacked and sleepless history of my trade, nothing had ever once answered back.

The glow pulsed again. And inside it, thin as a thread drawn through bone, terrified as a child under a bed, a dead woman's voice said:

*It's awake again.*
