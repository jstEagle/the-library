## 2. Roads That Remember

The road through Thornfell doesn't exist. That's the first thing I tell every family I take up it, and it's true in the way most useful things are true—technically, officially, on any map the Ashen Concord has ever drawn. There is no road. There is only a goat path that a hundred generations of smugglers have agreed to pretend is nothing, and goats are liars by trade, so they keep our secret well.

The Ferrow family did not look like they'd survive a lie of that magnitude. They looked like what they were: a miller's household from the lowlands, soft-handed and hollow-eyed, with everything they owned wrapped in two blankets and carried on a grandmother's back because Grandmother was the strongest of them now. The Concord had taken the mill, then the miller's sons for labor levies, then come back at night for the daughters. The family had run with what remained: Marta Ferrow, her mother Odile, and three children, the youngest of whom—a boy called Pim, all elbows and enormous eyes—had spent the first day of our journey asking me questions in the relentless rhythm of a woodpecker.

*Are we there yet? Why not? Is that a wolf? Are you sure? Have you ever been eaten by a wolf?*

I had answered none of them, which he correctly interpreted as encouragement.

We were two days into the high passes, camped in a hollow beneath an overhang that some long-dead shepherd had stacked into a windbreak, eating barley porridge that tasted mostly of the pot it was cooked in. Above us, cloud sat on the ridgeline like a lid on a pot, and under that lid, somewhere, the Concord patrolled roads that *did* exist, which was precisely why we didn't.

"Bone-lady," Pim said.

I closed my eyes briefly. "Quentin."

"Bone-lady Quentin." He scooted closer on his haunches, porridge forgotten, which his grandmother would have had opinions about. "My sister says you talk to dead people."

His sister, a girl of perhaps twelve named Wren who had not spoken to me directly once in two days, went rigid over her bowl. I felt the whole family go quiet around us, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"I carve bones," I said. "Keepsakes. Mementos. It's a trade."

"But do you *talk* to them?"

There it was. The question underneath the question, the one every refugee family eventually worked up the nerve to ask, usually in this exact register—the children sent ahead like scouts because children could be forgiven their curiosity where adults would be suspected of superstition, and superstition, under the Concord, was a word with a rope tied to one end of it.

"No," I said.

Pim considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Wren says our da talks to Gran sometimes. At the grave. He says she answers."

"Wren should eat her porridge."

"She made yours while you were checking the trail," he said, with devastating accuracy, "so she can't."

I looked down at my bowl. He was right. It always unsettled me, how thoroughly children saw, as though they'd been issued the eyes adults had to trade away.

* * *

Here is what I would have told him, if truth were a currency anyone could afford:

Yes. I hear them. Not voices in the air—nothing so dramatic, nothing the ballads would recognize. When I work bone, when the knife finds the grain and the grain opens under it, whatever the dead carried in that bone comes up through my hands like sap through a cut branch. A ring finger carved into a mourning bead gives up its owner's last words. A shinbone shaped into a flute remembers marching songs. I don't summon them. I don't speak *to* them. I listen, because listening is unavoidable if I want to carve anything worth selling, and then I forget, because forgetting is the only way to keep doing the work.

Carve, listen, forget. Three rules, learned in blood and worse, and I have kept them for eleven years since Vessarene fell, kept them the way other people keep faith.

The prayer I heard last week—the dead woman's fingerbone, the words rising through my knife like heat off a stone—I had not managed to forget. *Lady of the Threshold, keeper of the door, watch my daughter on the road.* A prayer to a god who hadn't answered anyone in twenty years. I had carved the bead anyway, sealed the words inside it under polished surface, and given it to the daughter, who wore it without knowing what slept against her collarbone.

That was the trade, really. Not bone-carving. Coffin-making for words. Everyone I guided carried someone's unspoken last wishes, and I was the mule that hauled them over the mountains.

"You're frowning at your food," Pim observed. "It didn't do anything wrong."

"Pim." His grandmother's voice, low, warning. Old Odile had been watching me all journey with the particular attention of a woman who has buried enough people to know what grief looks like wearing a workman's coat.

"It's fine," I said, and it mostly was.

* * *

We broke camp before dawn because the trail above the hollow crossed a scree field where we'd be visible to anyone on the eastern ridge, and the eastern ridge was where patrols liked to walk, because the Concord believed in roads too, just different ones.

Marta Ferrow carried the youngest on her back and the second-youngest by the hand. Odile carried the blankets and, I noticed, a small iron key on a cord around her neck that she touched at intervals like counting a rosary. Wren walked behind me, which was either trust or surveillance, and with twelve-year-olds the two are indistinguishable.

The scree was kind to us for an hour. Then the clouds lifted off the ridge like a curtain being checked, and light poured down the valley, and I saw them.

Three figures on the eastern ridge. Ash-grey cloaks. The slow, deliberate walk of men who own everything they survey.

"Down," I said quietly, and the family folded itself behind a shelf of stone with a competence that surprised me until I remembered: they'd been running for a year. Fugitives learn fast or die learning.

I bellied forward to the edge of the shelf and looked down the slope we'd need to cross within the hour. Open ground, maybe four hundred paces of it, tufted with mountain grass tall enough to hide a crouched adult but not a child, and certainly not a child the size and enthusiasm of Pim Ferrow.

Below and to the left, half-hidden in a fold of the hillside, there was an alternative. An old miners' cut, a drainage tunnel from workings abandoned before the fall, its entrance choked with rubble and probably home to something with more legs than a person wants to meet. It would let us bypass the open slope entirely and come out below the patrol's sightline.

It would also be dark, narrow, and the sort of place where the dead are thick. Mines remember their dead better than churches do. Every collapse, every bad air pocket, every man who went down a shaft and didn't come up—they settle into the rock and the timber props, and if I had to carve anything in there, even brace a splint, I'd hear them.

But I wasn't planning to carve anything. And hearing them was a risk I'd carried for eleven years. One tunnel more wouldn't kill me.

Probably.

* * *

Getting five civilians plus myself through a collapsed mine drainage cut is not a story I tell for entertainment, so I'll summarize: it involved mud, darkness, one screaming-adjacent moment involving bats (Pim), one genuinely frightening moment when a section of timber prop groaned like a dying animal (all of us), and roughly ninety minutes of moving by feel with Wren's hand locked on the back of my belt like a small determined anchor.

In the middle section, where the tunnel narrowed and the air went cold and mineral-sweet, I put my palm flat against the rock to steady myself, and the dead noticed me.

They always do. It's not sound, exactly. It's pressure—the sense of attention turning toward you, the way a room full of sleeping people becomes a different room when one of them opens their eyes. The miners of this cut had been dead a century and more; their bones had been gathered and buried decently before the fall, and what remained here was only impression, residue, the shape of waiting.

*Cold,* they said, in the way stone says things. *Long. Long.*

"I know," I murmured, too low for the living to hear. "Not much farther."

Behind me, Wren's grip tightened. "Did you say something?"

"The rock settles," I said. "Old tunnels talk."

"Do they say anything interesting?"

I almost laughed. The girl had a future in smuggling if the world ever stopped requiring refugees.

"They complain about the weather," I said, and moved on.

* * *

We came out of the cut into late-morning sun, below the patrol's line, in a fold of the valley where a stream ran loud enough to cover our footsteps for the next mile. By my reckoning we'd reach the waystation—an old shrine, pre-fall, tucked in the cliffs where the Concord hadn't bothered looking because the Concord didn't believe in anything worth hiding—by tomorrow evening. From there, Sefa's crew would take the family the rest of the way over the pass to the coast road and whatever waited beyond the borders of everything they'd known.

I let the family rest by the stream. Rest, when guiding fugitives, is not kindness; it's maintenance. Tired people make noise, stumble, panic. But watching the Ferrows drink and wash their faces and laugh—one short, startled burst of laughter from Marta when Pim fell in up to his knees—I felt something in my chest that I declined to examine, the way you decline to examine a lump.

Odile settled beside me on a flat stone, produced from somewhere a twist of paper containing dried apples, and offered it. Sharing food on the road is a contract older than any god, so I took two slices and chewed in silence until she spoke.

"My son prays," she said. "At the grave, like Wren said. To the Lady of the Threshold."

"That's not a crime yet."

"It will be. Everything old is becoming a crime. That's how you know it mattered." She looked at me sideways. She had her son's eyes, or he had hers. "You knew that prayer. When I said the name, you flinched."

"I didn't flinch."

"Sixty years of watching people lie to me, girl. I raised four of them." She handed me another apple slice whether I wanted it or not. "Do the old gods hear? Out here. In the mountains."

And there it was again, the question, coming now from the other direction—from the old instead of the young, which was worse, because the old asked it knowing the answer might break something they needed intact.

What I said: "No one knows. The shrines are empty. The prayers go up and nothing comes down. If they were real, they've gone, or they were never there."

Which was true as of last week. Which might already be a lie, given what I'd felt—or imagined I'd felt—when I sealed that dead woman's prayer into the bead: a faint warmth in the bone, like a coal remembering fire, gone before I could swear to it.

What I did not say: *Hold on, old woman. Something may be stirring. I'm not sure yet, and if I'm wrong, hope will hurt you worse than despair.*

Because here is the arithmetic of my life: every comfort I withhold is cruelty today and mercy tomorrow if the thing hoped for fails. Every lie I tell a child about wolves and gods keeps them walking. Hope is heavy cargo. I am paid to move people, not burdens.

So I lied to Odile Ferrow, gently, and she received the lie with a nod, and I could not tell if she believed it or simply recognized it as the kindness it was dressed up as. With grandmothers, the two are indistinguishable.

* * *

Pim found me at dusk, when we'd made camp in the lee of the stream's gorge and the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing. He sat down next to me without asking, which is a privilege children grant themselves and adults spend years earning.

"Bone-lady Quentin."

"Hm."

"When we get over the mountains. Will you still be here?"

"On the mountains?"

"No. Here. Walking people. Like a—" he waved a hand, searching—"a ferryman. For land."

"Some nights."

"And after?" He meant: forever. Children mean forever constantly. It's one of their best qualities and cruelest.

"After, I imagine someone else will do it. Or no one. The war ends someday, probably. Wars always end; that's the one nice thing about them."

"Then who'll walk the people?"

"People won't need walking."

He frowned at the fire. I could see him turning the sentence over, testing it for structural flaws, finding several. "Everyone needs walking sometimes," he decided. "Even you."

"Especially me," I agreed, before my mouth could outrun my judgment.

He looked up sharply, the way children do when an adult accidentally tells the truth, sensing blood in the water. But whatever follow-up question he was loading got interrupted by his mother calling him to bed, and he went, but at the edge of the firelight he turned back.

"Bone-lady? Are the old gods real?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, wind moved along the ridge like something pacing.

"No," I said.

He nodded, satisfied, and went to sleep believing me, and I sat by the fire feeling the lie sit in my chest like a swallowed stone, because the truth was: I didn't know anymore. Because the truth was: three days ago a dead woman's prayer had warmed in my hands like something answering, and I had told no one, and I had told myself it was nothing, and I was running out of days on which *nothing* would hold.

* * *

Near midnight, I took out my kit to check the stock—habit, ritual, the closest thing I have to prayer these days—and my thumb found the one piece I hadn't sold. The bead. The daughter's bead, the one I'd been carrying to finish polishing, that was my story and I was sticking to it.

It was warm.

Not body-warm. Not pocket-warm. Warm the way a hearthstone is warm long after the fire's gone, warm from the inside, and when I held it to my ear the way a child listens to a shell, I heard—not the dead woman's voice, not her prayer, but underneath it, beneath the words like water beneath ice—

Something shifting. Something vast turning over in a sleep that had lasted twenty years, and a whisper threaded through the bone grain, thin as wire:

*"It's awake. Oh gods, it's awake again—"*

I sat very still in the dark with a sleeping family around me and a warming relic in my palm, and I thought about the lie I'd told a seven-year-old at the fireside.

*I hope,* I thought, with no idea whom I was hoping to, *that I haven't just started a war.*

The bead pulsed once against my skin, patient as a heartbeat, like something knocking politely on a door from the other side.
