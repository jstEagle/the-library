## 3. A Prayer Answered

There is a particular kind of silence that comes with high altitude and low conscience, and I had been marinating in it for three days.

The Kestrel Line had gone to ground at a waystation called the Hollow Stair—a shelf of rock and rotting timber tucked into Thornfell's shoulder where even the Concord's maps gave up and drew a decorative squiggle instead of a road. Sefa had ordered two days' rest, which in captain-speak meant *stop carving until your hands shake less*, which meant someone had finally noticed my hands shaking. I'd blamed the cold. Nobody believed me. That's the trouble with found family: they watch you closely enough to catch you lying, but they love you enough not to say so out loud. It's a terrible arrangement. I recommend it against my better judgment, which is exactly how I ended up belonging to one.

So there I was, on the third evening, doing the thing I'd sworn off for two days, because rules are for people who can sleep.

I was re-cutting a keepsake. Not a listening piece—those were done, sealed, forgotten, per the rule—but a repair job for old Marta Venn, who'd lost her husband to a rockslide two winters back and wanted his memorial medallion re-strung before the anniversary. Simple work. Birch bone—no, that's not a thing; it was elk antler, but Marta called it birch bone because her husband used to joke that he was too cheap to be made of anything fancier—and a simple border pattern, nothing that would sing, nothing that would speak. Craft, not communion. The kind of honest work my mother would have approved of, if my mother had ever approved of anything, including weather.

The amulet in my pack started glowing warm at roughly the same moment I decided I deserved supper.

I want to be clear about the sequence of events, because later—when the Concord's records, if they keep records, describe what happened in the mountains that autumn—I want it noted that the first sign of the end of the world interrupted my dinner plans. There should be some record that I resented this.

* * *

Let me explain what "warm" means, for those of you blessed enough never to have carried the dead around in a leather satchel.

A carved relic has a temperature the way a person does. Most run cold—the dead are done, their prayers went nowhere, and cold is just the honesty of finished things. Some run neutral, like river stones, when what's left in them isn't a voice but an echo, a habit of grief with nobody home. In twenty years of carving, I had held exactly one warm relic, and that was my grandmother's, and she'd been dead forty years by then, and whatever warmth lived in hers was memory, not message. Warmth meant a prayer still moving. Still traveling. Still *going somewhere*.

Nobody's prayers had gone anywhere in a generation. The old gods were gone—or silent, or dead, or simply no longer taking deliveries; theology was above my pay grade, and my pay grade was mostly paid in goat cheese and distrust. The Concord had burned every shrine from the capital to the coast, and after the burning came the quiet, and after the quiet came carvers like me, who learned to listen to the dead precisely because the living gods had stopped answering. Carve, listen, forget. My rule. My liturgy. My entire spiritual practice could fit on a coin.

And now something in my pack was warm the way a hearthstone is warm. The way a hand is warm.

I didn't move at first. This is important. I sat very still with the half-finished medallion in my lap and let the fear walk through me room by room, checking the windows, the way fear does. Because there were only two explanations for a warm relic, and one of them was miracle, and I had built a life on the assumption that miracles, like roads through Thornfell, existed only on maps drawn by optimists.

The other explanation was worse. The other explanation was that something had found the prayers and answered them, and things that answer prayers after twenty years of silence have reasons, and reasons like that are never small.

I opened the pack.

It took me a moment to find it, because warmth doesn't glow—not at first, not visibly. It was buried under a spare shirt, a coil of waxed cord, and half a wheel of cheese I was emotionally committed to defending. But my fingers knew it before my eyes did: a small round amulet of dove-grey bone, worn smooth at the edges, strung on a leather cord gone soft with age. I'd carved it seven years ago. Maybe eight. For a girl named Odile Ferrane, in the last village before the passes, whose mother was dying of the lung-rot and who had come to my stall with a copper coin and a face like a closed door.

*I don't want a saint,* she'd told me. *Saints are for people with time. I want something my mother can hold.*

So I'd carved her mother a listening piece. Standard work, back then: a disc of shinbone—bovine, ethically sourced, I ran a respectable operation once upon a time—with a prayer-groove cut in a spiral, the old pattern, the pattern my grandmother's grandmother used, back when patterns like that got you invited to temples instead of burned. The mother had held it while she died. Odile had kept it after. And when Odile herself had passed through Thornfell three years later, fleeing east with the second wave of refugees, she'd pressed it into my hands at a checkpoint and said, *You take it. You're the one who talks to them. I'm done talking.*

I had listened to it once, that night. The mother's voice was still in there—faint, frayed, the way voices get when they've been praying into silence for years. She wasn't asking for anything anymore. She was just keeping her daughter company across the distance, the way the dead do, saying the same small things over and over: *eat something, wear the scarf, don't trust men with good boots.* Then I'd sealed the groove with wax, per the rule, and packed it away, and forgotten it, because forgetting is a discipline and I am, when I manage to be anything at all, disciplined.

Except apparently the wax had cracked somewhere along the trail. Or maybe wax doesn't matter anymore. Maybe nothing works the way it used to, starting tonight.

The spiral grooves were glowing. Faintly. A gold color with green underneath it, like sunlight through pond water, pulsing slow as breathing.

I did not touch it. I would like the record to show that I possess, occasionally, a survival instinct. Instead I crouched there in the doorway of the Hollow Stair's main room with the amulet lying in my open pack like a coal that hadn't gotten the news about winter, and I waited for it to do something worse.

It did something worse. It spoke.

* * *

Here's what hearing the dead is actually like, since ballads get it wrong and the Concord gets it wronger. It isn't voices in your head. It's more like pressing your ear to a door and hearing conversation on the other side—muffled, half-lost, arriving in pieces. You get fragments. Tone carries better than words. You learn to read grief the way sailors read weather, by the shape of what presses against you.

This was not muffled. This was not fragmentary. This was a woman's voice coming through the amulet's spiral like water through a broken dam, and it was not praying.

*It's awake again.* The words hit me like a slap of cold air off a glacier. *Oh—oh, it's awake, it's awake, I felt it turn over, I felt—I haven't felt anything move in twenty years and now it's AWAKE—*

The voice broke apart then, and what came through wasn't words at all but pure terror, undiluted, the terror of the dead, which is a different animal entirely from the terror of the living. The living are afraid of dying. The dead are afraid of things I spent years pretending not to understand, because understanding them would have meant admitting the dead knew things about the world's machinery that I preferred to leave unrusted.

I sat down. Not gracefully. The floor of the Hollow Stair is uneven slate and I met it the way most of us meet floors we weren't planning on: all at once, with commentary.

"All right," I said to the amulet, because apparently I was the kind of person who talked to glowing bones now, and honestly, the kind of person I'd always pretended not to be had been losing ground for years. "All right. Slowly. What's awake? Name it."

*The door,* said the dead woman, Odile Ferrane's mother, who in life had been a weaver and in death, apparently, had been promoted to prophet. *The door between. The Sigil. The Silent Sigil—it's awake again, carver, it turned in its sleep and I felt it through the dark, we ALL felt it, and it's waking up and nobody's holding the rope—*

"There is no door." My voice came out flat and certain and completely unconvincing, even to me. "The Sigil's a story. A seal, a legend, something grandmothers use to scare children off glaciers. It's not real."

*Child,* she said, with the infinite patience of someone who had been dead long enough to stop being impressed by denial, *you carve the bones of the dead and hear them answer. And you're telling ME what isn't real.*

She had me there. It's the great flaw in my epistemology and I've known it for years: I disbelieve in everything except the one impossible thing I do every day for money.

Outside, the wind moved through the pass with its usual Thornfell eloquence—which is to say it sounded like a large animal with grievances—and somewhere below, the Kestrel crew's fire crackled, and Tamir was laughing at something, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary for another few minutes, and I gripped that ordinariness like a railing.

"Talk me through it," I said. "What did you feel?"

*A turning,* she said. *Like a key in a lock seen from the wrong side. Twenty years of nothing—of all our prayers going down a well with no bottom—and then tonight, something at the bottom of the well said hello.*

Something cold walked up my spine wearing boots. Because here was the thing I hadn't said aloud, the thing I'd filed under *coincidence, handle later, preferably never*: my relics had been strange for weeks. Not warm—strange. Voices clearer than they should be. Prayers repeating that had never repeated before. Twice, in the last month, I'd heard the dead say words in unison, dozens of them, a chorus, which is not how the dead work. The dead are solitary as snowflakes. Unison requires direction. Unison requires somebody conducting.

I'd told myself it was altitude. I'd told myself a lot of things. Denial is load-bearing, in my experience; you can build quite a comfortable life on top of it, right up until someone kicks the wall.

"And the others?" I asked. "The rest of the dead. Do they feel it too?"

Silence. Long enough that I thought the connection had failed, that the warmth would gutter out and I'd get to keep my small carved-out life intact. Then:

*Carver,* she said, and her voice had changed, gone quieter, the way voices go quiet when they're about to tell you something that will follow you home. *They aren't frightened anymore. That's what woke me. All these years we've prayed and begged and grieved, and tonight the whole dark went still, like a forest when the wolf stands up. They've stopped asking for comfort.* A pause. The glow in the spiral pulsed, once, twice, patient as a heartbeat. *They've started asking for justice.*

I looked at my hands. Carver's hands, scarred and stained, steady now—the shaking had stopped somewhere during the conversation, which was its own kind of omen. These hands had kept a hundred small promises to the dead and broken only one rule: carve, listen, forget. Forget, forget, forget. It was the forgetting that kept me sane. It was the forgetting that kept me safe. Every relic I sealed was a door I closed on purpose, and I'd told myself for twenty years that closing doors was the same thing as keeping them.

The amulet lay warm in the pack between us, glowing its pond-water gold, and the dead woman waited inside it with the patience of the interred, and somewhere beneath a glacier I'd never see, something that had slept through the fall of a kingdom rolled over and began, slowly, to open its eye.

"It's awake again," I repeated. Saying it out loud didn't make it smaller. Words rarely do; that's the con everyone runs on themselves.

*Yes,* she said.

"And what happens when it finishes waking up?"

Another silence, and this one had teeth in it. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped to almost nothing—that door-to-the-other-side murmur, the true dead voice, the one I'd spent my whole career pretending was just echo.

*Then somebody had better be standing at the threshold, carver. Somebody with the hands for it. Somebody with the blood for it.* The glow flared, bright enough to throw my shadow huge against the far wall, gaunt and long-armed and crowned in flicker-light, looking for all the world like something out of the Concord's warning pamphlets. *Because the door remembers who locked it. And the door remembers whose family held the key.*

The warmth faded. Not gone—banked, like a fire slept in—but dimmed to a faint pulse, a lighthouse rather than a bonfire. Outside, Tamir laughed again at something, and the wind said its piece, and supper, I reflected, was definitely ruined.

I sat on the cold slate for a long time with the amulet cradled in both palms, feeling its heartbeat against mine, and I thought about my grandmother, who taught me the spiral pattern and never said why our family's version had an extra line. I thought about the word *blood*, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. I thought about the rule—carve, listen, forget—and realized, with the calm of a woman watching the last bridge behind her burn, that forgetting had just been recalled as an option.

From below, boots on the stair, and Sefa's voice, easy and carrying: "Quentin! You alive up there, or do I get your cheese?"

I closed my hand around the warm bone and made the oldest decision in the world, the one every doomed person makes: not yet. Not yet. One more night of being nobody.

"Yeah," I called back, tucking the amulet deep into the pack, where its light bled faintly through the leather like a secret through a smile. "Keep it warm for me."

And we both pretended I meant the cheese.
