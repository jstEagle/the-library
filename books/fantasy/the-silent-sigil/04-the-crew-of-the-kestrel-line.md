## 4. The Crew of the Kestrel Line

I've been running things through Thornfell for eleven years, and in that time I've learned exactly three rules worth carving into your own ribs. One: never trust a road that's too easy. Two: the Concord doesn't need a reason to burn you, only an excuse. Three: when Quentin Villalobos says she'll meet you somewhere, build in two hours and a prayer.

She was late. Of course she was late. She'd gone back for the Ferro family—the mother, the two girls, the boy who wouldn't stop asking questions—and that meant doubling through the switchbacks above Coldmere with an Ashen patrol camped at the ford like they owned the water. Which, I suppose, they did now. They own everything. That's the whole point of them.

So when my runner, little Tam with his crooked cap and his crookier grin, came pelting down the goat trail shouting that the bone-carver had been stopped at the checkpoint below Raven's Rest, I did what any sensible captain does. I swore in three languages, put on my least suspicious face, and went to go lie to some soldiers.

* * *

The checkpoint sat across the old salt road like a scab across a wound—two wagons, a brazier, six men in ash-grey cloaks, and a sergeant with the kind of mustache that suggests he grew it to compensate for something. Our cargo that night was honest enough: dried fish, iron nails, a crate of what the manifest called "medicinal spirits" and what everyone knew was plum brandy. And underneath the brandy, wrapped in oilcloth and packed between wheels of cheese, one Quentin Villalobos, curled up small as her bones would allow, breathing through her mouth so the dust wouldn't make her sneeze.

Because that's the thing about Quentin. She can walk a refugee family over a mountain in the dark without waking a single marmot, but put her in a room with more than four people and she starts vibrating like a struck bell. The Concord has instruments now—I've seen them, brass things like tuning forks on sticks—that listen for resonance. For whatever it is she carries in her blood. So we move her like contraband, because that's what she is: the most illegal object in Vessarene, and she fits in a cheese crate.

The sergeant pulled our tarp back and looked at the crates. Looked at me. Squinted.

"Oduya," he said. "Kestrel Line. You're on the list."

"Everyone's on a list," I said pleasantly. "Question is which one."

He laughed, which surprised him more than me. Then one of his men said, "Sarge, there's someone under the—" and I want you to understand that in the next four seconds I aged a decade, invented a new religion, and prepared my last words, which were going to be something dignified about how the plum brandy was excellent and he should try some before shooting me.

But then the man coughed. Doubled over, actually. Some stomach bug going through their camp, Tam told me later; half the garrison was retching into the latrines since morning. And the sergeant, who wanted this over about as much as I did, waved us through with a grunt and a "move along, Oduya, before I remember why I don't like you."

We rolled. Nobody looked under the cheese.

Quentin climbed out at the waystation two miles on, shaking, and the first thing she said to me wasn't thank you. It was: "You shouldn't have come through the checkpoint at all. There was a ford crossing eight miles north."

"There was," I agreed. "And a patrol on it."

"You didn't know that."

"I guessed it." I shrugged. "Guessing's most of my job. The rest is lying to sergeants."

She stared at me with those grey eyes of hers, the ones that look like they're always listening to something just behind your shoulder, and she said, "You could have gotten everyone killed."

"Could have," I said. "Didn't. Wine's in the wagon. You're coming to dinner."

"I'm not."

"You are."

"I don't—" She made a gesture like she was trying to hand me something invisible. "Sefa. I'm not crew."

That word again. Crew. I've been throwing it at her for three years like a man throwing stones at a locked door, and every time she just stands there being a door.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about the Kestrel Line, because Quentin will never tell you and someone has to. We started as seven smugglers with two mules and a grudge, running salt and medicine through passes the Concord hadn't bothered to map yet. Now we're nineteen, four wagons, a boat on Lake Verre that may or may not be registered to a dead bishop, and a network of safehouses from Coldmere to the coast that runs on trust, bribes, and the fact that nobody important remembers these mountains exist.

And threaded through all of it, like wire through a wall, is one small sharp woman who carves dead people's fingerbones into keepsakes and listens to what the dead have to say about it.

I found her in Year Three, in the burned market at Lowmark, sitting on a collapsed awning carving something small and white while the ash came down like ugly snow. A woman knelt beside her, holding out a cloth-wrapped bundle—a hand, I realized later, severed from her husband by a Concord blade, kept because she couldn't bear to let all of him go. And Quentin took it, and carved, and when she handed the finished piece back—a small bird, wings folded—the woman made a sound I have never been able to describe since, except that it was the sound of someone setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it wasn't part of their body.

I asked her afterward what she charged. She said, "Nothing. Grief isn't commerce." Which is either the most beautiful or the most infuriating sentence ever spoken to a businesswoman, and I still haven't decided.

What I decided instead was that I needed her. Not her craft—though God knows the refugees we move would follow her off a cliff if she asked, and half of them think she's some kind of saint, which she hates, which is its own entertainment. I needed her because she knows roads. Not maps—roads. The ones that remember. She walks a trail once and it's in her forever, the way other people remember songs. Eleven years of occupation and the Concord still hasn't figured out how a woman with no army keeps moving families through their checkpoints like smoke through fingers.

So I offered her a cut. She refused. I offered her a bunk. She refused. I offered her wine and a seat at my fire and the basic human decency of not eating alone every night of your life, and she refused that too, but slower, and she kept showing up anyway, and after a while I stopped asking and just started setting her place.

You learn to read her, if you pay attention. The frown means she's thinking. The deeper frown means she's listening to something you can't hear, and you should probably be worried. When she goes quiet mid-sentence and touches her pocket, there's a relic talking to her, and the smart move is to change the subject to weather. And when she laughs—which happens maybe four times a year, usually at something terrible—it sounds like she surprises herself, like the laugh escaped custody.

Tam adores her. Follows her around asking questions until even I want to strangle her, and she answers every single one, flat and patient, like each question costs her nothing. It costs her plenty. I know it does. But she pays it, every time, because that's who she is: a woman who has decided that tenderness is a debt and pays it daily and refuses to call it love.

Well. Tonight I'm calling it love, whether she likes it or not.

* * *

The fire at Hollow Camp was high and the wine was bad and everyone was loud, which is how I like it. Brannick was telling the story of the fish that got away, which grows a foot every season and is currently the size of a rowboat. Marta was cheating at dice and losing anyway, which takes talent. Old Wren was asleep sitting up, which is Wren's way of attending parties.

And Quentin sat at the edge of the light with her cup untouched, knees drawn up, watching us like a scholar watches a foreign tribe.

I got up, refilled her cup myself, and sat down next to her close enough that our shoulders touched. She flinched. I pretended she didn't.

"Drink," I said.

"It's swill."

"It's swill we stole together. That makes it communion."

"That's blasphemy."

"Everything I say is blasphemy. Drink anyway."

She drank. A sip, grudging, like the wine had personally wronged her. Around the fire, Brannick launched into the ballad of the Bishop's Boat, which involves me, allegedly, impersonating clergy, which is slander, and also completely true, and the crew roared, and Quentin's mouth did a thing—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday be built.

"Sefa." Her voice was low, pitched under the noise. "Why do you keep doing this?"

"Doing what?"

"This." A small gesture at the fire, the cups, the noise. "Every time. You set a place for me. You drag me to your fires. I carve your relics, I walk your roads, I take my share. Isn't that enough? Why do you keep—" She stopped. Started again. "People who get close to me end up on lists. Concord lists. You saw tonight. If they find out what I am, everyone within arm's reach becomes evidence."

I looked at her for a long moment. Firelight does good work on her face; it softens the edges she keeps sharpened.

"Quentin," I said. "Do you know what I see when I look at you?"

"A liability."

"A woman who walked the Ferro family over a mountain in the dark. Who went back for the boy who wouldn't stop asking questions. Who crawled into a cheese crate rather than risk my wagons getting searched on her account." I turned the cup in my hands. "I see the reason half the people at this fire are alive. And I see someone so busy protecting us from herself that she's forgotten we're allowed to protect her back."

"We can't. Protecting me means—" 

"Means what? Means the Concord finds out eventually?" I leaned forward. "Honey, the Concord finds out about everything eventually. That's their whole business model. The question isn't whether the storm comes. It's whether you face it alone or with people standing next to you. And right now you're choosing alone, and I am formally informing you that this choice is stupid."

She didn't argue. That was the strange part. She just sat there, and I watched something move behind her eyes—some door opening a crack onto a room she keeps locked—and then close again, deliberate as a shutter.

"My rule," she said finally. "Carve, listen, forget. It's kept me alive eleven years."

"And how's that working out? The living part?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the fire, Marta shouted triumphantly at dice, and Wren snored in agreement with himself.

"You know what I think," I said, softer now. "I think 'forget' was never really the rule. I think it's 'don't be loved,' dressed up in work clothes. Because grief you can carry. Love gets people killed—or that's what you believe. But here's the thing, Quentin. We're already killed. Every one of us at this fire is dead the day the Concord decides we are. All we get to choose is who's beside us when it happens."

She looked at me then, fully, and for a second—one second, no more—I thought she was going to cry. Instead she picked up her cup and drained it and said, "The wine really is swill."

"Yeah," I said. "It's ours, though."

She stayed by the fire another hour. Didn't talk much. Laughed once, at something Brannick said about the fish, and covered her mouth like she'd been caught at something. And when she finally rolled into her bunk, she left her cup by the fire instead of packing it away, which from Quentin is practically a signed contract.

I sat up a while after, watching the embers, feeling pretty pleased with myself. Belonging offered. Half-refused. That's progress, in her currency. In eleven years of smuggling I've learned to count partial payments as wins.

It was near midnight when Tam shook me awake, breathless, eyes wide in the ember-light.

"Sefa. Sefa, wake up. It's Quentin's pack."

"What about it?"

"The amulet. The one she made years ago, the one she keeps wrapped in the bottom—" He was talking too fast. "It's glowing. Warm. She says—" He swallowed. "She says the voice inside it is terrified. Says it's awake again."

I stood up slowly, and every rule I ever carved into my ribs went quiet at once, because none of them had prepared me for this.

Eleven years I'd run this line believing the gods were dead, the prayers were empty, the silence was permanent. And now something in the dark was answering.

I looked toward Quentin's bunk. She was already sitting up, staring at her own hands like they belonged to someone else.

"Well," I said, to no one, to everyone, to whatever was listening. "Hell of a time to start existing again."
