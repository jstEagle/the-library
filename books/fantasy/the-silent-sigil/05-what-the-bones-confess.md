## 5. What the Bones Confess

The man came to me on a Tuesday, which I only know because Sefa had marked the day for watering the mules, and mule-watering is the closest thing my life has had to a calendar since Vessarene fell.

He arrived the way most of them do — carried, half-conscious, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's fear. Bren and old Marette hauled him into the back room of the dye-house in Lower Cindral, where the vats still stank of madder root from before the Concord banned the trade, and they laid him down on the long table like he was cargo.

Which, in fairness, was what I'd asked for. "Bring me anything strange," I'd told the line's runners, months ago, in one of my less guarded moments. Anything carved, anything old, anything with a voice still caught in it. I'd meant relics. Fingerbones in lockets, christening spoons, a wedding ring with a name scratched inside. The dead whisper through worked bone and worn metal if you hold them right, and I'd been listening to their small confessions for eleven years as a kind of penance I couldn't explain to anyone.

I had not meant a man.

"Concord," Bren said, pulling back the blanket just far enough to show me the grey coat underneath, and then, when I didn't move, the wound: a spear-gash under the ribs, ugly, days old, dressed badly and gone septic. "Defector. Walked out of the garrison at Stonehollow three nights ago asking every smuggler he could find for *the carver*. Wouldn't say why. Said he'd die before going back."

"He'll probably get his wish either way," I said.

Marette gave me a look over the dying man's body that said, very plainly, *you are being cruel because you are frightened*, which is exactly the sort of insight I pay people not to have.

"I want to go home," the man said. His voice was a ruin. He'd been shouting at checkpoints, maybe, or praying. With Concord men it's usually both. "Before — before I can't."

"Where's home?"

"Doesn't matter." He tried to lift his head and failed, and settled for finding me with his eyes instead. They were ordinary eyes. That unsettled me more than fanaticism would have. Fanatics I knew how to hate. "You're her. The carver. They talk about you even inside. The one who hears."

My spine went cold in a way I've learned not to show. Eleven years of staying invisible, and here was my name — not my name, but my *trade* — drifting around garrison barracks like smoke under a door.

"You've got the wrong woman," I said. "I carve keepsakes. Buttons. Little birds. Nothing that hears anything but the knife."

"Please." He said it like a man setting down something heavy. "I don't want a priest. Priests are the ones who taught me to be afraid of it. I want — " A cough took him, wet and long. When it released him he was smaller somehow. "I helped do something, twenty years ago. Something that shouldn't have stayed done. And now it's coming apart, and I need someone who can hear what's left of it before I'm not here to tell anyone myself."

*It's awake again.* That's what the amulet in my pack had whispered, two nights ago, glowing warm against my hip like a coal that wouldn't die. The first answered prayer in a generation, and its message had been terror.

I looked at this grey-coated stranger dying on a table in a dye-house that hadn't dyed anything in years, and I thought about all the rules I keep, and how rule number one has always been *carve, listen, forget* — and how there was nothing in any rule about a living man asking me to open him up like a reliquary while he watched.

"All right," I heard myself say. "But you tell me everything. No editing. Dead men get to be honest; it's the one perk."

Something moved across his face that might have been relief, or might just have been the sepsis.

* * *

His name was Aldous Vane. He gave it freely, which surprised me until he explained: "It stopped being worth hiding the moment I walked out those gates." He was maybe fifty, though the fever made him look older, made his face run like wax. Once I got the blanket off him entirely I saw the rest of the grey coat, the rank-braid, and the ink on his forearm — the Concord's ash-tree sigil, the one they burn into shrines after they burn the shrines themselves.

"So," I said, laying out my tools on a cloth. Knives, mostly. Scrapers. The little hooked burin I use for fine work. To anyone watching it looked like surgery, and I suppose in a way it was. "Twenty years ago you were what? Nineteen?"

"Twenty-two. New-sworn. They picked us for the Sealings because we were young enough to believe and strong enough to dig." He watched my hands with a strange calm. "You know what the Ashen Concord actually is? Everyone thinks it's a church. It's not. It's a maintenance crew. We're the people who came after the gods died to make sure they stayed buried."

"They died," I said. It wasn't quite a question. Eleven years I'd built my whole cosmology on that word.

"The old gods? No." He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and I waited it out with my jaw tight. "Nothing dies that cleanly. They were *sealed*. Walled up behind the Silent Sigil by their own door-wardens, because they'd grown — wrong. Hungry. The war wasn't gods versus men, carver. It was the sane part of the world locking away the mad part of heaven. The Concord's founders were the wardens' servants. Their job was to guard the door and never speak of it. Somewhere in the last twenty years we forgot we were guards and decided we were kings."

I kept my hands moving. Scrape, clean, oil the blade. Work is the best lie I know.

"And you dug," I prompted.

"We dug." His eyes went somewhere else. Somewhere cold and blue, I'd have bet money. "There's a glacier above the Vale of Hesh. Underneath it, an antechamber. Older than any kingdom. And in the wall of it, the Sigil itself — a door so thoroughly unmade that it isn't stone anymore, isn't anything anymore. Just a shaped absence. You put your hand where the door should be and feel *nothing*, and the nothing has edges. Twenty of us spent a season carving the closing-words deeper. Reinforcing the seal. Chanting the litany the wardens left behind." He swallowed. "We sang ourselves hoarse telling the dark to stay dark, and then we went home and got medals, and nobody ever spoke of it again except in whispers, and eventually not even in whispers."

"Why are you speaking of it now?"

"Because three weeks ago the whispers started coming *back*." His hand found my wrist — grip weak, but insistent. "Not to us. To — things. Relics. Shrines. The old stones. Men in my company started having dreams in a language none of us speak. The resonance-readers — you know what those are?"

"Instruments," I said carefully. "The inquisitors carry them. Brass and crystal. They click near old shrines."

"They click near *resonance*. Near anything the Sigil touched, or anything descended from the people who built it. And three weeks ago every reader in Stonehollow started clicking at once, at nothing, at the walls, at the air." His grip tightened. "The seal is failing, carver. Whatever your gods did or didn't do, whatever prayers got answered this month — it's the door. It's thinning. And the Concord doesn't know how to fix it. All they know how to do is burn things, so that's what they'll do. They'll burn every relic, every shrine, every bloodline that resonates, trying to silence a sound that's coming from under a glacier."

I should have sent him away then. Or let him die quietly and reported nothing. Those were the options available to a sensible woman whose entire survival strategy was *be no one*.

Instead I picked up the burin.

"How does the carving work?" he asked, watching me position his shin — bare now, the grey trousers rolled to the knee, the skin there pale and unremarkable. A soldier's leg. A lifetime of marching in it.

"I don't fully know," I admitted. "It came to me young. My mother's family did it too, or something like it. You work the bone — any bone, but bone that loved somebody works best — and you cut the shape of what it remembers. And while the cutting's fresh, the memory speaks. Through the piece. To whoever holds it." I hesitated. "Usually the dead. Usually small things. Last words. Names of grandchildren. Where they hid the deed to the house."

"And mine?" He almost smiled. "What will my bones remember?"

"That depends what you did with them." I set the blade's edge against his shin, below the wound, where the bone ran close under the skin. "This will hurt. Not the cutting — the surface barely feels it. The rest of it, I can't say. Some of them come back from the listening shaken. One old woman kissed me. One threw a chair."

"Do it," he said. "Whatever's in me, I've been carrying it for twenty years and it weighs more than the leg does."

So I cut.

I won't pretend I went in calm. My hands were steady — my hands are always steady, it's the one inheritance I trust — but behind them I was reciting my rules like a rosary. *Carve. Listen. Forget.* Small pieces. Small voices. Never anything big enough to matter.

The first strokes were shallow guide-lines, the geometry every carver knows without knowing how she knows it — spirals within spirals, the pattern that shows up in Vessarene's oldest ruins and in the grain of willow burls and, if you believe certain drunks in certain taverns, in the whorls of your own fingerprints. Aldous flinched once, then went still, breathing slow, sweat standing out on his forehead.

And the bone began to speak.

I felt it before I heard it — the way I always do, a pressure behind the sternum, a sense of a room next door suddenly occupied. But it was different this time. Wrong-shaped. When I listen to a dead woman's fingerbone, the voice is *her*: small, particular, gone. This was —

Layers.

That's the only word. His shinbone held layers, like sedimentary rock, like tree rings, and each layer had a voice, and beneath the ordinary strata of a life — a boy falling from a fence, a mother's kitchen, a first posting, cold mornings at Stonehollow — there was something older and colder and vast, something that had soaked into him the season he spent underground, singing at a door that wasn't a door.

I heard the chant. In his own young voice, twenty years ago, hoarse and terrified and obedient: the litany of closing, syllables I'd never learned but recognized the way you recognize your own face in a warped mirror. I heard the antechamber — blue ice-light, breath steaming, twenty grey coats kneeling in a half-circle. And I heard, underneath the chanting, the thing they chanted *at*.

Not a roar. Not a scream. A patience.

Something behind the absence, pressing its palm — if it had palms — flat against the inside of the nothing, waiting for the song to wear thin. And it *had* worn thin. I could hear the difference between what the seal had been and what it was, the way you can hear a rope fray strand by strand if you hold it to your ear. The Sigil was leaking. Had been leaking for years. And everything I'd felt two nights ago — the warm amulet, the answered prayer, the voice saying *it's awake* — slotted into place with a click I felt in my teeth.

Then the deepest layer opened, and it wasn't about the door at all.

It was about names. Rosters. Aldous Vane, age twenty-two, holding a ledger in a freezing archive, copying out lists in his careful new-sworn hand. Lists of families. And beside each family, a notation in red: *lineage confirmed. resonance probable. disposition:* — and then one of three words. *Watched. Scattered. Ended.*

Door-wardens' descendants. The bloodlines of the people who had built the Sigil and sealed the gods behind it. Tracked for twenty years by the institution that grew out of their own servants. Watched, scattered, ended.

I heard him copy a particular page. I heard the name at the top of it, and the world went narrow and ringing, the way it does in the moment before a rockfall.

*Villalobos.*

My name. My mother's name, her mother's name, the name I haven't spoken aloud since the capital fell. On a Concord roster, in Aldous Vane's handwriting, twenty years ago. *Lineage confirmed. Resonance high. Disposition:* — and the word after that was smudged, water-damaged, illegible, and I have never in my life wanted to read a single word so badly.

I came back to the room with my knees on the floor and my hands shaking around the burin, and Aldous was looking at me with terrible clarity, the fever burned down to coals by whatever the listening had cost us both.

"You found it," he said. "The ledger."

"You *copied the rosters*," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "You wrote down my family. You wrote down every bloodline that could hear the door."

"Yes." He didn't flinch. I gave him credit for that. "And then I spent twenty years making sure some of those lines stayed ahead of the readers. You think defectors just happen, carver? You think a man walks out of Stonehollow by accident? I've been steering people along your roads for years. Whispering to smugglers. Losing paperwork. Every name on those lists that's still breathing is partly my doing, and I needed it to be somebody's doing, and tonight I need it to be *said*."

"Why?" The word cracked. "Why tell me the rest? Why the leg, why any of it?"

"Because the seal is failing and the Concord is going to respond by burning everything that resonates, starting with the bloodlines, and the only people who can do anything about a failing seal are the people whose ancestors built it." He reached out and gripped my arm — the arm holding the burin — and pulled, gently, until the blade's edge rested against his shin again, against the finished spiral. "Look at the last layer. Look at what I copied *under* the roster. Go on."

I looked.

Beneath the list of hunted families, in his younger hand, someone senior had written the founding text — the Concord's true charter, the one that lived in archives and never in sermons. Aldous had committed it to memory, and memory had sunk into marrow, and marrow had waited twenty years for a listener. I read it in the bone the way you read a scar:

*The Sigil answers only to warden-blood. Should the seal weaken, the door may be re-carved — closed, or opened — by a descendant of the nine wardens. Find them. Guard them. Or end them.*

Nine families. Nine bloodlines. And mine among them.

"There's a map," Aldous whispered. His grip on my wrist was failing; the fever was climbing back up, dragging him under. "Under the floor of my old quarters at Stonehollow. Third flagstone from the hearth. Routes, dates, the glacier, everything. Get it before the readers do." His eyes found mine and held them, and what was in them wasn't fear anymore. It was arithmetic. A man spending his last hour buying something. "Villalobos. That's the name, isn't it? From the roster. The smudged line."

"Don't," I said.

"*Disposition: lost during the fall of the capital. Presumed ended.*" A ghost of a smile. "Best mistake I ever copied." Then, softer, the words coming slower, each one a coin paid out: "It can be re-carved. Closed or opened. You understand? *Or opened.* The wardens sealed the gods because the gods had gone hungry — but hunger can change. Things behind a door for twenty years aren't the things that went in. Maybe they're worse. Maybe they're sorry. Nobody knows, because nobody with the blood has ever stood in front of it and listened." His breath hitched. "Until now."

The room tilted. I put a hand flat on the table to keep the floor where it belonged.

Eleven years of *carve, listen, forget*, and the forgetting had just been revoked — retroactively, comprehensively, by a dying man's shinbone. I wasn't a smuggler who happened to have a strange ear. I was inventory. I was a loose thread in the Concord's oldest ledger, a bloodline marked *presumed ended* by a clerical error, walking around with hands that could re-carve a god's prison door. Every checkpoint I'd ever slipped through, I'd slipped through by luck and ignorance, and ignorance had just expired.

And underneath the terror, quiet as a leak under ice, something else. Because the amulet in my pack had said *it's awake*, and I'd assumed the voice meant danger. But Aldous's bones said something the amulet hadn't: that the thing awake behind the door had been sung at by twenty frightened boys, and had waited, patient, for someone with the blood to come stand at the threshold and ask it a question.

Nobody knows what it wants. Nobody's asked.

"Stay with me," I said to Aldous. "The map. Third flagstone. What else — "

But his grip had gone slack, and his breathing had gone long and rattling, and when I pressed my ear to his chest the layers were silent. Whatever his bones had been saving, they'd spent it all in one evening.

I sat there a while. The dye-house creaked. Somewhere outside, Bren was arguing with a mule, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, stubbornly, as if a roster of my extinction hadn't just surfaced in a dead man's leg.

Then I cleaned my blades, rolled the cloth, packed the tools — and paused with my hand resting on my pack, feeling through the canvas the faint, unmistakable warmth of a little carved amulet that had no business being warm.

*It's awake again,* it had said. Two nights ago I'd heard it as a warning.

Now, with warden-blood cooling in my ears and a Concord roster folded into my skull, I understood it had been something else entirely. Not a warning.

A summons.

I stood up, shouldered the pack, and went to find Sefa — because whether I liked it or not, and I did not, there was a map under a flagstone in a Concord garrison, and I was apparently the only person alive who could read what waited at the end of it.

The trap had been built around my life before I was born. Tonight I'd finally heard its teeth.

The only question left was which direction to walk into it.
