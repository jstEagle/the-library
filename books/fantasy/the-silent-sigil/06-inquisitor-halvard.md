## 6. Inquisitor Halvard

There is a particular kind of silence that follows an inquisitor into a conquered province. It is not the silence of peace. Peace is loud, if you listen long enough—children arguing, bread being torn, someone's grandmother complaining about the weather. The silence that follows me is the other kind. The held-breath kind. Doors close as I pass. Conversations die mid-sentence like birds hitting windows. I have spent twenty years learning to walk through that silence without flinching, and I would like to report that I have succeeded.

I have not. Every closed door still lands somewhere behind my eyes, a small dark bruise.

They call me the chief inquisitor of the Ashen Concord now. Halvard Reine, Warden of Orthodoxy, Bearer of the Grey Seal, and eleven other titles I could recite in my sleep because I helped write them myself, back when titles felt like armor instead of chains. The Concord gave me a rank, a retinue, a warrant with my name stamped in ash-grey wax, and a mandate: find the anomalous resonances in Thornfell. Find whatever is answering prayers out here in the cold teeth of the mountains. Extinguish it.

Anomalous resonances. I chose those words myself, two years ago, for a classification ledger no one outside the Office of Inquiry will ever read. It was easier than writing what I actually meant. What I actually meant was: *the old gods are waking up, and we told everyone they were dead, and if they are not dead then everything we burned was murder and not mercy.* You cannot put that in a ledger. So I wrote *anomalous resonances* and slept about as well as you'd expect.

The road into Thornfell took nine days from the garrison at Lowmark, and every one of them was grey. Ash falls in this country even when nothing is burning—a fine pale silt that settles on shoulders and ledges and the lids of the dead, so that the whole province looks like it has been dusted by a baker who hates his work. My escort hated it. The horses hated it. I found it honest. This is what we made, I kept thinking as it settled on my sleeves. Look at it. Look at what we made.

We made it, I should say, with the best intentions. That is not a joke. I know how it sounds—the gallows humor of a man who has signed too many warrants—but I mean it plainly. When Vessarene fell, the capital was drowning in gods. Every street had its shrine, every family its patron bone-saint, every child a relic on a cord around its neck telling it things no child should be told by anything, let alone something dead. The bone-carvers heard voices in their work. The priests claimed to speak for powers older than the kingdom. And the kingdom starved while its lords consulted entrails and argued over which god owned which river.

The Concord said: one faith, or none. Order first, salvation later, maybe. We were young and the world was bleeding and someone had to say *stop*. Someone had to burn the shrines before the shrines burned the last of us. I believed it. I believe parts of it still, which is worse than believing all of it, because belief you can lose cleanly is a kindness, and mine comes apart in pieces, each piece leaving splinters.

Here is what nobody wrote in our founding documents: you cannot burn a thousand years of gods without burning the people who loved them. The faithful did not stand aside politely while we purified their altars. They hid their relics in floorboards. They taught their children prayers in whispers. They became, overnight, exactly what we said they were—heretics, smugglers, enemies—and we congratulated ourselves on our foresight. We built the heresy with our own hands and then hunted it for existing.

And the bone-carvers. The bone-carvers most of all.

* * *

Her name was Quentin Villalobos, and when she was nine years old she could make a dead man laugh.

That is not a figure of speech. In the carving quarter of the capital, in the long low workshop that smelled of marrow-oil and cedar shavings, there was a girl who took the fingerbones people brought her—keepsakes, mostly, grief made portable—and carved them into little wonders. A bird. A boat. A door, once, tiny and hinged, that opened onto nothing at all. And while she worked, she talked to them. Not *at* them. To them. She would tilt the little bone bird toward her ear like it was whispering, and then she would look up at the grieving customer and say, "He wants you to know he hid money in the flour jar," or "She says the blue coat, not the grey one," and the customers would cry and laugh and pay triple, and the workshop's owner pretended very hard not to notice, because a girl like that was worth more than any license.

I was the owner's son. I was twelve, solemn, already collecting doctrines the way other boys collected river stones, and I decided within a week of meeting her that Quentin Villalobos was either a fraud or a miracle, and that either way I intended to find out which.

It took me three years to learn the answer, which was: neither. She was a listener. That was all. The bones spoke—or something spoke through the bones, she never claimed to know which, and it drove me mad that she didn't care—and she listened the way other people breathe, constantly and without thinking it remarkable. "You hear your mother's voice when you read her letters," she told me once, exasperated, carving a sparrow out of some dead soldier's knuckle. "This is just that. With more steps."

With more steps. As if the veil between the living and the dead were a staircase and she simply knew where the landing was.

We were friends the way only children in workshops can be friends: entirely, unsupervised, built out of shared secrets and stolen plums and the unspoken treaty that I would not tell anyone what she heard and she would not mock how seriously I took my theology books. She called me Halvard-the-Hedge-Priest because I quoted scripture at her while she worked and she quoted the bones back at me, and for a few years the two voices got along fine. The dead, according to Quentin, mostly wanted small things. Rest. Acknowledgment. Someone to remember the flour jar. A god, she said, was just a dead person everyone remembered at once, and she said it lightly, the way you'd describe weather, and I went home that night and lay awake feeling the foundations of the world shift under me like ice on a lake.

Then the war came. Then the fall. Then the Concord, and the purges, and the lists.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because I have spent twenty years being imprecise about it in public and I am tired. When the Office of Inquiry drew up the registers of resonance-gifted persons—carvers, listeners, shrine-keepers—I gave them names. Not hers. I want that recorded somewhere, even if it's only here, in the privacy of my own skull where no confession counts. I withheld her name. I sat across a desk from a superior whose face I can no longer recall and I lied, smoothly, completely, and I told myself it was mercy, and it was, and it was also cowardice wearing mercy's coat, because if I had named her she would have been registered, watched, possibly spared through compliance. If I had named her, she would have been *somewhere findable*. Instead she vanished into the mountains with the rest of the unregistered, and I have been looking for her ever since, in the way you look for something you are terrified to find.

Every list of unidentified dead from the fall—I checked. Every refugee manifest. Every smuggler interrogation, and I have conducted hundreds, I always asked, quietly, at the end, almost apologetically: *any carvers come through? Anyone who talks to bones?* The smugglers always said no. Smugglers always say no. But once, four years ago, a woman with frostbitten ears said no in a way that made me think of Quentin—same flat refusal, same set of the jaw—and I released her without charge, off the record, and told myself it meant nothing, and thought about it for four years.

So. Anomalous resonances in Thornfell. Prayers answered where no prayers should be answerable. Relics glowing warm in packs, if the fragmentary reports are true—which they appear to be, because the reports keep coming, and they keep agreeing, and coincidence does not survive agreement.

Something out there is listening again. Something out there is *answering*. And the Office wants it found and extinguished before the provinces start praying aloud, because a population that prays aloud is a population that remembers, and a population that remembers is a population that asks why we burned the shrines, and we do not have an answer to that question that survives daylight.

I came to find the source. I know what the source is likely to be. I have known for twenty years.

I hope I'm wrong.

* * *

The garrison commander at Thornfell's edge met me with the obsequiousness of a man who has read my file and the wariness of a man who has heard the rumors, both of which were accurate, so fair enough. He spread his maps. He showed me the villages where the reports originated—a scatter of hamlets along the old pilgrim roads, places so small the Concord's census calls them "residual settlements," as though the people in them were a rounding error.

"Resonance events cluster here, here, and here," he said, tapping with a blunt finger. "Warm relics. Voices. One case of—" he consulted his notes with visible embarrassment— "one case of a prayer answered. A woman asked for her son's fever to break. It broke."

"And the boy?"

"Lived."

"Hm." I studied the map. Three clusters, strung along the mountain passes like beads on a wire. Whatever moved between them moved the way smugglers move—by night, by forgotten roads, avoiding every checkpoint we maintain. Which meant the source was mobile. Which meant the source was *guiding* someone. Resonance doesn't travel; people do. Something—or someone—with the gift was walking refugees through my mountains.

"You've conscripted local guides?" I asked.

"Those we could hold."

"And the ones you couldn't?"

He looked uncomfortable. "Gone, mostly. The mountain folk vanish when we come up the valley. Whole villages empty in a night. They have roads we don't have on any map. Old roads. Pre-Conquest."

Of course they do. The old roads remember. That was the phrase the shrine-priests used, back in the capital, back before—the old roads remember, the old stones remember, everything remembers except the people burning it. We confiscated the maps of Vessarene's royal cartographers during the first year. Burned most. Filed the rest. Somewhere in the Office archives there is a drawer full of pre-fall survey charts showing every goat path and pilgrim track in these mountains, and I could requisition them, and I have not, and I ask myself why, and the answer arrives immediately and unwelcome: because using the dead kingdom's own maps to hunt its survivors feels like a desecration even by my standards, and I apparently still have standards, God help me.

I interviewed witnesses for six days. I am good at interviews—that is the part of this work I can still do without flinching, the part that is craft rather than cruelty. You sit across from frightened people and you ask small questions and you listen to what they don't say. A tavern-keeper described a woman who passed through buying rope and dried fish, paying in old Vessarene coin, traveling with a family of strangers she clearly wasn't related to. A shepherd mentioned lights on the ridge at night—not fires, he insisted, *lights*, moving in single file, low and careful. A grandmother, questioned twice because her answers contradicted themselves, finally admitted—after I promised, sincerely, on my seal, that no harm would come to anyone she named, a promise I have learned to make carefully because I intend to keep it—that there was a carver. A bone-carver. Who had come down from the high passes before the war. Whose family had lived in the capital. Who had, the grandmother said, watching my reaction with the shrewdness of her years, "gone quiet after the fall, but not gone."

I stood in that smoky room and felt the ground tilt.

A carver. From the capital. Quiet, not gone. Walking strangers through the mountains by night. Leaving answered prayers behind her like breadcrumbs.

I did not ask the grandmother for a name. I could have pressed. I had the authority; I had the techniques; I have used them, and I am not going to pretend otherwise, not even here, not even privately, because pretending is how the rot starts. But I sat with my hands flat on the table and I did not press, and afterward I walked out into the falling ash and stood there until my escort sent someone to check whether I'd frozen.

Because here is the arithmetic I cannot escape. If the source in Thornfell is Quentin Villalobos—if the girl who made dead men laugh is now the woman keeping refugees alive in the high snows—then she is guilty of precisely one crime under Concord law: existing with the wrong gift. She has smuggled no weapons. She has raised no rebellion. She walks families through frozen passes because no one else will, and the dead talk to her because they always have, and the prayers getting answered are not hers—they're the desperate ones of people she guides, finally reaching something, and I cannot even swear that she knows why. She never cared why. That was the thing about her. She listened the way rivers run downhill. She never once asked what was on the other side of the staircase.

But the law does not care. The law I serve does not care, and I have fed it enough names to know exactly what it does with them. Registration. Interrogation. The grey rooms. And if the resonances continue—if the old powers are truly stirring, if the seal the founders threw over the whole haunted architecture of Vessarene is failing—then she is not merely a criminal to my Office. She is evidence. Evidence gets handled. Evidence gets contained. And I have seen what containment means when the evidence is a person, and I swore, twenty years ago over a lie I told to protect her, that I would never be the one to—

And yet.

And yet the reports keep coming. The resonances are strengthening. Last month a patrol found a wayside shrine that had been rebuilt—rebuilt, in occupied territory, openly—and inside it a carved reliquary warm as a living hand. The men who touched it reported hearing, distinctly, a chorus. Nine voices, they said. Counting. Reciting. Names.

Nine names. Nine voices. Counting.

I have read the founding purge records more times than any living member of the Concord, because someone had to archive them and I volunteered, in one of those decisions that reveals more than it conceals. The founding purge. The night we burned the capital's great shrine-row and executed the priesthood of the old rites. There were nine senior priests who refused to flee. Nine. Their names are in my archive, in my handwriting, catalogued with the meticulous care of a man filing his own guilt where he can keep an eye on it.

Nine voices, counting. Reciting names.

If the dead of the founding purge are speaking again—if they have waited twenty years in the dark and are now, through some carver's patient hands, reading their killers' names aloud into the cold air of Thornfell—then this is no longer a matter of anomalous resonances. This is a reckoning assembling itself in my jurisdiction, and I am the instrument my institution built to stop reckonings, and I do not know, sitting here tonight with the ash settling on my window ledge, whether I can do it.

Whether I can do it *again*, I mean. Whether I ever could. Whether everything since the fall has been a man running downhill ahead of an avalanche of his own making, calling it duty because duty has such good footing.

Tomorrow I ride deeper into the mountains. The trail is fresh—a family of seven guided through the Kestrel Pass three nights ago, and the garrison's best tracker swears the guide doubled back through Hollow Bridge, which means she is still in the region, still working, still leaving warmth in relics and silence behind closed doors wherever she passes.

I told my escort to prepare for a long campaign. I told the Office, in my dispatch, that I anticipated resolution within the month. Both statements were professionally sound and personally hollow, because the truth—the truth I will write nowhere but carry everywhere—is that I do not want to resolve this. I want to be wrong. I want the carver in the high passes to be anyone, anyone at all, some stranger with the same gift and none of my memories, so that when I finally stand in front of her with my warrant and my seal and my twenty years of ash, I will not have to look into her face and see the workshop, the cedar shavings, the little bone door that opened onto nothing, the friend who called me Hedge-Priest and never once lied to me.

I hope I'm wrong.

I have never less believed my own hope.
