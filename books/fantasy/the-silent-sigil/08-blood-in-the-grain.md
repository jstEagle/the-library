## 8. Blood in the Grain

I want it on the record that I did not go looking for trouble that night. Trouble and I have an understanding: it stays in the valleys, I stay on the ridgelines, and we only meet when the Concord gets ambitious. But the Kestrel Line was camped in a hollow below the Widow's Tooth with a fire banked low and a watch rotation I'd set myself, and I woke in the black hours because Quentin's bedroll was empty.

That, in itself, wasn't strange. Quentin sleeps the way a knife sleeps in a drawer—lightly, and ready to be elsewhere. Half the time I'd swear she doesn't sleep at all, just stands very still in the dark and calls it rest. She takes the middle watch more often than anyone should volunteer, and she's never once explained why, and I've never once asked, because that was the arrangement we had: I don't pry, she doesn't lie, and between the two of us we maintain a fiction that we're simple women running a simple business.

The fiction was getting harder to maintain. It had been getting harder since the defector.

* * *

His name had been Corvess, and he'd lasted four days after we pulled him off the pass road with Concord grey still smoking out of his wounds. Four days of fever and confessions, and Quentin sat with him through all of it, carving, because that's what she does—she carves bone and she hears the dead, and I have known this about her for six years the way you know a river is deep: you don't test it, you just don't let the children swim there.

But Corvess's last day, I'd come into the tent and found her white as glacier milk, and he was gripping her wrist hard enough to bruise, saying words I only caught the tail of. *Door-wardens. The seal is failing. You're of the blood.* And Quentin looking at him like a woman watching a trap close on her own leg.

He died at dusk. She burned the body herself, which she never does—usually the mountain does that work for us—and she didn't talk for two days after, which for Quentin is a silence so total the crew started leaving extra food by her tent the way you leave offerings. Which, I realized later, is more or less what it was.

So when I woke to her empty bedroll, I didn't panic. I got up, wrapped my coat over my shift, and followed the faint light leaking between the rocks at the hollow's edge, telling myself I was just doing rounds. Telling myself a lot of things, that night.

* * *

She was sitting with her back against the stone, sleeves shoved to the elbow, a knife in her right hand and her left forearm laid bare across her knee. And she was carving herself.

Not cutting—carving. There's a difference, and my stomach understood it before my head did. Cuts go across. This went in. She was lifting fine curls of something off her own skin—skin, and under it, in the firelight, lines that glowed faintly the way the amulet in her pack had glowed two weeks ago, the one that had made her go strange and quiet, the one I'd pretended not to notice because that was the arrangement.

The lines weren't random. They were geometric, deliberate, a lattice of interlocking marks that made my eyes hurt to follow, like a stairway drawn by someone who'd only ever heard of stairs.

"Quentin," I said.

She didn't jump. That's the thing about her—she never startles, as if some part of her is always listening to a room before she enters it. She just went still, the knife hovering, and said, without turning, "You should go back to bed, Sefa."

"You're carving your own arm."

"I'm aware."

"With what, exactly? Because from here it looks like you're opening a vein with a chisel."

"Blood-ink." She turned then, and I wish she hadn't, because her face was the worst part. Not guilty. Not ashamed. *Resolved.* Quentin's face does about four expressions on a good year, and this one I'd only seen once before, the night she pulled nine people out of a burning waystation and came back for a tenth that wasn't there. "It has to be blood-ink. It has to be me. The sigil won't bind to anything less."

"The sigil." I came closer, because my legs had apparently decided to commit treason without consulting me. "The Sigil. The Silent Sigil. The sealed door that the Concord burned a kingdom to keep shut. *That* sigil."

"Yes."

"And you're carving it into your arm in the dark like a girl scratching a boy's name into a desk."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Quentin's smiles are rationed like wartime sugar, and even her almost-smiles come at strange moments. "That's roughly the size of it."

I crouched down across the fire from her, close enough to see the work properly, and I made myself look. The lines were beautiful. That's what I couldn't get past, in that first minute—she was doing violence to her own body and it came out *beautiful*, fine as frost-fern, precise as a lock mechanism, which I suppose is exactly what it was. "How long?" I asked. "How long have you known?"

She was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Since Corvess," she said finally. "Known. Suspected since the amulet woke up. Suspected since I was nine, if I'm honest, in the way you suspect a thing you've spent your whole life not saying." She set the knife down across her knee, deliberate, the way you disarm yourself in front of someone you're about to ask something of. "You want the whole of it?"

"I want the whole of it," I said, "and I want you to understand that what happens after depends heavily on the whole of it."

* * *

So she told me. All of it, there in the hollow, in a voice so level it was clearly costing her everything she had to keep it that way.

She told me about the bone-carving, which I knew—carve, listen, forget, her three commandments, though she'd only ever admitted to the first two out loud. She told me about the amulet, the answered prayer, the voice inside it saying *it's awake again.* She told me about Corvess, and the sealing of the Sigil twenty years ago, and the bloodline—the door-wardens of Vessarene, the family that built the lock and held the key, hunted to extinction by the Concord's founding purge, except not quite extinct, except one girl smuggled out through the Thornfell passes who grew up to be very good at moving quietly and hearing the dead.

"And the dead," she said, and here her voice finally cracked, just a hairline, "have started asking for things. Not rest. Not remembrance. *Justice.* Nine names, Sefa. They say them in chorus now, through everything I own. And the only way they get their justice is if the Sigil opens, and the only way the Sigil opens is a door-warden's blood in the grain of the carving, and I am apparently the last grain there is."

I sat with that. The fire had burned down to a red eye, and somewhere up-slope one of the mules was dreaming loudly, and I found I was gripping my own knee hard enough to leave marks.

"Open it," I said. "You mean open it. The door between worlds. The one thing the Concord has spent twenty years and a hundred thousand lives making sure stays shut."

"I mean open it," she said, "and let the old gods come through and judge the people who did this. That's what the dead want. That's what the nine names are for. They don't want to escape, Sefa. They want their killers *accounted for.*"

"And then what? The gods sweep through, put the world to rights, pat you on the head—"

"And then I seal it again. Forever. That's the warden's part. Open, and close. That's the whole of the office." She looked at her arm, at the half-born lattice glowing faintly under her skin. "The defector's bones were clear on that. The door isn't meant to stay open. It's meant to be opened *once*, by someone with the standing to do it, and then closed by the same hand."

"And the Concord? The inquisitors? Halvard?" I'd heard the name from her exactly once, drunk, three years ago, and never again, but I'd been running the Kestrel Line long enough to know which Concord hounds had capital accents and old grudges. "They're in the mountains. You know they're in the mountains. Every checkpoint we've slipped since the amulet woke has been a little tighter than the last. You're carving yourself into a beacon, Quentin. You're carving yourself into the one thing they're looking for."

"I know."

"You *know.*"

"I know," she said, "and I've done the arithmetic every way it can be done, and every way ends with me doing this. If I run, the seal fails anyway—the dead said it's failing, Corvess said it's failing, and when it fails on its own it doesn't open for the nine names. It opens for whatever's been pressing on the other side for twenty years with no warden to shape the passage. If I turn myself in, they take my blood and my craft and they open it *their* way, for their gods, which are the kind that eat. And if I do nothing—" she spread her hands, and the glow caught the movement, "—then I'm just the last of a line that let the world rot because staying invisible was comfortable."

"And the crew?" My voice came out lower than I meant it to. "Me? Tam and Ivo and the twins and little Wren who calls you auntie because you taught her to whistle? Do they get a line in your arithmetic?"

The silence that followed was the longest of the night.

"They're in it," she said finally. "Every version. That's the part I—" She stopped. Started again. Quentin does not stumble over words; she stumbles over feelings, and you can watch it happen, like watching someone navigate a room in the dark by memory. "I didn't tell you because telling you makes you complicit, and not telling you makes you safe, and I chose wrong, didn't I. I've been choosing wrong for weeks. Maybe years."

"Yes," I said.

"Yes," she agreed, and there it was—the almost-smile again, bitter this time, aimed at herself. "You're not going to turn me in."

"Don't flatter yourself. I'm deciding."

"You've been deciding since you sat down. You sat down across from me instead of behind me. You always position yourself where you can see a person's hands." She nodded at my own hands, white-knuckled on my knee. "You've been reading my hands for six years, Sefa. You already know what I'm going to do. You're just deciding how angry to be about it."

I wanted to throw something at her. I want that noted too. Instead I said, "Tell me about the arm. The rest of it. What happens when it's finished."

"It binds me to the door. Wherever it is—between, the dead call it—I'll be able to find it, hold it, open it. It also—" she hesitated, "—marks me. To the Concord's instruments, I'll resonate like a struck bell. There's no version of this where I'm invisible anymore. That's the price of the arm. I stop being a ghost and become a target."

"And you did it anyway. Alone. At three in the morning. With a paring knife."

"It's a carving knife," she said, with dignity, "and I was going to tell you."

"When?"

"When it was done."

"*Quentin.*"

"I know," she said, and for the first time all night she sounded tired—not the body-tiredness she wears like armor, but the other kind, the kind underneath. "I know. I've spent my whole life being the only person who has to know things. It's not a virtue. It's just a habit. And habits are the hardest bones to carve."

* * *

We sat there while the fire died down to embers and the sky over the Widow's Tooth went from black to the bruised grey that comes before dawn. I made her show me the whole arm, and I made her tell me what each stage of the carving would cost, and she did—pain, fever, the binding, the resonance, all of it laid out flat and unadorned, because she'd understood by then that the price of not turning her in was the truth, all of it, on demand, forever.

And I told her things too. Not my secrets—I'd get to mine, God help me, but not that night; a woman can only survive one legacy per campfire. What I told her was simpler and harder.

"I don't care about the gods," I said. "I don't care about the nine names, and I don't care about Vessarene, may it rest, and I don't care about justice if justice means I bury more of my crew. I care about eleven people in this hollow and a mule line and a business I built out of nothing with my own two hands, and you, you infuriating woman, you put yourself at the center of a war without asking any of us if we were in."

"You'd have said no."

"I'd have said *let me think about it,*" I said, "which is worse for you, isn't it. Because then you'd have had to wait. You'd have had to sit with someone else knowing, someone else who could tell you you're being a fool, and you'd have had to decide anyway, with witnesses." I poked the embers with a stick, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "That's the real reason you didn't tell me. Not to keep me safe. So no one could talk you out of it. So you could be the lonely hero instead of the loved fool."

She didn't say anything for a long moment. The glow in her arm had faded to almost nothing, just a ghost of lattice under the skin, and she was rubbing her thumb over it the way you touch a bruise to check it's still there.

"You're not wrong," she said at last. "I've been alone so long I mistake it for competence."

"There it is," I said. "Put that on your headstone. *She mistook it for competence.*"

That got a real smile out of her, brief as a struck match, and I counted it a victory, and hated that I was counting anything as a victory while she sat there carved half-full of apocalypse.

Here's what I decided, in the grey before dawn, and I decided it the way I decide everything—fast, and then spend years justifying it: I wasn't going to turn her in. Not because the plan was good—it wasn't, it was terrible, it was the worst plan I'd heard since Ivo suggested smuggling brandy in a hollowed-out corpse, and that one at least had the advantage of being funny. I wasn't turning her in because the Concord's justice and the old gods' justice were both going to grind up the same people I loved either way, and the only variable I controlled was who stood next to Quentin when it happened.

"Conditions," I said.

She raised an eyebrow. Quentin's eyebrows are very expressive for a woman who otherwise runs on rationed expressions.

"One. You finish this with the crew, not around the crew. Tam and Ivo know by week's end, or I tell them myself, and I'm less gentle than you. Two. The arm waits until we're at the glacier, not before—I'm not hauling a resonating beacon through three Concord checkpoints because you couldn't stand to wait a fortnight. Three—" and here I leaned forward, and made her look at me, and I watched her look, because she's bad at it and it matters that she practice, "—you never lie to me again. Not to protect me. Not to keep the arithmetic clean. Not even by omission, not even the kind lies, not ever. I'd rather have the truth ugly at midnight than the lie comfortable at dawn. That's the price. That's the whole price. You pay it and the Kestrel Line rides with you to the end of whatever this is."

The dawn was coming up properly now, grey-gold through the pass, and her face in that light was doing something I'd never seen it do—coming apart, quietly, the way ice comes apart on a river in spring, no drama, just a slow structural surrender.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "The truth is going to get worse before it gets better. The dead are going to say things through me that you'll wish you'd never heard. There are things I know about people in this crew—the bones tell me things—that I've carried for years because carrying them was kinder than saying them."

"Then carry them to me," I said. "That's what I'm for. I'm not just the mule logistics, Quentin. I'm the place where the heavy things go."

She laughed then—actually laughed, wet-eyed and disbelieving, at the fire and the dawn and the whole ridiculous shape of our lives—and she put out her hand, the uncarved one, and I took it, and we shook on it like smugglers, because that's what we were.

"One more condition," she said, as we got up to wake the camp. "When this is over—if it's ever over—you teach me how to be part of something. I'm told it can be learned, like whistling."

"Wren can teach you the whistling," I said. "I'll handle the rest. Now get up, door-warden. We've got a lie-in to skip and a war to annoy."

We were breaking camp before the sun cleared the Tooth. And it was Tam, rolling up the map with his sleeves full of morning, who said the thing that put the cold back down my spine for good:

"Road's got visitors, Captain. Rider coming up the pass, alone, no colours. But he rides like the Concord teaches them to ride, and he's headed straight for us."

Quentin went very still beside me, the way she does when she's listening to something the rest of us can't hear.

"That'll be for me," she said.
