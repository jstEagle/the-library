## 9. The Concord Comes to Thornfell

The mountain does not want us. That was the first thing I understood about Thornfell, and the last thing I would ever be permitted to say aloud.

It announced this in a hundred small ways. The mules balked at the pass road as if it were made of insults. The water tasted of iron and old grief. And the villagers—when we finally descended into the first of them, Greyhollow, eleven chimneys and a shrine and a stubborn little mill—watched our column come down the switchbacks with the flat, unblinking patience of people who have watched worse things than soldiers arrive.

"They're not afraid," said Lieutenant Corvane beside me, in the tone of a man reporting a tactical anomaly.

"No," I said. "They're counting."

"Counting what?"

"Us. Our wagons. Our burnables." I kept my voice level. "They are deciding how much of this they can wait out."

Corvane was twenty-four, devout, and had never in his life waited for anything he could take instead. He was a fine officer by Concord measure, which is to say that everything in him worked smoothly except doubt. I envied him once. Lately I have found myself studying him the way an older man studies his own reflection in someone else's face—looking for what he'll lose.

I had lost it years ago, if I'm honest. Somewhere in the capital, before the fall, before the fires, before the word *anomalous* entered my vocabulary and refused to leave it.

* * *

We took Greyhollow by the book. The book is short: occupy the square, raise the Ashen standard on whatever tall thing stands nearest, summon the headmen, read the Writ of Harmonization in a voice that permits no questions, and then—this is the part they don't write down but everyone learns—wait to see who flinches.

The headman of Greyhollow was a woman named Maren Ostrek, sixty or thereabouts, with hands like tree roots and a face that had been arranged around some long-ago disappointment and never rearranged since. She stood through the entire reading with her arms folded, watching me rather than Corvane, who did the reading because protocol demanded the ranking cleric present and I had stopped performing that office in anything but name.

"You'll note," she said when he finished, "that your writ forbids us to shelter heretics."

"I'd hope so," Corvane said. "That is its purpose."

"Mm. And does it forbid you to burn shrines?"

"It instructs us to remove unsanctioned devotional structures."

"Instructs." She turned the word over like a coin she suspected of being clipped. "In my grandmother's time, the Concord instructed. In my mother's time, they requested. Now they arrive with pitch barrels before they've finished reading their own paperwork. You'll forgive me, inquisitor, if I've noticed the direction of travel."

She had addressed this to me, not to Corvane. Word travels ahead of rank; the grey coat and the quiet do more identifying in border country than any seal. I met her eyes and gave her nothing, because giving her something—agreement, apology, even anger—would have been a currency I couldn't afford to spend twice.

"The shrines will be assessed," I said. "Structures of purely local craft may be registered rather than destroyed."

"A kindness," she said, without a gram of gratitude, "from an army that burned the cathedral at Vessarene city with four hundred souls inside."

"That was not—" I began, and stopped, because the sentence had no honest ending. *That was not my doing.* True, and worthless. *That was not the Concord's intent.* False, and worse. The fire at Vessarene had been ordered by men whose names were spoken in the capital the way one speaks of weather: impersonally, with resignation, and always in the past tense before the damage was even done.

"Assess your shrines," Maren Ostrek said. "We'll assess your occupation. See which assessment lasts longer."

* * *

The burning, when it came, was smaller than the word suggests. That's how I have learned to survive my institution: by keeping a precise vocabulary. Not *burning*, then—*reductions*. Three shrines in Greyhollow, two in Lowmark, one in a hamlet so small it appeared on no map I owned and was named only in the way places get named, by the people who needed to say where they lived.

The carvings inside them were the problem. Old gods' work, or work in the old manner—hands pressed into wood, sigils cut into lintels, little offerings of bone and thread tucked into crevices like letters posted to an address that no longer existed. My clerics catalogued and destroyed with the brisk efficiency of men clearing a warehouse, and I stood in the doorway of the third shrine and let them, and told myself the thing I always tell myself:

*Faith that survives in secret becomes faith that survives anywhere. Better it dies here, cleanly, than festers.*

I have recited that sentence so many times it has worn smooth as a river stone, and like a river stone it has no grip left. Nothing catches on it anymore.

What caught on me instead was a single carving, half-charred, that a deacon tossed onto the pile at my feet: a door, rendered in walnut, no bigger than my palm, with nine small figures carved along its threshold. Not worshipping. *Waiting.* The posture was all wrong for worship—no upturned faces, no clasped hands. They stood like guards. Like wardens.

"Door-wardens," said a voice behind me, and I nearly drew steel, which would have been an excellent report to file about myself.

It was the hamlet's priest—or what passed for one out here, a lay-brother named Anselm whom we'd taken at the mill and who had volunteered, with the terrifying calm of the already-condemned, to identify the iconography for us. He looked at the little carved door in my hand the way a man looks at his own grave being measured.

"There were nine families," he said. "Keepers of the Sigil, back when there was a Sigil to keep. Royal blood, every one of them. Bone-carvers, all."

"The Sigil is superstition," Corvane said from the doorway. "A story to frighten children."

"Yes," Anselm agreed pleasantly. "Most true things start that way."

I put the carving down before my face could do anything with it. Because here is what no one in the Concord knows, what no one must ever know, what I have carried for twenty years like a coal swallowed whole: I knew those nine families. One of them, I knew better than scripture.

Quentin Villalobos. We were boys together—well, she was never quite a boy and I was never quite whatever the seminary wanted, and between those two failures we built a friendship the way smugglers build roads: quietly, in pieces, where no one official was looking. She could hear things in bone that I could only hear in confession, and even then only if the penitent was brave. She used to laugh at my vestments. She used to call the Concord's god "the polite one," meaning the god who never answered, and I used to tell her that silence was reverence, and she used to look at me with such pity that I wanted to throw her in the canal.

Then the fall came, and the purges, and I chose my institution over my friend the way one chooses a limb over a finger, and I have been telling myself ever since that the arithmetic was sound.

Now I stood in a burnt shrine in Thornfell holding the shape of a door she was born to open, and the arithmetic began whispering to me again.

* * *

By the fourth day we had conscripted guides. This too is procedure: no column moves in Thornfell without local feet, because the passes are a liar's geography—roads that remember being rivers, trails that exist only in certain weathers, bridges that appear and vanish according to principles the maps have agreed not to discuss. The village elders provided names with the reluctant generosity of men pulling teeth from their own mouths, and the young ones came sullen or frightened or, in two cases, with the flat eagerness of boys who saw a uniform as an exit from poverty, which is the oldest recruitment the world knows.

It was on the fifth day that I convened the amnesty.

I had argued for it in the capital and been refused, and then the anomalous resonances had begun—the dead instruments singing, the relics warming, prayers going somewhere—and suddenly the same council that wouldn't fund me a proper survey was authorizing full operational discretion. Bureaucracy has one consistent virtue: it always believes the emergency it created.

So I stood in the Greyhollow mill square with a clerk and a table and a proclamation, and I offered the mountains a bargain: information concerning the bone-carver known to operate in these regions, in exchange for amnesty, protection, and provision.

I said *bone-carver* and not her name. I have never once let her name enter an official document. Call it sentiment. Call it the last loose thread connecting me to a boy who believed silence was reverence. Either way, it was the one mercy my position still allowed me, and I spent it knowing exactly what it was worth—which is to say, nothing against the machinery itself.

They came to the table all morning. Most knew nothing and said so honestly. A few knew rumors and traded them carefully, testing each coin before spending it. An old woman gave us the location of a cache of relics her son had hidden, and wept while she did it, and I signed her amnesty paper with a hand that did not shake, because shaking is a luxury I surrendered along with everything else.

And then, near dusk, when the clerk was packing the ink and the light had gone the color of pewter, Maren Ostrek came to the table.

She did not sit. She stood over the table with her root-hard hands folded, and she looked at me for a long moment with that counting expression I'd seen on the first day.

"Inquisitor Halvard," she said. She knew my name. Of course she did; villages know more than armies ever suspect. "You want the bone-carver."

"I want information leading to her apprehension."

"You want her *found*." She said it mildly, the way one corrects a child's grammar. "There's a difference, and I think you know it, and I think it costs you something to know it. But I'm not here to save your soul. I'm here because my grandson is seventeen and your quartermaster pays in salt."

I waited. It is the only interrogation technique I have ever truly mastered: the willingness to stand in silence longer than the other person can bear it. Quentin used to beat me at it. I wondered, absurdly, whether she still would.

"There was a family came through Greyhollow last month," Maren Ostrek said. "Refugees, moving east under the Tooth. Paid in carved bone—a keepsake for the widow Tarn, whose husband died on the ice road. A little bird, they said. Carved from a fingerbone, if you can credit it. The widow swears it sang to her. First night she held it, it sang."

"Sang," repeated the clerk, writing.

"Sang," she confirmed, unbothered. "Words in it, the widow says. A woman's voice. Last words of whoever owned the finger." She shrugged. "Superstition. But the carver who made it—that's your trade, isn't it? Bone-work that answers back. There's one carver works these valleys does that. Never takes coin. Never stays. Moves refugees along the old roads like a ferryman for the living."

"And the rumor?" I asked. "You said you had a rumor."

"I have a direction." Her eyes held mine, and in them was something I recognized and hated: pity, offered to me by the occupied to the occupier, which is the most expensive pity in the world. "East of the Tooth, toward the glacier roads. And inquisitor—there's talk among the guides of another rider gone up that way alone. No colours. Riding like your lot teaches riding."

The cold arrived in my chest before my mind could assemble the thought. A rider, alone, no colours, headed into the high passes where the resonances sang loudest. A Concord-trained seat in the saddle.

Someone else was hunting her. Someone from my side, off the leash, or leashed by a different hand than mine.

"Describe the rider," I said.

"Couldn't. Talk only. But the guides crossed themselves after saying it, and my people haven't crossed themselves since you burned the shrines, so make of that what you like."

She took her salt. She walked away. She had sold a rumor for her grandson's winter and bought, with the same transaction, a debt against my conscience that I suspect she fully intended to collect someday, one way or the other.

* * *

That night I sat in the requisitioned millhouse with the little walnut door recovered from the shrine, turning it under lamplight, and I wrote the report I was required to write, and then I wrote, beneath it, in the cipher reserved for the Inquisition's private channel, the report I actually meant.

*Resonance source located approximately. Bone-carver active in eastern Thornfell, refugee lines. Third party operative possibly en route—unaffiliated, armed, Concord-trained. Request clarification of standing orders re: capture versus termination.*

I hesitated over the last line. Then I added:

*Note: subject may be reachable. Recommend capture. Alive.*

*Reachable*. What a word. As if she were a frequency I could tune. As if twenty years of ash hadn't settled between us. I wrote it anyway, sealed it, and gave it to the courier pigeon with the strange tenderness men sometimes feel for documents that will destroy them.

Then I sat alone with the carved door and the lamp, and I did the thing I had forbidden myself since the fall: I remembered properly. Not the curated version, the one where I chose duty with clean hands and she chose treason with dirty ones. The real one. Where two children sat on a canal wall in Vessarene city and she told me the dead weren't haunting anyone—they were just *talking*, the way the living talk, because talking was all anyone had—and I told her that was blasphemy, and she said, "No, Halvard. Blasphemy is pretending you can't hear them when you can."

I had laughed then. Twenty years later, in a millhouse in occupied country, I did not.

Outside, the mountains stood black against the stars, and somewhere beyond the Tooth, past the glacier roads, the machinery I served was grinding eastward—wagons and writs and pitch barrels and boys who had never doubted anything—and somewhere ahead of it, I knew with the certainty I usually only got from other people's bones, she could hear it coming.

The question I could not answer, the question that had followed me down every pass since Greyhollow, was which of us the machinery would reach first.

And behind me, unnoticed until morning, the little walnut door on the table had warmed slightly under the lamp—warm as a held hand—as if something on the other side of it had begun, very softly, to knock.
