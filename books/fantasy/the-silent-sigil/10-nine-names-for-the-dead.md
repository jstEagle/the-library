## 10. Nine Names for the Dead

The first name came at dawn, out of a hair comb.

Not even a relic, properly speaking. A comb of river-horn I'd carved a chevron into three years back and sold to a shepherd's widow in Lower Marn. It should have been leagues away, sleeping like everything else I'd ever made. Instead it sat on Sefa's trading shelf, because the widow had traded it back for passage east, and it was saying a man's name over and over in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

*Tomas Vey.*

I stood in the Kestrel Line's camp kitchen with my hands around a cup of barley tea that had gone cold without my noticing, listening to a dead man recite himself through a piece of horn.

"Tomas Vey," the comb said again. Not pleading. Reciting. The way you'd say your own name to a clerk who'd lost your papers.

"Quentin?" Pellin from the doorway, bleary, hair like a thistle gone to seed. "You've been standing there since before light. You're scaring the mules."

"Mules are wise." I set down the cup. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because inside my chest something had begun to move with the slow inevitability of an avalanche deciding where to stop. "Pellin, has anything in camp been... talking?"

He gave me the look he reserved for my more esoteric questions, which was all of them. "The parrot swears. Does that count?"

* * *

By midday I had every relic I owned laid out on a blanket in the lee of the supply wagon, and they were all doing it.

The fingerbone pendant of the woman from Chapter One of my life—Maren, her name had been Maren—was murmuring *Ilse Brandt*. The antler-handled knife I'd carried through six winters said *Corvo Anselm*. The little carved sparrow I'd made as a girl and never had the heart to sell, the one thing I kept from Before, sat in the center of the arrangement and said nothing at all, but when I touched it, nine names pressed against the inside of my skull like fingers against frosted glass.

Nine names. Always nine. Different voices, different ages—one cracked and old, one so young it barely qualified as speech—but the same nine names, in the same order, every time.

Tomas Vey. Ilse Brandt. Corvo Anselm. And six more I wrote on a scrap of paper with hands that only shook after the ink was dry: Denna Rook. Alaric Fen. The twins, Sable and Sorrel Marsh. Old Mother Quill, whose name turned out to be Odile Quill and who had apparently been a person before she was a story grandmothers told. And last, always last, spoken by the smallest voice:

*Mira Vessarene.*

That one stopped my breath. Vessarene. Not a common name before the fall. Not a name anyone had dared carry since.

I sat back on my heels and looked at my life's work spread out on a woolen blanket like a market stall of grief, and I understood two things with terrible clarity.

First: the dead were not asking me for anything anymore. For twenty years they had asked—asked for rest, for remembrance, for someone to finish their unfinished sentences. That had been the whole shape of my gift. Carve, listen, forget. A relay station for the dying, not a court of law.

Second: whatever had woken behind the Sigil three weeks ago had changed the terms. These names weren't requests. They were charges.

"They want justice," I said aloud, to nobody, because saying things aloud makes them smaller and I needed them small. "Not escape. Justice."

The sparrow stayed silent. The silence felt worse than the talking.

* * *

Sefa found me at dusk, still cross-legged on the frozen ground, having moved precisely zero times in six hours except to blow my nose. She dropped beside me with the graceless thump she used when she wanted you to know she'd chosen to sit near you, and studied the blanket of whispering bone and horn.

"Been like this all day?"

"Since dawn."

"And you didn't wake me."

"You were drunk."

"I was *celebrating*," she said. "There's a difference, and it's mostly hangovers. Quentin. Look at me." I did. Her face in the firelight had its captain-look on, the one that could hold a crew together across a collapsing bridge or a Concord checkpoint. "What are they saying?"

"Names." I handed her the scrap of paper. She read it twice, lips moving, and I watched her reach the same place I had—the last line—and watched her decide not to say it. That's Sefa. She knows which silences are load-bearing.

"The founding purge," she said instead, quietly. "When the Concord took the capital. They executed the royal household and everyone who served it. Nine... no. Hundreds died."

"Hundreds died. Nine were *named*." I picked up Maren's fingerbone pendant, warm as a held hand. "These nine. Over and over. Sefa, the dead don't repeat themselves unless the message matters. Rest is quiet. This isn't rest. This is—" I hunted for the word and hated the one that arrived. "*Testimony.*"

She was quiet a moment. "And what do they want done about it?"

"That's the part that keeps me sitting in the snow." I turned the pendant over. Beneath my carving marks, the bone had begun to show faint lines I hadn't put there—fine as spider silk, geometric, patient. Sigil-work. Growing in the grain like frost on a window. "They're not asking me to remember them. Remembering I can do. They're asking me to open a door I sealed—or my ancestors sealed—with their bones in the hinges, and let what's behind it come *through*."

"To escape," Sefa said. "Through to our side."

"No." The cold had gotten into my voice somehow; I couldn't warm it. "To judge. The gods didn't leave, Sefa. They were locked out. And whoever locked them out named these nine as the price. The dead aren't begging at the door anymore. They're holding it open."

Sefa looked at the blanket of relics, at the frost-lines creeping through Maren's fingerbone, and then at me, and what I saw in her face was the thing I'd spent my whole adult life avoiding: worry with nowhere to put itself.

"You're thinking of going back," she said. "To the glacier."

"I'm thinking that in twenty years of hearing the dead, I have never once been given a choice. People die, I carve, I listen, I forget. It's a trade. It has rules." I closed my fist around the pendant until the edges bit. "This morning the rules changed. Now there's a list of names, and a door, and apparently a bloodline involved, and I'm sitting in the middle of all three wondering when exactly I became an instrument instead of a woman."

"An instrument of what?"

"That's what terrifies me. I don't know yet. The dead? The gods? Whatever's been awake under the glacier since the defector talked?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Instruments don't get to ask questions, Sefa. Instruments get played."

She took my free hand—the one not white-knuckled around a dead woman's finger—and held it hard enough to hurt, which is how she says things she can't say in words.

"You're not an instrument," she said. "You're insufferable, which is completely different. Instruments don't argue back."

"The dead would disagree."

"The dead can file a complaint." But her grip gentled. "Quentin. If this goes where I think it goes—if you go back to that mountain—we go together. Whole crew. I'm not losing you to a glacier because you decided martyrdom was a solo project."

"It usually isn't. Martyrdom comes with an audience."

"*Quentin.*"

"I know." I squeezed back. "I know."

* * *

I couldn't sleep. Around midnight I gave up trying and went to sit with the relics again, because apparently I'd become the sort of woman who attends midnight meetings conducted entirely by other people's bones.

The chorus had changed.

All day it had been recitation—names, order, repetition. Now the voices had found each other, braided into something with grammar. And the smallest voice, Mira Vessarene's, the one that barely qualified as speech, spoke alone for the first time.

Not to me, I realized. Through me. Past me. As though rehearsing.

"When the door opens," the child's voice said, in the dark of the wagon-camp while the living slept around her, "the nine will stand at the threshold. And those who wrote our names in ash will be called by theirs."

Then, softer, in a tone no child should own:

"We are not asking to be saved, carver. We are asking to be *answered*."

I sat very still. Frost-lines crawled through every relic on the blanket now, converging toward the little wooden sparrow at the center, the one thing I'd carved as a girl with clean hands and no gift at all. As I watched, a single line of sigil-script etched itself into the sparrow's breast, fine as a vein.

It read: *Mira Vessarene, door-warden's daughter, aged seven.*

Door-warden's daughter.

My family's title. My family's burden. A seven-year-old girl murdered in the founding purge, whose name my bloodline had been built to answer for.

The avalanche in my chest found where it was going to stop.

I looked north, toward Thornfell's white shoulders and the glacier beneath, and for the first time in my life the dead and I wanted the same thing—and I couldn't decide whether that made me free or made me the most dangerous kind of instrument there is: a willing one.

Behind me, a twig snapped. Sefa stood at the edge of the firelight with her bedroll still under one arm, and the look on her face said she'd heard every word.

"Pack light," she said.
