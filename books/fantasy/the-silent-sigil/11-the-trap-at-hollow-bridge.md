## 11. The Trap at Hollow Bridge

I've run cargo through Hollow Bridge maybe forty times, and every one of those times I've thought the same uncharitable thought: whoever named it should have been made to walk it blindfold. The gorge below is deep enough to swallow a scream, the span is old Vessarene stonework that groans like a widow at a funeral, and there's exactly one way across, which means exactly one way for trouble to find you.

Forty crossings, and I'd never once had trouble there.

Make it forty-one.

* * *

We came down from the high passes two hours before dusk, eleven of us spread out loose along the trail so we didn't look like what we were—a smuggling crew with too much contraband in our packs and one bone-carver whose presence had started to feel less like luck and more like a lit lantern in a dry field. Quentin walked near the middle, hood up, hands tucked into her sleeves like she was trying to disappear them. She'd been quiet for three days. Not her usual quiet, which has a texture to it, a watchful hum. This was quieter than quiet. Like a room where someone had just finished crying.

"You could ride," I told her, nodding at the mule. "Dessa won't judge you."

"She judges everyone."

"Fair. Ride anyway. Save your feet for running."

That got half a smile out of her, which by Quentin standards was practically a sonnet. Then Brannick whistled twice from the point—low, flat, the signal for *road's clear*—and we moved toward the bridge.

Here's what I want written down somewhere, in case anyone ever asks me: I looked at that bridge and I felt nothing wrong about it. The mist sat in the gorge like it always did. The pines stood where pines stand. A crow argued with another crow about something upriver. If there was a trap waiting for us, then the mountains themselves had learned to lie, because everything wore an honest face.

Brannick went first, as he always did, with his long legs eating the stones and his crossbow slung casual across his back like he was going hunting, which technically he was—we all were, just usually for coin instead of survival. Marette followed him. She was nineteen and laughed like water going over rocks, and she'd joined us fourteen months ago off a Concord work-gang that had worked her brother to death in the slate quarries. She carried a carved wooden bird Quentin had made her, a wren, small enough to hide in a fist.

They were both nearly across when the trees on the far side spoke fire.

* * *

You don't get time to think in an ambush. That's the whole design of it. You get maybe three heartbeats to choose between the options some stranger has already laid out for you, and whatever you pick, they've priced it in.

Brannick picked forward. Of course he did. He was through the smoke line before I'd finished shouting his name, crossbow up, putting bolts into the treeline on instinct alone, buying seconds for the rest of us with the only currency he had. Marette turned back toward us—which was wrong, every instinct in my body screamed it was wrong, the bridge was death in both directions now—and she got eight steps before the second volley caught her.

I saw the wren go spinning off the edge of the span. Small thing. It fell into the gorge and I lost sight of it in the mist and I remember thinking, with a clarity that will probably haunt me until I die, *she'll want that back.*

Then the world became noise and motion and me screaming orders I don't fully remember giving. We scattered off the trail into the scree, because standing on a smuggler's road during an ambush is how smugglers stop being smugglers. I dragged Quentin down behind a granite shelf with a grip I'll apologize for someday if I ever find the nerve. Her eyes were huge and fixed on the bridge, on Brannick's silhouette still moving, still fighting, still buying.

"He can make it," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a prayer, and we both knew nobody answered those anymore—or we had, hadn't we, lately, and look how that was going.

The firing stopped as sudden as it began. That was worse. Concord regulars don't stop; they press. Whoever these were wanted something other than corpses, or wanted specific corpses, and the arithmetic of that landed in my stomach like swallowed ice.

Brannick made it back to the third pillar of the bridge. He was limping. He turned, and even at that distance I could see him grinning—that great idiot grin he wore when the odds were insulting him personally. He put one hand on the stone.

The bridge went.

Not collapsed—*dropped*, cut clean at the far pillars, some prepared charge or old Concord engineering trick, the whole middle span swinging down into the gorge with him and the mist and the crows. The sound it made was enormous and then very small, the way big sounds go small when they finish happening, and then there was no Brannick and no bridge and no answer when I screamed his name into the gorge until my voice tore.

Marette's body they left where it lay. Deliberately. Center of the far approach, laid out neat as a market display, arms crossed over her chest. A message. Beside her hand, someone had set a single white stone, the kind the Concord marks graves with when they bother to mark them at all: *judged and found wanting.*

They took nothing else. They burned nothing. They waited just long enough to be sure we understood, and then they were gone into the pines like they'd never been.

* * *

We buried Marette above the gorge because we couldn't reach the body without walking into whatever came next, and because Ryn—our quartermaster, sixty years old and made entirely of stubbornness—refused to let the birds have her. So we built a cairn on the near bluff with shaking hands and no words any of us trusted, and Quentin knelt beside it for a long moment with her palm flat against the topmost stone.

She didn't say anything. But I saw her lips move, and I saw her flinch right after, like something had answered, and I watched her decide not to tell us what.

Later. I told myself. Whatever it was, later.

*Later* lasted until the fire that night, in a hollow under a rock overhang that Ryn swore wasn't on any patrol map. We had nine people where we'd had eleven. We had no bridge, no route east, and no idea why a Concord kill-team had been sitting on the quietest crossing in Thornfell, aimed at us specifically, patient as a spider.

"It was the rumor," Pell said. He was youngest after Marette and grief had made him loud. "The elder at Stonhollow. The one who sold word of the bone-carver to the garrison. Everyone's been whispering about it since the village burnings."

"That's a week old and half hearsay," Ryn said. "And it pointed at a *bone-carver*, not at this crew. Half Thornfell's got carvers."

"Not like her."

Silence. The fire cracked. Nine people found somewhere else to look except at Quentin, who sat at the edge of the light with her hood down and her scarred forearms folded, taking it the way she takes everything—like weather.

"Say it plain," she said.

Pell had the grace to look sick. "If someone sold your description, your road, your habits—they sold *you*. And everything around you burns." He gestured at the dark where the bridge had been. His voice cracked down the middle. "Marette had a bird you carved her. She showed everyone. She talked about you. Maybe *she*—"

"Don't." I was on my feet before I knew I was standing. "Don't you dare finish that sentence."

"I'm saying what everyone's thinking!"

"No, you're saying what grief does when it can't find the enemy fast enough, so it starts eating the family!" I heard my own voice bounce off the overhang and come back uglier. Good. Let it. "The Concord killed Marette. The Concord dropped Brannick into a gorge. If you need to hit something, hit *them*, or hit me, but you don't get to carve up the dead to make a story fit."

"And if the story fits?" That was Odile, quiet, which was somehow worse than Pell's shouting. Odile, who'd sailed with me nine years, who'd held my hair back through fever and never once questioned an order. She was looking at Quentin now, and her face was doing something terrible and careful. "Sefa. The patrols have brushed us four times since she joined us and missed us four times. Then suddenly there's a kill-team sitting on *our* crossing, on *our* night, knowing our weight and our spacing. Either someone talked—and I'd stake my life it wasn't anyone at this fire—or—" She stopped herself. She was decent enough to try.

"Or what?" Quentin said. "Say it. I've been carved apart by better hands than yours."

"Or she *resonates*," Odile said. "That's the word the Concord priests use. Whatever she carries, whatever she does with the dead—it leaves a trace. Like blood in water. They're not tracking a person. They're tracking a sound."

Nobody breathed. The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope a night bird called once and quit, like even it wanted no part of this.

Quentin stood. Slowly. She looked smaller than she ever had, and older, and absolutely unbending, three things at once, which shouldn't be possible and yet there she was.

"If I'm a beacon," she said, "then every one of you has been dying by inches since the day you took me in, and the only question is whether you cut me loose tonight or let me keep killing you politely." She pulled her pack off and held it out—not offering, *offering*, the distinction visible in every line of her. "There's a fork in the trail two miles back. I can be gone before dawn and none of you ever hear the dead again."

And here is the thing I will carry to my own grave alongside everything else: for one full heartbeat, I considered letting her.

One heartbeat of cowardice, dressed up as strategy. One heartbeat of *eleven minus two is nine and nine might live.* I felt it move through me like cold water finding a crack, and I hated it, and I hated Odile for putting it in the air, and most of all I hated whoever had built that trap on Hollow Bridge, because grief was supposed to make us closer, that's what grief was *for*, and instead they'd turned it into a knife and handed it around the fire like bread.

"No," I said.

"Sefa—"

"I said *no*." I stepped between her and the rest of them, which was ridiculous, she needed protecting from none of us physically, but some things you do with your body because your voice isn't enough. "Here's what I know. Somebody sold a rumor in Stonhollow. Fine. Rumors are cheap and elders are frightened and frightened people sell anything. But that team on the bridge knew our route. Our timing. Our spacing. That's not a rumor—that's a *briefing*. Which means either there's a traitor close enough to hand the Concord my charts, or Odile's right and Quentin glows in the dark for anyone with the right instrument. And I don't know which it is. Neither do any of you."

"So what do we do?" Pell asked. "Wait for the next bridge?"

"We do what crews do." I looked around the fire, at nine faces with two holes torn in them. "We assume nothing and trust carefully and we *watch*. Odile, you take first watch with me. Ryn, double the perimeter. And Quentin—" I turned to her. She still had her pack held out. I pushed it gently back against her chest. "You stay. Not because I'm sure you're safe. Because I'm sure that cutting you loose in the dark to prove we're innocent would be the second-worst decision I've made today, and I set a high bar this morning."

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch. From Quentin, that was a collapse into my arms sobbing.

Odile didn't argue. That frightened me more than shouting would have. She just banked the fire and said, "Your call, Captain," in a voice with frost on it, and took the watch, and the crew broke apart into bedrolls and silence.

I sat up most of the night with my back against cold stone, watching Quentin sleep—or pretend to sleep—across the embers, and turning the day over and over like a stone with something underneath it.

Because here's what I hadn't said at the fire. Here's what kept my eyes open while the gorge wind sang through the pines. The white stone they'd left by Marette. Grave-marking is Concord doctrine, yes—but the placement, the *theater* of it, the neatness? That was a signature. I'd seen it before, years ago, in another life: a young officer in the capital who arranged his arrests like table settings, who believed terror should be legible.

Halvard. Inquisitor Halvard, if the titles had come through. The boy who used to steal plums with me off Widow Casserly's tree before the world broke.

Which meant this wasn't a patrol blundering into us. This was *him*. Reading our roads like an old letter, moving pieces with the patience of a man who'd known for months that the bone-carver he was hunting had a face he used to know.

And if Halvard was close enough to lay a trap on Hollow Bridge, then the fork in the trail two miles back wasn't an escape for Quentin.

It was a funnel.

I watched the dark between the trees and made myself admit the last thing, the one I'd been refusing since the gorge: somebody had given us up. Not a rumor—a briefing. Charts, timings, spacing. My charts. And there were only eleven people alive who'd ever seen them.

Two of them were dead.

Nine of us sat around a dying fire, and one of us was lying.

I reached over and touched Quentin's boot, just to feel something solid, and she opened her eyes instantly, fully awake, the way she always was, and I whispered, "Get your pack. We're moving before dawn—all of us, new route, off my charts."

Her gaze sharpened. "Why?"

"Because the trap wasn't built for a resonance," I said. "It was built for *us*. And I intend to find out which one of us built it."

Above us, on the ridge, something shifted in the pines—soft, deliberate, gone before the firelight could reach it.
