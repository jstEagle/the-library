## 12. Old Friends, New Warrants

I had arrested four hundred and eleven people before I understood that the hardest part was never the arrest. It was the paperwork afterward—the careful fiction that every name in my ledger deserved its line. The Concord liked its ledgers tidy. Sin entered on the left, penance on the right, and nothing bled through the margins.

Quentin Villalobos was going to bleed all over mine.

The market at Ferrow's Bend smelled of wet wool and frying onions, the way mountain markets do when autumn is thinking about becoming winter. I stood under a tanner's awning with a warrant in one pocket and a memory in the other, and only one of them was useful. Two of my squad worked the far stalls. The rest held the bridge out of town. If she came—and the elder's rumor said she came for bone-meal and lamp oil on the third day of every month—she would have exactly one road home.

She came wearing a man's coat two sizes too big and a scarf pulled up to her eyes, which fooled no one who had spent a decade learning how people hide. Guilt has a walk. So does grief. Quentin had both, and she moved through that crowd like someone apologizing to it.

"Ma'am," I said, stepping into her path, "you're wanted for questioning."

Here is what they don't tell you about catching an old friend: your body does the work while your heart files objections. My hand closed on her wrist before I had finished deciding to speak. She went rigid—then, horribly, she laughed. A short, cracked sound, almost fond.

"Halvard." She said it like a diagnosis. "You always did lead with 'ma'am.' Even when we were nine."

"You're under Concord authority," I said, because procedure is a kind of armor, and I have never owned enough of the other kind. "Come quietly and no one gets hurt."

"Someone already got hurt." Her eyes went past me, toward the mountains, toward Hollow Bridge, where two smugglers had died in an ambush built from a sold rumor—a rumor I had paid for. She didn't know that yet. Or maybe she did; the dead tell her things, if half the reports were true. "Everyone always gets hurt. That's the whole trade."

I cuffed her wrists in front of her because she offered them, and I want it recorded—I keep records even in my head, especially there—that she did not run. She could have. There were forty people between us and my squad, and any one of them might have been hers. She let me take her like a woman settling a debt.

* * *

The garrison house at Ferrow's Bend had been a wool merchant's home before the occupation: good hearthstone, bad plumbing, a parlor with a view of the river that the Concord had converted into an interrogation room by adding a table, two chairs, and a lamp. We are efficient with our cruelty. We add furniture.

I dismissed the sergeant. I told him I would handle the initial interview personally and log it by morning, which was true, and that the prisoner was low-value, which was the largest lie I had told since joining the Ashen Concord's service, which is saying something, because I once told a tribunal that a burned shrine had contained no relics when I myself had carried three of them out the back door to be destroyed properly rather than looted improperly. Integrity, in this work, is mostly about the direction you steal in.

Alone, I took off my gloves. It seemed important to do that. Interrogators keep gloves on so their hands stay neutral, instruments of the office. But I wanted her to see that these were still my hands. The ones that had taught her to gut a fish badly. The ones that had held her elbow the first time we ice-skated on the capital canal and she'd gone down and laughed so hard a guard asked if we were drunk.

We were not drunk. We were twelve, and the world had a kingdom in it.

"Sit down, Quen."

"Don't call me that."

"All right." I sat myself. "Villalobos, then. Bone-carver. Unlicensed practitioner of pre-Concord rites. Suspected conduit for anomalous resonances across four districts. Do you want me to read the whole warrant? It's very thorough. I wrote most of it."

"You wrote a warrant for me?" Something flickered in her face—not hurt exactly. Confirmation. "How long have you known?"

"That the bone-carver in Thornfell might be you?" I folded the warrant into quarters, unfolded it, folded it again. A nervous habit I thought I'd buried with the rest of my childhood. "Since the resonances started. Since the amulets. You have a signature, you know. Everyone does. Yours is—you leave the joints unpolished. You always did. You said polished joints lie."

"I said that about people."

"Well. Bones too, apparently."

The lamp hissed. Outside, a sentry coughed, and somewhere downstream the mill turned over its slow wooden thoughts. I had rehearsed this conversation for eleven days and had planned to open with the amnesty offer. Instead I heard myself say:

"Do you remember the Festival of Nine Lanterns? Before the fall. You made me a lantern out of a sheep's knucklebone. Carved a little door into it. You said—" I stopped.

"Say it, then. You brought it up."

"You said every door deserves a light, or the thing behind it can't find its way home." My throat had gone dry as ash-paper. "You were fifteen. You thought you were being funny."

"I was being funny," she said quietly. "It stopped being funny later."

And there it was, the whole country of our shared history, laid out between us on a table built for confessions. Quentin Villalobos and Isandro Halvard, children of the capital, sons and daughters of a kingdom that no longer appeared on maps printed by lawful authority. She had kept her faith and lost everything else. I had kept my commission and lost—well. I was sitting in a converted parlor interrogating my oldest friend, so the inventory wasn't difficult.

"The Sigil," I said. "Where is it?"

* * *

She didn't answer right away. That was the first mercy of the night, though I wouldn't understand until much later whose mercy it was.

Instead she looked at me the way she used to look at raw material—assessing grain, judging where the knife should go—and I felt, absurdly, carved open.

"You actually believe in it now," she said. "The Concord sent you to debunk it and you've come back a believer. When did that happen? Was it the amulet prayers? The dead speaking? Or was it just—" she gestured at the room, the warrant, the whole gray machine of the occupation, "—this?"

"My beliefs aren't the subject."

"No. Your beliefs are never the subject. That's the tragedy of you, Halvard. You file yourself under procedure." She leaned forward, wrists still cuffed, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was worse than anger. "So let me ask you something off the record. When you were a boy, did you believe in the old gods? Before they taught you the word 'anomalous'?"

"Yes," I said. It cost more than the amnesty offer would have. "Yes. I did."

"And when Vessarene fell, and the Concord burned the shrines, and your neighbors disappeared into reeducation camps—did you stop believing, or did you just stop *saying*?"

The lamp hissed again. I found I couldn't look at her, so I looked at my hands, ungloved, traitorously human.

"There is a version of tonight," I said finally, "where I offer you a deal, you refuse it, and I follow procedure. In that version, you go to the provincial seat in chains, and the questioners there are not me, and they will get the location eventually, because everyone talks, Quen. Everyone. The ones who don't talk simply haven't met the right questioner yet." I made myself meet her eyes. "I don't want that version."

"So here's another version," she said. "Tell me yours first. What's the deal?"

"Surrender the Sigil's location. Cooperate fully. In exchange, you walk out tonight. Not a trick, not a leash—walk out, cross the border, live under whatever name you like. I'll falsify the ledger. Escaped custody during transport. I'll take the demotion. Possibly the tribunal." I smiled, and it tasted like tin. "Possibly worse. The Concord has become creative about worse."

She stared at me. "You'd burn your own career."

"I'd burn considerably more than my career."

"Why?" And there it was, the real question, the one underneath all the others, the one I had been avoiding since I read her name in a resonance report and felt my stomach drop through three floors of certainty. "Why, Halvard? You're not doing this for me. You barely know who I am anymore. So why?"

Because the institution I serve burns children's lanterns. Because I have four hundred and eleven names in my head and I recite them some nights like a rosary, and lately the rosary has started feeling less like penance and more like rehearsal for my own trial. Because when the reports said *the dead are speaking*, something in me that I had carefully killed at twenty-two sat up in its grave and said *finally.*

"Because I'm tired," I said, which was the smallest true thing I could fit in my mouth without choking. "Because I watched them conscript guides last month and march them into passes they had no business surviving, and I signed the requisitions. Because a village elder sold a rumor that got two people killed at a bridge, and I paid for that rumor, and I have decided that I would rather be damned by the Concord than by—" I stopped. Started again. "Than by the boy with the knucklebone lantern."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, softly:

"They're going to mobilize, you know. Whatever you think you're buying with one night—it won't be enough. They've broken someone from my crew. You'll have the glacier within the week whether I talk or not."

"I know."

"So your deal is worthless."

"My deal is the only honest thing I have left to offer." I set the key to her cuffs on the table between us and pushed it across the wood with one finger. It made a small sound, metal on merchant's oak, and I will hear that sound for the rest of my life however long or short the Concord permits it to be. "One night. At dawn, my squad musters for rotation, and the cell will be found empty, and I will write my report, and after that we are enemies again. Properly. With warrants."

She picked up the key. She didn't use it. Instead she leaned close, and I braced for a curse—for whatever the old gods' faithful say to traitors and turncoats—but what she said was:

"Shep's Hollow. The girl who sells apples. Her father died in the second purge. She prays at the well every morning, out loud, because she doesn't know anyone can hear." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "That's why I do it, Halvard. Not for power. Not for the Sigil. For her. For all the ones praying into a silence your masters built." She straightened. "Remember that when you write your report."

Then she unlocked her own cuffs, set them neatly on the table like a returned library book, and walked out through my interrogation room, my garrison, my tidy life, leaving the door open behind her—which was either carelessness or commentary.

* * *

I sat alone in the parlor for two hours. Then I unlocked the drawer where I keep the things that don't fit in ledgers and took out a fragment of carved sheep's knucklebone, twenty-three years old, with a little door cut into it and a candle stub melted into the base. I had confiscated it from myself, in effect, years ago, when the Concord ordered personal effects screened for relic contamination. I had filed it under *pending destruction*. Some entries stay pending forever.

Every door deserves a light, or the thing behind it can't find its way home.

I lit the stub. It was a stupid, treasonous, sentimental act, the kind of thing that gets men like me unmade in basement rooms by colleagues with better posture. The flame guttered in the draft from the open door she'd left behind her, and steadied, and burned.

At dawn the cell was empty. I wrote my report in a clean, professional hand: *Prisoner escaped during transport delay due to staffing shortage; pursuing leads.* I signed it. Somewhere north, my institution was loading wagons and sharpening questions, marching on a glacier with fire in the manifest, and I had bought the world exactly one night.

One night, and a lit lantern, and a name added to a different ledger entirely—four hundred and twelve, the first entry I had ever written on the side marked *penance.*

Outside, snow was starting. It would slow the wagons. It would not stop them.

Nothing, I suspected, was going to stop anything anymore.
