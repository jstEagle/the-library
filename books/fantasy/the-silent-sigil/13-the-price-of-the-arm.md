## 13. The Price of the Arm

There is a rule every bone-carver learns before she learns anything else: the body keeps accounts. You can borrow from it—a little marrow here, a little sleep there—but it always collects. I had spent twenty years making small withdrawals. A scar on my thumb from a slipped chisel. The permanent ache in my right wrist from holding tools wrong because holding them right hurt worse. The calluses that meant I could no longer feel silk, or my mother's hair, or much of anything delicate at all.

Tonight I intended to empty the account entirely.

The cave Sefa had found was deep in the glacier's shoulder, past the antechamber's frozen mouth, small enough to hold one person and her ghosts. Which was fortunate, because I'd brought all nine of mine.

*You don't have to do this tonight,* said Maren, the dead woman whose fingerbone pendant I'd worn for so long I'd stopped thinking of it as separate from my own skin. Her voice came through the bone against my sternum, patient as snowfall. *The door has waited twenty years.*

"The door can wait," I said, laying out my tools on a strip of oiled leather. "I can't. Halvard bought me one night, and I spent half of it arguing with you."

*We won,* Maren observed.

"You argued in chorus. That's cheating."

I was talking to keep my hands busy while they decided whether they were going to shake. That's the truth of it, if we're being honest, which the dead insist upon even when I'd rather not be. My hands were shaking. Not from cold—the cave was cold enough to crack teeth, but I'd been cold for years and my body had filed it under *weather*. They shook because some animal part of me, the part that had kept Quentin Villalobos alive through the fall of a kingdom by keeping her head down and her name out of ledgers, understood exactly what I was about to do and wanted no part of it.

That part of me had been outvoted around midnight.

* * *

Let me explain what blood-ink is, since I'm told my explanations tend to run long and morbid, and this one deserves both.

Bone remembers. Any carver knows that. Carve a relic from the dead and the dead will speak through it—that was my gift, or my affliction, depending on whether I'd eaten recently. But flesh also remembers, and blood remembers loudest of all, because blood is where the body does its accounting. Ink made from a door-warden's blood carries her lineage in it. Carve the Silent Sigil into wood, into stone, into bone, and you get a symbol. Carve it into yourself with your own blood, and you get an address.

The Sigil existed "between"—the defector's bones had been clear about that before they went silent forever. A door removed from the world but not destroyed, hanging in the hinge-space where the old gods had stepped back through it and pulled it shut behind them. To find a thing between, you needed a marker in both worlds. Something that existed here, in Thornfell, in the cold and the ordinary pain of a living woman—

—and something that existed there, written in the medium the old gods had used to build the threshold in the first place.

Royal blood. Door-warden blood. Mine, as it turned out, though I'd gone thirty-one years without asking for the privilege.

*It will hurt,* said Ossian. He was the oldest of my nine, a stonemason who'd died in the Concord's founding purge, and he spoke the way he'd presumably built—load-bearing sentences, no ornament. *Not like cutting your hand. Like being unmade and remade, slowly, in the wrong order.*

"Thank you, Ossian. Very comforting."

*I am not here to comfort you. I am here to hold your hand steady, and I cannot do that if I lie to you first.*

I looked down at my forearm, bare in the lantern light, pale as the glacier above us. Somewhere beyond the ice, Halvard was presumably drafting his report explaining how he'd lost his prisoner to the night and the mountain. Somewhere south, Sefa was awake—I knew it the way I knew weather now, without hearing anything, just the shape of her worry—and furious at me for slipping away, and more furious that she'd promised not to follow.

*She'll forgive you,* Maren said.

"She'll forgive me eventually. There's a difference, and she'll make sure I know it."

*Good. People who forgive immediately are hiding something.*

I picked up the finest graver I owned—a needle of blackened steel I'd made myself from a broken Concord bayonet, which felt appropriate—and the small clay pot of ink I'd mixed at dusk. Blood-ink isn't complicated, which is somehow worse than if it were. Your blood, warmed, thickened with ash from a shrine the Concord hadn't managed to burn, and a single drop of glacial meltwater, because water that had waited ten thousand years inside a glacier understood patience better than any living thing.

My blood in the pot looked very red and very ordinary. Blood usually does. It's what it *means* that runs loud.

*Quentin.* All nine of them, together, in the chorus that still raised the hairs on my arms after months of it. *Say it back to us. What you're doing, and why.*

They made me say it back. This was new—they'd started insisting on it after Hollow Bridge, after Brannick and Tess were killed in an ambush built from a rumor someone had sold. After I'd learned that my resonance could be traced, that my gift had a signature, and that everything I did rang like a bell in a quiet room.

"I, Quentin Villalobos," I recited, "daughter of Ilse Villalobos, daughter of the door-wardens of Vessarene, carve the Silent Sigil into my own flesh so that the door may be found again. Not to escape through it. Not to hide behind it. To open it, so that the nine may speak their names to the living, and the old gods may pass judgment on those who earned it. I choose this. Nobody chose it for me. If I die doing it, I died choosing, which is more than most of us got."

*Better,* said Ossian.

*You left out the part about Sefa,* Maren said.

"The part about Sefa is private."

*Everything important is private. Say it anyway.*

I closed my eyes. "And when it's done—whatever happens after—tell her I wasn't running. Tell her I was walking toward something for once, instead of away." I opened them. "Happy?"

*Yes,* said nine voices, and for once they sounded like it.

* * *

The first line was the worst line, because it was the first.

I want to tell you I was brave about it. I want to tell you I thought of Vessarene, of the refugees I'd smuggled through Thornfell passes, of Sefa's crooked smile, and the pain became music. What actually happened was this: the graver touched my forearm, I cut the opening stroke of the sigil's outer ring, and every nerve I owned informed me, collectively and at volume, that I had made a catastrophic error and should stop immediately.

I did not stop. I screamed instead—which was fine, because the cave swallowed sound the way glaciers swallow everything, and because screaming turned out to be load-bearing too. You breathe in to scream. You have to keep breathing. The sigil has forty-one primary lines and each one takes three breaths to lay down properly, and if you count them, if you give each line its three screams, you get a rhythm, and rhythm is just another word for staying alive on purpose.

*Left hand,* Ossian said, sharp. *Your left hand is drifting.*

I couldn't feel my left hand. I could barely feel my left arm, except as a column of white fire from wrist to elbow. But drift was death—if a line wandered even the width of a hair, the sigil wouldn't be wrong, it would be nothing, hours of agony resolving into a pretty scar and a dead end.

Then the dead steadied me.

I don't know how else to describe it. I've heard the dead speak through relics my whole life—voices, pressure, presence. This was different. This was nine people putting their hands over my hands, except hands are the wrong word, because what settled over my grip was weight without touch, certainty without muscle. Maren, who in life had been a lacemaker and knew finer work than any of us. Ossian, with stone in his patience. Little Wren, who'd been twelve when the Concord burned her village, and who hummed while she guided my blade, a skipping rhyme from a country that no longer appeared on maps.

My hand stopped shaking. The graver moved like it had somewhere to be.

*Second ring,* Maren murmured. *Breathe. Three counts. Again.*

Somewhere around the seventh ring, I stopped screaming and started laughing, which worried me, so I asked them about it.

*Pain is information,* Ossian said. *Your body is reporting that it is being destroyed. But bodies exaggerate. Yours is not being destroyed. It is being rewritten, and rewriting hurts more than destroying, because destruction at least gets to be finished.*

"That," I gasped between strokes, "is the least comforting thing anyone has ever said to me, and I once had a conversation with a tax collector."

*He was a good man, that tax collector,* said Wren.

"He audited my mother."

*Still. Good man.*

The rings went down. The outer circles first—the boundary of the ward, the promise that what was carved would hold. Then the interlocking gates, nine of them, one for each name, each gate a knot of lines that took longer than the whole ring before it. My blood-ink sank into the cuts and did something ink should not do: it moved. Slowly, lazily, the way frost moves across a window, the lines settling deeper than I'd cut them, finding channels in my arm that I hadn't made and couldn't see. My body was learning the sigil. Or the sigil was learning my body. By the third gate I honestly couldn't tell the difference, and by the fifth I no longer thought it mattered.

The cold crept in around the fourth hour. Not the cave's cold—something else, coming up through the floor of the world, through the glacier, through whatever thin place I'd opened in myself by carving. The between, leaking toward its address.

*Hold the threshold,* all nine said, and for the first time they weren't talking about my nerve.

* * *

I fainted twice. I'm not proud of that, but honesty was the whole point of the exercise.

The first time was somewhere in the eighth gate. The world went soft and gray and very kind, and I remember thinking, with the serene stupidity of the nearly dead, *oh good, rest*, and then nine voices hit me like a wave breaking over a seawall.

*NO.* Maren, louder than I'd ever heard her, loud enough that the pendant cracked against my sternum. *UP. Quentin Villalobos, you look at me. You look at your hands.*

I looked. My right hand had kept moving. Even unconscious—or nearly—even gone gray and distant, my hand had laid two perfect inches of gate-line, guided by the dead, because they would not let the work stop and they would not let me die, and apparently both of those things could be true at once.

I came back to my body the way you come up from deep water: too fast, everything bright and hurting. I cried. I want that on the record too, since we're being thorough. I sat in a freezing cave with half a god-door carved into my arm and I cried like the refugee children I used to guide, the ones who asked me if the old gods were real, and I thought, *they are real, child, and they are expensive, and I hope you never learn the price.*

The second time I fainted was at the final line—the keystone stroke, the single cut that closed the innermost ring and bound the whole design. I knew it was coming. The dead had warned me: the last stroke goes deepest, because a door is only as strong as its seal, and the seal has to reach the bone.

To the bone. My bone. The material I'd spent my life listening through, suddenly the material I was writing on.

*Ready?* Maren asked.

"No," I said. "Do it anyway. With me. On three."

*One,* said Ossian.

*Two,* said Wren, humming.

*Three,* said all nine, and I cut.

The graver went through flesh and found bone, and the world went white—not gray this time, white, total, silent, and in that silence I heard something I had never heard before: the dead, singing. Not words. Just the sound nine voices make when they've been carrying grief for twenty years and finally set it down. And underneath them, older, vaster, patient as the glacier, something turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to listen.

Then I was face-down on the cave floor with Maren shouting my name and my arm on fire in a way fire should envy.

* * *

Here is what I saw when I could see again:

My forearm, from wrist to elbow, held the Silent Sigil complete—forty-one lines, nine gates, one keystone—drawn in lines that were no longer ink or blood but something between. Silver-dark, faintly warm, moving when I wasn't looking at it directly, the way stars move if you trust them with your peripheral vision. It didn't look like a wound. It looked like a window someone had installed in me, frame and all, and the glass was still cooling.

Here is what I felt: everything and then, strangely, almost nothing. The pain had burned itself down to a deep coal-glow, tolerable, almost companionable. My arm belonged to the sigil now; the sigil belonged to the door; and I hung on the far edge of that chain like a kite string, feeling the pull of the between through my own bones.

And here is what I heard, when I pressed my ear—as one does—to the carved arm:

Not the dead. For the first time since the defector's shinbone, not the nine, not the chorus, not Maren's patient voice or Wren's humming.

A knock. Faint, rhythmic, from very far away and very close by, from everywhere the world wasn't.

Something on the other side of a door, asking—in the politest possible way, the way you knock when you've been waiting twenty years and you want very much to be let in—whether the warden was ready to turn the key.

I sat up. My hands were steady. My eyes were wet and my mouth was bleeding where I'd bitten through my lip and I had never in my life felt more awake.

"Yes," I said aloud, to the arm, to the door, to the dark. "But you'll have to hurry. We have until dawn, and an army, and one night I intend to spend very badly indeed."

The knocking stopped. In its place, faint and unmistakable, came the sound of a lock beginning, at last, to turn—from the outside, and from within me, both at once.

Above the glacier, the sky had begun, very quietly, to change color.
