## 14. What Sefa Carries

There is a particular kind of quiet that settles over a crew after someone confesses to carving holy script into her own arm. I'd been captain of the Kestrel Line for eleven years, and I had never heard that quiet before. I hope to never hear its cousin again.

We were three days out of the antechamber's mouth, holed up in a shepherd's bothy that stank of wet wool and old smoke, and nobody was talking. Tam was re-fletching arrows he wasn't going to use. Marette was mending a sail we hadn't needed since the lowlands. And Quentin sat by the fire with her sleeve down and her jaw set like a woman who has said her piece and considers the conversation concluded.

It was not concluded. Not for me. Because she'd shown me hers, and there was a thing in my pack that had been waiting twenty-six years for exactly this conversation, and it weighed about as much as an egg and considerably more than a mountain.

I poured myself a cup of the plum wine we'd liberated from a Concord supply wagon—poor man's sacrament, but sacrament all the same—and I took out the relic.

* * *

You should understand something about me before I tell you what happened next. Sefa Oduya does not hand over leverage. Sefa Oduya does not cry at campfires. Sefa Oduya once talked her way past a Concord checkpoint while actively bleeding from a knife wound, complimenting the sergeant's boots the entire time, because showing weakness is how you die on the roads and I have made a long career of not dying.

But Quentin had shown me her arm. The sigil carved in blood-ink, the lines still raised and angry, the skin around them gone the color of storm-light. She had done it alone, in the dark, without telling any of us, because that is who Quentin Villalobos is: a woman who would rather bleed herself hollow than be a burden.

And I thought: *you absolute mule. You absolute beloved mule. Where do you think you learned that?*

"Quentin." I set the wine down between us. "Before you decide you're carrying this door business solo, there's something you should see."

She looked up. Those eyes of hers—the ones that had seen too many dead people's last words—went straight to my hand, to the shape under the oilcloth.

"That's a key," she said. Not a question. Of course it wasn't a question. The woman hears bones talk; a wrapped lump of metal was hardly going to stump her.

"It's a key," I agreed. "And I've known it was a key since I was nine years old. What I did not know—and I want this on the record, carved in stone if necessary—is *what in all the hells it opens.*"

* * *

My mother gave it to me the night before she died. That's the short version. Here's the longer one, which I've told no one, because telling it meant admitting my whole glorious criminal enterprise was built on a foundation of priests.

The Oduya routes through Thornfell weren't invented by smugglers. They predate the fall of Vessarene by—I don't know how long. Centuries, maybe. When I was young I thought my grandmother's grandmother had been very clever with maps. Turns out the roads that remember were remembering something older than us. Every safehouse, every cache, every blind trail through the passes—they follow the old processional ways. The paths pilgrims walked to reach the Sigil, back when the Sigil was a door instead of a rumor.

My family didn't run contraband. We ran *people*. Refugees, back then too—fleeing the founding purge, fleeing the Concord's first fires. Priests mostly, and their flocks, and anyone else the new order wanted ash. The Kestrel Line isn't a name. It's a *continuation*. There was always a line. There was always someone carrying.

And there was always the key. Passed hand to hand, mother to daughter, in my family since before anyone remembered why. Wrapped in oilcloth, kept close, never spoken of except in one sentence, which my mother made me memorize the night the fever took her:

*"When the door-warden comes, give it freely. Until then, guard it with your life and tell no one—not even yourself what you suspect."*

"I spent years trying not to suspect anything," I told Quentin. "Do you know how hard that is? I'm a professional suspicious person. It's my entire trade. And there's this—" I unwrapped the oilcloth.

The key was bone-white metal, or maybe actual bone, transmuted by whatever the old gods did to things they loved. About the length of my forearm. Along its spine ran a groove of characters, and I watched Quentin's face as she read them, watched the color leave it.

"That matches your arm," she whispered. "That matches the antechamber. Sefa, where—"

"My mother. Hers before her. All the way back." I turned it over in my hands. "I used to pretend it was a fancy letter opener. I used to sleep with it under my pillow when I was scared and tell myself it was just habit, just family nonsense. Twenty-six years I've hauled this thing across half a kingdom, guarding a door I didn't believe existed, for a door-warden I didn't know was coming." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And then you walk onto my boat with your haunted knickknacks and your rules and your terrible lying face, and I take one look at you and think: *oh no. Oh, it's her. It's actually her.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind worried the eaves of the bothy.

"You knew," Quentin said quietly. "Before the defector. Before any of it. You knew and you didn't say."

"I didn't *know* know. I suspected. My mother told me not to suspect, so I filed it under family madness and got on with smuggling." I met her eyes. "Yes. I knew enough. And I didn't say. Because saying it made it real, and real things get people killed, and I have buried enough of my people this season to last several lifetimes."

She was quiet a long moment. Then, in that flat way of hers that means the opposite of flat: "Hollow Bridge wasn't your fault."

"Don't."

"It wasn't. The rumor was sold, the trap was set—"

"Sefa." She leaned forward. "You held that bridge. You carried Denna out yourself. Whatever ledger you're keeping, write this down: you kept your oath. Guarded it with your life. All of ours."

I looked away, because my eyes were doing something undignified, and I found Tam and Marette pointedly not watching us, which is the kindest thing a crew can do.

"So," I said, when my voice worked again. "A priest-bloodline smuggler captain and a royal-bloodline bone-carver walk into a glacier. Sounds like the start of a joke my mother would've hated."

* * *

We talked until the fire burned down to coals. I told her about the caches—the ones marked with the kestrel sigil, which I'd always assumed was just our brand and now understood was a warding mark. She told me about the antechamber, the absence where the door should be, the dead whispering that it could be carved back into being. She showed me the defector's map, and I showed her where three of the route markers matched places my family's caches had sat for generations, and neither of us laughed, because it wasn't funny. It was the opposite of funny. It was two rivers turning out to be one river, and both of us having been swimming in it our whole lives without looking down.

"The priests founded the Line," she said slowly, working it through. "So when the seal started failing—"

"When the prayers started getting answered again, the network woke up. Or it was always half-awake. Waiting." I tapped the key against my knee. "You know what the worst part is? All those years I thought I was just good at my job. Turns out I'm a *custodian*. I've been a holy woman this whole time and nobody had the decency to inform me. I'd have dressed better."

That got a sound out of her that was almost a laugh. Progress.

Then she went serious again, the way she does, folding inward like a closing hand. "Sefa. If this works. If I open it. The gods coming through aren't going to be gentle. Judgment isn't gentle. People we care about are going to be standing too close."

"People we care about are standing too close *now*," I said. "The Concord burns shrines in villages full of children. At least your apocalypse has standards."

"And if I fail? If the carving goes wrong and I'm—" She didn't finish it. Her hand drifted toward her forearm, the sleeve, the secret underneath.

"Then I'll carve the rest myself," I said, and meant it, and watched her understand that I meant it. "Badly. With tremendous confidence. The gods will be appalled. It'll buy you time."

"You can't carve, Sefa."

"No," I agreed. "But I can hold a door open with my body, and I've been training for that my entire life by refusing to close doors when sensible people told me to."

She stared at me. Then she did the thing I will remember until I die: she reached out and put her hand over mine, over the key, over twenty-six years of guarded nothing, and squeezed.

"Give it freely," she said. My mother's words. She'd heard them once, hours ago, and she was already handing them back to me like they were hers.

"Not yet," I said, and my voice betrayed me entirely, going rough as gravel. "When we're at the door. When it counts. That's the oath. But Quentin—" I swallowed. "Thank you. For being the reason it finally makes sense. I have carried a question my whole life, and I didn't know how heavy it was until somebody answered it."

"Relief of shared weight," she murmured, almost to herself. "The dead say that sometimes. About graves. About grief."

"Well, the dead are occasionally right. Don't tell them I said so."

* * *

Near dawn, I slept—or half-slept, the way captains do, one ear on the world. And in that grey half-dream I heard hooves. Many. Too many to be weather.

I was up and at the window slit before my mind finished waking. Down in the valley below the bothy, moving along the old processional road—*my* road, the one my grandmother's grandmother walked—came a column of Ashen Concord. Not a patrol. An army. Lanterns strung out along the pass like a burning rosary, wagons, ladders for scaling the high trails, and at the head of it, banners I had only ever seen in nightmares: the white ash-tree of the High Command itself.

They weren't searching anymore. Searching spreads out. This was converging. Everything they had, aimed at the glacier like an arrow finding its heart.

Someone had broken. Someone had talked. I didn't know who yet, didn't know how, and some cold part of me noted that I'd need to know soon—but the larger part of me was already counting heads, counting rounds, counting hours.

Behind me, Quentin stirred at the fire. "Sefa? What is it?"

I kept my voice level, because that's what captains do, even when the arithmetic is ugly.

"Company," I said. "All of it. Everyone they have. And they're going to beat us to the door unless we move *now*."
