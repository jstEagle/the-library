## 15. The March of the Ashen Choir

They broke the boy in eleven hours.

I know because I timed it. Not out of cruelty—I've never had much appetite for the theater of interrogation—but because I wanted to see how long the Concord's new methods took against a smuggler who'd grown up on mountain roads and probably learned to lie before he learned to walk. Eleven hours. Under Director Veyra's questioners, with their iron collars and their patient, smiling liturgy, that's apparently what a human being costs now: eleven hours, plus whatever's left over afterward.

What was left over afterward was not much.

His name was Tam Wrenlow. Twenty-two years old. He'd crewed on the Kestrel Line for three years, according to the manifests we pulled from a burned waystation outside Hollow Bridge. He had a scar through his eyebrow from a fall off a rope bridge, a habit of humming when he was frightened—which, during those eleven hours, must have been constantly—and, until yesterday, a set of loyalties strong enough that our first four questioners logged him as *unproductive*.

Then Veyra's people were given the file, and by dawn Tam Wrenlow was singing like a chapel choir.

He told them about the antechamber beneath the glacier. He told them about the woman who carved bones, though mercifully—not for any reason I'll ever understand—he either didn't know her name or kept it locked behind his teeth even after everything else came loose. He described the door that exists "between," which is a phrase I have now read in six reports and which makes my head ache every time, and he gave them the route: the old pilgrim stair above Keldharrow, the frozen lake, the crevasse field where you have to walk single file and trust the ice more than God.

The ice, as it turned out, would be the more reliable of the two.

I stood at the back of the reporting chamber while a lieutenant read the transcript aloud, and I watched the high command take notes, and I thought: *this is how empires die. Not in battle. In transcription.*

* * *

You should understand something about me before this chapter of my life gets written down—assuming anyone writes it down, assuming there's anyone left with ink and an opinion. I joined the Ashen Concord at nineteen because I believed in it. There. I've said it once; let no one make me say it again. I believed in the founding purge, or rather I believed in the story we told about it—that the old gods were plagues wearing crowns, that faith had to be amputated to save the body, that silence was mercy administered on a civilizational scale. I believed it the way you believe in the floor under your feet. Not as an argument. As gravity.

And then I grew up in the capital alongside a bone-carver's apprentice who used to steal apricots from the temple orchards and give them away to children who couldn't pay, and I watched that girl become a rumor, and the rumor become a warrant, and the warrant land on my desk with my own handwriting already on it, requesting resources.

Gravity, it turns out, can shift.

"Commander Halvard." Director Veyra's voice has the texture of paper that's been folded too many times. She sat at the head of the strategy table in the Thornfell garrison, and around her sat the men and women who run the occupation now—the ones who came up after the purge, who never saw a god, who think the whole war is a filing problem. "Your assessment of the glacier operation."

"It's a march," I said. "Two thousand troops across a crevasse field in late autumn, guided by a broken boy who may or may not be telling the truth, toward an object that officially does not exist."

"The object exists enough to warrant two thousand troops."

"That's my point exactly, Director. If it doesn't exist, we're marching an army onto a glacier to die of weather. If it does exist—" I let it hang. Nobody picked it up. They never pick it up. The Concord does not discuss what the Sigil might actually be, because discussing it would mean admitting the founding purge was not extermination but imprisonment, and imprisonment implies the prisoner might one day finish serving the sentence.

Veyra studied me with her gray, unhurried eyes. "You have reservations."

"I have logistics concerns." That was a lie, but it was the kind of lie the room required, the social lubricant of institutions everywhere. "My recommendation remains what it was at Hollow Bridge. Small force, surgical, led by someone who knows the terrain and—" I paused, and decided to spend a coin I couldn't afford, "—who knows the bone-carver."

"You've said before that you knew her."

"I said I grew up in the same district. That isn't the same thing."

"It's more than anyone else in this army can claim." She tapped the transcript. "The prisoner says the carver opens the door. The prisoner says she's already cut herself binding to it. Commander, if even half of this is true, we are not chasing a smuggler anymore. We are racing a coronation."

*A coronation.* I felt that word go into me like a splinter. Because I'd seen Quentin Villalobos last at a market stall in Lowmarsh, three days before I let her walk out of my custody for one night—one night I told myself was tactical, a thread left loose so the spool would unroll—and every hour since I had been paying interest on that decision. Two of her friends dead at Hollow Bridge because the elder of Keldharrow sold us a rumor and I spent it. A boy broken into confetti. And somewhere under a glacier, a woman whose hands I used to watch shape scraps of horn into birds, carving a door back into the world.

If she succeeded, the Concord's entire architecture of lies came down in an afternoon. And if she failed, she died on the ice, and I would be the man who put the arrow in the air.

Either way, I was going to hell by a road I paved myself, one reasonable decision at a time.

"There's one further matter," I heard myself say, and my mouth went dry, because I hadn't planned to say it and here it was anyway, arriving like weather. "A request. Regarding rules of engagement for the glacier operation."

"Go on."

"I'm requesting writ of execution." The words tasted like coins. "For this target only. No capture protocols. No transport to questioning. If we find the carver and her associates in the antechamber, they are to be killed quickly, on site."

Someone down the table made a small sound—surprise, maybe approval. Veyra didn't move. "Explain your reasoning, Commander."

Because capture means the collars, I almost said. Because I have seen what the questioners do now, and I did what they do to Tam Wrenlow to no one when I wore the older uniform, the cleaner uniform, the one where we shot people and at least let them keep their names. Because if it ends on that glacier I want it to end fast, for her sake, and mine, and because some rotted corner of me still believes that quickness is the last courtesy an institution like ours has left to offer.

What I said was: "Capture operations in confined ice environments fail. Prisoner transport across the crevasse field doubles our exposure window. Execution minimizes risk to personnel."

It was good doctrine. It was even true. It was also, all three of us in that room who mattered knew, complete nonsense, because nobody had requested execution writs for tactical reasons since the first year of the occupation, and Veyra's eyes stayed on me the way frost stays on glass.

"Denied," she said.

The word landed in the silent room like a stone in a well. You could hear it falling for a long time.

"Director—"

"Denied, Commander." She gathered the transcripts into a square, aligned their edges with two small taps, a habit of hers that always made me think of a woman squaring accounts. "The Sigil is not a smuggling matter. Whatever opens under that glacier will need to be understood. Studied. The individuals involved will be taken alive for comprehensive questioning, and the resonance-bearing artifacts recovered intact. Your personal history with the target is noted in the file, incidentally. I'd recommend you stop pretending it isn't relevant."

"My history is twenty years cold."

"Nothing about you is cold, Halvard. It's why you're useful." She rose, and the room rose with her, and just before she passed me she added, quietly, without looking at me, the sentence I would carry up the mountain like a second pack:

"We don't kill what we can still learn from. That's what separates us from the gods we burned."

* * *

I want to be fair to her. I've spent a lot of sleepless hours trying. Veyra is not a sadist. She genuinely believes the collars and the questioners are refinements—mercy, even, compared to mass executions, information purchased instead of lives wasted. She believes knowledge sanctifies anything done to obtain it. And the terrible thing, the thing that frightens me more than any god waking under a glacier, is that ten years ago I would have agreed with her. Five years ago. Last spring, possibly.

But standing in that chamber, refused, I understood something with perfect clarity: the Concord I joined is gone. What's marching up the mountain behind me is its ghost, wearing its uniforms, reciting its catechism, and the ghost has learned to enjoy the work. We burned the gods for being tyrants and we built something gentler-looking and infinitely more thorough, and now the machine requires fuel, and the fuel is people, eleven hours at a time.

They denied my request for a quick death. For her.

Do you understand? I asked permission to end it cleanly, and the institution said no—not out of mercy, but appetite. And I stood there and saluted. I saluted. My arm went up on rails I installed myself over twenty years of service, and I smiled the correct amount, and I walked out, and only in the corridor, alone with the torches, did my hand start shaking hard enough that I had to press it flat against the stone.

That's what rot looks like from inside. Not monsters. Salutes.

* * *

The mobilization took two days, and it was magnificent, and I mean that in the worst possible way.

The Ashen Choir, the troops call it—the column itself, the whole procession of it. Two thousand gray-and-white soldiers in column on the frozen pilgrim road, banners snapping, censers swinging, hymnals droning in unison so the sound rolls ahead of us into the valleys like weather announcing itself. The villages along the route empty their shrines preemptively now. They've learned. They stand in their doorways with that particular mountain stillness and watch us pass, and I ride at the front of it all, and every pair of eyes that tracks me adds another stone to the weight I carry, and the horse doesn't complain, because horses haven't been ruined yet.

On the second day, near the tree line where the road becomes ice, we passed a shrine the vanguard had burned that morning. Standard practice. The shrine was a low stone cairn with a wooden lintel, nothing grand, the kind of wayside marker shepherds have maintained on that pass since before the Concord existed—and the fire had blackened the stones but hadn't reached the lintel, which had fallen face-down into the snow, preserved.

There were carvings on it. Old ones. Hands, mostly. Dozens of small carved hands, palm-out, pressed into the wood in rows—a gesture I didn't recognize, and then did, all at once, standing in my stirrups with the Choir droning behind me.

*Stay.* That's what the open palm meant, in the old iconography they taught us to despise. *Stay. Be held. You are not alone on this road.*

I looked at that lintel for perhaps four seconds. Then I ordered it left where it lay—we were burning shrines, not collecting evidence—and rode on, and did not look back, because the column does not stop, and neither do I. That is the arrangement. That has always been the arrangement.

But I'll tell you what I thought, since no one else will ever ask. I thought: they prayed to be held, and we answered with fire, and now the thing they prayed to is waking up under a glacier, and we're marching two thousand souls straight into its courtroom singing hymns to a silence we manufactured.

We are not an army approaching a battle. We are a congregation approaching its reckoning, and none of us knows the liturgy.

* * *

The camp at the glacier's foot went up in the blue hour, cook fires and ward-fires and the endless gray tents, and I walked the perimeter because sleep and I have been estranged since Lowmarsh. The sentries saluted. Somewhere a soldier laughed at a joke I didn't hear. The glacier stood over everything, pale and patient, cracked through with veins of ancient blue, and I caught myself doing something I hadn't done since I was a boy in the capital: I listened.

Just listened. To the ice ticking as it settled. To the wind coming down off the heights carrying, or so my traitor imagination insisted, a sound like carving—small steel on small bone, patient, precise, closer than it had any right to be.

She was up there. Somewhere under all that white, with her stolen family and her bleeding arm and a door that didn't exist yet, working. And below her, winding up the mountain at first light, came everything I had spent twenty years building, obedient to orders I could no longer defend, commanded by a man who had asked to make her death quick and been told no.

At midnight, a runner found me at the perimeter. Dispatch from the advance scouts. He held the lantern while I read it, and I read it twice, because the second time I was hoping I'd imagined it.

The scouts had found the pilgrim stair above Keldharrow clear of snow. Cleared recently. Cleared deliberately—shoveled, sanded at the switchbacks, ropes anchored at the bad traverses. Someone had prepared the road for travelers. Either our side, preparing our approach.

Or hers, preparing her guests.

I folded the dispatch and looked up at the glacier, white on white under a sky rinsed clean of stars, and I felt the strangest sensation, one I couldn't name for a long moment, and then could: it was relief. Cold, guilty, unmistakable relief.

She wanted us to come.

God help me—I hoped she was ready.
