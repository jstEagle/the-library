## 16. Siege of the Antechamber

The antechamber smelled of old ice and older incense, and I thought—absurdly, kneeling on a glacier floor with a bone stylus in my teeth—that someone had at least tried to make it welcoming. Niches cut into the walls for candles long since burned to nothing. A bench worn smooth by generations of waiting pilgrims. Somebody's grandmother had swept this floor once, probably daily, and if she'd known that twenty years of silence would end with me bleeding ink into granite while smugglers stacked crates into a barricade behind her, she might have left a note of complaint.

"Quentin." Sefa's voice came through the dark like a lamp being lit. "How long?"

"As long as it takes."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have left." I spat blood-ink from my palm and flexed my forearm. The sigil there had gone past pain days ago, into some country beyond it where the skin had stopped being mine and started being architecture. Nine glyphs done. The tenth waited beneath them, half-sketched, and the tenth was the hard one—the threshold glyph, the one that didn't mark a door but *became* one.

Behind us, down the throat of the passage we'd come by, the ice sang. Not the ordinary groaning of a glacier settling its weight. A marching song.

"They're close," said Dagny from the barricade, her voice flat in the way it went flat when she was counting things she'd rather not count. "I count torches. Forty. Maybe fifty."

"Fifty," said Rell, who always argued with Dagny even when agreeing with her, "is generous. It's sixty."

"Wonderful," said Sefa. "We're outnumbered eight to five. Anyone feel like negotiating?"

Nobody laughed. That was the trouble with this crew lately—we'd buried two of our own at Hollow Bridge, and grief sat in the Kestrel Line like ballast you couldn't dump. But nobody left, either. Nobody had suggested, even once, that Quentin Villalobos could finish this alone somewhere else while the rest of us ran for the passes. I kept waiting for someone to be sensible about it. Sefa just looked at me whenever the silence stretched too long, and her look said: *you already know the answer, stop auditioning for abandonment.*

I bent back over the floor.

* * *

Here is what carving a god's doorway out of a glacier sounds like, for the record, in case anyone asks you to do it:

It sounds like the dead finally getting what they want.

They'd been murmuring since the ninth glyph—a low tide of voices rising through every relic I carried, through the amulet warm against my sternum, through the defector's shinbone strung at my belt, through my own carved arm as though the bone beneath the ink had joined their chorus. Nine names, over and over, but threaded now with others, hundreds of others, the whole sediment of Vessarene's dead stirred up like a riverbed by a flood.

*Vessarene. Vessarene remembers. Vessarene is coming home.*

"Quiet," I told them, the way I'd told them for years, and for the first time in my life they mostly obeyed—not because I commanded them, but because they understood what I was doing. Even the dead can recognize work being done on their behalf. It may be the only thing they still respect.

The tenth glyph required precision I did not have alone. My hands shook—from cold, from the blood-ink burning its way up my arm toward the shoulder, from the simple animal terror of hearing sixty torches sing closer. So I let them steady me. That was the bargain I'd made in chapter and verse with myself on the march up: the dead could hold my hands if they held them *still*. No flinching. No mercy. Carve true or don't carve at all.

Four hands inside my two. Ancestors I'd never met, guiding a descendant they'd never seen, both of us strangers bound by a job neither of us had applied for. My grandmother would have called it possession. I preferred to think of it as the world's oldest apprenticeship.

The stylus bit into stone. Ice chips flew. And under the floor—under the whole mountain—something turned over in its sleep.

* * *

"Torchlight!" Dagny's shout cracked off the walls. "Passage mouth! They've got a—no. Two ladders. They're bridging the crevasse."

"Then we stop being polite." Sefa moved along the barricade, checking knots, passing oilskins down the line. In the candlelight she looked exactly like what she was: a woman who had spent her whole life making dangerous things arrive safely, and had run out of patience for the reverse. "Rell, left flank. Dagny, right. I'm center. Nobody advances, nobody chases. We are a wall with opinions, nothing more. Quentin—"

"Don't talk to me," I said. "Talking costs strokes."

"It doesn't cost anything to know we're here."

I almost answered that. I'm glad now that I didn't, because the honest answer was going to be something unforgivably tender, and there were Concord soldiers crossing a bridge of ice forty yards away, and tenderness is a luxury you pay for later with interest.

The first volley of fire-arrows came arcing through the passage mouth and burst against our barricade in blooms of orange. Sefa's crew had chosen the ground well—the antechamber narrowed to a throat before opening wide, which meant the Concord could only come at us four abreast no matter how many of them there were. Numbers mean nothing in a corridor. I'd learned that from a dead general once, carving his knucklebone. He'd also learned it dying, so I took the lesson with appropriate gravity.

"Hold," Sefa said, conversational, as arrows thudded into the crates beside her head. "Hold. Let them build their little courage first."

The first wave came anyway, impatient, six men in ash-grey armor crowding the throat of the passage with shields locked. Dagny dropped the first with a crossbow bolt through the eye-slit. Rell heaved a crate down the slope of the floor and it caught two more at the knees. Sefa stood in the middle with her boarding axe and did the thing she does that I will never understand and never stop watching: she made violence look like bookkeeping. Efficient. Unhurried. Every swing entered in a ledger that would balance.

The wave broke and withdrew, leaving three bodies steaming on the ice.

"Quentin." Her voice again, tighter now. "Tell me you felt that."

"I felt it." I had. When the first soldier died, the chorus inside me had *flinched*—not for him, but around him, the way water parts. The dead could see everything happening here, every death feeding into the door I was building. I was carving with one hand and conducting a funeral with the other. "Keep them alive if you can. Every death makes the glyph harder."

"Did you hear that, everyone?" Sefa called down the line, dry as tinder. "New orders. We're holding this position *gently*."

Dagny snorted. Rell reloaded. And absurdly, horribly, I loved them all so much I couldn't breathe for a second—these ridiculous people arguing over crossbow angles while I stitched reality shut with blood—and I bent my head and let the love go into the carving too, because the tenth glyph wanted everything and I had finally stopped pretending I had anything held back.

* * *

The second wave came with fire-pots.

I heard them coming—heard the Concord officer ordering them, a clipped grey voice full of institutional certainty—and then the first pot shattered against the ceiling above the barricade and burning pitch rained down, and the antechamber became a place of shouting and smoke and Dagny screaming that her sleeve was on fire, and Rell beating it out with bare hands, and Sefa's voice cutting through all of it: "Wall holds! Wall holds! Quentin, whatever you're doing, do it *faster!*"

Faster. As if the problem were speed. As if I hadn't spent the last hour learning that the threshold glyph resisted haste the way ice resists a hot knife—it wanted depth, patience, *truth*, each stroke sunk to the bottom of the stone and then further, into whatever lived underneath stone. The dead guided my hand and even they were sweating, if the dead sweat. The tenth glyph was nearly half-done and the mountain knew it. The floor hummed under my knees like a plucked string that wouldn't fade.

Then the key-relic woke up.

Sefa had carried it her whole life without knowing what it was—an iron pendant shaped like a closed eye, priest-forged, passed down through her family like a curse dressed as an heirloom. She wore it into battle because she wore it everywhere; it was simply part of her, the way my scars were part of me. She was ten feet away, axe bloody, shouting the wall into holding, when the pendant began to pull.

I saw it before she did. It strained against its cord toward me—toward my arm—like a compass needle finding north, and Sefa glanced down at it mid-shout and her face did something complicated.

"Quentin!"

"I know!"

"It's trying to—"

"I *know!* Bring it here!"

She crossed the distance between us in four strides, through arrow-smoke and falling embers, and pressed the pendant into my bleeding palm—and the iron *moved*. I want to be precise about this, because later I'll be asked to testify about it and precision is all I'll have. The pendant did not melt. It *flowed*, the way mercury flows, the way a river accepts its bed. It slid across my palm, up my wrist, along the channels of the blood-ink like water finding furrows plowed for it, and it sank into the tenth glyph's unfinished curve and became part of the design.

The pain was—

There isn't a word. There's a family of words and none of them volunteer. It was the price of the arm paid again all at once, compounded, with the interest Sefa had once joked I'd owe someday. I heard myself make a sound I hope no one describes to me afterward. I heard Sefa catch my shoulders before I toppled. And through it all, through the white roar, the dead held my hands *still*, because that was the bargain, because even drowning I had signed a contract, and gods forgive me but I finished the stroke.

Half the tenth glyph now shone with fused iron, black and gleaming, warm as a living thing.

"The door," whispered the chorus, and for once they weren't many voices. They were one voice, vast and close, speaking through my own carved bones. "*The door begins.*"

* * *

After that, time went strange. I've tried since to put the rest in order and the order won't hold, so here it is as it comes:

Rell took a spear-graze across the ribs and stayed standing. Dagny ran out of bolts and started throwing things, including, at one point, a candlestick, which struck a Concord sergeant with such perfect accuracy that I intend to bring it up at every anniversary dinner forever. The barricade lost its left corner and Sefa rebuilt it out of a pew she ripped from the wall—pilgrim seating, two hundred years old, repurposed at last for the faith it was built for.

And between waves, in the gaps where both sides breathed, Sefa came and crouched beside me with her back to the enemy, shielding my work with her body the way you shield a match in wind.

"How much longer?" she asked. Third time she'd asked. Each time the answer got smaller.

"Minutes. Real ones."

"Good." She wiped blood from her cheek with the back of her axe-hand, missed most of it. "Because I'm nearly out of gentle."

"You were never gentle. You're *fair*. It's different."

Her mouth twitched. Even here. Especially here. "Don't make me laugh, I'm working."

"So am I." I nodded at the floor. At the half-born door humming under the smoke. At the light beginning, very faintly, to rise out of the carved lines themselves—a light with no source, the color of the moment just before dawn, the color the Silent Sigil must have worn when it was young and open and the world had room in it for more than one kind of weather.

Sefa stared at it. This woman who had smuggled everything illegal under the sun, who claimed to believe in nothing she couldn't load onto a cart, looked at that impossible light and said, quietly, with her hand resting on my shoulder:

"My grandmother used to sing a song about a door. I thought it was about a tavern." She swallowed. "Carve, Quentin. We'll keep your minutes."

She went back to the wall. That was the whole speech. Found families don't monologue; they delegate.

* * *

The last stroke wanted the blood I was already losing, so I gave it.

Iron-light poured along the tenth glyph and met the fused key-relic and the whole design—arm, floor, antechamber, glacier, mountain—shivered once, like a loom completing a pattern. The niches in the walls lit by themselves, candles that hadn't existed lighting flames that needed none. The chorus of the dead rose to something that wasn't sound anymore but *pressure*, joyous and terrible, nine names becoming nine hundred becoming a nation of names, and under the floor, under everything, I felt the far side of the door press gently against the near side, the way a traveler presses a hand to a gate from outside, asking permission.

The door began to exist.

Not open. Not yet. But *present*—a seam of pre-dawn light hanging in the air above the carved floor, thin as a blade, real as debt. Through it I could hear breathing that wasn't the dead's. Something enormous and patient, waiting to be asked in properly, the way my mother taught me guests must be.

"Quentin." Sefa's voice, hoarse now, from the barricade. "They're regrouping. Big push coming. Whatever happens next—" she paused, and I heard her choose honesty, because I'd demanded it of her and she'd demanded it back, "—whatever happens next, it's been an honor smuggling you."

"The honor," I said, pressing my palm flat against the seam of light, feeling it warm and alive and *almost*, "was entirely mine."

The light widened a finger's width.

And down the passage, faint but unmistakable, came a new sound cutting through the Concord's war-drums: horns. Not assault horns. Command horns. The high command had arrived at the mouth of the glacier—and with them, I knew without knowing how, the man they'd sent to burn us all.

The door trembled, hungry and half-made, and the first of Halvard's firepots arced through the dark.
