## 1. The Knucklebone Winter

The cold came early to the Voss valley that year, sliding down from the Thornfell peaks like a debt collector who knew the door would be opened. Elodie Voss heard it in the timbers of her workshop before she saw it — a faint creak and settle, wood contracting, the mountain exhaling through every seam she'd never quite caulked. By dusk the first snow had arrived in earnest, fat flakes falling with the unhurried confidence of something that intended to stay until spring.

She did not mind. Winter was her season. In winter the smugglers stayed off the passes, the Crown's Wardens kept to their garrison at Greyford, and the only visitors she received were the ones who could not leave.

"Patience," she said to the bone on her bench.

It did not answer, of course. Not yet. It was only a finger-bone — an index finger, left hand, taken with the widow Maren's written consent and a sealed writ from the parish clerk, all proper and paid-for — and it lay on her workbench like a small pale question. She had cleaned it that morning, boiled it with ash and rosemary until it gave up its last ghosts of grease, and now it sat drying in the lamplight, smooth as a river pebble and just about as silent.

Elodie pulled her shawl tighter around her shoulders and looked out the window at the darkening yard. The workshop stood apart from her cottage by twenty paces of packed snow, a low stone building she'd inherited from her mother along with the tools, the reputation, and the silence. Her mother had called it *the listening house*, though never where anyone could hear her say it.

In the valley they called her other things. Bone-witch, when they were drunk or brave. Mistress Voss, when they wanted something. The carver, when they spoke of her to strangers with a careful flatness that meant *we don't fully claim her, but she's ours*. Elodie had long ago stopped keeping score. Names were weather; they passed over you and moved on, and if you built your roof well enough, none of them got inside.

She lit a second lamp and turned back to the bench.

The work itself required no gift at all. That was the part no one understood — not the widow Maren, not the merchants in Lowbridge who paid in silver for carved keepsakes, not even Renn Carrow, who had seen more of the strange world than any man should and still watched her hands like they might do a trick. The carving was craft. Ten years of apprenticeship under a mother who believed sentiment dulled blades: how to read the grain of a bone the way a carpenter reads timber, where the hidden channels ran, how much pressure the material would forgive and how much it would remember. Any competent carver could take a finger-bone and render it into a bead, a charm, a little sainted figure for a mantelshelf.

What Elodie did afterward was hers alone.

She picked up the bone and turned it in her fingers. Warm from the lamp, faintly porous, alive-seeming in the way dead things sometimes were when you spent enough hours with them. Somewhere in Lowbridge, a woman named Sera Colm had died in the autumn fevers, aged thirty-four, leaving behind a husband who could not stop talking to her empty chair and two children who had already begun to forget the sound of her voice. That forgetting was the true enemy. Grief-service, her mother had called the trade. You didn't sell the dead back to the living. You sold the living one last honest hour with what remained.

Elodie set the bone in the clamp, took up her finest blade, and began.

* * *

The knife whispered along the first channel and the workshop changed.

It was never dramatic. People imagined lights, voices thundering, spirits wringing their translucent hands. What actually happened was quieter and far worse: the room simply acquired a second occupant. The air thickened by a degree. The lampflame leaned, very slightly, toward the bench, as if eavesdropping.

And then, beneath the sound of the blade, a voice.

Not in the ear. Never in the ear. It arrived the way a tune arrives that you realize you've been humming for an hour without knowing it — suddenly present, suddenly undeniable, threaded into the scratch of steel on bone.

*—cold,* said the voice of Sera Colm. *Why is it always cold at the end of things.*

"They say the fever breaks feeling first," Elodie murmured. She kept carving. It went easier when she spoke aloud, though she'd never been able to explain why, and she had stopped expecting the world to explain itself to her some years ago. "Warm now. I've a fire going."

*I can't feel it.*

"No. I expect you can't."

This was the etiquette of it, learned over years and paid for in sleepless nights: you didn't pretend. The dead knew they were dead, or knew something was profoundly wrong, and lying to them was cruelty dressed up as kindness. You gave them the truth in small pieces, the way you'd give bread to someone starving — slowly, or they'd choke.

The blade found the second channel. Outside, the wind picked up, worrying the shutters, and the snow came down thicker, erasing the path between cottage and workshop, between the world and the world.

*He'll let them forget me.* Sera's voice had texture to it now, the way cloth has texture — rough in places, worn smooth in others. Fear, mostly, worn smooth by three months of being dead. *That's what I keep thinking. Tam will be kind, Tam was always kind, but kind men tidy things away, and I don't want to be tidied. I want — * the voice frayed, caught, steadied — *I want Nellie to know I sang to her. Every night. Even when she was too young to know what singing was. Tell her that. Someone tell her that.*

"I'll carve it so he can't help but hear it," Elodie said.

*Can you do that?*

"I'm doing it."

And she was. This was the gift, if gift was the right word for a thing that had never once asked whether she wanted it: as the blade opened the channels of the bone, whatever the dead woman had held closest rose up through the material like sap through cut wood, and Elodie's hands — not her conscious mind, her *hands* — translated it into form. A curve here that would catch lamplight like the curve of a lullaby. A groove there whose depth held the exact weight of a name spoken nightly into small ears. Anyone could look at the finished piece and see a lovely ornament. But the husband would hold it, and the husband would weep, because somewhere in the geometry of it lived his wife's voice, and grief recognizes its own the way a dog knows its name.

Her mother had called it translation. The Wardens, if they ever bothered to write it down in their ledgers, would call it traffic with the deceased, third-class offense, fine and confiscation. Elodie called it Tuesday, most weeks. Work was work.

*The cold again,* Sera said, after a while. *It comes in waves. Is that normal?*

"For the dead? I honestly don't know. You're the expert."

A pause. Then — and this was rare, this was the part that made the lonely hours almost bearable — the dead woman laughed. It was a small laugh, frayed at the edges, but real, and it moved through the bone and up Elodie's wrists and settled somewhere behind her sternum where she kept everything she didn't know what to do with.

*Tam said you'd be frightening. He said the carver in the mountains is half corpse herself.*

"Tam sounds charming."

*He's frightened. Frightened men insult what they're afraid of.* Another pause, longer. *You're not frightening. You're just very alone, aren't you.*

Elodie's blade paused for exactly half a heartbeat. Then it moved on.

"Mind your own dead," she said, "and I'll mind mine."

But the words sat wrong on the way out, the way a lie sits wrong, and she was still turning that over — the strange indignity of being seen through by someone with no eyes — when the last channel opened and the voice poured through it whole, complete, the final reservoir of Sera Colm emptying into the carving:

*Nellie. My song. My girl. Even when you couldn't understand me, I was singing. Find the song. It's in the shape of it. Mama's in the shape of it.*

Then quiet. The particular quiet of a room after a guest has gone — not empty, exactly, but rearranged. The lampflame straightened. The wind filled the space the voice had left, hectoring the shutters, and Elodie sat with her hands flat on the bench and breathed until her chest unclenched.

Done, then. She would wrap the piece in wool and waxcloth tomorrow, and the courier would come up from Lowbridge within the week, weather permitting, and somewhere a man named Tam Colm would hold his wife's finger-bone in his cupped palms and hear, without knowing how, the shape of a lullaby. And Nellie, years from now, grown and angry at the world the way children of the dead often were, would find the song in the shape of it.

Good work. Honest work. She wiped down her blades and told herself she felt nothing at all.

* * *

Later, in the cottage kitchen, she ate standing up — a habit her mother had deplored and death had apparently failed to correct — bread and hard cheese and pickled cabbage, the meal of a woman who cooked for one and refused to make ceremony of it. Through the window over the basin she could see the workshop, dark now, its roof wearing six inches of new snow. Beyond it, the valley dropped away into white vagueness, and beyond that, the black shoulders of Thornfell held up a sky going hard and clear with frost.

Someone was moving down the track.

She saw the lantern first — a smudge of yellow swinging in the whiteout, too steady to be a lost animal, too slow to be a Warden patrol. Patrols moved in pairs and pairs moved fast, wanting their rounds done and their fires lit. This was one person, picking a careful line past the frozen wash-hole, and Elodie set down her bread and did the arithmetic she always did, the arithmetic that had kept her neutral and therefore alive for fifteen years.

Smugglers came to her in autumn, before the passes closed, paying good silver to have bones read — a dead partner, a dead rival, occasionally a dead lawman whose last words had market value. She served them and said nothing to anyone, and in return they left her caches and her clients strictly alone. The Wardens came to her in summer, courteous as tax collectors, asking nothing and noting everything, and she served them tea and said nothing worth writing down, and in return they filed her existence under *harmless, monitor annually*. Between the two, balanced like a stone in a wall, she had built a life. Narrow, cold, entirely her own. The trick, her mother had said, is to be useful to everyone and necessary to no one. Necessary things get fought over. Useful things get left alone.

The lantern reached her gate.

A man's silhouette, broad-shouldered, snow to the knee, and even before he raised the light she knew the set of him — the rolling seaman's walk adapted to ice, the hat brim crushed by a hundred storms. Renn Carrow. Smuggler, occasional hero, permanent complication. He stamped the snow off his boots at her gate with the exaggerated care of a man announcing himself as harmless, which was itself a thing to be wary of.

Elodie opened the door before he could knock, denying him the pleasure of the request.

"You're a fortnight early," she said. "Passes are closed."

"Passes are closed to people who respect passes." He pushed back his hood, and the lantern showed her a face she knew too well: handsome the way a well-worn knife-handle is handsome, forty-odd years of weather and wit carved deep around grey eyes that never quite finished laughing. Tonight the laughter was banked, though. Something else sat behind it. "Evening, Mistress Voss. Cold enough for you?"

"It's cold enough for sense, which is why I'm surprised to see you. Come in before you freeze, and mind the floor, Bracken muddied it."

Inside, he thawed by the stove with the shameless efficiency of a cat, accepting tea, refusing food twice and accepting it the third time, as was ritual. He talked — the road, the price of tin in Lowbridge, a Warden sergeant named Corvane newly posted to Greyford who was, by all accounts, neither corrupt nor lazy, which made him the worst kind. Elodie listened with half an ear and all of her attention, the way you listen to weather reports. Renn's chatter was a surface; the current ran underneath.

He set down his cup. "I've a job, if you want it."

"I have work."

"Different kind." He turned his hands to the fire, and for a moment the charm dropped away entirely, the way a mask drops when the wearer thinks no one's looking, and what was underneath looked old. Tired. Guilty, maybe, though with Renn it was always impossible to say what of. "There's talk on the high trails. Old-tongue singing, they're saying. Up near the Spire side. Shepherds heard it before the snows came and won't go back up. And the Wardens heard the shepherds." He glanced at her. "Corvane's asking questions in Lowbridge. About you, among others. Who reads bones, who hears things, who might know old songs."

"I don't know old songs."

"I know you don't." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "That's rather the point of telling you. Keep your head down, keep your door shut, and if anything — anyone — comes to that door that shouldn't, you send for me before you send for anyone else. There's coin in it for the warning and more in it for the silence."

"What kind of anyone?"

Renn looked into the fire for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window glass. When he answered, his voice had gone carefully light, which from Renn Carrow was the loudest alarm there was.

"Odd kind," he said. "Small kind. The kind that makes old bones sing instead of whisper." He stood, draining his tea, all business again, the mask firmly back in place. "Forget it. Probably shepherd nonsense. Just — keep your door barred, Elodie. Winter's coming in mean this year."

He left an hour later, back into the storm, and she watched his lantern shrink down the track until the snow took it. *Keep your door barred.* She laughed once, without humor, and banked the fire.

Her mother had taught her the bar, the bolt, the careful neutrality. Fifteen years of narrow, cold, entirely her own. Necessary to no one. Useful to everyone.

She was blowing out the lamp when the wind shifted, carrying up from the valley floor a thread of sound beneath the storm — faint, high, wordless, unmistakably a child's voice humming a melody that no kingdom she had ever heard of had written.

Elodie Voss stood in the dark of her kitchen, hand flat against the cold window glass, and listened.

Down the valley, her finished carvings — a shelf of them, wrapped for delivery, silent for months — had begun, very softly, to whisper.
