## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The blizzard came down the valley like something hunting.

Elodie Voss heard it before she saw it — a low pressure in her ears, the pines on the ridge above the workshop groaning in a register that had nothing to do with wind and everything to do with weight. Thirty years of living alone under Thornfell's shoulder had taught her to read storms the way other people read faces. This one meant business. It meant shutters latched, fire fed, door barred, and no reason under heaven or the Crown to open either before morning.

She was doing exactly all of those things when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when they have been taught that knocking too hard is a sin, or when their knuckles simply haven't the strength left for anything louder.

Elodie stood with her hand flat against the bar of the door and did not move. The fire popped behind her. Snow hissed against the shuttered window like sand thrown at glass. And outside, in the white roaring dark, whoever had knocked — whatever had knocked — waited without knocking again.

That was the part that undid her. A lost traveler would hammer. A beggar would wail. A Warden would announce himself with the courtesy of a man who owns the door he's standing behind. But this — three small knocks and then silence, patient as a grave — was not how the living asked to be let in.

*It's how the dead ask*, thought some unhelpful corner of her mind, the same corner that had been whispering since she was nine years old and first heard her grandmother's finger-bone murmur *the kettle's on* two days after the funeral.

"Go away," Elodie said to the door. Her voice came out rough from disuse; she hadn't spoken aloud since dawn, since the mule driver who'd brought her salt and taken away a carved knucklebone wrapped in linen, payment for a widow in Lowmarsh whose mother had died midwinter with words still caught between her teeth. *Tell my daughter the debt is forgiven,* the bone had said, over and over, until Elodie had carved the last syllable smooth and sent it down the mountain. That was the trade. That was the whole of it: bone in, voice out, coin across the counter, and nobody's grief becoming hers.

The knocks did not come again. But something else did — a sound so thin she almost mistook it for the wind finding a gap in the eaves.

Singing.

No. Not singing. *Humming.* A melody without words, rising through the blizzard's roar with the impossible clarity of a thing that refuses to be weather. Three notes up, two notes down, then a pause like a held breath, then the phrase again — and Elodie's workshop began to answer.

She turned slowly, her breath gone shallow in her chest.

On the shelf above her workbench sat the finished pieces: the widow's knucklebone already crated for delivery, a jawbone she'd carved for a merchant in Greyford whose father had been a taciturn man even in life, a small rib-whistle commissioned by no one and kept because its voice had pleased her, half a dozen other relics waiting on their words like letters waiting on postage. They were silent. They were always silent unless she touched them, unless she asked, unless she paid the price of listening with an hour of her sleep.

They were not silent now.

The jawbone chattered softly against its cloth, a dry rattle like dice in a gambler's fist. The rib-whistle had begun to keen — a high, wavering thread of sound that slid beneath the humming outside and harmonized with it, note for note, as though it had been waiting thirty years for someone to sing its key. And the knucklebone, the dead woman's finger-bone she had carved only that morning, was whispering.

Elodie crossed the room without deciding to and put her ear to the shelf.

Not words. Not any words she knew. The dead spoke to her in the tongue they'd last used alive — Lowmarsh burr, valley dialect, once a trader's clipped border-speech, once, memorably, the formal court-Tongue of a hanged tax assessor who had wanted his wife told exactly where he'd hidden the second purse. She had never heard a dead person speak anything she couldn't name.

This was older. The sounds came out of the little bone round and strange, vowels that opened like doors into dark rooms, consonants that clicked and flowed like water over stones. And threaded through it, underneath it, unmistakable even though she could not translate a single syllable:

*Joy.*

The bone was singing back to the child in the snow.

Elodie Voss had spent her whole adult life building a fortress out of competence. She rose early, worked late, charged fairly, lied to no one, owed no one, loved no one, and needed no one, and if the loneliness of that arithmetic sometimes woke her at three in the morning like a stone dropped into a well, well — the well was deep and the splash never reached the top where anyone could hear it. Smugglers bought her silence with fair prices. Wardens tolerated her neutrality with grudging respect. The valley called her the bone-witch of the high hollow and left her be, which was the closest thing to affection she'd ever asked for.

Every instinct she owned, every rule she had carved into herself harder than any bone, said the same thing: *Do not open that door. Whatever sings your work to life is not your business. Nothing is your business. That is how you survive.*

Her hand lifted the bar anyway.

Cold hit her like a wall of hands. Snow swirled into the doorway, fat flakes glowing orange in the firelight, and there in the drift against her threshold — half buried, lips blue, lashes furred with ice, wearing a coat that might once have belonged to someone twice this child's size — stood a girl of perhaps eight years, maybe nine, with dark hair plastered to her forehead and eyes that were open far too calmly for a body that should have been screaming with cold.

The humming stopped. The child looked up at her.

And Elodie, who had heard ten thousand last words and never once flinched, felt her heart stutter at what she saw in that small frost-rimed face: not terror, not pleading. *Recognition.* As though Elodie were the one who had been expected. As though the knock had been a formality.

"Well," Elodie said, to the storm more than the child, "you can't stand there."

It was not welcome. It was surrender dressed in irritation. She hauled the girl inside by the collar of the enormous coat, kicked the door shut, and threw the bar across it with a violence that surprised them both.

* * *

The child did not shiver properly, which frightened Elodie more than screaming would have.

She stripped off the frozen coat, rubbed the girl down with rough wool, wrapped her in the good blanket — the one Elodie herself used, the only soft thing she owned — and held a cup of melted snow warmed with honey to cracked lips. The child drank mechanically, obediently, like a puppet remembering its strings, and all the while her skin stayed cold in a way that made the hair rise on Elodie's arms. Not corpse-cold. Something between. Cold the way a cellar is cold: preserved.

"Frostbite takes fingers," Elodie said, chafing the small blue hands between her own. "Toes. Noses. Ears. So you'll tell me where you came from, and you'll tell me now, and we'll hope to every god I don't recognize that it wasn't from over the pass, because nothing crosses the pass in this."

The child looked at her. Opened her mouth.

What came out was the language from the bones.

It poured out of the girl fluently, urgently, a full sentence or a speech or a lament — Elodie couldn't tell — delivered with the earnest intensity of someone explaining something vitally important to someone who ought to understand. The vowels rolled. The consonants clicked. And in the corner of the room, the rib-whistle keened softly along.

The girl stopped. Watched Elodie's face. Tried again, slower, and this time Elodie caught something — the shape of a question, perhaps, or the shape of a name, offered and repeated and pointed at with one thawing finger pressed to a small chest.

"Ash," Elodie guessed. Or something like Ash — a sound like ash, like the grey residue of a fire long out.

The finger tapped the chest again. *Ash.* Then the finger swung around and pointed at Elodie, and the eyebrows went up, and the mouth opened expectantly.

"Elodie," she heard herself say. "Voss. Bone-carver. Fool, apparently, for letting in whatever you are."

Ash considered this. Repeated it — mangled it, rather, into something with far too many vowels — and seemed satisfied. Then, with the terrifying practicality of children everywhere, the girl pointed past Elodie's shoulder at the workbench, at the jawbone still trembling faintly under its cloth, and said something brief and wondering, and the dead man's bone rattled once in answer, and Ash laughed.

It was a small, rusty laugh, the laugh of someone who hadn't used the machinery in a while. But it was a laugh. And it did something to Elodie's chest that she resented immediately and completely.

"No," she said, to the child, to the whistle, to the bones, to the storm, to God. "No. You will not talk to my work. My work is inventory. My work is coin. You don't get to make friends with it."

Ash tilted her head, and her eyes — dark, calm, old in a way that no child's eyes had any right to be — moved deliberately from the jawbone to Elodie's face and back again, as if to say: *too late.*

* * *

By full dark the storm had buried the world and the child had finally begun to shiver, which Elodie greeted with disproportionate relief, wrapping her closer to the fire and forcing broth into her spoonful by spoonful. Warmth returned to the small hands. Color crept back into the face. And with warmth came exhaustion — the great, boneless collapse of a body that has been holding itself together past all reason and has finally found permission to stop.

Ash fell asleep sitting up, mid-swallow, and Elodie caught the cup before it spilled and sat for a long time afterward with the sleeping weight gradually tipping sideways until it rested against her arm, and she did not move it.

This was the moment, she would think later, when she lost. Not at the door. Here. In the firelight, with a stranger's child breathing against her sleeve, and the storm sealing the valley shut behind them, and the knowledge — cold and certain as a blade laid flat against skin — of what came next.

Because Elodie knew what the valley would say. Knew what the Wardens would say, when word traveled, as word always traveled, of the bone-witch harboring some wild child who sang in no tongue any priest or soldier could name. She knew the law about unregistered persons found in the high country — knew it the way smugglers know tariffs, the way everyone in Thornfell knew the things that kept them alive: *report, surrender, ask nothing.* She knew that the truce the Crown kept was older than the Crown itself and enforced with a thoroughness that made honest men careful and clever men silent. She knew that people who asked questions about old things in this kingdom had a habit of stopping asking anything at all.

And she knew — with the gift that had been her livelihood and her curse since childhood — that the child was soaked in the old language the way a wick is soaked in oil. That the bones in this room had answered her. That whatever Ash was, wherever she had come from, the Spire was in her somewhere, in her blood or her breath or her name, and the Crown had spent six hundred years making very sure that nothing of the Spire walked free.

The sensible thing — the survivable thing, the thing the fortress of her competence demanded — was to feed her tonight, warm her tonight, and carry her down to the Warden post at Greyford the moment the storm broke. Collect whatever bounty was offered. Return to her quiet life with her rules intact.

Ash stirred in her sleep. Murmured — two soft syllables in the old tongue, a question or a comfort — and the knucklebone on the shelf whispered back like a mother answering from another room.

"Oh, hells," Elodie said quietly, to no one, meaning *I cannot believe I am doing this.*

She built the fire up. She set the last of her bread to rise. She took the unfinished carving from the vise — a hip-bone, a farmer's, awaiting its message — and set it aside, because there would be no more messages going down the mountain tomorrow, no deliveries, no traffic, no trace. Tomorrow she would begin deciding how to hide a child.

Outside, the blizzard roared on like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and beneath the wind — faint, persistent, threading through the cracks in the shutters as though the storm itself had learned the melody — Elodie could hear the mountains humming.

Three notes up. Two notes down.

The bones listened. And this time, so did she.
