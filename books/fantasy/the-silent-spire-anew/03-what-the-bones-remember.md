## 3. What the Bones Remember

The child had been with her for six days, and Elodie Voss still hadn't decided whether she was keeping it.

*Keeping.* As if the child were a stray dog, or a stolen horse. She caught herself using the word and disliked it, and used it again anyway, because there was no better one. The child ate what she gave it — slowly at first, then with the desperate efficiency of someone who had learned that food was not a promise but an accident — slept in the alcove behind the stove, and watched everything with eyes the color of wet slate. It did not cry. That was the part that unsettled her most. Even in grief-work, Elodie had heard enough weeping to fill a cathedral. This child made no sound of sorrow at all, as though sorrow were a language it had been ordered not to speak.

She had taken to calling it *the child* even in her own head, which was its own kind of cowardice. A name was a claim. A name was a door you couldn't shut again.

On the sixth morning, the blizzard finally broke, and the light through the workshop's south window came down so hard and white that the hanging carvings threw shadows like a forest of ribs across the floor. Elodie was working at the bench when she noticed the child standing among them, barefoot on the cold stone — it refused the wool socks she'd knitted, wore them instead wound around its wrists like bracelets — looking up at the finished pieces swaying on their cords.

Bone dust hung in the light. The child reached up and touched one small carving, a knuckle-bone bird Elodie had made for no one, from nothing, just to keep her hands busy on a night she didn't want to think about — and the bird whispered.

Not creaked. Not clicked against its neighbors in the draft. *Whispered.*

Elodie set down her graver very carefully, the way one sets down a blade one might need.

The whisper was hers to hear alone; she knew that much after twenty years. Clients heard only the click of bone and her voice relaying what came through, and they went away comforted or devastated, depending. But this — the child's fingertips resting on the wing-joint, the bird's carved throat moving through sounds that were almost words, almost the old grief-language her gift translated without effort — this was different. The whisper wasn't the dead woman whose finger-bone had gone into the bird. The whisper was the child.

Or rather: the whisper was passing *through* the child, the way water passes through a reed, and coming out shaped by something older than the reed.

"Stop," Elodie said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word.

The child turned. Looked at her. Did not stop — or could not. Its hand stayed on the bird, and the whisper thickened into syllables, and Elodie's gift, which had never once needed coaxing, began to translate against her will.

*Cold,* said the bones. *Long cold. But warm here. Warm where the small hands are.*

It was nonsense. It was the bird-bone woman, dead eleven years, speaking a sentiment no client had ever paid for, and yet every word arrived in Elodie's mind wearing meaning like a coat cut precisely to fit. She pressed both palms flat against the bench to steady herself.

"I said stop." Louder now. The carvings overhead swung gently on their cords, and a dozen whispers stirred at once, a soft rustle like leaves deciding to fall.

The child took its hand away. The silence that followed had weight, like the pause after a bell.

They looked at each other across the dusty shafts of light. Elodie realized her heart was going too fast, and that she was afraid — properly afraid, the old animal fear, not the professional unease her work sometimes brought. Twenty years of hearing the dead had taught her exactly one thing about fear: it was always worse before the bones spoke than after. Once they spoke, it stopped being the unknown and became merely terrible, and terrible could be managed.

So she did what she always did. She worked.

"Come here," she said, and beckoned, and to her surprise the child came.

***

She chose the jawbone deliberately.

It sat in the third drawer of the cabinet, wrapped in linen that had once been white, in a bed of ash she'd raked smooth herself. A man's jaw, weathered gray, found by a shepherd on the high scree two winters past and carried down wrapped in a coat because the shepherd's grandmother had told him once that some bones shouldn't be left out under the open sky. He'd paid Elodie nothing. He'd only wanted it *heard*, he said, and gone away pale when she told him what it said.

The jaw belonged to a man who had died alone above the treeline, frozen kneeling, facing east. His last words had been a prayer to a god whose name Elodie hadn't recognized — and she recognized every god worshipped within three hundred miles, because the dying named their gods the way drowning men name the shore. This man had prayed to something older. Something with a name like stone grinding on stone.

She had never carved it. Some pieces you carve and some you simply keep, the way you keep a letter you can't answer.

Now she unwrapped it on the bench, and the child leaned in close, and its breath fogged the lamplight, and its slate-colored eyes went wide — not with recognition exactly. With *homesickness*. That was the only word for it. The child looked at the ancient jaw the way Elodie imagined a person might look through a window at a lit house in a country they'd been exiled from before they could walk.

"That's not yours," Elodie said quietly. "No one's, really."

The child looked up at her. And then it did the thing that ended the life Elodie Voss had been living, though neither of them knew it yet.

It touched the jawbone with one finger.

***

The sound that came out of the bone was not a whisper.

Every piece Elodie had ever handled had spoken in whispers — the dead, in her experience, were quiet the way rivers are quiet from far off, their noise worn down by distance and time until all that remained was murmur. This was not murmuring. This was a *choir*.

The jawbone opened — not physically, the hinge did not move, she would swear afterward on anything anyone asked her to swear on — but the sound that poured from it was the sound of a mouth full of voices, hundreds of them, thousands, layered like sediment, like rings in wood, and they were all speaking the same language at different ages. Children's voices. Old men's voices. Voices that sounded the way starlight looks.

And Elodie's gift translated.

It had never done this. Her gift translated the recently dead, the ones whose language was her language or near enough, and occasionally the pious dead whose gods she could half-name. It had never taken a tongue that predated every kingdom she knew, every border on every map, and rendered it into meaning inside her skull like a key turning in a lock that had waited all her life for this particular key.

*We kept the count,* said the voices, in the tone of people reciting something learned so young it has become breathing. *We kept the count through the dark and the dark through the count. Six hundred turnings of the mountain's breath. We are patient. We are so patient.*

The child's finger stayed on the jaw. Its face had gone calm in a way that frightened Elodie more than terror would have — the calm of someone hearing a familiar song, following along.

*But the Spire wakes,* the voices said, and here the harmony fractured, splintered into dissonance, voices stepping on voices, urgency climbing over patience like fire climbing a stair. *The Spire wakes, the Spire wakes, the seal is thinning, we feel it thinning, who thins the seal? Who walks above us? Who—*

And then, cutting through all of it, one voice alone, closer than the others, a voice like a hand laid flat on a table:

*Ash.*

Elodie's breath stopped.

The word — the *name*, because that was what it was, she understood it the way you understand a thing falling on you, without any process at all — arrived carrying its meaning whole. In the old language it meant the grey residue of burning. What remains when everything else is consumed. What is left behind to prove there was fire.

The child lifted its finger from the jawbone.

The choir cut off mid-syllable. Silence slammed back into the workshop like a door in wind. The hanging carvings stilled. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, snow slid off the roof and hit the drift below with a soft whump that sounded, in the ringing quiet, enormous.

"Ash," Elodie said aloud. Her voice sounded strange to her, scraped thin. "That's your name?"

The child looked at her. And nodded — once, slow, solemn as a coronation.

Something in Elodie's chest performed a maneuver she had no intention of permitting and could not prevent. She covered it by being practical, which was her lifelong method for surviving her own heart.

"All right," she said. "Ash, then." She pulled the stool out from the bench. "Sit down. Because either I've finally lost my mind — which, between us, has been a live possibility for years — or those bones just said things I need to understand. And you're the only person in this valley, possibly in this kingdom, who knows what they said."

Ash sat. Folded its hands in its lap with a gravity no child should own. Waited.

Elodie wrapped the jawbone back in its linen with fingers that were not quite steady, and thought about the shepherd's grandmother, and her warning about bones left under the open sky. She thought about the word *seal*, and how it had arrived in her mind already heavy with meaning — a seal like wax, a seal like a wall, a seal like the lid on something that very badly wanted out.

"The Spire," she said. "There's no Spire in Thornfell. There's the Grey Needles, there's Widow's Rest, there's the old watchtower at Fordham that isn't even—" She stopped. Rubbed her face with both hands. "You know what I'm talking about, don't you. You know where it is."

Ash tilted its head. Then, deliberately, it raised one hand and pointed — not out the window, not toward the pass, but *down*. Straight down, through the floor, through the mountain's roots, through however many hundred feet of cold stone stood between the workshop and whatever lay beneath.

Down.

"And they're waking up." Elodie's mouth had gone dry. "The ones talking through that jaw. They're waking up."

Ash's expression did something complicated then — the first truly unguarded thing Elodie had seen it do. Fear crossed it, yes, but underneath the fear was a longing so naked and so vast that Elodie had to look away from it, the way you look away from a stranger weeping in public. Homesickness for a place that was a prison. Grief for a door that was also a grave.

Then Ash shook its head. Slowly. And held up one small hand, palm out, fingers spread, and closed them one by one — *not waking*, the gesture said, *not yet* — before pointing down again, and then, with two fingers, walking them across its own knee toward the edge and letting them drop off into empty air.

Going there. Falling toward it.

"Oh no," Elodie said, to the child, to the room, to the mountain underneath them all. "Oh, absolutely not. Whatever is down there, whoever sealed it and whatever it costs to open it — that is Crown business, Warden business, war business, and I am none of those things. I carve bones for grieving widows and I stay neutral and I stay *alive*, which is a profession, not an accident. I have spent twenty years making sure nobody important remembers I exist."

Ash watched this speech with the patient attention of someone who has already seen the ending.

"You're a child," Elodie said. "You fell out of a blizzard into my yard. You don't get to point down at buried empires and expect me to—"

The knock, when it came, was three measured blows on the workshop door. Iron-tipped. Spaced like a heartbeat.

Neither of them moved. Outside, the afternoon light had begun to fail, and the snowfields beyond the window had gone the blue-white color of old scars. Elodie knew that knock. Every smuggler and Warden in Thornfell knocked differently, and that knock — polite, unhurried, certain of its welcome — belonged to the Crown.

She looked at Ash. Ash looked back, slate eyes steady, one small hand still resting on the table beside the linen-wrapped jawbone that had sung with a thousand voices of the long-buried dead.

"Stay quiet," Elodie whispered, and rose to answer the door, and hated how calmly her feet carried her across the room, as if the world had not just tilted beneath them.
