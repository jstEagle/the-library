## 4. The Warden's Courtesy

The snow had stopped falling by the time Warden-Sergeant Corvane rode into the valley, which he took as a small professional courtesy from the weather. Snow made men sloppy. It softened tracks, muffled voices, and gave liars an excuse to keep their doors shut. A clear, hard morning after a blizzard, on the other hand, laid everything bare: every footprint pressed into fresh powder, every chimney smoking where it shouldn't be, every door in the village of Thorn's Hollow opening just far enough to let a pair of frightened eyes peer out before closing again like mouths that knew better than to speak first.

He counted eleven chimneys breathing smoke as his column of four riders came down the switchback road. Eleven households. He had inspected this valley twice before, in six years of service along the Thornfell frontier, and both times the number had been twelve. The twelfth belonged to the bone-carver on the mountainside — Voss, Elodie, age thirty-one, licensed under the Grief-Trade Act to render last words for the bereaved. Her chimney was cold.

Corvane did not draw conclusions from a cold chimney. Drawing conclusions was what lieutenants did; sergeants collected facts and arranged them until the conclusions drew themselves. But he noted it, the way he noted everything: the crooked fence post at the mill, the boy splitting wood who stopped mid-swing when he saw the grey cloaks, the innkeeper whose smile arrived half a beat too late and stayed two beats too long.

"Something wrong, Sergeant?" asked Warden Tullis, riding at his left. Young, eager, with a new sword he polished more often than he used it.

"The opposite," Corvane said. "Everything is exactly as wrong as I expected it to be."

The rumor had come down from a shepherd three days ago — a man who traded wool for salt and salt for silence, and who had broken that silence because what he'd heard frightened him more than the Wardens did. *Singing*, he'd said. Singing in the high pasture near the old cairns, in a voice like a child's but wrong somehow, singing words no shepherd should know. Words the shepherd's grandfather had been beaten for repeating. Old-tongue singing, in the year 607 of the Truce, in a valley so quiet the Crown used it as a place to forget things.

Corvane had read the report twice, burned it, and ridden out at dawn.

They watered their horses at the trough by the mill and Corvane dismounted into snow up to his shin. He was a broad man gone lean with middle years, grey at the temples before his time, with a face that people described as patient and feared as patient — because they understood that his patience was not kindness. It was a trap with good manners. His mother, a fishwife in Greyport who had buried two husbands and one dream of better things, had taught him that the surest way to make a man confess was to give him room enough to hang himself. He had built a career on her wisdom.

"Tullis, take Hesk and walk the east pasture. The shepherd's cairns. Look for anything carved — bone, horn, stone, doesn't matter. Anything with marks on it that aren't Crown marks." He paused. "Do not touch it. Mark its place and come back."

"And if someone's there?"

"Then you'll have met a shepherd. Shepherds are all anyone ever is out here." He said it without bitterness. It was simply true, and truth was the only tool he permitted himself to love.

Tullis went. Corvane straightened his cloak, settled the weight of the warrant-tube against his hip — empty today, mercifully, though tubes could always be filled — and walked toward the inn called the Drowned Ox, because in Thornfell even the taverns sounded like places where things went to die.

* * *

The innkeeper's name was Marta Brann, and she was the best liar in the valley, which Corvane knew because he'd watched her practice smaller versions of it for six years. Her husband had run contraband through the pass until his lungs gave out; her son ran it now, or claimed to have given it up, which in the Carrow trade meant he'd found better routes. She poured him tea he hadn't asked for, in a cup that was clean, which meant she'd been expecting him.

"Sergeant Corvane." Her smile was warm as a hearth and about as informative. "Cold morning for riding."

"Cold mornings are the honest ones," Corvane said, accepting the tea. He drank it slowly. Good tea — too good for Thorn's Hollow, which meant smuggled, which meant he could burn her stores whenever the mood took him, which meant they both knew it, which meant the tea was itself a message: *we know what we are to each other.* He appreciated efficiency in all its forms. "I'm looking for a singer."

"Choirs are thin on the ground here."

"A particular kind. Old songs. Songs older than the tax rolls." He set the cup down and turned it a quarter-turn, watching her eyes track the motion. "Someone heard one. Up past the high pasture, night of the blizzard. You get many travelers through a blizzard?"

"In a blizzard? Sergeant, nothing walks in that but fools and wolves, and the wolves stay home."

"Mm." He smiled at her, gently, the way one might smile at a child presenting a drawing of a horse that was clearly a dog. "Your son. Where is he?"

"Renn? Off earning an honest living, if he can find one."

"He owes me a visit. Tell him the debt's come due — not the money debt. The other kind." He stood, leaving coin on the counter for the tea, exact change, because he never accepted gifts he couldn't return and never left debts he couldn't collect. "You've been helpful, Mistress Brann. As always."

"I haven't told you anything."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You've been very careful about that. It's almost information in itself."

Outside, Tullis was waiting by the horses, and something in the young Warden's face made Corvane's stomach settle into the flat, cold readiness it always found before the finding. Tullis held out a cloth-wrapped bundle without being asked. Inside: a sliver of antler, no longer than a finger, scored with lines that were not decoration. They spiraled. They repeated. They meant something, or had meant something once, to people who had been dead longer than the Crown had existed.

"Where."

"Half-buried by the second cairn. Fresh-turned earth around it. And—" Tullis hesitated, and Corvane waited, because waiting was free and hesitation always filled the silence eventually. "There were small footprints, Sergeant. Going up the slope toward the ridge. Toward the carver's workshop. Small ones. A child's, maybe. Half snowed over already."

Corvane looked at the antler a long moment. Then he wrapped it back in the cloth with a care that Tullis would later describe to Hesk as almost tender, and which Corvane would have described as evidence handling.

"Well," he said. "Let's go pay our respects."

* * *

The workshop sat above the tree line like a knuckle of bone itself, grey stone, turf roof, one window glowing faintly amber against the afternoon. Smoke now, Corvane noted. The chimney had remembered how to work. He climbed the path alone, having ordered the others to wait below — not out of courtesy, though courtesy was the instrument, but because four armed Wardens at a door got him fear, and fear got him noise, and noise got him nothing true. One Warden at a door got him conversation. Conversation got him everything.

She opened before he knocked. That interested him. Either she'd watched them come up the whole way — likely — or she'd felt them coming, which was a thing the superstitious said about bone-carvers and which Corvane filed away unexamined, because examining it would require deciding what he believed about the dead, and he had spent thirty years declining to do that.

"Warden-Sergeant." Her voice was level, dry, the voice of a woman who had decided exactly how much of herself each visitor deserved and had allotted him very little. "You'll want to come in out of the wind. Your men can wait in the shed if they're civilized."

"They're civilized," Corvane said, stepping inside. "Mostly."

The workshop smelled of tallow and dust and something else beneath — something mineral and old, like the air in caves. Bones hung in ranked rows from the rafters, sorted by size, each tagged with lead seals showing license numbers. Shavings curled on the floor like pale wood-shavings, except paler, softer-looking. A workbench by the window held a half-finished piece — a jawbone, he thought, mid-carving, its surface still rough. Tools lay beside it in neat order.

Corvane looked at none of this directly. He looked at the fire, the kettle, the single chair, the bed behind its curtain. He counted what wasn't there. No second cup. No second bedroll. No boots but hers by the door, women's boots, worn at the outside edge the way a woman's wear who climbs the same mountain path daily. No small footprints melted on her hearthstones.

But the house was warm. Warmer than one fire and one woman required, though he conceded that a woman who worked with her hands all day kept a hot hearth out of habit.

"Tea?" she offered.

"Please." He sat in the single chair, making himself her guest in her own home, another small violence dressed as manners. She poured without comment, and he drank, and the tea was poor — mountain scrub and pine needle — which pleased him obscurely. Honest tea. "You had a visitor recently."

"Did I."

"A child, perhaps. Small feet on your ridge, day after the blizzard."

"I carve bones for a living, Sergeant. Children come to look. They always have. They think the skulls are spooky." She said it flatly, without the little laugh most people added to such deflections, and Corvane noted the absence. Most liars decorate. The good ones strip it bare and dare you to find the seam.

"The shepherd heard singing," Corvane said. "Old-tongue singing. Near the cairns."

"Then the shepherd's been drinking his own still-water. Or grieving. Half the valley comes up here wanting their dead to say something, and when I tell them the dead only say what they said in life, they invent the rest." She refilled his cup. "People hear what they need, Warden. You of all people know that."

"Do I?"

"You burn caches and call it keeping the peace. The valley hears justice. What they need." Her eyes met his, brown and unhurried. "We're both in the comfort business."

It was a good line. It was a very good line, delivered without heat, and Corvane felt the particular pleasure he always felt upon meeting a craftsman, whatever the craft. He set down the cup.

"Show me your register."

She fetched it without being asked — the ledger every licensed carver kept, names of the dead rendered, dates, fees, the whole gray accounting of grief. He turned the pages slowly. Twelve entries since midwinter. A miller's father. Two children lost to fever, entered together, fee waived — noted in her own hand, *no charge, neighbor*. An old woman named Sela Brightwater whose last words, according to the entry, had concerned the whereabouts of a hidden will.

Nothing recent. Nothing since the blizzard. If a child had come to her door, it had never been written down.

"You keep a clean ledger," he said.

"It's the only clean thing in my life."

"May I see the workbench?"

"You may see anything you like, Sergeant. You will anyway."

He rose and crossed to it, and there he saw what he had known he would see and had hoped not to: the jawbone, half-carved, and beneath the bench, a scrap basket. In the basket, among ordinary shavings, lay a curl of bone dust and one small sliver of rib-bone, scored with a single spiral mark. Fresh. Days old at most. The same spiral as the antler from the cairn.

She had carved it. Recently. Which meant either she had handled material marked in the old tongue — forbidden material, sealed-material — or she had marked it herself, which was worse, or someone in this house had.

Corvane picked up the sliver between finger and thumb and turned to face her. He did not accuse. Accusation invited defense, and defense invited lies, and he had heard enough lies for one lifetime. Instead he held it up where she could see it and simply waited, giving her room, giving her rope, watching her face with the patience his mother had taught him across thirty years and one grave too many.

Elodie Voss looked at the sliver of bone for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was as dry as ever, and her eyes did not move from his.

"Scrap," she said. "Failed stock. Cracked down the middle — see? Can't carve a commission on cracked bone. It goes in the basket with the rest." She shrugged, one shoulder. "If you're asking whether I put the spiral there myself — sometimes I doodle while thinking. Old patterns. They come up from memory. My grandmother's grandmother came from over the pass, Sergeant. There's old blood in these valleys and it leaves marks on everything, including my idle hands."

Every word was a lie. Corvane had interrogated smugglers, deserters, murderers, and one very memorable forger of royal seals, and he knew the architecture of lying the way a carpenter knows joinery. This was load-bearing falsehood, well-planed, sanded smooth, finished with a shrug. She had rehearsed it, or she was simply that good, and he genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing itched at him like a splinter under a nail.

"That blood," Corvane said mildly, "is why the Truce exists. Six hundred years ago, the Spire-folk were sealed beneath their tower, and the Crown's first law was that the seal holds. No old tongue spoken. No old marks carved. No old songs sung. Not because the language is dangerous, Mistress Voss — languages rarely are — but because the *singing* is. Because the seal listens." He let that sit in the warm air between them. "So when I find fresh spiral-work in a licensed carver's scrap basket, days after someone sings the old songs in the high pasture, I don't think about grandmothers. I think about my oath."

"Which is?"

"To hold the peace." He returned the sliver to the basket, gently, precisely, placing it exactly where it had lain. "Whatever the peace costs."

"And if the peace costs a child?"

The question landed harder than she perhaps intended — he saw the flicker behind her eyes, quick as a fish turning — and Corvane understood suddenly, completely, the way one understands a lock's mechanism the moment the key turns: there *was* a child. There had been a child, and the child had been here, and the woman in front of him had decided somewhere in the last three days that she would rather lie to a Warden-Sergeant than surrender whatever small, frozen creature had sung to the cairns.

He felt no triumph. That was the part Tullis would never understand, the part that made young Wardens either break or harden in their first year: that catching a liar brought him no joy at all, only the tired satisfaction of a wall confirming it was a wall. Somewhere behind those brown eyes a frightened child was warming itself by a fire he would soon, by oath and office, have to bring the full weight of the law down upon. And the law did not care that the child was small. The law cared that the seal held. The seal held because six hundred years ago two armies had butchered each other for a generation, and the men who ended it had chosen silence over slaughter, and silence, once purchased at that price, does not tolerate exceptions.

"Does it?" he asked quietly. "Cost a child?"

"Not that I know of," said Elodie Voss, and lied to his face one more time, beautifully.

Corvane nodded slowly. He finished his terrible tea to the dregs — politeness completed is pressure applied, another of his mother's lessons — and set the cup down.

"I'll want to inspect the shed," he said. "And then we'll trouble you no further today."

The shed held tallow, tools, cured hides, and nothing else. He thanked her. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around.

"For what it's worth, Mistress Voss — and it isn't worth much — I hope I'm wrong about you. I've been wrong before." Once, he thought. Once, six years ago, a girl in Greyford with a smuggled letter and a sob story he'd believed, and the letter had contained coordinates that led to a cache, and the cache had contained old-tongue bone-works, and three people had gone to the fire for it. He had checked her story afterward, piece by piece, hunting his error, and never found it. He had stopped sleeping well that winter. "But if a child passes through this valley needing help, the law says it comes to me. Not to a bone-carver. To me."

"And what does the law do with it?"

"What the law does," Corvane said, "is what keeps six hundred years of graves closed. You may not thank it. Neither do I. But we both sleep under it."

He went out into the blue evening. Behind him, the door closed — not slammed, not quickly, but with the deliberate softness of a woman sealing a tomb.

* * *

They camped that night in the mill-yard, and Corvane burned the smuggler's cache at moonrise.

It was a small thing, as these went: a false-bottomed grain wagon in Marta Brann's own barn, forty pounds of salt-tax-evading salt, a keg of brandy, and — tucked inside a hollowed Bible, because smugglers loved their ironies — a packet of letters in cipher that would hang her son if he was fool enough to be holding them when the wagon was found. Corvane burned the contraband in the yard and let the letters burn too, unread, and let Marta Brann watch from her doorway with her arms folded and her face like flint.

"Why?" Tullis asked, watching the flames. "The letters were evidence. Real evidence. We could—"

"We could hang Renn Carrow," Corvane agreed. "And then every smuggler from here to Greyford goes to ground for a season, and the real cargo moves at night through paths we don't know, and I spend spring chasing ghosts instead of listening for songs." He stirred the embers with his boot. "Burn the small sins, Tullis. Keep your ears open for the large ones. A salt-wagon tells me nothing. A cold chimney, a fresh spiral, and a liar too good for her own good — that tells me everything."

"And the child, Sergeant? If there is a child?"

Corvane watched the fire eat the last of the brandy-keg. For a moment — a moment he would deny under oath — he saw not a warrant, not a threat, but a small shape in a blizzard, walking toward the only lit window on a mountainside, because children always walked toward light, and the world punished them for it.

"Then the law knows what it must do," he said. "And I know what I must do. And some nights, Tullis, those are the same thing, and some nights they aren't, and a Warden's whole life is learning which nights are which."

He posted the watch, wrote his report by lantern-light — *inspection conducted, no violations confirmed, valley quiet* — and paused, quill hovering, over the space where a lesser officer would have written *further surveillance recommended.*

He wrote it anyway. Duty was duty. But he wrote it small, in the corner of the page, where a reader might miss it, and where a careful one would not.

In the morning they would ride the eastern ridges. In the morning, the hunt would truly begin.

And three miles uphill, behind a door closed softly as a tomb-lid, a lamp burned late, and packing had already begun.
