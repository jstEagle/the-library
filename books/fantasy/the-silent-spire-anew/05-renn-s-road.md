## 5. Renn's Road

Renn Carrow had a theory about debts: they were like wolves. Ignore them long enough and they stopped being a problem and started being a pack.

He'd been ignoring this particular debt for three years, and it had grown teeth in the dark the whole time.

The mule complained beneath him as the trail dropped out of the high pine and into the valley — *his* valley, though he'd never owned so much as a fencepost in it. Thornfell's southern reaches didn't belong to anybody, which was why men like Renn could make a living there, and why men like Renn eventually died there with their ledgers unbalanced and their names misspelled on cheap grave markers. He intended to avoid that fate through the time-honored methods of charm, speed, and never sleeping in the same hollow twice.

The valley opened below him the way it always did: sudden, green, and deceptively gentle. Terraced fields stitched the lower slopes. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, well-behaved columns. And at the valley's throat, where the river bent and the old trade road crossed it, sat the workshop of Elodie Voss, bone-carver — a low stone building with a steep roof and one window that glowed amber at dusk, like an eye that had seen everything and decided to keep most of it to itself.

Renn had done business with her for six years. She carved grief out of dead people's bones, which sounded morbid until you saw what it bought the grieving: a last word, a final kindness, something to hold at the graveside besides dirt. Half the smugglers on the mountain carried tokens she'd made — knucklebones that whispered warnings, vertebrae that hummed when Wardens came near. She charged fair prices, asked no questions, and took no sides, which in Thornfell was the closest thing to sainthood anyone had managed since the Spire went silent.

She also owed him. Not coin. Something heavier.

Three years ago, in the dead of winter, a Warden patrol had caught his brother Tam on the ice road with a sledge full of unmarked cargo. Tam hadn't been carrying salt or spirits. He'd been carrying bones — a whole crate of them, old ones, Spire-old, that Renn still didn't know the provenance of. The Wardens burned the cargo and would have burned Tam if a certain bone-carver hadn't walked into their camp, calm as Sunday, and sworn under her gift-oath that the bones were hers, legally purchased, papers lost in a flood, terribly sorry. They'd believed her. Wardens always believed people who looked them in the eye without flinching, because flinching was how they sorted the guilty from the useful.

Tam had died two winters later anyway — fever, nothing dramatic — but Renn kept accounts the way other men kept saints. Elodie Voss was owed. Or he was. The arithmetic got muddy when he thought about it too hard, so mostly he didn't.

He'd come today to collect on a different debt entirely: a shipment of carved wards sitting in her workshop, promised to a buyer in Lowmark who paid in silver and didn't ask about the whispers. Simple job. In and out before dark.

That was the plan, and Renn's plans were famously reliable right up until they weren't.

* * *

The workshop door stood open when he rode up, which was wrong. Elodie Voss guarded that door like a dragon guards a hoard — not from greed, but because everything behind it was fragile and hers.

He left the mule tied at the rail and stepped inside without knocking, which earned him a look that could have cured ham.

"You're supposed to knock," Elodie said. She was at the workbench, wrapping something in oilcloth with quick, precise hands. Her sleeves were rolled up. There was ash on her forearms.

"Knocking's for people with something to hide," Renn said. "I'm an open book."

"Your book has pages torn out." She didn't look up. "You're early. The wards aren't ready."

"I can see that." He drifted toward the bench, casual as a man admiring weather, and glanced at what she was wrapping. Not the wards. Her tools. Her good chisels, the ones made of Warden-forbidden star-iron, wrapped like relics. "Interesting. Packing your knives. Going somewhere?"

"No."

"Because it looks like—"

"It's *maintenance*, Renn."

"Maintenance." He picked up a finished carving from the shelf — a small songbird in yew wood, of all things, not bone — and turned it over. It hummed against his palm, faintly, like a bee trapped in a jar. "Your maintenance involves oilcloth and travel knots? I taught you those knots."

"Someone taught me. It might have been you. Memory is unreliable after thirty."

He set the bird down carefully. It kept humming. That was new — her carvings whispered, yes, murmured, breathed. Humming was different. Humming was almost like singing.

"Elodie." He let the charm drain out of his voice, which took effort; the charm was load-bearing. "What's happened?"

For a moment she said nothing, and he watched her do the thing she did — weigh him, measure him, decide how many truths he could carry without dropping any. Most people found her cold. Renn knew better. She was a woman who lived alone with the voices of the dead, and she rationed herself the way a besieged town rations grain.

Then, from the back room, someone sneezed.

Small. High-pitched. Definitely not Elodie.

Renn's hand went to the knife at his belt out of pure habit, and Elodie moved between him and the doorway with a speed that reminded him, unpleasantly, that she handled blades for a living.

"There's a child," she said.

"A child." He looked past her shoulder. "In your back room. Where you keep the femurs."

"She's not in with the femurs. She's asleep."

"*She.*" He relaxed slightly and immediately regretted it; relaxing around Elodie was like relaxing around a trapline. "Whose?"

"If I knew whose, I'd know more than I do." She stepped aside, arms folded, and told him. All of it, or enough of it: the blizzard three nights ago, the small figure half-frozen at her door, barefoot in snow that should have killed her. No name she could pronounce. No tongue anyone knew — except that when the child sang, the finished carvings answered. The songbird hummed in his hand. Everything else on the shelves was doing a quieter version of the same thing now that he listened. A whole workshop full of dead people, holding their breath.

"And the Wardens?" he asked, because that was the only question that mattered in Thornfell. "They've heard?"

"They've heard *something*. Old-tongue singing, they're calling it. There was a sergeant through yesterday. Corvane. Polite as a headsman." Her jaw tightened. "I lied to him. It worked. It won't work twice."

"So you're running." He gestured at the wrapped tools, the emptied shelves, the travel knots. "Alone. With a mute child who makes dead things sing, across mountains currently crawling with Wardens looking for exactly that."

"When you say it like that it sounds foolish."

"It sounds *suicidal*. Which is a specialty of yours, apparently." He dragged a hand down his face. "Where are you even going? Don't say it. Let me guess. You're going to the one place in the world where taking that child is a hanging offense and the road there is a graveyard with footpaths."

Elodie said nothing, which from her was a signed confession.

"The Silent Spire," Renn said. "You're going to the Silent Spire."

* * *

The child woke while they argued, which Renn suspected was deliberate, though he'd never prove it.

She came into the main room trailing Elodie's spare blanket like a ghost's train, and stopped when she saw him. Small thing — seven, eight years maybe, all elbows and watchfulness, with hair the color of wet slate and eyes that were doing something eyes shouldn't do. Looking *through* him. At something behind him. Or inside him.

"Evening," Renn said, crouching to her level out of long habit with other people's children, none of whom ever looked at him like that. "Renn Carrow. Professional scoundrel. You may have heard of me — actually, don't answer that."

The child tilted her head. Then she hummed — three notes, low and strange, like wind through a chimney — and every carving in the workshop shivered at once.

A knucklebone rattled in its dish. The songbird sang one clear note. And from the wrapped bundle of tools, muffled by oilcloth, something that might have been a chisel gave off a sound like a sigh.

Renn stood up very slowly. "Right," he said. "So that's a thing that happens."

"She doesn't mean anything by it," Elodie said. "I think it's how she talks."

"That's worse. That's so much worse, Elodie." He paced to the window, checked the road out of habit — empty, dusk gathering blue between the trees — and paced back. "Do you understand what they'll do? Not arrest. *Do*. The truce is older than the Crown, and the Crown keeps it the way you keep a fire banked — with ash and denial. A child walking around speaking the Spire-tongue isn't a curiosity to them. It's a lit match in a granary. Corvane finds her, she dies, and you die being brave next to her. I've watched brave people die next to other people my whole life. It's the most common way to go in these mountains."

"I know the arithmetic," Elodie said quietly. "I did it before I opened the door."

"Then why—" He stopped. Looked at the child again. She was watching them argue with the patient attention of someone who understood every word and was choosing, graciously, not to mention it. "Why does it matter? One kid. Out of all the impossible things in Thornfell. Why is she your problem?"

And Elodie Voss — who had spent six years being the most neutral party on the mountain, who sold comfort to smugglers and solace to soldiers' widows and never once tipped her hand — looked at the child, then at him, and said:

"Because nobody else will be. Because she knocked on *my* door. That's the whole reason. That's all of it."

It wasn't all of it, probably. But Renn recognized the architecture of the answer, because he'd built the same kind himself, over the same buried rooms.

He thought about Tam. He thought about the crate of old bones his brother had carried through the winter ice — bones no smuggler had commissioned, no buyer had ordered, that Tam had taken on faith from a contact in Lowmark who'd paid him strangely and spoken strangely and vanished after. Bones that Tam, feverish near the end, had tried to describe to him once: *warm, Renn. They were warm, and they were humming, and I swear on our mother they were saying thank you.*

He'd filed it away as fever-talk for three years. He was having trouble filing it anywhere now, with a slate-haired child humming in a room full of answering dead.

"What was your brother carrying?" Elodie asked suddenly, sharp as one of her own chisels. She was watching his face the way she read bones — for what lay underneath.

"Salt," Renn said.

"Renn."

"Mostly salt." He exhaled. Somewhere outside, an owl called, and every carving went quiet to listen, which was going to give him nightmares for a decade. "Bones. Old ones. From the Spire country. A stranger paid Tam to move them north, out of the valley, and never said why, and Tam never got the chance to ask. Then Tam died, and I decided it was fever-talk, because the alternative was deciding my brother spent his last winter as a courier for ghosts, and I liked fever-talk better."

The child had gone very still. When Renn glanced over, she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read — and didn't want to, not yet.

"Say I help you," he said to Elodie, roughly. "Say I take you both through Thornfell, up the smuggler roads, past the tolls and the patrols and the places where even I have to pay in things worse than coin. What's the plan at the end of it? Walk up to a sealed tower full of entombed dead and knock?"

"Yes," Elodie said.

"You're insane."

"Probably. Are you coming?"

He should have said no. Every survival instinct he'd honed over twenty years of illegal commerce said *no*, said *walk away, sell the story to the Wardens instead, buy yourself a pardon and a farm*. It was, objectively, the smart play. He'd have done it, too — he'd have done it for anyone else on this mountain.

Instead he heard himself say: "I'll want my wards eventually. And expenses. And if we're doing this, we leave tonight, because Corvane struck me as a man who checks his arithmetic twice."

Elodie blinked. For a heartbeat something moved behind her eyes — relief, maybe, or its cousin — and was gone before he could tax it.

"Tonight," she agreed.

* * *

They loaded the mule by lantern light. Renn carried the crates out while Elodie banked her hearth and covered the workbench, moving through her workshop the way a woman moves through a house where someone has just died — touching things, apologizing to them silently.

The child helped, in her fashion. She carried nothing, but she walked beside Renn on his trips to the mule, and each time, she touched one finger to the nearest carved stone on the gateposts — little ward-stones Elodie had set years ago — and each stone gave a single soft note as she passed, like a bell rung by someone very polite.

"They like her," Renn said, when Elodie came out with the last bundle.

"They like everyone. They just usually can't say so." She paused at the gate, looking back once at the dark windows of the only home she'd known in twenty years. Renn had watched men say goodbye to houses, wives, sons on the gallows steps. This was quieter than any of that, and costlier. "Which way?" she asked.

"North road until the fork, then east into the high passes before first light. We'll be on Warden ground by tomorrow midday if we dawdle, so we won't dawdle." He swung up onto the mule, then held out a hand to the child. She considered it gravely, the way she seemed to consider everything, and climbed up behind him without touching it.

They went down through the sleeping valley, hooves muffled in new grass, three shadows and a mule. Behind them the workshop window was dark for the first time in years, and ahead of them the mountains rose black against a sky just beginning to bruise with stars.

Somewhere on that road, in a pass Renn knew better than he knew his own sins, the Wardens were already asking questions. He could feel it the way he felt weather in his knee — pressure building, patience ending.

The child leaned forward against his back, small and warm, and hummed four notes this time. Low. Almost like a lullaby. Almost like a warning.

Behind them, far down the valley, a dog began to bark.

Renn didn't look back. He'd learned long ago that looking back only tells you what's already true.

"Hold tight, little ghost," he murmured. "We're going to see the family."
