## 6. Leaving the Valley

The decision came to her the way decisions always did in the mountains: slowly, like frost creeping across a windowpane, and then all at once, like the glass cracking.

Elodie stood at her workbench with a half-finished carving in her hands — old Marta Halloway's collarbone, commissioned by a daughter who wanted one last word from her mother before the grief set into something livable — and she understood that she would never deliver it. The bone was cool against her palm. It said nothing. Bones only spoke when she gave them voice through the knife, and her knife-hand had been shaking for two days.

Ash sat on the floor by the cold hearth, arranging splinters of offcut pine into patterns that meant nothing to Elodie and everything to whatever memory lived behind the child's dark eyes. Every few minutes the child hummed — that same melody, low and circular, the one that made the finished carvings on her shelves whisper in their sleep. She had taken to draping cloth over them at night, the way a farmer covers his mirrors during a death-watch. It helped. Not enough.

The Wardens would come back. Corvane's visit had been courtesy; the next one would be arithmetic. He would count her carvings and listen for what they said, and even if Ash was hidden in the root cellar breathing through a straw, the child's presence had already soaked into the wood of the house like smoke into wool. Elodie could feel it herself when she held her own work now — a second language threaded beneath the dead's last words, old as bedrock, patient as water.

They could not stay. And if they could not stay, then nothing here could be allowed to remain.

She set Marta Halloway's collarbone down gently, the way you lay down anything that trusted you, and began to pull the cloths from the shelves.

* * *

It took her until noon to decide what deserved saving, which was its own kind of grief. A lifetime's work fit into one satchel: three carvings she could not bring herself to burn — her mother's knucklebones, strung on wire; the first piece she'd ever sold, a robin's skull no bigger than a thumbnail; and a small blank stone she had carved years ago on a bad night, when the voices had been too loud, working it smooth and featureless just to have something silent in her hands. She had never been able to part with it. Silence, it turned out, was also a keepsake.

Everything else went into the hearth.

Not everything at once. That was the discipline of it. A bone-carver's workshop burned hot and strange — tallow, linseed oil, the waxy reek of marrow dust, the resinous bite of pine shavings — and a fire that leapt too eagerly would be seen from the valley road long before she wanted it seen. So she fed it piece by piece, kneeling at the hearth like a woman saying rosaries, and each piece she named as it went in.

"Old Tam Wicker's finger-bone." The man who'd died drunk in a snowdrift and whose last word, when she finally cut it free, had been *more*, and she had never told his widow whether he'd meant ale or life. The bone cracked softly in the flames, and for one vertiginous moment she thought she heard him say it again — *more* — and then it was only wood-smoke.

"Marta Halloway's collarbone." Unfinished. Unpaid. She whispered an apology to the daughter she would never meet, and to the mother, who had waited eleven years in that bone to speak and would wait forever now. Some debts could not be carried across a border. This one she left burning on the mountain.

Her tools went in last, and that was the worst of it. Knives are honest things; they hold the shape of your hand after twenty years the way a riverbed holds the shape of water. She had ground and honed and hafted every one of them herself. She stood a long time with the roll of leather open on her knees, the blades catching the grey light, before she understood that keeping even one was keeping the evidence — a bone-carver without bones is a woman with a hobby, but a bone-carver's kit found in a smuggler's pack is a confession. Renn Carrow would carry that pack past checkpoints she couldn't imagine. She dropped the whole roll into the coals and watched the leather curl and blacken, and she did not look away, because looking away was a luxury of people with futures.

Ash watched from the doorway, silent as ever, but humming. Low. That melody again. And this time — she would swear this later, to herself, in the dark — the whispering from the hearth was not grief. It was something almost like singing along.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the chimney was drawing clean, or clean enough — green wood over the coals made a white haze that read as a careless hearth, not a pyre. She had thought about the story on the walk to the well and back twice: a grease fire, a moment's carelessness, most of the stock lost. People would believe it because people believed what flattered them, and the valley had spent thirty years believing that Elodie Voss was competent but cold, precise but passionless, a woman who kept her ledger balanced and her heart locked. Let them add *careless* to the list. Carelessness was a mercy. It explained things without requiring anyone to look closer.

The neighbors would come tomorrow, or the day after, with soup and narrow eyes and offers to help rebuild that were really offers to inventory what remained. Mrs. Aldery from the mill would stand in the ruined doorway and click her tongue and say something about how it was bound to happen eventually, living alone up here, playing with the dead. They would find the ash of the bones and think it ash of wood, because no one in this valley had ever once looked closely at what Elodie Voss did. That had been her armor. Today it was her alibi.

She caught herself planning the rebuilding — beam there, bench there, the good north light for detail work — and stopped, appalled at herself. Thirty years of habit building a house she was never coming home to. That was the thing about severance that no one warned you: your mind kept living in the amputated limb.

Ash tugged her sleeve. The child pointed at the satchel, then at the door, then made a gesture Elodie had learned to read as *when* — fingers opening like a bird taking flight.

"Soon," she said. Then, because the child deserved more than one-word rationing, she crouched to eye level. "When the light goes off the far ridge. We move in the blue hour, when the Wardens change their rounds and the road is empty. Can you be quiet until then?"

Ash nodded. Then hesitated, and touched Elodie's cheek with one cold finger, and pointed at the hearth.

"I know," Elodie said quietly. "I know."

And the terrible thing, the thing she would carry longer than the loss itself, was that some small traitor corner of her felt lighter. Lighter. As if the workshop had been a weight she'd mistaken for a self. She hated that corner of herself, and she packed it up along with everything else.

* * *

Renn Carrow arrived at dusk, as promised, leading a single mule and wearing the expression of a man who has already rehearsed all his complaints. He looked at the thinning smoke, then at her face, then at the satchel, and whatever joke he'd loaded died unspoken.

"You burned it," he said.

"I tidied."

He winced — actually winced, quick and involuntary, the way men do when a wound gets named. "That was your whole—"

"Yes."

He didn't say *I'm sorry*. She was grateful. Pity from Renn Carrow would have been unbearable; respect, apparently, he could manage. He busied himself checking the mule's girth strap with tremendous concentration, and when he spoke again his voice had shed its road-worn charm entirely. "Blue hour, then. There's a drover's path behind the tor that joins the old quarry road. No tollhouse, no rounds. By dawn we're below the snowline and by tomorrow night we're in Thornfell proper, where even Corvane's writ gets thin."

"And after Thornfell?"

"After Thornfell," he said, tightening the strap a beat too long, "we'll see what the mountain says."

She let that lie. Everyone on this road was carrying something they weren't ready to show, and she'd learned long ago — carving other people's secrets out of dead bone — that you don't pry. You wait. Secrets surface the way bodies do in spring.

* * *

The blue hour came the way it always did in the high valley: fast and total, the shadows pooling upward from the hollows until the world ran out of color. Elodie stood in her doorway one last time and made herself look.

The workshop was smaller than she remembered it being. That was the trick of leaving, she supposed — the place shrank as you stepped outside it, all those years contracting to four walls and a smoking chimney. She had built it herself at nineteen, hauling stone with hands that bled, because no landlord in the valley would rent to a bone-witch and no neighbor would sell her timber without tripling the price. She had slept on the floor the first winter. She had carved her first commission by rushlight, old widow Grieve's husband's jawbone, and the coin from it had bought her first proper knife, which she had put into the fire that very afternoon.

Thirty years. Two hundred and some souls given their last words. And not one of them, she realized now, standing in the cold with her one satchel, had ever asked her a question in return. Not one of the living, anyway. The dead had asked plenty. But the living of Thornfell valley had come to her the way people come to a well — needing, taking, never wondering what it cost the water.

She had told herself for decades that she preferred it that way. Standing here, she tested the sentence again, the way you test a bone before trusting it.

It rang false.

"Ash." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Time."

The child appeared at her side out of nowhere, the way Ash always did, soundless as snowfall, and slipped a small cold hand into hers. When had that started? When had the touching started? Somewhere between the blizzard and the burning, the child had decided that Elodie Voss was safe to hold onto, and Elodie had discovered that being held onto was a sensation she had no defenses against whatsoever.

They went down the hill behind the workshop, avoiding the road, Renn ahead with the mule and Ash between them, and Elodie did not look back. She had heard too many last words not to know the difference between remembering a place and haunting it. She intended to remember.

But at the treeline, where the path bent north and the valley dropped away behind them, the wind shifted and brought her the smell of her own hearth still smoldering — tallow and pine and burnt marrow — and underneath it, faint as a fingerprint, something else. The carvings. What was left of them. Speaking one last time into the smoke.

She couldn't make out the words. That was the mercy of it. Or perhaps — and this thought followed her all the way down into the dark, all the way to the drover's path, all the way into the first hard miles of a road with no end yet visible — perhaps the words were not for her. Perhaps they were for whoever came next. Perhaps the mountain itself was learning to talk, and she had merely been its first reluctant throat.

Behind them, high on the ridge, a lantern swung into view on the valley road. Too distant to be sure. Too steady to be innocent.

"That'll be the night round," Renn murmured, not turning. "Or it won't. Either way — faster now, friends. The mountain doesn't wait, and neither does the law."

Elodie tightened her grip on Ash's hand and walked north, carrying nothing of her old life but three keepsakes, one child's trust, and the growing, terrifying certainty that whatever waited for them beneath the Silent Spire had been waiting a very long time — and knew her name.
