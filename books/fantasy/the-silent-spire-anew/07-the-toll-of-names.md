## 7. The Toll of Names

Sleep did not come to Ash the way it came to the bone-woman.

Ash had watched her sleep on the first night, curled in the chair by the cold hearth with her carving knife still in her fist, her breath going slow and even as a tide. She slept like someone who trusted the dark to stay where it belonged. Ash had not slept that way in as long as Ash could remember, which was not long, which was the whole trouble. Memory came to Ash the way water came through stone: not all at once, not in order, but in seeps and drips and sudden floods that left everything rearranged.

That night, in the wagon that smelled of tar and turnips, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of the smuggler-man's pipe, sleep took Ash anyway, because bodies are tyrants. And in sleep, the water rose.

* * *

First there was the tower.

Not seen — *felt*, the way you feel a tooth before it aches. A tower so tall that its top was weather and its base was law, and Ash had lived somewhere along its length without ever knowing which. The walls of it were not built the way the bone-woman's workshop was built, stone laid on stone by tired hands. The tower's walls were *grown*, ribbed like the inside of a shell, and when wind moved through the high galleries they made a sound that was not wind at all but the tower breathing, and everyone who lived inside it learned to breathe when it breathed.

There had been others. This came to Ash not as an image but as a warmth at the back, the particular warmth of many bodies in one great dim hall, shoulder to shoulder, all of them humming the same low note so the smallest among them could find their way home in the dark. Ash had been small once. Ash had been carried. There had been hands — long hands, gray-skinned, with nails like polished horn — and a voice above saying words that meant, though Ash could not have translated them then and could barely hold them now:

*This one listens. This one keeps.*

Kept what? The question turned over in the dream like a stone in a streambed, showing its pale underside. Kept the names. That was the answer that surfaced, and with it a smell: cedar smoke and crushed white flowers, the smell of the Naming Room, where the walls were carved floor to ceiling with every name the people had ever borne, and where Ash had gone, small and solemn, to press both palms against the stone and learn three names by heart. Not names of the living. Names of the *kept*. And the stone under Ash's palms had been warm, warmer than stone should be, and Ash had known even then — the way children know things and are never believed — that the wall was not a wall.

The wall was full of people.

* * *

Then fire, because there was always fire after.

It came into the dream sideways, as fire does, first as light on faces looking up, then as heat on the skin, then as sound — a roaring that was somehow also a singing, because Ash's people sang when they burned, that was the custom, that was the *law*, you met the end of your name with your throat open so the name would not die shut up inside you. Ash remembered standing at a window very high up, watching the lower terraces glow orange, watching figures move through the smoke below with their arms raised, and the arms were not raised in surrender. They were raised in the gesture that meant *we go ahead of you; keep the road.* And small Ash had pressed both hands over small Ash's mouth to keep from answering, because answering was forbidden now, because silence was the whole of the mission, because —

Because Ash had been chosen. Chosen to carry the language out. Chosen the way you choose a single seed from a burned field: not because it is the best, but because it is small enough to hide, and because if you do not send something out, everything ends with you.

There had been a last lesson. It arrived in the dream as a voice speaking directly against Ash's ear, close enough to feel breath, though the breath smelled of smoke and the voice shook:

*They will seal us, little keeper. That is the bargain, and we will keep our side of it, because the other road is ash. But hear me. Sealed is not ended. Buried is not finished. You will go out into the loud world where no one speaks true anymore, and you will be silent, silent, silent, until the day comes when the mountain forgets how to hold its breath. And on that day you will open your mouth, and whatever comes out of it will decide whether six hundred years of patience was wisdom or only a slower kind of dying.*

*Do you understand?*

And Ash, in the dream, in the memory, had understood perfectly, and had hated understanding, and had said yes.

* * *

Ash woke in the dark of the wagon with the taste of smoke in the mouth and the blanket soaked through with sweat, and for a moment did not know where the body was. The wagon swayed. Wheels ground over stones. Somewhere ahead, the bone-woman's voice murmured low to the smuggler-man, two voices braiding together in a language that sat wrong in Ash's ears — too many hard edges, too few held vowels, a language that had forgotten how to grieve.

Ash lay still and took inventory, the way the teachers had taught, the way that kept the screaming on the inside where it belonged.

Body: here. Cold feet, hot face, a bruise on the hip from the wagon's bench. Breath: working. Heart: too fast, but slowing.

Name: — and here the inventory always snagged, because Ash's true name was not a word, it was a chord, three tones struck together, and there was no one left alive to strike it. *Ash* was the bone-woman's word, given kindly, meaning the grey soft thing that fire leaves behind. It fit better than the bone-woman knew.

People: gone. Tower: sealed. Task: —

The task. Ash turned toward it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The task was the reason for the silence. The task was why Ash had walked out of the mountains on burned feet, down roads where every shepherd's whistle sounded almost like home and never was, past villages where children pointed and mothers pulled them close, through winters that tried to finish what the fire started. The task was to wait. To watch. To listen for the mountain forgetting how to hold its breath.

And the mountain — Ash felt this now, nightly, a hum in the teeth that the bone-woman seemed to feel too, though she blamed her carvings — the mountain was beginning to forget.

* * *

Memory gave Ash no more rest than waking did, so Ash stopped fighting it and let the water rise again, deliberately, the way you press on a bruise to learn its shape.

The tower again, but lower now, deeper. This was the part the teachers had saved for last, the part they taught in the year before the sealing, when the bargains had already been struck and the Wardens' iron was already gleaming on the horizon like a promise no one wanted kept.

Here is what the loud world did not know, what the Crown had made sure it would never know: the Spire-folk had not lost the war. The Spire-folk had *ended* it. There was a difference, and the difference was bought with everything.

The enemy kings had come with fire, yes, but fire alone had not driven the people down. What drove them down was arithmetic. Their own healers had counted. Their seed-stores, their wells, their wounded. Stay and fight, and the language died in a season, screamed out on battlefields. Go down into the deep galleries beneath the tower — the ones already carved, already named, the oldest holy places — and seal the doors, and the language lived. Sleeping, but living. Six hundred years, the star-readers said. Six hundred years, and the world above would forget its hatreds the way it forgot everything, and the doors could open onto a country that no longer remembered why it was afraid.

So they went down. All of them. The old ones first, walking steady so the young ones would not be frightened, singing as they went because that was the law. And the doors — the great door, cut from a single vertebra of the living mountain, older than the tower itself — the doors closed behind them, and the masons who closed them were the last to remain outside, and they wept as they worked, and when the work was done they scattered into the loud world and let their own names fall out of use so no one could follow the thread back.

But not everyone went down.

Someone had to stay outside. Someone had to carry the key — not iron, never iron, the key was a song, three notes, the same three notes as Ash's name — someone had to walk out into the years and keep the chord alive in a body, hidden, silent, waiting for the appointed hour. One child in each generation, chosen and taught and sent away, and each one grew old in exile and passed the chord to another child, whisper to whisper, down six hundred years of whispers.

Ash was the last whisper. Ash knew this the way you know a wound is mortal: quietly, completely, without any need to be told twice.

* * *

And here was the thing Ash could not say, could not sign, could not carve into any surface the bone-woman might read, the thing that sat heaviest of all:

Ash missed it.

Ash lay in the dark of the rattling wagon and missed the tomb.

Missed the great hall where ten thousand people breathed in unison. Missed the Naming Room and its warm walls. Missed the teachers' voices, the cedar smoke, the low communal hum that meant *you are not alone, you have never once been alone.* Missed even the fear, because the fear had been shared, passed hand to hand like bread, and shared fear is nearly a kind of love.

The loud world had given Ash cold, and hunger, and the endless grinding loneliness of hearing a hundred languages a day and none of them true. The loud world had given Ash the bone-woman — and something in Ash's chest went strange and painful whenever the bone-woman looked directly at Ash, something that felt dangerously like the hall, like the hum, like home — but the loud world had mostly given Ash silence, and silence was supposed to be the mission, and instead it was just quiet.

Homesick for a prison. Ash turned the thought over and found it ugly and true. The teachers had warned of this too, in the last lesson, the last words spoken into Ash's ear before the sending-away: *You will hate us a little, little keeper. That is permitted. Hate us and keep the chord anyway.*

Ash kept the chord. Had kept it every night since the fire, lying awake, striking the three tones silently against the roof of the mouth like a tongue checking for a loose tooth. *Home. Door. Open.* That was what the chord meant, all three meanings at once, which was the cruelty and the mercy of the old tongue: one word could be a place, a promise, and a plea.

*Home. Door. Open.*

Outside, the wagon hit a rut and the bones in the cargo crate behind Ash's head clicked together, and — Ash went rigid — they answered. Faintly, sleepily, in the flat dead speech the bone-woman heard and translated: a murmur of the recently buried, complaining of the road. But underneath their muttering, underneath even the wagon's groaning wheels, Ash heard the other thing, the thing growing louder every night now, rising out of the earth itself like water into a cellar.

The mountain. Humming. Off-key. Forgetting how to hold its breath.

And threaded through the hum, unmistakable, terrifying, a second sound: hoofbeats. Many. Far off yet, back along the road they had taken, moving fast, moving in the disciplined rhythm that Ash's people had learned six hundred years ago to dread — iron keeping time.

Ash sat up in the dark. Ahead, the bone-woman's voice stopped mid-sentence.

She had heard it too.
