## 8. Smuggler's Justice

Renn Carrow had a rule about waystations: never sleep in one longer than it took to eat, never drink anything you hadn't watched poured, and never — *never* — let the people you were protecting stand between you and the door.

He broke all three rules within an hour of reaching the Drowned Lantern.

It wasn't his fault, exactly. The storm did it. Three days out of Elodie's valley, with the burned smell of her workshop still living in his memory like a splinter he couldn't dig out, the weather had turned from bad to biblical, and the Drowned Lantern was the only roof between them and the high passes of Thornfell. It crouched at the junction where the smugglers' road met the old toll road, a squat stone inn built by someone who'd expected siege and gotten tax collectors instead. Its walls were two feet thick. Its windows were slits. Its common room smelled of wet wool, tallow, and the particular despair of travelers who had paid too much for too little.

And it was full of refugees.

They'd come up from the lowlands in ones and twos over the past day — families flooded off their farms when the rivers jumped their banks, or so they said, though Renn had counted four men among them with sword-calluses in the wrong places. They huddled around the fire in layers of damp clothing, children asleep on straw pallets along the wall, a woman with a nursing infant rocking slowly in the corner. The innkeeper, a broad man named Hesk whose face suggested he'd once been generous and had since been cured like leather, took Renn's coin without meeting his eyes.

"Back room's taken," Hesk said. "You'll have the benches."

"I'll have the back room," Renn said pleasantly, "or I'll have a conversation with your other guests about what this place is really for." He let that sit. The Drowned Lantern sat on no honest map; its cellars held goods that had never seen a customs seal. Hesk knew it. Renn knew Hesk knew it. That was how business worked in Thornfell — everyone holding everyone else's throat, gently, like partners.

Hesk grunted and found the room.

Which left the problem of dinner, which meant crossing the common room, which meant Elodie and Ash sitting at the fire like any bone-carver and any child, except that Ash was humming.

Low. Almost under breath. One of those melodies that made the hair on Renn's arms lift even though he couldn't have said why — a tune with no edges to it, that seemed to go somewhere behind the ears rather than into them. Around the hearth, three of the refugee children had stopped their squabbling to listen. One of them, a girl of maybe six with chilblains on her fingers, had crawled closer and was staring at Ash with the naked fascination only children and cats possess.

"Don't," Renn murmured as he passed, bending to adjust a bootlace that needed no adjusting. "Not here."

Ash looked up at him with those gray eyes that were too old for the face and too young for everything else, and stopped humming, which was somehow worse, because the silence after felt like something being withheld.

Elodie caught his sleeve as he straightened. Her grip was stronger than it looked; carver's hands, all tendon. "There are men by the window," she said quietly. "Four of them. They came in after us. They've been watching the child since we sat down."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He did. He'd known before she pointed them out, because one of the men wore a river-pearl earring that didn't match his coat, and river pearls came from the drowned country downstream, and men from the drowned country who traveled in fours in winter weren't refugees. They were wreckers — the kind who worked the flooded valleys and the river crossings, taking whatever the water spared and whatever the desperate carried. Renn had done business with their kind. He'd also buried their kind, on one memorable occasion, behind a chapel in Marrowford.

"Eat," he told Elodie. "Keep the child close. I'm going to go be charming."

"You're not charming," Elodie said. "You're glib."

"Glib is charming with better timing." He squeezed her hand once — an accident, mostly, though he noticed he didn't take it back quickly enough — and crossed the room with the loose, unhurried walk that had kept him alive through more taverns than he could count.

The four men by the window watched him come. Good. Men who pretended not to watch were worse.

"Gentlemen." He dropped onto the bench across from them uninvited, set his back against the wall — always the wall — and smiled the smile that opened doors and closed coffins. "Cold night for traveling."

The nearest one, a slab-faced man with the pearl earring, gave him the flat look of a man deciding whether a stranger was prey, weather, or paperwork. "We're not looking for company."

"Lucky. I'm not selling any." Renn tipped his head toward the fire. "Your friends there. Refugees?"

"What's it to you?"

"Nothing. Nothing at all. Terrible thing, the floods." He stretched, casual as a cat showing its belly so you'd forget about the claws. "Lot of desperate folk on the roads this season. Lot of folk carrying everything they own in a sack, because the sack's all they've got left." He met pearl-earring's eyes. "Terrible temptation, a road full of sacks."

The man's expression didn't change, but the man beside him shifted his weight, and that was answer enough.

"Whatever you're selling," pearl-earring said, "we're not buying."

"Good thing. I'm not selling." Renn stood, unhurried. "I'm just a man who hates waste. You seem like men who understand value." He let the word hang — *value* — the way a butcher lets the cleaver hang, and walked back to the fire feeling four sets of eyes between his shoulder blades like sunburn.

* * *

Elodie Voss, Renn decided somewhere past midnight, lied beautifully and fought terribly and forgave far too easily, and watching her pretend to sleep on the bench with one hand resting on Ash's shoulder made something in his chest ache in a way he refused to name because naming things gave them power over you, and Renn Carrow had spent forty years making sure nothing had power over him except debts and weather.

She wasn't sleeping. Neither was he. Neither, judging by the small rigid shape beside her, was Ash.

The inn settled into its night sounds — wind worrying the slitted windows, the fire collapsing into itself, someone snoring like a sawmill. And beneath all of it, faint but growing, the sound Renn had been waiting for with the weary certainty of a man who has played this scene before: boots in the yard. Soft ones. More than one pair.

He rose without a whisper of cloth, crossed to the back room, and looked out through the gap in the shutters.

Five figures moving toward the inn's rear door. Not four. They'd picked up a fifth, or one had been outside all along doing the arithmetic of exits. They moved well — not soldiers' precision, but the practiced economy of men who had done ugly work often enough to stop thinking about it. One carried a coil of rope. Another had a short heavy club, the kind that left bruises instead of questions.

They weren't coming for Ash. That was the first thing Renn understood, and understanding it changed everything. They were going out the back, down to the barn, where the refugees who could afford a pallet of straw instead of the common-room floor had bedded down. A family with two oxen. A widow with a strongbox sewn into her mattress. The easy pickings, sorted from the hard ones while everyone sat by the fire.

Renn stood in the dark and did the math he hated most.

Option one: wake Elodie, gather Ash, slip out the front and be three miles gone before anyone screamed. Clean. Sensible. The kind of choice that had kept him alive and rich and, if he was honest, hollow in the places where other men kept their souls. The child was the mission. The mission was the child. Everything else on this road was scenery.

Option two.

He thought about the girl with the chilblains, who had listened to Ash's humming like it was bread. He thought about the oxen family, who would wake tomorrow to nothing, and then perhaps not wake at all, because wreckers didn't leave witnesses to describe their faces. He thought about the law, and laughed silently at himself, because the law in Thornfell was whatever the nearest armed man said it was, and tonight, at the Drowned Lantern, the nearest armed man was him.

*Gods damn it,* he thought, with real feeling.

Then he woke Elodie.

She came awake the way he'd learned she did everything — instantly, completely, no fog of sleep, as if she'd only ever been pretending to rest inside her own body. He put a finger to his lips and told it to her in six words: "Men in the yard. Barn. Wreckers."

Her face went through three expressions in a heartbeat — fear, calculation, and then something harder than either. "The refugees."

"The refugees."

"We could leave."

"We could," Renn agreed. "And then tomorrow you get to explain to the child why the singing girl with the cold hands is dead, and I promise you that conversation goes badly. Ash has opinions about abandonment. Strong ones. Old-tongue ones, probably, with subtitles."

Something flickered across Elodie's mouth that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "What do you need?"

"Ash stays with you. Front door. If I'm not back in a quarter hour, take the mule and the north track and don't look back, and I mean that, Voss, don't look back, it's bad luck and bad tactics." He was already pulling on his coat, checking the knife at his hip and the second knife at his ankle and the third knife that lived somewhere he declined to discuss in mixed company. "I'm going to go ruin these gentlemen's evening."

"Renn." Her hand closed on his wrist. In the dark her eyes caught the last ember-light and held it. "Don't die. I've grown accustomed to your glibness. It would be inconvenient."

"That," he said softly, "is the nicest thing anyone's said to me since the war," and went out through the kitchen.

* * *

The barn fight lasted ninety seconds and cost Renn more than he wanted to pay.

He got the first one honestly — the sentry at the barn door, a boy of nineteen with a club and no imagination, who turned at the wrong moment and met Renn's fist with the hinge of his jaw. Down like a dropped sack. No killing there; the boy was somebody's son and hadn't yet graduated to genuine evil.

The second and third were inside, already at the widow's pallet, the strongbox half-torn from the ticking. Rann came through the door loud — noise was a weapon, noise woke the whole barn, and a whole awake barn was armor — shouting that the Wardens were coming up the toll road, torches and crossbows, run now and live. Two of the refugees bolted upright screaming. The wreckers hesitated, and hesitation against Renn Carrow was a luxury billed at knife-point.

He put his second knife into the third man's shoulder — flesh, not depth; he wanted him bleeding and disarmed, not dead — and took a hay-hook across the forearm from the fourth, a wiry bastard who fought like a ferret. The pain was white and immediate. Renn gave it back to him twice over, elbow to teeth, knee to groin, and then the ferret-man was down and the big one with the pearl earring was in the doorway with a drawn blade and murder in his face, and for one long moment the two of them stood in the trampled straw with five terrified families watching and the wind screaming past the open door.

"Pearl," Renn panted, blood dripping off his fingers, smiling with all the warmth of a drawn blade. "Walk away. Last offer. Free of charge, which should tell you how much I want to avoid the paperwork."

Pearl-earring charged.

Later, Elodie would ask him what happened, and he would tell her a version with fewer details, and she would know it was a version, and she would not press, and that was one of the ten thousand reasons he —

No. Later he'd think about that. Not now.

Now there was only the fight: steel ringing off his parrying knife, the shock up both arms, the barn wall slamming against his back, Pearl's blade scoring a hot line across his ribs, and then the moment — the terrible, necessary moment — when Renn stopped fighting like a man defending himself and started fighting like what he was. His thumb-knife came out from under his wrist. He drove it up under Pearl's arm, into the armpit, deep, angling for the lung, and held on while the man shuddered against him, breath going wet and ragged, until the big body sagged and Renn lowered it to the straw with a strange, terrible gentleness, the gentleness of a man laying down a debt he could never repay.

The barn was silent except for the wind and the widow sobbing into her hands.

Renn stood over the dying wrecker and did not look away. That was the code, such as it was — his own, unwritten, enforced by nobody, costing him everything every time he invoked it. If you kill, you watch. If you take a life outside the law, you owe the dead your attention, because the law will never collect. He knelt, pressed his palm briefly over the man's eyes when the rattling finally stopped, and said, in the flat voice he saved for ledgers and graves:

"Debt entered. Against my name. Noted."

Then he stood, bound the wounded wrecker and the unconscious boy back to back with their own rope, relieved them of weapons, coin, and the coil of rope's twin, and turned to find the entire barn staring at him.

The ox-farmer, gray-faced: "Who — who *are* you?"

"Nobody," Renn said, wrapping his bleeding forearm in a strip of the widow's torn mattress-ticking that she pressed on him without being asked. "Go back to sleep. Door's barred. Nobody's coming in." He paused at the threshold. "If anyone asks tomorrow, the Wardens did this."

"They'll know that's a lie," the farmer said.

"Yes," Renn agreed. "But it's a lie you can survive. Mine, you couldn't."

* * *

Elodie was waiting in the common room, and the look on her face when he came in — bloody, limping, pale as milk — was not horror and was not gratitude and was some third thing he had no map for. She took his arm without asking and sat him by the rekindled fire and cleaned the rib-gash with spirits she produced from her pack, and her hands were steady and her jaw was tight and Ash stood behind her, watching him with those gray eyes that missed nothing.

"You killed a man," Elodie said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd heard the barn. Everyone had heard the barn.

"One. Wounded three. Robbed two." He hissed as the spirit found the cut. "I'd call it a productive evening."

"It isn't funny."

"No," he said, and the glibness fell off him like a shed coat, and what was underneath surprised them both — tired, plain, human. "It isn't. But the alternative was waking up tomorrow to a barn full of corpses that weren't mine to make, and I've done that math before too, Voss, and it comes out worse. There's no law out here. There hasn't been for years. Just men like me, choosing, night by night, what we're willing to be. Tonight I chose this." He looked at his red-stained hands. "Some nights the choosing costs. That's the whole trick of it. That's the whole price."

Ash moved then. Crossed the space between them, small bare feet silent on stone, and touched Renn's wounded arm — just laid those strange fingers lightly over the bandage, the way you'd touch something fragile, or something owed.

And hummed. Three notes only, low, that same edgeless melody, and the fire leaned, and somewhere in Renn's pack his mother's carved whistle — dead twenty years, packed away dead — gave one soft, clear note in answer, like a voice remembering itself.

Renn went very still.

"What," he said carefully, "was that."

Elodie was staring at the pack. "The dead," she whispered, "answering him. Renn — they don't do that. They don't answer. They only speak to me." Her eyes came up to his, wide and frightened and lit with wonder. "Unless they've been waiting for something."

Outside, far off down the black throat of the toll road, a horn sounded — long, disciplined, unmistakable. Iron keeping time.

And answering it, from the heights above the Drowned Lantern, faint as breath: the mountain, humming back.
