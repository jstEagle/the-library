## 9. Voices in the Pass

The mountain began to hum on the third morning above Greywater, and by noon it was singing.

Elodie felt it first in her teeth. That was how her gift always announced itself — not as sound but as pressure, a vibration lodged in the jaw, as though someone had slipped a word between her molars and left it there to swell. She had spent thirty years learning to live with that pressure, to sort it, to carve it into something bearable. But this was different. This was not one voice asking to be heard through a piece of worked bone. This was the stone itself, the whole grey shoulder of Thornfell, resonating like a struck bell that no one had rung.

She stopped on the trail and pressed her palm flat against a boulder the size of a haywain. The rock was cold — mountain-cold, the kind of cold that had never once known sunlight — but beneath the cold she could feel it: a tremor, fine as a spider's footfall, rhythmic and patient.

*There are words in here,* she thought. *Old ones.*

Renn Carrow stood a few paces up the trail with his hands on his hips, squinting at the ridgeline the way he squinted at everything — as if the world were a ledger and he was auditing it for discrepancies. He had been uncharacteristically quiet since dawn. For a man whose silences usually had the texture of calculation, this silence had the texture of unease.

"You feel that?" he asked, when Elodie straightened.

"I feel it."

"It's not right." He said it flatly, without his usual flourish of complaint. "Mountains don't sing. I've crossed Thornfell eleven times. Twelve, if you count the winter I'd rather forget. Rock doesn't do this."

"Rock remembers," Elodie said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It had come out of her the way the dead's words sometimes did — arriving fully formed, wearing her mouth like a borrowed coat. She looked at Renn and saw him go still, the way men went still when they brushed against something their ledgers couldn't price.

Ash was ahead of them, twenty paces up the switchback, and had stopped too. The child stood facing the cliff wall with both hands raised, palms out, fingers spread wide — a gesture Elodie had seen before, back in the workshop, on the night the jawbone had spoken. Ash's small body was rigid with attention, a tuning fork of flesh, and the humming in the stone thickened around the child like smoke around a flame.

Elodie watched, and what she felt watching was not fear. Not yet. It was awe — the raw, unaccommodated kind, the kind that arrives before you have time to dress it in theology or dread. Something in this mountain knew her child. Something older than every kingdom whose coins Renn smuggled and whose laws Corvane enforced was reaching toward the small figure on the trail with the unmistakable hunger of recognition.

And it was angry.

She could hear that now, as the hum resolved into something like language. Her gift translated the way a bad ferry crosses a river — lurching, leaking, arriving at the far bank with half its cargo lost — but even through the leaks she caught the shape of it. Grief first. Then accusation. Then a word she had carved once, years ago, into a dead soldier's knucklebone at his widow's weeping request, a word from a tongue so old the scholars called it pre-kingdom: *thessaly*. Betrayal. The bones remembered it. Now the mountain was saying it, over and over, a wound rubbed raw across six centuries.

"They know," Elodie whispered. She wasn't sure she was speaking to Renn. Perhaps she was speaking to herself, or to the mountain, or to whatever fragment of the old people still lived in the stone. "They know what's coming up their trail."

Renn glanced at her sidelong. "Know what?"

"Us." She wiped her palms on her coat. "Ash. Me. What we're carrying."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the singing grew.

It came in waves now, rising out of the rock on either side of the trail like heat off summer stones, except that it carried words. Elodie had learned, over years of listening to the dead through bone and ivory and river-clay, that anger has a grammar all its own. The freshly dead speak in fragments — a name, a debt, a door left unlatched. But the long-dead, the ones who have had centuries to compose themselves, speak in arguments. They build cases. They stack grievance upon grievance like masons stacking stone, and the structure they raise is terrible because it is load-bearing: it holds up everything they died believing.

These voices were building a case.

She caught pieces of it as they climbed. *We were promised.* *The seal was sworn in blood and they called it peace.* *Our names were taken from us and given to the wind.* Phrases that repeated, shifted, braided together into something that was less a lament than an indictment. And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain, a single demand that made the hair on her arms lift:

*Bring her back. Bring the voice back. We have waited in the dark long enough.*

They meant Ash. Elodie understood that with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, the way she always understood the dead — not through reasoning but through resonance, the way a string answers a note sung nearby. The mountain wanted the child. And the mountain was very large, and very old, and very angry, and it had been waiting six hundred years, and it was beginning to sound less like grief and more like appetite.

By late afternoon the trail narrowed where it cut beneath an overhang of black granite, and the voices concentrated there, pooling in the hollow of the stone like water in a cupped hand. Walking through that stretch was like wading upstream through a river of speech. Elodie's skull ached. Her finished carvings — the small protective tokens she carried in a leather roll against her spine, each one holding a paid-for voice, a settled account — began to whisper all at once, waking each other like a rookery at dusk. Even the dead she had served, the ones whose last words she had faithfully delivered and whose bones she had laid respectfully to rest, were stirring now, drawn up out of their quiet by the gravitational pull of the mountain's fury.

Renn walked faster. He would not admit he was frightened — Renn admitted nothing, ever; charm was armor and he wore it riveted shut — but Elodie saw his hand rest twice on the knife at his belt, and she saw him glance back at Ash with an expression he probably believed was unreadable and was not.

Ash, meanwhile, had gone pale. Not frightened-pale. Attending-pale. The child walked with head tilted, lips slightly parted, drinking the mountain in the way a parched traveler drinks from a spring — desperately, gratefully, with both hands cupped. And Elodie realized, watching, that for Ash this was the first time since the workshop, perhaps the first time in memory, that the world had spoken back.

Everywhere else, Ash's language had been a wall. In villages, in waystations, on the road — the old tongue met only blankness, suspicion, the flat stare of people who heard noise where meaning should be. Ash had lived inside that wall for however many years a child carries before arriving frozen at a stranger's door. Elodie had broken through it a little, in fragments, with her gift and her patience. But this — this mountain answering in full-throated, furious, fluent *home* —

This was the first conversation Ash had ever had with the world at large.

No wonder the child had stopped walking. No wonder those small hands were pressed against the stone as though greeting a face.

* * *

Dusk caught them in a hanging valley, a bowl of scree and stunted pine ringed by cliffs on three sides, and it was there that the mountain stopped humming and started demanding.

Elodie felt the change before she heard it. The pressure in her jaw sharpened to a point, then split, then multiplied — one voice becoming ten, ten becoming a hundred, a hundred becoming a weather system of grievance descending on the little valley. The dead of Thornfell were not the dead she knew. The dead she knew were individuals: a woman's last word to her daughter, a soldier's unfinished letter, a miller's complaint about his brother. Manageable griefs, one at a time, each with a name and a price. These were not individuals. These were a *people*, and they spoke with the accumulated weight of a people wronged, and they had found their audience.

Because Ash had stopped again. Stood still in the center of the hanging valley with arms slightly away from the body, head back, eyes closed — and the voices had all turned toward the child at once, the way iron filings turn toward a lodestone, and now they were pouring downhill along the scree slopes, through the pine roots, out of the cliff faces, converging.

*Rise,* the voices said. Or that was the nearest word Elodie's gift could find. *Wake. Open. Come down. Come out. You were sent — you were sent — why have you waited — why have you let them keep us —*

The demand had teeth. Elodie could feel them. Six hundred years of sealed darkness does not make the dead gentle; it makes them precise. They had had six hundred years to refine exactly what they wanted and exactly whom they blamed, and they were reciting it now with the fluency of a liturgy, and the liturgy ended at Ash's feet like a wave ending on sand.

And Ash — Ash was swaying. Swaying under the weight of it, a sapling in wind made of voices, and Elodie's heart clenched with a fear that had nothing to do with Wardens or truces or anything so clean. This was the older fear. The fear of watching a child be asked to carry something no child should carry, and knowing the askers would not stop, because they had waited six hundred years and did not know how to wait anymore.

"We have to move," Renn said quietly. He had gone practical the way he always did when terror got too large to be charming about. "Camp's another mile up. We can outrun—" 

"No." Elodie heard herself say it and was surprised. "We can't outrun a mountain, Renn. And we shouldn't."

He looked at her. "Then what?"

What indeed. She didn't know. She only knew that running from this would be a second sealing — Ash sealed away from the voices, the voices sealed away from hope, everyone locked behind doors built of fear. She had spent her whole life behind such doors. She recognized their architecture intimately: the careful neutrality, the competence-as-armor, the loneliness dressed up as discipline. She had built hers herself, board by board. She would not help build one around a child.

So she did the only thing her strange life had taught her to do. She stepped closer to Ash, close enough that her shadow fell across the child's shoulders, and she listened harder than she had ever listened — opened herself the way she opened when she carved, all the gates down, the gift running free — and she tried to hear past the anger to whatever lay underneath it.

Underneath, there was a question.

Not *wake us*. Not *avenge us*. Underneath all the stacked centuries of grievance, at the very bottom of the case the dead had built, there was a smaller voice, an older voice, asking something almost unbearably simple. Elodie strained after it the way she strained after a whisper in a cracked knucklebone, and caught it, and it undid her:

*Does anyone remember our names?*

That was all. After six hundred years. After the fire and the seal and the Crown's iron and the long dark. Not freedom, not vengeance — remembrance. Whether they had been erased or merely entombed. Whether somewhere above, in the world of the living, anyone still knew that they had existed at all.

Elodie's throat closed. She turned to Ash and found the child already looking at her — had been looking at her, she realized, for some time, with an expression she had never seen before: expectant. Waiting. As if Ash had heard the question too, long ago, and had been waiting all this time for someone to answer it.

"Ash," she said softly. "Can you tell them? Can you tell them they're remembered?"

The child's eyes widened. And then Ash did something that Elodie would carry for the rest of her life, the way she carried the dead woman's last words, the way she carried every voice her gift had ever held.

Ash began to sing.

* * *

It was not a song Elodie knew. It was not a song any living person knew. It rose out of Ash the way water rises out of a spring — unforced, untaught, as though the melody had been waiting in the child's chest all along for exactly this hour, this valley, this convergence of grief and starlight. The notes were strange to Elodie's ear, built on intervals her culture had forgotten, sliding between tones the way water slides between stones. But the structure of it — the *shape* of it — she recognized instantly, because she had heard its skeleton in every carving she had ever made. It was a naming-song. A song whose entire purpose was remembrance.

And as Ash sang, the mountain answered.

The voices did not fall silent — Elodie would learn later that they never truly fell silent, that silence was the one thing the seal had taught them and the one thing they refused — but they *changed*. The anger drained out of them the way fever breaks, not vanishing but breaking, dispersing, resolving. The stacked indictment came apart into its individual stones, and each stone became itself again: not a people's grievance but a person's name. One voice sang back a single syllable and stopped, satisfied. Another answered with two. Another with a long rolling phrase that sounded like a river being described to someone who had never seen one. Name after name after name, hundreds of them, thousands, the dead of Thornfell calling themselves into the dusk one by one, and Ash receiving each one and folding it into the song, weaving the syllables into the melody like a weaver adding threads.

Elodie stood and listened and wept without shame.

This was what her gift had always been groping toward, she realized. All those years alone in the workshop, all those finger-bones and jawbones and knucklebones, all those last words delivered for coin to the grieving — she had been practicing, without knowing it, for this. Translation. Not of words but of *intent*, of the thing beneath the thing. And here was a child doing natively, effortlessly, what she had labored decades to approximate — standing in a hanging valley conducting a choir of the six-centuries dead with nothing but breath and memory, and the dead were not demanding anymore. They were *introducing themselves*.

Renn took off his hat. Elodie noticed that. Renn Carrow, who mocked piety the way other men breathed, who had a joke loaded for every solemnity, stood bareheaded in the cold and did not say one word. His hat hung from his hand. His face, in the last blue light, wore an expression Elodie had never seen on it — stripped, unguarded, young. Whatever he owed Ash's people, whatever guilt he wore beneath the charm like a stone sewn into a coat lining, it was standing exposed in front of him now, singing.

The song went on for a long time. Long enough for the stars to come out hard and clear in the mountain cold. Long enough for the fire they finally dared to light to burn down to coals. And through all of it the valley rang gently with names — not the furious convergence of afternoon but something softer, a vast slow circulation, the dead moving among themselves like guests at a gathering, greeting, remembering, settling.

At some point near midnight, Ash stopped singing. Sat down abruptly by the fire, wrung out, hollow-eyed, and leaned against Elodie's side without a word. Elodie put her arm around the child and felt the tremor still running through that small body, the aftershock of having held a mountain's grief in a throat the size of a reed.

"They asked," Elodie murmured, in the trade-tongue Ash understood best, the tongue of gestures and glances they had built between them over the weeks of flight. "You told them. That's enough. That's more than enough."

Ash shook her head slowly. Then, in the fragmentary way the child used Elodie's language — picking up words the way a magpie picks up bright things, hoarding them, deploying them at need — Ash said something that Elodie would turn over in her mind for days afterward.

"Not enough," Ash said. "Names is door."

Elodie stared at the fire. *Names is door.* The dead had not been comforted by remembrance, Ash was telling her. They had been *opened*. Every name spoken was a door unbarred, and behind each door was everything the seal had shut away — the memories, the knowledge, the fury, the hope, the whole buried nation of the Spire, stirring now like coals under ash. Remembrance had not satisfied them. Remembrance had *begun* them.

And high above the hanging valley, at the head of the pass, Elodie could feel it: a deep pulse in the stone, slow and immense, like a heartbeat heard through a cathedral floor. The Spire. However far it still was, however many days of climbing remained, it had heard the naming-song come echoing up the rock of the world, and it was answering — one great beat at a time, patient as geology, awake as a thing that has slept six hundred years and just been reminded it has a name.

Renn fed the fire another branch and looked from Elodie to Ash and back again, and finally said what he had been not-saying all day.

"That pulse," he said. "That's not echo, is it. That's not the mountain repeating the song."

"No," Elodie said.

"It's something *answering*."

"Yes."

Renn nodded slowly, and put his hat back on, and said, in the tone of a man reading a ledger entry he had hoped never to see: "Then we've got maybe three days before whatever's down there decides it's done waiting. And I'm guessing the Crown's Wardens will have heard it too. Sound carries in these passes. I've used that fact myself, more times than I can count."

He was right, and Elodie knew it, and the knowledge sat in her stomach like swallowed ice. Somewhere behind them — a day back, perhaps two — Warden-Sergeant Corvane was climbing with his kill-orders and his courteous voice and his conscience buried so deep even he had lost the map to it. And mountains carried sound the way rivers carried boats. The naming-song, the answering pulse, the waking of a sealed people — none of it was private. The whole spine of Thornfell was broadcasting now, and every listener in the world had six hundred years of reasons to be afraid of what it was saying.

Ash leaned heavier against her side. Elodie pulled the blanket up over the child's shoulders and looked into the fire, and felt the pulse in the stone beneath her, steady as a second heartbeat, and understood that the choice she had fled her valley to avoid making had followed her up the mountain and was waiting for her at the top.

The dead wanted to wake. The Crown wanted them sleeping forever. And between those two immovable things stood a mute child who had just proven that a song could open doors, and a bone-carver who had just proven she could hear what the doors concealed.

Three days, Renn had said. Elodie watched the coals settle and thought: *the mountain has started counting, and it is counting faster than we are.*
