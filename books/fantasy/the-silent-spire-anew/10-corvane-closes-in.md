## 10. Corvane Closes In

The holding cell beneath the Warden garrison at Greyford smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Warden-Sergeant Dain Corvane had been awake in it for eleven hours.

He did not sit. Sitting, he had learned long ago, was how interrogations went soft — the questioner's back found a chair, and then his voice found a rhythm, and then his questions found a pattern, and men learned patterns the way rats learned traps. So he stood, hands clasped behind him, boots planted on the flagstones as though the floor might at any moment try to argue with him, and he watched the man chained to the wall across from him with the patient attention of a falcon watching a hedgerow for something small and foolish to move.

The something small and foolish was named Tobin Marsh. Renn Carrow's cousin by marriage, or so the garrison's records claimed; Corvane put no great stock in records. He was a wiry man in his thirties with a smuggler's sun-creased neck and a smuggler's habit of looking at every door in a room before looking at the person asking him questions. His left wrist was chained high on the wall so that he could not quite sit comfortably either, which was not an accident. Discomfort loosened tongues faster than kindness ever had. Kindness made men think there was something to protect. Discomfort made them want only for it to end.

Corvane had been doing this work for nineteen years. He knew what the work was. He had never once pretended otherwise, not even to himself — that was the discipline of it, the thing that separated a Warden from a thug with a warrant. A thug enjoyed it. A Warden understood it. The understanding was supposed to be the armor.

"Water?" Corvane asked, for the fourth time.

Tobin Marsh said nothing. He had said nothing for eleven hours, which was longer than most men managed, and Corvane noted it without admiration. Silence was a currency, and everyone spent it eventually. The only question was the price.

He let another hour pass. He stood. He watched. The cell's single lamp guttered once when the draft came down the stairwell, and in the flicker Corvane saw Marsh's eyes track toward the sound of the door above — the heavy oak door, the one that opened onto the yard where the horses were stabled, where the supply wagons came and went. Men in cells listened to doors. It was all they had left of the world.

At midnight, Corvane sent for bread and salt fish and set it on the stool just beyond the reach of the chain, and he walked out, and he closed the door, and he waited in the corridor with his hand resting flat against the wood so that he could feel, through the grain, whether the man moved toward the food.

He felt nothing for twenty minutes. Then, faintly, the chain scraped stone.

* * *

They ate together, Corvane and the prisoner, sitting on opposite sides of the little table bolted to the floor, because Corvane had found over the years that men lied less with food in their mouths — not out of gratitude, nothing so noble, but because chewing slowed the lies down enough that he could hear the seams in them. He ate the same ration he'd given Marsh. That mattered too, though he could not have said why. Perhaps because it was true: they were both eating the Crown's bread tonight.

"You've been quiet a long time," Corvane said.

"I've got nothing worth saying."

"Everyone has something worth saying. Most people just don't know its value until someone names a price." Corvane tore his bread, unhurried. "You were taken on the Thornfell road with forty pounds of untaxed tin and a Warden's seal-stamp forged badly enough to insult us both. That's a hanging offense under the trade statutes. I don't much care about the tin."

"Then what do you care about?"

"The valley," Corvane said. "Kellin's Hollow and the ridges above it. Who lives there. Who passes through. What's been sung lately."

Marsh's eyes flickered — a small movement, quickly mastered, but Corvane had spent nineteen years learning to read the small movements, the ones men didn't know they made. There it was. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The moment a man realizes the question isn't random, that the net was drawn around him specifically, that his capture wasn't bad luck but design.

"I'm a tinsmith's runner," Marsh said. "I don't sing."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But you run for a man whose routes cross that valley, and a week ago there was a report out of Kellin's Hollow. Old-tongue singing. Do you know what the old tongue is, Tobin?"

"I've heard songs in taverns."

"You've heard tavern songs about the old tongue. Different animal entirely." Corvane set down his bread. "The report came from a shepherd who doesn't drink. Says he heard singing in a language older than the kingdom coming off the mountain near a bone-carver's workshop, and that the carver's finished pieces — bone, mind you, dead women's fingers and such — were whispering along with it. The shepherd is a sober man. Sober men are the worst kind of witness, because they can't be dismissed."

Marsh chewed slowly. Corvane watched the tendons in his jaw work and waited. Patience was the whole craft. Every man had a seam; you found it by pressing gently in many places until one gave.

"You burned something," Marsh said finally. Quietly. Almost against his will. "Didn't you. On your way through the valley."

Corvane went very still.

It was not the content of the sentence that caught him. It was the direction. Prisoners asked questions to probe what their captors knew — that was standard, expected, a game as old as chains. But this question was different. This question was *worried*. Marsh wasn't probing. Marsh was checking on something, the way a man checks on a wound he can feel has gone bad.

"What makes you say that?"

"Because Renn—" Marsh stopped himself. Swallowed. Started again, more carefully. "Because if the Wardens went up that valley asking about singing, and there was a workshop up there with carved bones in it, and the carver was the one keeping whatever-it-was quiet—" He shook his head. "Renn would have burned it. If she didn't burn it herself first. To protect the child."

*The child.*

Corvane had not mentioned a child. No report mentioned a child. The shepherd's account had described singing, whispering bone, a woman alone. And yet here was a smuggler in a hole beneath Greyford, speaking of a child as though the child were the fixed point around which everything else turned — the thing to be protected, the reason for fires, the center of gravity of an entire hidden life.

Corvane kept his face still. Inside, cold water began to move.

"There's a child," he said. It was not a question, and he did not make it sound like one.

Marsh looked at him for a long moment — looked at him the way men look when they realize they have already said too much and are deciding, in real time, how much worse to make it. Then something shifted in his expression, something Corvane had seen perhaps a dozen times in nineteen years and had never once learned to predict: not fear, not bargaining, but a kind of grim relief. As if the silence had become heavier than the truth, and the truth, terrible as it was, was at least a weight he could set down.

"There's a child," Marsh said. "Half-frozen, come down out of the high passes during the white storm two months back. Elodie Voss took it in. She's the bone-carver — she does grief-work, reads the last words out of the dead for families who can pay. She keeps her head down, pays her levies, sells to nobody on the wrong side of anything. Twenty years neutral." He laughed, short and bitter, with no humor anywhere in it. "Twenty years, and one child walks out of a blizzard humming songs that make dead bones talk, and now the whole valley's a lit candle in a dry field."

Corvane felt the words land in him one at a time, each with its own small weight.

*A child out of the high passes.* The high passes above Thornfell led nowhere living. Every route, every map, every report in the Warden archives agreed on that much: the high country above the Spire's shadow was empty, had been empty for six hundred years, empty by decree and by death and by the particular emptiness of places everyone had agreed never to look at again. Nothing came down out of those passes. Nothing had come down out of those passes since the sealing.

And yet here was a child. Humming. In a language that predated the kingdom. Making the dead answer.

"Describe the child," Corvane said, and his voice came out flatter than he intended, stripped of the pleasantness, and he heard the change himself and did not correct it, because some part of him understood that the time for pleasantness was passing.

"Small. Seven, eight years maybe. Dark-haired. Eyes like—" Marsh frowned, searching. "Like river ice. You know how ice looks when there's water moving underneath? Like that. Doesn't speak any tongue anyone knows. Doesn't cry. Doesn't laugh, neither, far as anyone saw. Just watches everything like it's taking inventory." He paused. "Voss named it Ash."

"Ash," Corvane repeated. He wrote nothing down. He never wrote things down in the room; writing made prisoners perform. He carried it instead, the way he carried everything, in the vault behind his eyes that had never once leaked. "And Voss burned her own workshop."

"So I heard. Secondhand — a drover who came through Greyford market three days back, before your people picked me up on the tin road. Said smoke over the hollow, and when he asked after it, folk said the bone-carver's place had gone up in the night, clean fire, and the carver gone, and no one would say gone where." Marsh's chained wrist shifted; the iron clinked softly. "That's why I figured Renn. He collects debts in that valley. He'd have moved her. He moves anyone, if the price is right, or if—" Again the stop. Again the swallow. "Or if he owed them."

"Owed whom?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters a great deal."

"It matters to *you*," Marsh said, and there was something almost fearless in the way he said it, the recklessness of a man who has already fallen and noticed, somewhere on the way down, that the ground was further away than he'd thought. "Sergeant. I'll tell you what I know, and I'll tell it straight, because straight is all I've got left. But I want you to understand something first. Whatever you think that child is — whatever your orders say it is — I held it once. At the waystation, this autumn, before the snows. Renn brought it through, and it was feverish, and somebody had to sit up with it, and I drew the short straw." He stopped. His voice roughened. "It talked in its sleep. All night. In that old language. And I don't speak any language older than my grandmother, but I'll tell you what I heard in it, clear as church bells."

Corvane said nothing. He waited, because waiting was the craft, and because some treacherous part of him wanted to hear it.

"It sounded like homesickness," Marsh said. "That's all. Homesick singing. Like something that got locked out in the cold a long, long time ago and never stopped walking home." He met Corvane's eyes. "Now you tell me what the Crown does to homesick children, Sergeant, and I'll tell you whether I sleep tonight."

* * *

Corvane did not answer immediately. He rose, gathered the plates, and did the small domestic work of the cell with his back to the prisoner, because backs turned were also instruments, and a man spoke differently to a shoulder than to a face.

What the Crown does to homesick children. He turned the question over as he stacked the tin plates, and it cut him in a place he had not permitted anything to cut him in nineteen years.

He knew the doctrine. Of course he knew the doctrine. Every Warden knew it, though it was never written down in any manual a recruit could hold — it was passed along the way dangerous knowledge is always passed, in low voices, in the space between one posting and the next, usually by a sergeant who had learned it from a captain who had learned it from a legend. *The Spire sleeps. The Spire must go on sleeping. Anything that comes down from the high country carrying the old tongue is not a refugee. It is a leak. And leaks are sealed.*

He had absorbed it young, the way one absorbs the shape of a room one grows up in — completely, unquestioningly, as simply the architecture of the world. The truce was six centuries old. The war it had ended had burned whole provinces. Whatever slept beneath the Silent Spire had been put there at a cost so terrible that the peace itself was built on the grave, and you did not dig up the foundation of a bridge to ask the dead beneath it how they felt about traffic. That was the reasoning. It was good reasoning. It had held for six hundred years, and Corvane was not a man who believed he was cleverer than six hundred years.

But.

There was always the *but*, and he had spent nineteen years stepping around it the way a man steps around a loose flagstone he refuses to look at directly. The doctrine said *leak*. The doctrine said *seal*. The doctrine had procedures, and the procedures were efficient, and efficiency was a virtue in the Warden service, prized openly, rewarded visibly.

The doctrine had never once, in all its careful machinery, accounted for a child humming in its sleep.

He thought of the reports he had filed over the years. Not many — the work was rare, which was itself reassuring, proof that the seal held, that the high country stayed empty. Twice before, in his earlier years, there had been incidents. A hermit on the northern ridge who was found to keep stones carved with characters no scholar could read; the hermit had died of exposure before the Wardens arrived, which the file recorded as natural causes, and which Corvane, younger then, had recorded in the same words without examining why the timing had been so convenient. A family of tinkerfolk detained outside Vael's Crossing after their mule was found carrying a bundle of old-tongue amulets; the family had been released after questioning, the amulets burned, the incident closed. He remembered the mother's face. He remembered it now, suddenly, with a clarity that felt less like memory than like summons: a tired woman with silver rings in her ears, saying, *We bought those at a market, we didn't make them, please, we have a baby* — and being released, and walking away, and Corvane standing in the road watching them go and feeling nothing at the time except the satisfaction of a correctly closed file.

He had felt nothing. That was the discipline. That was the armor. Feeling nothing was what the work required, and he had been very good at the work.

And now a smuggler in a cellar was telling him about a feverish child singing homesick songs in a dead language, and the armor had a crack in it, and through the crack came a sound Corvane had not heard in nineteen years, which was his own conscience clearing its throat.

He turned back to Marsh.

"This Renn Carrow. Describe him."

"Tall. Quick. Charming the way weather is charming right before it kills you. Smuggles tin mostly, sometimes medicine, once — I heard — a Warden's daughter out of an arranged match, though he denies it and the girl won't say." Marsh almost smiled. "He's the best route-man in Thornfell. If Voss and the child are moving, they're moving with him, and if they're moving with him, you won't find them on any road you're watching."

"Then I'll watch the roads he isn't on."

"That's the spirit," Marsh said dryly.

Corvane studied him. "Why are you telling me this? Your cousin-by-marriage runs with Carrow. You're handing me your own kin's trail."

Marsh was quiet for a while. When he answered, his voice had dropped into something low and unperformed, the register men find only when they have stopped negotiating.

"Because I sat up all night with that child," he said, "and because I've done the sums since. Whatever comes of this — whatever your superiors decide, whatever happens to Voss and Carrow and the rest of us — that child didn't choose any of it. It walked out of a place no one walks out of. It's eight years old. And the law you serve doesn't have a category for it, Sergeant. That's the truth underneath everything. The law has categories for smugglers and traitors and leaks, and this isn't any of them, and rather than build a new category, someone will just shove it into the nearest old one and hang the label on." He shrugged, as much as the chain allowed. "I'd rather it be me that shoved. Feels different, choosing your sins."

Corvane looked at him for a long moment.

"You'll be charged with the tin," he said. "Smuggling, forgery of a Warden's seal. The magistrates at Greyford are reasonable men; with cooperation on record, you'll see a fine and two years' labor detail, not the rope. That is the full extent of what I can promise, and I don't promise it lightly."

"And the child?"

Corvane picked up the lamp.

"The child," he said, "is a matter above both our weights."

* * *

He climbed the stairs out of the cell into the grey hour before dawn, and the garrison was quiet in the way garrisons were quiet — not silent, never silent, but hushed into the small necessary sounds of a machine idling: the rattle of a tack-room door, a sentry's boots on gravel, the stable-smell of horse and hay drifting from the yard. He crossed to the records room, unlocked it with his own key, and sat at the scarred oak desk with the shutters half-open to the last of the dark, and he wrote.

He wrote the report the way he wrote all his reports: precisely, sparely, without adjectives that could be construed as opinion. *Source in custody confirms presence of unregistered minor in Kellin's Hollow vicinity, exhibiting vocalization consistent with proscribed linguistic material. Primary custodian, one Elodie Voss, bone-carver, has destroyed premises and departed with subject. Movement likely facilitated by Renn Carrow, route-runner, known associate. Subject assessed as originating from the high country above the Thornfell massif.*

He stopped there, pen hovering, and looked at the blank space where the recommendation went.

This was the part of the craft no one taught, because teaching it would mean admitting it existed: the space between the facts and the recommendation, the narrow gap where a Warden's soul lived or didn't. The facts were fixed. The recommendation was not. The same facts could be framed a dozen ways, and the framing determined what came down from above, and what came down from above determined whether a child lived or died, and none of it — not one word of it — appeared anywhere in the record as a choice.

He thought about the phrasing.

*Subject poses containment risk; recommend immediate interdiction.* That was the honest frame, the doctrinal frame. Interdiction. Everyone in the service knew what interdiction meant when the subject was old-tongue material from the high country. It meant the leak was sealed. It meant the child was killed, quietly, and the killing was recorded as a sanitation measure, and the file was stamped and shelved, and in thirty years some other sergeant would pull it and read *interdicted* and feel nothing, the way Corvane himself had felt nothing about the tinkerwoman at Vael's Crossing, and the machine would turn another revolution, smooth and clean and fed.

*Subject is a minor of unknown provenance displaying no hostile behavior; recommend observation and assessment prior to action.* That was also honest. Also defensible. Also slower — and slowness, in this work, was often the difference between a sealed leak and a living child, because slow gave the hunted time to be far away, and distance gave everyone room to forget, and forgetting was the most merciful instrument the Crown possessed, though no one ever called it mercy.

He sat with the two sentences in front of him like a man sits with two roads.

Nineteen years of discipline said the first. Nineteen years of discipline said that doubt was a luxury of people who didn't carry the seal, that the peace of six hundred years rested on men willing to do the small terrible things early so the large terrible things never came, that he had accepted that bargain with open eyes at his swearing-in and had no right to renegotiate it now because a smuggler's story about a homesick child had gotten under his collar.

And underneath the discipline, faint as a vein of ore in grey rock, ran the other thing. The thing he had stepped around for nineteen years. The loose flagstone. The question he refused to look at directly, which was simply this: *if the seal is just, why does maintaining it require that no one ever look at what it seals?*

Justice bore examination. Only shame required darkness.

He had never spoken that thought aloud, not to himself even, not in the privacy of his own skull — he kept it the way one keeps a splinter worked deep into the palm, unnoticed until it ached, aching until it was undeniable. Tonight it ached. Tonight a man in irons had called a child homesick, and the word had gone through the leather and the mail and the scar tissue and found the splinter, and pressed.

Corvane dipped his pen.

He wrote: *Subject is a minor of unknown provenance, non-hostile, in company of armed adults. Recommend immediate interdiction with minimum necessary force. Given the nature of the material involved, interdiction is to be understood as a mercy — to the subject, to the valley, and to the peace. Prolonged pursuit risks panic, rumor, and the mobilization of sympathies better left dormant. A swift resolution is kinder to all parties than a protracted one.*

He read it twice.

It was masterful. He hated how masterful it was. Every clause was technically true. *Minimum necessary force* — yes, he would order that, genuinely; he had never been a butcher and would not start now. *A mercy* — and there was even a reading in which it was one, in which a quick end in a snowy pass was kinder than what the alternative machinery might eventually devise, in which the child's own people, entombed six centuries ago, had been granted precisely such a mercy by wiser or wearier men, and the whole edifice of the peace stood on mercies exactly like it. *Kinder to all parties* — true, true, all of it true, arranged in an order that made the killing sound like triage.

That was the devilry of it, he thought, setting down the pen. Not lies. Lies were easy; lies announced themselves; a Warden could refuse a lie and feel clean. No — the devilry was truth, ordered. Truth assembled with such care that it built the one conclusion you had already decided upon, so that when you signed, you could tell yourself you had merely reported. The report did the killing. The signature was just paperwork.

He signed it anyway.

His hand did not shake. Nineteen years had seen to that. He blotted the page, folded it, sealed it with the garrison stamp, and marked it for the morning courier to the regional commandant at Highmoor Keep — priority handling, eyes of the commandant only. By tomorrow night, orders would come back down. He knew what the orders would say, because he had written them, in advance, dressed as a request. The machine was elegant that way. A good sergeant never asked permission for what he could arrange to be commanded.

He sat for a while after, in the grey light thickening toward dawn, and the doubt came back, and he let it, briefly, the way a man lets himself look at a wound through a slit in the bandage — briefly, controlled, on a schedule. He thought of the child's eyes as Marsh had described them: river ice with water moving underneath. He thought of the singing. He thought, absurdly, of his own sister's boy, seven years old, who hummed when he was falling asleep, tuneless and endless, driving everyone in the house to distraction, and how Corvane had once sat on the edge of the boy's bed listening to it and felt something in his chest he had no name for and had never tried to name, because naming it would have meant admitting it was there the other nights too, all the nights, under the mail.

The boy hummed because he was safe. That was what humming was, maybe. The sound of a creature with nothing to guard against.

And somewhere in Thornfell, a child was humming in a language older than kingdoms, in the dark, in flight, guarded against everything — and the sound of it had frightened a valley, alarmed a shepherd, and summoned him.

*Interdiction is a mercy,* his report said.

He blew out the lamp, because the dawn had arrived and the lamp was now an affectation, and he sat in the growing light with his hands flat on the desk, and he performed the final act of the craft, the one that came after the signing, the one that made everything else possible: he put it away. The splinter, the crack, the boy's humming, the tinkerwoman's face, the homesick song — all of it, folded down small and stored in the vault behind his eyes, which had never once leaked, which would not leak now. Discipline was the armor. The armor had held for nineteen years.

It would hold a while longer. It had to. That was the whole of the oath, reduced to its skeleton: *it has to.*

* * *

By mid-morning the courier was gone, and Corvane was in the yard checking saddle girths with his own hands, because he trusted no groom's knot where his neck might depend on it, and because there was comfort, real comfort, in the dumb honesty of leather and buckles after a night of sentences that killed politely.

He was taking a patrol up-valley. Not to Kellin's Hollow — the Hollow was ash and absence now, the workshop a black tooth in the hillside, nothing left to find but a foundation and questions. He was riding instead for the waystations and the drovers' camps, the thin places where news moved with the mule trains, because if Voss and Carrow had gone to ground anywhere, it would be in the economy of small favors that kept the high roads alive: a sack of meal here, a repaired axle there, a name whispered to a ferryman. Smugglers moved through charity the way water moved through limestone. You found them by finding who had been paid in kindness.

Two riders would go with him — Warden Osk, steady and incurious, and young Warden Pell, three years in the service, sharp-eyed, a little too eager, the kind of recruit who still believed the work was simple. Corvane had chosen Pell deliberately. Eager men saw clearly; they simply hadn't yet learned what it cost to see. Their clarity was useful. Their education, when it came, was not his problem today.

He swung up into the saddle and looked once down the length of the Greyford road, running east toward the river country, toward the villages where the next chapter of this would unfold whether he rode or not — toward the bone-readers and the ferry-camps and the small warm rooms where hunted people were sheltered by strangers who owed them nothing, because the world, despite everything, kept producing grace in defiance of its own laws.

Somewhere out there, a bone-carver was buying passage with the voices of her dead. Somewhere out there, a child was deciding what six centuries of silence would become. Somewhere out there, Renn Carrow was choosing roads that did not exist on any map Corvane owned.

The sergeant gathered his reins.

"Highmoor's orders will be down by tomorrow," he told Osk and Pell, in the flat tone that brooked no discussion. "Until then, we ride quiet, we ask plain questions, and we bring back answers, not rumors. Move out."

The three riders went through the gate at a trot, iron-shod and purposeful, and the sound of them faded down the east road like a sentence being carried to its conclusion — and behind them, in the records room of the Greyford garrison, the sealed report lay in the courier tray, already gone, already working, mercy and murder folded together in four paragraphs of immaculate prose, traveling toward a decision that had, in every way that mattered, already been made.
