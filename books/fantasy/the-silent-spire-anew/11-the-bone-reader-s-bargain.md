## 11. The Bone-Reader's Bargain

The village was called Marrow's Rest, and it had earned the name honestly.

Elodie saw it first from the ridgeline above the river valley — a scatter of grey stone roofs huddled at the confluence of two streams, where the water ran slow and brown with tannin from the fens below. Smoke rose thin and straight from a dozen chimneys. There was a shrine to some river-god whose name had been worn off the lintel, a tithe-barn, and a single longhouse that served as tavern, meeting hall, and everything else. It was the kind of place the road forgot on purpose.

"We need horses," Renn said beside her, "or we need shoes, or we need both, and Ash needs feeding before she starts gnawing on my bootlaces again."

Ash, walking between them with the tireless, eerie steadiness she'd shown since Greyford Bridge, said nothing. She never said anything in words they understood. But she looked down at the village with an expression Elodie had learned to read like weather: a slight tilt of the head, a narrowing of the eyes, the way her small hands curled inward as if cupping something invisible.

*Listening,* Elodie thought. *She's listening.*

"What is it?" she asked quietly.

Ash turned to her and pressed one palm flat against her own chest, then extended it outward toward the village, fingers spreading like roots.

"Something's there," Elodie translated for Renn's benefit. "Something old."

"There's always something old," Renn said. "Old is cheap. What I'm worried about is something new, namely Warden patrols, because if Corvane's people have reached the east road—"

"They won't have," Elodie said. "Marrow's Rest isn't on any patrol route. That's why smugglers use it."

Renn glanced at her sidelong. "You know this village?"

"I know *of* it." And then, because lying to Renn Carrow had become more effort than it was worth: "There's a bone-reader here. Or there was, years ago, when I still traded by letter with others of my… trade." She almost said *craft*. It wasn't a craft. It had never been a craft. "Her name is Maren Ostwick. She does grief-work for the fen-folk. Drownings mostly. The river gives them back sometimes, and someone has to ask the bones what happened."

Renn was quiet for three steps. "You never mentioned you had colleagues."

"I don't." The word came out harder than she intended. "I have correspondence. Occasional. We send each other recipes for preparing bone so it holds a voice, and warnings about Wardens, and nothing else. Bone-readers don't gather, Renn. Two of us in one room is a market. Three is a coven. You know what happens to covens."

They came down into the village as the light went long and amber across the fens. The folk of Marrow's Rest watched them from doorways with the flat, assessing stares of people who lived by the water's leave and trusted nothing that arrived on foot. A woman mending nets didn't look up. A man splitting alder wood let his axe rest mid-swing until they'd passed. Ash walked closer to Elodie, her shoulder brushing Elodie's hip, and Elodie found herself doing what she'd been doing for weeks now without ever deciding to do it: putting her body slightly between the child and the world.

The longhouse smelled of peat smoke, wet wool, and eel stew. The keeper — a broad, grey-whiskered man with forearms like mooring posts — took Renn's coin for bread, cheese, and a corner near the hearth, and asked no questions, which meant he'd already asked them all silently and decided the answers could wait.

It was while they sat eating that Elodie felt it.

She felt it the way she felt all dead things: not as sound but as pressure, a faint ache behind the eyes, like standing too close to a struck bell. She set down her spoon.

Across the room, half-hidden in the shadow of the stair that led to the loft, an old woman sat alone with a cup of nettle tea. She was small and dry as winter reeds, with white hair braided tight against her skull and hands folded in her lap — hands Elodie recognized even across the distance, because they were shaped like her own. Short-nailed. Scarred at the knuckles from knives and files. The hands of someone who spent her life persuading hard material to give up its secrets.

The old woman was looking directly at her. Not curious. *Knowing.* And beneath the table, where no one else could see, her right hand lay open on her knee, palm up — and resting in the palm was a carved knucklebone, polished smooth, its surface worked with the fine spiral grain that every bone-reader used to open a listening.

Elodie's mouth went dry.

"She sees us," she murmured. "Or she sees *me*. Renn—"

"I see her," Renn said, not turning his head, voice pitched low under the clatter of the room. "Been watching us since we came in. Left side of the stairs. Old, but not slow — she tracked Ash when Ash crossed to the fire, tracked her the way you track something you've got a ledger entry about."

"Bone-reader?" Elodie nodded fractionally. "Maren Ostwick?"

"If it is, your correspondence has been quiet too long, because she hasn't taken her eyes off you since you sat down."

Ash had gone very still beside her. The child's gaze moved from the old woman to the little carved bone in her palm and back, and her breathing changed — quickened — and she made a soft sound in her throat, two notes falling, the pattern Elodie had learned meant *recognition*, meant *known thing*, meant *this matters*.

Then the old woman did something no stranger should have known to do. She lifted the knucklebone to her lips, breathed on it once — the old courtesy, *I come listening, not demanding* — and set it down on the table before her, pointing its spiral toward Elodie across the smoky room.

An invitation. An opening. In the language of their trade, as formal as a sealed letter.

*I will speak first. Will you answer?*

Elodie sat motionless, her supper cooling, and felt the trap of her own nature close around her with its familiar, gentle teeth.

* * *

They met in the old woman's cottage an hour later, because of course they did. Elodie told herself it was prudence — better to control the encounter than be controlled by it — and told Renn to stay with Ash, and knew as she said it that both lies were transparent.

The cottage stood apart from the others, at the fenward edge where the ground went soft, built on pilings like all the houses here. Inside it smelled of tallow and dried herbs and, underneath, the faint mineral sweetness that Elodie knew from her own workshop: the smell of worked bone. Shelves lined the walls, and on them sat rows of carvings — finger-bones incised with spirals, jaw-fragments in oiled cloth, small skulls of marsh birds, and larger pieces wrapped and labeled in the careful script of a working professional. A life's inventory. A graveyard organized by request.

"Maren Ostwick," the old woman said. Her voice was dry and level, a reed's voice. "You'll be Voss. Your handwork came through Highmoor four winters past — a rib carving, done for a drowned ferryman. I've seen a great deal of work, and yours was the finest I've seen outside the old books. So." She gestured to a stool. "Sit. And tell me why a bone-reader of your quality travels the Thornfell roads in the dead season, with a smuggler and a child who hums in the elder tongue."

Every instinct Elodie owned screamed at her to lie. She was good at lying; she'd lied to a Warden-Sergeant's face with her heart steady and her voice bored. But Maren Ostwick was not a Warden, and lying to another bone-reader was like lying to a mirror.

"The child is being hunted," she said. "By the Crown."

"Yes. I heard singing in my bones nine nights ago. All of them at once, every piece on those shelves, humming like struck wire. Nothing has done that in my sixty years of this work." The old woman poured tea without asking, pushed a cup across. "The elder tongue. The Spire-tongue. My grandmother heard it once, from a relic, and it killed the hearing in her left ear for a year. You're carrying it around in a child's throat."

"You've reported it?"

"To whom?" Maren smiled without warmth. "The Wardens burned a cache at Coldfen last month. Killed a boy named Tam for running it. The Crown pays me for drownings, girl, not betrayals. Now." She drank, set down the cup, and folded those scarred hands together. "You want horses, shoes, provisions, and the fen-path south, which avoids the bridges and the tolls. I can give you all of it. I have cousins who owe me, and a punt that goes down the black channel tomorrow at dawn."

"And the price," Elodie said. Because there would be a price; there was always a price, and bone-readers especially understood that gifts between their kind were debts with interest.

"My son was murdered eleven years ago." The old woman's voice did not change at all, which was how Elodie knew how much it cost her. "His name was Joren. He was twenty-two. They found his body in the black channel, weighted with chain, and the Crown's men called it a smuggling dispute and closed the book. I have carried his wrist-bone for eleven years." From her bodice she drew a small oilcloth bundle, unwrapped it on the table between them. A slender bone, carved already — partially — with a listening spiral that stopped unfinished, the grooves going shallow and lost halfway through. "Eleven years, and I cannot finish the carving. Every time I cut the final turn of the spiral, my hand shakes. I hear him start to speak and I stop, because—" For the first time her voice frayed. "Because I am afraid of what he'll say. Who gave the order. Whether it was someone I've since shared bread with. Whether he called for me at the end."

Elodie looked at the unfinished spiral and felt the whole weight of her gift settle onto her shoulders like a yoke.

"You're asking me to finish it."

"I'm asking you to *listen*. Finish the spiral and listen, and tell me his last words. That's all. That's the price of the punt, the horses, the safe passage." The old dark eyes held hers. "You understand why I can't do it myself. We never can, for our own. You taught me that yourself, four winters ago — you wrote that grief-work for kin is a knife with no handle. So I'm asking the one person on this coast who can hold the handle."

* * *

Elodie walked out to the edge of the pilings afterward and stood in the cold looking at the black channel water, and hated herself thoroughly and specifically.

Not for considering refusal. Refusal was reasonable; refusal was survival; she'd built her whole life on the wisdom of refusing entanglement. No — she hated herself because, in the space between Maren's request and her own answer, there had been a moment — a real, living moment, maybe two breaths long — in which she had weighed whether the boy's last words were worth a punt ride. She had *priced* him. Some cold clerk deep inside her had run the sums: risk of hearing, risk of involvement, weight against horses and a clear road south, and the scales had balanced with the boy on neither side, just a variable, just a term.

That was what her gift had made of her, after thirty years of practice. Not a healer of griefs. An accountant of them.

And underneath the self-hatred sat a colder, truer fear: she knew what listening cost. She had heard a hundred dying voices and kept her face still through all of them, and every one had lodged somewhere in her like grit in a joint. If she finished Maren's spiral, she would carry Joren Ostwick's death alongside all the others. If she refused, Maren would carry the not-knowing for whatever years remained to her, and Elodie would carry the refusal, and the gift that could not refuse — because that was the truth no one said aloud about bone-hearing: the dead spoke to her whether she opened the spiral or not, and every silence she sold was a silence she'd chosen, and the choosing wore grooves in her deeper than any knife.

Behind her, bare feet whispered on the boards. Ash came to stand beside her, small and warm against the wind, and followed Elodie's gaze out to the water.

"He's in there," Elodie said, though Ash couldn't understand the words. Maybe she understood anyway; she often seemed to. "Under the channel. Eleven years. His mother can't ask him why." She exhaled slowly. "And I'm standing here doing arithmetic."

Ash touched her arm. Then she pointed at the water, and pressed her hand to her own chest, and made the two-note sound — *known thing* — and then, deliberately, she pointed at Elodie's chest.

*You know things. This is what you're for.*

Or perhaps: *This is what you're allowed to be.* Elodie couldn't tell anymore where the translation ended and her own wanting began.

* * *

She finished the spiral that night in Maren's kitchen, by tallow-light, with the old woman sitting rigid across the table with her hands locked in her lap like a woman holding herself to a mast.

The wrist-bone took the cuts well. Eleven years in oilcloth had kept it sweet; the groove turned clean under Elodie's smallest blade, and she worked the final curve of the spiral with the same care she'd give any client's grief — except her own hands were not entirely steady either, and she noticed, and despised the noticing.

When the last cut closed the spiral, the room changed.

It was always the same, at the opening: a pressure drop, a stillness, the tallow-flame bending though there was no draft. Then the voice came — not through the ears, through the bone, through the teeth, a young man's voice with river-mud in it, rough and hurried and terribly alive:

*—cold, Mam, it's cold, I can't feel my hands, they put the chain on and—*

Maren made a sound like tearing cloth and gripped the table's edge.

*Tell her it wasn't Osk Brade. Tell her it wasn't anyone from the Rest. It was the toll-man's brother, the one who keeps the ledgers at Greyford, because Joren wouldn't carry for him anymore, wouldn't move what was in the boxes, and he said, he said—*

The voice thickened, watered, sank:

*He said the Crown buys silence cheaper than iron. Mam, tell her I didn't call out. I want her to know I didn't call for help because I didn't want them coming back for her. I was quiet, Mam. I was quiet on purpose—*

And then, fainter, going down through the cold water of eleven years, almost amused, almost tender:

*Tell her the trade was worth it. Not the boxes. The people. Tell her—*

Gone. The spiral stood open and empty, a finished doorway with no one in it, and the flame stood upright again, and the only sound was Maren Ostwick breathing like a woman surfacing from deep water.

For a long time neither of them moved.

"The toll-man's brother," Maren said at last, hoarse. "At Greyford. I've broken bread with that family." She looked up, and her eyes were dry now, scoured clean, terrible and calm. "Eleven years I fed my hatred to the wrong names. And he was *quiet*, the fool, the beautiful fool, he went down quiet to spare me—" She pressed both hands over her mouth, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and then she mastered it, the way bone-readers master everything, folding grief away like a tool into oiled cloth.

She rose. She went to the chest by the wall and began setting out goods: a punt-key, a pouch of silver, a written token for her cousin at the black channel. And when she spoke again her voice was level, but she did not look at Elodie while she said it.

"You could have refused. I'd have given passage anyway — eventually. But you didn't refuse, and you didn't flinch, and you told it whole, even the part about Greyford, which puts your friend the smuggler in danger and would have been easy to leave out." She set the last item down — a small carved bird-skull, fen-wren, worked with a tiny listening spiral. "Take this. Tap it twice against any bone and it will tell you if the bone is lying. Old trick. You'll want it, going where you're going." She finally turned. "One more thing, Voss, freely given, price of nothing. Nine nights ago, when every piece on my shelves sang — the last thing they sang, before the humming faded, was a warning. Same words, over and over, in the elder tongue. My grandmother wrote the sounds down once, from her relic. I've matched them." She held Elodie's gaze. "It means: *the seal remembers who broke it. Bring the key home, or the mountain opens wrong.*"

The wind moved through the pilings, and somewhere out over the fens, distant and unmistakable, a dog began barking — and did not stop — and down the lane, hooves.
