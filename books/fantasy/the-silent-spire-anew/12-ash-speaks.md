## 12. Ash Speaks

The bone-reader's village fell behind them by midmorning, and Elodie could not stop washing her hands.

There was no blood on them. She had checked a dozen times. But she could still feel the texture of the murdered boy's knuckle — the way it had sat in her palm like a river stone, warm from the mother's clutching, cold from the six years since he'd drowned in the millrace. She could still hear his last words, delivered in her own voice because that was how her gift worked: *it was an accident, Mama. I slipped. Don't blame the miller's boy.* Six years of a mother's guilt, dissolved or confirmed by a stranger's carved bone and a coin paid grudgingly.

Elodie had taken the coin. That was what gnawed at her. The passage through the marsh road had been offered freely — freely in exchange for a service — and she had hesitated before accepting, and the hesitation had lasted only as long as it took her to look at Ash, thin and silent in the doorway of the bone-reader's hut, watching with those dark eyes that understood everything and said nothing.

She hated herself for hesitating. She hated herself more for not hesitating longer.

"Almost there," Renn said from ahead, where he walked with the easy rolling gait of a man who had never once doubted his right to occupy space. "Marsh ford's half a day if we push. There's a drover's hut on the far side — dry roof, old hearth. We can rest proper."

Ash trotted beside him, matching his long strides with quick small steps, and something in the sight made Elodie's chest ache in a way she refused to name. The child had taken to Renn with a speed that bordered on betrayal. With Elodie there was always that watchfulness, that careful distance, as though Ash were cataloguing her the way Elodie catalogued bones: structure, age, fracture lines.

*You're doing the same thing,* she admitted, trudging through the bracken. *You're both waiting for the other one to leave first.*

The land changed around them as they walked. Thornfell did not soften so much as it shifted its manner of hostility — the high crags gave way to black pine forest, then to fen country where the trees stood knee-deep in brown water and the air smelled of rot and iron. Crows followed them for a mile, then lost interest. Somewhere distant, a marsh-bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

It was Ash who stopped first.

The child went rigid mid-stride, one foot raised over a puddle that mirrored the grey sky, and turned their head slowly toward the north — back the way they'd come, toward the high passes they'd crossed two days past. Toward, though Elodie couldn't have said how she knew it, the direction of the Silent Spire, invisible behind sixty miles of mountain.

"Ash?" Renn said. He caught the child's shoulder. "What is it? Riders?"

Ash shook their head. But they didn't move. Their lips were moving — that soundless shaping Elodie had seen before, the ghost of speech, the habit of someone whose voice had been taken or surrendered long ago. And beneath their feet, beneath all their feet, Elodie felt it begin.

*The hum.*

She had felt it once before, high in the pass, when the mountain itself had seemed to vibrate with a language older than stone. Then it had been distant, a fever in the bedrock, and Ash had sung it quiet with that wordless melody that made her finished carvings whisper. Now it rose through the fen like water finding a crack in a cellar floor — low, insistent, threaded through with something she had never heard in it before.

Urgency. The dead here weren't murmuring. They were *calling.*

"No," Elodie breathed. Her hands went to the satchel where her carvings rode against her hip — three pieces now, the finger-bone she'd carved in another life, the jawbone Ash had woken, and the small whorl of vertebra she'd bought from the bone-reader along with her silence. All three were warm. All three were *singing*, faint as a struck glass, harmonics she could feel in her molars. "No, no — not again, not here—"

The voices came.

They always came the same way: not through her ears but through her hands, through the bones she'd shaped, rising up her wrists and into the base of her skull where thought became language. Normally they arrived one at a time, polite as petitioners, each dead soul granted its moment at her workbench. This was different. This was a crowd pressing against a door, dozens of voices layered over hundreds, and underneath them all the great slow pulse of the fen itself, the accumulated dead of a thousand drowned years, all of them turning toward her — toward *Ash* — with the focus of a lighthouse beam.

She went down to one knee in the wet moss. The world tilted. Somewhere far away Renn was shouting her name, and Ash had dropped beside her, small hands hovering over her shoulders without touching, and the voices were saying—

Saying too much. Saying everything at once. Fragments broke through like ice floes grinding:

*—the seal is thinning, we feel it thinning—*

*—the chosen one walks above us, the chosen one walks ABOVE—*

*—ask it, ask it whether—*

*—six hundred years and no one comes, no one comes, no one—*

*—WAKE US—*

That last voice was louder than the others, and older, and it did not come from the fen. It came from far away and deep down, from beneath sixty miles of granite, and when it spoke every other voice flinched into silence, the way birds go quiet when the hawk's shadow crosses the field.

Elodie clawed her way back to herself. Her nose was bleeding; she could feel it cooling on her lip. Renn had her under the arms, hauling her upright, his face pale under its weathering. "What happened? What did you *do*?"

"Not me." She wiped her lip with the back of her wrist. Her hand wouldn't stop shaking. "Ash."

The child stood three feet away, absolutely still, and Elodie saw now that Ash was crying without any of the mechanics of crying — no sobbing, no gasping, just tears sliding down that still face as though the body had forgotten grief required noise.

And then Ash spoke.

Not the old tongue. Not the humming, not the melody, not the soundless mouthings. Words — broken, accented, assembled like a child stacking stones into a wall that might hold or might collapse — but words in the common speech of Thornfell, words Elodie understood, and they landed on her one at a time like stones dropped into still water.

"They are not dead."

Renn made a sound low in his throat.

Ash's hands moved as they spoke, small gestures that seemed to be translating the old tongue's grammar into shape, as though speech alone couldn't carry the weight. "They are *waiting*. Under the Spire. Under the mountain. Under—" the child pressed both palms flat against the earth, hard, as if holding down something that struggled, "—under everything. Sleeping. Breathing. Counting."

"Counting what?" Elodie whispered.

"Days." Ash's chin trembled. "Six hundred years of days. They count them so they don't forget they are alive."

The fen had gone utterly silent. No marsh-birds. No wind. Even the water seemed to have stopped moving. Elodie had the mad, lucid impression that the entire landscape was leaning in to listen.

"I was sent," Ash said, and here the child's voice cracked clean in half, the composure shattering, and suddenly Ash wasn't a vessel of ancient portent but exactly what they were — a child, small and terrified, standing in a swamp at the edge of the known world. "I was sent to decide."

"Decide what?" Renn asked. His voice had gone very quiet, which from Renn Carrow was more alarming than shouting. "Decide *what*, little bird?"

Ash looked at him. Looked at Elodie. And Elodie watched the child weigh the truth the way she herself weighed a bone before carving it — testing for the flaw that would split it whole.

"Whether they wake," Ash said. "Or sleep forever. Whether the door opens or stays shut. I carry the key. I carry the *choosing*." A breath that shuddered going in. "I was chosen because I am nobody. Because my family is ended. Because I cannot be bought." The tears were falling faster now. "But I was seven years old. I did not want to be the choosing. I ran. I ran across the mountains and the snow and I thought if I got far enough the choosing would fall off me like a coat."

"And instead you knocked on my door," Elodie said.

Ash nodded once.

* *

They made the drover's hut before dusk, mostly because standing still in the open fen felt like standing on a stage. The hut was exactly as promised — dry-roofed, soot-blackened hearth, a floor of packed earth trodden smooth by a century of cattle — and Renn got a fire going while Elodie sat against the wall with Ash curled against her side, and discovered that she had put her arm around the child without deciding to.

The dead had withdrawn. Not gone — she could still feel them the way you feel weather through a window — but quieter, patient, as though having finally spoken their case through Ash's mouth they were content to wait for the verdict. It was the patience that frightened her most. Dead things should not be patient. Patience belonged to the living.

"So let me see if I have this straight." Renn crouched by the fire, feeding it peat with the exaggerated calm of a man handling something explosive. "There's a whole people asleep under the Silent Spire. Alive. Six hundred years. Sealed in by — someone — for reasons I'm guessing weren't charitable. And this entire time, the Crown's Wardens haven't been guarding a tomb."

"They've been guarding a prison," Elodie said.

"And the keys to the cell are walking around in a nine-year-old."

"Ten," said Ash.

Renn closed his eyes briefly. "Ten. Forgive me. Ten-year-old." He opened them again and looked at Elodie across the fire, and what she saw in his face stripped away the last of her composure: he wasn't surprised. Not entirely. Some part of Renn Carrow had suspected this from the beginning — had perhaps *known* — and had been carrying that knowledge the way he carried everything else, behind the charm, in the dark.

"You owe them something," she said quietly. "Ash's people. You said it on the road. What did you mean?"

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. He turned his hands toward the fire, and she noticed for the first time the burn scarring across his left palm, old and glossy, the kind of mark a man stops explaining because the explanations stopped being believed.

"My grandfather ran cargo out of Greyford," he said at last. "Grain, mostly. Salt. Sometimes things the Crown didn't tax because the Crown didn't know about them." A pause. "One winter — this would be forty years back — a family came down out of the high passes. Wrong clothes. Wrong speech. Half-frozen. The kind of folk that make honest smugglers nervous, because anything the Wardens want that badly is trouble, and my grandfather could read a Warden's mood like weather."

"What happened to them?"

"He turned them away." Renn's voice was flat. "Turned them away from his own hearth, in his own house, in a blizzard. Told himself it was prudence. Told himself they'd find shelter lower down." He fed another block of peat to the fire, harder than necessary. "They didn't. Warden patrol found them in a drift two days later. And the Wardens didn't just bury them, Elodie. They burned the bodies and salted the ground and told everyone the family had been plague-carriers, and my grandfather — who knew better, who *knew* — kept his mouth shut for the next twenty years until it ate him alive. Died coughing blood and apologizing to a room full of empty chairs."

The fire popped. Ash had gone very still against Elodie's side.

"So when a mute child shows up speaking the same wrong speech," Renn finished, "and asks me — well. Doesn't ask. Just *looks* at me. I figure the Carrow family's debt has come due."

"That's why you agreed so fast."

"That's why I agreed before you finished asking." He managed half a smile, but it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Guilt makes excellent ballast. Keeps a man steady in rough water. Doesn't mean he enjoys the voyage."

* *

Later, when Renn had taken the first watch and the fire had burned down to a red heart, Ash stirred against her and whispered, in that broken careful common speech:

"You heard them. In the marsh. What they said."

"Some." Elodie kept her voice equally low. "Fragments. My gift translates the old tongue badly. Like reading through frosted glass."

"What did you hear?" And there it was — the question beneath the question, the child asking not what the dead had said but what *they had said about Ash*, and Elodie understood suddenly that this was the thing Ash feared most in the world: not the Wardens, not the Spire, not the choosing itself, but the judgment of the ones who had sent them.

Elodie considered lying. She was good at lying; she'd lied to a Warden-Sergeant's face and slept fine afterward. But lying to Ash felt like carving a false inscription on real bone — a desecration of the material.

"I heard them say the seal is thinning," she said. "I heard them say you walk above them. I heard..." She paused. "Some of them want to wake, Ash. Badly. Six hundred years is a long time to want anything."

"And the others?"

"Others were afraid. Others wanted to ask you something and couldn't finish the sentence before the loud ones drowned them out." She looked down at the small face, firelit, shadowed. "What happens if you decide wrong?"

Ash was quiet for so long Elodie thought the child had fallen asleep. When the answer came it was barely a breath.

"If I wake them, maybe there is war. Maybe they are angry. Six hundred years angry." A swallow. "If I do not wake them, they sleep until the seal breaks on its own, and then there is war anyway, but no one will have asked them, and no one will have warned anyone, and it will be worse." Small fingers found the fabric of Elodie's sleeve and gripped it. "Either way, it is my fault. That is what 'chosen' means. It means your fault arrives before you do."

Something in Elodie's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there. She thought of her own life — the workshop, the careful neutrality, the decades spent keeping every door locked so that no one's weight could ever come to rest against hers. She had built that life brick by brick precisely to avoid this: to be the person no one chose, no one needed, no one blamed. And this child had been handed the burden Elodie had spent fifty years dodging, at seven years old, by people who loved them enough to be cruel.

"It shouldn't be yours alone," she heard herself say. "Whatever you decide. It shouldn't sit on you alone."

"The dead do not ask permission of the living."

"Well," said Elodie Voss, bone-carver, liar, coward, "the living are allowed to volunteer."

Ash looked up at her. In the firelight the child's eyes held something Elodie hadn't seen there before — not trust, not yet, but the place where trust might someday be built, cleared ground, foundations marked.

Outside the hut, in the black fen, a crow called once and was answered by nothing at all.

Then, from very far away — so far it might have been memory, might have been imagination, might have been the wind moving through sixty miles of stone — Elodie heard the mountain hum again. One note. Low and clear and unmistakably expectant.

And on the southern horizon, faint as breath on glass, a line of torchlight was moving along the ridgeline where no honest travelers went by night.

Renn appeared in the doorway, all levity gone. "We've got riders. A lot of them. Flying Warden colors." He looked at Ash, then at her, and his jaw set. "Corvane's found our trail. We need to be gone before dawn — and Elodie? Wherever we run next..."

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. The Silent Spire lay ahead of them now, and behind them came the law, and between the two stood a ten-year-old carrying an empire's oldest sin in a body barely big enough to hold it.
