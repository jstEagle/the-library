## 13. The Truce of Ash and Iron

Renn Carrow had made a hundred fires in a hundred bad places, and he knew them the way other men knew their mothers' faces: by smell, by temper, by what they asked of him. This one was a good fire — dry larch, gathered from the leeward side of the pass where the wind couldn't strip it, built small and deep in a hollow of stone so its light wouldn't leak down into the valley below. A smuggler's fire. The kind you built when you didn't want to be seen, or when you wanted to see something else entirely.

He crouched at its edge and fed it a stick and watched Elodie across the flames. She sat with her back to a boulder, her hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea she hadn't drunk, and Ash was curled against her side like something that had learned, recently and hard, that warmth could be taken away. The child's eyes were open, watching the fire the way Renn watched Wardens — cataloguing, measuring, remembering.

Three days out of the valley. Two days ahead of the pursuit he was certain they had, though Elodie still believed they'd slipped it clean. He hadn't told her about the smoke he'd seen behind them on the second morning, thin and disciplined, moving the way Crown men moved. There'd be time for fear later. Tonight there was this hollow in the mountain's shoulder, and the fire, and the question he could see building behind Elodie's eyes like weather.

She'd been patient, he'd give her that. Three days of road, and she'd only asked him twice — once with words, once with that look she had, the one that went through a man like a gimlet through green wood. *Why are you doing this? Why does a smuggler who owes nobody anything walk into Thornfell with a bone-carver and a mute child, toward the one place in the world the Crown kills people for going?*

He owed nobody anything. That was the story he told, anyway, and he'd told it so long it had calluses.

But Ash had sung last night, low, half-asleep against Elodie's shoulder — a thread of melody in that old tongue that wasn't a language so much as a place, a country you entered rather than spoke — and Renn had sat up all night staring at the stars and seeing instead a different fire, twenty years smaller and twenty years colder, and a woman's voice saying words he'd spent two decades pretending he hadn't heard.

"You're going to tell me," Elodie said now. Not asking. She turned the cup in her hands. "Whatever it is you're not telling me. I've watched you look at Ash for three days like the child is a debt come due."

Ash stirred against her side. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound almost like breathing.

"Your tea's getting cold," Renn said.

"Renn."

He sighed, and took off his cap, and turned it in his hands the way she turned her cup, because some things needed holding while you said them.

"All right." He looked into the fire. It was easier than looking at either of them. "You know how every kingdom has a story about how it began? Some founding hero, some righteous war, first king stepping out of the mist with a sword and God's blessing? What they teach children in Thornfell is that King Aldric the First united the warring clans and built the first crown from the iron of his enemies' broken weapons, and that peace came after, like morning comes after night. Clean. Inevitable. The way stories go when the winners write them down."

Elodie waited. Ash's eyes had gone very still.

"That's not how it happened," Renn said. "Or it is, but it's not the whole shape of it. The wars weren't clan against clan. Not at the end. At the end there was one enemy left, and it wasn't a clan. It was a people." He nodded at Ash without meaning to, an involuntary motion, apology folded into gesture. "The Spire-folk."

The word dropped into the hollow like a stone into a well. Even the fire seemed quieter.

"They weren't conquerors," he went on. "That's the part nobody tells you. They were old — older than any of the clans, older than the idea of a kingdom. They lived in the high places, in towers they grew rather than built, and they kept a kind of knowledge that the clans called sorcery and they called something else — I don't know what, the word doesn't come down to us. But here's what matters. They could hear the dead. All of them, not just the odd gift-born one in a generation. Every child born under the Spire could sit down at a grave and have a conversation. And the dead—" he paused, choosing— "the dead remember everything. Every wrong ever done. Every debt. Every name of every person anyone had ever wronged and forgotten wronging. You can't build a kingdom on forgetting when there's a whole people whose whole art is *remembering*."

"So they were killed for it," Elodie said quietly. Her hands had gone still around the cup.

"Worse." Renn fed another stick to the fire and watched it catch. "The clans couldn't beat them. Twenty years of trying, and the Spire-folk just — withdrew. Upward. Into their tower. They didn't fight back, not really. And that was worse, in a way, because a man can hate an enemy who fights him, but an enemy who won't fight, who just looks at you like you're a grief waiting to happen — that eats a man alive. So Aldric did what kings do. He found the one thing worse than war." 

He let it sit. The wind breathed through the pines.

"The clans besieged the tower," he said. "Not to take it. To close it. There's a door at the Spire's heart — carved from a single vertebra of the mountain itself, if even half the stories are true — and Aldric's masons sealed it with the Spire-folk inside. Alive. Men, women, children. Thousands of them, singing as the stones closed over them, because singing was all they had left. And the seal didn't kill them, see. That was the horror of it. The seal *kept* them. Some working or other, some bargain cut in desperation — the sleepers wouldn't die. Wouldn't wake, either. Just... held. Suspended between one breath and the next, for six hundred years."

Elodie's face had gone white in the firelight. Beside her, Ash had drawn up their knees, arms locked around them, and was looking into the flames with an expression Renn couldn't read and didn't dare try to.

"And the Crown's first act," Renn said, "the very first law Aldric signed as king of a united realm, before roads, before coin, before courts — was the Warden Charter. An order sworn to maintain the seal. To make sure no Spire-blood survived outside the tower. No language, no song, no memory. Because if any of it got out — if any voice of theirs returned to that door—" He stopped.

"Then what?" Elodie asked. Her voice was very steady, which meant she already knew, or guessed, or both.

"Then the seal opens," Renn said. "And six hundred years of sleeping dead wake up in a world that buried them alive, with every grievance intact. That's why the Wardens exist, Elodie. Not to protect the realm from some ancient evil. To protect the realm from its own founding crime. Every road, every law, every coin with Aldric's face on it sits on top of that door. And everyone agreed — the clans, the Church, the guilds, everyone — to stop looking at it. Six hundred years of not looking. You can build quite a lot on ground you refuse to examine."

The fire cracked and settled. For a long moment nobody spoke.

"My grandmother sang songs," Elodie said finally, so quietly it was nearly lost in the wind. "Lullabies. She said they came down from her grandmother, and no one knew where they started. I always thought they were just... mountain songs." She looked down at Ash. "They weren't, were they?"

"Probably not," Renn said gently. "Old tongue gets everywhere, like water. Seeps down through the cracks. Half the folk songs in Thornfell are probably fragments of it. Nobody remembers anymore. That's rather the point."

Ash moved. It was a small motion — a hand lifting from their knees, reaching toward the firelight — but it stopped both of them mid-breath. The child turned their palm upward, fingers slightly curled, and looked at Renn with those old, unchildlike eyes, and then slowly closed the fist, and drew it in against their chest.

Renn felt the gesture land somewhere beneath his ribs like a swallowed coal.

"I know," he said softly, uselessly. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry we're the ones telling you what you already know better than we do."

Ash looked at him a moment longer. Then they leaned back against Elodie's shoulder, and closed their eyes, and did not open them again.

* * *

Later — the fire burned down to a red heart, Elodie asleep sitting up with her chin on her chest, Ash breathing slow and even beside her — Renn stayed awake and watched the dark beyond the stones.

He'd told them the clean version. The version with edges, the story-shaped history, beginning and end. He hadn't told them about the woman.

Her name had been Sera. He'd been nineteen, running his first solo route through the high passes, carrying nothing worth hanging for and lying about it anyway, and he'd found her in a snowed-in waystation with a broken leg and no food and the particular calm of someone who has decided not to be afraid. She'd been old — sixty, seventy, hard to say — with white hair and hands like knotted rope, and she'd spoken the trade-tongue well enough, but underneath it, when she hummed to herself while he splinted her leg, there was another music. Old. Old the way bedrock is old.

She'd stayed three days until the snow cleared. He'd brought her food. She'd taught him four words of her language, laughing at his butchered pronunciation, and told him — plainly, without drama, the way you'd report the weather — that she was the last of a line that had kept itself hidden since the sealing, scattered families, thinned blood, hiding in plain sight among the valleys for twenty generations. That the Wardens hunted them still, quietly, patiently, the way you hunt a fire that smolders under the floorboards. That her own son had been taken two winters past, and she was walking to Thornfell's edge to see, one last time, the mountains where her people's tower stood.

"Why?" he'd asked her. Stupidly. Nineteen years old. "Why go back? If they'll kill you for it."

She'd looked at him with such kindness that he'd wanted to cry.

"Because someone should stand near it," she'd said. "Someone living. Someone who remembers we were never the monsters. The dead can't testify if no one will walk to the courthouse."

He'd carried her as far as the lower passes, and left her at a shrine with food and a week's wood, and she'd pressed her forehead to his — a blessing, he understood later, the kind her people gave — and said four words in her own tongue that he never forgot and never learned to translate, though he'd asked scholars, drunk priests, one very expensive linguist in Greyford. Four words. He'd hummed the melody of them to himself for twenty years like a man worrying a loose tooth.

Two years later, drinking in a river-town, he'd heard a Warden sergeant describe — to another officer, not knowing who listened — the capture and quiet execution of an old woman found walking toward the Spire. No trial. No record. *Charter business.*

He'd done nothing. He'd finished his drink. He'd walked out into the evening and been sick in an alley, and then he'd gotten up the next morning and run his cargo and told himself there was nothing he could have done, and told it to himself so many times over twenty years that the telling wore smooth as river stone, and it was a lie, and he had always known it was a lie.

And then a bone-carver's letter had reached him through three intermediaries — a favor owed, a debt collected, the usual currency — saying: *I have a child who sings the old tongue, and the Wardens are coming, and I am going to the Spire.* 

And Renn Carrow, who owed nobody anything, had packed his gear in under an hour.

He glanced across the dying fire. Elodie slept. Ash slept. Above the hollow, through a seam in the clouds, the peaks of central Thornfell showed white under starlight — and somewhere among them, higher than any peak, the Silent Spire stood in the dark with its thousands of sleepers, holding its single breath, waiting.

*You owe them,* the woman's voice said in his memory, gentle as snowfall. *You owe them the walk.*

"Aye," he murmured to the fire. "I know."

He banked the coals, checked the perimeter out of habit more than hope, and settled back against the stone with his knife loose in his hand. Tomorrow they'd cross into the high valleys, where the maps went vague and the Wardens went hungry. Whatever came next would come fast.

He just wished — watching the child breathe, watching the carver dream — that the road ahead didn't feel so much like a debt coming due.

Above them, faint as a thought, the mountain seemed to hum.
