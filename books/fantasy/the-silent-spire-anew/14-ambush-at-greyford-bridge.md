## 14. Ambush at Greyford Bridge

The bridge announced itself before they saw it, the way all old things do — by sound. Greyford Bridge had stood over the Sere for four hundred years, and its stones hummed under cartwheels and hooves with a note like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Renn Carrow heard it from half a mile off and felt his stomach drop through the soles of his boots.

"Wrong," he said.

Elodie, walking beside him with Ash's small hand tucked in hers, glanced up. The morning was thin and grey, mist still clinging to the river valley below them like wool not yet carded. "It's a bridge. They're supposed to be loud."

"It's *too* quiet." He stopped on the track and turned a slow circle, reading the landscape the way other men read letters. The alder thickets to their left. The scree slope above. The long, open approach to the span where nothing taller than gorse grew. "No birdsong. No woodcutters. No carts waiting at the toll post." He nodded toward the little stone hut by the bridge's far end, where the toll-keeper was supposed to sit from dawn till dark collecting two coppers a wheel and complaining about it to anyone who'd listen. Its door hung shut. Its chimney was cold. "And no toll-keeper."

"He could be ill."

"Toll-keepers aren't ill, Elodie. Toll-keepers are the most durable form of life in Thornfell. They survive winters that kill shepherds." He spat, thinking. "Someone paid him to be elsewhere this morning, or he looked out his window at first light, saw what was coming down the north road, and decided his coppers weren't worth dying for. Either way—"

He didn't finish, because Ash made a sound.

It wasn't a word. It was the low, resonant hum they'd been hearing more often these past days, the one that rose out of the child without warning, that made the carved bone at Elodie's throat stir against her skin like something waking. But this time the hum had an edge to it. A warning in it. Ash stood very still, face tilted up toward the bridge, eyes wide and dark, and pointed one small finger at the scree slope above the far end of the span.

Renn followed the gesture and saw nothing. That was the trouble with scree slopes; they were made of nothing, ten thousand loose stones lying in patient ambush, and any one of them could be a helmet.

"How many?" Elodie asked quietly. She'd learned to ask questions like that now, as if asking the mountain itself.

Ash held up fingers. Four. Then, after a pause, folded two down and raised three more elsewhere — a hand toward the alder thicket, another toward the ruined shepherd's cot upstream.

"Seven," Renn translated grimly. "Maybe eight. Split positions covering the whole crossing." He laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "Corvane. Has to be. This is his work — he doesn't scatter men like bandits, he builds a box around you and then closes the lid politely."

Elodie's face had gone pale but steady. "Can we go around? Ford the river?"

"Not here. The Sere runs fast and deep below the bridge — snowmelt. A grown swimmer would last a minute. A child—" He shook his head. "Downstream there's an old ferry crossing at Marlow's Stair, six miles. If we can get past the thicket unseen, we might reach it by dark."

"And if we're seen?"

"Then we're dead on this road, and the only question is whether we die bored or not." He grinned at her, that quick crooked grin that never quite reached his eyes anymore, and began unbuckling the straps of his pack. "I'm going to give you both a head start."

"No." Elodie said it flatly, immediately, the way you refuse a bad price at market.

"Elodie—"

"You are not staying behind to hold seven Wardens with a knife and that ridiculous crossbow."

"I'm not holding them." He slung the pack off his shoulder and set it down among the gorse, already pulling out the coil of oiled rope he kept for exactly this kind of morning. "I'm *delaying* them. There's a difference, and the difference is about four minutes, which is all I need." He looked up at her, and for once the charm fell away entirely, and what was underneath was older and wearier and much more honest. "Listen to me. You can't outrun them carrying her. You know it and I know it. But they don't want you, Elodie — Corvane wants the child, and maybe your head as an afterthought. If I'm here making noise, being difficult, being *me*, every eye stays on me. Every boot chases me. And I'm good at being chased. It's practically my profession."

Ash tugged at Elodie's sleeve. When she knelt, the child pressed something into her palm: a small carving, bone-white, no bigger than a thumb. Elodie recognized her own work — one of the practice pieces from the early weeks, a rough figure of a woman with her arms open wide. Ash had been carrying it hidden for who knew how long. The child closed Elodie's fingers around it and held them shut, and hummed three notes, soft and certain, and though Elodie couldn't have translated them if her life depended on it, she understood anyway.

*Come back.*

"We'll both come back," Elodie said, and hated how her voice shook. "Both of us. You hear me, Renn Carrow? You don't get to die heroically on a bridge and leave me explaining to her why."

"Wouldn't dream of it. Dying heroically is for people with better boots." He checked his crossbow, kissed his own knuckles like a gambler greeting a lucky coin, and gave Ash a wink. "Keep her off the high ground, little one. She argues with mountains."

Then he was moving, low and fast along the gorse line toward the bridge, and Elodie gathered Ash against her side and turned downstream, into the alder thicket, and did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people with time.

* * *

Warden-Sergeant Dain Ferro was nineteen years old and had been a Warden for eleven months, and until this morning the worst thing he had ever done was confiscate a peddler's unlicensed charms and listen to the man cry.

He lay in the scree above the bridge's far end with his belly in the stones and his orders folded in his breast pocket, and he read them again for the fourth time, because reading them again might change them.

*Subject: child, approx. 8–10 years, mute, suspected carrier of proscribed language. Take alive if possible. If capture risks exposure of Spire matters, terminate. Signed, Warden-Captain Vess, by authority of the Crown.*

*Terminate.* The word sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. He'd joined the Wardens because his father had been a Warden, because the green cloak meant something in the border valleys, because it meant order, meant protection, meant standing between the scattered farms and the things in the dark. Nobody had ever mentioned that the things in the dark might be children.

"Hold positions," Sergeant Maren Breck said beside him, her voice barely a breath. She was twenty years his senior, grey at the temples, and she watched the bridge approach with the flat patience of a hawk. "Wait for the signal. We take the smuggler on the span — narrowest point, no room to run. The others will flush the woman and child from the trees."

Dain nodded and put his eye to the gap between two stones, and that was when he saw the smuggler walk out onto the bridge.

Renn Carrow came strolling across Greyford Bridge like a man late for his own wedding, hands loose at his sides, whistling. Actually whistling — some bawdy river song about a miller's wife — loud enough to carry across the water. He stopped dead center on the span, directly over the deepest part of the Sere, and cupped his hands around his mouth.

"Good morning!" he called cheerfully, to the scree, to the trees, to the empty toll-house. "Lovely day for an ambush! I'd have brought pastries if I'd known!"

Breck exhaled slowly through her nose. "Carrow," she muttered. "Of course it's Carrow."

"You know him?"

"Everyone knows him. He's been a thorn in the Crown's side since before you were shaving." Her hand lifted, two fingers extended. "On my mark. Take him alive if he's sensible. He usually isn't."

But Carrow wasn't waiting to be taken. As the first three Wardens rose from cover near the toll-house and started onto the bridge — moving fast, cloaks snapping, boots hammering that old humming stone — Carrow did something Dain took a full second to understand, and then wished he hadn't.

He cut the rope.

The rope had been rigged — Dain realized only now, only too late — along the bridge's upstream rail, anchored to the old toll-post and running the length of the span, and tied into it, every few yards, were bundles. Brush bundles. Tar-soaked sacking. The dry summer stores of the toll-keeper's own firewood pile, dragged out and lashed on in the night, or the hour before dawn, while seven Wardens lay in the wet scree watching an empty road.

Carrow touched flame to the nearest bundle and the whole line went up with a *whump* like the mountain clearing its throat, and suddenly Greyford Bridge was a wall of smoke and fire between the Wardens and everything else in the world.

"Now!" Breck was up and running, and Dain ran too, coughing, eyes streaming, the world reduced to orange glare and black rolling smoke and the drum of his own heart. The bridge wouldn't burn — it was stone, four hundred years old, it had burned a dozen times and shrugged it off — but the smoke was a country unto itself, and somewhere in it a smuggler was laughing.

Dain burst out the downstream side of the smoke wall onto the clear span and there was Carrow, backing away along the rail, crossbow leveled, and beyond him — movement in the alders below, downstream, a flash of a grey cloak, a small figure carried —

"There!" Dain shouted, pointing. "The trees! They're in the trees!"

Carrow fired. The bolt meant for Breck's shoulder smashed instead into the rail an inch from her hand, and she ducked, swore, and Dain did the arithmetic every soldier does in the space between one heartbeat and the next: the smuggler was alone, reloading, outnumbered five to one on a burning bridge with nowhere to go.

Except behind him.

Because Dain, lying in the scree all morning, had also seen what the sergeant hadn't: the downstream end of the bridge had its own path, a goat-track down to the shingle banks, and the woman and child weren't fleeing along the river — they were *crossing* it, somehow, on the stepping-stones below the falls, the ones nobody used since the flood two winters back. They were coming up the bank on the far side. Toward the bridge. Toward the only route left.

Toward him.

They came out of the alders thirty feet below his position — the woman first, mud to her knees, blood on her forehead where a branch had caught her, and behind her the child, small and dark-eyed, stumbling and catching herself, and Dain was already sliding down the scree with his sword drawn and his orders crinkling in his pocket like a living thing, and he was faster, he was downhill and they were exhausted, and he cut the angle and stepped out onto the path in front of them with his blade up and his voice doing what it was trained to do:

"In the name of the Crown, stand where you are!"

The woman stopped. Put her body between his sword and the child, which was stupid, which was hopeless, which was the bravest thing he'd ever seen anyone do. "Please," she said. "She's just a child. Whatever you think she is—"

"She's a proscribed voice," Dain said, and hated the words even as his mouth made them. "I have orders."

And then the child looked at him.

Not the way people look at a drawn sword. Not fear, exactly — or not only fear. Ash looked at him the way you look at a door you remember from a house you lived in before a war. The child's head tilted. And then Ash hummed.

Three notes. Low, and sad, and old — old the way bedrock is old, the way winter is old — and they went into Dain Ferro through his ears and his chest and the soles of his feet in the stones, and for one impossible moment he was not nineteen years old on a riverbank in Thornfell. He was standing in a tower full of sleeping people, and they were singing to each other in the dark, and someone had sealed the door, and the singing had gone on and on and on, growing fainter, growing fainter, until—

Until it stopped.

Dain's sword arm dropped. Just — dropped, the point sagging toward the mud, his whole body forgetting its training at once. His eyes were wet and he didn't know why. "What," he whispered, "what *was* that—"

"Dain!" Breck's voice, from the bridge above, raw with smoke. "DAIN! Do you have them?"

He had them. He had a woman shielding a child with her own body, and a child looking at him with ancient, ancient eyes, and a sword in his hand, and orders in his pocket, and every single thing he had believed about the green cloak he wore was on fire along with the bridge behind him.

The moment lasted the length of one breath.

Then Carrow's bolt took him in the side.

Dain didn't even hear the shot. He was simply knocked sideways, the world tilting, the mud coming up gentle and cold to meet him, and the last thing he saw — craning his neck, because he needed to know, needed it more than air — was the child turning back, turning *toward* him, one small hand reaching out against the woman's grip, reaching for him, mouthing something in a language with no words he knew and every meaning he'd ever lost.

Then the smoke from the bridge rolled down the bank and took them all.

* * *

Renn found them at the stepping-stones, white-faced and shaking, Elodie pressing her torn sleeve against a wound on Ash's palm where a stone had cut her. Behind them, upstream, the bridge still smoked and Wardens' voices shouted commands at each other across the water.

"He's down," Elodie said, before Renn could ask. Her voice was hollow. "The young one. Your bolt. He was just — he was a boy, Renn. He lowered his sword. She *showed* him something, and he lowered his sword, and then you—"

"I saved your lives." Renn was breathing hard, a graze along his jaw bleeding freely where a Warden's blade had found him in the smoke. He didn't look at the shingle bank behind them, where a shape in a green cloak lay very still, half in the water. "That's what I did. I'm not going to pretend it was anything prettier."

"He was faltering."

"Faltering men still arrest children. Faltering men still follow orders tomorrow, and the day after, and every day until someone puts them in front of a child again." He took Elodie's shoulder, gently, and turned her away from the river. "Look at me. He knew what his cloak meant. I knew what my bolt meant. Nobody on that bridge this morning gets to be clean — least of all me. Now move. Breck won't stay stalled forever, and the ferry at Marlow's Stair leaves at dusk whether we're on it or not."

Ash pulled free of Elodie's grip for just a moment. Walked back, two steps, three, to the edge of the shingle where the young Warden lay. Knelt. Touched two fingers to the mud beside him — not touching him, never quite touching him — and hummed the three notes again, softer this time. A sending. A blessing, maybe. Or an apology.

Then the child rose, took Elodie's hand, and walked downstream without looking back, and none of them spoke for a mile.

Behind them, Greyford Bridge smoked against the sky like a candle guttering, and in the cold water of the Sere, the river carried what it always carries — patiently, indifferently, toward the sea.
