## 15. River of Small Mercies

The wound in Elodie's side had stopped announcing itself somewhere past the second bend of the Sere and had settled instead into a dull, patient argument — a low voice in her body that said *you are leaking* every time she shifted against the boat's wet boards. She had bound it with a strip of her own shirt and a fistful of river moss that Renn swore by and she did not believe in, and now she lay half-sunk in the bilge of a stolen skiff, watching the grey belly of the sky slide past between the overhanging alders.

Ash sat at the prow with their knees drawn up, humming.

Not the three notes from the bridge. Something else now — longer, winding, a melody that rose and fell like the river itself, and the river, Elodie noticed, was listening. Fish rose in slow spirals beneath the surface where the sound touched the water. A heron stood motionless on a deadfall as they drifted past it, not startled, only attentive, the way a man is attentive when an old friend is speaking softly in a room.

"You shouldn't do that," Renn muttered from the oars. He rowed without rhythm or urgency — there was no current to fight this far downriver, no one to outrun yet, and he seemed to be conserving something more than strength. "Sound carries on water. Every fisherman from here to Vell's Landing will know something's moving through his stretch."

Elodie started to answer and found that laughing hurt too much, so she settled for a dry breath. "Renn Carrow, worried about being noticed."

"I'm worried about *you* being noticed." He didn't look at her when he said it. He kept his eyes on the water ahead, on the pale seam where river met sky, and his jaw worked once around words he decided not to spend. "There's a difference."

She let it go. The pain made her generous.

They had taken the skiff from a peat-cutter's mooring two miles below the bridge, along with a coil of rope, a crooked fishing spear, and half a loaf gone hard as brick. Renn had left a silver bit under a stone on the mooring post, which was either conscience or bookkeeping; with Renn it was impossible to tell, and Elodie suspected he kept the distinction deliberately blurred even from himself. The peat-cutter's dog had watched them go with the philosophical resignation of an animal that understood theft as weather.

Now the banks widened. The alders gave way to willows, the willows to reed-beds whispering with wind and hidden birds, and the smell of the water changed — less stone and cold, more mud and green and the faint sweet rot of things growing and dying together, which is the smell of any living place. Somewhere behind them, Greyford Bridge would be crawling with Wardens. Somewhere behind them, a young man lay in the mud with Ash's blessing pressed into the ground beside him, and Elodie found that she could not make herself feel what she supposed she ought to feel about that. Grief, maybe. Guilt. What came instead was a strange, floating clarity, the kind the dying report and the wounded borrow: everything at a distance, everything lit from within by something cold and clean.

*He saw your face,* she thought, watching Ash's small back at the prow, the child's shoulders moving gently with the song. *The Warden boy. He saw your face and he faltered, and for that he died.* She did not know if it was true — Renn's knife had been faster than anyone's mercy — but it lived in her now, lodged like a splinter: the idea that Ash's face was a thing men could die from seeing. That beauty and horror could share one small countenance.

"Here," Renn said suddenly, and shipped the oars.

Ahead, half-hidden behind a curtain of reeds, a landing of blackened stakes jutted into the current, and beyond it — Elodie blinked, because she had been looking straight at it and seen nothing — a village. Not a village built so much as *accumulated*: houses of woven reed and driftwood timber perched on stilts above the waterline, connected by walkways of lashed plank, roofs thatched with sedge so artlessly mounded that from ten paces away the whole settlement read as nothing but a thickening of the bank. Smoke rose thin from a dozen vents and vanished into the low cloud as though the air itself were complicit in hiding it.

"Reedfolk," Renn said. "Sere-gypsies, the tax collectors call them, when they call them anything. They pay no tolls, keep no ledgers, appear on no maps worth trusting." He glanced back at her, and for a moment the charm dropped off his face entirely and left something rawer underneath. "I've run cargo through here twice. They don't love me. But they don't love anyone, and they owe me nothing, which is its own kind of honesty." He hesitated. "You're bad hurt, Elodie. The moss isn't holding. We need hands older than mine, and I'd rather they be strangers' than Wardens'."

"And if they give us up?"

"They won't." He said it with a certainty she couldn't account for. "They've been given up themselves often enough to know what it costs. That's all I'll promise."

Ash turned at the prow. The child looked at the village, then at Elodie, and hummed a single questioning note — rising, unfinished. Asking.

"Yes," Elodie said, and was surprised to find she meant it as an answer to whatever the question truly was. "We'll try."

* * *

The Reedfolk received them the way the river receives a fallen branch — without alarm, without welcome, with a kind of total practical attention.

An old woman met them at the landing, squat and brown as a root, wearing a coat made of fish-skin that gleamed dully like armor. She looked at Renn and spat, elaborately, into the water. She looked at Elodie and her eyes went immediately, expertly, to the soaked bandage at her side. She looked at Ash last, and longest, and something moved across her seamed face that Elodie could not name — recognition, or its opposite, a memory of having once expected something that never came.

Then she spoke a rapid stream of river dialect that Renn translated in fragments — *"hurt... inside, not the walkway, the old woman's house... and you"—* jabbing a finger at Renn—*"can sleep with the nets, where the smell covers lesser smells."*

"She's charming," Renn said.

"She said you can sleep with the nets," Elodie told him, "where the smell will cover yours."

The old woman barked a laugh like a gull and led them up the swaying walkway.

Inside the house — one room, round-walled, smelling of woodsmoke and drying herbs and the deep mineral scent of river-bottom — Elodie was eased out of her ruined shirt by hands that were gentle in the way of people who have done this work since before they had names for it. The old woman's daughter appeared from somewhere, younger, silent, with quick dark eyes, and between them they peeled back the moss and the shirt-strip and examined the long shallow gouge the Warden's blade had opened below Elodie's ribs.

"Not deep," the daughter said — in slow, accented trade-tongue, better than her mother's. She pressed around the wound with competent fingers while Elodie breathed through her teeth. "But dirty. Bridge-water. Bridge-water kills." She looked up, and her gaze held Elodie's with a frankness city folk would have found rude. "You are running from the iron-coats."

It wasn't a question, and lying seemed suddenly, exhaustingly pointless. "Yes."

The daughter nodded as though this confirmed something unremarkable. "Two years ago, iron-coats burned the winter stores at Blackreed Landing. Said we were hiding smugglers. We were hiding smugglers." She shrugged, a whole philosophy in the gesture. "We hid them anyway after. It is the same river that floats everyone."

She went to work then — packing the wound with something bitter and green that made Elodie's vision swim, stitching with a bone needle fine as a heron's quill, her mother chanting low over the whole business in a language that was not quite prayer and not quite song. And Ash, who had been crouched in the corner watching with the absolute stillness the child could summon, began, very quietly, to hum.

The chant and the hum braided together. Elodie felt it happen in her stitched side first — a warmth spreading outward from the wound like sunlight finding a crack in a wall — and then in her ears, because the old woman's chant had shifted, subtly, to follow the child's melody, or perhaps the child had followed the chant; it was impossible to say which came first, and afterward Elodie would believe both. The language of the chant was river-dialect twisted into something older underneath, the way a folk-song keeps the bones of a hymn nobody remembers learning.

When it was done, the old woman sat back on her heels, wiped her hands on her fish-skin coat, and studied Ash for a long time.

"Old-blood," she said finally, in trade-tongue, as if tasting the word. Her eyes flicked to Elodie. "You know what you carry?"

"No," Elodie said honestly.

"Neither did we, once." The old woman said it lightly, but the lightness had weight under it, like ice over deep water. Then she smiled, gap-toothed and sudden, and the moment passed. "You eat now. Sleep after. River decides the rest."

* * *

They stayed three days.

On the first day Elodie slept fourteen hours in a hammock slung from the roof beams, rocked by the house's own slight sway, and woke to find Ash curled at the hammock's foot like a cat who had decided the arrangement was permanent. On the second day she hobbled out onto the walkway and watched the life of the village unfold, and understood slowly that she was watching something she had never actually seen before, though she had lived forty years within sight of a mountain.

The Reedfolk worked constantly and called none of it work. Men poled flat punts through the reed-beds, cutting sedge in rhythms so practiced they resembled conversation. Women wove fish-traps with fingers that never stopped moving even while they argued, gossiped, sang. Children ran the swaying walkways barefoot at speeds that should have ended in drownings and never did, because — Elodie realized, watching — the walkways themselves seemed to steady under them, each plank catching the next runner's weight like a hand offered without looking. Everything in the village was borrowed from the river and lent back to it. Nothing was owned outright, except perhaps the songs, and those were shared so freely they might as well have been.

Nobody asked them anything. That was the thing that undid her, quietly, over those three days. Nobody asked where they had come from. Nobody asked what they were fleeing. An old man taught Ash to bait a hook without ever asking why the child already seemed to understand fish better than fishermen did. A girl of eight sat beside Elodie on the walkway for an entire afternoon, silently splitting reeds into weaving-strands, and handed Elodie a half-finished mat without explanation, and when Elodie fumbled it, took it back without judgment and showed her again, slower. No one asked. Need was simply visible here, the way weather was visible, and was answered the way weather was endured: collectively, without ceremony, without keeping accounts.

Elodie had spent her whole adult life believing she preferred it otherwise. In her valley she had been competent, self-contained, careful — the bone-carver who served grief for coin and asked nothing of anyone, because asking created debts and debts created entanglements and entanglements, she had always believed, were how people like her got destroyed. Neutrality among smugglers and Wardens alike. A closed door, well maintained.

But lying in a stranger's hammock with her side sewn shut by a stranger's needle, fed from a stranger's pot, watching her stolen child learn to laugh — actually laugh, a bright startling sound, at some joke of gesture exchanged with the reed-splitting girl — Elodie felt the whole architecture of that belief groan and shift like a walkway under new weight.

Belonging wasn't granted. That was the lesson the valley had taught her wrongly, or that she had learned wrongly from the valley. She had waited her whole life to be *given* a place — by the village that tolerated her oddness, by the Crown whose laws she kept scrupulously, by the dead whose voices she carried — and had concluded, from the waiting, that she was the sort of person places were not given to.

But these people had not been given the river either. Look at them. Driven to the margins, taxed by no one because claimed by no one, burned out of Blackreed Landing, hunted when convenient and ignored when not — and still they had *made* this. Made it out of silt and sedge and stubborn mutual attention, one plank lashed to another, one stranger fed and another, year after year, until the making itself became a home. Belonging was not a door you were admitted through. It was a walkway you helped build, and the building was the belonging, and it swayed, and sometimes it got your feet wet, and it held anyway.

She was still turning this over on the third evening when the trouble came — because trouble always comes, and grace is not the absence of it but what arrives in spite of it.

A punt slid up the landing at dusk bearing a man from downriver, a eel-fisher with nervous hands, and the news he brought moved through the village faster than fire through thatch: iron-coats at Vell's Landing, six boats, asking questions. Asking about a woman with a carved-bone trade mark, a smuggler known on the Sere, and a child who sang songs no one knew.

The village gathered on the great central walkway in the blue dusk, and though Elodie understood perhaps a tenth of what was said, she understood all of it. Some argued for turning the strangers out — quietly, tonight, before the iron-boats came poking up the channels. Others argued the opposite, and the old healer's voice cut through both sides like a knife through soft cheese: *the same river floats everyone.* And in the middle of it all, Ash stood at Elodie's side, small hand gripping hers hard enough to hurt, and Elodie felt the tremor in that small grip and knew the child had followed every word, because the child always did.

In the end it was the eel-fisher himself who settled it, the man who'd brought the warning, the one with the most to lose. He looked at Ash — just looked, for a long moment, the way the young Warden had looked, though this look held no faltering, only something quieter and harder to kill — and said something short in river-talk that the crowd went silent around.

"What did he say?" Elodie whispered to Renn.

Renn's voice was rougher than usual. "He said his grandmother used to sing a verse of that song. Before the iron-coats made it dangerous. He said—" and here Renn stopped, cleared his throat, started again. "He said it's been sixty years since anyone sang it whole. He says the child sings it whole."

The crowd murmured. The old healer raised her hand, and it was done — not voted, exactly, nothing so formal; simply *decided*, the way rivers decide. The strangers would leave at first light, by the hidden channel south of the rookery, the one no map had ever carried. And until then, they would eat at the common fire, and sleep warm, and be sung to.

That night, the whole village sang.

It was nothing grand — work-songs mostly, and lullabies, and one long comic ballad about a pike that swallowed a magistrate's wig that had Ash laughing until they hiccupped. But threaded through it, once, briefly, almost casually, the eel-fisher's cracked old voice lifted the verse his grandmother had kept — three phrases of the old tongue, worn smooth by sixty years of private humming — and Ash went utterly still, listening, tears sliding silently down their face in the firelight, and answered him. Two voices, one ancient and one impossibly old, braiding across a fire of river-driftwood on a stilted walkway in the middle of nowhere, and every Reedfolk present listened the way people listen in churches, if they are lucky enough to have churches like this.

Elodie sat with her arm around the child and did not cry, because she had spent forty years practicing not crying, but it was a near thing, and she knew, with the calm certainty of the gravely wounded, that the practice was over. Whatever came next — the Spire, the Wardens, the war everyone feared sleeping beneath the mountain — some part of her had already crossed the river that mattered. She was not going back to being the woman with the closed door. There was no door anymore. There was only the walkway, and the people on it, and the long water carrying them all, patiently, toward whatever sea awaited.

* * *

They left at first light in a narrow punt poled by the eel-fisher's son, through a channel so secret it was less a route than a rumor — a tunnel through the living reeds where the stalks closed overhead and the morning sun came down green as underwater. Ash rode in the bow with their chin on their knees. Renn dozed against the cargo. Elodie lay propped against the bundles, her stitched side aching in a way she was beginning to suspect meant more than thread and herbs could mend, watching the green light ripple overhead.

Near midday the reeds opened onto open water, and the eel-fisher's son pointed south with his pole.

"There," he said in trade-tongue, "past the drowned church, the Sere bends east. Three days' poling brings you to the marsh-roads. After that—" he hesitated, and his nervous hands stilled on the shaft— "after that, you're in Spire country. Old country. My father says the water tastes different there." He looked at Ash, and lowered his voice as though the river itself might repeat it. "Says it tastes of waiting."

Ash looked south, where the river bent toward the horizon and the horizon bent, faintly, upward — toward a shape so distant it might have been cloud, might have been mountain, might have been a tower standing alone in the world's memory.

And in Elodie's side, under the stitches, under the bitter green poultice, something pulsed once — not pain, exactly. Attention. As if the wound itself had heard the word *Spire* and turned its face toward it.

The punt slid on, southward, into the widening light.
