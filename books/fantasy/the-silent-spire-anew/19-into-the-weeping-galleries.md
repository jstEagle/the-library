## 19. Into the Weeping Galleries

The entrance did not look like an entrance. It looked like a wound in the mountainside, a seam of black water weeping out of the rock and threading down through moss-slick stone into the valley below. Renn had called it the Weeping Galleries because of that — because the mountain itself seemed to cry here, endlessly, quietly, as if it had been doing so for six hundred years and had never once been offered comfort.

Elodie stood at the lip of it with Ash's hand in hers and felt the dead before she saw anything at all.

They were not like the dead she knew. The dead of her workshop had been individuals — a grandmother's last word to a granddaughter, a drowned fisherman humming his wedding song. Their voices came to her one at a time, politely, the way guests knock before entering. She had built her whole life around that courtesy. She had told herself, in the long solitary years, that the dead were gentle things, that they only wanted to be heard, that hearing them was a kindness she could sell by the finger-bone without ever being diminished by it.

The dead behind this seam of rock were not polite.

They pressed against her like floodwater against a door. Not one voice but a multitude, not words yet but *wanting*, a pressure behind her eyes and along her jaw and deep in the bones of her hands, where her gift lived. It was the difference between a single candle and standing inside a burning house. She staggered, caught herself on Renn's arm, and heard him say something — she would remember later that his voice sounded far away, as if heard through water — but she could not answer him. All of her was turned toward the mountain, listening.

*Please,* said the mountain. *Please. Please. Please.*

Not angry. That was the horror of it, when she finally understood what she was feeling. She had expected anger. Anger she knew how to survive; anger had edges you could brace against. This was older than anger. This was the sound of ten thousand throats that had been silenced mid-word, holding their last syllables for six centuries, still waiting for permission to finish speaking.

"They're pleading," she said aloud, and her voice came out cracked and strange. "Renn. They're not whispering. They're begging."

Renn looked at her, then at the black water, then at Ash, who had gone very still in that particular way the child had — the stillness of someone listening to a room no one else could hear. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. The mountain hummed its low note, the one Elodie had first heard high in Thornfell's pass, except here it was not a hum anymore. Here it was almost speech.

"We don't have to go in today," Renn said. He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his hand on her shoulder was steady and warm and real. "There's a ledge half a mile back. Dry ground. We can wait out the weather."

"The weather isn't the problem," Elodie said.

"No," he agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Ash tugged her hand. The child pointed into the dark, then touched their own chest, then pointed again. *In there,* the gesture said. *That is where I come from. That is where I must go.* And beneath the gesture, faint as breath on a winter window, Elodie felt the old language move through the child like current through reeds — a word she was beginning to know now, a word that meant *home*, though whether it named a place or a people or a wound, she could not yet say.

She had burned her workshop for this child. She had crossed Thornfell with Wardens at their backs and bandits in the passes and a river full of strangers' kindnesses. She had let a murdered woman's son go unavenged so she could carry a bone-reader's grief instead. Every mile of it had been leading here, to a hole in the world that wept.

"All right," she said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. "Light the lamps."

* * *

Inside, the water was everywhere and nowhere. It ran down the walls in thin glass threads, gathered in channels cut into the floor by hands long dissolved, pooled in basins carved from living rock. Someone had engineered this weeping once — or perhaps the mountain had simply begun to grieve when its people were buried, and the Spire-folk, even sealed away, had taught the stone to carry their sorrow outward. Elodie did not know which answer was worse.

Renn went first with the lamp held high, and the light did a terrible thing: it showed them the walls.

"Oh," Elodie said. Just that. A small, foolish sound, because there was nothing else in her.

Names. Millions of names.

They covered every surface the lamplight touched, carved in rows so fine and so dense that from a distance they looked like fabric, like rain frozen mid-fall against the stone. They climbed up beyond the reach of the light and descended below the black water and continued, she understood with a lurch, for miles — gallery after gallery after gallery, each wall a ledger, each name a person who had been shut behind this mountain while it was still breathing. Some names were large and careful. Some were small and hurried. Some were tiny, tiny marks near the floor, at the height of a child's hand, and Elodie had to stop walking when she understood those.

"Six hundred years," Renn said softly. His charm was gone; what remained was rawer and more decent than his charm had ever been. "They knew they were going to be sealed. So they spent their last days carving everyone in. Everyone. Not just the ones inside." He lifted the lamp toward a section of wall where the names changed character — newer cuts over older, generations of additions made before the door closed. "Every name they could remember. So no one would be forgotten, even if no one ever came."

"And no one came," Elodie whispered.

"No one came."

Her gift woke fully then, and she stopped fighting it.

The names began to speak.

Not all at once — that mercy was denied her — but in waves, in tides, the way surf works a shore. A name would catch the light or catch her attention and its owner would rise through her like a bubble through dark water. A woman named *Selith-who-mended-nets*, pleading — not for freedom, Elodie realized, not at first, but for her sister outside the walls, begging anyone who heard to tell Marrow-of-the-river-clay that she had forgiven her, that the quarrel about the boat had been nothing, nothing at all, please, please tell her. A man whose name was a sound like a bell struck underwater, asking over and over whether his children had gotten out, whether the small names near the floor were his, whether any of them had been tall enough to run. A name so old its owner had forgotten their own ending and simply repeated the beginning, the first day of their life, over and over, a child's morning, sunlight on a threshold, gone now, gone, gone —

Elodie pressed her back against the wall and slid down until she was crouching, arms over her head, and the names kept coming, kind and desperate and endless, and the worst of it — the thing she would carry forever — was that none of them raged. Six hundred years entombed alive, and they begged. They apologized. They asked after their families. They thanked her, *thanked her*, for listening, as if being heard were a gift rather than a debt six centuries overdue.

"I can't," she gasped. "I can't hold them all—"

"You don't have to." Ash crouched beside her, small and certain in the lamplight. The child took her face in both cold hands and sang — three notes only, low and soft, the notes from the pass, the quieting song — and the tide of voices receded like water drawing back from sand. Not silenced. Never silenced. But held, the way you hold a sobbing friend: not stopping the grief, just bearing some of its weight.

Through the child's fingers, through the fading song, one voice remained, closer than the others, and it spoke in fragments Elodie's gift could turn into something like sense:

*You carve. You carve the dead true. You are a hearth for us. You are so small a hearth and we are so many and we are sorry, we are sorry, we will try to be fewer, we will try—*

And Elodie, crouched in the dark with a mute child's hands on her cheeks and a smuggler's lamp trembling overhead, did the most undignified thing she had done since childhood. She answered out loud.

"Don't be sorry," she said. "Don't you dare be sorry. You were the ones wronged."

The silence that followed was enormous. Somewhere deep in the galleries, she felt thousands of dead souls go still around that sentence like birds around a dropped seed, turning it over, tasting it, not believing it. Six hundred years of believing themselves the burden. Six hundred years of apologizing for existing too loudly in their own tomb.

Then, softly, from everywhere and nowhere, the mountain sighed — and the weeping water on the walls ran a little faster, and Elodie understood that the Galleries were crying harder now because someone had finally told them their tears were permitted.

* * *

They went deeper because Ash insisted, and because the alternative was camped at the mouth of a necropolis with Corvane somewhere behind them and the mapmaker's treachery somewhere ahead. Renn scouted each chamber before letting them rest, checking the water depth with a pole, marking the safe ledges with chalk from his pocket. He moved like a man who had done this in a hundred bad places, and Elodie watched him and thought, not for the first time, that she had misjudged him thoroughly and repeatedly, and that she was beginning to run out of ways to keep misjudging him.

The third gallery was flooded to the knee. They waded it in silence, and the silence was worse than the voices, because in the silence Elodie could feel the names waiting, patient as sediment, and patience like that is its own kind of scream.

Here the carvings changed. The names grew larger, deeper, cut with tools wielded in desperation. And among them, new marks — angular, brutal, foreign. Crown script. Warden tally-marks. Records of inspection, scratched over the memorial like graffiti over a grave.

"They came," Elodie breathed. "Wardens came down here."

"Once a generation," Renn said. His voice had gone flat, the flatness he wore when decency and survival pulled opposite directions. "To check the seal holds. My father ran cargo for the inspection parties. He never told me what they checked. I think he didn't know either." He dragged his fingers through the knee-deep water, watching it swirl. "Maintenance, not memory. That's what I heard a Warden captain say once, drunk. I thought he meant a road."

Ahead, Ash had stopped wading. The child stood hip-deep in the black water with their head tilted, listening, and the listening had a quality Elodie had learned to fear — the absolute stillness of someone receiving something vast.

Then Ash raised one hand, palm out toward the far darkness, and spoke aloud for the second time since the valley. Not the old language this time. Broken, halting common words, each one placed like a stone across a stream:

"They. Are. Closer. Than. Before."

"Who?" Renn asked, though Elodie suspected he already knew.

"The. Waiting. Ones." Ash's eyes found hers in the lamplight, and they were wide and ancient and terrified. "I. Can. Hear. Them. Breathing."

Breathing. The word went through Elodie like cold iron. Dead did not breathe. Sealed did not breathe. Six hundred years was not a number a body survived — and yet the decree clause Renn had described around their fires, the clause about *a voice returning willing*, had always implied someone left inside to accept the return. She had filed that thought away in the place where she kept things too large to look at directly.

Now the thought looked back at her.

"How far?" she asked Ash.

Ash held up both hands, palms together, then opened them slowly outward — a gesture meaning *near*, meaning *one sleep away*, meaning the distance a body drifts in a night's rest. Then the child did something new: pressed both palms flat against the surface of the black water, and the water *sang*. A low chord rolled out through the flooded galleries, echoing and multiplying, and from the deep places of the mountain a reply came — faint, slow, unmistakably rhythmic.

Like breathing. Like the breathing of something asleep.

Renn's lamp shook. Elodie watched the flame tremble and thought about how flame is honest; it cannot pretend calm. "Right," he said, and his voice was lighter than his eyes. "Well. I've smuggled past scarier things than a sleeping mountain. Probably. There was a customs official in Vail's Crossing who came close."

But his joke landed in silence, because Elodie was looking at the tally-marks on the wall — the Warden counts, generation after generation, each inspection recorded in neat bureaucratic strokes — and she had just understood something that made her stomach drop through the black water beneath her feet.

The counts had changed.

The oldest tallies were long. Dozens of inspections, thorough, frequent. But the recent ones — the newest marks, cut within her own lifetime — were short. Sparse. And the last mark of all was different: not a tally but a symbol she recognized from the seal-decree fragment Renn had once described, the sign of the Warden-Commandant's office, cut fresh, the stone dust still pale in the grooves.

Someone had been here recently. Recently enough that the mountain had not yet finished weeping over the scratch.

"They know we're coming," Elodie said. "Or they're already ahead of us."

Renn turned the lamp toward the passage ahead, where the water deepened from knee to thigh and the ceiling lowered until the three of them would have to walk bent nearly double, bent like mourners, into the dark where something slept and breathed.

"Then we'd best not keep them waiting," he said.

Ash took Elodie's hand. And from the walls all around them, soft as rain beginning, the million names started to plead again — not to be freed, not yet, not first — but to warn her:

*Careful. Careful. Careful. The ones in iron do not weep. The ones in iron do not beg. Go gently, hearth-woman. Go gently past the sleeping heart.*
