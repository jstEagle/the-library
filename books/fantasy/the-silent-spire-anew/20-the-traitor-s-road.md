## 20. The Traitor's Road

Renn Carrow had been sold out before. It was, he liked to say, one of the hazards of a career built on being worth selling out.

He had never gotten used to it.

He told himself that as he crouched in the flooded dark of the Weeping Galleries' outer reach, water to his chest, lamp hooded under his coat, listening to two men argue three turns back along the passage. Their voices carried strangely down here — flattened by stone, softened by water, stripped of everything but their shape. One voice was young and frightened. The other was old, wet, and familiar.

Vessik.

The mapmaker.

Renn closed his eyes and let the arithmetic finish itself, because there was no use stopping it now. Vessik had found them at Greyford — no, not found them. Had *waited* for them, which was worse. Had offered the true route through the Galleries with tears standing in his rheumy eyes and his dead wife's name carved on a locket he wore like a license to be believed. And all Renn had to give him was coin, and when the coin wasn't enough —

When the coin wasn't enough, Vessik had named his price: the child.

And Renn had refused, had put a knife on the table between them, had walked away feeling righteous and clean, and had made an enemy who knew every road, ford, and rat-run from Thornfell's spine to the Spire's drowned threshold.

Righteous and clean. That was the joke. That was always the joke.

"—told you they'd come this way," the frightened voice was saying. "I don't want any part of what happens down here. I'm a guide, not a —"

"You're a man who wants to keep breathing," said Vessik, "and so am I, and so is everyone the Crown sends its sergeants after. Breathe quieter."

They were moving. Not toward Renn — away, up and eastward, along the ledge path that climbed out of the flooded galleries toward the dry country above. Toward, if Renn read the acoustics right, the Warden camp.

The Warden camp.

Because of course there was a Warden camp. Because Corvane did not burn caches and question bone-carvers and issue polite, terrible courtesies across half a valley without following the thread all the way down to its knot. Because Renn himself had watched the sergeant work once, years ago, from across a customs yard, and had thought: *there is a man who will not stop until either the answer or the world ends, whichever comes first.*

And Renn had led that man straight to the only two people alive who mattered more to him than breathing.

*Careful,* the walls whispered around him, the million names murmuring through wet stone, patient and pleading. *Careful. Careful.*

"I know," Renn breathed, and hated how much it sounded like a prayer.

* * *

Here was the shape of the trap, laid out in the ledger of his mind where he kept all his debts:

Elodie and Ash were a quarter-mile deeper into the galleries, past the flooded throat, in the long hall where the names grew so thick on the walls they overlapped like scales on a fish. They were moving slowly because Ash moved slowly now — the fever had broken but left the child thin and quiet, walking with a hand on Elodie's arm, listening to the dead the way other children listened to lullabies. Every hour they spent below ground was an hour the Wardens spent tightening the net above it.

There were two ways out of the Galleries. Renn had made sure of that before ever leading them down; he was a smuggler first and a romantic a very distant second, and he never entered anywhere he couldn't leave by another door.

The first way was the way they'd come: the low flooded passage, south and down, emerging near the river-folk's stilted camps where Elodie's wounds had been stitched by women who owed them nothing. If the Wardens held the upper exits, the southern passage was still theirs. Wet, slow, miserable — but open.

The second way was the ledge path east and up, the dry route, the fast route, the one Vessik had promised ended at a shepherd's stair that would put them on the moor above the Spire within a day instead of four. It was the reason Renn had gone back to Vessik at all. Elodie was wounded. Ash was small and sick. Four days underground with the whole mountain humming warnings was four days too many.

Except Vessik hadn't sold them a route. He'd sold them a corridor. And now he was walking it with a frightened local guide and — Renn strained his hearing against the dripping dark — the unmistakable clink and shuffle of men who wore iron the way other men wore skin.

Two choices. He could see both of them hanging in the black like lamps.

Go back. Warn Elodie, take Ash's hand, turn them all south into the flooded passage, and lose a day — maybe two, maybe three, with her thigh wound weeping again in cold water and the child barely able to wade. Maybe lose everything, if the southern passage wasn't as open as he remembered, because he'd scouted it six years ago and six years was a long time for a smuggler's map to stay true.

Or go forward. Up the ledge path ahead of them, alone, and do what Renn Carrow was actually good at, which was making problems disappear quietly in the dark before they reached the people they were aimed at. Buy the corridor with whatever it cost. His blood, probably. Possibly his freedom. Almost certainly any future in which he walked out of these mountains at all.

Warn the others, or secure the exit.

Save the family, or clear the road.

He stood chest-deep in the water of a dead people's necropolis, and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that the choice was no choice at all — and that this was precisely how guilt worked. It didn't ambush you. It invoiced you. Every kindness he'd ever accepted, every debt he'd ever shrugged off, every friend he'd ever failed — Ossa and the Kettle Street boys, the widow Marren whose cargo he'd lost and never repaid, the whole long ledger of a charming life — came due at once, compounding, here in the dark, in the form of a mute child and a woman who burned her own home to keep him safe.

He thought about what Elodie would say. She wouldn't say anything. She'd look at him with those gray eyes that saw straight through charm to the machinery underneath, and she'd know in a heartbeat which choice he'd made, and she would hate it — not because it was wrong, but because it was him deciding, again, alone, that his life was the cheapest thing on the table.

So don't decide alone, some voice said — his own voice, younger, from a night years ago on a rooftop in Greyford when he'd still believed debts could be forgiven rather than merely paid.

But there was no time to go back and consult. The voices above were fading eastward, and behind them, fainter but unmistakable, came the sound Renn dreaded most in all the world: boots. Many boots. Iron-shod, keeping step, descending the shepherd's stair into the dry upper galleries with the disciplined patience of men who had been told exactly where to go and by whom.

Vessik hadn't just sold the route. He'd sold the timing.

* * *

Renn went forward.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. He told himself he'd take one look at the numbers, count lanterns, mark positions, and slip back south to raise the alarm before anything was committed. He was aware, even as the lies assembled themselves, that he was doing what he always did — dressing the sacrifice up as tactics so it would go down easier.

The ledge path climbed out of the water in long switchbacks, the ceiling rising until he could walk upright, the walls narrowing until he could touch both sides at once. Here the carved names changed character. Below, in the flooded halls, the names had pleaded. Up here they had a different tone entirely — shorter, sharper, cut deep and hurried, the way a person writes when the soldiers are already in the street.

*Hide us. Remember us. Avenge—*

That last one had been chiseled off. Deliberately, centuries ago, by someone standing exactly where Renn stood. He ran his thumb over the scar in the stone and felt, absurdly, like a trespasser at a grave.

The air changed. Woodsmoke. Tallow. The sour-metal smell of Warden-issue lamp oil, the smell of his nightmares since he was nine years old.

He killed his own lamp and went forward by touch, along the final stretch of ledge where it opened into the Gallery of the Shepherd's Stair — a vaulted chamber the size of a tithe-barn, its far wall broken by the stair itself climbing toward a distant coin of gray daylight. And there they were.

Thirty men, perhaps thirty-five. Corvane's column, or most of it, bivouacked in the mouth of the ancient dead like a knife left sitting in a wound. They'd stacked their shields against the carved walls — leaning them directly against the names, Renn noticed, as if the stone were just stone, as if six hundred years of screaming had never soaked into it. Two sentries on the stair. One at each lateral arch. A quartermaster's table under a proper lantern, and at it, bent over maps with a courier's satchel open beside him —

Vessik.

The old man looked terrible. He'd aged a decade in three days, or maybe Renn was only now seeing him clearly. He was pointing at something on the map with a trembling finger, and Corvane — Corvane was not even looking at the map. The sergeant stood with his hands clasped behind his back, listening to Vessik the way a man listens to weather, and even across forty yards of gloom Renn could see the quality of that attention: total, patient, and utterly without warmth. Taking everything. Promising nothing.

Renn counted exits. Old habit, older than guilt. Lateral arch, west: sentry, but the gallery beyond branched, and branching galleries meant options. Stair: locked down tight. And the third way — the one that made his stomach drop — was the low arch directly opposite him, south and down, the one that fed into the long flooded hall where Elodie and Ash were right now, moving slowly, singing softly to a million pleading names.

The Wardens had posted nothing at the south arch. Either they didn't know it connected through, or —

Or Vessik was saving it. Holding it back as his last coin, to be spent when the Crown finally asked the question Renn knew was coming: *and where exactly are they now?*

Which meant Renn had minutes. Maybe less. All it would take was Corvane asking the right question, or Vessik deciding that survival beat sentiment, and thirty-five men in iron would pour down that arch into a flooded hall where a wounded woman and a sick child had nowhere to run except deeper, toward the sleeping heart, toward the thing the dead had begged them to approach gently.

*Go gently past the sleeping heart.* The walls had warned them. Now Renn understood the warning differently. It wasn't the sleepers the heart endangered. It was anyone driven desperate enough to wake them.

His hand was on his knife. He noticed it the way you notice your own heartbeat — without having decided anything.

One sentry, he calculated. Take the western sentry quiet, take his cloak and lantern, walk into the camp as one of Corvane's own — he'd worn enough stolen uniforms to make the walk convincingly — get close to the quartermaster's table, and put a knife between Vessik's ribs before the old man finished his next sentence. Thirty seconds of work. He'd done harder things for worse reasons. He'd done easier things for money.

And then thirty-five Wardens would know someone was among them, and the south arch would be sealed within the hour, and the choice he'd tried to buy for Elodie and Ash would be taken out of all their hands forever.

No. Not like that.

Think, Carrow. You've talked your way out of graves before. Talk your way into this one.

What did Corvane want? Not Vessik's map — the man barely glanced at it. Corvane wanted certainty. He wanted to know, before he committed his column to the flooded deeps, that the intelligence was true, that the bone-carver and the child were really below, that he wasn't about to march his men into a drowned labyrinth on a traitor's word. That was why Vessik was still breathing. He was a coin being tested by biting.

And a tested coin can fail the test.

Renn eased back from the ledge, found a side niche where the names crowded thick — someone had carved a whole family here, seven names descending in size like steps — and sat down in the dark with his back against their voices and let the plan assemble itself out of the oldest material a smuggler has: other people's greed.

Vessik wanted to survive. Corvane wanted truth. The Wardens wanted orders followed. Nobody in that camp, Renn realized, actually wanted to go down into the water. He'd seen their faces. Soldiers fear flooded ground the way sailors fear fire — it takes away everything their discipline is built on. Give Corvane a reason to doubt Vessik, and the sergeant wouldn't storm the galleries. He'd seal them. Post his men at every known exit, sit, and wait — believing time was on his side, believing he'd penned his quarry in a tomb.

A tomb. Yes. But tombs have doors, and the people inside them had spent six hundred years learning patience, and one of them was a bone-carver who heard the dead, and somewhere below her feet was a great vertebra of a door that answered to willingness.

Seal us in, Sergeant. Please. It's exactly what we need you to do.

It was elegant. It was awful. It required him to stand in front of Corvane himself, wearing a dead man's cloak and a liar's face, and dismantle Vessik's credibility piece by piece while standing close enough to the sergeant to count the stitching on his gloves.

Guilt compounded like debt, he'd thought earlier. Well. Here was the payment plan.

* * *

He got the cloak off the western sentry the honest way, or as honest as his methods ever got: he waited in the dark for two hours until the man's relief was late — late because Renn had spent one of his precious coins arranging it, a bribed runner and a forged order diverting the relief detail to the stair — and then he stepped out of the shadows with a sap and a whisper and lowered the boy gently to the stones. Young. Freckled. A Thornfell accent under the Warden training. Renn took the cloak, the lantern badge, the helmet, and left the boy his knife, because there were lines even now, and because the walls around them were watching, and he found he could not rob a sleeping child in a house of mourning names.

*Good,* whispered one of the names, approvingly, in the voice of someone's grandmother. *Good lad.*

"Shut up," Renn muttered, and buckled the cloak.

Walking into that camp was the longest forty yards of his life. He kept his chin down, his stride bored, his lantern held at the regulation angle he'd memorized from watching Wardens for twenty years from the profitable side of the law. He passed the shield stack. He passed two men arguing over dice. He came around the quartermaster's table into the light, and there was Vessik's face, gray and sweating, and there — turning toward him with eyes the color of a frozen lake — was Corvane.

For one heartbeat the sergeant simply looked at him, and Renn felt the look go through the borrowed cloak, through the borrowed face, down to the actual man underneath, the debtor, the charmer, the coward, the friend, all of it laid open like a dissected cadaver.

Then Corvane said, mildly, "You're late, Warden Hesk."

"Yes, sir." Renn's voice came out steady. Twenty years of practice, steady. "Detour on the stair. Runner's orders."

"Mm." Corvane turned back to Vessik. "You were telling me, master cartographer, how certain you are that they entered the flooded halls. Three days ago, I believe you said. Yet my scouts found no sign at the southern egress. No tracks older than a week. Explain to me again why I should spend my men's lives on your word."

And there it was. The opening, handed to him across a lamplit table by the cold machinery of duty itself.

Renn stepped forward, cleared his throat, and began to bury Vessik alive — the false survey of '41, the map of Greyford's undercrofts that had gotten two freeholders hanged, the little habit of selling the same road twice — every sin true, every sin damning, delivered in the flat respectful drone of a subordinate protecting his sergeant from bad intelligence. Vessik's face went from gray to white to something ancient and ruined, and his mouth worked, and his eyes found Renn's over the lantern flame with a terrible dawning recognition.

*Renn Carrow,* the old man's eyes said. *You're the death of me.*

"I'm sorry," Renn said, and did not break cadence, and meant it, and hated himself, and kept talking.

Corvane listened with his lake-colored eyes half-lidded, and when Renn finished, the sergeant was silent for a long moment. Then, without looking away from Vessik, he spoke the words Renn had crawled through a necropolis to earn:

"Seal the galleries. Every exit. Nothing goes in or out until I say otherwise." A pause, and the ghost of something almost human crossed his face — doubt, maybe, or its fossil. "We are not butchers. We are patient. Whatever is down there will come up eventually, or it won't, and either way the Crown's peace holds."

Boots began to move. Orders rippled outward. Somewhere above, iron rang against stone as the sealing parties formed up.

And Renn Carrow stood in a stolen cloak in the heart of his enemy's camp, victorious, his exit secured, his family unknowingly entombed for their own protection — and felt the full weight of the debt settle onto his shoulders like wet stone. He had bought them time. He had bought them a door that only opened inward.

Now all he had to do was find a way to tell them what he'd done — and pray to every god who'd ever listened to a guilty man that the dead below would forgive him for locking their house from the outside.

In his ear, soft as rain, one of the carved names whispered: *Careful, thief. Careful.*
