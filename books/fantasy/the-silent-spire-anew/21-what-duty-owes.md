## 21. What Duty Owes

The archive beneath Wardenhold smelled of cold stone and older lies.

Warden-Sergeant Corvane had been granted access to the Restricted Vault at the fourth bell of the morning, by written order of High Warden Aldous Vey himself — an honor, the clerk had said, smoothing his mustache. An honor, Corvane thought now, standing alone among shelves of iron-bound ledgers that no living Warden was meant to read, was a strange word for what this felt like. It felt like being handed a knife and told not to look at the blade.

He had come for one document. He had told himself it was diligence: if he was to hunt a bone-carver and a child across half of Thornfell, he ought to know precisely what law he enforced. Thirty years in the grey-and-iron, and he had learned that the men who made mistakes were the men who stopped asking questions. He asked questions. That was his virtue. That was what they praised him for at commendations, what junior Wardens whispered about with a mixture of fear and admiration — *Corvane reads everything, Corvane misses nothing.*

He should have known that reading everything would be the end of him.

* * *

The seal-decree lay in a lead-lined case on a reading table scarred by centuries of elbows. The clerk had worn gloves to lift it out; Corvane wore none. The vellum was stiff as frost, the ink gone the brown of dried blood, but the hand that had written it was steady and beautiful, the kind of script learned by candlelight by monks who knew their words might outlive the kingdoms that commissioned them.

*Decree of the First Seal,* the header read. *In the Year of Ashes 612. By command of Aurelian Voss, first of that name, King over the Thornfell Marches and the Riverlands beyond.*

Corvane's jaw tightened. Voss. The same name as the bone-carver's. Coincidence, probably. Names repeated like weather in these mountains. He set the thought aside and read on.

The decree was short, as founding documents often are when the truth they contain is long. He knew its public form by heart — every Warden did, recited it at induction with a hand on the iron badge:

*That the tower-folk, whose arts woke the dead and unmade the peace of nations, be sealed beneath their Spire until such time as the world forgets them. That the seal be maintained in perpetuity. That any voice of theirs found abroad be silenced, and any who harbor such voices be judged as accomplices to the old war.*

He had sworn to that. He had killed for less than that. In the border years, in the purges of the eastern covens, he had put down old women whose only crime was humming tunes their grandmothers had taught them, and he had slept afterward, because the decree was clear and he was a clear man.

But the public form was not the whole form.

His eyes went to the final clause, the one omitted from every induction recitation, every training manual, every copy posted in every Wardenhall from here to the coast. It was written smaller than the rest, cramped against the bottom margin as though the scribe had hoped the king's eye would pass over it — or perhaps as though the king himself had dictated it last, in a voice grown quiet, after the feasting was done and the hall emptied and the weight of what he'd done settled onto him like snow on a roof.

*Provided always, and let no successor strike this out: should a voice of the sealed folk return to them willing — not taken, not driven, but returning of its own desire — then the seal is broken lawful and clean, and the sealed may rise, and no man, Warden or king, shall call it crime.*

*A voice returns willing.* Corvane read it three times. Then a fourth, moving his finger under the words as though they might rearrange themselves into something he could live with.

They did not rearrange.

* * *

He sat down slowly on the reading stool, because his legs had informed him they were no longer entirely under his management.

Thirty years. He ran the arithmetic the way he ran all his arithmetic, coldly, completely, refusing himself the mercy of rounding. Thirty years of service. Four hundred and eleven names in his own hand in the judgment rolls — he had counted once, drunk, on an anniversary he never spoke of, and never counted again because the number did not change and did not shrink. Four hundred and eleven people silenced, transported, burned, or broken, all under a law whose full text contained its own repudiation, a clause carved into the founding stone itself that said: *if they come home freely, let them.*

And the clause had been struck from every copy. Every one. Which meant someone, at some point in six centuries, had sat where Corvane sat, had read what he had just read, had understood what he had just understood — and had chosen the seal over the truth. Had chosen the machine over the mercy. And then had handed the edited version down to the next generation, and the next, each Warden-Sergeant polishing the lie a little brighter before passing it on.

*We are not keepers of the peace,* Corvane thought, and the thought arrived with the flat certainty of a blade going in. *We are maintenance men. We are the ones who keep the tomb door shut and call it justice.*

He thought of the smuggler they'd taken at Greyford — Carrow, the one who'd held the bridge with a river-cutter's hook and a madman's grin while the woman and the child slipped away downstream. Carrow had spat blood on the cobbles and laughed at him. *"You don't even know what you're guarding, do you? You're a dog barking at a door, and you've never once wondered what's behind it."*

Corvane had ordered him held for transport and told himself the words were the ravings of a cornered criminal.

Now, sitting in the dark of the Restricted Vault with the true decree open before him, he heard them again, and they were not ravings. They were testimony.

* * *

There was more in the case. There always was, with founding documents — the way a wound has threads running through it deeper than the cut. Beneath the decree lay a sheaf of addenda, warrants, and marginalia spanning the first century after the sealing. Corvane read them all. He was, whatever else he was now, still a thorough man.

The third document stopped his breath.

It was a warrant, dated Year of Ashes 619 — seven years after the sealing — authorizing the pursuit and silencing of "a singing child recovered from the river-roads near Greyford." Recovered. Singing. A child. The warrant bore the signature of the second High Warden, and across its foot, in a different hand, a note:

*Clause invoked. Voice returned not willing. Silenced per statute. Note for successors: the clause stands. One day a voice will come home wanting to come home. Pray we are not the men who stop it.*

Pray we are not the men who stop it.

Six hundred years of prayer, unanswered. Six hundred years of children — how many? The archive would know, if he asked, and the archive never lied, which was the single decent thing about it. Children found singing songs no one had taught them, humming melodies that made animals go quiet and old people weep without knowing why. And every time, some Warden — some clear man, some dutiful man, some man exactly like him — had read the statute, obeyed the statute, and silenced the voice. And every time, somewhere in the chain of command, someone had filed the report knowing, *knowing*, that the law they enforced contained the exception that would have saved the child, and had buried it anyway.

Corvane put his palms flat on the table and stared at the grain of the wood until his vision swam.

He thought of the young Warden at Greyford Bridge. Pellam, nineteen years old, first posting, dead on the span with a crossbow bolt through him — no. Not dead. Dying. Corvane had reached him in time to hear the last of it, and what Pellam had said, gasping, holding Corvane's wrist with a grip that had nothing left in it, was not *avenge me* or *the traitors fled south*. What Pellam had said was:

*"Sergeant — the child looked at me. When I raised the bow. It looked at me like — like I was the thing to be afraid of, and it was sorry for me. Sergeant, why was it sorry for me?"*

And Corvane had said, because there was nothing else in him to say: *"Because it's frightened, son. They're always frightened."*

Pellam had died believing that. Believing the child was the hunted thing, the dangerous thing, the wrong thing. Believing his sergeant stood on the side of right.

Corvane had believed it too. Right up until this morning.

* * *

The bell rang the hour — seventh bell, the change of watch — and Corvane did not move. Dust drifted through the shaft of grey light from the vault's high slit-window, turning slowly, indifferent. Somewhere above him, the machinery of Wardenhold went on with its day: reports filed, patrols dispatched, kill-orders drafted in language so clean it could be read aloud at dinner. His own order sat in his dispatch case downstairs, signed by High Warden Vey, bearing the phrase the hierarchy used when it wanted to feel kind about murder: *terminate the anomalous voice with minimal distress to all parties.*

Minimal distress. As though the child would appreciate the courtesy. As though the bone-carver would thank them for the efficiency.

He took out the order and read it again, and for the first time in thirty years, the words looked foreign to him — the handwriting of a stranger, the seal of an enemy. *Terminate.* He had drafted orders with that word in them. He had received them and executed them and never once, not once, asked the question that a man who reads everything ought to have asked: *terminate according to what law, exactly?*

According to a law with a hole in its heart. According to a law whose authors had left a door in the wall and whose successors had bricked it up and painted over the bricks and called the paint tradition.

A voice returns willing.

The child was returning willing. That was the whole shape of it, wasn't it — the thing Carrow had hinted at on the bridge, the thing the bone-reader's village gossip carried like fever, the thing the reports from the Weeping Galleries described in the flat bureaucratic prose of men who did not want to think about what they were describing: the child was going home. Walking back to the Spire. Back to the sealed folk, of its own desire, singing the opening verses as it walked.

Every step that child took toward the Spire was a step through the door the founders had left open. Every mile was lawful. Every mile was *sanctioned by the first king's own hand*.

And his orders were to stop it. To kill it. To make certain the clause stayed a dead letter for another six hundred years.

Corvane rose. His knees cracked in the silence, an old man's sound, and he heard it as if from outside himself: *how long have you been old, Corvane? How long have you been following?*

He returned the decree to its case with both hands, carefully, the way a man handles the evidence of his own crime. Because that was what it was. Not the child's crime. Not the smuggler's. Not the bone-carver's. *His.* The crime of thirty years of obedience performed in ignorance — and worse, the crime now available to him in knowledge, the crime he would commit tomorrow, next week, at the Spire's threshold, if he did nothing. Ignorance excused nothing anymore. He had read the clause. He could never unread it.

The clerk was waiting outside the vault door, keys in hand, face arranged into servile pleasantness. "A productive morning, Sergeant?"

"Seal the vault," Corvane said. "No further access. Not to me, not to anyone, until I return from the field."

"A most unusual—"

"Seal it," Corvane said again, and the clerk's face did the small calculation clerks' faces do, and he bowed and began turning keys.

Corvane walked the long corridor toward the armory, past the induction hall where new recruits were drilled in the recitation — the *edited* recitation, the clean one, the one with the hole in it. Through the open doors he could hear a sergeant-instructor's voice, flat and rhythmic as a millwheel: *"...that any voice of theirs found abroad be silenced, and any who harbor such voices be judged as accomplices..."*

Forty faces. Nineteen-year-olds. Pellams, every one of them, waiting to die believing in something with a lie at its center.

Corvane kept walking. He collected his field kit, checked his blades with hands that moved by habit while the rest of him stood very still inside, and gave the quartermaster his departure time. Two hours. Enough to reach the Galleries road by nightfall. Enough to close the distance to the bone-carver and the child before they reached the sealed heart.

He had two hours to decide what he was.

* * *

He spent them in the muster yard, ostensibly reviewing the escort detail, actually standing at the rail watching the mountains he had policed for three decades. Thornfell in late afternoon: the peaks going copper, the passes filling with blue shadow, the Spire's distant silhouette just visible from the north wall — a grey finger of stone against the brightening sky, patient as a debt.

Vey found him there. The High Warden moved like money, unhurried, entitled, his cloak smelling of beeswax and something faintly medicinal. "You've read it, then," Vey said, without greeting. "The original."

Corvane turned. "You knew."

"I know many things." Vey's smile was thin and correct. "It's the burden of rank. The clause is a historical curiosity, Corvane. Scribes' sentimentality. No voice has ever returned *willing* — the sealed are dust, the matter is closed, and the clause is moot."

"The sealed are not dust." Corvane heard his own voice come out level, and was distantly proud of it. "The Galleries reports say breathing dust. Living sleepers. Your own surveyors logged respiration at the inner door seven years ago. You signed the log, High Warden. I've seen your mark."

Something moved behind Vey's eyes — not guilt; Corvane had learned long ago to distinguish guilt from inconvenience, and what crossed the High Warden's face was inconvenience. "Maintenance," Vey said smoothly, "requires accurate records. Whatever persists below is not life as the law understands it. And regardless—" he stepped closer, and his voice dropped into the register he used for kindness, which was the most frightening register he owned, "—regardless of clauses, the order stands. The voice is terminated. The carver and the smuggler are dealt with as circumstances require. You will execute, Sergeant, because you have always executed, because you are the best instrument this office has, and because the alternative—" the smile again, brief as a knife-flash, "—the alternative is that you become the anomaly. And we deal with anomalies."

There it was. The threat inside the courtesy, the way it had always been, the way he'd always known it was and had called it structure.

Corvane looked at the mountains a moment longer. Then he said: "I'll execute my duty, High Warden."

Vey's shoulders eased a fraction. "Good man."

"I haven't finished reading," Corvane said. "Duty owes accuracy. I'll see you at the Spire."

Vey studied him for three seconds too long, then nodded and walked away, satisfied, a man who had just checked a lock and found it holding.

Corvane stood at the rail until the light failed, and understood, with the calm of a man reading his own death warrant in a language he'd finally learned, that Vey was right about one thing: the alternative was becoming the anomaly. There was no third road. Execute the child and spend what remained of his life as a man who had read the clause and killed anyway — a maintenance man, a tomb-keeper, a brick in the wall. Or refuse, and be hunted by the machine he had served, by men like the man he had been, with the same clean orders and the same minimal-distress phrasing, until some nineteen-year-old Pellam put a bolt through him on some bridge or other and went home believing he'd done right.

Both roads ended in ruin. Only one of them ended in truth.

* * *

He rode out at dusk with twelve Wardens at his back, down the Galleries road, into the blue dark coming off the peaks. The column's lanterns swung in rhythm, and the hooves beat the old stones, and behind him Wardenhold's lights shrank to a smudge and vanished.

At the first switchback he raised a hand and the column halted. He dismounted, walked to the edge where the mountain dropped away into cloud, and stood looking north, toward the Spire, invisible now but present the way a wound is present.

Somewhere ahead of him, a bone-carver who heard the dead was walking a mute child home. Somewhere behind him, an order with his name on it waited to be obeyed or broken. And between those two facts stretched the whole length of his life, thirty years of it, suddenly legible, suddenly damning, the way a ledger looks when you finally find the page where the numbers stopped meaning anything.

*A voice returns willing.* Six hundred years, and the door had been standing open the entire time, and he had been the lock on it.

He remounted. "Forward," he told the column, and his voice did not shake, because whatever else Corvane was now — traitor, heretic, anomaly, the first honest man Wardenhold had produced in six centuries — he was still, God help him, a man who did his duty.

He simply no longer knew, riding down into the dark toward the Spire and the child and the choice waiting at the sealed heart, whether his duty owed its debt to the Crown that paid him —

or to the law the Crown had buried.

The lanterns swung. The column moved. And far below, deep in the mountain, something that had been silent for six hundred years seemed, very faintly, to be listening.
