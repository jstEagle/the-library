## 22. The Dead Vote

The voices came all at once.

Elodie had spent her whole life hearing the dead one at a time — one bone, one grief, one whisper coaxed from carved ivory like water from a dry well. She had learned to build walls against them, to keep her gift behind doors of routine and coin and careful neutrality, because even one voice was enough to hollow out a life if you let it in too far.

She had never imagined what it would be to let in ten thousand.

It happened at the third gallery's turning, where the flooded passage narrowed and the ceiling dropped so low that Renn's lantern threw shadows of the name-carvings across the black water like a second, drowned script. One moment Elodie was walking with Ash's small hand in hers, counting her own footsteps to keep panic at bay; the next moment the mountain opened its throat, and every name on every wall began to speak.

She went down onto her knees in the shallows without feeling it happen. The cold bit through her skirts. Somewhere above the roar of blood in her ears she heard Ash crying out her name — *mother-of-voices*, that word from fever-dreams, sharp now with fear rather than tenderness — and she heard Renn swearing as he splashed toward them, lantern swinging wild.

But she could not answer any of them, because the dead were answering *her*.

Not whispers. Not fragments. Voices — full, furious, grieving, argumentative voices, layered over each other like storm-waves, like a city's worth of conversation compressed into a single instant. And they were not speaking to her in the old tongue alone. Her gift, which had always translated haltingly, painfully, a phrase at a time, was drowning her in meaning:

*— six hundred years and no one comes, no one comes, we sealed ourselves in for nothing —*

*— do not wake us, child, do not wake us, we chose this once and we will choose it again —*

*— I was twenty years old. I was twenty years old when they closed the door. I want the sky. I want the sky. I have wanted the sky for six hundred years —*

*— forgive, forgive, forgiveness is the only door that opens both ways —*

*— my children are dust. My children turned to dust in my arms and you speak to me of forgiveness? —*

*— who carries our names now? Who remembers? If no one remembers then waking is only dying twice —*

Elodie pressed her palms flat against the carved wall, an instinctive gesture, the bone-carver's oldest habit — touch the dead to hear them better — and instantly understood her mistake. The contact was a key turned in ten thousand locks. The voices swelled, doubled, became a parliament in session, and she was the hall they convened in.

"*Elodie.*" Renn's hands were under her arms, hauling her back from the wall, his voice rough with a fear he'd never admit to. "What is it? What's happening?"

"They're—" She gagged on the words; there were too many of them, crowding her mouth like birds fighting over a single nest. "They're arguing. All of them. About whether—"

Whether to wake. Whether to forgive. Whether six hundred years of patience had been faith or folly. Whether the world above deserved them back. Whether vengeance or covenant or simple forgetting was owed to the living, who had built their kingdoms and their truce upon a sealed door and never once asked what was behind it.

She got the words out in pieces, gasping between them, and saw understanding dawn on Renn's face and curdle immediately into dread.

"Ten thousand ghosts having a quarrel," he said flatly. "Inside your head."

"Not a quarrel." Elodie laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "A *vote*. Can't you hear it? They're counting themselves."

And they were. That was the terrible wonder of it — beneath the fury and the grief, beneath the centuries of accumulated argument, the voices were organizing themselves the way water organizes itself downhill. Factions forming. Positions stated and restated. The eldest voices — she could feel them, vast and slow as bedrock — calling the assembly to some kind of order, and being ignored, and insisting anyway, patiently, the way mountains insist.

Ash knelt in the shallow water beside her and took her face in two small cold hands.

The moment the child touched her, something shifted in the flood. The voices did not quiet — but they *turned*. Elodie felt it happen, felt ten thousand attentions swing like a field of flowers following the sun, felt herself cease to be a vessel and become instead a window. A doorway. A place where two rooms shared a wall.

They could see Ash through her.

*The little one,* said a voice close to her ear, or inside it, there was no difference here. *The one who went out. The one we sent.*

*Ours. Ours, come home.*

*Sent to decide, they told it. Sent to carry the language past the door. Six hundred years, little messenger. What did you find?*

Ash's hands trembled against Elodie's cheeks. The child looked at her — really looked, gray eyes wide in the lantern-light, and Elodie realized with a lurch that Ash could hear them too, had perhaps always heard them more clearly than anyone, and had been carrying their silence the way other children carried stones in their pockets.

"I can't," Elodie whispered — to Ash, to the dead, to no one. "There are too many. I can't hold them all. I can't be fair to all of them."

Ash shook their head slowly. Then, deliberately, the child pressed one hand flat against the wet carved stone beside them, and the other hand against Elodie's chest, over her heart, and stood between the wall and the woman like a bridge between shores.

And began to sing.

* * *

It was not the melody from the blizzard night, though it grew from the same root. It was older, slower, structured — and Elodie understood, with the part of her mind still her own, that this was procedure. This was liturgy. This was how the Spire-folk had governed themselves before the sealing: not by decree, but by song, each verse a question put to the gathered voices, each refrain an invitation to answer.

The dead answered.

They answered in waves. Elodie felt each verse move through her like weather — first those who cried *wake*, their voices rising like a gale off the sea, sixty generations of suffocation given shape: *the sky, the sky, we were promised a return, we kept faith, why should we keep it longer —* Then those who cried *wait*: *the world that sealed us has not asked our pardon, waking without welcome is only another tomb with better light —* Then the faction she had not expected, the ones whose voices were softest and most terrible, the ones who sang of forgiveness: *we chose this. Do not forget that we chose this. We gave our bodies to end a war. Let the choice mean something. Let it be ours still.*

Renn held the lantern and did not run. Elodie noted that distantly, through the torrent — noted the smuggler standing hip-deep in a haunted river with his jaw set and his knuckles white on the handle, refusing to leave them, refusing even to look away. Whatever debt he believed he owed Ash's people, he was paying it here, in the only currency he had left: presence.

Verse after verse. Question after question. And through it all, Elodie felt the vote assembling itself inside her — not a shout, not a mob, but something patient and granular, thousands of judgments accreting like sediment, layer upon layer upon layer, until the weight of it threatened to crack her open.

*I can't carry this,* she tried to say again. But Ash's hand was steady on her chest, and through that small palm came something that was not words and was better than words: reassurance. *You don't carry it. You only hold the door. We pass through. That is all a door ever does.*

The singing slowed. Deepened. Elodie sensed the assembly narrowing itself, the way a river narrows toward a fall — the great slow voices of the eldest sleepers rising at last above the rest, the ones who had been alive when the door closed, whose memories reached back to green valleys and open gates and the taste of rain.

And then the dead spoke their verdict — not to her, she understood suddenly, but *through* her, addressed to the living air, to the child, to whoever above might still be listening:

*We do not agree.*

The verdict landed in Elodie's chest like a stone dropped into a well, and she nearly laughed at the absurdity of it, tears streaming down her face into the black water. Of course. Of course they didn't. Ten thousand souls entombed alive did not resolve six hundred years into a single tidy answer. There was no unanimous dead. There never had been.

*We do not agree,* the voices repeated, and the eldest among them took up the thread, and Elodie translated aloud, haltingly, her voice raw, because someone had to give the words breath:

"We do not agree. We have not agreed in six hundred years and we will not pretend agreement now. Some of us would rise tomorrow and burn the world that buried us. Some of us would sleep until the mountain itself crumbles rather than owe the living anything. Some of us forgive. Some of us cannot. Some of us—" and here her own voice broke, because the fragment that came through next was small and unbearably human, "*—some of us only want to know if the stars are still up there, and whether anyone up there ever wondered about us.*"

"But—" Renn started.

"Let me finish," Elodie whispered, and the dead finished for her:

*So we do not send a command. Commands are what sealed us. Instead we send a question, carried in our smallest voice, in the one who went out willing: ask the living what they will do with us if we wake. Ask them truly. Ask them with the door already opening. And whatever they answer —* the voices rose together here, all ten thousand of them, one sound at last, not agreement but accord, the accord of people choosing a process rather than an outcome — *whatever they answer, let it be spoken to our faces. No more decrees written over our sleeping heads. No more truces built on our bones without our consent. We were silenced once by those who feared our answer. We will not be silenced twice by those who fear our question.*

The last note faded down the galleries like water finding its level. The pressure lifted. Elodie sagged forward into the shallows, caught by Renn's arm, gasping, empty, scoured clean the way a beach is scoured by a storm — everything rearranged, nothing destroyed.

For a long moment there was only the drip of water and the gutter of the lantern flame and the three of them breathing in the dark.

"Well," Renn said finally, hoarse. "I've sat through worse parliaments."

Elodie laughed — a real one this time, broken and wet and human. She pulled Ash against her side and the child came willingly, small body shivering, and she pressed her lips to the damp hair and said nothing, because there were no words in either language adequate to what had just passed between them.

"You heard it?" she asked Ash softly. "All of it?"

Ash nodded. Then hesitated. Then, slowly, the child lifted one hand and traced shapes in the air — not the old tongue's flowing signs, but simpler, deliberate ones Elodie had learned on the road: *afraid.*

"What of?" Renn asked gently.

Ash looked back the way they had come, toward the distant, unseen light of the galleries' entrance. Toward the surface. Toward the world where kings wrote decrees and sergeants carried orders framed as mercy. Then deeper, toward the sealed heart, where the door waited.

The child made two more signs, unhurried, unmistakable:

*Both.*

Elodie's stomach tightened. Because she had heard it too, hadn't she, threaded through the great chorus — the one voice she had not repeated aloud, the one that had come not from the galleries but from beyond the sealed heart, faint as a heartbeat under a floor. The first sleeper. Still alive. Still waiting. And waiting, she suspected, not only for freedom.

Somewhere far above them, muffled by a hundred yards of stone, came a sound none of them could mistake: the ring of iron on rock, rhythmic and purposeful. Boots. Many boots, descending.

Renn snuffed the lantern without being asked. In the sudden dark, the name-carvings seemed to glow faintly, as if the dead had opinions about their visitors too.

"The vote's cast," Elodie breathed into the blackness, pulling Ash close. "Now we learn whether the living can answer it — before the Wardens reach the door."

The boots rang on. And down in the dark, ten thousand voices held their breath.
