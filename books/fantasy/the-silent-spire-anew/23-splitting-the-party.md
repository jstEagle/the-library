## 23. Splitting the Party

The dark came down on them like a held breath.

Renn Carrow had spent half his life in places where light was currency — smuggler's holds, sealed cellars, the black veins beneath Thornfell where a lantern was worth more than a knife. He knew how to read darkness. He knew its textures: the damp velvet of river caves, the dry rasp of old mines, the cold that pooled in stone like water finding its level.

But this was different. This was the Weeping Galleries, and the dark here had weight, had *witness*. The walls around them were carved with names — millions of them, he'd stopped counting at the third gallery, stopped being able to count because the carvings didn't stop and neither did the dead who owned them. The water that seeped through the limestone wept over each one, tracing letters with patient fingers, and every drop made a sound, and every sound was a voice, and all of them were pleading.

*Hear us. Remember us. Say us aloud.*

Renn had learned to walk through it without flinching, mostly. You couldn't answer a million voices; you'd go mad trying. So you did what Elodie did — you listened to the ones nearest, the ones with something urgent to say, and you let the rest wash past like rain on a roof.

Except now there were boots behind them. Many boots. And the voices in the walls had gone quiet, which was somehow worse than the pleading, because the dead only fell silent when something worse than death was coming.

"They've breached the upper galleries," Elodie said. Her voice came low and even from somewhere ahead of him in the dark, and Renn marveled, as he always did, at how steady she could make herself when everything else was shaking. "I can hear them through the stone. Corvane brought thirty men at least. Maybe more."

"Thirty." Renn turned the number over. Thirty Wardens in a flooded necropolis, hunting a child. It should have been absurd. It wasn't. The Crown had sent worse after less. "How far?"

"Two galleries up. They're moving fast — they have the mapmaker's route." She said the name without saying it, and Renn felt the old familiar heat rise in his chest, the one that lived just under his charm, the one that wanted to go back up those stairs and find Vessal Thorne and introduce him to the business end of his own compass. Later. If there was a later. "They'll reach the junction within the hour."

Ahead of them, Ash walked with one hand trailing the wet wall, fingers reading the carved names like a blind man reading scripture. The child hadn't spoken since the fighting started above. Hadn't sung. Just walked, small and silent, deeper into the mountain, toward the thing they'd come all this way to face.

Renn watched the small shoulders and thought about what it cost to carry a language no one alive could speak, a verdict no one alive had asked for, and a name — *the Decider*, the sleepers called Ash in their dreaming, though Elodie translated it gentler — that meant a whole buried people had put their last hope in someone barely tall enough to reach a door latch.

He thought about what he would do if he were the kind of man who ran.

He wasn't. He'd known that about himself for years, actually, and had done his best to keep it a secret, because a smuggler with convictions was a smuggler with a short ledger. But the knowledge sat in him now like a swallowed coal.

"Elodie." He caught up to her, touched her elbow in the dark. "There's a side passage ahead. I remember it from the survey maps — the old drainage cut, runs east toward the cisterns. Narrow. One person wide, maybe two pressed close. Wardens won't fit in armor."

She looked back at him. Even in the gloom he could see her eyes, grey as slate weather, and the question in them before she asked it.

"And you?"

"I'm going up," he said. "Toward them."

* * *

They stopped at the junction because they had to — the drainage cut went one way, the main gallery stair the other, and the dead in the walls had opinions about which door opened for whom. The voices here were louder, older, the carvings worn nearly smooth by centuries of water, and when Renn pressed his palm flat against the stone he felt the hum of them through his bones, a vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

Elodie heard what they said. She always did. Renn had watched her do it a hundred times now — tilt her head, close her eyes, let some dead stranger's last word move through her like wind through a reed. But this time her face changed in a way he hadn't seen before. Not grief. Not dread. Something almost like recognition.

"What is it?" he asked.

"The dead are arguing," she said. "About you."

"About me." He huffed out a laugh, mostly to keep his hands from shaking. "Never figured myself for ghost politics."

"They're saying—" She paused, listening harder, her brow furrowing. "One of them says *the thief with the honest hands*. Another says *he owes them still*. And an older voice, deep in the wall, says..." Her throat worked. "*Let him choose. Let him choose as we were never allowed to.*"

Renn stood very still. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, each drop landing on six hundred years of silence.

"Well," he managed. "No pressure, then."

Ash looked up at him. In the lantern light the child's face was pale and grave, and there was something in those dark eyes that Renn had been avoiding for weeks — a knowing, an oldness, the look of someone who had already counted the cost of things and found the arithmetic unbearable. Ash reached out and took his hand. Small fingers, cold from the cave air, gripping hard.

And then the child spoke. Not in the old tongue. In broken, halting, hard-won Common, the words Ash had been hoarding like coins, spending them now all at once:

"You come back. That is the rule. You come *back*."

Renn's chest did something complicated and painful. He knelt, because you didn't take a vow like that standing over someone, you took it eye to eye.

"That's the rule," he agreed. "You hear me, little sparrow? I don't break rules I make for other people. Ask anyone I've robbed."

"That is not comforting," Elodie said dryly, but her voice cracked in the middle of it, and Renn loved her a little more for the crack.

He rose. And here was the thing he'd learned about farewells, the thing the road had taught him across twenty years of leaving: you never said goodbye. Goodbye was a door closing. Goodbye was permission to grieve. What you said instead was logistics. Next steps. Practicalities. You kept the future open by refusing to mark its edge.

So he didn't say goodbye. He said: "Elodie. The cistern route joins the Spire approach below the sealed galleries. There's a choke point — a collapsed stair, maybe forty yards of crawl. If they follow you down, that's where you hold it. One blade in a crawlspace is worth ten in a hall. Take my knife." He unbuckled it, the good one, Thornfell steel with the bone grip he'd carved himself in another life, and pressed it into her hands. "And take this." A folded square of oilcloth from inside his coat. "Flint, striker, three candles. Don't argue. You'll need light more than I will — I'm going to be making plenty of noise, and noise draws torches."

"You're giving away your fire," she said softly.

"I'm lending it." He tapped the oilcloth. "Interest due on return."

She took it. Their fingers touched around the bundle, briefly, and neither of them made it mean anything, because meaning it would have undone them both, and there wasn't time, and anyway everyone in these galleries already knew. The dead knew. The stone knew. Probably the water knew. Only the two of them were still pretending, and the pretending was itself a kind of tenderness, the last courtesy they could afford each other.

"The signal," he said. "If you reach the Spire heart, sing. Ash sings, I mean — anything in the old tongue carries down these galleries like a bell rope. I'll hear it. Or the dead will tell me. Apparently they're taking an interest." He glanced at the humming walls. "First time in my career I've had informants in the masonry."

"Renn." Elodie's voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet before she said something true. "Whatever happens—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, smiling, and the smile cost him more than the knife had. "Don't eulogize me while I'm standing here breathing. Bad luck. Terrible manners. I'll haunt you for it."

She didn't smile back. She stepped forward instead and kissed him — quick, fierce, tasting of cave-cold and candle smoke and every unsaid thing between them — and then stepped back before either of them could drown in it.

"For luck," she said. "Not goodbye."

"Not goodbye," he agreed, and his voice held, mostly.

Then he turned to Ash, and crouched one more time, and pressed his forehead briefly against the child's, the way he'd seen the river-folk do, the way he'd seen Elodie do, the greeting that meant *you are mine to worry about*.

"Decide well, sparrow," he whispered. "That's all any of this is for. Not the running, not the fighting. The deciding."

Ash's hand came up and touched his cheek, once, solemn as a benediction. Then the child turned and walked into the dark beside Elodie, small boots splashing, growing smaller, until the drainage cut swallowed them and the lantern glow shrank to a star and then to nothing.

Renn stood alone in the Weeping Galleries with a million names and no light but the memory of one.

* * *

He gave them a quarter hour. Long enough to clear the junction. Short enough that the Wardens' torchlight would still be bleeding down the main stair when he moved toward it — because that was the whole point, that was the entire architecture of the plan: light attracts light. Thirty men underground fixate on whatever moves in front of them. Give them a figure in the dark, give them a reason to shout, and they'd funnel themselves down the gallery like water down a chute, and every man who followed him was a man not following Elodie and Ash.

It was, he reflected grimly, exactly the sort of plan he'd have charged a client double for and then cheated on anyway. Simple, brutal, dependent entirely on him being interesting enough to chase and fast enough to survive it.

He'd been interesting before. Fast was newer.

He climbed the main stair with his boots in his hand, barefoot on wet stone, silent as a debt collector. The galleries narrowed as he rose, the carvings thickening, the names crowding closer together until the walls seemed less like stone than like flesh with writing on it. The dead grew louder here — or rather, they grew *nearer*, their pleas losing the distant ocean-roar of the lower reaches and becoming individual, distinct, terrible.

*My name was Sefa. My name was Sefa. Say it.*

*I built the third arch. I built the third arch and no one wrote it down.*

*We voted to stay. Tell them we voted. Tell them we chose—*

That last one stopped him. A voice, deep and cracked, repeating itself with the patience of the long-buried: *we chose, we chose, we chose.* Renn pressed his hand to the wall, an instinct he'd learned from Elodie, and felt the hum jump against his palm like a startled bird.

"We chose," he murmured back, not knowing why, knowing it mattered. "I heard you. I'll carry it."

The hum settled. Somewhere in the stone, something unclenched.

He was beginning to understand, in the wordless way you understand things in dreams, that the Galleries had been waiting six hundred years for exactly this — not rescue, not waking, but *witness*. Someone to walk these halls and say yes, we see, we hear, it happened, it was real, you were real. The Crown had sealed the Spire to end a war. The Wardens patrolled it to keep a secret. But the dead down here didn't want freedom the way the living understood it. They wanted acknowledgment. They wanted their choosing counted.

Renn Carrow, smuggler, liar, debtor, was apparently going to be the one to count it. Life had a sense of humor. He'd always suspected.

The torchlight appeared above him then — a wavering orange stain on the wet ceiling of the next gallery, growing, resolving into shapes. Boots. Armor. The particular cadence of Warden march, disciplined, joyless, the sound of men who had been told what they were doing was mercy so many times they'd begun to believe it.

Renn put his boots back on. Some entrances deserved ceremony.

He waited until the lead torch rounded the corner, until he could see faces — young, mostly, boys in Warden grey with their mothers' chins and the Crown's orders — and then he stepped into the light, arms loose, sword deliberately left sheathed, wearing his best roguish grin like a mask over a man doing arithmetic with his own life.

"Evening, gentlemen," he announced cheerfully. "Terrible night for it. Mind the locals — they bite."

The shout went up immediately, exactly as designed. *Contact! Man on the stair!* Torches swung toward him. Crossbows leveled. And Renn Carrow turned and ran, deeper into the mountain, away from the drainage cut, away from Elodie and Ash, drawing thirty armed men after him like a lure drawn through dark water.

Behind him, in the walls, a million dead names began — very softly, very slowly, in a language older than kingdoms — to sing.

* * *

He led them a long way. Through the Gallery of Masons, where the carvings showed hands at work, tools and trestles and the proud inscriptions of builders who'd never known their tower would become a tomb. Through the Gallery of Mothers, where the names were smaller, clustered, children's names tucked under women's names like chicks under wings, and Renn had run faster through that one, jaw tight, because some rooms you didn't linger in even at spearpoint.

Through the Hall of the Vote, vast and columned, where the floor itself was carved — he saw it by torchlight as he sprinted across it — with tens of thousands of tally marks in neat rows, and understood in one lurching instant what they were. Ballots. Six hundred years old. The Spire-folk had *voted* to stay. Had stood in this hall and chosen entombment over war, generation after generation renewing the choice, carving their assent into the floor with their own hands, and the Crown had called it a prison and the Wardens had called it a seal and nobody, in six centuries, had called it what it was.

A covenant. Kept by the very people it was used to imprison.

*Tell them we chose,* the walls sang as he ran. *Tell them we chose.*

"I'm telling them!" he shouted back, breath tearing. "Hold your stones!"

The Wardens were falling behind — he'd chosen the route well, narrow galleries where armor snagged and torches guttered, and half their number had dropped off to search side passages, which meant half their number was now lost in a necropolis full of singing walls, which meant morale was about to become a casualty. But eight or ten still came, led by a lieutenant with a scarred jaw and a voice like a slammed door, and they were gaining on the straight stretch, and Renn's lungs were filing formal complaints.

He ducked into the passage he'd been saving. The old ventilation shaft — a crack in the mountain, barely shoulder-width, rising at a cruel angle, the kind of climb that separated athletes from corpses. He went in on his belly, elbows and knees, stone scraping his back, and behind him the pursuit hit the entrance and stalled with satisfying curses.

But not for long. He heard the lieutenant ordering men in after him, one at a time, and knew the arithmetic had turned ugly: he couldn't out-climb armored men forever, and the shaft had no exit he knew of except the one the mapmaker's map had promised — and Vessal Thorne's maps had a habit of promising beautiful lies.

Halfway up, wedged in the dark, chest heaving, Renn did something he hadn't done since he was nine years old and his father's boat went down off the Teeth with all hands.

He prayed. Not to any god — he'd never met one he trusted — but to the dead. To the million names in the walls below, to Sefa and the mother-voices and the deep cracked elder who kept saying *we chose*.

"Look," he panted into the stone. "I know I owe most of you a name spoken aloud, and I'm good for it, I swear I'm good for it. But I need a bit of credit now. Buy me an hour. One hour, and Elodie and Ash reach the Spire heart, and everything we bled for counts for something. Do that, and I'll come back down there and say every name I can carry. Every one. On my mother's grave, which — fair warning — is also somebody's gallery by now."

The shaft hummed.

Not the distant hum of the lower galleries. Here, now, against his ribs, through the stone he was crawling over — a vibration like a great bell struck once, far off, gathering itself. And from somewhere below, faint but unmistakable, threading up through six hundred feet of mountain, came a thread of melody in the old tongue.

High. Clear. Child-sized.

Ash was singing.

Either they'd reached the Spire heart, or they were close enough that it didn't matter, and either way the sound went through Renn like a lance of clean water — hope with an edge on it. He laughed, breathless and disbelieving, and hauled himself up the shaft faster, hands bleeding, heart hammering out a rhythm to match the song.

Below him, the first Warden entered the shaft, torchlight flickering up the crack like a predator's eye.

Above him, the mountain hummed, and the dead — who had waited six hundred years for someone to ask — began, quietly, patiently, to clear the way.

Renn climbed toward the singing, and did not look back.
