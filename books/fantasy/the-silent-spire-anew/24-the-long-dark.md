## 24. The Long Dark

The stair went down the way grief goes down: without end, without light, and always one step further than you thought you could bear.

Elodie counted the first hundred steps because counting was a thing her hands could do when her mind wanted to fly apart. After that she lost the numbers, or the numbers lost her, and there was only the descent — the spiral of black stone worn smooth in its center by feet that had climbed it six centuries ago and never come back up. Her palm rode the wall, and the wall was cold in a way stone should not be cold, cold like the inside of a closed mouth.

Behind them, above them — it was impossible to tell direction in this vertical dark — the sounds of pursuit came muffled through rock. Boots on stone. A shout bent strange by the galleries' acoustics. Once, the flat crack of a crossbow loosed at something that was not them, followed by Renn's voice, distant and profane and gloriously alive, doing what Renn did best: making noise so that others could be quiet.

He had drawn them off alone. He had kissed her knuckles — the scarred right hand, the one that held the carving knife — and said *finish it*, and walked back toward the torchlight with his knife already out, wearing that crooked grin he wore when he intended to lie to death itself about whose side it was on.

She had not said goodbye. Neither had he. Goodbye was a word for endings, and they had both silently agreed not to give the mountain the satisfaction.

"Mother-of-voices." Ash's whisper came from just below her, small in the vast dark. "Hold my hand. Please."

She found the child's fingers in the blackness — thin, cold, gripping hers with the desperate strength of someone who had once let go of something and never forgiven themselves. She thought of all the hands she had carved from bone over the years, elegant and lifeless, perfect in their stillness, and how none of them had ever been worth this one warm imperfect grip.

"I have you," she said.

They descended.

* * *

There were lamps.

Not lit ones — not anymore. But every thirty paces a niche opened in the wall, and in each niche sat a lamp of greenish glass, and behind the glass floated a soft phosphorescent ghost-light, dying by inches after six hundred years. They gave barely enough glow to see the next three steps. It was like walking inside a dying breath, one exhalation at a time, down into the lungs of the mountain.

Ash touched each lamp as they passed. Wherever those small fingers brushed the glass, the light flared — briefly, gratefully, like an old dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep — and then dimmed again as they moved on. Elodie watched this happen four times before she understood what she was seeing.

"They know you," she whispered.

"They know the song," Ash corrected softly. "I am only the last one who remembers it whole."

The old language sat differently in Ash's mouth now than it had that first night in the workshop, when the child had hummed melodies that made finished carvings whisper. Then it had been music without meaning, water running over stones. Now each syllable landed with weight, deliberate, chosen — a key being fitted to locks one by one as they descended.

And the bones around them answered.

That was the thing Elodie could not make her body stop reacting to. The walls of the stair were carved — she felt it under her palm more than saw it, millions of fine lines cut into the living rock — and every line was a name. She knew names in stone the way she knew voices in bone; it was the same sense, the gift that had been her livelihood and her prison both, and here it was drowning her. The Weeping Galleries above had been a necropolis of the silenced, pleading. This was deeper. Older. Quieter. The dead down here were not pleading.

They were waiting. And waiting, done well for six hundred years, has its own terrible gravity.

*What do you carry?* The question pressed against her skull from a thousand directions, gentle as snowfall. Not words, exactly. Weight. Intention. The way she had learned to read intent through chisel and bone.

"A child," she answered aloud, because silence felt like lying. "A child of yours. Going home."

The pressure changed. She couldn't have described how, except that it was the difference between a crowd watching a stranger and a crowd watching one of their own come up the road. Something eased. Something else sharpened.

Below them, very far below but rising, came a sound that stopped Elodie's heart for two full beats.

Singing.

Faint, vast, many-voiced, rising up the shaft of the stair like heat from a forge — the sleepers, singing in their sleep, or whatever passed for sleep after six centuries sealed alive behind a door carved from the mountain's own vertebra. The sound was beautiful the way a flood is beautiful. It harmonized with Ash's remembered fragments perfectly, call and answer across centuries, and Elodie realized with a lurch that the child had been hearing this song their entire journey — had perhaps been *humming along to it since the blizzard night*, half-heard instructions from a home that had never stopped calling.

Ash had gone rigid on the step below her.

"No," the child breathed. It was almost a whimper. "No no no—"

"Ash." Elodie crouched, awkward on the narrow stair, and took both the small shoulders in her hands. In the guttering lamp-light the child's face was grey, eyes huge, and Elodie saw for the first time not the messenger, not the chosen carrier of a language, but exactly what Ash had been all along: a child sent out alone into a world that wanted them dead. "Talk to me. Now. While we still can."

For a long moment Ash only shook, and Elodie held on, and the pursuit noises far above seemed suddenly very far away compared to the trembling under her palms.

Then it came out of the child in a rush, in Elodie's tongue, stumbling, the words clearly rehearsed a hundred times in secret and never dared aloud:

"When I touch the door — when I sing the opening verse — they will wake. All of them. And they will be angry, mother-of-voices, they will be so *angry*, six hundred years of angry, and I—" The breath hitched. "I left. Do you understand? Everyone stayed. Everyone was brave and stayed in the dark together, and they chose me, they gave me the whole language to carry, and I ran. I ran and I ate warm food and slept under roofs and learned your words and someone combed my hair and I—" Ash's voice broke apart entirely. When it reassembled, it was smaller. "They will ask me why I got to leave. And I will not have an answer that is kind."

The lamps flickered. Down the shaft, the sleeper-song swelled and subsided like a tide deciding.

Elodie pulled the child against her chest and held on, one hand cradling the back of Ash's head, the other pressed flat against the name-carved wall as though bracing the whole mountain against collapse.

"You want to know what the dead tell me," she said into the child's hair. "In sixty carvings a year, for fifteen years. Every grief-service, every coin, every widow's finger-bone and drowned son's jaw. Do you want to know the thing they say most?"

Ash nodded against her shoulder.

"*I wish I had been allowed to go.*" She said it plainly, without ornament, because the dead deserved plainness. "That is the sentence I carve into air a hundred times a year, Ash. The dead do not envy the ones who stayed. They grieve for the ones who weren't permitted to leave. If any of them ever got a door, do you know what they would say to whoever walked out of it?" She drew back and took the small face in her hands. "They would say *run*. They would say *live enough for all of us*. That is not cowardice. That is the errand."

"But what if they hate me anyway?" A whisper. "What if being angry is bigger than being glad?"

Elodie considered lying. She was good at lying; she had lied beautifully to Warden-Sergeant Corvane in her own doorway while Ash hid in the flour bin. But the walls around her were carved with a million honest names, and the dead consulted her now rather than haunting her, and some part of that covenant had gotten into her blood.

"Then they will be wrong," she said, "and they will have six hundred years of anger to blame it on, and you will stand there shaking and tell them the truth anyway. Because that is what courage is, Ash. It is not the absence of the shaking. I have been shaking since the day I burned my workshop, and I will probably shake until the day I die. Courage is walking forward while you shake. Nothing more heroic than that has ever existed."

Ash looked up at her. In the ghost-lamplight the child's eyes were wet and fierce at once, and something in them settled — not stilled, nothing about this child would ever fully still, but *settled*, like sediment finding the bottom of a jar.

"Walk forward while shaking," Ash repeated, tasting the words in both tongues. Then, with the devastating practicality of children everywhere: "Your hand is shaking too. I can feel it."

"Yes," Elodie said, and laughed despite everything, a wet cracked laugh that echoed strangely down six centuries of stair. "Well. Come on, then. We'll shake together. It's less frightening in company."

* * *

They went down.

The stair widened as it deepened, as though the mountain were opening its throat. The niches grew grander — glass lamps became bronze lanterns became great sculpted braziers shaped like sleeping birds, wings folded over extinguished hearts. The carvings on the wall grew denser too, and Elodie began, horribly, to recognize things among them.

Small handprints. Pressed into the stone beside certain names, hundreds of them, thousands, each print no larger than a child's.

She stopped at one and put her own palm against it. It fit almost exactly.

"The ones who were too young to carry the language out," she said. Her voice sounded foreign to her, scraped raw. "They stayed anyway. Even knowing."

Ash stood very still beside her. Then the child reached out and laid one small hand over the ancient print, fitting finger to finger across the centuries, and sang — quietly, three notes only, a fragment of the sleeper-song turned gentle — and Elodie felt the wall *answer*. Not words. Warmth. The faintest diffusion of heat through cold stone, like a hand returning a squeeze.

Above them, faintly, came a new sound threaded through the pursuit-noises: shouting, but different shouting. Renn's voice raised not in defiance but in declaration — and beneath it, unmistakable even distorted by rock, the crisp cadence of Warden-Sergeant Corvane barking orders that were, for the first time in that man's long career, *hesitant*.

Something was happening up there. Something involving Renn alive and talking too much, which meant either rescue or catastrophe, and possibly both.

"We don't have long," Elodie said.

"I know." Ash's hand lingered one heartbeat longer on the small stone print. "Goodbye," the child whispered to it — in the old tongue, and then, deliberately, in Elodie's language, as if translating were itself a kind of prayer. "Not forever. Just for now. I'm bringing help."

They descended faster.

* * *

The stair ended.

It ended the way rivers end, not abruptly but by becoming something else — the steps flattening, spreading, opening into a gallery so vast that the ghost-lamps along its walls were stars in a buried sky. The ceiling was lost in darkness overhead. The floor was smooth black stone polished by ages of bare feet, and it sloped gently downward toward the thing at the center, the thing the whole mountain had been built around, the reason for every name on every wall.

The door.

Even from a hundred yards Elodie felt it the way she felt the voices in bone — except this was no whisper, no plea. This was a presence, vast and patient and *breathing*. Slow. Enormous. One breath every minute, maybe less, the respiration of something that had learned to need almost nothing and had been needing it for six hundred years.

The door filled the end of the gallery floor to unseen ceiling: a single slab of stone the color of old ivory, carved from one piece of the mountain's spine as the histories swore, and across its face ran carvings unlike anything in the galleries above. Not names here. Here it was a story — figures ascending stairs, figures sealed behind lines, one small figure slipping out through a crack at the bottom, carrying something bright and tiny away into the dark.

Ash stopped ten paces from it, as if the air thickened there, and Elodie stopped with the child, her heart slamming against her ribs hard enough to hurt.

"It knows we're here," Ash whispered.

"I know," Elodie said. "I can hear it."

Because she could. Beneath the breathing, beneath the weight of six centuries, a single voice was reaching for her — not through carved bone, not through her chisel and her craft, but directly, the way sunlight reaches through a window. The first sleeper. The eldest. And the voice was saying something over and over, gentle and broken, and it took Elodie three full attempts to translate it even partially, because it was not asking for freedom, not demanding vengeance, not cursing the world above.

It was asking, in a language older than any kingdom, in a tone that undid her completely:

*Who is willing? Who has come willing? Six hundred years, and is anyone finally willing?*

Elodie Voss, who had spent fifteen years keeping every door of her life locked, who had burned her own home rather than open it, stood in the deepest dark in the world holding a shaking child's hand, and understood that the shaking had never once stopped her from arriving where she was needed.

"Yes," she said aloud, to the door, to the voice, to the million names on the walls. "We came willing. Both of us."

Far above, boots thundered onto the top of the stair. Torches bloomed in the high dark like a wound opening. And Ash lifted their chin, wiped their face with one sleeve, took a breath that shuddered all the way through, and stepped forward — shaking, walking forward anyway — and began to sing.
