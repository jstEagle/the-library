## 25. Renn's Last Road

The blow that put him down came from behind, which was the only reason it landed.

Renn Carrow had counted six Wardens in the torchlight — no, seven, the seventh hanging back by the gallery mouth with a crossbow he clearly didn't want to use — and seven was a number Renn could have handled if handling meant running. Running had always been his first language. But the galleries were flooded to his knees here, black water swallowing sound, and Elodie and Ash had already gone down the far stair with nothing between them and the Wardens but him.

So he'd turned around and made himself loud instead.

"Oi!" he'd bellowed, arms spread, standing in the shallows like a man greeting a ship. "Which one of you fine iron-clad gentlemen wants to lose to a smuggler? Line up. Take turns."

It had bought them perhaps forty heartbeats. It had also gotten him a Warden pommel across the back of the skull, and now here he was: face down in cold water, someone's boot on his neck, tasting stone and old copper and his own blood, and thinking, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, *well. This is where the road runs out.*

Except it wasn't. He knew it wasn't. That was the strange thing, the thing sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal: for the first time in thirty-one years of lying, cheating, running, and surviving, Renn Carrow knew exactly what happened next, and it wasn't him escaping.

*Get up anyway,* said a voice in his head that sounded annoyingly like Elodie Voss. *You're not done.*

He got up anyway. They let him, mostly because they wanted him upright to drag.

* * *

They bound his wrists with cord and marched him back up through the Weeping Galleries, and Renn used every step of the climb to do what he did best, which was count.

Seven Wardens. Six names he could read off their pauldrons as they passed torch-sconces: Halloway, a broad woman who kept flexing her sword hand like she regretted holding it; Petrin, young, jaw still soft, eyes too wide; the twins Doss and Merrick, who moved like mirror-images and hadn't spoken since the ambush; Osk, gray-bearded, methodical, the kind of man who checked knots twice; and Sergeant Vael, lean and humorless, walking point. The seventh — the crossbow — was named Brandt, because he'd whispered an apology when they cuffed Renn, and Renn had made a point of asking his name in return. Names mattered. Names were levers. A man without a name couldn't be turned, but a man with one was just a person wearing armor, and persons could be talked to.

And then there was Corvane.

Warden-Sergeant Corvane walked at the rear of the column like a funeral he was conducting for himself. Renn had met hard men all his life — had been one, on his worst days — but Corvane was something rarer and worse: a believer whose belief had recently begun to cost him. Renn had seen it at Greyford Bridge, in the half-second the sergeant had stood looking at Ash's face before giving the order to fire. He'd seen it again in the way Corvane interrogated the captured smuggler back at the waystation, the questions precise but somehow reluctant, a man reading from a book he no longer liked.

Renn had built his whole life on knowing what people wanted. Corvane wanted to be right. And somewhere between Thornfell and the Galleries, Renn would have wagered his last coin, Corvane had started to suspect he wasn't.

That suspicion was the only weapon left in the world, and Renn intended to swing it like a hammer.

They stopped in a wide chamber where the ceiling wept in slow silver threads and the walls rose up into carvings — names, thousands of names, cut so deep into the stone that the torchlight caught each letter's shadow separately, so the whole wall seemed to breathe. The Wardens' torches made the names dance. Renn watched young Petrin look up at them and go pale.

"Sit him there," Corvane said.

They sat him against a fallen pillar, and Corvane crouched in front of him, unhurried, pulling off one gauntlet finger by finger. Up close the man looked tired in a way that went past the body. There was ink on his fingers — actual ink, the stain of a man who'd been reading documents by candlelight, not sleeping.

"Carrow," Corvane said. "We meet again."

"Third time." Renn grinned through split lips, because grinning through split lips was a skill, too. "You're getting rougher. First time you just burned my cache. Second time your lads shot at me over a bridge. Now this. A man could take it personally."

"A man could stop smuggling children past Crown checkpoints."

"Ah, see, there it is." Renn shifted against the pillar, testing the cord. Tight. Osk's work, obviously. "You say 'smuggling' like I'm moving contraband brandy. What exactly is the cargo, Sergeant? Say it plainly. I'm curious what word you've trained yourself to use."

Corvane's eyes flicked — just once, just briefly — toward the wall of names.

"There," Renn said softly. "You couldn't even look at me while you thought about it. That's interesting."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *always* stalling. Stalling is my profession. But stall toward what, that's the question, isn't it?" He leaned forward as much as the cords allowed. "You're going to ask me where they went. Down-stairs, deep stairs, the sealed heart. And I'm going to tell you, eventually, because you'll make me, or because I'll decide to, and either way you'll go down there and find a child standing in front of a door older than your Crown, and then somebody's orders are going to get carried out. So before that happens, Sergeant — before you're standing in front of that door with your blade out — let me ask you something you can answer honestly, since nobody's watching but ghosts."

He nodded upward at the carved wall.

"Do you know what's written there?"

* * *

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. The water dripped. One of the twins coughed. Somewhere above, faint as memory, the mountain hummed — that low old-tongue resonance that had followed them for days, the sound Renn had privately decided was the Spire dreaming.

"They're names," Petrin said quietly, before anyone told him to shut up. "Sir. Are those — are those all—"

"Burial records," Vael said flatly. "Plague dead. Old war dead. Doesn't matter."

"It matters," Renn said, and heard his own voice come out wrong — come out level, come out serious, stripped of the charm he wore like a second coat. Several heads turned at the change. Good. Let them look. "That's the lie, right there. That sentence. *Doesn't matter.* You've all been carrying that sentence your whole careers like a coin you never spend. Well. I spent some time lately with a man who reads decrees — original ones, seal-era parchment, the founding instruments of your order — and I'll tell you what's written on these walls, Sergeant. Not plague dead. Not war dead. These are the people your truce buried alive."

"Enough," Corvane said.

"Six hundred years ago," Renn went on, relentless now, watching faces the way he watched card tables, "your Crown ended a war by sealing an entire living nation under their own tower. Not killing them — killing makes martyrs, martyrs make wars. Sealing. Entombing. Writing a decree that says the peace holds as long as the seal holds, and putting the enforcement of that in the hands of men with good boots and bad consciences. Every Warden oath ever sworn traces back to it. You're not soldiers. You're *locksmiths.*"

Vael's hand had gone to her hilt. Osk put a hand on her arm — not restraining, exactly. Considering.

"And how," Corvane said, very quietly, "does a smuggler come to read seal-era decrees?"

"Because your own archive has a leak, and the leak has a price, and I pay my debts." Half true, which was the best kind of true. "But here's the part you actually need, Corvane, the part I'd trade you straight across this floor if you'd unbind my hands: clause nine. The covenant clause. Your founders weren't idiots — monstrous, yes, but not idiots. They knew seals fail, they knew locks get picked, they knew someday a voice of the Spire-folk might come home on its own two feet. So they wrote it into the law itself. Read it. It's short. *The seal may be opened, without breach of truce, should a voice of theirs return willing.*"

Silence. Even the dripping seemed to hold its breath.

"That's not—" Petrin started.

"It is," Corvane said.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't moved. His bare hand lay open on his knee, and Renn watched him stare at it as though it belonged to someone else.

"You've read it," Renn said. It wasn't a question anymore. "When? Recently?"

"Investigating reports." Corvane's voice was dry as old paper. "A burned workshop. Old-tongue singing. A child." He looked up, and his eyes found Renn's with terrible steadiness. "I pulled the original instrument four days ago. Clause nine exists. It has never once been cited. Do you know why?"

"Because citing it means admitting everything else."

"Because every officer who learned of it ruled it *inoperative.*" The word came out of him like a tooth. "'Inoperative pending threat assessment.' For six hundred years, Carrow. Generation after generation. Men like me finding the clause and burying it again, each of us certain we were the first to be reasonable about it." He stood. He looked at his Wardens, and Renn saw him make the decision to stop lying to them at roughly the same moment they saw it. "Clause nine is operative. It has always been operative. Our standing orders regarding the child are unlawful under our own founding law."

"Sergeant." Vael stepped forward, and her voice had an edge Renn recognized from back-alley negotiations gone sour. "You're tired. You've been in the field too long. Those documents—"

"Are in my saddlebag, Vael. In facsimile, with provenance. Check them yourself. That's an order." He said it without heat, and that was the frightening thing — the absence of heat where there should have been fire. "Check them tonight, all of you, and then tell me what we are."

* * *

They didn't check them that night. They argued.

Renn sat bound against his pillar through three hours of it, and it was the strangest night of his life, stranger than any heist, any escape, any fever-dream: seven Wardens of the Crown of Thornfell arguing theology in a necropolis while the prisoner listened. Vael held the line of duty longest — *orders are orders, the alternative is chaos, we don't get to choose* — and Renn let her run herself out before he spoke again, because a man convinced against his will is of the same opinion still, but a woman who runs out of arguments in front of a wall of ten thousand murdered names is another matter.

"Vael," he said finally, gently, and she rounded on him like a wolf.

"You don't speak."

"I know. Indulge me. One question, and then I'll be a good quiet criminal." He waited until she gave a curt nod. "Your oath. The one you swore at your commissioning. Recite the last line of it."

Her jaw worked. She didn't answer.

"I'll save you the trouble," Renn said. "*To guard the people of the Crown, wheresoever they dwell.* Wheresoever. Funny phrasing for a lock-smith's guild, isn't it? Somebody wrote that line hoping you'd remember it someday." He tipped his head back against the pillar, at the breathing wall of names. "Every soul in this rock was a person of the Crown, Vael. Nobody guarded them. Here's your chance to guard somebody. Costs you nothing but a comfortable lie."

She didn't answer. But she stopped arguing, and she sat down apart from the others with her sword across her knees, and she didn't look at Renn with hatred anymore. She looked at him like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Petrin came over near midnight with water and hard bread and knelt to feed it to him, awkward, avoiding his eyes.

"You knew about the clause," the boy said. "Before tonight."

"I knew about it the way everyone knows about things they don't want to be true. Heard it, filed it away, hoped somebody official would deal with it." Renn chewed, drank, felt the blood taste fade. "Difference between us, Petrin, is I ran out of places to file it."

"Why are you telling us?" The boy's voice dropped. "You're the prisoner. Every hour you keep talking is an hour they gain. Why help us catch them?"

Renn looked down the dark throat of the stair where Elodie and Ash had vanished days ago — hours ago, time down there meant nothing — and found, to his complete lack of surprise, that he didn't want to lie.

"Because I owe their people," he said simply. "Debt of mine. Old one. Can't pay it in coin." He smiled, and for once it wasn't armor. "Never thought I'd pay it by getting beaten senseless and lecturing the law. Life's funny that way."

Petrin lingered, then said, very low: "Brandt won't shoot. If it comes to that. I've seen him aim all week — he aims at the ground beside whatever he's aiming at." The boy stood quickly, embarrassed by his own treachery, and walked back to the fire.

Renn closed his eyes and added it to the ledger. Vael, neutralized by arithmetic. Petrin, turned. Brandt, already turned and ashamed of it. Halloway, uncertain, flexing her hand. Osk, checking knots twice — a careful man, and careful men recheck their beliefs too, given cause. The twins, unknown. Corvane—

Corvane he couldn't count yet. Corvane was the whole game.

* * *

Near dawn, Corvane came and sat across from him, alone, having dismissed the others to the upper galleries. The torches had burned low. The names on the walls had gone soft and shadowed, less like writing than like breathing.

"The stair below," Corvane said. "How deep?"

"Deep enough that you'll want to decide who you are before you reach the bottom."

"You understand I could simply have you dragged down ahead of us. As a shield."

"You could." Renn rolled his shoulder, wincing where the pommel-blow had stiffened it. "But then you'd be the man who does that, and you'd have to live in that uniform forever. Whereas if you walk down those stairs and stand in front of that door and hear what's on the other side of it — Sergeant, it's *breathing.* Did you know that? Six hundred years sealed, and it breathes. Whatever your superiors told you is in there, whatever you've told yourself — it's alive, it's waiting, and a child is standing in front of it right now deciding whether the rest of history happens well or badly. The only question in the world worth asking tonight is whether you arrive as a locksmith or a Warden."

Corvane was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had dropped its officer's cadence entirely, and what remained underneath was just a tired man in the dark.

"When I was a boy in Harlow Fen," he said, "there was a woman the village called mad, who sang in a tongue nobody knew. The Wardens took her when I was nine. I remember being proud of them." He laughed once, without humor. "I've been proud of the wrong things for a very long time, Carrow."

"I know the feeling. I was proud of never needing anybody."

"What happened?"

Renn thought of Elodie burning her workshop and walking away from the smoke without looking back. Thought of Ash asleep against her side in the boat, small and fierce as an ember. Thought of a bridge, and a choice, and staying.

"Met people worth failing," he said. "Changes a man."

Corvane studied him for a moment longer, then reached out and — deliberately, so the watching shadows of the upper gallery could see it — drew his knife and cut Renn's bonds.

"Don't mistake this," the sergeant said, standing. "You're still a criminal, and you're still in custody. But you'll walk at my side, unrestrained, because I find I trust a man better when he could run and doesn't." He paused at the head of the stair, torch raised, the descending dark drinking its light. "And because if what's down there is what you claim, I'll need a translator of a different kind than the child. Someone who speaks smuggler. Someone who speaks *lie.*"

"Flatterer."

"Down, Carrow."

Renn rubbed his wrists, flexed the feeling back into his fingers, and looked down the stair into the deep. Far below — so far it might have been imagination, might have been the mountain itself — he could feel a vibration coming up through the wet stone, through the soles of his boots, through the bones the Wardens hadn't broken. A hum. A gathering. Thousands upon thousands of voices converging somewhere beneath the earth on a single sealed door, and behind it, fainter than everything, steady as a heartbeat:

Breathing.

"Well then," Renn said to no one, and started down.
