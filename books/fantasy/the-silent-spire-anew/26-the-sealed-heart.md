## 26. The Sealed Heart

The last stair was not a stair.

It had been a stair once, Elodie thought — a grand one, wide enough for twenty to walk abreast, its treads carved with spirals that caught the light of Ash's small flame and threw it back in slow, drowning ripples. But six centuries of weeping water had worn the center of each step into a smooth trough, a groove of descent polished by the feet of the dead walking down and never walking up. She put her palm against the wall to steady herself and felt the names there, thousands upon thousands of them, cut so fine they felt less like stone and more like cloth, like the nap of velvet rubbed the wrong way. They pressed against her skin the way words press against a closed mouth.

*We are here,* said the wall. *We are here. We are here.*

Not whispering anymore. Not pleading anymore, either. Something past both — the low, patient insistence of rain on a roof, of roots against a foundation. A sound that had made its peace with being endless.

"Elodie." Ash's voice, from three steps below. The child had spoken her name in the common tongue only since the galleries began, as if the old language had burned away whatever remained of their silence, leaving ordinary speech behind like ash after fire. "It is close now. Can you feel it?"

She could. She had been feeling it since the second gallery, a pressure behind her sternum that she had first mistaken for her wound pulling — Renn's knife-work on her shoulder at the river-folk's camp had been quick and clean, but the bruised muscle remembered — and then understood for what it was when the pressure began keeping time with her heartbeat. Something below was breathing. Something below was breathing *in rhythm with her*, and she did not think it was coincidence. The seal had held for six hundred years by listening, perhaps. By matching itself to every living thing that came near, learning the shape of the living so it could better hold back the not-living.

Or holding in something that was neither. She would know soon.

"I feel it," she said.

Ash nodded once — that grave, economical nod that had replaced, over these long weeks, all the fidgeting of an ordinary child — and turned, and went down. Elodie followed the small flame and the smaller figure carrying it, and tried not to count how few steps remained between her and the thing the whole kingdom had agreed, for six hundred years, to pretend was nothing at all.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was round, and vast, and wrong in a way her mind kept trying to correct.

Her eyes wanted it to be a well-room, a cistern, some mundane vault of the kind cut beneath every old fortress. It was domed above, floored below, maybe forty paces across, and lit — this was the wrongness — lit faintly from within the walls themselves, as though the millions of carved names had absorbed six centuries of darkness until the darkness became luminous, the way deep water glows. No torches. No vents. And yet air moved against her face, slow and warm, in and out, in and out, and Elodie stood at the edge of the final step and realized she was standing inside a lung.

At the center of the chamber stood the door.

She had imagined it a hundred ways on the long road: iron, oak, bronze, a slab of granite banded with the Crown's first seals. Nothing had prepared her for what it actually was. It was bone. Not shaped like bone — not carved into resemblance — but bone, unmistakably, a single vertebra grown rather than built, the size of a chapel door, its great transverse spurs sweeping out to either side like folded wings, its body smooth and ivory-pale and faintly translucent where Ash's flame touched it. Through the translucence, deep inside, something dark moved with the slow rhythm of the air. The spinal channel of the mountain itself, she thought. They had not built their tomb. They had closed the mountain's own throat.

And over the surface of it, covering every hand-span, were names — but different here. In the galleries the names had been small, dense, humble, a necropolis written in whispers. These were large. Deliberate. Cut by hands that had known they were writing the last thing anyone would read for six hundred years, and had chosen to write, over and over, in the old tongue, in letters half a finger deep:

*WE CHOSE THIS. LET US KEEP THE CHOOSING.*

Elodie's breath went out of her slowly.

Ash stopped before the door and did not touch it. The child stood with the flame cupped close, and Elodie saw that Ash was trembling — not the shaking of cold or fear exactly, but the shaking of someone standing before a house they had fled, knowing everyone inside could hear them arrive.

"Ash," Elodie said softly. "You don't have to—"

"It will speak," Ash said. "When I am near, it speaks. That is why I was sent. Not to open it." The child's eyes came up, dark and enormous in the bone-light. "To answer it."

Before Elodie could ask — answer *what*? — the door spoke.

* * *

It did not come through her carvings; she carried none down here, having left them all burning or buried or given away along the road. It came through the floor, through her boots, through the soles of her feet and up the bones of her legs as if her skeleton were simply another carving the voice had found convenient. She had heard ten thousand dead voices in her life. She had heard the drowned and the burned and the murdered, the old who went gently and the young who went screaming, and she had believed herself past flinching at any of them.

She flinched.

Because this voice was not dead.

That was the thing her gift told her instantly and absolutely, the way a tuning fork knows its twin across a crowded room: the voice speaking through the vertebra was *alive*. Thin as thread, dry as paper left two lifetimes in the sun, slower than anything human ought to be able to sustain — but alive, alive, alive, each word arriving minutes apart as if the speaker had to walk a great distance between them, gathering breath like a woman climbing a hill.

"*...someone... stands... outside.*"

"Yes," Elodie said, and her own voice cracked in the middle like green wood. She knelt — she didn't decide to kneel; her knees simply went, the way they go before a grave — and laid her bare palm flat against the bone. It was warm. Warm as a sleeping child's forehead. "I'm here. My name is Elodie Voss. I'm—"

*A carver,* the voice said, and there was something in it that might, in a living mouth, have been wonder. "*A listener. Oh. Oh, it has been so long since the door heard a listener. The Wardens come with hammers and prayers and they hear nothing. But you — you have the ear. The old ear.*"

"I hear the dead," Elodie said. "That's all my gift ever was."

"No," said the voice, gently, the way you correct a child who has misnamed a star. "*No one hears the dead. There are no dead behind this door, Elodie-Voss-who-listens. That is the whole of our sorrow, and the whole of your kingdom's lie.*"

The air moved again, in and out, and Elodie understood that the breathing was the door. Was the seal. Was — her mind refused it, and then offered it anyway, the way the mind offers unbearable things in small installments — was the sleeper herself. Sustained. Kept. Six hundred years of not-dying, pressed into the mountain's marrow by a covenant signed in the blood of everyone who might otherwise have asked why.

"How many of you are still—" She couldn't finish it.

"*Fourteen,*" the voice said, without self-pity, which was the worst part. Fourteen people alive inside a tomb built for thousands. Fourteen people who had watched, over centuries, the others go — not dying, the voice would explain later, but *finishing*, choosing at last to set down the terrible burden of continued breath, one by one, until only the eldest remained to hold the seal and the youngest remained because she had been too stubborn, too angry, too full of songs yet unsung. "*Fourteen of us chose the seal instead of the war. Do you know what that means, listener? It means that every year your kings celebrate the Truce of Iron, they are celebrating the fact that we consented to be buried breathing. And every year, no one asks us if the consenting has expired.*"

Elodie's eyes were wet. She wiped them with the back of her wrist, impatient with herself, and failed entirely.

"You could stop," she said. "Couldn't you? You're the one holding it. You could just — let go. Stop sustaining it. Wake, or die, whichever came."

A long silence. Long enough that she checked the warmth under her palm, terrified.

"*Yes,*" the voice said at last. "*I could. Do you know why I haven't? Six hundred years, and I will tell you the truth, because you have walked so far and bled so much to stand here: I am afraid.*"

"Afraid of waking?" Ash whispered. "Of what the world would do?"

"*No, little singer. I am afraid of what we would do. I am angry, Elodie-Voss. Six hundred years of anger, fed on the dark, and I do not trust it. If I open this door tomorrow, I cannot promise you forgiveness walks out of it. I can promise fourteen voices, and four hundred years of grief older than mine still sleeping deeper down, and a hunger — oh, there is a hunger in the deep galleries, among those who finished badly — a hunger to be *answered*, and answers given in rage taste of ash.*" The breathing slowed. Deepened. When it resumed, the voice was quieter. "*So I hold the seal. Not to keep the world safe from us. To keep me safe from me. That is my prison and my throne and my cowardice, all in one bone.*"

Elodie pressed her forehead against the vertebra. It was, absurdly, the most intimate thing she had ever done — more than any touch of Renn's, more than holding Ash fevered through the nights — forehead to the skull of a woman older than kingdoms, listening to her heart through the stone the way you listen to a child through a door.

"What are you asking for?" she said. "Freedom? You could have it. I don't know how, but Ash was sent to find out, and the decree—"

"*Not freedom.*" For the first time, the voice sharpened, and the whole chamber rang with it, the walls answering in a thousand carved throats: *NOT FREEDOM NOT FREEDOM NOT FREEDOM* — and then, contrite, soft again: "*Forgive me. The walls get excited. They have waited longer even than I. No, listener. Ask me properly. Ask me what I want. No one in six hundred years has asked me what I want. They asked if I would surrender. They asked if I renounced. They asked, once, terribly, whether I repented — as if burying my children alive had been my sin and not my sentence.*"

The tears came freely now and Elodie let them. On the road she had learned to spend them sparingly; here, before this door, extravagance seemed owed.

"What do you want?" Elodie asked.

"*Choice,*" said the first sleeper, and the word came out of her like something drawn from a wound. "*I want to choose. Not to be freed like a prisoner whose debt is paid. Not to be woken like a weapon taken down from a wall. Not to be destroyed like a disease, which is what your Wardens' orders say, little singer — yes, I know what they carry down the mountain toward you even now; the stone tells me their bootfalls the way yours tells you voices. I want what was stolen from us in the signing: the right to say yes or no and have it mean something. Let us choose to wake. Or let us choose to rest, truly rest, and be mourned instead of maintained. Either way — let it be *ours*. That is all. That is everything. Six hundred years, and all I want is the smallest thing in the world: to be asked.*"

Ash sank down beside Elodie then, cross-legged, the flame set carefully on the bone floor where it threw the child's shadow huge against the door. And Ash began to sing — very softly, the opening verse, the one from the pass, the one that had quieted mountains — and Elodie felt the words move through her palm into the bone like water into drought-cracked earth.

The first sleeper listened for a long time.

Then she laughed. It was a rusted sound, a sound like a gate opening for the first time in centuries, and underneath the rust Elodie heard the young woman she had been before the sealing, laughing around some fire in a tower that still stood unburned.

"*Oh,*" she said. "*Oh, they sent a child. Of course they did. They always send the ones with the most to lose. Listen to me, Elodie-Voss-who-listens, and you, Ash-who-was-sent-and-may-yet-decide: the door does not open to blood, or to song, or to force, or to time. It opens to willingness — ours and yours together, both sides saying yes with no hammer at anyone's throat. Your kings' own decree says it, in a clause they have never read aloud. So go. Wake your smuggler. Meet your Warden — he is close now, closer than you know, and he is not the monster his orders make him; I hear doubt in his footsteps, and doubt is a door too. Bring them all here. Bring the whole sorry quarrel down to my door. And then, for the first time in six hundred years, we will *ask each other*.*"

The breathing slowed. The warmth under Elodie's palm gentled, like a hand relaxing in sleep.

"*And listener,*" the voice added, already far away, already sinking back into its long endurance, "*one thing more. When they come — and they are coming — remember what I could not say to the men with hammers: we were never your kingdom's enemy. We were its mirror. Break the mirror if you must. But know that every crack shows you your own face.*"

Silence, then. The good kind — the kind that follows a finished conversation, not an ended one.

Elodie stayed kneeling a moment longer, her hand on the warm bone, memorizing it. Then Ash touched her shoulder, and pointed upward, and Elodie heard it too, rising down through six hundred years of stairs:

Boots. Many boots. And above them, faint but unmistakable, the creak of a man's voice giving quiet orders — Corvane's voice, coming down into the dark with his blade drawn and his conscience, if the sleeper was right, hanging by a thread.

They had brought the war to the door after all.

Now it remained to see whether anyone, on either side of it, could remember how to ask.
