## 29. Voices Rising

The old man opened his eyes.

They were the color of the mountain's heartstone — grey shot through with veins of gold, the same mineral light that threaded the walls of the Spire — and they did not blink against the lamplight as living eyes should have. He looked at Ash first, and Elodie felt the whole chamber lean toward that gaze like grass bending before wind.

Then he looked at her.

Six hundred years of sleep looked back at her out of a face carved thin as driftwood, and Elodie Voss, who had spent her life listening to the dead, discovered there was an entirely different thing in being listened to.

"You are not one of ours," he said.

The words arrived in the old tongue, but they did not arrive alone. They came wrapped in meaning the way her carvings came wrapped in voice — she understood him the way she understood bone, directly, without translation standing in between. Beside her, Ash made a small sound, half gasp, half song, and pressed their hand flat against Elodie's sleeve.

"No," Elodie said. Her voice came out rough. "I'm a bone-carver. From Thornfell. Above."

"Above." The old man tasted the word. Around him, the other sleepers were stirring now — she could see them down the long rows of the sealed heart, dozens of them, hundreds, breath deepening in chests that had held only dust for centuries, fingers twitching against shrouds gone soft as moth-wing. "There is no above. There was no above. There was the tower, and the fire, and the door."

"The door is open," Ash said.

Ash said it in the old tongue, and then — deliberately, the way a child shows a grown-up how to skip a stone — they said it again in Common. *The door is open.* And the chamber took both versions of it and carried them outward, into the galleries, into the flooded dark, into the million carved names, and the singing in the walls changed key.

It went from dawn to thunder.

* * *

Elodie had heard the dead angry before. She had heard them grieving, vengeful, lost, petty, kind. She had spent thirty years learning every weather of the grave.

She had never heard anything like this.

It came up through the floor first, through the soles of her boots, a vibration that was almost sound — the galleries waking all at once, all together, six hundred years of entombed voices rising in a single tide. Not bodies. She understood that even as terror closed its hand around her throat. The drowned ones, the ones whose flesh had failed in the dark centuries, they rose as voice only, pouring up through stone and water and the roots of the Spire itself, and they filled the sealed heart until the air itself seemed to have syllables in it.

And every syllable was a name.

No — not names. She caught herself, gasping, gripping Ash's shoulder. Every syllable was a *demand*. Names were woven through it like rebar through concrete, but the substance of it was older and simpler: *out, out, open, owed, owed, owed.*

"They want—" she began, and couldn't finish, because what they wanted was written in the pressure of the air, in the trembling of the lamps, in the fine white dust sifting down from the vaulted ceiling like snow falling inside a church.

"Vengeance," said the old man. He had risen from his bier without seeming to move, unfolding upward joint by joint like something that had been folded away for winter. He stood taller than any man Elodie had ever seen, though she suspected he had not been tall in life; it was the standing that was enormous, the simple fact of verticality after six hundred years of lying down. "Yes. That is the word. We rehearsed it for a very long time." His golden-veined eyes found hers again. "You hear us. All of us. How?"

"She's a listener," Ash said quickly. "A mother-of-voices. She heard me when no one else could—"

"I asked her, little courier." It was not unkind. It was worse than unkind; it was patient. Everything about the old man was patient, the patience of stone, of sediment, of things that wait because waiting is all they have ever been permitted. "How does a woman of Thornfell — of the kingdom that nailed our door shut — come to carry our tongue in her bones?"

The singing in the walls rose. Dust fell faster. Somewhere behind them, far up the galleries, Elodie heard a crash — stone giving way somewhere it had stood for centuries — and beneath it, fainter, another sound: boots. Many boots. Iron-shod. The Wardens were in the Weeping Galleries, and they were running.

"We don't have time for this," she said.

The old man tilted his head, and around him the nearest sleepers were sitting up now, and Elodie saw their faces — faces she knew. Not individually, but *knowingly*, the way you know a melody you've never heard performed. These were the faces from the gallery walls. These were the names she had read by lamplight while the water dripped. A woman with a scar across her jaw who appeared forty times in the carvings, always holding someone's hand. Two children carved side by side with their names interlocked so they could not be separated even in stone. An old soldier whose name was followed by a mark Elodie had translated once as *the one who stayed last.*

All of them alive. All of them breathing. All of them looking at her the way a starving crowd looks through a baker's window.

"The door has been shut six hundred years," the old man said. "We can find sixty more seconds. Speak."

* * *

So she spoke.

She told him about the blizzard, and the knock at dusk, and the child half-frozen on her step humming melodies that made finished carvings whisper. She told him about burning her workshop to hide one child from men whose duty was kinder than their orders. About the pass where the mountain itself hummed the old language, angrier than any ghost she'd ever met, and how Ash had quieted it with a song. About Greyford Bridge, and the young Warden who had died seeing Ash's face and forgetting, for one crucial heartbeat, what he'd been sent to do. About river-folk who owed nothing and gave everything. About the fever, and the word *mother-of-voices,* and the night she had wept over a child she had never wanted and could no longer imagine the world without.

She told it plainly, because she did not know how to tell it any other way, and because some animal instinct told her that these people — buried alive, sung to sleep with lies, sealed under the first law of the Crown that had murdered them — these people would smell ornament the way wolves smell fear.

"And we were sent," Ash added, when she finished, "to decide. That's what I am. Not a messenger. Not a weapon. They chose me before the end and taught me the leaving-song and put me in the ice-channels, and I don't know why me, I've never known why me, but I know what I saw." Ash's voice cracked and held. "I saw the whole of it. The towers burning. The bargain nobody asked us about. Six hundred years of my people breathing dust and calling it peace. And I walked out, and I kept walking, and every road led here, and every road led to *her*—" Ash's hand found Elodie's again, gripped hard enough to hurt, "—and I decided. I decided the door opens. Not for war. For *witness*. For someone to finally stand where we stand and see what was done."

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Elodie had ever heard.

Because the galleries had stopped singing.

Every carved name, every drowned voice, every fragment of the entombed multitude — they had all gone still to listen, and now they hung in the silence like a held breath, and Elodie realized with a lurch that she could feel them *waiting*, thousands upon thousands of wills suspended on a single point, the way she had felt griefs hang on her chisel-tip waiting to be given shape.

Waiting to vote.

* * *

"They will not accept witness," said a new voice, and Elodie turned.

The woman with the scarred jaw had risen from her bier. Up close she was gaunt and magnificent, and the scar had never healed quite straight, and when she moved the others moved with her, a rustle through the rows like wind through wheat. "I died at thirty-one years old in a room with no doors," she said, in the old tongue, and the translation came to Elodie raw and bleeding. "I watched my children fall asleep beside me and I did not know if I would wake to them or wake alone. Six hundred years, keeper-of-the-door. You offer us a stranger's account and call it witness?"

"I offer you the truth," Ash said, chin up, voice shaking, standing there in a stolen coat two sizes too big.

"The truth." The woman laughed, and it was a terrible sound, laughter with rust in it. "Here is truth: the king's men came singing a truce. Here is truth: we believed them, because we had never lied to anyone and could not imagine it. Here is truth: they bricked the doors while we slept off the feast they'd drugged, and when the eldest among us understood, he walked to the wall and pressed his palm against it and said, *they will remember us,* and the wall said nothing, and the wall has said nothing for six hundred years." Her voice climbed. Around her the biers creaked; the dust fell in curtains now; the galleries began to stir again, a low rising note, fury gathering like storm-water. "You want to know what we want? We want the kingdom to feel what we felt. We want their children to wake in sealed rooms. We want—"

"*Sera.*"

The old man said only her name, softly, and the whole chamber flinched.

He had not raised his voice. He simply stood there amid the rising storm of his people's rage, this folded-paper man with stone-gold eyes, and waited the way stone waits, until Sera's chest heaved once, twice, and she sat back down on the edge of her bier like a puppet with cut strings.

"You are right," he said to her. "Everything you said is true. I watched you die too. I watched all of you die, a little, every year, the way the living die out of the dead." He turned back to Ash and Elodie. "And you are right as well, little courier. Witness. But witness is a door that swings both ways, and I think your friend knows it."

Elodie's mouth was dry. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that vengeance is also testimony." He began to walk, slowly, down the aisle between the biers, and where he passed, the sleepers reached out and touched his sleeve — Sera, the soldier, the interlocked children's parents, dozens of hands brushing fabric gone grey with age — as though he were a wick and they were flame deciding whether to catch. "I mean that the galleries are full tonight. Have you noticed? The ones whose bodies failed — they rise with us. Voice without flesh. Do you understand what that means, bone-carver?"

"It means," Elodie said slowly, "that they're angry enough to stay."

"It means they have been angry enough to stay for centuries, and anger is the only mortar left holding them, and if we walk up those stairs tonight and give the kingdom what Sera wants—" he paused beside a bier near the wall, and Elodie saw that the sleeper there was the smallest of all, a child no older than Ash, hands folded the same way the old man's had been folded, "—then these voices will do what angry dead do. They will haunt. They will take. They will teach the kingdom's children to wake in sealed rooms. And in a hundred years there will be a new seal, and a new truce, and somewhere a new child in the ice-channels, carrying the next leaving-song. The wheel turns. We would be turning it."

"But the kingdom did this," Elodie said. Her own anger surprised her, rising hot in her throat. "Your people were murdered. Entombed alive. The Crown built its first law on your grave. If anyone has earned the wheel—"

"Earned." The old man smiled, and it was the saddest expression she had ever seen on a living face. "Yes. We earned it. That is the trap of earning, bone-carver. Vengeance always collects." He crouched — slowly, joints cracking like green wood — beside the small sleeper, and touched the folded hands. "When we sealed ourselves in — for it was a sealing, whatever the histories say, we drank the cup willingly rather than let the war eat the world — we swore an oath. Not to the kingdom. To each other. Do you know it?"

Ash whispered it before he could. "*We keep the door so no one else must be the door.*"

"Yes." The old man looked up at Ash, and something passed between them that Elodie could not follow, some current of the old tongue that ran deeper than grammar. "And now the door opens, and the oath comes due, and I find I do not know how to keep it. Because keeping it might mean walking past every wrong ever done to us and calling it peace, and I am not certain my people will follow me, and I am *certain* the voices in the walls will not." He rose. Turned to face the galleries — face the dark, the water, the million names. Raised his voice for the first time, and it rang against the stone like a bell struck underwater.

"Ancestors! You who stayed when flesh failed! You have heard the account. You have heard the child who was sent to decide. Six hundred years ago, kings decided for us and we paid in centuries. So I ask you now, all of you, every name on every wall—" his voice dropped, and yet somehow grew larger, "—*what do you want now?*"

* * *

The answer did not come as words.

It came as Elodie had learned answers came from bones — as pressure, as weight, as meaning arriving whole in the marrow. But scaled beyond anything her gift had ever carried. Thousands of voices answered at once, and they did not agree, and the disagreement itself was the answer: a vast, roiling parliament of the dead, vengeance and forgiveness and grief and exhaustion all speaking simultaneously, wave crossing wave —

— and through it all, one thread rising, thin and persistent, repeated and repeated and repeated until Elodie caught it and gasped, because she recognized the cadence.

It was a question. They were asking a question. Thousands of dead voices, asking one question, over and over, in the cadence of children:

*Who remembers us? Who remembers us? Who remembers us?*

"Oh," Elodie breathed. Her eyes were burning. "Oh, you fools. All of you. Both sides. It was never about the door."

The old man turned sharply. "Speak."

"They don't want vengeance. Most of them. They don't even want freedom — the free ones are past wanting anything." She was crying now and did not care; the tears came from somewhere deeper than shame. "They want to be *remembered*. That's all. Six hundred years sealed in the dark and the one thing they cannot do is be witnessed — that's what they were begging for in the galleries, that's what the pleading was, not *let us out* — *let us be known.* And the Crown sealed the tomb AND burned the songs AND outlawed the tongue, so that no one would ever even know there was anything to remember." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "You've been fighting over who gets to punish whom, and the dead just want their names spoken in daylight."

Silence again. But a different silence — a silence like snowfall, like held grief beginning to thaw.

Sera stared at her. "Is that true?" she asked the walls. "Is that what you want? After everything — you want *songs?*"

From the galleries, softer now, ragged, unmistakable: *yes. yes. yes.* And beneath it, threading through, other answers — *and justice, and justice* — but quieter, fewer, a minority report filed by the furious. The dead were not unanimous. The dead were a people, and people were never unanimous, and that, Elodie thought wildly, was perhaps the most hopeful thing she had learned in her entire strange life.

The old man closed his eyes. When he opened them, the gold in them seemed to have settled, like silt finding a riverbed.

"Bone-carver," he said. "Listener. Mother-of-voices. You have done what no king, no Warden, no prophet managed in six hundred years. You have made my people audible to themselves." He extended one hand — ancient, spotted, steady — toward her, and toward Ash standing rigid at her side, and toward the stairs beyond, where the boot-treads of armed men were growing louder by the second. "But audibility is not agreement, and the Wardens are coming, and my people's fury is not spent, only *confused*. What happens in the next hour will decide whether this night ends in covenant or in war. I am six hundred years old and I do not know which it will be." His hand remained extended. "Will you stand with us when they arrive? Not behind us. With us. Where everyone can see what the kingdom's silence cost, and what it might yet buy."

Above them, muffled by stone and water, a great door burst somewhere in the galleries, and iron rang on stone, and Ash's grip tightened on Elodie's hand.

Elodie looked at the offered hand of a man who had been buried alive by her ancestors' king. She thought of a burned workshop, a smuggler holding a bridge, a fevered child calling her *mother* in a dead language that was not dead at all.

She took it.

"Then come," said the eldest sleeper of the Silent Spire, as the first torchlight spilled down the stairwell, "and let us go meet the kingdom — and may whichever god kept us breathing keep us wise."
