## 30. The Parliament of Dust

The hall beneath the Spire had not held a living voice in six hundred years, and it did not seem to know what to do with one now.

Elodie felt the room's confusion in her bones — literally, in the carved vertebra pendant she wore, which trembled like a struck tuning fork as they filed through the doors. The space was vast beyond sense, a hollowed throat of stone rising into darkness where the ceiling should have been. Rings of benches descended toward a central floor of pale bone-stone, and every surface — every pillar, every rail, every inch of wall — was covered in names.

Millions of them. She had walked the Weeping Galleries and thought she understood. She had not. Here the names were not pleading. They were *watching*.

"They're listening," Ash said softly, in the old tongue, and Elodie translated without thinking, her voice carrying strangely far in the acoustics. "All of them. Every name on these walls."

"Then let them listen well," said Warden-Captain Vess Corvane, and his voice had the flat steadiness of a man who had decided to be afraid later. He still wore his grey cloak, though he had surrendered his blade at the door — Renn had insisted, with a smile that promised violence if refused. "Because what I say next will be repeated to every Crown official from here to the capital, and I want no ambiguity about who said it first."

He stepped down onto the central floor alone.

"My name is Vess Corvane," he said. "Warden-Sergeant, Third Vigil. For nineteen years I enforced the seal beneath this tower. I believed it was memory. I have learned this month that it is maintenance." He paused, and Elodie saw the effort it cost him — a man dismantling himself in public, plank by plank. "I am here to testify that my orders were crimes. And I am here because someone has to speak for the people above ground who are afraid of you, and I would rather it be a man who knows exactly how much there is to be afraid of."

A murmur moved through the hall — but not from the sleepers seated along the eastern arc. From the walls. The names shifted, faintly, like leaves deciding on a wind. Elodie's pendant sang a low warning note.

*They remember him,* she realized. *Not this man. His office. The grey cloaks at the door, six hundred years ago.*

* * *

Renn Carrow leaned against a pillar near the western entrance and tried very hard to look like a man with no stake in any of this.

He was failing. He could feel it in his hands, which wanted to fidget; in his jaw, which wanted to clench. Across the central floor sat perhaps forty sleepers — the eldest ones first woken, those whose bodies had kept best through the long dark — robed in dust-grey linen, their faces narrow and their eyes the deep amber of lamplight through honey. Among the villagers from Greyford and the river settlements, maybe sixty souls crowded the upper benches, having come at Elodie's request or plain curiosity, armed mostly with fear and farming implements left at the door. The Wardens stood in a disciplined block under Corvane's second, Sergeant Hale, who kept glancing at her captain as though checking whether treason was contagious.

And between all of them, small and straight-backed, sat Ash — on a stool placed at the center of the floor, where everyone could see the child and the child could see everyone.

*Renn thought: That's either the wisest thing I've ever seen or the cruelest.*

Elodie stood beside the stool. Not behind it. Beside it. He'd noticed that too, in the days since the waking — how she arranged herself always at Ash's shoulder, never looming, never sheltering, a wall that pretended to be furniture.

Corvane finished his testimony. Silence took the hall, and Renn heard, beneath the silence, something he had learned to dread on this road: the hum. The galleries' dead, stirring in the stone, following words they could not understand through a translator they trusted.

Then an old sleeper rose — the eldest, the one they called Serevan, whose skin lay over his skull like parchment over a drum. His voice came out rusted, and when he spoke, Elodie translated, and Renn watched the two languages braid together in the air.

"He says: *Six hundred years ago, your ancestors sealed our gates from outside while we sang inside. They poured mortar over our own children's voices.*"

"We know what was done," Corvane said.

"Do you?" Serevan's amber eyes found the Warden block. "*Your first king signed his name at the bottom of the decree. Ours signed theirs at the top — consenting, starving, betrayed by half our own who bargained for mercy. Both signatures are in this room. Ask your captain which one he carries.*"

Hale went white. Corvane didn't flinch. "Neither," he said. "I carry the third signature. The one that says the seal may be opened if a voice returns willing." He turned, deliberately, to face the villagers' benches. "Which means every Warden who ever enforced it — including me — enforced a law we had already broken the spirit of. I won't pretend that away to make this easier."

Renn exhaled slowly. *Damn him,* he thought, without heat. *The man learns honesty at seventy years old and deploys it like a siege weapon.*

But it was a farmer from Greyford — a broad, sunburnt woman named Maren Ott, whom Renn had once sold contraband salt to and never paid for — who broke the fragile thing Corvane had built.

"Easy for him to confess," she said, standing, her voice rough with old anger. "My grandmother's grandmother kept a story. Of the Spire-folk's last war. Of fields burned so deep nothing grew for a generation. Of villages made into graveyards. You want us to sit in a hole in the mountain and negotiate with the people who did that?"

Serevan turned to her, slow as a door on rusted hinges.

"*Woman,*" Elodie translated, and her voice shook slightly, because the intent underneath the old words came through her like heat through iron — grief, enormous and specific, "*we burned the fields because yours were already taken. We were driven into this tower and the door was mortared shut while we still breathed. Whatever your stories say we did, ask what was done to us first. Then ask why only one side of the story kept grandmothers to tell it.*"

Maren sat down slowly, as though her legs had been taken from her.

And Elodie swayed on her feet.

* * *

Elodie had translated grief before. It was, in a sense, her whole trade — she pulled last words out of finger-bones for coin, wrapped other people's endings in careful language, handed them back to the grieving with her face composed.

She had never translated *intent* before. That was what the Spire's dead demanded of her gift now, what the strange deepening of it since the waking required: not just the words, but the weight beneath them. When Serevan spoke of burning fields, she felt the burning. When Maren spoke of graveyards, she felt the graveyard. Every sentence passed through her body like a blade being handed across a table — and both parties could see, in the translator's face, whether the blade had an edge.

It was unbearable. It was also, she understood with terrible clarity, the entire point.

"You're doing it on purpose," she murmured to Ash during the first recess, when the benches emptied for water and whispered counsel.

Ash looked up at her with those old, old eyes in a young face. "*The dead insisted,*" the child said. "*Words can lie. A speaker cannot. That is why they chose me — not to carry the language out, but to carry it out honestly. If you translate, they trust the translation.*"

"And if I refuse? If I'm too tired, too frightened—"

"*Then the parliament fails, and the walls decide instead.*" Ash glanced up at the millions of names. "*You do not want the walls deciding. They have waited six hundred years, mother-of-voices. Their patience is a kind of hunger.*"

* * *

The second session began badly and got worse.

A young sleeper named Ilvaen — quick-voiced, sharp-eyed, one of the few whose body had kept its strength — rose to demand reparations: land returned, granaries opened, the Warden order dissolved root and branch. Sergeant Hale answered with a trembling recitation of everything the Crown feared: plague, invasion, the collapse of six centuries of order built on a foundation everyone now admitted was rotten. Voices rose. Maren Ott shouted about graves; Ilvaen shouted about mortar; somewhere on the upper benches a villager began weeping loudly, and the hum in the walls deepened until Elodie's teeth ached with it.

It was Renn Carrow — of all the improbable people in that impossible hall — who stopped it.

He didn't stand. He didn't raise his voice. He simply said, in the bored drawl of a man discussing freight rates, loud enough to carry:

"You know what I did for a living, before all this? Smuggling." Heads turned. He shrugged. "Moved anything anyone wanted moved, over any border, past any Warden. And I'll tell you the first thing they teach you on the Thornfell roads." He pushed off the pillar and strolled down onto the central floor, entirely uninvited. "Nobody smuggles a thing nobody wants. Every route I ever ran existed because two groups of people needed each other and were too proud or too scared to say so. Salt for wool. Medicine for passage. Your war, six hundred years back — I've read the histories, the honest ones, the ones the Crown doesn't burn — and you know what I found? Both sides were trading partners right up until the year before. Grain for craftwork. Mountain timber for valley iron." He spread his hands. "Somebody made money on the breaking, same as always. And everybody else forgot they needed each other. That's the whole trick. That's always been the whole trick."

Silence. Then Serevan said something long and dry, and Elodie translated, and to her surprise a ripple went through the sleepers that might, in living people, have been laughter.

"*The smuggler says the oldest crime is forgetting need,*" she relayed. "*He says the dead agree. He says — *" She paused, listening to the intent beneath the rusted words, and her mouth curved despite everything. "*He says he has met our descendants in the valleys, selling them knives, and they haggled fairly.*"

"I did," Renn admitted. "Lovely hagglers, your lot. Terrible tippers."

* * *

They argued for three days.

On the second day a compromise nearly died when Ilvaen demanded the Warden order's dissolution and Hale, cornered, blurted that half her Wardens' pay came from estates seized in the old war — that the Crown's peace had been bought with stolen land and maintained ever since, a machine feeding itself. The hum in the walls became a roar. Stones shed dust. Elodie, translating, tasted blood.

It was Ash who stood, on the third morning, when the parliament had torn itself down to raw hatred and exhausted silence, and asked the question in the old tongue that stopped everything.

Elodie translated it and felt the hall change.

"Ash asks: *When you were sealed in, did any of you want to forgive?*"

Serevan looked at the child for a long moment. Then, slowly, around the eastern arc, hand after weathered hand rose — not all, barely half, but enough. And among the villagers, hesitantly, Maren Ott raised hers too, and then others, and Hale after a visible struggle, and even Corvane, standing apart as always, lifted one hand high and kept it there.

Elodie looked at those raised hands — dead and living, guilty and wronged, all of them tired — and understood suddenly what the dead had been arguing about in the galleries all along. Not vengeance versus forgiveness. Choice versus its absence. Six hundred years ago, no one had asked the sleepers anything. Today, hands were going up. It was the crudest, most human form of miracle: a vote.

"The walls are quiet," Ash whispered, wonder cracking the child's voice young for the first time in weeks. "*Mother-of-voices — the walls are quiet.*"

* * *

But parliaments end, and covenants must be written, and it was as the scribes — two Wardens and one sleeper, working in three scripts apiece — bent over the draft terms that evening that the trouble came.

Renn saw it first, because Renn always saw exits first. A runner at the far door — one of Hale's, mud to the thighs, breathing like a bellows — conferring urgently with the sergeant, whose face went through three colors before settling on grey.

He drifted over, casual as tide. "Bad news travels fast this far underground, Sergeant."

Hale hesitated — then, in the new spirit of the place, told him the truth. "Rider from the capital. The Lord Protector's council got word of the waking." Her jaw worked. "They've declared the covenant negotiations treason in progress. There's an army column moving on Thornfell. Two thousand men. And the orders—" she swallowed, "—the orders specify the child."

The hum in the walls did not rise. That was the worst part. The millions of names went perfectly, utterly silent, the way a forest goes silent before something comes through it.

Across the hall, Elodie had gone still, her hand on Ash's shoulder, her eyes finding Renn's over the child's head with a look he had learned to read on the road: *not again, not this child, not while I breathe.*

Above them, faint through six hundred feet of stone, the Spire itself began — very softly — to ring.
