## 31. Terms of the Anew

The Spire hall had not held a living parliament in six hundred years, and it showed no interest in pretending otherwise.

Elodie felt it the moment she crossed the threshold — the way she always felt a room's dead, except that now the dead were *here*, breathing dust and waking slowly into their own bodies, and the hall itself was the oldest voice of all. The walls rose in ribs of black stone veined with something pale that might have been bone or might have been marble worn to bone by centuries of silence. High above, where the ceiling should have closed into darkness, the Spire's hollow throat opened skyward, and morning light fell down through it in a single column so wide and so slow that dust turned in it like fish in still water.

They had arranged the seats badly, everyone agreed, and then everyone disagreed about how to arrange them better.

The sleepers had been awake for two days now. Two days of staggering, weeping, touching their own hands as though confirming ownership. Two days of Corvane's Wardens standing in an uneasy ring around the hall's edge, blades sheathed but hands never far from hilts, while their arrested superior sat under guard in a side chamber, insisting loudly through the stone that this was all a mistake that would be corrected from the capital. Two days of Renn Carrow moving through it all with his arm bound to his chest and his charm running on fumes, finding water, finding food stores that had somehow survived six centuries in sealed jars, finding — because he was Renn — the one old sleeper who remembered how the hall's great doors had been worked before they were sealed.

And two days of Ash sitting at the center of everything, small and grave on a chair someone had found for them, translating.

Not translating words. Elodie had learned by now that Ash did not translate words. Ash translated *intent* — which was a far more dangerous trade, and one the world had no honest currency for.

She stood near the column of light with her arms folded, watching the hall assemble itself for what everyone was calling, in half a dozen languages, *the terms*. The word had spread the way words do in the aftermath of catastrophe: quickly, carelessly, taking root wherever it landed. Terms. As if peace were a bill of lading. As if you could weigh it, sign for it, store it in a warehouse.

Well. She was a bone-carver. She knew better than most that anything could be carved if you were patient enough, and that the patience was the whole craft.

* * *

The eldest sleeper's name was Vessane, though she had needed most of a day to remember it, and when she finally spoke it aloud the entire hall had gone quiet, because her name was also a place-name — there was a valley in Thornfell called Vessane's Hollow that had been named, apparently, after her, before anyone alive had known why.

She was impossibly old. Not in the way of the dead Elodie had spent her life listening to, whose age was fixed and finished, a coin already struck. Vessane's age was *ongoing*. Six hundred years of suspension had not killed her; it had merely postponed her, and now the debt of all those years was coming due at once, and she was paying it in front of witnesses, slowly, with tremendous dignity.

She sat wrapped in the rotted remnant of her own ceremonial robes, which Wardens' cloaks had been draped over, because mercy is often just logistics. Her face was seamed like drought-cracked clay. Her eyes were the color of the light in the column above them — pale, patient, falling from a very great height.

It was Vessane who had asked the question yesterday, the question that had cracked the whole grim arithmetic of vengeance open: *What do you want now?*

She asked it again this morning, formally, to open the proceedings. She asked it in the old tongue, and Ash gave it in Common, and Elodie heard both at once, the way she heard everything now — layered, doubled, the meaning arriving slightly ahead of the sound.

"We are six hundred years late," Vessane said. "We have a right to be angry. We have a right to demand. But rights are for the living, and we do not yet know if we are that. So I ask first: what do *you* want now? All of you. Crown, Warden, smuggler, carver, child. Speak plainly. We will know if you lie." A pause, dry as old paper. "We have had six hundred years to learn what lies sound like."

* * *

They spoke plainly. It went about as well as plain speaking ever does.

The Crown's representative — a gray functionary named Aldous Merrin who had arrived breathless from the capital two days behind Corvane, carrying emergency writs he clearly hoped would solve everything and clearly suspected would solve nothing — spoke first. He wanted order. He wanted the Spire-folk to accept resettlement "in designated territories," which was a phrase with a long and ugly history attached to it, and Elodie watched several sleepers hear the shape of that history inside the words even without translation, because some phrases carry their crimes in their bones.

He wanted the seal renounced, yes — that much the decree-clause had forced, and Corvane's testimony had made renunciation politically survivable, barely. But he wanted it renounced *quietly*. He wanted no trials. He wanted no public accounting of the six hundred years of maintenance-murder that the Wardens' hierarchy had administered like a chronic disease, kept alive on purpose, fed on purpose, never named. He wanted, in short, exactly what the Crown had wanted since the beginning: for the past to be an agreement rather than a wound.

Renn Carrow, leaning against a pillar with his bad arm cradled, laughed out loud at that. It was not a kind laugh.

"Forgive me, Master Merrin," he said, in the tone of a man asking forgiveness for nothing whatsoever. "But I've moved contraband past your Crown's checkpoints for fifteen years, and I'll tell you what I've learned about quiet arrangements. They're only quiet for the people they benefit. Everyone else hears them fine."

A murmur ran through the sleepers — not anger, Elodie noted. Something worse. *Recognition.*

* * *

It was midday, by the angle of the light-column, when the argument over land began in earnest, and it was midday when Elodie understood what her gift was actually for.

A sleeper named Orenn — young by sleeper standards, perhaps forty when the tomb closed, which made him four hundred and forty now and looking every suspended year of it — rose to answer Merrin's proposal of designated territories. He spoke in the old tongue, low and controlled, and Ash translated:

"He says the valleys you offer were ours first. He says the farms on them were built on our orchards. He says—" and here Ash stopped, head tilting, listening to something beneath the words, the way Elodie listened to bones. Ash's face changed. "He says he wants the families who live there now to be driven out. He says they have lived well on stolen ground for six hundred years and it is time."

The hall tightened. On the Warden side of the floor, hands drifted toward hilts. Merrin began composing, visibly, the diplomatic sentence that would concede nothing while appearing to concede everything.

And Elodie heard it.

Not the words. Under the words. She had spent three weeks learning the old language the way you learn a river — by falling into it repeatedly — and somewhere in the flooded galleries it had stopped being foreign to her gift. The dead spoke to her in intent; the living spoke to her in words; and Orenn, straddling both states like a man astride a wall, was leaking intent into his speech the way a wound leaks blood.

Beneath *drive them out*, she heard: *I am afraid the ground will never be mine again.* Beneath *six hundred years*, she heard: *I missed my daughter's whole life and I cannot say it in any language that will make them understand.* Beneath the fury, thin and constant as a pulse: *do not make me have come back for nothing.*

She stepped forward before she decided to. That was how it always happened, in the end. The gift did not ask permission; it simply used her mouth.

"He doesn't want the families driven out," she said, and the hall turned to her, and Orenn's face went through shock into something rawer. "He wants the ground to be *acknowledged*. He wants a marker. He wants the names read aloud. He wants—" and here she faltered, because the next part was private, grief-shaped, and she had built her whole life on not handling other people's grief carelessly. But the hall was waiting, and silence would be its own cruelty. "He wants someone to know he had a daughter. Lirren. Who was seven. Who is the reason he volunteered to stay awake during the sealing, to keep the others calm, and who died outside the door."

Orenn made a sound that was not a word in any language. Several sleepers bowed their heads. And Master Merrin's carefully composed sentence died unspoken, because there was no diplomatic construction in the world that could stand up against a dead man's daughter's name spoken aloud in front of him.

That was when Elodie understood. Her gift had always been a grief-service for coin. Hear the last words, carve them into bone, hand the bone to the bereaved, take the payment, close the door. But this — this was different. This was translation not of words but of *wounds*, and wounds, unlike words, could not lie, and could not be politely reframed, and could not be signed away in designated territories.

The hall began, haltingly, to use her that way. Every time the argument sharpened — and it sharpened often; six centuries of grievance does not dissolve in two days of daylight — someone would look at her. And she would listen past the words, and speak the fear or the grief underneath, and the argument would collapse into something smaller and truer and survivable.

It was exhausting beyond anything she had ever done. It was also, she realized with a strange vertigo around the second bell, the first time in her life her gift had been *wanted* rather than merely purchased.

* * *

By late afternoon the shape of the covenant had emerged, and it was ugly and beautiful in the way all honest things are ugly and beautiful.

The Spire-folk would return — but as voices and stewards, not conquerors. No territory seized. No families displaced. Instead: the standing stones, the naming-rites, the old tongue taught openly in the towns of Thornfell, the galleries opened as places of mourning for both peoples. The sleepers would walk among the living as advisors, teachers, rememberers — the keepers of six hundred years of witness. Where their old lands were farmed now, the farmers would hold them still, but the names underneath would be restored, spoken, carved. The dead would be heard in daylight. That was the whole of the restitution, and when Vessane proposed it, Orenn wept, and accepted, and Elodie heard beneath his acceptance the thing he could not yet say: that being heard was, in the end, the only home he had left.

The Crown would renounce the seal — fully, publicly, with the original decree read aloud in the capital and the maintenance-orders burned in the same square where the first seal had been proclaimed. Merrin fought that until the sun moved a full hand-width down the light-column, and lost, because Corvane stood up with his flat Warden's voice and said, "I carried out those orders for nineteen years. I will describe what they required. In detail. Unless they are burned." And Merrin, who was many things but not stupid, understood that some silences cost more than confessions.

And Ash—

Ash became the first Speaker-ambassador. It was Vessane who proposed it, and the hall that ratified it, and Ash who sat very still through all of it with their hands flat on their knees, wearing the expression of a child watching adults build a machine and hand them the key.

"One voice of theirs returned willing," Vessane said, invoking the clause that Corvane had found in the decree — the clause no one had obeyed, the clause that had made his career a crime. "One voice, willing, to walk between. Not a hostage. Not a trophy. An ambassador, with the right of refusal, renewed each year." She looked at Ash across the hall. "You were sent to decide. You decided. Now let your deciding be a bridge and not a verdict."

Ash looked at Elodie.

That was the moment she had been dreading all day, and she had known she was dreading it, and she had prepared nothing, because there was nothing to prepare. A child she had taken in against every instinct for survival, a child who had called her mother-of-voices in fever, a child who had shouldered an empire's sin and set it down — that child was looking at her across a hall full of the resurrected and the armed, asking, without words, in the language older than kingdoms, whether it was allowed. Whether choosing this would mean losing her.

Elodie nodded. Once. And added, in her rough, mispronounced, bone-carver's old tongue, the words she had been saving: *"Both worlds. Always both."*

Ash's face did something complicated and young, and then Ash stood, and said in clear Common, the first full sentences Elodie had ever heard from them outside of crisis: "I accept. For one year. Then I choose again." A pause, and the ghost of the stubbornness Elodie had come to love: "Every year. Forever. That is my term."

Vessane smiled — a slow, geological event, six hundred years in the making. "Then it is agreed," she said, "that the ambassador will be stubborn," and for the first time in two days, the hall laughed.

* * *

There was one matter left, and everyone had seen it coming the way you see weather coming over a ridge.

Titles.

Merrin, salvaging what dignity remained to him, proposed that the woman who had translated the covenant into existence be recognized formally. Voice of the Accord, he suggested. Envoy of the Crown. There was talk of a stipend. There was mention, horrifyingly, of a residence in the capital.

"No," Elodie said.

Merrin blinked. "Lady Voss—"

"I'm not a lady. I'm a bone-carver from a valley you'd need a map to find." She kept her voice level, but she could feel the hall listening, the living and the dead alike, and she chose the words the way she chose cuts into bone: precisely, because there was no undoing them. "I watched what titles did in this room today. They turn grief into leverage. They turn witness into property. The moment I hold one, every word I translate becomes negotiable — people will start wondering what the Crown's envoy wants to hear, instead of what the dead actually said." She shook her head. "I'll translate. I'll teach the stones, I'll carve the names, I'll sit in whatever room needs an honest ear. But I do it as nobody. That's the only rank that can't be bought."

Silence. Then, from the pillar where he'd been propped all day, Renn Carrow drawled, "For the record, I offered her a partnership in a smuggling concern once, and she refused that too. Some people are just incorruptible. It's very annoying."

Vessane, who had learned in two days to read the living almost as well as Elodie read the dead, said quietly: "Then we will call you what you already are. Mother-of-voices. No crown required." And the sleepers bowed — not to a title, but to a name given freely, which is the only kind worth bowing to.

Elodie felt it land somewhere under her ribs, in the place where loneliness had lived so long she'd mistaken it for a skeleton. She did not cry. She had used up her crying in the galleries, and in the fever-nights, and at Greyford Bridge. But she stood very still in the column of light for a long moment, dust turning around her, and let herself be *named* — properly, wholly, by people who owed her nothing — and discovered that belonging, which she had assumed was granted like citizenship, was in fact built like a carving: chip by patient chip, and mostly by other people's hands.

* * *

The covenant was written that evening, in three scripts, on vellum and on slate and — at Ash's insistence, and over Merrin's bewilderment — carved into a single piece of Spire-bone by Elodie's own hand, so that the dead could hear its terms forever, and correct anyone who misquoted them.

She carved by lamplight in the side chamber while the hall outside settled into its first true night of peace, sleepers and Wardens and smugglers sharing watch rotations with the easy friction of people who will be strangers again tomorrow but are comrades tonight. Renn sat with her, one-handed, holding the bone steady when her carved fingers tired, saying nothing, which between the two of them had become its own fluent language.

Near midnight, Ash came in, and stood watching the letters rise under her blade, and said, softly, in Common: "It's not perfect."

"No," Elodie agreed, blowing dust from the groove. "It isn't."

"Orenn is still angry. Some of the Wardens won't salute the new watch-captain. Merrin will find a way to break the burning-order, probably. And next winter someone in some village will throw a stone at a Speaker." Ash's voice was steady, but the weight in it was adult, and ancient, and six hundred years deep. "Is that what peace is?"

Elodie set down the knife. She thought of her workshop, rebuilt by neighbors who had never quite welcomed her. Of river-folk who owed nothing and gave anyway. Of a smuggler holding a bone steady with his good hand. Of a knock at a door, one winter ago, that she had nearly not answered.

"Peace isn't the absence of the fight," she said. "It's the craft of it. Imperfect joints, visible seams, mended twice a season. Anyone can carve something flawless out of a single piece. It takes a real maker to join broken ones and let the seams show." She picked the knife back up. "This covenant will crack. And we'll mend it. And it'll crack somewhere else. And we'll mend that too. That's not failure, Ash. That's what keeping something looks like."

Ash considered this with the gravity of a four-hundred-year-old civilization and the face of a child. Then: "Mother-of-voices. When we go home—" and stopped, caught on the word, testing it. *Home.* Both meanings at once. "When we go back to the valley — will you teach me to carve?"

And Elodie smiled, and said yes, and reached for the knife, and made the first cut of the final line —

— when the great doors of the hall boomed open behind them, and a Warden's runner stumbled in, snow on his shoulders, dispatch in his fist, shouting that riders had been sighted on the north road: not the Crown's, and not the Spire-folk's, and flying no banner at all.
