## 32. The Price Paid

The fever came on the third night after the runner, and it came the way debts always came to Renn Carrow: all at once, with interest, and at the worst possible hour.

He remembered fragments of that first bad evening. The hall of the Spire still smelled of dust and old incense and, faintly, of iron — the iron of Warden armor standing down, of a blade lowered by a man who had spent his whole life learning to raise it. There had been shouting in three languages. There had been Elodie's voice cutting through all of them like a knife through wet parchment, translating not just words but *wants*, dragging honesty out of men who had made careers out of burying theirs. And Renn had stood at the edge of it all, holding up a wall that wasn't there, smiling his way through what should have killed him.

Then the wall came down.

He was told afterward — he was told a great many things afterward, most of which he refused to believe until the evidence grew too heavy to argue with — that he had simply folded, mid-sentence, in front of two sleepers, a Warden-Sergeant, and a child who carried an empire's language in her throat. That he had said something charming even as he went down. He hoped so. If a man had to collapse in front of history being made, he ought at least to be quotable.

What he remembered himself was heat. Not the clean heat of a forge or a summer road, but the wet, creeping heat of a wound gone wrong — the slash across his ribs from Greyford Bridge, sewn shut with river-folk thread and smuggler's pride, finally opening like a door that had been waiting six hundred years for someone stupid enough to knock. And under it, the deep bruised ache where a Warden's boot had found him in the galleries, again and again, while he bought time with blood because time was the only coin he had left to spend.

*You knew the price when you drew them off,* he told himself, drifting. *You've never once paid less than full rate.*

The trouble with bargaining was that someone always had to hold the scale.

* * *

Consciousness returned to him in installments, like a merchant paying out a debt in grudging coins.

First: warmth. A fire somewhere close, burning low and steady, fed with the dry old timber of the Spire's upper rooms. Someone had been keeping it fed. Someone had been keeping *him* fed, if the taste in his mouth — broth, bitter herbs, honey — meant anything.

Second: weight. A blanket heavier than any he owned, smelling of cedar and stone-dust and something older, some herb the Spire-folk must have kept in their sealed stores against exactly this kind of emergency. Six hundred years underground and they'd still packed a medicine chest. Renn decided, in his haze, that he liked these people enormously and would have to tell them so.

Third: pain, arriving last because pain, like Renn, preferred to make an entrance.

He opened his eyes to a ceiling of pale stone carved with names. Of course it was. Everything in this mountain was carved with names now; you couldn't spit without hitting somebody's grandmother in letterform. He'd have laughed, except laughing pulled at the stitches and turned the laugh into a grunt, and the grunt into a long gray minute of simply breathing.

"You're awake." A voice from the fire. Low. Careful. The voice of someone who had been sitting vigil long enough to be afraid of hope. "Don't move. The bone-setter said — actually, she said quite a lot, none of it repeatable in company."

Elodie rose from her chair by the hearth and crossed the room, and Renn got his first real look at her in — how long? Days. A week. Long enough that she looked like a stranger who had stolen a friend's face: thinner, paler, shadows under her eyes deep enough to store things in. Her hands, those precise carver's hands, were stained with herb-juice and lamp-black instead of bone-dust.

She looked, he thought, terrible.

She looked, he thought, like she hadn't slept properly in a week, and there was only one explanation for that, and the explanation hit him harder than the fever ever had.

"How long," he managed. His voice came out like gravel poured through a cracked pipe.

"Nine days." She sat on the edge of the cot — sat, not perched, not hovered, *sat*, like someone settling in for a siege. "Three of them we weren't sure you'd keep. Ash sat with you every evening and sang the quiet songs. The ones that don't wake anything." A ghost of a smile. "The healers here say the old songs mend bone. I think they mostly just gave you something worth waking up for."

"Flatterer." He tried for his usual grin and got about sixty percent of it. "And you? What was your post?"

"Carving." She said it lightly. Too lightly. Then, because Elodie Voss lied well about everything except the things that mattered: "I stayed. Nights. When they let me. You talk in your fever, did you know that? Mostly nonsense. Prices and routes and apologies to people whose names I didn't know." Her fingers found a loose thread on the blanket and worried it. "Once you said my name. Just that. Over and over. Like you were checking it was still there."

Renn looked up at the ceiling of names, because looking at her suddenly required more courage than facing Corvane's blade had.

"I remember the bridge," he said. "Greyford. Holding the span. I remember thinking — this is a fair trade. My blood for their road out. Simple arithmetic." He paused. "I've done the arithmetic my whole life, Elodie. Every job, every favor, every debt. In, out, owed, paid. It keeps a man honest the way a cage keeps a bird safe." His breath caught; she leaned forward; he waved her back with two fingers. "But lying there — drowning in my own heat — I kept trying to work out what I owed *you*. For the road. For Ash. For staying. And the sums wouldn't come out. They wouldn't balance. Do you know why?"

"Because you're delirious?"

"Because there's no currency for it." He turned his head, finally, and met her eyes, and let her see whatever it was he'd been hiding behind charm since the day he'd knocked on her workshop door collecting a debt and found a reason to stay. "I'm not good at this. I'm good at exits. I've got more exits than a rat has holes. But there's no exit from this one, and I find—" his voice failed, steadied, failed again, and he pressed on raw— "*I find I don't want one.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere far below, faint as memory, the Spire hummed its low new note — the sound of a silence ending, sustained now by covenant instead of seal.

Elodie didn't speak for a long moment. When she did, her voice was rough as whetstone.

"I know," she said. "I heard you say my name nine times a night for a week, Renn Carrow. I know exactly what you're trying to admit, and I'll save us both the dignity, since you haven't any left to spare." She took his hand — carefully, avoiding the splinted fingers, cradling it like something carved. "I stayed. Every night. Because I have spent my whole life listening to the dead tell me what mattered to them *after it was too late to matter*, and I am not going to spend one more minute of my life pretending I can't hear what matters while it's still warm."

"That," said Renn, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever threatened me with."

"And I'll do it again," she said, and leaned down, and kissed him — gently, mindful of the stitches, thorough in everything else — and the ceiling full of names watched over them, every carved soul in that mountain bearing witness to the first honest thing either of them had said aloud.

When she pulled back, her eyes were wet, and she was smiling, and she looked terrible, and she looked like home.

"Ash is going to be insufferable," Renn croaked.

"Ash already knows." Elodie wiped her cheek with the back of her wrist, unashamed. "Ash has known longer than either of us. Says we're 'slow for adults.'"

"Outrageous. Accurate, but outrageous."

She laughed — really laughed, the first one in days — and settled beside him on the narrow cot, her shoulder careful against his unhurt side, and outside the snow was falling on Thornfell, on the valley, on the road north where bannerless riders had been sighted and questions waited that no fever could burn away. But that was tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight the sums, for once, balanced.

It was Ash who brought the news, hours later — slipping into the room on bare feet, pausing at the sight of them, and grinning the grin of a child who has won a very long bet. Then the grin faded, and Ash held out a strip of birch bark covered in hurried charcoal marks, translated already in a second hand beneath.

"They found the riders' camp," Ash said quietly. "Corvane sent word. The banners are blank because—" the child swallowed— "because they carry no crest at all. Only words. Painted in the old tongue."

Renn read the translation, and felt the warmth drain out of the room.

The words on the banners read: *We buried them once. We will not need to dig twice.*

And beneath, in smaller letters, addressed by name:

*To the mother-of-voices, the smuggler, and the child who decided: we are coming to finish what our grandfathers started.*
