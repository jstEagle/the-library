## 33. Reckonings Above Ground

The tribunal chamber had once been a chapel.

Corvane knew this because he had memorized the building's history the way he had once memorized the Warden code—line by line, stone by stone, in the belief that knowing a thing's bones meant you could trust it. The vaulted ceiling still bore soot-scars from candles burned by monks three centuries dead. The stained glass had been replaced with plain leaded panes when the Crown requisitioned the hall, and now grey winter light fell through them in flat, honest sheets, illuminating nothing and forgiving everything.

He sat at the witness table with his hands folded on its scarred oak surface, and he waited to be called.

Below him, arranged in a shallow crescent, sat the Tribunal of Reckoning: two Crown justices in slate-grey robes, one delegate from the new Spire covenant—a woman named Sereth Vail whose skin bore the fine silver tracery of six hundred years of sealed sleep—and, at the end of the row, a seat that had been empty for nine days and would remain empty for nine more: the seat reserved for the Speaker-ambassador, who was a child, and whose presence was required only for the sessions touching the old tongue.

This was the third day of testimony. Corvane had listened to eleven hours of it yesterday. He had heard Warden-Major Aldric Vess describe, in a voice like a man reading an inventory, the disposal protocols for "linguistic anomalies." He had heard the phrase *mercy disposition* used forty-one times. He had counted.

The counting was a sickness. He recognized it as such. It had begun in the galleries beneath the Weeping Galleries, when he had knelt before the great door carved from the mountain's single vertebra and read aloud the original seal-decree—the clause no one obeyed, the clause about *a voice of theirs returning willing*—and understood in one vertiginous moment that his entire career had been the maintenance of a crime so old it had calcified into law.

"Warden-Sergeant Corvane."

He rose. His knees ached. He was fifty-one years old, and until four months ago he had believed that ache was simply what service felt like.

"State your name and rank for the record."

"Warden-Sergeant Dain Corvane, formerly of the Third Vigilance, Thornfell circuit. Twenty-six years' service."

"You are here voluntarily."

"I requested this hearing." His voice came out level. He had practiced that too, in his quarters at night, saying the words to the dark the way a man rehearses stepping off a cliff. "I requested the right to testify against my own hierarchy, and against myself."

Justice Orlane, the elder of the two, leaned forward. She had kind eyes, which Corvane distrusted on principle; kindness in a tribunal was a tool like any other. "Sergeant, the Tribunal has heard testimony regarding the seal, the kill-orders issued under Warden Directive Nine, and the events at Greyford Bridge and the Weeping Galleries. What we have not heard—from any officer of your seniority—is an account from inside the machinery. You understand what you're offering."

"I understand I am offering evidence against men I served beside for half my life. I understand some of them will hang." He let that stand in the cold light a moment. "I also understand that if I do not offer it, the ones who gave the orders will retire on pensions, and the ones who carried them out will carry them alone, forever, without a name attached."

Sereth Vail spoke for the first time, her voice carrying the strange doubled resonance that all the waking Spire-folk had—their own voice, and beneath it, faintly, the choir of voices they had kept company with in the dark. "You were present at the interrogation of the smuggler Renn Carrow. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"You struck him."

"I did." No flinch. He had decided, months ago, that he would not soften a single blow aimed at himself. Softened testimony was just another form of the lie he had lived inside. "Twice. Once with an open hand, once with a baton. He had information about the child's location. My orders were to extract it. I told myself afterward that he was a criminal and I was a soldier and the arithmetic was simple." A breath. "It was not simple. It has never once been simple. That is the thing I most want entered into the record, if nothing else: every man in that hierarchy told himself the arithmetic was simple, and none of us believed it, and we obeyed anyway, because obedience was easier than believing."

Orlane made a note. "And do you believe it now? That obedience was the sin?"

"I believe obedience was the *habit*," Corvane said. "The sin was older. The sin was deciding, six hundred years ago, that a people could be sealed alive behind a door and called peace instead of murder. Everything after that was just housekeeping. We were not soldiers, Justice. We were janitors of a tomb, and we swept the floor every day and called it honor."

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere above, in the old bell-loft, a pigeon shifted its weight and dislodged a crumb of mortar that ticked down onto the flagstones like a small clock.

*

They broke at midday. Corvane ate alone in the courtyard, as he had eaten alone for four months, standing against the wall of the colonnade where the sun reached for perhaps an hour past noon. The bread was fresh; the kitchens fed witnesses well. He tasted none of it.

Renn Carrow found him there.

The smuggler looked better than he had any right to. The wounds taken in the galleries had nearly killed him—one lung had filled, they said, and for three nights the healers had not been sure—but he walked now with only a slight favoring of his left side, and his charm had reassembled itself around him like a coat put back on after a beating, hiding the bruises but not quite sitting right.

"Sergeant." Renn leaned on the colonnade beside him, uninvited. "You look like a man auditing his own funeral."

"That is approximately accurate."

"Heard you told the Tribunal we were janitors of a tomb." Renn turned his face up to the thin sun. There was a new scar along his jaw, pale against the weathered brown. "Poetic. Didn't have you down for poetic."

"I wasn't always a Warden. Before that I was a boy who read too much."

"Mm." Renn was quiet a moment. When he spoke again the charm had dropped away entirely, and what remained underneath was harder and younger than Corvane had expected. "The young one at the bridge. Pellam. Nineteen. He saw the girl's face and he couldn't do it, and Vess shot him for faltering." He didn't make it a question. "I've thought about that every day since. How close it came. One boy's hesitation, one smuggler with a bridge to hold, one bone-carver who wouldn't hand over a child." He shook his head slowly. "Six hundred years of it balanced on that. And the whole time, your lot knew. Knew they were alive down there. Knew the decree had a door in it."

"Yes."

"And you're testifying because?"

Corvane considered the question honestly, which was more than he had managed for fifty years. "Because justice that costs the guilty nothing is just bookkeeping," he said at last. "And because a man who finds the ledger rotten can either burn it or keep writing in it. I kept writing in it for twenty-six years. This is me putting down the pen."

Renn studied him sideways. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, rough sound with no mockery in it. "Elodie said you'd come around eventually. Said she could hear it in how you asked your questions back in the valley. Like a man checking a lock he hoped was already broken."

"She said that?"

"To Ash, actually. In the old tongue, half of it. But yes." He pushed off the wall, wincing slightly. "For what it's worth, Sergeant—you held the span at Greyford Bridge against men under your own command rather than let Vess's squad cross after us. I know why you did it. So does the Tribunal. Make sure they write *that* in the ledger too."

He limped away across the courtyard, and Corvane stood in the failing warmth and felt something in his chest that was not quite forgiveness and not quite hope, but occupied the same territory.

*

The afternoon session went worse.

Vess was brought in in irons to answer for Directive Nine—the standing order classifying old-tongue speakers as *existential threats requiring mercy disposition*—and Corvane watched the man he had served under for eleven years perform a masterwork of self-erasure. Vess did not deny the order. He reframed it. He spoke of stability, of the truce, of the *unfortunate necessity* of acting before understanding, of how history would judge them gently because history judged all guardians gently. He had a justice laughing at one point—a dry, appalled laugh, but a laugh—and Corvane felt something curdle in him as he recognized the technique, because he had used it himself, in briefing rooms, for decades. Make the horror administrative. Make it dull. Dullness was the best armor ever forged; nothing pierced it.

When Vess was led out, he passed Corvane's table, and for one moment their eyes met.

There was no hatred in the old man's face. That was the worst of it. There was only pity—pity, from the architect of mercy-dispositions—and beneath it a flicker of something almost like appeal, as though Vess were looking at a younger version of himself who had wandered off the path into the snow, and thinking: *he'll come back. They always come back.*

Corvane looked away first. He would remember that he had looked away first, and he would be glad of it, later, in the way a man is glad of small mercies done to his own soul.

"The Tribunal notes the witness's distress," Justice Orlane said quietly. "We will recess."

"I'm not distressed," Corvane said. "I'm finished being distressed. Distress was the last luxury I permitted myself. Ask your questions."

Sereth Vail's doubled voice, when it came, seemed to fill the old chapel to its sooted rafters. "Then we will ask the question that matters. Sergeant Corvane. If the Tribunal grants full clemency—if you walk from this room tomorrow with your rank restored, your pension intact, your name clean—what would you do with it?"

He had expected many questions. Not that one. He sat with it, and the honest silence stretched long enough that the justices exchanged glances.

"Nothing," he said finally. "I don't want it back. Clemency given to a man who wants his old life returned isn't clemency—it's a leash with a longer rope. What I want—" and here his voice did something he had not authorized, dropping rough and low— "what I want is a road."

"A road."

"There are roads out of Thornfell that haven't been safe in six hundred years because Wardens made them unsafe. Roads the river-folk won't travel, roads the villages whisper about. And now the covenant says the Spire-folk return as stewards, that the old-tongue may be sung in daylight, that the names get carved on standing stones where travelers can read them." He thought of Elodie Voss's hands—carver's hands, scarred and steady—doing exactly that work somewhere north of him even now. "Someone has to guard those roads. Not the secret. Never again the secret. The *road*. The people on it. The child walking it home for the season, if she chooses, and the carter hauling stone, and the singer learning verses her grandmother was beaten for knowing. That work needs doing and no one in the Vigilance is fit for it except the ones who broke."

He stopped, aware he had said more than rehearsal allowed, and found he did not regret it.

Justice Orlane wrote for a long moment. Then: "The Tribunal will consider your petition. Sergeant—do you understand that guarding a road is not glory? It will be weather and bandits and broken cartwheels and drunkards. Some days it will look like ruin, next to what you might have been."

"Some days," Corvane agreed, "justice looks like ruin. I've learned to tell the difference between ruin and demolition. Demolition is what happens before something honest gets built." He rose, knees aching, and bowed—not to the justices, he realized afterward, but to Sereth Vail, and through her to the six hundred years behind her doubled voice.

*

That night he could not sleep, and so he did what he had done every night for four months: he took out the seal-decree copy and read the clause again.

*...save that should a voice of theirs return willing, the seal shall render up its charge, for the mountain keeps faith with choice and not with chains.*

Six hundred years. Six centuries of Wardens reciting the decree by rote in their induction ceremonies—he had recited it himself at nineteen, voice cracking, candlelight on the old vellum—and not one of them, apparently, had ever read past the part about enforcement to the part about release. Or worse: they had read it, and understood it, and buried it, because a truce built on a tomb requires the tomb stay shut, and a door in the wording was a crack that might someday let in a child with ash-grey eyes and a language older than kingdoms.

He set the decree down. Outside his window, the capital lay under its usual haze of chimney smoke and lamplight, ordinary and oblivious. Somewhere north, beyond the passes, a tower that had been silent for six hundred years was learning to ring.

A knock at his door—soft, uncertain, near midnight.

He opened it on a courier, mud-spattered, half-frozen, holding out a letter bearing no seal but a small pressed sprig of rowan, which meant nothing to the postal service and everything to anyone who had traveled the Thornfell roads in autumn.

He broke it open by candlelight. The hand was unfamiliar—careful, unhurried, the script of someone accustomed to carving letters rather than writing them.

*Sergeant Corvane. I'm told tribunals ask their questions and go quiet, and men left waiting fill the silence with dread. So: the Tribunal voted yesterday. Your petition is granted. You will have your road, and a warrant signed jointly by the Crown and the Spire—which is a thing that has never existed before, so forgive us if the ink is still wet on the precedent.*

*I am writing for a second reason. Ash returns north next month for the winter season, traveling the Greyford road. The Council of Voices requests—requests, mind, not orders—an escort. Someone the child knows. Someone who lowered his blade when it mattered. Renn Carrow cannot ride yet; the healers say another month. Elodie is needed at the stones. Which leaves, the Council observes with what I suspect is deliberate mischief, precisely one man qualified.*

*Come to Thornfell, Sergeant. The pass is beautiful in winter. The dead sing there now, gently. And there is a workshop at the valley's edge, rebuilt by neighbors who owe you nothing, where the kettle is always hot and the woman who owns it says—her words—that a man who tears down a rotten thing owes the world a season of guarding what grows in its place.*

*The road will be open at first light. — E.V.*

Corvane sat for a long while, the letter in his hands, the candle guttering, the soot-scarred saints of a forgotten chapel keeping their plaster vigil overhead.

Then he began, methodically, to pack.
