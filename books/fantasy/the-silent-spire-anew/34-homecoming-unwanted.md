## 34. Homecoming, Unwanted

The valley announced itself before they could see it — by smell first, then sound. Pine resin and cold stone and woodsmoke from a dozen chimneys, the particular mineral tang of the Knucklebone stream where it ran over slate. And beneath all of it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, the hum of the mountain: gentler now than it had ever been in her memory, a lullaby where there had once been only silence pressing down like snowfall.

Elodie reined the mule to a stop at the last bend of the road, where the pines opened onto the valley floor, and did not move for a long moment.

Below her, the village of Harrow's Rest lay exactly as she had left it — which was to say, not at all as she had left it, because nothing ever was. Smoke rose from crooked chimneys. A dog barked somewhere near the mill. The terraced fields wore their winter stubble like shaved heads. And there, on the rise above the stream where her workshop had stood for nineteen years —

She had expected ash. She had prepared herself for ash, for the black scar of it, for the sight of her life reduced to a stain on the hillside. She had composed, over three hundred miles of road, the exact shape of the grief she would allow herself to feel: brief, contained, dignified. A bone-carver's grief. Clean cuts.

Instead there was a building.

It stood where her workshop had stood — larger, actually, its roof freshly thatched, its walls new-milled timber still pale as bone in the morning light. Smoke curled from its chimney. The great south window she'd never been able to afford was there anyway, glass and all, catching the sun. A woodpile stood stacked against the eastern wall with the obsessive neatness of someone who cared about such things. And hanging beside the door, swinging gently in the valley wind, was a sign.

She squinted. Then she made out the letters, burned into the plank in careful, workmanlike script:

*E. VOSS — BONE-CARVER*

Beside it, smaller, someone had added: *and other services.*

Elodie sat very still on the mule's back. Beside her, Ash looked up with that expression the child wore when reading her face — the old-tongue word for it, she'd learned, translated roughly as *weather-reading*, as though a person's feelings were a sky to be studied for what came next.

"It's still standing," Ash said. Not a question. The child spoke the common tongue now, haltingly, with an accent like water moving over stones, but some sentences came out whole and unbroken, and this was one of them. "You thought it would be gone."

"I thought—" Elodie stopped. Started again. "I set fire to it myself, Ash. I watched it burn. I stood in the snow and I made sure every carving, every tool, every scrap of evidence that you had ever been under my roof went into the flames." Her hands, wrapped around the reins, were steady. They were always steady. That had been her armor for two decades: steady hands, a closed door, a reputation for competence so complete that no one thought to wonder whether she was lonely behind it. "I don't understand what I'm looking at."

Renn Carrow, riding behind them on his shaggy bay mare, leaned forward in the saddle and grinned the grin that had gotten him thrown out of half the taverns in Thornfell and welcomed into the other half.

"You don't understand kindness, is what you mean," he said. "Which has always been your tragedy, love. You can hear the voices of the dead but you've got no ear at all for the living."

"Careful."

"I'm always careful. It's my defining trait." He nudged the mare forward, past her, and paused alongside, lowering his voice — though Ash heard everything now; the child heard everything; that was rather the point of Ash. "Look at the joinery on that window frame before you decide how you feel about any of this. Somebody spent real money on that glass. Somebody carried it up from Lowmarket on a cart, over roads that would shake teeth loose. That's not guilt, Elodie. Guilt sends bread. That's something else."

She didn't answer him. She put her heels to the mule and started down into the valley, toward the house that should have been a ruin, and felt — beneath the confusion, beneath the wariness that nineteen years of careful neutrality had built into her like grain into wood — something small and dangerous beginning to move in her chest.

* * *

The neighbors found them before they reached the village proper.

They came out one by one, then in clusters, then as a loose procession — Marta Ostrewe from the dyeshop, her forearms still blue-stained as they always were; old Tam Hedgerow leaning on his grandson's arm; the miller's family entire; children who scattered ahead shouting the news the way children do, so that by the time Elodie dismounted at the foot of her own hill, half of Harrow's Rest had assembled in the lane below it, keeping a distance that was itself familiar, respectful, the distance of people who had never quite decided what to make of the bone-woman on the hill.

Nineteen years, and they had kept that distance. They brought her their dead, yes — wrapped in linen, carried up the path with bowed heads and coins counted out in advance. They paid her well and spoke to her politely and invited her to nothing. When the Wardens came through, they looked away from her windows. When the smugglers came through, they looked away too. She had thought this was the arrangement, and she had told herself she preferred it.

Now Marta Ostrewe pushed through the little crowd, wiped her hands on her apron, and said, "The roof took us most of a month. The thatch wanted doing twice — Tam insisted, after the frost came early. He's stubborn about thatch."

Elodie stared at her.

"You rebuilt it," she said. It came out flat, almost accusatory, and she heard the accusation and couldn't soften it. "I burned it. You saw the smoke. Everyone saw the smoke. The Wardens questioned—"

"The Wardens questioned," Marta agreed, "and we told them what we saw, which was a chimney fire, badly flued, very sad, happens every winter to somebody." Her chin came up a fraction. She was a broad, weathered woman with dye-stained hands and the unbothered gaze of someone who had argued with merchants for thirty years and won more often than not. "We're not clever people here, bone-carver, whatever else you may think of us. But we know what a lie looks like when we choose to tell one, and we knew whose house was burning and why."

"Why?" The question escaped before Elodie could cage it. It sounded, even to her own ears, raw. "Why would you do this? I lived among you nineteen years and none of you—" She stopped. Closed her eyes briefly. Opened them. "I'm not ungrateful. I'm trying to understand."

Marta studied her for a moment, and something in the woman's face shifted — softened — in a way Elodie had never seen directed at her. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"You want to know why nobody asked you to the midsummer feast," Marta said. "Why we left you up on your hill with your dead and your quiet."

"I didn't say—"

"You didn't have to. It's written on you the same way it's written on Tam, who hasn't spoken to his brother since the flood year. Same way it used to be written on Widow Corren before she died, gods rest her, alone in that big house with all her grudges arranged around her like furniture." Marta shrugged, and the gesture was oddly gentle. "People are afraid of what you do. That's true, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But fear keeps its distance, and what we gave you was worse than distance — we gave you *politeness*. Politeness is just distance that's had manners drilled into it." She glanced up at the workshop, at the pale new timbers, and her voice roughened slightly. "Then the Wardens came asking questions about a child, and Sergeant Corvane — that cold man, that terrible polite man — he asked me if I'd seen anything unusual at your house, and I said no, ma'am, nothing unusual, quiet neighbor, keeps to herself. And walking home afterward I thought: that's what we did to her. We made her *nothing unusual*. We watched a woman live alone with the dead for nineteen years and called it keeping to herself, and then we watched her house burn and called it a chimney fire, and both times we told ourselves we were protecting ourselves, but the truth—" Marta's jaw tightened. "The truth is the night your workshop burned, Tam Hedgerow stood in the lane and cried. Old Tam, who hasn't cried since his wife died. Because he'd carried his mother's finger-bones up your path eleven years ago, and he remembered how you'd come to the door in the middle of the night without complaint, and made tea, and listened to a dead woman speak through her own knucklebone for four hours so Tam wouldn't have to grieve blind."

Elodie's throat had gone tight. "That was my work," she managed. "I was paid for it."

"You were paid *for it*, yes." Marta's eyes glinted. "And the baker is paid for bread, and we don't call the bread a miracle either, but we thank him anyway. That's the whole of it, bone-carver. That's the ordinary, stupid, small truth of it. You did your work, and we took it, and we never once said thank you, and when your house burned down we discovered we owed you a debt we didn't know how to carry." She gestured at the hillside, the new roof, the stacked wood, the sign. "So we built. Badly, some of it — Tam's joinery is a crime and everyone knows it — but we built."

Behind her, the crowd shuffled. Old Tam raised a hand, embarrassed, and lowered it again. One of the miller's daughters was staring at Ash with open fascination, and Ash, to Elodie's astonishment, stared back and then — deliberately, solemnly — hummed three notes. Just three, soft, the opening phrase of the river-song the Spire-folk had taught them at the parting. The girl's mouth fell open. Somewhere in the crowd, someone whispered, *"that's the song,"* and Elodie understood that the old-tongue melodies had traveled ahead of them, had arrived in Harrow's Rest before they did, carried in taverns and letters and the unstoppable traffic of human mouths, and that her neighbors had spent the winter humming the music of a people they'd been taught were monsters, and finding it beautiful.

Belonging, she thought. It doesn't knock at the front door. It comes up the hill while you're looking the other way, carrying lumber.

* * *

They went inside in ones and twos throughout the afternoon, and each visit undid her further.

The interior was her workshop and not her workshop. Her bench was there — her actual bench, hauled out of the flames' path by somebody, scorched along one leg but sound, sanded and oiled until the burn mark showed like a dark vein in pale wood, left unrepaired on purpose, she suspected. Her tools hung on the wall, or tools enough of them; the rest had been replaced, bought piece by piece from Lowmarket smiths, and the new chisels gleamed unused beside the old ones whose handles bore the polish of her grip. On the shelf above the bench, where she had once kept her finished commissions waiting for collection, someone had placed a single jar of honey, a bundle of dried sage, and a folded note in a child's careful hand: *welcome home.*

She read the note four times. Then she set it down and walked to the south window — the impossible window, the expensive one — and stood looking out at the valley with her arms crossed tight over her chest as though holding something in.

Renn found her there. He had a genius, she had long ago admitted, for finding her at exactly the moments she least wished to be found, and for saying precisely the wrong-seeming thing that turned out to be right.

"Marta tells me the glass cost more than most folk here earn in a season," he said, leaning against the window frame. "Pooled it. Forty households. There's a ledger — Tam kept it, of course Tam kept it, the man alphabetizes his nails. Forty names. They wouldn't let me see it, but Marta says some of the entries aren't money. Firewood. Cart-hauling. One family gave a pig."

"A pig."

"A very fine pig. Don't ask me to explain the economy of pigs; I'm a smuggler, we deal in things with clearer prices." He was quiet a moment. Outside, Ash was in the yard showing a ring of village children how to fold paper boats — paper Renn had bought them in the last town, already sacrificed, already sailing the irrigation channel — and the sound of their laughter drifted up through the glass, thin and bright as birdsong. "You know what I keep thinking about? Greyford Bridge." His voice lost its levity entirely. "That boy. The young Warden who died with your name half-spoken and Ash's face in front of him. I think about him more than I'd like to admit. And I think: he never got a window like this. Never got forty households deciding he was worth a season's wages. Nobody built him anything." He turned to look at her, and his dark eyes held the thing he usually kept behind charm, the debt he never named, the reason he'd driven them through Thornfell when every instinct of self-preservation said ride the other way. "You got out, Elodie. You got the covenant, you got the Spire singing, you got the child folding boats in your yard. And now you've got this — the least dramatic miracle imaginable. Some neighbors and some lumber and a jar of honey." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired at the edges. "Let it be hard. Let it wreck you a little. That's allowed."

She laughed — a wet, cracked sound that was mostly not laughter at all — and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"I spent nineteen years telling myself I preferred it," she said. "The distance. The closed door. I told myself the isolation was *discipline*. Professional neutrality. A bone-carver can't be entangled; entanglement makes you dishonest with the grieving families, it makes you want to give them comfort instead of truth." Her voice frayed. "And it was all true, and it was all a lie, and I cannot tell you how furious I am that it took a war and a burned house and forty names in a ledger for anyone — including me — to notice."

"Forty-one," Renn said.

She looked up. "What?"

"There's forty-one entries in the ledger." He tapped the window frame, once. "Last page. No name, just initials, and an amount that would embarrass the pig. I may have made inquiries. I may have bribed Tam Hedgerow with a bottle of very good brandy, which he accepted with the dignity of a bishop taking communion." His grin returned, crooked and undefended. "R.C., Elodie. Paid in full, dated the week your workshop burned — before I even reached Thornfell, before I knew if you were alive. I heard the smoke was visible from the pass and I sent money to strangers to build you a house I wasn't sure you'd live to sleep in." He shrugged, and the shrug tried for carelessness and missed by a mile. "Guilt wears charm as armor, I've told you. But apparently sometimes it takes off the armor and buys window glass."

Elodie looked at him for a long time. Nineteen years of solitude, one winter of war, and this — a smuggler's initials hidden in a carpenter's ledger, a dyewoman's blunt mercy, a child's note beside a jar of honey. None of it earned. All of it given sideways, awkwardly, by imperfect people in an imperfect valley, which was, she understood suddenly and completely, the only way belonging had ever been given to anyone, anywhere, in the whole history of the world.

"Renn Carrow," she said, "you are the worst sentimental fool in Thornfell."

"I contain multitudes."

She kissed him. Briefly, fiercely, on the mouth, and then stepped back and pointed at the door with a hand that was not quite steady. "Out. I have a workshop to inspect, and I intend to cry into this honey jar in private, and you will pretend not to know about it forever."

"Forever," he agreed solemnly, and went, whistling, and outside in the yard Ash looked up at the window and smiled the small, weather-reading smile of a child who had known all along exactly what sky was coming.

* * *

Dusk came down blue and gold over Harrow's Rest, and Elodie lit her own fire in her own hearth for the first time in a year.

Ash sat cross-legged on the floor nearby, working on a boat that would never float, and the workshop around them settled into its evening sounds — wood ticking as it warmed, wind testing the thatch, the stream below. And underneath, woven through everything, the mountain's gentle hum, the six hundred voices of the Spire-folk sounding through stone, through soil, through the roots of the pines. They were quieter here than at the Spire itself, but present, patient, a choir learning to whisper instead of wail. Sometimes, in the last months, Elodie had begun to catch individual voices in it — the eldest sleeper, dry and amused; the young one who had died at Greyford's bridge, mourned by both sides now, his name carved on a standing stone at the pass. The dead consulted her now. It had changed the work entirely. Grief-services were becoming conversations.

A knock came at the door.

Not a strange knock. A familiar one — the double-rap of Marta Ostrewe, who entered without waiting, as she evidently now intended to enter forever, carrying a covered basket and followed by old Tam Hedgerow, who was holding, of all things, a piglet.

"We thought," Marta began, "seeing as you're home and all, and seeing as the ledger mentions a pig that has since become several pigs, that you might want—"

"Mother-of-voices," Ash said quietly, and both visitors stopped.

Elodie froze. Ash had used the old-tongue name only twice since the Spire, both times asleep, both times in fever-dreams of the galleries. Now the child said it in full wakefulness, looking at the door, at the basket, at Tam and his piglet, at the ordinary domestic abundance of it all — and translated, deliberately, for the room:

"In the old tongue it isn't a title. It means: *the one the voices chose to stay with.*" Ash smiled. "I only just understood it properly. It doesn't mean you belong to the dead, Elodie. It means the dead noticed where you belonged. They were waiting for you to notice too."

Silence held the room for a heartbeat. Then Tam cleared his throat, thrust the piglet forward with tremendous ceremony, and said, "Her name's Duchess. She eats anything. Welcome home, missus."

And Elodie Voss — who had spent nineteen years perfecting the art of the steady hand and the closed door, who had faced Wardens and ghosts and the parliament of dust itself without weeping — took the piglet, and burst into tears in front of her neighbors, and found, to her lasting astonishment, that she did not mind being seen.

Outside, far down the valley road, where the light was failing and the first stars were pricking through, a rider had stopped at the bend and sat looking at the lit windows of the bone-carver's house. A tall figure in a Warden's grey cloak, travel-worn, unhurried. Warden-Sergeant Corvane — no longer sergeant, no longer Warden at all, if the rumors that had traveled the roads ahead of him were true — sat his horse in the gathering dark for a long moment, as though rehearsing something. Then he dismounted, tied his horse at the gate like any ordinary traveler, and knocked.
