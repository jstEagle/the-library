## 35. The Child Who Decided

The Spire had a way of looking at you.

Ash felt it now, standing in the great hall where the sleepers had woken into their second lives — felt it the way one feels sunlight through a window that has been shuttered for six hundred years: warm, and heavy, and impossible to ignore. A thousand faces turned toward the dais where the eldest sleeper sat wrapped in grey wool that had been dust an hour before anyone thought to weave it. The hall smelled of stone and honey-smoke and the faint green sweetness of things beginning to grow again in places that had forgotten how.

They were waiting for Ash to answer.

The eldest sleeper's name was Vessane, though she had not used it aloud since before the sealing. She spoke in the old tongue, slow as roots growing, and every word fell into Ash's chest like a stone dropped into deep water.

*You were sent out to decide,* she said. *And you have decided. The door opened. The voices rose. The covenant is struck.* Her eyes were the color of river ice, ancient and clear. *Now we ask what we should have asked six hundred years ago. What do you want, little carrier of our language? Name it, and it is yours.*

Ash understood, then, that this was the trap.

Not a cruel trap. Not even a deliberate one. But a trap all the same, the kind woven from love and guilt and six centuries of silence. Because Ash could feel what they wanted Ash to want. It pressed against the skin like heat from a forge. They wanted Ash to stay. To be the bridge, the first Speaker, the child who walked out of the dark carrying the language and came home crowned in it. They wanted to give Ash a place at Vessane's right hand, a tower room with windows facing the sunrise, a title older than any kingdom's — *the Voice Returned*. They wanted, more than anything, for the choosing to be over. For someone to belong to them the way they had once belonged to each other, before the door closed.

And Ash wanted it too. That was the terrible part. Ash wanted it the way a drowning thing wants air.

* * *

Ash had been three kinds of person on the road, and each one had been real.

There was the Ash who spoke no tongue any kingdom knew, who hummed melodies that made bone whisper, who was a riddle and a burden and a hunted thing. There was the Ash who lay fevered in a river-boat while Elodie held cool cloths to a burning forehead and wept without knowing why, calling her — calling Ash — by a word that meant *mother-of-voices*, meaning it, needing it, being answered. And there was the Ash who stood before the sealed heart of the Spire and sang the opening verse with shaking hands and a throat full of fear, who shouldered an empire's sin because no one else could lift it.

All three of those people lived in Ash's chest now, crowded together like refugees around a small fire.

The Spire wanted only the last one. The hero. The symbol. The Voice Returned.

But the Spire did not know about the fire in Elodie's workshop the night they left the valley — how Elodie had stood in the snow watching her whole life burn and had not looked away, and how Ash had taken her hand, and how neither of them had let go until the cold made them. The Spire did not know about Renn Carrow teaching Ash to cheat at knucklebones on the road, laughing his low smoky laugh when Ash won, cheating worse when Ash lost so the losing wouldn't sting. The Spire did not know about the river-folk who owed nothing and gave everything, or the way Elodie's workshop had smelled of cedar shavings and tallow, or the sound a bone-carver's knife makes against a morning quiet enough to hear it.

The Spire knew Ash as the child who decided. It did not know that deciding was not finished.

* * *

*I want,* Ash began, and stopped.

The old tongue had words for everything the dead needed said — grief, warning, covenant, the particular sorrow of a tower full of living people listening to the mortar set. But it had grown thin in some places, starved in others, and there was no clean word for what Ash meant. So Ash did what Ash had always done: reached past the language to the thing underneath it.

Ash looked at Vessane. Then Ash looked past her, at the doors of the hall, open now for the first time in six hundred years, letting in wind that smelled of pine and rain and the ordinary world.

Then Ash sang.

Not the formal verses. Not the covenant-speech the elders had been rehearsing among themselves since the waking, Ash could hear them humming it even now, half-formed, hopeful. Ash sang the small song — the one learned on a smuggler's cart between Thornfell passes, a Renn Carrow original, four lines long, mostly nonsense, about a mule named Grudge who refused to die. Ash sang it in the old tongue, which made it stranger and funnier, and heard the melody land in the hall like a pebble in a still pool.

Some of the sleepers flinched. Some frowned. And down near the back, one old man whose face was still learning how to be a face again — one of the first to wake, one of the ones who had argued loudest in the Parliament of Dust for forgiveness over vengeance — began, helplessly, to laugh.

That was the point. That was the whole point, and Ash watched it move through the hall like light through water: the understanding that the world outside had not been waiting, empty and hostile, for the Spire-folk to return. The world outside had been *living*. Making songs about stubborn mules. Building families out of strangers. Burning workshops and rebuilding them sideways, through neighbors who never quite said welcome but showed up with hammers anyway.

*I will come back,* Ash said, and the old tongue found its footing again, steady now. *Every year. When the mountain sings for the remembrance, I will stand here and sing with you. I will learn the verses I don't know yet. I will carry your words out and bring the world's words back.*

Vessane's ice-colored eyes did not blink. *And the rest of the year?*

*The rest of the year,* Ash said, *I go home.*

The word landed in the hall like a dropped knife.

*Home,* Vessane repeated, and there was no anger in it, which was somehow worse. There was something rawer than anger. *This is your home. Your people are here. We waited six hundred years in the dark for—*

*For a voice to return willing,* Ash said. *I know. I have read the decree. I have sung the seal open.* Ash stepped forward, off the dais step, down among them, close enough to see the dust still caught in the fine lines around Vessane's eyes. *But listen to me. Listen the way you listened in the galleries, when you argued among yourselves about waking and forgiving. You told me then that the worst crime done to you was not the sealing. It was that no one asked. That they decided for you, and locked the door, and called it peace.*

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

*If you keep me here because I am yours, because I carry your language, because destiny sent me out and so destiny must send me back — then you are doing to me what was done to you. You are deciding for me. You are locking a door and calling it love.*

Ash had never spoken so many words at once in either language. The effort of it left Ash trembling, hands cold, heart hammering against ribs like a bird against glass. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began quietly to weep. The old man who had laughed at the mule song put his hand on the shoulder of the person beside him, steadying himself or them, it was impossible to tell.

Vessane looked at Ash for a long time. Six hundred years looked out of her face, and beneath the six hundred years, something younger stirred — something that remembered being a person before being a symbol.

*Your mother-of-voices,* she said at last. *The bone-carver. She taught you this?*

*No,* Ash said. *She would never teach it. She doesn't believe she has the right to tell anyone what to choose. She only believes you should get to make the choice at all.* Ash swallowed. *She taught me by refusing to do anything else.*

Something moved across Vessane's face that might, in a younger person, have been called a smile. It was tired and crooked and true.

*Then we have been freed twice,* she said. *Once from the seal, and once from ourselves.* She raised her voice, and the old tongue carried it to every corner of the hall without effort, the way it had carried warnings and lullabies through six centuries of stone. *Hear me. The child decides. Not the Spire. Not the covenant. Not the dead. If the Voice Returned chooses the world above, then the world above is part of us now, and we had better learn to be worthy of it.*

She turned back to Ash, and her voice gentled to something only Ash could hear.

*Part of each year,* she said. *And the rest?*

*The rest,* Ash said, *I spend with my mother.*

* * *

Elodie was waiting in the outer court, pretending very hard to be interested in the carved columns.

Ash saw her from the doorway and stopped, just for a moment, to look. She stood with her arms crossed and her weight on one hip, the posture of a woman enduring an ordeal, but her eyes kept moving — up the columns, across the restored friezes, over the sleeping-faces carved into the vaulting — and there was something in her face Ash had learned to read on the road, in fever-nights and ambush-mornings and the long grey drift down the River of Small Mercies. It was the look of a woman seeing a cathedral built out of everything she had spent her life hearing secondhand. Wonder, wearing suspicion as a coat.

Renn stood beside her, leaning on a column with the studied laziness of a man who had nearly died in these galleries and would rather chew glass than admit the place impressed him. He was thinner than he had been. The wounds taken holding the span at Greyford Bridge, and later in the dark below, had taken their toll and were paying it back slowly. But he was upright, and he was here, and when he caught Ash looking he winked with the shameless confidence of a man who had talked his way out of a Warden prison using nothing but the truth and a broken nose.

Ash crossed the court at a run.

Elodie caught Ash the way she did everything — awkwardly, bracing too late, with a startled sound that was half protest and entirely surrender — and then her arms came around properly and her chin settled on top of Ash's head and she held on the way she had held on during the fever, when she thought Ash couldn't hear her praying to gods she didn't believe in.

*Well,* she said, her voice suspiciously rough. *Did they give you a tower?*

*A tower room,* Ash said against her coat. *With a window facing the sunrise. And a title older than any kingdom.*

*Renn,* Elodie said over Ash's head, *they gave the child a title older than any kingdom.*

*I heard. Ungrateful little wretch. In my day we'd have killed for a window facing anywhere.*

Ash pulled back far enough to look up at both of them. *I told them no.*

Elodie went very still. It was the stillness she got when a carving whispered something she wasn't ready to hear. *No?*

*I told them I'd come back every year. For the remembrance. For the singing. I'll learn the verses. I'll be the Speaker-ambassador, if that's what they need — Vessane says the Crown wants a face for the covenant, and apparently my face will do.* Ash took a breath. *But I told them home isn't a place you're assigned. It's a place you choose. And I choose—*

The sentence snagged. All the eloquence of two languages, and the sentence snagged, because some things were too large for the mouth and had to come out through the hands instead — and Ash's hands had always known what to do. Ash took Elodie's right hand, the one with the carver's calluses and the burn scar from the night the workshop burned, and turned it palm-up, and laid Ash's own hand in it.

*—this,* Ash said. *I choose this. Every year. As much as you'll have me.*

Elodie Voss, who had lied beautifully to a Warden-Sergeant, who had bargained with a bone-reader for a murdered son's last words, who had defied the Crown and the Spire and six hundred years of buried history without her voice shaking once — Elodie Voss looked down at the small hand in hers and cried.

Not prettily. Not discreetly. She cried the way she carved: completely, with her whole self, indifferent to who was watching. Renn turned away and became intensely interested in a column, which fooled no one, because his shoulders were shaking too.

*You absolute menace,* Elodie managed, after a while, wiping her face with her sleeve like a farmer's wife and not a woman who had shaken an empire. *I never wanted children. Do you know that? I had a whole life planned. Quiet. Orderly. Bones in, coin out, nobody's fate in my hands.*

*And now?*

*Now,* she said, gripping Ash's hand hard enough to hurt, which was how she said everything that mattered, *now I've got a daughter who negotiates with ghosts and a smuggler who cries at columns, and I wouldn't trade either of you for the Silent Spire entire.*

*Careful,* Renn said, recovering, strolling over with his grin reassembled. *Flattery. Next you'll be asking me to stay the winter.*

*The winter,* Elodie said, and Ash watched her weigh it, watch her decide not to be afraid of how easily the decision came. *The winter and the spring planting. Someone has to teach you to carve, since you insist on having opinions about my work.*

*I have excellent opinions about your work.*

*You have one opinion. It's that the noses are too big.*

*They are too big.*

Ash stood between them in the morning light of the outer court, in the shadow of the Spire that had been silent for six hundred years and was silent no longer, and felt the two halves of the life settle together inside — the tower and the workshop, the old tongue and the mule song, the dead who consulted and the living who argued about nose size. Destiny had sent Ash out of the dark to decide. Destiny, it turned out, had imagined a smaller answer.

* * *

They left the Spire three days later, at dawn, on foot, because Renn refused on principle to ride anything that hadn't earned a name.

Behind them the mountain hummed — gently now, the way it hummed for the remembrance, a sound like a mother checking on a sleeping house. Ahead the road ran down through Thornfell, through valleys where Elodie's standing stones were already rising one by one, carved with gallery names, so the dead could be heard in daylight. Word traveled ahead of them faster than they walked: villages preparing, taverns learning old-tongue choruses badly, Wardens on the roads wearing new orders and old doubts.

Halfway down the first ridge, Elodie stopped at a flat grey boulder at the trailside, shrugged off her pack, and took out her knives.

*What are you doing?* Renn asked.

*Working,* she said, and set her chisel against the stone.

Ash crouched beside her and watched the first letters take shape under her hands — and recognized them, because Ash had sung them into the seal itself, because they were the opening verse, the willingness-word, the word that meant *choose*. Elodie was carving it where every traveler on the road would pass. Not a monument. An invitation.

The chips flew bright in the morning sun, and somewhere below, faint on the wind, a village bell began to ring — and Ash smiled, because it was going to be a long walk home, and every mile of it belonged to them.
