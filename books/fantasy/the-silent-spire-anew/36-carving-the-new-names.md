## 36. Carving the New Names

The stone remembered being a mountain.

That was the first thing Elodie had learned in thirty years of carving — that every block of granite or limestone or river-smoothed slate carried the memory of its own making, of pressure and fire and the long patient centuries of becoming. She had learned it as a child, learning her trade from a mother who thought bone was the only honest medium, who had taught her to listen with her fingertips and never let her face show what she heard.

Her mother had been wrong about many things. Bone was not the only honest medium. But she had been right about listening, and Elodie listened now as she had never listened before, kneeling in the cold morning grass beside the first standing stone on the road out of Thornfell, her chisel moving in slow, deliberate strokes while the stone sang under her hands.

Not whispered. Not haunted. *Sang.*

It was a difference she was still learning to trust.

For most of her life, the dead had come to her uninvited. They had seeped into her carvings like damp into plaster, filling whatever hollow she made for them with their unfinished business — the merchant's last accounting, the drowned boy's terror of water, the old woman's finger-bone that carried nothing but a name repeated over and over until Elodie wanted to scream. She had built a life around containing them: careful neutrality, careful isolation, careful silence. A workshop at the edge of the valley where no one visited and no one had to see what the work cost her. She had told herself it was competence. She had known, in the hours when the carvings would not stop murmuring, that it was only a locked door with something scratching patiently on the other side.

Now the door stood open, and what came through was not a flood but a conversation.

*Deeper,* the stone said, in the way stones say things — not words exactly, more like the memory of weight, of sediment settling, of roots finding their way down through cracks. *The letters want depth. Shallow carving is a shout; deep carving lasts.*

Elodie adjusted her grip and drove the chisel deeper. Chips of pale stone scattered across the wet grass. The morning was cold enough to make her breath visible, and her knuckles ached in the way they always did now, the ache of a body asked to do more than it had ever done, but she did not stop, because behind her, waiting in the cart, were forty-seven more stones, and beyond those, roads running in every direction across Thornfell, and along every one of those roads, names.

So many names.

She had copied them by lantern-light through the whole of the winter, working from rubbings Ash had taken in the Weeping Galleries before the flooding passages were sealed for the sleepers' safety — thousands upon thousands of names carved into cavern walls by hands that had known they were going to die in the dark and had refused, with the last stubborn strength of a people being buried alive, to be forgotten. Some were simple. Some were elaborate, wrapped in genealogies and titles from kingdoms that no longer existed. Some were just marks, the signatures of children.

Ash had wept over the children's marks. Elodie had held the small shaking body and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the dead in the walls were not already saying, and they said it better: *We were here. We were here. Remember that we were here.*

Well. They were being remembered now, in daylight, in stone that travelers could touch.

She set down the chisel and flexed her fingers, studying the work. The opening verse of the willingness-word rose down the face of the stone in letters half a hand deep, the old-tongue characters flowing into one another the way Ash sang them, each syllable leaning into the next. Below it, beginning where the verse ended, the names started — the first hundred, chosen by lot among the gallery dead themselves, because there were too many for one stone and the dead had insisted on fairness even in this, arguing about it for three days in voices only she could hear until Elodie had finally thrown up her hands and told them to draw straws like living people.

They had found this hilarious. The dead of the Spire, six hundred years entombed, had apparently retained a very specific sense of humor.

*Good,* the stone rumbled beneath her knees, satisfied. *Now I will hold them. Now I will say them to everyone who passes. That is good work.*

"Thank you," Elodie said aloud, because you did, now. You thanked the stones and the bones and the rivers, and they thanked you back, and somewhere in the exchange — she could not have said exactly when it had happened — the loneliness that had defined her whole life had quietly died and been buried without ceremony.

Footsteps crunched through the frost behind her. Renn, carrying two cups of tea with the easy swagger of a man who had nearly died four times in the last year and decided swagger was cheaper than fear. He handed her a cup and lowered himself onto the cart's bench with a grunt, surveying the finished stone the way he surveyed everything: as if appraising it for smuggling potential.

"That's the third," he said. "Road to Greyford, road to the pass, road to the valley." He sipped his own tea. "Thirty miles apart. Any traveler walks past one within a day."

"Forty-eight to go." She blew steam off her cup. "The villages south of the marshes are fighting over placement. Both want theirs at the crossroads. The dead have opinions about which crossroads."

"The dead have opinions about everything. You've mentioned."

"They're *right* about everything, that's the problem." She leaned against the cart wheel, shoulder to shoulder with him, and watched the sun climb over the ridge where — one year ago almost to the day — the three of them had stood and watched the Spire ring for the first time in six centuries. "Yesterday one of the gallery dead spent four hours explaining why the stone near the ferry crossing should face downstream rather than up. Four hours. Something about how the river used to carry their songs before the sealing, and they wanted the words to travel with the current again."

Renn was quiet for a moment. "Did you move it?"

"I moved it." She smiled despite herself. "And the ferryman says the crossing has been calm as glass ever since. Fishermen are calling it blessed. I haven't the heart to tell them it's just an old woman who missed hearing herself sung."

This was the shape of her days now, and she still sometimes woke at night expecting the old weight — the scratching at the locked door, the whispers leaking from finished carvings, the endless unchosen intimacy with grief — and found instead this: consultations. Negotiations. The dead of Thornfell, freed from six centuries of enforced silence, treating her less like a haunted woman than like a magistrate. They brought disputes. They brought requests. One of the older ghosts of the Galleries, a stonemason named Veth who had helped carve the original seal and had spent six hundred years ashamed of it, had appointed himself her supervisor and critiqued every letter she cut with the fussy precision of a master reviewing an apprentice.

*Your serifs are lazy,* Veth had informed her that very morning. *In my day a name was carved so true you could read it by candlelight from across a room.*

"In your day you helped wall people into a mountain," Elodie had replied, chipping away.

*I know,* Veth had said, and gone quiet, and she had felt the old ghost's shame move through the stone like cold water. *I know what I did. That is why the letters must be perfect. Let me help you with the next one. My hands remember the craft, even if they cannot hold the chisel.*

And so she worked with a ghost's hands guiding hers, and the letters came out truer than anything she had cut alone in thirty years, and if that was haunting, then she had been haunted all wrong her whole life.

She drained her tea and stood. "Next stone. The pass road — the one where the mountain hums."

Renn winced theatrically. "The angry one?"

"He's not angry anymore. He's *dramatic.*"

*The pass road.* The high stone above the tree line, where wind came funneling down from the heights and the rock itself still remembered the old language, older than any kingdom, older than the Crown, older than the war that had ended with living bodies sealed in a tower. When she pressed her palms against it, she did not hear one voice but a chorus — the mountain's own long memory braided together with the gallery dead whose songs had once traveled these passes, before anyone thought to silence them.

She chose her spot in the lee of the wind and began to rough out the border, and the mountain hummed approval through her boot-soles, and for a long while there was nothing in the world but the ring of steel on stone and the cold bright air and the sense — new, still astonishing, likely always to be astonishing — of being *consulted*. Of being part of a conversation instead of its unwilling confessional.

Halfway through the morning, a traveler stopped on the road below. An old man with a peddler's pack, gray-bearded, breathing hard from the climb. He stood looking up at the stone for a long time, reading the letters he could not possibly read, and then he took off his hat.

"My grandmother used to sing something," he called up, hesitant. "Before she died. Nobody knew the words. We thought she'd made them up." His voice cracked. "Is that — is that what this is?"

Elodie climbed down to the road. "Sing it for me," she said.

He sang, badly, tunelessly, six notes of a melody worn smooth by generations of mouths that hadn't understood what they were carrying. Ash, appearing silently from behind the cart where Ash had been mending a strap, went absolutely still.

"That's the fourth verse," Ash said softly, in the common tongue, and then answered the old man in the old language, completing the melody, and the mountain itself seemed to lean closer, and the old man wept openly on the frozen road with his hat crushed in his fists, saying over and over that he'd thought his grandmother was mad, that the family had laughed at her, that he owed her an apology he could no longer give.

"You can give it here," Elodie told him, and guided his weathered hand to the fresh-carved stone. "She'll hear it. They all will."

He stayed an hour. He left lighter than he'd come. And when his footsteps faded down the pass, Elodie stood with her palm flat against the cold granite and felt the gallery dead murmuring among themselves — pleased, moved, adding the old man's grandmother to their rolls, a voice recovered from exile, returned to the choir.

*This,* Veth said, wonder plain even in a ghost. *This is what I thought I was doing, when I helped build the seal. I thought I was making peace. This is what peace actually looks like.* A pause. *I am sorry it took me six hundred years to learn the difference.*

"We're all late learners," Elodie said. "Some of us took thirty years and a burned workshop."

*You burned your workshop?*

"For a child I'd known three days." She picked up her chisel again. "Best trade I ever made."

The light shifted toward afternoon. Ash sat nearby sketching the stone's placement in a journal — maps, now, and records, and the beginnings of what the scholars in the capital were already calling the Anew Archive, though Ash refused to call it anything grander than *the list of names and where they live now.* Renn dozed on the cart bench with his hat over his eyes, one hand resting near the knife he still slept with, some habits outliving their reasons.

Elodie carved.

Letter by letter, name by name, she cut the dead into daylight. Her hands ached. The wind bit. Somewhere below, a village bell rang the hour, and the sound traveled up the pass and met the mountain's low hum and harmonized with it, briefly, perfectly, as if the whole of Thornfell were one instrument slowly remembering how to play.

And then, as the shadows lengthened, she felt it — a new voice at the edge of her awareness, faint, coming not from the stone or the mountain or the passing wind, but from far away, from the direction of the valley, from the direction of home.

A voice she knew. A voice that had no business reaching her across fifty miles of mountain.

Small. Young. Frightened.

Knocking, in the only way a voice can knock.

* * *

She dropped the chisel.

It clattered against the stone and rolled, and Veth exclaimed something sharp in a dialect dead six centuries, and Ash looked up from the journal with the sudden total alertness of a child who had learned, young, to read adults' fear.

"Aunt Elodie?" — the title had stuck since midwinter, to her secret undoing. "What is it? What did you hear?"

She stood there in the fading light with her heart hammering and her scarred fingers still curled around the shape of a voice, and she thought: *fifty miles, impossible, exhaustion, imagination* — and then the voice knocked again, clearer now, unmistakable, and it was not imagination, and it was not exhaustion, and it was not any dead she had ever carried.

It was a living child, far away in her valley, speaking in a tongue that made the mountain itself go quiet to listen.

Renn took his hat off his eyes and sat up, reading her face the way he read everything — instantly, accurately, and with visible reluctance. "Trouble?"

"No," Elodie said slowly. And then, because the gift had changed and she had changed and lying to herself was the one habit she could no longer afford: "Yes. Someone's knocking at a door fifty miles away." She looked at Ash, and saw her own expression from a year ago reflected back at her — the blizzard, the half-frozen child, the closed door she had opened anyway.

"It seems," she said, shouldering her pack, "we've walked ourselves into the middle of our own story. Pack the tools. We're going home early."
