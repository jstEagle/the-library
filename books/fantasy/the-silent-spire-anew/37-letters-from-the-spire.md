## 37. Letters from the Spire

Renn Carrow had never been a man for letters.

Words on paper were traps — confessions waiting for a magistrate's eye, debts written down so they could be collected, promises that outlived the mood in which they'd been made. In thirty years of smuggling he'd signed his name perhaps a dozen times, and two of those signatures had cost him blood. So it was a strange thing, that first spring after the Spire, to find himself seated at a tavern table in Greyford with a quill in his fist and a blank page before him, staring at it the way he'd once stared at a locked strongbox.

The tavern keeper's daughter had sold him the paper. Three sheets, a stick of sealing wax, and a look of pity he pretended not to notice.

*Dear Elodie,* he wrote, and then sat for a long while, because everything after that felt either too much or too little.

In the end he settled for the truth, or the shape of it.

*Dear Elodie,

The river road is open again. First caravan came through Greyford three days past — silk from the coast, iron from the foothills, and not a single Warden checkpoint between here and Thornfell's edge. Corvane kept his word, which I confess I'd have bet against. He's posted his men at the crossings now instead of the secrets. They check cargo like honest soldiers and wave folk through like neighbors. It's unsettling. I keep waiting for the trick.

Business is good. Too good. Turns out a man who helped open the Spire can charge double for safe passage and half the valley calls it fair. I've taken on two drivers and a boy who cooks worse than you do, which I didn't think possible, so take that as the compliment it isn't.

Ash will tell you about the song. I'll only say this: when the ferryman started humming it at the toll bridge, I nearly put us in the water. Thought for one black moment the old curse had come back. But it was just a tune. Just a tune, drifting up the river like it belonged there.*

He read it back twice. It was a coward's letter — all freight rates and jokes, nothing underneath. But he sealed it anyway, because underneath was exactly what he couldn't put down yet: *I think about that night in the galleries more than is healthy, and I think about who stayed beside my cot through the fever even plainer than that.*

Some things a man said face to face or not at all. And some things, maybe, a man earned the right to say by surviving long enough.

* * *

Her reply came with the summer rains, carried by a carter who owed Renn a favor and delivered it with the smugness of a man holding something valuable.

Elodie's hand was small and precise, each letter carved as clean as her bone-work, and reading it was like hearing her voice across four hundred miles of wet road.

*Renn —

The workshop smells of cedar shavings and rain, which means the neighbors have been at my woodpile again without asking. I have decided to be grateful rather than furious, which is a new discipline for me and harder than carving. Marta Hale leaves bread on the step most mornings. She has never once knocked. I believe we are friends now, though neither of us would survive saying it aloud.

The standing stones are going well. Three placed since you left — the miller's field, the high pasture, and one at Greyford Bridge, which caused some argument until the bridge-warden's widow spoke for it. People come to them now. Not crowds. One or two at a time, mostly at dusk. They stand there listening to stones, and the stones listen back, and then they go home quieter than they came. I did not know a valley could learn gentleness at its age.

Tell your cook-boy that if he ruins another stew, Ash and I will come south and take over his kitchen ourselves. This is not an idle threat. Ash has opinions about salt that would frighten you.

The dead say hello. That sounds mad in ink, but you know what I mean. The gallery names I carry in my memory — they consult me still, mostly small things. A woman wants to know if her descendants kept the orchard. A soldier asks after a dog. I answer what I can. It is strange work, being a post office for the departed, but it is better than being their prison, which is what I was before.

Come north when the roads dry. Or don't, and I'll assume you've finally been hanged.

— E.*

P.S. *Ash says: "Tell him the song is called 'The Water Remembers' and he sang it wrong at the bridge, the ferryman was laughing AT him."*

Renn laughed alone in the taproom until the serving girl asked if he'd been drinking. He showed her the postscript. She didn't understand it. He hadn't expected her to. There was a whole language now — three years deep, built on burned workshops and flooded galleries and a child who had carried an empire's sin and set it down — and it belonged to exactly three people, scattered across two hundred leagues of mountain and river, held together by ink and memory and the stubborn animal fact of having survived the same nights.

* * *

Autumn brought a letter that was not from Elodie.

It arrived by Warden courier, which was alarming enough. Renn turned it over twice before opening it, half expecting a warrant for some old crime resurrected out of spite. Instead:

*Carrow.

This is not a social correspondence. Do not treat it as one.

You are listed in the Crown's new registry as a licensed carrier on the Thornfell route. I opposed your inclusion. My objection was overruled on the grounds that you know the passes better than any three of my officers and have, per the record, no outstanding warrants. Per the record. We both know what that phrase is worth.

I am writing to inform you that Warden patrols will conduct random inspections on the high road through winter. If your drivers cooperate, there will be no delays. If they perform theatrics, there will be fines. Inform them accordingly.

This concludes the official portion of this letter.

Unofficially: the registry clerk tells me applications for the Speaker-ambassador's escort detail outnumber positions forty to one. Your name was submitted without my knowledge or consent. I have not struck it from the list. Draw from that whatever you will. I draw from it nothing.

Corvane*

Renn read it three times, grinning wider with each pass, and read it aloud to his drivers that evening purely to watch their faces. Old Corvane, writing unofficial portions. Old stone-face Corvane, leaving a man on a list he claimed to oppose. Somewhere in the last year, the sergeant's armor had grown hinges, and whether he knew it or not, he'd begun letting people see through the gaps.

Renn wrote back within the hour.

*Sergeant —

Received your letter. Understood every word of it, including the parts you didn't write, which by my count were most of them.

My drivers will cooperate fully. I'll tell them the same thing you told your superiors when you arrested one: cooperation looks better on a man than obedience, and lasts longer too.

Regarding the escort list: keep my name on it or strike it, whichever costs you more sleep. I'll be on that road either way. Some of us don't need a uniform to stand guard.

Give my regards to the men who turned at the door. Buy them a drink if you can manage it without breaking your oath of grimness.

— Carrow

P.S. There is no unofficial portion to this letter either. None whatsoever.*

* * *

Winter closed the passes, and the letters slowed, and Renn learned what the others had always known and he had somehow missed: that distance is not measured in miles but in the space between one voice and the next.

He wrote to Ash directly for the first time in December. It felt presumptuous — the child was an ambassador now, with scribes and schedules and a seat in the Spire hall that had been empty six hundred years — but the reply came back in Ash's own careful script, learned that year from Elodie's lessons, each word chosen like a stone set in a wall.

*Renn,

Elodie is teaching me to write and she says I should practice on someone patient, which means you, because she is not patient, she is precise, and those are different.

Things here are strange and good. The Galleries are open now above the floodline. Pilgrims come and read the names. Some find their own great-grandparents' names and cry. I used to think crying was weakness but Elodie says it is just the body telling the truth, and the Spire-folk agree, and they have been right about almost everything else so I am trying it.

The elders asked me to ask you something. They remember a smuggler line that ran salt through Thornfell during the famine years, before the truce went hard. They want to know if your family kept records. They say debts of kindness are the only kind that never rot, and they intend to repay theirs. I told them you probably never wrote anything down. I told them you were probably proud of that.

Was I right?

— Ash*

He sat with that one a long time by the winter fire, his driver's boy snoring in the loft above. His grandfather had run salt through Thornfell in the famine years — grain too, when the Crown's granaries stayed shut — and had never spoken of it except once, drunk, saying that the folk under the hill paid their tolls in songs that could mend a broken axle or a broken heart, and that a Carrow took payment honestly or not at all.

No records. Never any records. But the old man had hummed, sometimes, tunes in no tongue anyone knew, and said they were *owed*, and Renn had thought it madness.

Now the eldest sleeper wanted to settle accounts with a dead smuggler's line, and the only ledger was melody.

*Ash —

Tell the elders yes. No records, but I know three of the old route-tunes myself, taught to me by a man who said they were payment. If your people can hear their own coin in my bad whistling, then the debt runs both directions and we can call it square, or better, call it open. Debts between families shouldn't close. They should just change hands.

Also: practice your letters on whoever you like, but save your arguments for Elodie, because watching you two spar is the finest entertainment in two kingdoms and I get the reports secondhand.

Keep the fire fed. Keep the elders honest. They're politicians now, poor devils, and politicians forget what they were before.

— Renn*

* * *

Spring came up the river like a rumor, and with it a letter that Renn read standing in the yard with mud to his ankles, and read again sitting down, and read a third time slowly enough to feel every word land.

*Renn,

Marta Hale died on the ninth of the month. Quietly, in her sleep, with bread dough rising on her table. I heard her before anyone told me — her voice came up through the floorboards of the workshop at dawn, not frightened, just surprised, like someone stepping through a door they'd only ever looked at.

I sat with her family through the wake. They asked — her daughter asked, halting, ashamed to ask — if I would carve something for her. A keepsake. You understand what that means. What it has always meant.

I said yes.

Here is what I need you to know, and why I am telling you and not the wind: three years ago I would have said no. Three years ago every carving was a transaction and every voice was weight and I built my whole life into a shape that nothing could enter. Then a child knocked on my door in a blizzard and I opened it against every instinct, and everything since — the road, the Galleries, the covenant, you — everything grew out of that one stupid, soft, unguarded moment.

I am not writing this to be sentimental. I am writing it because you are the other person alive who knows what it cost to become unguarded, and I suspect you are fighting the same war I am, in the same slow way, and I want you to win it.

The Spire rings on midsummer's day. The remembrance. Ash will lead the singing; the elders have promised the mountain will answer gently this time, and I confess I want to hear it. I want to stand on that ridge again with the two of you and hear six hundred years of silence finish ending.

Come north, Renn. Not for the ceremony. For the ordinary hours after it — the bad stew, the long walk down, the talking that doesn't stop until the lamps burn low. I miss it. I miss you both. There. Written down. Sealed in wax. Let history record that the bone-carver said a plain thing plainly.

— E.*

Renn read it four times. On the fourth pass, his driver's boy found him still standing in the muddy yard, letter in hand, with an expression the boy later described to the ferryman as *"like a man who'd been robbed and couldn't stop smiling about it."*

He wrote back one line only:

*Midsummer. I'll bring the salt. Somebody owes somebody a debt, and I aim to watch it change hands.*

Then he took out a second sheet, dipped the quill, and addressed it north to the Spire itself, to the child who had knocked on a door in a blizzard and changed the shape of the world.

*Ash —

Your ambassadorship comes with duties, I'm told. Here is one I'm assigning you personally, ambassador's privilege: on midsummer's day, when the Spire rings, sing the verse the ferryman got wrong. Sing it the way you sang it in the pass when the mountain was angry and you made it gentle. Don't sing it for the crowd. Sing it for the three of us who know what it cost, and let everyone else eavesdrop.

That's how the best songs have always worked. Ask your elders.

See you on the ridge. Both of you. I'm counting the days, which is a thing I have never done before and refuse to apologize for.

— Renn*

* * *

He sealed them both, set them in the courier's satchel, and stood watching the rider until horse and road and letter vanished into the green.

Then Renn Carrow went inside and began, for the first time in his life, to count.
