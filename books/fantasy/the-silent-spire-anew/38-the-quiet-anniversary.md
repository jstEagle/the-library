## 38. The Quiet Anniversary

The road to the Spire was kinder now, and that alone would have been enough to make Elodie suspicious in another life.

A year ago it had been smugglers' track and Warden patrol, mud that swallowed cart-wheels whole and passes where the wind sang in a language that made grown men walk faster without knowing why. Now there were waystones — standing stones she had carved herself, or supervised the carving of, forty-one of them scattered across Thornfell like teeth set back into a broken jaw — and travelers paused at each one to read the names. Some read them aloud. Some only touched them, the way you touch a doorframe in passing, an old habit of courtesy. The names did not whisper at them anymore. They simply were, patient as geology.

Elodie rode behind Renn on a borrowed mule and watched Ash ride ahead on nothing but enthusiasm, perched sideways on the packhorse's rump because sitting properly was, in Ash's opinion, a form of surrender.

*You're going to fall off,* she signed, when Ash twisted around to grin at her.

*I'm going to fly off,* Ash signed back, which was not reassuring, since Ash's sign for *fly* was also the sign for *fall*, differentiated only by expression, and Ash's expression never changed between the two.

Renn glanced over his shoulder. "If the child dies, I'm telling the Wardens it was your idea."

"The Wardens don't take complaints from smugglers."

"Ex-smuggler." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but the word still sat strangely on him, a coat cut for someone else. A year of running legitimate freight — or as legitimate as Renn Carrow got, which meant he paid his tolls and only cheated the people who deserved it — had not yet worn smooth the edges of him. He kept checking horizons out of habit. He still slept with his boots by the door instead of beside the bed, ready for a departure he no longer needed to make.

She understood that. Her own hands still woke her some nights, curled around a chisel that wasn't there, aching with the memory of bone.

The valley opened ahead of them in the late afternoon light, and there it was: the Silent Spire, which was silent no longer.

It rose from the shoulder of the mountain like a needle drawn up through green cloth, black stone shot through with veins of pale quartz that caught the sun and held it. Six hundred years it had stood sealed, its galleries flooded, its heart locked behind a door carved from a single vertebra of the living rock. Now the great doors at its base stood open — had stood open for a year — and in the hour before dusk, if the wind came down the pass just right, the Spire *sang*. Not loudly. Not the war-hum that had once shaken scree from the cliffs and turned the dead of the Weeping Galleries into a chorus of pleading voices. Just a low, sustained tone, like a bell heard through water, like a throat clearing itself after six centuries of enforced silence.

The first time Elodie had heard it, a year ago this very week, she had wept into Renn's shoulder and pretended afterward that she hadn't.

She didn't pretend anymore. That was what the year had done to her, more than anything else. It had taken a woman who hid every feeling behind competence and carved her open, gently, the way she herself carved bone — patiently, along the grain, until what was inside could finally be seen.

*There it is,* Ash signed, both hands wide, the sign for *home-that-isn't-home* — a compound Ash had invented in the first month after the covenant, because no single language they knew contained the idea.

"It," Renn said. "Not it. *Her.* You told me yourself the Spire-folk call towers by women's names."

Ash rolled their eyes with the magnificent weariness only a child can produce. *You always say that.*

"Because it's always true."

They came down into the valley as the light went amber, past the first of the guest-lodges the Crown had funded and the Spire-folk had redesigned — because the Crown's architects had built them square and grim, and the sleepers' descendants-by-covenant had curved every wall within a season, muttering, in the old tongue, things about straight lines that Elodie was fairly sure translated to insults. Lanterns were already lit along the processional road. People were walking toward the Spire from every direction: villagers from Thornfell in their good coats, river-folk who'd come up the new trade road with bundles of reed-woven gifts, even a knot of Wardens in undress uniform, gray cloaks over gray wool, walking unarmed and slightly awkward among civilians, the way large dogs look awkward in parlors. Corvane would be there somewhere. He came every year. He stood at the back and he did not sing and he did not leave until the last verse was finished, and Elodie had stopped expecting him to be anything other than exactly that.

At the lodge where guests were housed, a steward met them — one of the awakened, an elderly man named Vessil whose face still bore the faint waxy smoothness of long sleep, whose voice was rusted at the edges but who had insisted, from the first week, on doing something useful rather than being grieved over. He bowed to all three of them, deepest to Ash, and said in his cracked voice:

"Tomorrow at dusk. As before." His eyes crinkled. "The eldest asks whether the mother-of-voices will speak this year."

Elodie's chest did the thing it always did at that title — a tightening that was half grief, half something warmer that she still hadn't found a better word for than *belonging*.

"Tell her I'll listen," Elodie said. "That's mostly what I do."

Vessil laughed, dry as autumn leaves. "Yes. And the listening is the speaking. She knows that better than anyone." He bowed again and shuffled off toward the lantern-light, humming — actually humming, an old-tongue melody, tuneless and content.

Ash watched him go with that expression Ash had, the one that was ancient and nine years old at once.

*He used to be afraid of doors,* Ash signed. *Now he opens all of them.*

"Yes," Elodie said. "People do that. Sometimes."

* *

That night, in the guest room with its stubbornly curved walls, Elodie took out her kit.

She hadn't planned to work. But the anniversary always did this to her — pulled the year backward through her like thread through an eye of a needle, and her hands needed occupation or they would start remembering things she'd rather they hold loosely. So she sat by the window with a piece of river-stone she'd picked up at Greyford Bridge on the way north — deliberately, because she collected her materials from places now, not from bodies — and began to shape it while the moon came up over the Spire.

Greyford Bridge. She let herself remember it fully, for once, instead of in the flinching fragments she usually permitted. The ambush. Renn holding the span alone, his blade catching lantern-light, shouting at her to run. The young Warden — barely older than a boy — who had raised his crossbow at Ash and then seen Ash's face, really seen it, a child's face under the grime and terror, and lowered the weapon with a sound like something breaking in him. He'd died anyway, later, in the melee; she'd learned his name months afterward, Tam Wilder, nineteen years old, from a farming family two valleys over. She had carved his name into a waystone herself. She had stood in the rain while his mother touched the letters and asked, "Did he suffer?" and Elodie had told the truth, because her gift did not permit anything else: "No. He hesitated, and the hesitation was kind, and then it was quick."

The mother had gripped her hand so hard the bones ached, and said thank you, and Elodie had walked away and been sick behind a hedgerow, because kindness given honestly sometimes costs more than lies.

The stone took shape under her hands. A bridge, eventually. Small enough to sit in a palm. She worked until the candle guttered and then sat a while longer, listening.

Listening was different now. Before, the dead had come to her uninvited, leaking through whatever she carved, a hauntings-as-trade arrangement she'd never chosen and never quite refused. Now — after the galleries, after the covenant, after the thousand small negotiations of a year spent as the bridge between two peoples — they *asked*. She felt them the way you feel weather coming. Most nights the answer was no, nothing needed saying, rest well. Some nights a name wanted carrying to a living relative, a debt wanted acknowledging, a grief wanted witnessing. Tonight, high above her in the Spire, six hundred sleepers' voices and the ten thousand dead of the Weeping Galleries were settling into themselves like birds at roost, murmuring, waiting for tomorrow.

And underneath it all, one voice, familiar as her own pulse, drifting up through the floorboards because it had no sense of appropriate hours:

*Elodie.* Renn's voice, though Renn was three rooms down and very much alive, projected through the stone the way he'd learned to do during his convalescence — because the stone remembered everything now, and the stone liked him, and the stone was a terrible gossip. *Are you awake?*

"I'm working."

*A likely story. Are you all right?*

"Go to sleep, Renn."

*A year ago I nearly died and you held my hand for eleven days and neither of us has said a single sensible word about it since—*

"Goodnight, Renn."

A pause. Then, softer, through the stone, almost shy — a word Renn Carrow had probably never been shy about anything in his life: *I'm glad you're here.*

She looked at the little stone bridge in her hands. "So am I," she said aloud, to the room, knowing the stone would carry it, knowing he'd hear it, knowing she'd deny in the morning that she'd said it at all.

From three rooms away, muffled and delighted, came the sound of Renn Carrow laughing.

* *

The remembrance began at dusk, as it had begun a year ago, on the broad terrace before the Spire's doors.

But oh, how it had changed.

A year ago there had been perhaps two hundred people: the awakened sleepers, gaunt and blinking in their first daylight in six centuries; a handful of Wardens under Corvane's command, weapons surrendered but hands nowhere near relaxed; Elodie, Renn, and Ash, exhausted and half-healed, standing together at the front because no one knew where else to put them. The eldest sleeper — Sereth, her voice like wind through a canyon, her patience like bedrock — had asked her impossible question then, the question that had turned vengeance aside: *What do you want now?*

Tonight there were thousands. Terraces that had stood empty for centuries were crowded with blankets and lanterns and children too young to know they should be solemn. The old-tongue songs that had once been contraband — songs that tavern-keepers had been jailed for permitting, songs mothers had taught in whispers — rang openly across the valley. Elodie recognized fragments of them everywhere, worn smooth by a year of living mouths, verses bent slightly out of true the way songs always bend, and she found she didn't mind. The dead didn't mind either. She could feel them listening from the mountain's bones, amused, proprietary, *delighted*.

Sereth stood at the terrace's edge, wrapped in gray-white robes, her waxy sleep-smoothness faded now to merely ageless. Beside her stood the Speaker — Ash.

They had argued about the title. Of course they had. The covenant named Ash the first Speaker-ambassador, the living voice of the returned, and Ash had hated the weight of it for approximately four days before discovering that ambassadors got to miss lessons and eat first at banquets, at which point the objections became intermittent. Tonight Ash wore the formal robes — deep blue, silver-threaded, sized for a child and therefore constantly threatening to trip their wearer — and stood straight-backed before the crowd with a composure that Elodie recognized, painfully, as borrowed. From Sereth. From her. From everyone who had ever told Ash, *you can carry this.*

Ash raised both hands, and the terrace went quiet the way snowfall is quiet — total, soft, attentive.

Then Ash began to sing.

No instrument accompanied. None was needed. The song was the opening verse of the waking-song, the same verse Ash had sung before the vertebra-door a year ago, when willingness had proven mightier than bloodline — but sung differently now. Slower. With the grief left in it. Ash's clear voice carried the melody out over the terraces, and the crowd took it up in pieces, the awakened first, then the villagers who'd learned it, then everyone, a tide of human voices rising toward the tower.

And the Spire answered.

That was the part that undid Elodie every time. A year ago the mountain had answered Ash's song with a groan, a reluctant grinding of seals, the sound of a wound being reopened. Now it answered the way a grandmother answers a grandchild's knock: immediately, warmly, with a tone that rose through the black stone and out through the quartz veins until the whole tower shimmered with it, harmonics stacking on harmonics, the mountain singing back the verse in the deep register only it possessed.

The dead of the Galleries joined in. Elodie felt them rise through her feet, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow the gift lived in — ten thousand silenced voices adding their note, not pleading anymore, not arguing, just *present*, counted, witnessed. Grief moved through the crowd like weather. She saw Vessil weeping without shame. She saw Warden-Sergeant Corvane at the back, arms folded, jaw tight, eyes wet, refusing absolutely to acknowledge either fact. She saw Renn beside her, hat off — Renn Carrow, hatless by choice, in public — singing the words badly in the wrong key with total commitment.

She sang too. Her voice was ordinary, untrained, a carver's voice, a listener's voice. It didn't matter. The song was not a performance. It was a ledger being balanced, a name being read aloud, a debt acknowledged across six hundred years, and every voice in it weighed the same.

When the last verse ended, the Spire held its tone a long moment — a breath held between worlds — and then released it, gently, the way you lay down something heavy that you've decided to stop carrying.

Silence followed. But it was a new silence, a chosen one, and everyone on the terrace understood the difference now. Silence could be a tomb or it could be a held breath. This was the second kind.

Sereth turned to the crowd. Her gaze crossed Elodie like a hand laid on a shoulder.

"A year ago," she said, and her canyon-voice reached every ear without effort, "we were asked what we wanted. It was the first time in six centuries anyone thought to ask. Tonight, I ask again — of all of you, living and sleeping and singing in the stone. What do you want *now*? Because wanting does not end. Peace is not a door that locks. It is a fire that must be fed."

And the crowd answered — not with speeches but with the small, various, human truth of themselves. A river-woman called out for open markets. A Warden called for roads that belonged to no army. An awakened sleeper with a child on her hip called for schools that taught both tongues. Ash, beaming, called for fewer lessons entirely and was overruled by universal acclamation.

Elodie, when Sereth's eyes found hers, said only: "Keep asking. Every year. That's all I want. Don't ever stop asking."

Sereth smiled, slow as sunrise. "Agreed, mother-of-voices. For as long as any of us endure."

* *

Later — lanterns low, crowds thinning, the mountain humming its contentment into the dark — the three of them climbed the ridge path above the Spire, out to the flat shelf of granite where they always ended the day. Their spot. Unremarkable to anyone else. Everything to them.

Ash sat between them, legs dangling over the drop, robes abandoned in favor of the patched traveling coat Ash refused to give up. Below, the valley glowed with dying fires. Above, the stars came out thick over Thornfell, and somewhere in that darkness the waystones Elodie had carved stood sentinel over the roads, forty-one small voices saying *we were here, we are remembered*.

"One year," Renn said. He had a flask of something illegal and passed it around; Ash got a swallow of cider, heavily negotiated. "A year since the door opened. Feels longer. Feels like several lives ago."

"That's because you lived several," Elodie said.

"Mm. The one before this one, I'd like a partial refund."

Ash leaned their head against Elodie's shoulder — casually, the way Ash did everything, as if affection were an accident that happened to them — and signed one-handed against her arm, the private grammar the three of them had evolved: *Next year, same place?*

"Same place," Elodie said.

"And the year after," Renn said. "And the year after that. I've committed to the route now. Legitimate freight, Thornfell circuit, annual stop at the Spire. Terrible business model. Wonderful retirement plan."

Ash laughed — the real laugh, the one that had taken months to coax out of a child who'd fled a living tomb, and Elodie closed her eyes briefly and let the sound settle into her like mortar into joints.

Grief didn't end. She knew that better than most. Tam Wilder's name still stood in the rain at Greyford. The Galleries still held their dead, who forgave but did not forget, and forgiveness that doesn't forget is the only kind worth having. Somewhere in the world there were always going to be sealed doors and people willing to maintain them.

But tonight, on a granite shelf above a singing tower, with a smuggler-turned-freightman pouring illegal brandy into the wind and a child humming the waking-song off-key against her shoulder, Elodie Voss — orphan of no family, bone-carver, mother-of-voices, woman who had once kept her door bolted against the entire world — felt the grief sit where it belonged. Behind her. Carried, not carrying. Integrated into the architecture the way load-bearing stone is integrated: invisible, essential, no longer the whole building.

Joy, she was learning, was not the absence of grief. It was what you built on top of it, carefully, over years, with people who helped hold the stones.

They stayed until the moon set. On the walk back down, drowsing between the two of them, Ash suddenly stopped on the path and lifted their head, listening — that particular tilt, the one Elodie knew from the mirror of her own gift.

*What is it?* Elodie signed.

Ash frowned at the dark valley below, at the distant lantern-glow of the lodges, at something Elodie couldn't see at all.

*The stones are restless,* Ash signed slowly. *Not here. Far. South, maybe. One of them keeps... repeating. Like it's trying to get a word out.* Ash's frown deepened. *It sounds like knocking.*

Elodie stood very still on the mountain path, the old cold moving up her spine — the cold she knew from a blizzard night two years ago, from a small figure half-frozen at her door, from every beginning she had ever failed to see coming.

"Knocking," she said.

*Like someone asking to be let in,* Ash said.

Far below, the Spire hummed on, gentle and unaware, and Elodie looked south into the darkness and felt the next year already turning toward her like a page.
