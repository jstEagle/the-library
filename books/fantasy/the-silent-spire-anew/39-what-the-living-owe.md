## 39. What the Living Owe

The workshop smelled of juniper oil and woodsmoke, the way it had for two winters now, and Elodie Voss had learned to love that smell the way other people loved the smell of bread — as proof of ordinary days, strung together like beads on a cord.

She sat at the long bench by the western window, where the afternoon light came down the valley in a slow gold flood, and she carved.

The bone in her hands belonged to Tam Wicker, a drover who had died in the autumn when his horse went down on the ice of the Millbrook ford, and his widow had brought the finger-bone up the mountain path in a cloth the color of unripe plums, apologizing the whole way for the trouble, the way people still did, the way they probably always would. Elodie had stopped telling them it was no trouble. It was trouble. It was the whole shape of her life, and she had finally made her peace with that.

The knife whispered along the grain. Tam Wicker's last words were close to the surface, the way a drover's death would be — close to the skin, close to the cold. She could feel them waiting under her blade like water under ice.

*Careful,* she thought at the bone, in the old habit of a woman who talked to her work. *Tell me when you're ready.*

The bone hummed.

That part had changed, in the year since the Spire. The dead no longer startled her out of her skin. They no longer arrived like intruders, like burglars of her sleep. They came the way neighbors came — with a knock, a clearing of the throat, a certain courtesy that she suspected they had learned from her as much as she had learned it from them. The eldest sleeper had called it *consultation*, the first time they'd spoken to her through the standing stones, and the word had lodged in her like a splinter she'd decided to keep.

*The ford,* Tam Wicker said, in a voice like gravel turning over under a boot. *The ice was singing. I heard it singing and I thought it was pretty.*

*It was pretty,* Elodie murmured, carving. *That's how it gets you.*

*Tell Marta the third rail of the byre fence is mine. I mended it in the spring and I never told her. She'll want to know what's hers.*

*She'll want to know you're still mending fences from the other side of the world, you mean.*

A pause. Then, with a drover's dry humor: *Aye. That too.*

Elodie smiled and kept the knife moving. Outside the window, the valley lay under its second snow of the season, the roofs of the village below smoking gently, the rebuilt workshop's new timber already weathering to match the old. They had rebuilt it while she was still on the road home — Renn had told her, with a grin she'd wanted to kiss off his face, that half the village had shown up with hammers the morning after the news came down from Thornfell. *The same ones who wouldn't lend you a cup of salt,* he'd said. *People are strange, Voss. Best thing about them.*

She had cried, when she'd walked up the path and seen it. She didn't tell anyone that either, though she suspected Ash had known. Ash always knew.

The child was at the far end of the bench now, in fact — if *child* was still the word, and Elodie suspected it would be the word forever, the way *home* was the word for this drafty room full of bone dust and juniper. Ash sat cross-legged on the stool, copying the old-tongue glyphs into a ledger the Crown's new Office of Covenant had sent up with far too much ceremony and far too little sense of humor. Ash's hand moved with the patience of a scribe and the occasional wildness of a fourteen-year-old, which was to say that every third glyph had a little flourish on it that no ancient civilization had ever intended.

"You're putting tails on the water-mark again," Elodie said, not looking up.

"The water-mark *has* tails."

"It has ripples. Tails are for the hound-mark. You're writing *the river is a dog.*"

"The river *is* a dog," Ash said, with the serene authority of someone whose people had been entombed alive for six centuries and therefore got to decide what the language meant. "It barks all spring. You've heard it."

Elodie laughed and let it go. Some arguments you won by losing them.

The knife reached the place where Tam Wicker's words lived closest to the surface, and the voice came up through her hands and into her chest the way it always did — not sound, exactly, but something older than sound, a pressure that became meaning. She closed her eyes and let him finish. The ice singing. The pretty, treacherous singing. The last thing he'd thought, before the cold closed over him, had been of Marta's hands around a cup of tea, and how he'd never once told her they were the best hands he'd ever seen.

Elodie carved that into the bone's heart, in the tight private script only bone-carvers read, and set down her knife.

"There," she said softly. "You're done, Tam. You'll sing pretty at the remembrance, and Marta will hear about her hands, and the fence."

*Good,* said the dead man, and was quiet, the way the dead were quiet now — not gone, just resting, the way a neighbor rests after helping with the harvest.

The light had gone amber. Ash had fallen asleep over the ledger, cheek on the glyphs, one hand still curled around the stylus. Elodie rose, stiff in the way the mountain cold wrote into her joints, and draped a blanket over the small shoulders without waking them. In the two years since the blizzard, since a half-frozen child had appeared at her door speaking a language older than any kingdom, Ash had grown three inches and filled out and learned to laugh out loud, a sound Elodie still caught herself stopping to listen to, the way you'd stop to listen to a bird that had come back after a long absence.

She banked the fire. She put the kettle on. She thought about supper — barley and the last of the smoked river-fish, if the river-folk's autumn gift had held, and it had, because the river-folk's gifts always held — and she was reaching for the pot hook when the knock came.

Two knocks. Small ones. The kind a small fist makes when it is trying very hard to be brave about it.

Elodie stood in the middle of her warm kitchen with the kettle beginning its low song, and something in her chest went very still and very wide at the same time, the way a field goes still and wide before rain.

She knew that knock.

Not the specific fist — no, that was new, that was a stranger. But the *kind* of it. The kind that had come through her door on a night of screaming wind two winters ago, half-dead, humming melodies that made finished carvings whisper. The kind that came with snow in its hair and something impossible in its eyes. The world had a habit, she had learned, of leaving its impossible things on doorsteps, and most people had the good fortune never to answer, and she had not had that good fortune, and had turned out, against every expectation of her own closed and careful life, to be the sort of person who did.

*You could pretend you didn't hear it,* said a voice that sounded like her old self, the self from before, the bone-carver who kept her shutters latched and her neutrality polished like a blade. *You could let it knock and knock and go away, and keep your barley supper and your quiet.*

But that voice had been losing arguments for two years now, and it knew it.

Ash stirred under the blanket. "Someone's at the door," they mumbled, not fully awake, and then, more sharply, waking the rest of the way all at once the way children do: "Someone's *at the door.*" And then, in the old tongue, a single word that Elodie knew now — knew it in her hands and her ribs the way she knew the names of her knives — a word that meant roughly *the thing that comes before a thing*, the word Ash's people used for omens, for the first tremor before the mountain speaks.

"I know," Elodie said. "Eat something first. Whatever it is, it'll still be impossible after supper."

But she was already crossing the room.

* * *

The wind had come up while they worked, and the snow came at the door sideways, needling, white on white, the whole valley erased down to a hand's breadth of visibility. Elodie opened the door onto that whiteness and for a moment saw nothing at all, only the storm and the dark coming down off the Spire's shoulder, and she thought — absurdly, calmly — *I should have brought the lamp.*

Then the whiteness moved, and a child stepped out of it.

A girl, Elodie saw at once. Young — younger than Ash had been, surely no more than eight or nine. She was wrapped in a coat that had been made for someone twice her size, cut down badly, the sleeves bound at the wrists with twine, and she was barefoot. Barefoot, in this. Her feet were blue to the ankle and she was standing in the snow as though she had been standing there for some time, as though she had walked a very long way and then simply run out of the strength to knock harder than twice.

And she was humming.

The sound came out of her small and steady, a thread of melody against the wind, and Elodie's whole body knew it before her mind did. Not the old tongue — not quite. Something older than the old tongue, the way a river's source is older than the river. The melody went through Elodie like a fish through water and into the workshop behind her, and from the shadows inside, every finished carving on every shelf began, very softly, to whisper.

The jawbone of the drowned ferryman. The finger-bones of the plague year. Tam Wicker, fresh-carved, still warm with his last words. All of them murmuring at once, a shelf full of the dead leaning in to listen, and the girl's eyes — dark, enormous, rimmed with frost — went past Elodie's shoulder to the sound, and she stopped humming.

"I know that song," Ash said, from behind Elodie, in a voice gone strange and young. "I know that song. That's the *lullaby*. That's the — that's the one from before. The one even the Galleries don't have." A pause, and Elodie heard Ash swallow. "Mother-of-voices. Where did she *come* from?"

"I was going to ask," Elodie said.

The girl looked up at her. Snow had gotten into her eyelashes and she did not brush it away, and Elodie understood with a lurch that it was because she had no strength left for small motions, that all of her was spent on standing and humming and knocking twice, and that the knocking itself had probably cost her something she could not name.

The girl spoke. The language was nothing — no, that was wrong, it was everything, it was the oldest thing Elodie had ever heard, and her gift, her strange unwanted gift that had spent a lifetime translating the last words of the dead, seized it and turned it over and gave her the shape of it in fragments, the way dawn gives you the shape of a landscape a few ridges at a time.

*Cold,* the girl said. That part was easy. Cold was cold in every language.

And then something longer, something that Elodie caught only the edges of — a word like *tower*, a word like *singing*, a word that might have been *waiting* or might have been *waking*, and a name. A name repeated twice, with a rising inflection at the end, the way you say a name when you are looking for someone.

Ash made a sound like they'd been struck.

"That's my name," Ash whispered. "In the first tongue. That's my name, the old way, the way it was said before — before the sealing. Nobody says it that way. Nobody alive knows it that way." Their hand found Elodie's sleeve and gripped it. "Elodie. Elodie, the Galleries don't know it that way. The *sleepers* don't say it that way. Where has she *been?*"

The girl swayed. The wind pushed her and she leaned into it like a fence post leaning into weather, and Elodie saw the moment her knees decided they were finished with the whole enterprise.

She caught her. Of course she caught her. There had never been, not for one single moment in two years, any other possible ending to this scene. The girl was light as a bird and cold as the river in thaw, and Elodie lifted her across the threshold and into the warmth and the light and the whispering of the dead, and she heard herself say the thing she had said once before on a night of screaming wind, when every instinct for survival had told her to bar the door:

"You're safe now. Come in out of the weather. We'll figure out the rest."

* * *

They figured out the rest the way they figured out everything — by lamplight, by increments, by the patient work of hands.

Hot barley water first, sweetened with the last of the honey, in sips, because Elodie had learned on a mountainside two winters ago what starving bodies did with a full meal. Ash worked the girl's feet in warm oil, chafing the blue out of them, murmuring in the old tongue the whole time — questions, mostly, soft ones, and the girl answered in that older tongue, and the two languages met in the middle like hands meeting across a table, close enough to hold on to, not quite close enough to be sure.

Elodie sat across from them with her own hands wrapped around her cooling cup, and she watched, and she listened with the part of her that was not ears.

The girl's name — the name she gave them, in her own tongue — was a sound like wind through a narrow place. Ash translated it, hesitantly, as *Little-Last* or possibly *The-One-Who-Was-Sent-After*, and the possibility in that translation sat in the warm room like a third guest nobody had invited.

"She says she was sent," Ash said at last, sitting back on their heels. Their face in the lamplight was very pale. "Sent to follow the song. She says the song went out of the mountain two years ago — when the Spire sounded — and it went out *looking*, and she was the one who could hear it best, so they —" Ash stopped. Started again. "Elodie, she says she's been walking since the spring. She says the song led her over the mountains and down the rivers and she lost it in the low country and found it again three days ago." Ash's voice cracked, and they didn't try to hide it. "It led her *here*. It led her to *you*. The song was your name. It was your name the whole time, in the first tongue, the one that means —" and Ash said the old word, the word they'd taught Elodie in the fever winter, *mother-of-voices* — "and she followed it to the door it came from."

The girl, wrapped now in the good blanket, the one Renn had bought in Greyford and pretended was a bargain, looked at Elodie with those enormous dark eyes, and said something short and simple, and this time Elodie didn't need Ash to translate it, because some things arrive in a person's chest already translated:

*Are you her? Are you the one the song was about?*

And Elodie Voss — who had spent forty years keeping her door latched and her heart latched tighter, who had built a whole life out of the principle that the dead were business and the living were risk — heard herself answer in the old tongue, badly, with Ash's patient correction arriving a half-beat too late to save her grammar:

"I don't know what I am. But you're not walking anywhere else tonight. Or tomorrow. Or —" and here her voice did something embarrassing that she elected to ignore, "— or as long as it takes. Whatever you were sent to do, you didn't walk all this way to do it cold."

The girl's face did something then that Elodie had seen only once before, on a different child, on a different blizzard night — a kind of unclenching, a letting-down of some weight carried so long it had become the shape of the body itself. She reached out one small hand and touched Elodie's wrist, right over the pulse, the way you'd touch a carving to hear what it remembered.

And Elodie felt it — felt the girl's gift brush against hers, felt the dead of the workshop lean in, felt the standing stones down in the village, three miles off through the storm, wake one after another like lamps being lit down a dark road, and heard, faint and far and unmistakable, the mountain itself begin to hum.

Not the angry hum of Thornfell's high passes. Not the pleading of the Weeping Galleries. Something else. Something that sounded, Elodie thought, with the last of her old skepticism going out like a candle in a draft, almost like a question.

Ash stood up so fast the stool went over. "Elodie. The Spire. It's — listen —"

Outside, through the latched shutters, through the storm, through the whole sleeping valley, the Silent Spire — silent no more, gentle now, a year into its sounding — was ringing. Not its remembrance toll, not its slow covenant chime. A new note. A note Elodie had never heard it make, high and clear and joyful, the way a bell sounds when it's been waiting a very long time to be rung.

The girl smiled — the first time, Elodie suspected, that she had smiled in a very long road — and said one word in the first tongue, and Ash laughed and cried at the same time and translated it with their whole heart:

"*Found.* She says: found, found, found."

Elodie sat in her warm kitchen with the storm howling and the dead whispering and the mountain singing, with one impossible child asleep under a blanket and another impossible child holding her hand, and she thought about a knock at a door, two knocks, small ones, the kind a small fist makes when it is trying very hard to be brave about it.

Two winters ago, she had stood on the far side of such a door with her hand on the latch and her whole closed life balanced on the question of whether to open it.

Tonight she had opened it before the second knock finished falling.

*Some things you only get to learn once,* she thought. *And then the world spends the rest of your life checking whether you meant it.*

She meant it.

She got up to put more logs on the fire, because it was going to be a long night, and because there would be questions by morning — from the village, from the Office of Covenant, from Corvane, who would arrive within the week with his gruff courtesy and his reformed conscience and at least three forms to fill out — and because the girl's feet would need tending again before dawn, and because the kettle, in this house, was never allowed to go cold for long.

And far to the north, through the storm, the Spire rang on — and Elodie could have sworn, though she would deny it to her grave, that its new note sounded exactly like a door being opened.
