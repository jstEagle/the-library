## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with forbidden commissions, Ada had learned over twelve years of taking them, was not the danger. It was the tedium.

Anyone could ward a doorway against fire or theft; the guild-approved charm-sellers did brisk business in such things, stamping their sigils with official wax and charging three times what the materials cost. But when Lady Vess of Copperlane wanted her husband's study warded against *her own curiosity*—because she'd promised his ghost she wouldn't read his ledgers until the year of mourning ended, and because her willpower was, by her own cheerful admission, made of wet paper—that required craft that existed nowhere on any guild ledger.

Ada sat at the workbench in the back room of their shop, sleeves rolled to the elbow, threading a hair-fine copper wire through a ring of river-iron. The candle had burned down to a stub shaped like a small melting tower. Outside, Corvane settled into its nighttime noises: the groan of cart wheels on cobbles, a drunk singing something patriotic and getting most of the words wrong, the distant clang of the Warden patrols changing shift at the Bellgate.

"Against curiosity," Ada murmured, twisting the wire into its ninth knot. "Not against reading. Against *wanting* to read." They snorted softly. "You could have just asked me to ward it against yourself, my lady. It's cheaper."

But Lady Vess paid well, and she laughed at Ada's dry jokes, and more importantly she asked no questions about why a modest charm-shop in the Tanners' Quarter kept irregular hours and never seemed to run out of rare components. Twelve years of customers like Vess had kept Ada fed, housed, and—most crucially—invisible. Not invisible in any magical sense. Invisible in the way that mattered: unremarkable, unremarked upon, a person whose name appeared on no list worth consulting.

Ada Kavanagh, seller of charms, middling quality, slightly overpriced. That was the whole of the story anyone in Corvane knew, and Ada had spent a decade making sure it stayed that way.

They reached for the quenching basin—and stopped.

The wire in their fingers had gone cold. Not cool. *Cold*, the way metal gets in deep winter, though the night was mild enough that Ada had left the window cracked to let out the smell of solder. Frost bloomed across the surface of the quenching water in delicate fern-patterns, spreading as they watched.

"That's not right," Ada said quietly, to the empty room, because saying things aloud to empty rooms was one of the few habits they'd allowed themselves to keep.

Then two things happened at once.

The first was outside: a sound like a bell the size of the sky being struck once and never allowed to finish ringing—a note so deep Ada felt it in their back teeth, in the fillings of old scars, in the floorboards. The window rattled. In the street, the drunk stopped singing mid-word.

The second was inside: the wards of Corvane shattered.

There is no honest way to describe this except that Ada felt it *in their chest*, which should have been impossible, because Ada Kavanagh had no magic. Everyone knew this. Ada knew this better than anyone; they had spent twelve years, thousands of hours, and a truly impressive amount of money ensuring that everyone knew this, including themselves, mostly successfully, most days.

But the wards broke like glass in their ribs anyway—every ward at once, the great border lattice that had stood since before the founding, the rings within rings within rings that the Wardens maintained with ceremony and the citizens ignored with the easy faith of people who have never known anything else. Ada felt each ring go. The outermost first, a soundless shattering like ice off a roof in spring. Then the next. Then the next, faster now, a cascade, a collapse, glass breaking inward—

And underneath the breaking, something in Ada *woke up.*

"No," Ada said. Their voice came out strange, doubled, as if someone else were speaking along an instant behind them. "No, no, no—"

They gripped the edge of the workbench. The frost on the quenching basin raced outward, climbed the walls, feathered across the ceiling in white lace. The candleflame stretched tall and thin and turned a color Ada had no name for—not blue, not violet, some color that existed only in maps they'd drawn and then burned.

Their hands were glowing.

Not metaphorically. Light seeped up from under their skin like lantern-light through paper, following lines—the lines of veins, and then other lines, lines that didn't belong to anatomy at all. Ada watched, frozen between horror and a horrible familiarity, as those lines drew themselves up their forearms: straight strokes, junction marks, contour curves, the patient geometry of elevation rendered in light beneath their skin.

"It's been twelve years," Ada whispered. "Twelve years. I buried you. I buried you properly. I said the words and everything."

The magic did not answer, because magic never answers, it only *insists*. It poured up through them like water finding an old channel, and every barrier Ada had built—the meditations, the lead-lined amulet they wore against their sternum for six years until it went inert, the deliberate mediocrity of their craft—all of it was a child's sandcastle before this tide.

They stumbled backward from the bench. The room tilted. Through the window, above the rooftops of the Tanners' Quarter, the sky was—

Wrong.

Ada turned fully toward the window and their stomach dropped out from under them, the way it does on stairs that aren't where your foot expected.

The stars were going out.

Not clouding over—there wasn't a cloud from horizon to horizon. Not dimming—this was not a gradual thing, a candle guttering. The stars were going out the way lamps are put out: deliberately, one by one, or rather in patterns, in *constellations*, each figure vanishing whole. The Spearwright went dark from haft to head. The Drowned Queen sank last, her crown of five bright stars holding on for a breath longer than the rest, and then the sky where she had been was simply black, a black with texture to it, a black like ink on vellum.

Ada knew that black. They had mixed that exact black once, grinding it by hand, for a map of a place that officially did not exist.

"Oh," Ada said. Very calmly. The calm of a person standing on a burning building's roof admiring the view. "Oh, you absolute *fool.*"

Because they knew. Of course they knew. There was exactly one structure in all the known realms whose sealing would pull the border wards down like rotten teeth and drink the stars like wine, and Ada had mapped it, and the mapping was the reason there was no record anywhere that Ada had ever mapped anything.

The Silent Spire.

The lines on their arms were no longer faint. They blazed now, gold-white, and they had spread past the elbows, past the shoulders, crawling down Ada's spine with a sensation like cold water poured inside the shirt. And they weren't random. Even through the panic—even through the animal, screaming part of them that was already calculating escape routes and cover stories—Ada's trained eye could see what was being written on their body.

A floor plan.

Corridors. Stairwells. A great spiral descending through nine levels into a chamber at the bottom that Ada's skin rendered as a perfect circle, ringed in fine concentric strokes, like a target, like an eye, like a door seen from directly above.

They hadn't thought about the Spire's interior in twelve years. Had refused to. And yet here it was, exact to the half-inch, every line of it surfacing from whatever sealed cellar of themselves they'd locked it in—as if the tower had spent all this time patiently waiting inside them, folded up small, and tonight it had simply unfolded.

"You could have picked anyone," Ada told their own forearms, with feeling. "There were forty apprentices. Forty! Orenne had a whole *cohort*. Half of them are still alive, probably, living perfectly quiet lives, and did the tower choose them? No."

The glow pulsed, once, slow, like a heartbeat. Like agreement. Like something vast and patient finding the one keyhole cut to its shape after a century of trying wrong keys.

Outside, Corvane was waking up badly. Bells had begun to ring—not the measured change-marking of the patrol bells but the ragged, overlapping alarm of every temple tower at once. Doors banged open down the street. Voices rose, asking each other questions no one could answer. A dog began to howl somewhere near the glue-works, and was joined by another, and another, a rising chorus of animals who understood something the humans did not yet.

Ada pressed their back against the wall and slid down until they were sitting on the cold floorboards among the frost-flowers, knees up, glowing like a festival lantern, watching the starless sky through their own window.

Think. They were good at thinking; it was the one tool that had never failed them. Options. One: run. Out of the city before dawn, before the Wardens finished cataloguing what had happened and started cataloguing *who*. But the wards were down—which meant the roads beyond Corvane were suddenly, for the first time in a hundred years, open in both directions, full of whatever had been waiting outside them. Running meant walking into that.

Two: hide. Douse the glow, wrap the arms, claim a charm accident, a component mishap. Plausible. They'd rehearsed plausible things for twelve years. But the glow was coming from *inside*, from bone-deep, and Ada suspected—with the sinking certainty of long experience—that whatever this was would not respect linen sleeves and a good lie. It had waited too long to be lied to.

Three—

There was no three yet. Three would arrive tomorrow, or the day after, wearing either a Warden's grey coat or a face Ada had spent twelve years not thinking about, and Ada would deal with it then, the way they dealt with everything: one careful hour at a time.

For now there was only the floor, the frost, the ringing bells, and the map of the Silent Spire written in light along their skin, patient as an epitaph.

Ada laughed—a single, unamused bark that echoed strangely in the little room.

"Twelve years," they said. "I want it noted, by whoever keeps notes on these things, that I was *extremely* careful."

The glow flickered—Ada chose to interpret it as sarcasm—and held.

Somewhere far to the north, past the dead wards, past the borders that no longer quite bordered anything, something in a tower that had been silent for a hundred years had just, very quietly, begun to pay attention.

And in the morning, Ada knew with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, the Wardens would come knocking on the doors of every charm-seller in Corvane.

Starting, if the city had any sense left, with the Tanners' Quarter.

Starting, if the night had any irony left in it at all, with theirs.

* * *

It took until nearly the third bell past midnight for the fear to settle into something Ada could work with, which in their experience was how fear always behaved: loud and useless at first, then, given time, oddly practical. Fear, properly aged, became a checklist.

So Ada made a checklist.

Item one: douse the glow. This proved harder than expected. Willing it away did nothing; reciting the suppression verses Master Orenne had drilled into them—verses Ada hadn't spoken aloud in twelve years and found, to their irritation, that they remembered perfectly—dimmed it only slightly, like drawing a curtain across a lit lamp. In the end what worked was mundane exhaustion: Ada wrapped their forearms in strips of linen soaked in cold well-water, and the light showed through the cloth like sun through a shutter, but faintly, and only if you already knew to look.

Which, Ada reflected grimly while winding the last bandage, was exactly the problem. The Wardens would know to look. By dawn, every grey coat in the city would know to look.

Item two: the shop. If soldiers were coming, the shop needed to look like the shop of a boring person. Ada spent an hour moving stock—hiding the genuinely clever pieces, the ones that required real craft, and dragging out the crates of mass-produced traveler's wards, the kind sold at every crossroads stall in the province. They arranged everything with a connoisseur's eye for mediocrity. A little dust, artfully applied. A price list with errors in it, because competent people checked their price lists and incompetent ones did not.

Halfway through, they caught themselves and had to sit down on a crate and laugh until their eyes stung, because this was it, this was the shape of their life: a grown adult, in the middle of the most important night in a century, rearranging trinkets to look less competently made. Twelve years of invisibility, and the best defense it had left them was the ability to fake being worse at things.

Item three: the story. A component mishap. An unstable resonance in a batch of river-iron—yes, river-iron was known to hold charge badly, any guild examiner would nod along—and the flash of light had been the batch discharging. The marks on their arms were burns. Burns from the discharge. Of the iron.

"It's thin," Ada admitted to the room, binding the last bandage. "It's insultingly thin. But it's thin in a way that takes an hour to disprove, and an hour might be all—" They stopped. Looked at their hands, wrapped and aching and still faintly warm, and finished honestly: "An hour might be all I get."

Item four, the last item, the item they wrote nothing down for and spoke no word about: *do not think about the Spire.* Do not think about its corridors, now traced in their own flesh. Do not think about the chamber at the bottom, the circle within circles. Do not think about the fact that the stars had gone out in constellations, in *patterns*, in the ordered sequence of something being switched off by someone who knew the switches—and that Ada, looking up at that blank black sky, had understood the sequence, had *recognized* it, the way one recognizes handwriting.

Do not think about whose handwriting it resembled.

Ada blew out the ruined candle stub, lay down on their narrow bed fully dressed with the bandaged arms crossed over their chest like a mummy in a cheap museum exhibit, and stared at the ceiling until the bells stopped and the dogs gave up and the city's panic wore itself down into a low, uneasy murmur, like a sea that has decided to stay angry about something.

They did not sleep. But in the grey hour before dawn, drifting on the border of it, they dreamed—not of the Spire, they were careful even asleep—but of Master Orenne's classroom, of chalk dust and forty desks, of the old woman's voice saying the first thing she'd ever said to them:

*"A map is a promise, little one. Every line you draw, you promise the world will hold still where you drew it. Break that promise often enough and the world starts keeping score."*

Ada woke with the sun—or with where the sun should have been.

The light through the window was the color of weak tea. Cold, thin, faintly wrong. On the windowsill, where frost had bloomed the night before, the fern-patterns had not melted. They had *grown*, climbing down the wall in silver threads, and at the end of each thread hung a tiny bead of ice shaped—Ada leaned close, heart beginning its slow sickening roll—shaped precisely like a five-pointed star.

From the street below came the sound of the morning crowd, louder than any market day, edged with something new.

Fear.

Ada sat up, flexed their bandaged hands, and got to work pretending, one more time, to be nobody.

It was going to be a very long day.
