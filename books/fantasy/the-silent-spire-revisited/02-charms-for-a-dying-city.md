## 2. Charms for a Dying City

The knock came before dawn, which in Corvane meant someone wanted something badly enough to be rude about it.

Ada Kavanagh pulled on a coat over the long-sleeved shirt they'd slept in — sleeves were a matter of policy now, had been since last night — and opened the door to find their neighbor Mrs. Pell standing there in a nightgown and three shawls, holding an empty basket like a weapon.

"They're saying the stars are gone," she announced.

"I heard."

"And the wards." Her voice dropped to the register Ada privately called *the doomsday whisper*, the one marketwives used for bad harvests and worse marriages. "Gone too. My cousin's boy works at the border post and he says the whole western line just — " she made a gesture like a bird flying apart, "— went."

"Mrs. Pell, I make charms out of tin and river-glass. I don't know anything about wards."

"You know more than you say, is what my mother would've said about you." But she thrust the basket forward anyway. "I need six protection charms. No — eight. One for the cat."

"The cat doesn't need — "

"He's been strange since last night. Yowling at the ceiling. There's nothing on the ceiling, Ada."

*There was nothing on the sky either,* Ada thought, and did not say it.

They sold her eight charms. The cat got the nice one, with the blue glass.

* * *

By midmorning the stall — a rickety table under an awning in Tanners' Row, between a cobbler who sang off-key and a spice merchant who smelled like everything good in the world — had a line down the street.

This had never happened. In twelve years of selling charms, Ada's busiest day had been the Festival of Lanterns, when tourists bought little luck-birds as souvenirs. Today there were people pushing. There was an actual queue, with the queue etiquette that Corvanians brought to everything: muttered complaints, strategic shoulder-angle, one elderly gentleman who had clearly arrived at four in the morning and treated the line as his personal property.

"Protection charm," said a baker's apprentice, slapping down coins. "Strongest you have."

"These are all the same strength," Ada said, handing one over. It was a tin disc stamped with a knot pattern, strung on leather cord. It cost four coppers. It contained, as far as any honest assessment could determine, tin.

"My grandmother says yours work better than the temple ones."

"Your grandmother is very kind."

"She says you've got the touch."

Ada smiled the smile they'd practiced for twelve years — modest, slightly self-deprecating, the smile of a person with no secrets whatsoever — and made change.

Here was the thing about Ada's charms: they didn't work. Not really. Not the way people believed. The knot pattern was decorative; the river-glass was glass; the incantation Ada murmured over each piece was a shopping list recited backwards, because it sounded occult and kept them from laughing. What actually happened was that people wore a small object they believed in, and belief steadied the hands, and steady hands survived winters. That wasn't magic. That was craft. Ada could defend it to any god or magistrate.

Except that last night, when the wards shattered, Ada had felt it *in their chest* — felt the city's great protective lattice crack like ice on a pond, felt something answer from inside their own ribs, something that had lain quiet for twelve years and then stood up and screamed. And this morning their left forearm, under two layers of sleeve, carried a burn in the shape of lines and circles that Ada had not put there and could not explain and refused, so far, to look at directly.

And here was the other thing: they were charging four coppers for tin.

"Three for my daughters," said a woman Ada recognized from the fishmonger's, eyes red-rimmed. "And one for my husband, he's a Warden auxili'ry, they're sending them to the border." She swallowed. "Will it hold?"

"It'll hold what it can," Ada said, which was the most honest sentence they'd spoken all day, and hated how it sounded like comfort.

By noon the basket under the stall was empty. Ada had sold every charm in stock, taken deposits on forty more, and earned in six hours what usually took three weeks. The spice merchant next door leaned over and said, "Terrible business, the stars," with the tone of a man discussing weather that was excellent for commerce.

"Mm," said Ada.

"You should raise your prices."

"I should absolutely not raise my prices."

"Grief-purchasing, friend. Basic economics."

Ada looked down the street at the queue — at the woman counting coppers twice because she needed a fourth charm but only had three coppers' worth; at the old man who'd guarded his place since dawn; at children pressed against their mothers' coats, looking up at a pale washed-out sky where stars should have been visible even by day, faintly, if you knew how to look, and weren't.

Something turned over in Ada's chest, and it wasn't the magic. Or maybe it was. Maybe guilt lived in the same neighborhood.

"I'll keep my prices," Ada told the spice merchant, "because if I gouge frightened people on tin, then when whatever happened last night happens again, I'll deserve exactly what's coming to me."

The spice merchant considered this. "You're a strange one, Kavanagh."

"Yes," Ada said. "Frequently."

* * *

The afternoon crowd thinned around the third bell, when the Wardens began marching patrols down Tanners' Row in pairs, tall figures in grey-and-iron livery with the city's eye sigil stitched at the collar. The queue reorganized itself around fear of queues being broken up, which in Corvane meant it became quieter and somehow longer.

Ada was wrapping a charm for a nervous clerk when a shadow fell across the stall.

"Warden-Captain Brannoc wants charm-sellers registered," said the Warden — young, freckled, apologetic in the way of someone delivering a message they hadn't written. "New order this morning. Martial readiness protocols. Nothing personal."

"Of course," Ada said. "What does registration involve?"

"Just this." The Warden produced a small stamp of dark metal, no bigger than a thumb, etched with a spiral. "Back of the hand. Standard surveillance sigil. Lets the ward-network track charm output per vendor. Inventory accounting."

"Does it hurt?"

"No. Tickles."

It did tickle — a cold crawling sensation, like a spider made of frost walking once across the skin, then settling. Ada watched the spiral sink into the back of their right hand, fine lines fading to nearly invisible, present only if you knew to look.

And then Ada felt it *notice* them.

There was no other word for it. The sigil woke, oriented itself, and looked — not at Ada's hands or face or coins, but *through*, downward, toward the place under the breastbone where something had been sleeping for twelve years and was now, unmistakably, awake. The sigil paused. Considered. Recorded.

Ada kept their breathing level through sheer professional discipline. Twelve years of hiding taught you things: how to slow a heartbeat, how to flatten the face, how to stand inside your own body like a tenant pretending the landlord never visits.

"Done," said the Warden cheerfully. "You're registered. Oh — " they consulted a slate, "— one question. Standard for all vendors in your category. Did you experience any unusual phenomena during last night's ward failure? Lights, sounds, physical sensations?"

"Physical sensations?"

"Some citizens reported feeling a 'lurch.' Others heard ringing. We're compiling data."

"I slept through it," Ada said. "I'm a heavy sleeper."

"You slept through the wards collapsing?"

"I slept through the Festival of Lanterns fireworks going off outside my window for eleven consecutive years. Ask my neighbors. Ask Mrs. Pell. She's complained about it in writing."

The Warden laughed, marked something on the slate — *unremarkable*, hopefully, or possibly *insufferable* — and moved down the row to the cobbler, who immediately began describing the strange dream he'd had involving a giant boot.

Ada exhaled slowly and looked at the spiral on their hand.

It had recorded *something*. They'd felt it happen, the way you feel a ledger entry being written against your name. Whatever the surveillance network fed its masters tonight, Ada's name would be on some list or other — maybe a short list, maybe a long one, maybe filed under a heading like *anomalies* or *follow up* or *heavy sleepers, suspicious*.

Twelve years. Twelve years of tin and river-glass and backward shopping lists, of being nobody from nowhere with a stall and a rented room and opinions about the correct way to sharpen a pencil. And in one night the sky emptied, the wards broke, their skin grew a map they hadn't drawn, and now the city itself had planted a small cold eye in their flesh.

Ada began packing up the stall early, hands moving through the familiar motions — charms into the padded box, awning folded, coins counted twice — while their mind ran ahead of them like a dog that had caught a scent.

Because here was the thought they'd been circling all day, the one shaped like a hook:

The burn on their forearm was a map. They'd only glanced at it once, in the grey light before Mrs. Pell knocked, and even that single glance had been enough to make their stomach drop, because the lines weren't random. Lines that radiated from a central point. Circles nested within circles. A floor plan, or the ghost of one — and there was exactly one building in the world whose floor plan Ada knew by heart, whose every corridor and stair and sealed door they had once drawn from memory in a master's study while candles guttered, a building officially unmapped for a hundred years, erased from every chart, unmentioned in every schoolbook.

They would have to look at it properly. Tonight. Sleeves rolled up, lamp lit, door bolted.

Ada tied off the last crate and pulled their coat tight over both arms — the hand with its new cold eye, the forearm with its impossible inheritance — and started home through streets full of people looking up at a starless sky, carrying four coppers' worth of hope apiece and none of it real, while under Ada's sleeve lay the one true map in the city, burning quietly, waiting to be read.
