## 4. Wardens at the Door

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which told Ada two things before they'd even reached the door.

First: it was official. Ordinary neighbors knocked at all hours and apologized for it. The city's functionaries knocked precisely when their schedules said to knock, and they did not apologize.

Second: whoever was behind that door had been standing there long enough to count the seconds between the first knock and its repetition. Three of them, evenly spaced, patient as a metronome. Nobody waited like that except someone who had been trained to believe that waiting was a form of pressure.

Ada set down the mug of chicory they had been pretending to drink, took one steadying breath, and opened the door wearing the expression of a mildly inconvenienced shopkeeper, which after twelve years of practice required no effort at all.

The man on the step was built like a piece of civic architecture—broad, gray-stoned, designed to make you feel that anything you did against him would constitute vandalism. His Warden's coat was charcoal wool without a single insignia, which somehow made it worse; only people very high up could afford to be unmarked. Behind him stood two juniors whose youth was still winning the argument against their uniforms.

"Warden-Captain Brannoc," he said, and produced a badge the way other men produced a knife. "Corvane Civic Ward Authority. You are Ada Kavanagh, proprietor of the charm stall on Tallow Row?"

"That's me." Ada leaned on the doorframe, casual as laundry. "If this is about the permit renewal, I filed last month. The clerk sneezed on it, but I'm fairly sure it went through."

Something flickered across Brannoc's face—not amusement, exactly. More like a man noting down the existence of humor for later analysis. "May we come in?"

"You may ask," Ada said, "and I may say yes, because my kettle is already hot."

They stepped aside. The three Wardens filled the little shop the way water fills a hull—quietly, thoroughly, from the bottom up. The junior pair began moving along the shelves without being asked, and Ada watched them do it and made themselves keep breathing slowly.

The shop was, on its face, innocent. Charm-work was legal in Corvane; it always had been, so long as the charms were small. Luck-threads. Damp-wardings. Little braided tokens against bad dreams. Everything on display was licensed, stamped, and honestly priced—which was to say, overpriced, because fear had been good for margins this week and everyone knew it.

What was *not* on display was anything that mattered. Ada had spent twelve years making sure of that. The back room held nothing but flour, thread, and an embarrassing quantity of tea. The floorboards under the counter were just floorboards. The map of Corvane on the wall behind the till—the one customers liked to point at while asking directions—was deliberately wrong in four places, errors small enough that no honest person would ever notice them.

Only a cartographer would notice them. That was the trap, and Ada had built it for exactly this kind of morning.

Brannoc did not look at the shelves. He looked at Ada, which was considerably more unnerving.

"Where were you last night," he said, "between midnight and the second bell?"

"Sleeping."

"Alone?"

"Alas."

He let the silence sit. Ada poured chicory into a chipped cup and offered it around; nobody took it, so Ada drank it themselves, mostly to have something to do with their hands.

"Your neighbors report light in your window until well past midnight," Brannoc said.

"My neighbors report everything. Mrs. Halloway once reported a cat for looking at her suspiciously. It turned out she was right, mind you, but you can't build policy on it."

One of the junior Wardens made a sound that might have been a swallowed laugh. Brannoc silenced him with a glance of such administrative finality that the boy would probably be filing reports about his own breathing by noon.

"The sky went out last night, Master Kavanagh."

"I noticed. Very upsetting. I'd planned to sell star-charms this season."

"And your wards—" Brannoc stepped closer, and now his voice dropped into something less official, more careful, like a man walking on ice he suspected was thin. "Every warded thing in this city felt the border fail. Every charm-seller in Corvane woke with a headache and a dead stockroom. Except you."

"I slept wonderfully," Ada lied.

It came out smooth. It came out easy. It came out with exactly the right degree of sleepy irritation, the small self-deprecating shrug, the sip of chicory at the perfect moment—and Ada hated it. Hated how easily it came, hated that twelve years of hiding had honed lying into a craft as fine as any map-drawing. Somewhere under the smoothness was the apprentice who had once wept because Master Orenne caught them shading a river valley too prettily. *"A map is a promise, Ada. Never draw what isn't true."*

Well. Orenne would be furious with them now.

"May I see your hands?" Brannoc asked.

"Buying or selling?"

"Inspecting."

Ada set down the cup and held out both palms, palms up, fingers spread—a gesture of surrender so old it predated language. Brannoc examined them with the detached thoroughness of a physician. Calluses from the awl. Stains of walnut ink that never fully washed out. A healing nick on the thumb from Thursday's rush order.

No burn. No scorch. No map.

Ada had scrubbed the arm raw at dawn, then wrapped it in linen and told themselves the bandage read as a kitchen injury. Under the sleeve, the lines still itched faintly, like a scar remembering itself. They had not yet dared look at it properly in daylight. In the dark hours, by candlelight, they had seen enough: fine ink-black tracery running from wrist to elbow, precise as engraving, and unmistakably, horribly familiar.

But Brannoc saw nothing. He released Ada's hands and stepped back, and Ada understood with cold clarity that the inspection hadn't really been about the hands at all. It had been about watching their pulse. Watching whether the offer of the hands had come too fast or too slow.

"Tell me about the Silent Spire," Brannoc said.

The room got very quiet. Even the junior Wardens stopped pretending to browse.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The Silent Spire. The tower at the center of the old district. Sealed a hundred years ago. Officially unmapped. Unofficially—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—*unremembered*. What do you know of it?"

"It's a tower," Ada said. "It's sealed. It has a very rude name for a building, if you ask me. What am I supposed to know about it? I sell luck-knots."

"Last night, when the wards fell and the stars went out, every registered magic-worker in Corvane felt the shock. We interviewed forty-one of them before dawn. Do you know what they described?" Brannoc's eyes were pale, unhurried, terrible. "A sound. A single note, very deep, like a struck bell heard through stone. All forty-one, the same note. And one of them—an old woman, a hedge-witch off Tanner's Lane—described something else besides. She said that in the moment the wards broke, she felt a *second* thing go through the city. Smaller. Brighter. Like a lamp being uncovered after years behind a shutter."

He let that hang in the air.

"She couldn't say where it came from. But she said it came from *nearby*, and Tanner's Lane is four streets from yours."

"Then I'd start with Tanner's Lane," Ada said. Their voice was steady. Inside, everything had gone white and ringing, the way the world goes when a floor gives way beneath you and you haven't started falling yet. A lamp uncovered. Twelve years behind a shutter, and in one breath—

"Master Kavanagh." Brannoc moved to the wall and stood beside the map of Corvane. He did not look at it. He looked at Ada looking away from it. "I am going to tell you something I do not say to many people, because most people wouldn't understand it, and the ones who would understand it don't need telling."

"Do go on. I love a riddle before breakfast."

"The Wardens were not founded to suppress magic." He said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it land harder. "That's the story we're given and the story we give out, and it keeps everyone comfortable. But our founding charter—one page of it survives, in a vault I've seen twice in twenty years—says something different. It says we were founded *to wait*. To keep a watch on a certain place, and to hold the city steady, until such time as—"

He stopped. Not because he wanted to. Because some instinct older than discretion closed his mouth for him.

"Until such time as what?" Ada asked, and was disturbed to find genuine curiosity under the fear.

"Until a cartographer comes home," Brannoc said.

* * *

They left shortly after. Brannoc gave no threats, issued no summons, made no arrests. At the door he paused and said, "We'll speak again, Master Kavanagh. Soon. There's rather a lot happening at present"—which was the understatement of a decade, given the frost creeping up the windowsills even as he spoke, delicate fern-patterns blooming in July—and then he was gone, and his juniors with him, and Ada locked the door and put their back against it and slid down to the floor among the dust motes.

"A cartographer comes home," they repeated to the empty shop.

The words sat in the air like smoke.

Because here was the thing Ada had not told anyone, not even themselves in daylight: last night, chest full of shattering glass, skin burning, they had not merely been *marked* by whatever erupted out of them. They had been *addressed*. The burning had a direction. It had come from the center of the city—from the old district, from the tower that officially did not exist—and it had arrived with the unmistakable tone of something that had been waiting a long time and recognized a knock at the door.

And here was the second thing, worse: Brannoc had said *cartographer*, singular. Not a guild. Not a tradition. One person. Which meant the Wardens' charter—however corrupted its modern keepers might be—had always known the Spire was waiting for somebody specific.

Ada unwound the bandage from their forearm.

In daylight the map was worse. Finer. It had deepened overnight, new contours surfacing like features rising through fog—stairways now, and a central shaft, and rooms branching off in patterns that made no architectural sense unless you understood that the building was drawn *from the inside outward*, the way you'd map a place you knew blindfolded.

Ada knew it. That was the horror of it. They knew it the way you know your childhood street—with the body, with the feet, with a familiarity no study could account for.

They had mapped the Silent Spire once, at nineteen, under Master Orenne. And the maps had been seized. And Ada had testified—under pressure, under threat, they told themselves, though the memory had gone soft and convenient around the edges—that the maps were real, that the work was theirs, that the names of everyone else in the workshop were true as written. And then the workshop had burned out of the records, and Orenne had vanished, and Ada had run to Corvane and buried themselves alive in a shop full of harmless charms.

Twelve years. A hundred years. However long the Spire had waited, it had apparently gotten tired.

"All right," Ada said aloud, to the map on their arm, to the tower across the city, to the frost climbing the glass. "You have my attention."

The lines seemed to shift—very slightly, very smugly—as if something far away had heard.

Ada got up, splashed their face, rewrapped the arm, and opened the shutters onto Tallow Row, where the morning crowd was already gathering with their frightened coins. If the Wardens were going to watch the shop, then the shop would look exactly like what it appeared to be: a tired merchant gouging a terrified city for luck-knots. Let Brannoc chew on that.

They had sold perhaps thirty charms to people who needed hope more than protection when the stranger arrived.

She came up the row against the current of the crowd, moving like water finding its way downhill, and she wore a soldier's coat dyed the gray-brown of wet slate, and her hair was shorter than Ada remembered and her face was harder than Ada dreaded, and she stopped three paces from the stall and looked at Ada with an expression that contained, in roughly equal measure, recognition and indictment.

"Hello, Ada," Sefa Ilan said. "You look well. That must have taken practice."

Ada's hands went still over the charm-basket, and somewhere behind them, unnoticed by either of them, a junior Warden across the street began, very quietly, to follow her.
