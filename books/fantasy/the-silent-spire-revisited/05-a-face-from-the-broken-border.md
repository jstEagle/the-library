## 5. A Face from the Broken Border

The wards of Corvane had been the finest Sefa Ilan had ever breached, which was why breaching them now felt like walking through a door someone had politely left open.

She crouched in the drainage culvert beneath the eastern wall and waited for the third bell of the night watch, as her scouts had instructed, though the instruction had turned out to be decorative. The ward-lattices that should have flared at her approach hung dead in the air, their threads severed, drifting down like ash from an invisible fire. She could see them—or rather, she could see where they *weren't*, gaps shaped exactly like a person moving quickly and trying not to be seen about it.

"Twelve years I planned around these," she murmured to Doss, her second, who was wedged behind her in the culvert and complaining quietly about the smell.

"You planned around them or through them?" Doss asked.

"Both. It took me four years to map the patrol rotations alone." She pulled herself forward into the gap. "And last night something unmapped them for me."

"That's not unsettling at all, Commander."

"It's extremely unsettling," Sefa agreed. "That's how you know it matters."

They came up out of the culvert into a garden of frost-killed roses behind a tanner's yard, and Sefa stood a moment in the shadow of Corvane's wall and felt twelve years fall away and then pile back on all at once, heavier than before. The city smelled the same—tallow and river water and bread from the ovens on Tanner's Row—and that was the cruelest part. Cities got to stay the same. People did not.

*Find the last cartographer.* That was the order. Her own order, drafted in her own hand after the night the stars went out, when the rebel camps in the Gray Hills had watched the sky go black over Corvane and every scrying bowl in the valley had filled with the same image: a tower, white and sealed, and a single figure standing before it whose face the bowls refused to show.

Her intelligence officer, a nervous former archivist named Pell, had cross-referenced the old regime's seized records—the ones that survived the burning—with tax rolls, guild registries, market licenses. And had come up, after three weeks, with a name so ordinary it made Sefa laugh out loud in the command tent.

*A. Kavanagh. Charm-seller. Stall 14, Lamplighters' Market. Licensed eleven years.*

Eleven years. One year less than twelve. One year less than the time since Vell's Crossing.

"They wouldn't," Sefa had said. "They'd have to be insane. Hiding in the city that hunted them, selling *charms*, out of all the—"

"Best place to hide," Pell had said mildly, "is inside the thing looking for you. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knew that. Ada Kavanagh had known that better than anyone. Ada Kavanagh, who had once mapped the borderlands so precisely that the old regime's soldiers had walked straight to the rebel villages without getting lost once. Ada Kavanagh, whose testimony before the Commission of Public Safety had named names, including three names that used to eat dinner at Sefa's family's table.

Sefa had spent twelve years perfecting the art of not thinking about Ada Kavanagh, and had gotten so good at it that thinking about them now felt like a muscle torn fresh.

* * *

Corvane by daylight was a city pretending very hard that nothing had happened.

The stars were still gone—that was the thing nobody would say aloud. Last night's sky had been a smooth black ceiling, no moon, no constellations, and this morning the city had responded the way cities respond to catastrophe: by opening the shops. Bakers baked. Gossips gossiped, at triple volume. A man outside the Wardens' garrison was loudly explaining to anyone who'd listen that it was a Warden test, a drill, they do this every decade, and everyone nodded because the alternative was to admit the sky had been switched off.

Sefa moved through it in a borrowed cloak, hair pinned under a wool cap, and let the crowd carry her toward Lamplighters' Market. Doss trailed at a discreet distance, doing his impression of a man interested in fish, which was unconvincing because he kept making faces at the fish.

The market hit her in waves: noise first, then smell—roasted chestnuts, wet wool, lamp oil, the particular sweetness of charm-smoke—and then, threaded under all of it, fear. You could read a crowd's fear the way you read a map, if you'd spent enough years reading both. This crowd was buying. Panic-buying. Protection charms, weather charms, anything with a sigil on it. Every stall selling them had a queue.

Stall 14 had the longest queue of all.

Sefa found a position at the mouth of the lane between a pickle-barrel and a lamppost, close enough to see, far enough to run, and looked at the person behind the counter for the first time in twelve years.

Her first thought, absurdly, was: *they're shorter than I remembered.* Her second thought was that she hadn't remembered them at all, really—not as a whole person. Memory had given her pieces: ink-stained fingers, a crooked half-smile, the way they'd tap a pen twice against their teeth before saying something clever. The rest she had filled in with accusation, year after year, until the accusation was the person.

But here was the actual shape of them, hunched slightly over their work in a coat too thin for the cold, selling charms to a frightened city with quick hands and a sales patter she could hear snatches of even from the lamppost.

"—no, madam, the hearth-ward goes on the *inside* of the lintel, otherwise you're protecting the street, which is generous but—" a pause, a smile Sefa felt in her ribs like a hooked line, "—yes, well. Your neighbor's chimney is also your business, I suppose. Two coppers."

*They look tired,* was her third thought, arriving unwelcome and soft. *They look like someone who hasn't slept since the sky went out.* Which was true of half the city, but on Ada's face it looked less like fear and more like recognition—as if whatever happened last night, they'd known it was coming. Had maybe been waiting twelve years for it.

An old woman at the front of the queue held out her palm for a blessing-charm, and Sefa watched Ada take the woman's hand gently, turn it over, and press a small wooden disc into it—then lean in and say something too quiet for the crowd. Whatever it was, the woman laughed, wet-eyed, and squeezed Ada's hand before shuffling off.

Sefa's chest did something complicated and unforgivable.

Because she knew charms, you see. She'd grown up in a village two days' ride from Corvane where charm-sellers passed through twice a season, and she knew what a protection charm was worth now that the wards were down and the stars were gone: nothing. Less than nothing. The whole lattice that small magic hung from had been cut, city-wide, and any charm-seller worth their salt would know their stock was dead weight.

Which meant either Ada Kavanagh didn't know—and twelve years of hiding had dulled them into mediocrity—or they knew perfectly well, and were selling worthless wood to terrified people anyway, taking their coppers, letting them walk away feeling safe.

*There,* said the twelve-year-old wound, satisfied. *See? They never change.*

It was almost a relief, how easily the anger came. Like putting on an old coat.

She waited. Patience was the one discipline she'd mastered completely; you couldn't survive the Gray Hills winters without it. She waited through the morning rush, through a Warden patrol that paused at stall 14 and spoke at length to Ada—she saw them go very still, very pleasant, hands flat on the counter, and something in their stillness told her this wasn't the first such conversation today—through the noon bells, through a boy who bought a luck-charm with a button and half an apple, which Ada accepted with grave solemnity.

Then, past midday, the crowd thinned. Ada began packing up—stacking trays, folding cloth, moving with the economy of someone who'd done ten thousand times—and Sefa peeled away from the lamppost and crossed the lane.

Ada heard her coming. Of course they did; they'd always had that trick, hearing a specific footstep out of a hundred. Their shoulders went tight a half-second before they turned, and Sefa understood, with a strange lurch, that they'd felt her coming the way you feel weather.

Their eyes met.

Twelve years collapsed like a bad roof.

"Oh," Ada said.

It was, Sefa reflected later, possibly the least eloquent greeting ever exchanged between two people who had once—well. Who had once.

"Hello, Ada." Her voice came out level. She'd rehearsed this. She'd rehearsed it in the command tent, on the road, in the culvert: *Hello, Ada*, cool as a blade going back in its sheath. Twelve years of rehearsal, and it worked, mostly, except her hand had closed hard around the strap of her satchel without asking permission.

Ada set down the tray they were holding. Very carefully. As if the tray might explode.

"You look well," Ada said, and then visibly heard themselves, and shut their eyes briefly. "I mean—you don't. I mean, you look—there's a war on, obviously, and the hills are cold, I've heard, and—" They stopped. Took a breath. "You look alive. That's what I mean. I'm glad you're alive. That sentence got away from me badly and I apologize."

Something in Sefa cracked sideways. Not softened—*cracked*, like ice underfoot, dangerous in both directions. Because that was Ada, that was exactly, precisely, maddeningly Ada, the person who could chart a mountain range from memory but couldn't survive a compliment without structural collapse, and she had hated them for twelve years and hated them now, and none of it helped, because hatred requires a stranger and this wasn't one.

"The stars went out," Sefa said. Flat. Businesslike. Safe ground.

"Yes."

"The wards fell."

"So I'm told. So everything is telling me." Ada glanced past her shoulder, at the market, at the world. "You came through the breach."

"I came through the breach."

"To find me."

"To find the last cartographer," Sefa said, and watched the words land. Watched them land like stones in still water. "That's what my people are calling you. There was a sighting, the night it happened. Someone standing at the edge of the Spire district with light coming off their skin like—"

"Don't." It came out fast and raw, all the sales-patter warmth gone. Ada's hand had gone to their left forearm, unconsciously, covering it through the sleeve. "Don't say it like that. I didn't ask for—last night wasn't—" They stopped again, jaw working. "What do you want, Sefa?"

Straight to it, then. No more fumbling. She'd forgotten Ada could do that too—go from wreckage to steel in one breath. She'd sat across from that person in a candlelit room above a map shop while they traced borders with one finger and talked about a future that had lasted another four months.

"What do I want," Sefa repeated. She leaned in over the counter, close enough to speak low, close enough that no one in the thinning crowd would mark anything but a customer haggling. "My family is dead, Ada."

Ada went white. Not pale—*white*, blood leaving the face entirely, and their hand on the counter curled inward.

"I know about Vell's Crossing," Sefa said. "I know what happened there after the maps were seized. I know what your testimony bought the Commission. I've had twelve years to learn it in detail, and I promise you I have learned it in detail." Her voice stayed level. She was proud of that, somewhere underneath, in the small room of herself that was still watching. "So when I ask what you want, understand that what *I* want is complicated. What my faction wants is simple. We want the Silent Spire opened. And every scrap of evidence says the only person alive who can redraw it is standing in front of me, selling dead charms to scared grandmothers."

"Your faction." Ada's voice was barely air. "You joined—of course you joined. After—" They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes for a moment. When they lowered it, they looked older. Good. Let them look older. "The charms aren't dead," they said suddenly, and it was so far off the path Sefa blinked. "Before you say it. The charms—I know what they're worth. I know the lattice is down. But the old woman this morning, the hearth-ward—it's not magic, not anymore, it's just oak and oil and a sigil she believes in, and belief is holding better than the wards did, so don't stand there and tell me I'm robbing people, because the only thing I've robbed anyone of in twelve years is my own—"

They cut themselves off. Hard. Like slamming a drawer.

"Careful," Sefa said softly. "Almost said something true there."

Ada looked at her. And there it was—the expression she'd dreaded finding and found anyway: not guilt exactly, not defiance exactly, but the terrible naked readiness of someone who has been waiting a long time to be punished and will stand still for it. It was worse than an apology. An apology she could have refused.

"You think I don't know what my maps did?" Ada said quietly. "You think I sleep?"

"I think you sleep fine. Stall 14. Eleven years licensed. That's not a man losing sleep, that's a man *settled*."

"And what would you have had me do instead?" For the first time Ada's voice rose, cracked, drew a glance from a passing vendor. They lowered it. "Run? To where? They had my face on a placard in every garrison from here to the coast. Stay and fight? With what? I'm a *cartographer*, Sefa. I draw lines. Lines don't stop soldiers. Lines showed soldiers where to go, once, and I have paid for that every single day in a currency you can't—"

"Vell's Crossing," Sefa said, and the name dropped between them like a coin down a well.

Silence. The market noise went on around them—haggling, bells, a child laughing somewhere, the ordinary machinery of a doomed afternoon—but the space behind the stall had gone absolutely still.

"My mother," Sefa said, "held the cellar door shut for six hours. Did your testimony mention the cellars? No. Of course not. Maps don't show cellars. Maps show what's *useful*." She heard her own voice go somewhere cold and distant and let it go there; it was safer there. "Three hundred and eleven people, Ada. I was sixteen. I was in the hills with my uncle's company when it happened, which is the only reason I'm standing here, and I have spent every winter since wondering whether that makes me lucky or just the one who got to remember."

Ada's lips moved. Nothing came out.

"Yeah," Sefa said. "That's about what I thought."

She stepped back from the stall. Her heart was hammering, which infuriated her; this was supposed to feel like justice, closure, some clean thing, and instead it felt like tearing a bandage off a wound that had healed wrong and needed reopening anyway.

"The offer stands until sundown tomorrow," she said. "Come with me willingly. Draw the Spire. Help us open it before the Wardens decide the solution to a dying city is a final fire." She reached into her cloak and set a token on the counter—a gray iron disc stamped with the rebels' sign, the mountain-and-star. "Or don't, and we'll come back for you anyway, because we can't afford to leave the last cartographer sitting in Warden territory. Either way, Ada, this is the last day of your twelve quiet years. Choose what you do with it."

She turned and walked away, and made herself not look back, and failed at the corner, and looked back.

Ada stood motionless behind the stall, the iron token untouched on the counter, staring at it the way people stare at a door they're afraid is locked—and afraid, worse, that it isn't.

* * *

Doss caught up with her two lanes later, breathing hard.

"Well?" he said. "Was it them?"

"It was them."

"And?"

Sefa kept walking. The frost was creeping up the walls now, she noticed—frost in broad silver ferns, in patterns too regular, too *deliberate*, spreading from the direction of the Spire district like something writing itself across the city.

"They said my name like it still fit them," she said.

Doss, wisely, said nothing.

"And then," she added, before she could stop herself, because the small honest room in her had pried its door open and wouldn't shut again, "they apologized to a tray."

She stopped at the mouth of the lane and looked back toward the market she couldn't see anymore, and admitted the other thing, the thing she'd carried out of that stall along with everything else.

"They didn't defend themselves, Doss. Not once. Twelve years I built the case against them, and when I finally read out the charges, they just—" Her jaw tightened. "They just stood there and took it. And I don't know yet whether that's guilt or whether it's worse. Whether it's someone who's been waiting so long to be punished that punishment feels like *relief*."

The frost ferns reached the top of the wall beside her and kept growing, delicate and relentless, and somewhere beyond them the Silent Spire stood sealed and dark, keeping whatever it kept.

"Commander," Doss said carefully. "If they don't come by sundown tomorrow—"

"They'll come," Sefa said.

She started walking again, faster, toward the breach and the hills and the camp full of people waiting on her word.

"How do you know?"

"Because I saw their face when I said Vell's Crossing." She didn't slow down. "Nobody looks like that unless they already know they owe a debt. And cartographers, Doss—they always pay their debts. It's the one rule of the trade."

Behind her, in Lamplighters' Market, the iron token sat gleaming on a charm-seller's counter, and the frost wrote its patient sentences across the city, and the longest winter in living memory tightened its grip on everything that was still warm—including, though she would not have admitted it under torture, a small stubborn coal of the feeling she'd sworn she'd buried at sixteen, in the ruins, with the rest of her family.

It had not burned out. That was the problem.
