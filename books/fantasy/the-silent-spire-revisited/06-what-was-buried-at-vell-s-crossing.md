## 6. What Was Buried at Vell's Crossing

Sefa Ilan had learned to sleep anywhere—in ditches, in saddle, standing up against a wall during a three-day bombardment—but she could not sleep in Ada Kavanagh's back room. The bed was too soft. The blankets smelled of cedar and ink. Everything in this room smelled of ink, actually, and it made her want to laugh and break something at the same time.

Twelve years, and the traitor still sharpened their own quills by hand. Some habits outlive their usefulness like old soldiers.

She sat on the floor instead, back against the doorframe, listening to Ada breathe in the next room through the wall that wasn't as thick as they clearly believed it was. She'd come for a cartographer. She'd found a ghost who sold luck charms to frightened people and flinched when Sefa said her own name aloud—as if Sefa were the one who should have stayed dead.

"You're not sleeping either," Ada's voice said through the wall, muffled, resigned.

"I'm keeping watch."

"On what? My stock of dried lavender?"

"It could be dangerous."

A pause, and then—gods help her—a small, tired huff of laughter from the other side of the plaster. That was the worst part. That was the part that made her want to put her fist through something. Because twelve years ago, in another life, in a summer that smelled of cut grass and hot slate, Sefa had known that laugh intimately. She had chased it across a rooftop in the Spire district while a young Warden patrol whistled below. She had traded half her supper ration for it, once, in a famine year, just to hear it twice in one evening.

Now it came through a wall, thin as thread, and she hated how much it cost her to hear it anyway.

"Ada." She said it before she could stop herself. "We need to talk about Vell's Crossing."

The breathing stopped.

"I know you know some of it," she went on, because she had led soldiers into breaches and she knew how to keep walking when the ground was burning. "I know you know *some*. But you don't know all of it, and you've had twelve years to not-know it comfortably, and I'm done letting you be comfortable."

The door opened. Ada stood there in a nightshirt with an ink stain on the collar, hair flattened on one side, looking less like the last cartographer of the Silent Spire and more like someone who'd been caught skipping lessons. Twelve years older than the person Sefa had loved, and somehow exactly the same age.

"There's tea," Ada said, which was the most Ada sentence in existence. When the world ended, Ada Kavanagh would offer you tea and apologize for the cups being chipped.

"Ada."

"Or wine. I have one bottle of wine I've been saving for—" They stopped. Sefa watched them decide, visibly, whether to finish the sentence *for an occasion* or admit it had been saved for twelve years for a homecoming that never came. They didn't finish it. "Sit down. I'll listen."

* * *

They sat across the little table from each other, and Sefa told it the way she'd rehearsed telling it a hundred times on the march, and discovered that rehearsal was worthless.

"Vell's Crossing is a bridge town," she began. "You know that much. South bank farms, north bank market, one stone bridge over the Sere. Four thousand people, maybe forty-five hundred. It mattered for exactly one reason: every road between Corvane and the eastern realms passed over that bridge, and every road needed a map to tell it where to go."

Ada's hands were wrapped around a cup of tea they weren't drinking. Steam rose past their chin.

"When the regime seized your atlas—the one you drew, the survey of the borderlands, the one with—" Sefa stopped herself. "You remember what was in it."

"The crossing points," Ada said quietly. "The fords. Where the wards were thin enough to walk through."

"Where the wards were thin enough to walk an army through." Sefa kept her voice flat. She'd learned flatness from a sergeant named Doss who'd died at Vell's Crossing, so it seemed only fair to borrow it back. "You testified that your maps had been stolen. You testified under Warden seal that you'd never intended them for military use. Both things true. Both things useless. The regime didn't care whose hands the atlas came from. They cared where it pointed."

"They crossed at Vell's Crossing." Ada's voice had gone thin. "I read the reports. I read—"

"You read *reports*." Something in Sefa cracked, just slightly, and flatness abandoned her. "Yes. Reports. Let me give you the version that doesn't fit in reports, then. The Third Column came down from the hills at dusk on the ninth of Harvestmonth. They didn't need to fight for the bridge, because your map told them about the mill ford, two miles upstream, where the water runs shin-deep in autumn. Except nobody in Vell's Crossing thought of that ford as a military feature. It was where children caught minnows. It was where my mother did the wash."

Sefa heard her own voice go strange and distant, the way it did when she talked about her mother, and she let it, because Ada needed to hear it strange and distant or they would find a way to make it smaller.

"The town had a militia. Thirty men with hunting bows and one ancient cannon that misfired more often than not. My father drilled them on Sundays. He was very serious about it. He wore his grandfather's breastplate that didn't fit and he called everyone 'soldier' until they laughed." A breath. "When the Third Column came through the ford at midnight, the militia formed up on the north bank to hold the bridge approach. Holding the wrong place. Defending the thing the enemy didn't need. Your map told the column exactly that: *the town will defend the bridge; the ford is undefended.*"

"That's not—" Ada started, and stopped, because whatever they were going to say, they heard it themselves, and it died in their mouth.

"My father died on the bridge," Sefa said. "Defending a bridge no one attacked. My mother died in the wash-house by the ford, because she'd gone back for the laundry. The fighting lasted four hours. Then the column burned the granaries—not for strategy, but because their quartermaster was corrupt and they were hungry—and marched on, and left Vell's Crossing to its winter with nothing stored against it. The famine after took more than the battle did. Three hundred souls, all told. Out of forty-five hundred."

She looked at her hands. They were steady. They were always steady now.

"I was fourteen. I was visiting my aunt in Corvane, learning to write proper letters, being a nuisance. That's why I lived. My entire family fit inside a decision somebody else made about lines on paper."

The room was silent except for the tick of the stove. Outside, somewhere in the warded city, a watch bell rang the second hour, and both of them flinched at the sound—because bells meant alarms now, since the night the stars went out, and probably would forever.

"I didn't draw the ford for them," Ada said finally. It came out barely above a whisper, and Sefa watched them hear their own words and understand how small they sounded. "Sefa, I swear to you—I mapped those crossings for travelers. For pilgrims. So people wouldn't drown trying to reach the shrine towns. I never—"

"I know."

"—and I testified, I told the Wardens everything, I—"

"*I know.*" Sefa leaned forward. "That's the whole cruelty of it, Ada. Nobody did anything wrong. You drew honest maps for honest reasons. The regime stole them and used them honestly, in their way—an army wants accurate information too. And my family died in the gap between your honesty and their accuracy. There's no villain in it. That's what I spent ten years unable to forgive. Not you. The *shape* of it."

Ada set down the untouched tea. Their hands were shaking now, fine tremors, the hands of someone who worked at fine scale and couldn't hide it.

"I told myself hiding was survival," they said. "Twelve years. I told myself if I made myself small enough, dull enough, if I sold charms that were mostly wishful thinking and glue, then no one would ever use me again. No one would ever take something out of my hands and turn it into—" They pressed the heel of their palm against their eyes. "And meanwhile people were freezing in queues outside my stall this morning, buying protection charms that don't work, and I *took their coins*, Sefa. I took their coins and gave them nothing and called it survival."

"Yes," Sefa said. "You did."

"You're supposed to argue with me."

"No. I'm supposed to be honest with you. We've both had enough of the other kind." She paused, and then, because the flatness had served its purpose and she was tired down to the marrow, she let a little warmth leak back in. "For what it's worth, the charms were never entirely nothing. Your knot-work held better than any other seller's. People noticed. That's partly why we looked for you—the rebels trade in your charms. Half my company wears one. They call them Kavanagh knots."

Ada made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Lucky charms named after me. That's the most cursed joke the world has ever played."

"Wait until you hear what they think the K stands for."

"Don't tell me."

"'Keep safe.'"

Ada dropped their face into their hands. Their shoulders shook—grief or laughter or both, and Sefa recognized, with a lurch, that she'd missed even this. The way Ada broke apart into pieces that were somehow also funny. The way they could stand at the edge of an abyss and complain about the price of rope.

But then the shaking stopped, and when Ada lifted their head, their eyes were wet and clear and looking directly at her, and Sefa felt the look land somewhere beneath her breastplate, in the unarmored place.

"Tell me the rest," Ada said. "All of it. Don't soften it."

"There isn't more. That's all of it. Four hours of battle and one winter of hunger and three hundred names, and a map that told the truth the whole time." Sefa stood, because sitting still had become impossible. "Except—one more thing. One thing I've carried, and you should carry it too, because it's the reason I'm here and not just to recruit you."

Ada waited.

"The Third Column's commander kept a journal. Standard issue—regime officers logged everything. After the war collapsed, our people raided the archives and I read it. He wrote about the crossing. About how easy it was. And then he wrote one line I've never forgotten." She recited it slowly, the way you handle something with edges. "*'The maps of this country are worth more than its armies. Whoever holds the cartographer holds the realm.'*"

Silence.

"He never learned your name," Sefa said. "He wrote 'the cartographer' like it was a position to be filled. Like a chair at a table." She turned toward the window, where the sky beyond the glass was starless and wrong. "And now the stars are gone, and the wards are down, and every faction in this broken country has remembered that line. The Wardens want you found and filed. My faction wants you found and used. And the winter wants you dead along with everything else that remembers the old shapes of the world."

"And what do *you* want?" Ada asked. Very quietly.

Sefa opened her mouth to give the answer she'd prepared—duty first, always duty first, the answer that had gotten her through twelve years and two wars—and found she couldn't say it. Not in this room that smelled of cedar and ink. Not to the person who had once traded her an apple for a bad poem and laughed at the poem and eaten the apple anyway.

"I don't know yet," she said, and it was the most honest thing she'd said all night. "Ask me again when we're not sitting in a city with no stars."

Ada nodded slowly. Then they reached across the table—not far, the table was small—and touched the edge of their own forearm, where the scorched map showed faintly through the rolled-up sleeve. Even in the lamplight Sefa could see it now: lines, contours, the unmistakable spiral footprint of a tower.

"Sefa," Ada said. "This mark on my arm. I've been reading it since the night the sky went black. And there's something in it I don't understand. Something that shouldn't be there."

"What?"

"It's not just the Spire's floor plan." Their voice had changed—flatter now, careful, a cartographer reporting a fault line. "There's a second structure drawn underneath it. Deeper. Older. And it goes *down*, Sefa. Further down than any cellar, further than the foundations. Whatever the Silent Spire was built to be—" they swallowed, "—it was built on top of something else. Something sealed. And my skin is the only map in the world that shows the way in."

Sefa stood very still. Through the window, the starless dark pressed against the glass like a hand.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we start walking."
