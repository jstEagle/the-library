## 7. The Long Winter Begins

The frost came for Corvane the way an honest debt comes—slowly enough to be denied, then all at once.

Ada noticed it first on their windowsill, three days after the stars went out. A lacework of ice had climbed the glass from the outside in, and it was not behaving like frost should behave. Frost, in Ada's experience of twelve careful winters in Corvane, formed in feathers and ferns, pretty and mindless. This frost had *structure*. It branched like a river delta. It branched like a map.

"Oh no," Ada said to their kettle, which did not answer, being a kettle. "Oh no, no, no."

They touched one fingertip to the window and pulled it back fast. The cold wasn't the dry bite of ordinary winter. It hummed. Somewhere under the chill was a frequency, faint as a plucked string two streets away, and every cartographer's instinct Ada owned stood up and pointed north, toward the Spire district, where the frost was thickest and where, officially, nothing at all had ever happened.

By noon, half the market's awnings wore white rime on their southern edges—the *southern* edges, in the sun, in late autumn—and Old Marta the fishwife was swearing creatively about her catch freezing solid in the barrel while the barrel itself sat in open air.

"It's an early winter," said the man selling turnips next to Ada's stall, with the determined cheer of someone who has decided not to think about something.

"Turnips don't freeze from the inside out," said Marta.

"They might if they're *ambitious* turnips."

Ada sold eleven protection charms that morning and felt like a fraud with every one. The charms weren't worthless, exactly—they'd keep a draft out of a shutter, calm a frightened horse, warm a bedroll for a night or two. But they were candles against what was coming down from the Spire district, and Ada knew it, and the buyers didn't, and their hands shook when they took the coins.

*You could tell them,* whispered the map on Ada's forearm, in the wordless way maps have. *You could tell them what you are.*

"I could also be arrested," Ada muttered under their breath, "drawn and quartered, and *then* useless."

"You say something?" asked the turnip man.

"I said lovely weather."

He looked at the sky, which was the color of dirty pewter and dropping flakes that fell too straight, without wind, like ash. He decided, generously, not to argue.

* * *

Sefa Ilan had survived three winters in the borderlands, and she had a soldier's taxonomy of cold. There was workable cold, which stiffened fingers and made cursing efficient. There was dangerous cold, which killed the careless. And then there was the cold coming off Corvane now, which she smelled before her scouts reported it—a clean, mineral smell, like snow off stone, like the air inside a tomb that had been shut a very long time.

"It's not weather," said Dagny, her second, hunched over the brazier in their camp among the ruined orchards east of the city. Dagny was ex-Warden herself, broad as a door, and she did not scare easy, but she kept her hands near the coals the way other people kept hands near weapons. "Weather moves. I've watched this stuff for two days. It doesn't move *in*. It spreads *out*. From the center."

"The Spire district."

"You say that like you're surprised."

Sefa didn't answer. She was looking at the city—Corvane's walls gray against the gray sky, its chimneys smoking thin and desperate—and doing arithmetic she hated. Inside those walls was the last cartographer of the Silent Spire, the person who had drawn the maps that got her family killed, the person whose face she could still summon with humiliating clarity: the ink-stained fingers, the laugh that started in the eyes before it reached the mouth, the terrible, practiced innocence.

Twelve years, and she still caught herself wondering if Ada Kavanagh had been warm the night the Wardens came for the maps. She hoped not. Hoping otherwise felt like a betrayal of Vell's Crossing, and she had enough of those to carry.

"Commander." One of the pickets slid into the firelight, breath steaming. "Refugees on the east road. Farmers, mostly. Says their fields died overnight. Not failed—*died*. Turned to white glass under the soil."

"All of them?"

"The whole valley, far as we can see."

Dagny spat into the frost. "That's the grain supply for half the province."

"That's the point," Sefa said quietly. "Whatever's waking up in that tower, it isn't angry. It isn't even cruel. It's just—" she searched for the word, and found it in an old memory, a classroom, a voice she'd loved saying it, "*indifferent*. Like a glacier. We're not its enemy. We're its terrain."

The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark, the frost creaked as it grew, a sound like a ship's timbers, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"We go in tomorrow," Sefa said. "Through the broken wards. Find the cartographer."

"And if they won't come?"

Sefa held her palms to the heat and thought of a market stall, a familiar silhouette bent over charms, twelve years of smallness worn like a disguise.

"Then I'll remind them," she said, "what their maps cost. Everyone remembers eventually. It's the one lesson I can guarantee."

* * *

Warden-Captain Brannoc had commanded the Third Circle garrison for nine years, and in that time he had learned that cities told you the truth through their queues. Bread queues meant hunger coming but not arrived. Fuel queues meant panic but not collapse. But on the fourth morning of the unnatural frost, the queue outside his garrison doors was neither bread nor fuel—it was people simply standing together, because standing alone in that cold had begun to feel like dying, and Brannoc read it for exactly what it was: the beginning of the end of the city's belief in itself.

Time, then, for the measures he'd been drafting since the stars vanished.

"Martial law," he told his officers in the map room, tapping the chart of Corvane with one gloved finger. "Curfew at sundown. Rationing by district, grain reserves opened under guard. And—" here he paused, because he knew how this next part would land, "—conscription of licensed charm-makers. Every registered seller reports to the garrison within two days. They'll be assigned to warming stations and ward-repairs. Paid, fed, housed."

"Charm-sellers, sir?" Lieutenant Vess's lip curled with the particular disdain the military reserve for tradesmen. "Half of them are frauds. The other half sell love potions to teenagers."

"Then we'll soon find out which half is which, won't we, Lieutenant." Brannoc kept his voice flat. "Because the frost is not seasonal, and the granaries are frosting from the *inside*, and last night two of my own sentries froze to death at their posts wearing full winter kit. Whatever is happening, it is happening *at* us, and I will not have this city freeze arguing about jurisdiction." He rolled up the chart. "There is also a list. Names of charm-sellers flagged for special attention. Anyone with irregular licensing. Anyone who came to Corvane in the years after the Border Sealing. Anyone whose paperwork is too clean."

Vess frowned. "Too clean, Captain?"

"Frauds have messy papers," Brannoc said. "Liars have perfect ones." He did not explain further, because explaining would require admitting what he suspected, and what he suspected—that somewhere in his city moved the last living cartographer of the Silent Spire—was knowledge the High Wardens had spent a century pretending was superstition. His orders on the matter were old, sealed, and signed by men long dead, and they amounted to a single instruction: *if such a one appears, bring them gently.*

Brannoc had re-read the order eleven times since the night the stars went out. He had a feeling the word *gently* was about to become the hardest word in his vocabulary.

* * *

The summons came to Ada's stall at midday, delivered by a Warden runner who did not wait for a signature.

**ALL LICENSED CHARM-MAKERS TO REPORT TO THIRD CIRCLE GARRISON WITHIN TWO DAYS FOR CONSCRIPTION TO CIVIC WARMING SERVICE. NONCOMPLIANCE WILL BE TREATED AS SEDITION.**

Ada read it twice. Around them, the market was dissolving—not in panic, but in a slow, orderly despair, stalls closing early, vendors hauling goods home through streets where the frost had begun climbing the walls in branching, delta-shaped patterns that everyone pretended not to look at.

"Well," said the turnip man, reading over Ada's shoulder. "Sedition. That's new. Usually they start with fines."

"They've escalated," Marta agreed grimly, wrapping her frozen fish in sacking. "My cousin says the garrison's got braziers on every corner and soldiers counting loaves. Martial law, she heard."

"Conscription of charm-sellers," Ada said, mostly to themselves. Which meant: *they know the charms aren't working. They want to see who can make them work.* Which meant: *a room full of frightened craftspeople, tested one by one, by Wardens who already suspect that one of them is not a craftsperson at all.*

Two days. Two days to decide whether to walk willingly into the one building in Corvane designed to find people like Ada—or to run, which meant the broken border, the wilds, the frost that was spreading outward from the Spire like a tide with no moon to answer to. Or—or—

Or the third option, which had been sitting in Ada's chest since the night the stars went out, patient as the ice. The option shaped like a tower. The option whose floor plan was written on their arm in their own scalded skin, waiting to be read.

They were packing up the stall—hands numb, breath steaming, coins cold enough to burn—when they saw the child.

A little girl, maybe six, wrapped in a blanket that had been a dress in better times, standing at the mouth of the alley beside the fishmonger's. She was watching the frost on the wall with the total absorption of the very young, and as Ada watched, she reached out and touched it—and the frost *bloomed*, rippling outward from her fingertip in a perfect six-pointed flower, silver and precise and utterly impossible.

The girl laughed. A bright, startled, delighted sound in the dead gray street.

Ada's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

It wasn't just the Spire, then. It wasn't just the tower waking and the world freezing around it. The magic was *leaking*—seeping out of the sealed dark and into the ordinary, into fishwives' walls and farmers' fields and small cold children who ought to have no gift at all. The seal wasn't holding. The seal was *thawing*, and everything it had imprisoned was running everywhere at once, like water finding every crack in a dam.

And somewhere in that spreading white, a rebel commander with a soldier's memory and a grudge twelve years deep was already walking toward Ada's door.

Ada closed the stall. Pulled their coat tight. Looked once, only once, at the north, where above the rooftops the Spire district glowed faintly under its frost, pale as bone, like a lamp lit behind a curtain.

Then they turned south, toward the garrison, toward the queue of frightened charm-sellers already forming in the snowless falling ash—and began, with the steady hands of a liar whose papers were far, far too clean, to walk directly into the trap.

*If I must be found,* they thought, *let me at least choose the finding.*

Behind them, unnoticed, the little girl traced another frost-flower on the wall, and hummed a tune she had never been taught.
