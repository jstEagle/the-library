## 8. The Rebel Bargain

The frost had reached the third shelf of my stall by morning.

I knew this because the third shelf was where I kept my inks, and inks do not freeze politely. They freeze with intent. The madder-red had cracked its bottle overnight and bled a long red stain across the wood that looked, if I squinted, like the coast of a country that no longer existed. Which was, I suppose, on theme for my entire life.

"Your prices are criminal," said Widow Hesk, breathing steam at me through the gap in her scarf.

"My prices are the same as they were last week," I said. "It's the frost that's gone criminal."

"Frost doesn't have to answer to anyone. You do." She slapped two coppers on the counter anyway and took the hearth-charm I'd made her, the one I already knew was about as useful against magical winter as a kind word against a landslide. She tucked it into her shawl like it was treasure. That was the worst part. Not the lying—lying I was good at, twelve years of practice—but the gratitude. People kept thanking me for things that couldn't save them.

I watched her go down the queue line, past forty-odd souls stamping their feet in the grey light, breath rising from them like the city itself was exhaling its last warmth. Somewhere behind the rooftops to the north, invisible from anywhere but my nightmares, the Silent Spire sat under its snowfall that didn't melt and didn't stop.

I sold thirty-one charms before noon. Every one of them worthless. Every one of them bought with money people needed for bread.

Business, as my mother would have said, is booming.

* * *

Sefa Ilan came back at dusk, which told me something right away. The first time she'd appeared at my stall—three days after the wards fell, wearing a fishmonger's apron over armor I could see the lines of through the canvas—she'd come at midmorning, bold as brass, because she wanted me to know she could be bold. Dusk meant caution. Caution meant the Wardens were watching me now too, not just the streets.

Or it meant she wanted to talk somewhere private, and had decided my freezing hovel of a shop counted as private because nobody sensible would linger there.

"You're out of everything," she observed, picking up an empty charm-rack and putting it down again. Her voice hadn't changed in twelve years. Low, dry, with the faintest scrape of amusement underneath, like a boot heel on gravel. It used to undo me completely. It still did a little, which I resented enormously.

"I'm out of everything," I agreed. "Would you like to buy nothing? I can give you a good price."

"Ada."

"Sefa." I let the silence sit between us. In it, all the old things stirred: Vell's Crossing, the testimony I gave at nineteen, the way she'd looked at me across a courtroom when the maps were read out and the names of the dead began. Twelve years, and we had exchanged perhaps sixty words since she'd walked into my stall and said *hello, cartographer* in a voice like a knife being drawn slowly.

"You look terrible," she said.

"The winter's killing your crops, your livestock, and possibly the concept of spring itself, and you're here to critique my complexion."

"It's a professional interest. You look like a man who hasn't slept since the stars went out."

"A woman," I said automatically, the way I always did, and she shrugged the way she always had—*doesn't matter to me*—which was, annoyingly, one of the reasons I had loved her in the first place.

She pulled up the stool behind the counter without asking, the way she always had, and sat down, and for a moment we were just two people in a cold shop pretending the world wasn't ending, and it was almost bearable.

Then she put a folded paper on the counter between us, and the moment died quietly, like everything else that winter.

* * *

I should explain what the paper was. This requires context, and context requires admitting things I've spent twelve years not admitting, so bear with me.

Before the Spire was sealed—a century ago, officially; twelve years ago, effectively, because sealing is a process and not a door slamming—I had been its youngest apprentice cartographer. Not a mapmaker of places. A mapmaker of the Spire itself, which rearranged its own interior the way other buildings rearrange dust. Master Orenne taught me that a true chart of the Spire couldn't be drawn from outside. It had to be drawn from memory, from blood, from the tower learning the shape of the person drawing it. I had been very young and very proud of how well it learned me.

Then came the tribunal. Then came my testimony—the carefully true, cowardly, catastrophic testimony that named names and confirmed locations and gave the old regime exactly what it needed to seize every working map of the Spire's interior. Then came Vell's Crossing. Then came twelve years of smallness, of charm-stalls and false names and a scarred forearm I wore gloves over even in summer.

The paper Sefa put on my counter was a copy of a Warden requisition ledger. I read three lines and felt my stomach drop through the floorboards and keep going.

"They're moving the seized maps," she said. "Out of the archive-vaults under the Citadel. To somewhere new. Brannoc's orders, personally signed."

"Why does a rebel commander have Warden requisitions?"

"Because we have friends who are tired of losing people to the cold." She leaned forward. "Ada. Listen to me. The Wardens aren't guarding those maps. They're *using* them. There's a faction inside the Citadel that thinks if they can unseal the Spire themselves, they can control whatever's inside. Whatever caused this winter. Whatever the sealing was *for*."

"And you want it first."

"I want it *nobody's*. There's a difference." Her eyes held mine, steady, unblinking, and I remembered hating that steadiness once, in a courtroom, and loving it before that, on a rooftop in the old quarter with the whole city lit below us. "But to make sure nobody gets it—to unmake it, if it comes to that—we need someone who can read what's left of the charts. Someone who can redraw what the regime destroyed. There is exactly one person alive who ever charted the Spire's living interior, and she sells worthless charms in the Saltmarket and looks terrible."

"That's flattering."

"I'm not here to flatter you. I'm here to offer you protection. Real protection. My people can move you tonight, out of Corvane, past the frozen river, somewhere Brannoc's surveillance sigil can't follow. Food, shelter, safety. In exchange—" she tapped the ledger "—you redraw the Spire for us. The routes in. The seals. Everything Orenne taught you."

"And then what? Your faction marches in and takes power over the thing that's freezing the world? That's not *nobody's*, Sefa. That's just changing whose hands are on the wheel."

"Better our hands than theirs."

"Is it?" I heard my own voice go sharp and hated it and couldn't stop. "Twelve years ago everyone's hands seemed better than someone else's. Everyone had excellent reasons. And Vell's Crossing happened."

The words landed. I saw them land. For one raw instant her composure cracked—not into anger, worse, into something older and more tired—and then it sealed itself shut like a wound clotting.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Vell's Crossing happened. Two hundred and six people. My mother among them. Because a frightened nineteen-year-old with beautiful handwriting handed the regime a key." She stood. "I'm not asking you to forget that. I'm asking you to decide whether the next Vell's Crossing happens because you hid again, or because you finally did something."

"I refused," I said. "That's my answer. No."

She nodded slowly, and I braced for fury, and it never came. She just looked at me for a long moment—with something that might, horribly, have been pity—and said, "You know what the difference between us is, Ada? I thought you'd say no. But I thought you'd hate saying it. I see now you're practiced."

She left the ledger on my counter. She took nothing else, not even the hearth-charm I found myself holding out to her, uselessly, stupidly, halfway to offering her the only thing I had.

The bell above my door rang her out into the dark.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told myself it was the cold.

Instead I sat by my one dying candle and looked at the ledger, and at my forearm where the burned map pulsed faintly under the skin, and I did the arithmetic of a coward, which is the most exhausting arithmetic there is. If I stayed hidden: the Wardens already suspected me, Brannoc's sigil was already on my doorframe humming like a trapped fly, and it was only a matter of time before suspicion became certainty became a cell became whatever they did to the last living cartographer of the Silent Spire. If I ran with Sefa: I traded one set of masters for another, and the rebels' hands were not as clean as her eyes pretended. If I did nothing at all: well. The winter was doing that for everyone.

Every road led somewhere bad. The only question was which bad thing I could live with, and I discovered, sitting there in the small hours, that I no longer knew. Twelve years ago I'd known—or thought I'd known—that hiding was survival. Now survival felt less like a plan and more like a habit I couldn't break.

Near dawn I gave up on sleep and opened the shutter early, because the queue would form regardless and Widow Hesk would be first, and there was a strange comfort in the routine of failing people on schedule.

That was when I saw the child.

She was fourth in line, wedged against a woman I took for her grandmother, and she was wrong in a way that took my mind a horrible second to parse. Too still. Frost on her eyelashes that wasn't melting. The grandmother was rubbing the girl's hands and talking to her—low, fast, coaxing—and the girl wasn't answering, and the frost was climbing her scarf in delicate fern-patterns like the frost on my ink shelf, like the frost everywhere, like the frost that had crawled out of the Spire district and swallowed the farms and was now, patiently, methodically, swallowing children in a bread-and-charms queue in the Saltmarket.

I was around the counter before I'd decided to move.

"Inside. Both of you. Now." My voice came out in the Warden-Captain register I'd learned from Brannoc, all command and no negotiation, and the crowd parted the way crowds do when someone sounds like authority. I got the girl onto my workbench, swept the ink-stained maps aside—my precious, secret, worthless maps—and pressed my palms flat against her small sternum.

Nothing. Of course nothing. My charms were worthless. My craft was worthless. I was worthless, a fraud with a famous name buried under a fake one, selling sugar pills to a dying city—

And then the map on my arm woke up.

I felt it happen. The burned lines lifted off my skin like heat-shimmer and poured down through my palms into the child, and for one blinding second I *saw* her—not flesh and bone but geography, a living terrain, her warmth pooled in low valleys and drained from the high ground, the cold sitting in her chest like a lake of white. And the map knew what to do with a landscape. A map has one sacred law, the first law Orenne ever taught me: *water flows downhill, warmth flows toward need, and the cartographer's job is only to draw the channel.*

So I drew it. With my hands. Without meaning to. Warmth moved.

The girl gasped—actually gasped, air rushing into her like a border reopening—and color came back into her face in a tide, and the frost on her eyelashes melted and ran down her cheeks like she was crying, though she wasn't, she was blinking up at me with enormous brown eyes and saying, "You're warm. Why are you warm? Nobody's warm."

The grandmother was staring at my bare forearms, at the fading glow of lines sinking back beneath the skin, and I saw her understand exactly what she was seeing, and I waited for the scream, the accusation, the *Wardens!*

Instead she took the girl's hand and bowed to me. Actually bowed. Low and slow, in the old style, the way you bow to a well that hasn't run in years and has suddenly given water.

The rest of the queue was staring too. Forty faces. Word travels through a frozen city faster than fire.

I stood in my ruined shop with a child's warmth still tingling in my palms and understood, with perfect clarity, that the choice Sefa had offered me was no longer on the table. I could refuse to serve the rebels. I could refuse to serve the Wardens. What I could not do—what I had proven, in front of forty witnesses and one small brown-eyed girl, that I could no longer do—was stand behind this counter and be worthless while people froze.

Self-preservation was a fine strategy. It had worked beautifully for twelve years.

But the water flows downhill, and warmth flows toward need, and apparently so did I.

I closed the shop. I wrote a note for Widow Hesk—*back tomorrow, tell no one where I've gone,* knowing she'd tell everyone—and I took the ledger, and my coat, and the glove I no longer saw any point in wearing, and I went looking for a fishmonger's apron that walked like a soldier.

It found me first. Of course it did. Sefa was leaning against the wall across the lane, arms crossed, snow gathering on her shoulders like she'd been there for hours, watching through my window the whole time.

"Well?" she said.

"Don't gloat," I told her, pulling the door shut behind me. "Just take me to your maps."
