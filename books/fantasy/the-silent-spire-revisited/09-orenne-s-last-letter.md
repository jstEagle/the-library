## 9. Orenne's Last Letter

The parcel arrived on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Catastrophes, Ada had always believed, should come on significant days—the solstice, the anniversary of the Sealing, at minimum a Sunday, when the city's grief had time to settle in properly. Instead it came on an ordinary gray Tuesday, three days after the stars went out and one day after Warden-Captain Brannoc had sat in their kitchen drinking their tea and believing—Ada hoped, prayed, sweated—that none of his suspicions were correct.

The parcel was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the kind of knot cartographers used: a double hitch with a release-loop, pullable with one hand, teachable in ten seconds, forgotten by everyone who wasn't trained to notice it. Ada noticed it from across the shop, before they even saw the handwriting.

They knew the handwriting.

*Orenne*, said the label on the outside, in a clerk's indifferent scrawl—*Effects of deceased. Unclaimed. Return to sender or dispose.* But underneath the label, half-hidden where the paper had been pasted over it, was a single line in that familiar cramped left-slant: **For K. Only K. Burn this if anyone watches you open it.**

K. For Kavanagh. Orenne had never once called them Ada. *K*, always, as if names were coordinates and coordinates were dangerous things to say aloud.

Ada stood very still in the middle of their shop, among the shelves of worthless protection charms and the rack of love-knots and the drawer of honest compasses—which had stopped being honest the night the stars went out, all twelve of them swinging slowly counterclockwise like drunkards trying to remember the way home.

The bell above the door jangled. A woman came in wanting a frost-ward for her cellar, and Ada sold her one, and lied about its strength with the same easy fluency they'd hated themselves for using on Brannoc, and took her coin, and hated that too. The queue outside stretched down the street now. It had stretched down the street every morning since the cold began. People queued for charms the way people in stories queued for miracles, and Ada handed out trinkets and watched their breath fog in a shop that had never, in twelve years, needed a hearth until last week.

When the shop emptied, Ada turned the sign to CLOSED, drew the ward-curtain—not that wards meant anything anymore, but habits were load-bearing—and carried the parcel to the back room.

They sat with it for a long moment before touching it.

Orenne was dead. They had known that for six years; the news had come in a formal letter from the Cartographers' Guild, four lines long, expressing condolences and requesting return of all guild property, of which Ada, having fled the guild at nineteen, technically possessed nothing except everything that mattered. Master Orenne Vell, First Surveyor of the Inner Reaches, keeper of the Spire charts, dead of what the letter called "prolonged decline," which was guild-speak for anything from heart failure to being pushed down a staircase. Ada had grieved the way they did most things: privately, thoroughly, and in denial about how much it cost.

Now here was Orenne, arriving six years late, in a box the size of two fists.

"You absolute bastard," Ada said quietly, affectionately, to a dead man. "You waited."

They pulled the release-loop. The knot sighed apart the way it always had, as if glad to be undone.

Inside the oilcloth: a tin of surveyor's chalk, worn to a stub. A brass scale-rule, notched where young Ada had dropped it off the Vell's Crossing bridge and been made to climb down and fetch it while Orenne watched from above saying, "Respect your instruments, K, they are the only witnesses we have." A folded square of tracing-cloth covered in Orenne's hand—a partial map, abandoned mid-line, of somewhere Ada didn't recognize. And beneath all of it, flat and heavy and smelling faintly of cedar and old smoke, a ledger bound in gray leather with no title at all.

Ada opened the ledger and found, pressed into the front cover, a letter.

It was not sealed. Of course it wasn't; Orenne had never believed in seals. "A seal tells the reader exactly where to look, K. Secrecy is not hiding a thing. Secrecy is making sure nobody thinks there is anything to find." The letter was written in that same cramped slant, dense, unbroken, no paragraph breaks because Orenne had considered white space a moral failing. It was dated eleven years ago. One year after Ada had run. One year after Vell's Crossing.

Ada read it once standing up. Then they sat down on the floor, because the floor seemed wiser, and read it again.

*K.*

*If you are reading this, then I am dead, and someone has disobeyed my instructions regarding disposal, and I find I am pleased about both. You will want to know why I sent this now rather than when I was alive. The answer is cowardice, which you already suspected of me, and timing, which you did not.*

*You believe you know what the Silent Spire is. You mapped it. I taught you to map it. I let you believe the story we tell the apprentices: that the Spire was raised by the first great mappers as a monument, that it grew unstable, that the Sealing was an act of desperate preservation—a tomb built around our own greatest work so that its power would not leak into the world unshaped. You copied those charts yourself. Your lines are in the official record. That is precisely why I chose you, and precisely why I have ruined you, though you will not understand the second part for some years yet, if ever.*

*Here is what your beautiful maps do not show, because I made certain they could not.*

*Nothing was leaking out of the Spire, K. Something was being kept in.*

*The Sealing was not preservation. It was imprisonment. The tower is not a monument. It is a cell. Whatever the founders found at the root of the world—the thing your guild histories call the First Chart, the Source, the Well of Measures—it did not agree to be studied. It agreed to nothing. It was taken. Bound. Locked beneath a hundred floors of deliberately misremembered architecture, and every map drawn since has been a lie layered over a lock. We do not chart the Spire. We maintain its disguise. That is what the Guild is, though the present members have mostly forgotten it. We are not cartographers of the Spire. We are its jailers' descendants, drawing bars in ink and calling them corridors.*

*I helped draw those bars. So did you, child, though you didn't know it. Your testimony before the Commission—you remember, I hope, the day you swore the inner chambers matched the historical record? You were telling the truth as you understood it. Your understanding was manufactured. I manufactured it. I have regretted it every day since, and I am not asking forgiveness. Masters do not get to ask forgiveness. We get to leave instructions.*

*You will ask: kept in for what purpose? I can only give you the shape of the answer, because the shape is all any of us were permitted. The thing below does not destroy. Destruction would be simple, and we know how to fight simple. The thing below gives. It gave the first mappers the ability to write a coastline and have the coast obey. It is the reason realms could be shaped at all, the wellspring every working draws from whether the worker knows it or not. And it was decided—by men whose names are on buildings you've walked past without looking—that such a source must belong to someone. Must be held. Guarded. Owned. They could not share it. Sharing was never considered. Consider this carefully, K: the oldest war in our history was fought not over whether the power should exist, but over who alone should hold the key.*

*Twelve years ago the Commission asked me to verify that the binding still held. I verified something else instead. I found that the binding was not failing. It was being fed. Slowly. Deliberately. Someone inside the Guild wanted the cell ready to open, and had been preparing it for longer than my career. I reported nothing. Reporting was how the last surveyor who found this died, though officially she retired to the coast. I began instead to erase—to scrub the true geometry out of every record I could reach, starting with the ones bearing your name, because yours were the finest, and the finest maps make the best weapons.*

*Then Vell's Crossing happened, and I need to say this plainly because you deserve it plainly: the seizure of your field-maps was not an accident of war. They were taken on specific instruction, by specific officers, because your survey contained—in a margin note you probably don't even remember writing—an observation that should have been impossible. You noted that the Spire's shadow fell wrong. That at noon, measured against the sun, the tower's shadow pointed the opposite direction from its height. You called it a drafting error. It is not an error. The Spire does not cast its own shadow, K. It casts the shadow of whatever holds it. Someone read your note and understood that a cartographer existed who could see through a century of lies without knowing she was looking at them. They took your maps to find what you'd seen. The village was simply in the way.*

*I cannot prove this. Proof is another thing they taught us to bury. But I was at Vell's Crossing three days after, and I walked the burn line, and burn lines do not curve like that unless the fire was told to.*

*So. Instructions, since instructions are all I'm owed.*

*First: stay hidden. Not for your sake—for theirs. If they find you, they will not kill you. Killing a cartographer who can see the truth is wasteful. They will use you. There are worse fates than death; I have spent my life cataloguing them.*

*Second: the ledger enclosed contains the true geometry, the real floors, the actual stairs. It is incomplete—I burned what I could not carry—but it is more complete than anything else in existence. Learn it only if you must. Knowing it changes what you are. I say this as fact, not poetry.*

*Third, and I write this with a steady hand and no sentiment: if the wards of Corvane ever fall—if the city's protections fail on the same night the sky goes dark—do not wait to be sought. Run to the Spire before anyone can bring you an army, theirs or anyone else's. Because the binding was designed to be opened by a living map, K, and you are the only living map I ever made.*

*I am sorry. I have been your teacher for too long to pretend that covers it, and too proud to say what would.*

*Burn nothing. O.*

* * *

Ada did not remember getting up. They became aware, some minutes later, of standing at the window of the back room with the letter crushed slightly in one fist and the other hand braced against the glass, watching frost fern its way up the pane from the outside in—up, against gravity, climbing like something with intent.

The vertigo the letter promised arrived late and all at once.

Every map Ada had ever loved rearranged itself behind their eyes. The apprentice charts, the proud clean linework, the hours bent over the drafting table while Orenne circled behind muttering *again, K, again, your north is drifting*—all of it reassembling now into something else. Not a record. A cage diagram. They had spent their girlhood drawing prison bars and calling it a monument, and the man teaching them had held the chalk and known.

And Vell's Crossing.

Sefa's face floated up, unbidden—the way it had looked in the market three days ago, older, harder, lit with twelve years of a hatred Ada had assumed was grief misfiled. *Your maps. They came for your maps, and then they came for us.* Ada had carried that accusation like a stone in the chest, had answered it a hundred times in the dark with the only defense available: *I didn't know. I couldn't have known. I was nineteen and I ran.*

The letter did not absolve them. That was the terrible elegance of it. Orenne confirmed everything Sefa believed and made it worse: the maps hadn't merely been seized, they'd been *read*, and what was read out of them had painted a target on a border village. Ada's margin note. Ada's little observation, scribbled between surveys, *shadow falls wrong, check at dusk, likely my error.* Likely my error. Twelve years of guilt, and it turned out the guilt had been pointing the right direction all along, just at the wrong crime. Not carelessness. Visibility. They had seen truly without knowing it, and seeing truly had been the most dangerous thing they'd ever done.

Ada laughed. It came out wrong, a dry cracked sound with nothing under it, and they put a hand over their mouth as if the room might report them.

"Shadow falls wrong," they said aloud, testing it. On the desk, the twelve dishonest compasses swung in their slow counterclockwise circle, and Ada looked at them properly for the first time since the stars went out. North was drifting. Orenne used to say it a hundred times a session, a joke, a scold. *Your north is drifting, K.*

Except north didn't drift. North was the one fixed thing, the anchor of the whole discipline. If north drifted, it meant the reference point itself was moving—or lying. Or being held somewhere underground by people who wanted it still.

The frost on the window had reached the top of the pane. Beyond the glass, Corvane lay under its second morning of unnatural winter, chimneys smoking desperately, queues forming at bakeries and charm-stalls alike. Somewhere in that frozen sprawl, the Wardens were conscripting charm-makers and asking quiet questions about quiet people. Somewhere else—Ada's stomach tightened—somewhere else, a rebel commander who kissed like a promise and fought like a verdict was waiting for an answer Ada hadn't given yet.

*Run to the Spire before anyone can bring you an army.*

Ada looked down at the ledger in the gray leather cover. They hadn't opened it past the letter. Some part of them, some deep cartographic instinct, understood that once they read the true geometry, they could never unknow it—would walk through the world thereafter seeing the false walls over the real ones, the way you could never again see the duck once you'd seen the rabbit. Orenne had warned them: *knowing it changes what you are.*

But the wards had fallen. The stars were gone. The frost climbed windows against gravity, and children froze in bread lines, and somewhere below a tower that cast the wrong shadow, something was being fed.

"Knowing it changes what you are," Ada repeated softly. "Master, everything's already changed what I am. You're late."

They untied their sleeve—they had taken to wearing long sleeves even indoors now, since the night the map burned itself into their forearm—and looked at the dark lines there, the floor plan no one alive should have been able to draw, curling from wrist to elbow in Orenne's own beloved style. Their skin had been keeping secrets from them. Their teacher had been keeping secrets from them. The entire history of their profession had been a cover story with their name signed neatly at the bottom.

Ada set the ledger on the desk, laid both hands flat upon it, and opened it to the first page.

The true geometry looked back up at them, and it was beautiful, and it was terrible, and it was—Ada realized with a lurch that started in the burned map on their arm and ended somewhere below their ribs—familiar. Not learned-familiar. *Remembered*-familiar. As if they had walked these halls before ever holding a pencil.

On the third floor of the true Spire, according to the ledger, there was a door that appeared on no surviving chart, guarded by nothing except the condition that it open only for blood that remembered.

The knock at the shop's front door came then—three slow strikes, patient, deliberate, nothing like a customer.

Ada closed the ledger. Through the gap in the ward-curtain they could see the street, and the street was empty of everyone except a figure in a soldier's coat, hood down despite the cold, red hair going silver at the temples, standing in the falling snow as if she had all the time in the world and intended to spend none of it waiting.

Sefa Ilan raised one hand—not a wave, a summons—and mouthed two words through the glass:

*"Time's up."*
