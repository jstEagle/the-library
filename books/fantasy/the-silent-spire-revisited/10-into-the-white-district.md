## 10. Into the White District

The White District had been called that before the frost came, Sefa remembered, because of the chalk-pale stone the old merchants had quarried for their warehouses. Now it was white for a simpler reason. Everything was buried in ice a hand-span deep, and the ice did not melt.

She led Ada through what had once been the Tallowmarket, stepping where she stepped and nowhere else, because the frozen cobbles lied about their depth. Beneath the glassy crust there were gaps where drains had collapsed, and twice already Ada had nearly gone through to the hip. The second time, Sefa had caught their arm without thinking, and had spent the next fifty paces regretting it, because her hand still remembered the exact shape of them and her body had been traitorously glad to hold on.

"Left," she said, when they reached the mouth of Cooper's Row.

"There *is* no left," Ada said. "There's a wall of snow."

"There's an alley under the wall of snow. I scouted it yesterday." She said it flatly, the way you reported terrain. "You go first. If the drift gives way, I'll pull you out, and then we'll both pretend it didn't happen."

Ada looked at her sidelong — Sefa felt the look even with her back turned — but climbed into the drift. It held. Mostly. A small avalanche poured down inside their collar, and they emerged on the far side looking like a disgraced snowman, shaking powder from their hair with as much dignity as a person could salvage.

"You've gotten bossier," Ada said.

"You've gotten slower. Twelve years behind a market stall."

"Twelve years of not being shot at. It softens a person."

"It should have softened your lying," Sefa said, before she could stop herself.

Ada went quiet. The wind moved down the row, carrying fine ice-dust that hissed against the warehouse walls like something whispering.

Sefa hadn't meant to start the argument yet. There was a version of this journey — she'd rehearsed it, actually, walking the route alone yesterday — where she stayed professional and distant until they reached the Spire, and only then let the twelve years come pouring out, in some defensible order, at some tactically sound moment. But Ada had a talent for dismantling her plans just by existing near her. They'd always had it. That was half of why she'd loved them, and half of why she'd stopped trusting them, because a person who could rearrange your intentions without trying could certainly rearrange other things too.

They walked. Somewhere behind them, faint as a struck bell, a Warden patrol horn sounded two streets east. Sefa raised a fist; Ada froze mid-step, absurdly obedient, one boot hovering over the ice like a heron. The horn faded toward the river. She counted to sixty out of habit, then waved them onward.

* * *

The chapel had been Our Lady of the Ledgered Flame, back when Corvane still bothered with gods of bookkeeping. Its roof had come down years ago in some forgotten collapse, and now the frost had sealed the broken vault with a ceiling of pale blue ice, so that light fell through it filtered and strange, like being underwater in a place that had never known water. Broken pews made a maze. In the corner where the altar had been, someone — rebels, probably, or just refugees before the cold finished — had left a brazier ring of blackened stones.

Sefa got a fire going with practiced economy: tinder, oil-cloth, the striker from her belt pouch. Small heat, contained. Heat drew Wardens' thermal charms if you were careless, but a fire this size read as nothing more than squatters, and squatters were everywhere now.

Ada sat down across from her with the careful movements of someone whose whole body ached, which after six hours in the White District, theirs certainly did. Their forearm — the map-scarred one — they held close to the flame, and Sefa watched them watch it, the way you'd watch a wound you weren't sure was yours anymore.

"Eat," Sefa said, and tossed them a wax-wrapped bundle of hardtack and dried apple. "You map better fed."

"I don't map at all anymore. Officially."

"Unofficially, then."

Ada unwrapped the food. For a while the only sounds were chewing and the tick of the fire and, somewhere high above, the ice ceiling settling with a groan like an old man changing position.

Then Ada said, quietly, "You're going to ask me. You've been circling it since the border."

"I'm not going to ask you anything," Sefa said. "Asking implies I don't know the answer."

"That's exactly the kind of sentence that makes people want to hit you."

"I've been told."

Ada set down the food. Sefa watched their hands, which had always been eloquent — cartographer's hands, restless, always wanting to draw the thing they couldn't say — and now those hands pressed flat against their knees, deliberately still, the way a person holds themselves when they've decided to stand in the open instead of running.

"The testimony," Ada said. "At the tribunal. After the border seizures. You think I gave them the names."

"I know you did."

"How?"

"Because my mother was taken three days after your testimony was filed, and she wasn't taken by accident. Someone told them where Vell's Crossing kept its people. Someone who'd been inside our camps, who'd eaten at our fires, who'd—" Her voice snagged. She pushed past it. "Someone who'd slept beside me. And of everyone who could have done it, everyone who had the access, you were the only one who walked away clean. Untouched. Free to open a little charm stall in Corvane and live quietly for twelve years."

The fire popped. Ada didn't flinch from any of it. That was new, Sefa thought. The Ada she remembered would have argued by now, hands flying, building the case breath by breath. This one sat very still, taking the accusation like weather.

"I didn't give them anything," Ada said finally. "I need you to hear the whole thing, because I've only ever heard pieces of it myself, secondhand, and maybe we've both been living off rumors."

"Then tell me the truth."

"I'm going to. But first — what happened at Vell's Crossing? Not what the pamphlets say. What you saw."

It was such an unexpected turn that it took the wind out of her prepared anger entirely. She'd built a fortress of grievance over twelve years, and here they were, asking her to describe the siege works.

"They came at dusk," she heard herself say. "Two companies in Warden grey, but not city Wardens — regime men, the ones with the black cuffs. My father had moved the families into the grain cellars because we thought they'd want the stores, that they'd take the harvest and go. They didn't want the stores." She looked at the fire. "They wanted the maps. Your maps. The ones showing the realm-roads, the ward-lines, every crossing point between Corvane and the outer realms. Because with those, they could close the borders wherever they chose, and starve whatever enclaves they liked."

"And?"

"And when my father refused to hand them over — he'd hidden them, he'd split them among three couriers — they burned the granaries anyway. With the cellars underneath. Forty-one people. Nine of them children." Her voice came out level, which cost her more than breaking would have. "I was sixteen. I was outside, running supply, which is the only reason I'm talking to you now. I came back and the snow over the cellar doors was black."

Ada had gone the color of the ice overhead.

"I testified about the seizure," they said slowly. "That part's true. Orenne ordered me to — said the tribunal needed documentation of what the regime was doing with confiscated cartography, said a licensed apprentice's testimony carried weight. I stood up in that marble room and described the maps they took from me, the survey work, the routes. I named the crossings because they asked me to confirm what had been seized *where*, and Vell's Crossing was one, because I'd surveyed it, because I'd—"

They stopped. Pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes.

"I didn't know there were people hiding below. I didn't know the granaries mattered. I gave coordinates, Sefa. I gave coordinates because that's what I do, that's what I was *for*, and I never once thought about what lived at the coordinates." They lowered the hand and looked at her, and their eyes were wet and furious, mostly at themselves. "And then I ran. That's the part you have right. I testified, and the regime fell apart around us, and I understood exactly what my words had been used for — I learned it within the year, I promise you, I learned it and it ate me alive — and instead of standing up and saying *I did that, my testimony did that*, I bought a stall license and disappeared. So yes. I betrayed you. Maybe not the way you think I did. But I betrayed you, because I knew what I'd cost you and I hid anyway, because hiding was easier than facing you."

The chapel was silent except for the fire.

Sefa found that her prepared arguments — all of them, the whole arsenal — lay in ruins, and what was left underneath was just a tired woman sitting near a boy she'd loved who had grown into someone who could say *I hid because it was easier* and mean it. She hadn't planned for honesty. Honesty was terrible tactics.

"My mother's name was Ilan Vess," she said, because Ada deserved to know it had a face. "You met her. She gave you that terrible honey-brandy you pretended to like."

"I liked it," Ada whispered. "It was awful. I liked it."

"She'd have made you drink it again tonight if she could see us, sitting here, having feelings near a fire." And to Sefa's own horror, her voice cracked on the last word — not grief exactly, something older and more ridiculous than grief.

Ada laughed. It was a broken, wet laugh, but it was a laugh, and it broke something loose in her chest that she'd have sworn on any map was calcified permanently.

* * *

Later — how much later she couldn't say — they had migrated. It hadn't been decided; it had simply happened, the way heat happens, the way two bodies in a freezing room will drift together without anyone signing anything. Ada sat against her side under the single blanket from her pack, and Sefa's arm was around them, and neither of them acknowledged it, which was its own kind of acknowledgment.

"Your feet are blocks of ice," Sefa said.

"Your ribs are all wrong. Have you been eating?"

"I eat."

"You eat like a soldier. Standing up, fast, guilty."

"I am a soldier."

"You're a woman who used to hum while sharpening things, which is a different profession entirely."

Sefa snorted despite herself. Above them the ice ceiling had begun to glow faintly gold where the afternoon light struck it, and the whole ruined chapel filled with that underwater amber, and it was so beautiful that it made her angry, because beauty kept happening in the middle of everything terrible, indifferent to the dead and the frozen and the two people under a blanket who had no business leaning on each other.

"If this is a trick," she said quietly, "if you're leading us somewhere that serves you and not the city, I will put you down myself. I want you to know that. The old feeling doesn't buy you a thing."

"I'd expect nothing less." Ada's head tipped back against her shoulder. "But Sefa — the Spire. Whatever the rebels told you is down there, whatever they promised you the unsealing would do — I've started reading the map on my arm. Really reading it, the way Orenne taught me. And there are rooms in that floor plan that aren't rooms. There are lines that don't lead anywhere a line should go. Whatever's sealed in the Spire, it isn't treasure, and it isn't a weapon you can aim. I think it might be the other way around. I think it might be aiming at *us*, and has been, for a hundred years, waiting for someone to open the door."

"You could be lying."

"I could."

"You could also be right, and then I've marched forty good people to their deaths."

"You could ask Brannoc," Ada said. "He knows more than he says. He looked at my arm in that interrogation like a man reading scripture he'd been taught was heresy. The Wardens aren't hunting the Spire, Sefa. They're *guarding* it. Somebody corrupted the charge along the way, but the charge is real, and it's older than the regime."

She was silent a long time. The fire burned low. Against her side, Ada had gone heavy with exhaustion, trusting her enough to sleep — which was either the most honest act possible or the most calculated, and she genuinely could not tell, and was beginning to understand that she might never be able to tell, and that she would walk with them anyway.

That was the treacherous part. Not the doubt. The *anyway*.

She was still turning it over when she saw the light change — not the warm gold of the ceiling, but a cold blue flicker, strobing faintly through the chapel's broken doorway, out in the street. Wardens' sigil-light. More than one patrol. Moving not past the district, but *into* it, methodically, block by block, the way you search when you know exactly who you're looking for.

Sefa closed her hand over Ada's shoulder and shook them awake.

"Don't make a sound," she breathed. "We have company — and they're sweeping the White District house by house. Someone told them where we're going."

Ada's eyes opened, and in the blue flicker Sefa saw them go to their own scarred forearm — saw them realize, as she had, the obvious and terrible arithmetic.

Only three people knew this route.

And one of them wasn't in the chapel.
