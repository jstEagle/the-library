## 13. Brannoc's Truth

The rope around Warden-Captain Brannoc's wrists was, he had to admit, competently tied.

He had spent thirty years judging people by the small things—the way a sentry checked a knot, the angle of a picket's chin, whether the cook salted the soup before tasting it. Whoever had trussed him in the back of this swaying supply wagon had checked their knots twice. He approved. It made captivity easier to bear when your captors were professionals.

"You're staring at my hands," said the young rebel across from him. She had a crossbow across her knees and a face like she'd been told scowling was a duty.

"I'm assessing them," Brannoc said. "Your left thumb is frostbitten. You should have seen the medic at Vell's Crossing instead of wrapping it in a sock."

"It *was* a clean sock."

"Cleanliness is not the same as treatment." He flexed his own bound fingers, testing circulation. His hands were the one part of him he'd never allowed to degrade—thirty years of paperwork and arrests and standing in the cold, and still they could sign a warrant or draw a sigil with the precision of a jeweler. "You'll lose the nail if you're careless."

She looked at him the way soldiers look at generals who have just complimented their boots: suspicious, and slightly betrayed that suspicion didn't feel warranted.

The wagon jolted over a frozen rut. Somewhere ahead, muffled by canvas, someone was arguing about firewood rations in the particular exhausted cadence of an army that had been losing a war against winter rather than against men. Brannoc listened with professional interest. The rebels were starving too. That was worth knowing.

They had taken him at the White District's edge, which still embarrassed him more than the capture itself. Four of his Wardens dead—not by rebels but by the cold itself, frost climbing into their lungs between one breath and the next—and Brannoc had stopped to pull a child out of a collapsed tenement, and when he turned around there were six crossbows and a voice saying, very politely, "*Captain. We'd be honored if you'd come quietly.*"

He had considered his options for approximately two seconds. Then the child sneezed on him, and he decided that dying heroically while holding a snotty five-year-old lacked dignity, and came quietly.

Now they were taking him somewhere. The wagon smelled of tallow and old wool. His shoulder ached where a rebel had shoved him, unnecessarily—he had been cooperating—but he supposed shoving came with the uniform. It had come with his.

* * *

They put him in a chapel.

Or what had been a chapel. Half the roof had come down under snow-weight, and the remaining vault was furred white with frost that grew in patterns no weather made—spirals, mostly, spiraling inward toward nothing, like the building itself was listening for something that had stopped speaking. Brannoc knew those spirals. Every Warden did, though none of them were permitted to say so.

They sat him near the one intact brazier, which he noted was positioned so its heat reached him without reaching the door. Considerate. Or calculated. With Sefa Ilan it would be both.

She arrived twenty minutes later, shaking snow off her cloak, carrying two bowls of soup. She set one down in front of him, untied his hands with a knife that moved close enough to his throat to make a point, and then sat opposite with her own bowl, eating with the mechanical efficiency of someone who had forgotten food was meant to taste of anything.

Brannoc picked up the spoon. Barley, salt, a ghost of dried onion. "You've salted it correctly," he said.

"Don't make me regret feeding you."

"I'm making an observation. In thirty years I've arrested four hundred and eleven people, Ilan. Do you know how many offered me soup?"

"How many?"

"Two. Both were guilty of worse things than you can imagine, and both were trying to soften me up." He took another mouthful. It was genuinely decent soup, which annoyed him, because he wanted to dislike everything about today. "This isn't softening-up. This is you being short-staffed and unable to waste a prisoner who might talk."

Sefa's eyes narrowed. "And are you going to talk?"

"That depends entirely," Brannoc said, "on whether anyone here asks me a question worth answering."

* * *

It went on like that for a while. She asked about Warden deployment in the White District; he told her, accurately, because withholding it would only get her people killed walking into frost-pockets they couldn't see. She asked about the conscription lists; he told her the truth, which was that half his own Wardens had deserted rather than drag charm-sellers from their stalls, and that he had filed the desertions as *lost in weather*, because some lies were load-bearing.

"You're not what I expected," Sefa said finally.

"What did you expect?"

"A man. A wall. Thirty years of the regime wearing a captain's badge." She set down her bowl. "I expected someone who'd tell me he was just following orders."

Brannoc was quiet for a moment. Outside, wind worried at the broken roof, and the frost spirals on the walls seemed to tighten, faintly, like a fist remembering how to close.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "I stood in front of the Spire."

Sefa went very still.

"Not at the threshold. No one gets to the threshold. At the base of the hill, at the old boundary stone—the one the regime replaced with a checkpoint in '61, but before that, before all of us, there was a stone, and carved into it—" He paused. Saying it aloud felt like breaking something. He had kept this whole and unspoken for three decades; it had grown roots through him like a tree through a fence post. "Carved into it were words in Old Corvane. *We do not guard against the Spire. We keep watch until the cartographer comes.*"

"The Wardens' founding charge," Sefa said slowly. "That's in our archives too. Everyone thought it was propaganda. Guard-dog language dressed up as scripture."

"Yes. So did I, at nineteen, standing in the snow, thinking myself very clever for noticing the discrepancy between the stone and the oath we swore the next morning." He rubbed his freed wrists. "Our oath says we suppress unlicensed magic. Contain anomalies. Protect Corvane from the Spire's influence. The stone says we are *waiting for someone*. Do you see the difference? One makes us jailers. The other makes us—" He searched for the word and hated the only one that fit. "Servants."

"Servants waiting for a master."

"Waiting for a *cartographer*. Which is worse, from a certain angle, because a master you can depose. Someone you've sworn your whole order to await—you either betray them or you spend your life looking at every face you meet and wondering." He laughed, short and dry. "Thirty years I've been wondering. Every charm-seller with steady hands. Every child who drew maps in the dirt too accurately. I pulled files on forty-one suspected cartographers over my career. Forty-one people whose lives I disrupted because a stone older than my grandmother's grandmother told me to keep watch."

"And none of them were real."

"None of them were real." He looked at the frost spirals. "Until twelve days ago, when the wards fell and the stars went out, and somewhere in the Lowmarket a stall full of worthless protection charms lit up like a signal fire, and every Warden in Corvane felt it in their teeth." He met her eyes. "Ada Kavanagh."

Something flickered across Sefa's face—there and gone, quick as a card sharp's mistake. Brannoc catalogued it and said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. It was most of his job.

"You knew," Sefa said carefully. "Before we took you. The Wardens knew who Kavanagh was."

"We suspected. We *hoped*, if you want the ugly truth. Half the senior officers have spent their careers praying for the cartographer to appear so we could stop being what the regime made us." He leaned forward. "But here is what you don't understand yet, Ilan, and what Kavanagh doesn't understand, and what will kill all of us if nobody says it plainly: the founding charge has a second half. It's not written on the boundary stone. It's written nowhere. It's passed mouth to mouth from each Warden-Captain to their successor, on the night of appointment, in a sealed room, alone."

Sefa had stopped breathing. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"My predecessor spoke it to me in the crypt beneath the Citadel," Brannoc said. "'When the cartographer comes, the Wardens kneel. But first test them. For the Spire does not open to the worthy. It opens to the willing.' Do you understand the difference? Worthiness is a judgment. Willingness—" His jaw tightened. "Willingness is an appetite. The Spire will open its doors to anyone hungry enough. And what's inside it will give them exactly what they ask for, and it will be the end of the world, and the world will thank them while it dies."

* * *

The fire had burned low. Someone had brought more wood; Brannoc noticed that they'd also brought a blanket and draped it, without comment, over the bench beside him. He decided not to read anything sentimental into it. He was cold, and he took the blanket, and sentiment could mind its own business.

"So the regime corrupted the charge," Sefa said. Her voice had changed since the story began—flatter, faster, a soldier recalculating a battle she'd thought was already mapped. "Turned guardians into hunters."

"Hunters, jailers, tax collectors. My order became the regime's fist, and we told ourselves the stone was a relic and the oath was the truth." He shook his head. "But corruption doesn't erase a purpose, Ilan. It just buries it. And buried purposes grow strange. There are officers in the Citadel right now—I won't name them, you'd waste time arresting the wrong ones—who read the old texts and drew the wrong conclusion. They believe the Spire shouldn't wait for a cartographer at all. They believe the Wardens should claim it themselves. Command it. Weaponize it, if the weaponizing comes to that."

"They'd need someone to open the door."

"They'd need Ada Kavanagh." He let that sit. "And they know where Kavanagh sleeps. They've known since the night the stars went out. The only reason your people found Kavanagh first is that I delayed the retrieval order. Twice. I filed weather reports. I am a bureaucrat of long standing and I can lose a document like nobody living."

"Why?" Sefa asked, and it wasn't an interrogation anymore; it was almost gentle, which unsettled him far more than the crossbows had. "Why delay? If your order's true purpose is to serve the cartographer—"

"Because I watched what happened to the last person the regime decided to *use*." He thought of the archives, the seized maps, the redacted names. He thought of a woman named Orenne whose file he had once pulled and closed unread, out of a cowardice he'd never confessed to anyone. "A worthy cartographer handed to willing men becomes a key cut for a lock that eats worlds. If the Spire opens for Kavanagh, it must open because Kavanagh chose it—not because my masters held a knife to someone's family, and not because yours held a cause up to someone's guilt." He looked at her steadily. "Yes, Ilan. I know about you too. The soldier whose testimony helped doom her family. I read that file before you were old enough to hate me properly. The regime used grief as a crowbar then, and your faction is using it now, and I have spent thirty years watching crowbars, and I am telling you: the door remembers what opens it."

Silence filled the ruined chapel. In it, Brannoc heard something he hadn't heard in decades—the faintest hum from the direction of the Spire, carried on wind that had crossed six miles of frozen city. The frost spirals on the walls pulsed once, slow, like a sleeping thing turning over.

Sefa stood. "You could be lying about all of it. The stone. The charge. The second half."

"I could."

"But you're not."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "Lying requires wanting something. I'm past wanting. I'm seventy pounds lighter than I was in autumn, my city is freezing to death one district at a time, and the only thing I have left is the truth I was sworn to bury. Take it or leave it."

Sefa studied him for a long moment. Then she did something that made the young rebel with the crossbow audibly inhale: she knelt. Not deeply—one knee, briefly, the way soldiers kneel to a standard rather than a throne. When she rose, her face was wet-eyed and furious about it.

"The stone said the Wardens kneel," she said. "Someone should start keeping the old promises, even sideways."

"Get up," Brannoc said gruffly. "Kneeling's wasted on me. Save it for the person who matters." He nodded toward the window, toward the distant dark shape of the Spire against a starless sky. "There's a door up there that remembers everyone who touches it. And there's a cartographer somewhere in this city deciding right now whether to open it—for your cause, my masters' ambition, or something no one's planned for."

"Which do you think it'll be?" Sefa asked.

Brannoc pulled the blanket tighter around his shoulders and thought of forty-one disrupted lives, one delayed order, and a stone in the snow that had waited three hundred years to be believed.

"Neither," he said. "And that frightens me more than either one, because it means the choice is actually free—and free choices, Ilan, are the only ones the world can't survive getting wrong."

Above the broken roof, where the stars should have been, the darkness pulsed—once, softly, like something below the Spire beginning, after a very long sleep, to wake.
