## 15. What Sefa Saw in the Ice

The chapel had a hole in its roof shaped like a question mark, and through it the snow fell in a slow gray curtain that never quite reached the floor. It melted somewhere around the third pew—Ada's doing, though they'd deny it if asked, the way they denied everything warm about themselves.

Sefa sat on the altar steps because the pews were frozen solid enough to crack teeth, and she watched Ada sketch by candlelight. They'd been at it for an hour. Their breath made little ghosts above the page. Their burned forearm glowed faintly whenever the charcoal touched down, as if the map on their skin were checking the copy for errors.

"You're holding the charcoal wrong," Sefa said.

"I'm holding it exactly right."

"You hold it like a dagger."

"It *is* a dagger," Ada said. "It's currently threatening me into drawing a corridor I've only seen in my sleep." They paused. "Also, you've told me three times how to hold a charcoal stick, and I was mapping realms before you could grow a proper braid."

"My braids were magnificent."

"Your braids were a war crime against your own scalp."

Sefa laughed before she could stop herself—the sound came out strange in her own ears, too light for this place, this winter, this mission. That was the problem with Ada Kavanagh. Twelve years, one betrayal, a massacre, and a civil war brewing outside, and still they could make her laugh about *braids*.

She stopped laughing. She looked at her hands.

"Sefa?" Ada had gone still, the way they went still when they noticed something on a map that didn't belong. They'd always been able to read her like terrain. It had been the most wonderful thing about them, once. It was going to be the worst thing about them in about thirty seconds.

"I need to tell you why we really came," Sefa said.

*

Here is what Sefa Ilan had learned about confessions in twelve years of soldiering: do not rehearse them. Rehearsed confessions come out smooth, and smooth things sound like lies even when they aren't. So she simply opened her mouth and let the truth fall out, ugly and unarranged, the way a satchel drops when you stop gripping it.

"The Concord doesn't want the Spire unsealed," she said. "Not exactly. The Concord wants the Spire *opened*—by us, controlled, aimed. There's a faction on the council—Ilyra's people, maybe half the inner circle—who believe whatever's inside can be turned outward. A weapon. Point it at Corvane, break the Wardens' citadel in a single night, end the regime before the winter ends us."

Ada's charcoal had stopped moving entirely.

"They sent me to find the last cartographer of the Silent Spire," Sefa continued, because stopping now would be worse than any of it. "Not to *recruit* you. To retrieve you. You were never supposed to have a choice, Ada. You were the key, and keys don't get consulted about which doors they open."

The candle flame leaned sideways in some draft from the broken roof. Snow hissed faintly against stone somewhere above them. Ada set down the charcoal with exaggerated care, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it.

"When did you know?" they asked. Quiet. Terribly quiet. "That that was the mission."

"Before I crossed the border."

"And when did you decide not to follow it?"

Sefa opened her mouth. Closed it. Because here was the part she'd been circling for three days, through ice fields and Warden patrols and one deeply undignified crawl under a collapsed granary, and it wouldn't come out in soldier's order. It would only come out true.

"That first night," she said. "Your stall. You looked up at me and your face did—that thing it does. Like you were reading me and finding yourself in the margins." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I spent eleven days on the road planning how I'd handle you. Levers. Pressure points. Your guilt, mostly; there was so much of it, it was practically load-bearing. And then you handed a charm to a boy who couldn't pay and told him to tell everyone it cost two coppers so his mother wouldn't feel like charity—" Her voice cracked. She hated that. She pushed through. "And I sat there thinking, *I am going to betray this person again.* Again. As a career."

"*Again* is doing a lot of work in that sentence," Ada said, but without heat. Without much of anything. That frightened her more than shouting would have.

"Yes," Sefa said. "It is."

*

Twelve years ago, Sefa Ilan had been nineteen years old and stationed at Vell's Crossing, a border town so small it appeared on maps only as a smudge where the ink got tired. Her family lived there. Her mother kept bees. Her brother Tam was fourteen and could name every bird in the marshlands and had recently developed a catastrophic crush on a ferryman's daughter.

Then the old regime seized the cartographers' guild archives. Then they seized the cartographers. Then they found Ada Kavanagh's field maps—every route through every hidden realm, including the passes behind Vell's Crossing—and decided that a town full of smugglers' tunnels was a town full of rebels, whether or not it agreed.

Sefa had told Ada none of this in detail until now. In the rebel camp, in fragments, she'd given them the shape of it: *my family died after your maps were taken.* Tonight she gave them the whole weight of it, standing in a frozen chapel while the candle burned down, and she watched Ada take each piece like a blow they'd been bracing for since chapter one of their acquaintance and never actually prepared for.

"They marched the men of the crossing into the marsh," she said. "Tam among them. The official record says bandits. There were no bandits, Ada. There was a lieutenant with your map spread on his table, tracing the tunnel routes with a greasy finger, saying *here, and here, and seal these exits first.*"

"I didn't draw those maps for them," Ada whispered. "I drew them for travelers. For—so people wouldn't die in the white wastes the way Orenne's first apprentice died—"

"I know."

"You *know?*"

"I've known since the archive fire," Sefa said. "I recovered a fragment of your survey journal. Marginal notes. Little sketches of Tam's birds, Ada—you used to draw birds in the margins when you thought no one was looking, and there was a marsh wren on the Vell's Crossing page, and under it you wrote *good luck to whoever lives here.*" She let out a breath that fogged the air between them. "You blessed my home in ink and then your blessing got used to kill it. I know the difference between a knife and the hand that sharpened it. I've known for years. Knowing it and feeling it are different animals, and I fed the wrong one for a long time."

Ada had both hands pressed flat against their thighs now, hard, as if holding themselves down. The map on their forearm pulsed slow and unhappy.

"So the Concord," Ada said. "They want me to open the Spire as a weapon. And you volunteered to fetch me."

"I volunteered before I remembered how to be a person," Sefa said. "And then I un-volunteered somewhere between your stall and the frozen chapel, and I haven't told the council, and Ilyra will have my rank for it, possibly my head. But you were going to find out eventually. Either from me or from a council courier with fewer scruples, and I—" She stopped. Started over, plainer. "You trusted me once and I sold you to the regime to save my own skin. I'm not doing it twice. Even if it costs everything. *Especially* if it costs everything. That's not forgiveness, Ada. That's just arithmetic. Two betrayals cancel if you refuse the second one. Maybe. Ask a mathematician."

Silence. The snow filled the question-mark hole in soft gray strokes.

Ada stood. Walked to the altar. Stood next to her, not touching, close enough that Sefa could feel the small warmth their suppressed magic leaked constantly, like a banked stove. They stared at the far wall, where someone centuries dead had painted a saint whose face had faded into suggestion.

"Say what you're thinking," Sefa said. "You get that line between your eyebrows otherwise."

"What I'm thinking," Ada said slowly, "is that I hid for twelve years. I watched the city choke on fear and I sold charms I knew were worthless and I told myself survival was virtue. And you—" their voice wobbled, steadied, "you walked back into the city that destroyed your family, straight toward the person who enabled it, and your grand plan was apparently *confess immediately.* We are such idiots. We deserve each other. Is that—is that terrible? It sounds terrible."

"It sounds accurate."

"Because here's the thing." Ada finally turned to face her, and their eyes were wet and furious and alive in a way Sefa hadn't seen since before Vell's Crossing. "I betrayed you. Not the way history says—I didn't *give* them anything, they took it—but I ran. When they came for the guild, I ran, and you stayed, and staying is what got your family killed, and I have known that in my bones for twelve years and built my whole coward life on top of it. So if you're asking forgiveness—"

"I'm not asking yet."

"—then you should know it flows both directions or not at all," Ada finished. "That's how it works. I read it in a book about rivers. Rivers don't apologize one bank at a time."

"That's not what rivers do at all."

"I'm a cartographer, Sefa, not a hydrologist."

Despite everything—despite the confession sitting between them like a third person, despite the winter killing the world outside—Sefa felt her mouth twitch. "You're impossible."

"I'm a *last* cartographer. There's no one left to contradict me." Ada picked up the charcoal again. Turned it over in their fingers. "The Concord wants a weapon. Brannoc's Wardens want a guardian or a prisoner, depending on the hour. And you—" they looked at her directly, "what do *you* want? Not the commander. Not the survivor. You."

Sefa considered lying. Old habit. Then she considered that she had just emptied her entire soul onto a chapel floor and lying now would be like robbing a house she'd already donated to charity.

"I want the winter to end," she said. "I want Tam's birds to come back to the marshes. I want—" and here her voice did fail her, briefly, and she let it, "I want to sit across a breakfast table from you and argue about charcoal grip without an army on either side of us. I want the version of us that existed for one summer before everything. I want to stop being a woman who plans betrayals. I think I already am, but I'd like it confirmed in writing."

"I'll draft something," Ada said. "I'm told I'm good with documents."

*

They worked after that—not on the Spire corridor, but on something smaller. Ada drew the chapel. Just the chapel: the roof wound, the melted circle on the floor, the two of them rendered as simple marks, X and X, side by side on the altar steps. Sefa watched them do it and understood, with a lurch, that this was the answer. Not the First Map hoarded in one pair of hands, not the Concord's weapon, not the Wardens' guarded relic. This. Small maps. Shared ones.

"You're planning something," Sefa said. "You get that look. Like a cat deciding which shelf to knock things off of."

"I'm always planning something. It's my whole personality." Ada blew on the ink. "But yes. Possibly. If it works, everyone's going to be very angry with me, including probably you."

"Wonderful. My favorite genre."

"There is one thing I need first," Ada said, and hesitated in a new way—not guilty, not afraid. Almost shy. "When we go back in. Past the door that remembers. The Spire will offer things. It offered me *everything*, last time, and I nearly—" They shook their head. "If I start to take it, I need someone in the room who knows what I look like when I'm lying to myself. You've had practice. You're the foremost expert in the region."

Sefa reached over and took their hand—the scarred one, the mapped one. The lines of the Silent Spire's floor plan glowed faintly under her thumb, warm as a sleeping animal.

"Foremost expert," she agreed. "Twelve years of field research." She squeezed. "But Ada—if you fall, I'm coming in after you. That's not devotion. That's a threat."

"Noted," Ada said softly. "Filed under *levers, mine.*"

Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere north, past the white district, the Silent Spire waited with its door that knew blood, and beneath the Spire, the First Map dreamed its old dreams of ownership. And somewhere south, in a rebel camp, a council courier was already saddling a horse—because Ilyra's patience had run out, and the order had gone out at dusk, written in a hand Sefa would recognize anywhere:

*Retrieve the cartographer. By any means.*

Sefa didn't know that yet.

But the chapel door rattled against its latch, and hoofbeats sounded on the frozen road—too many, too disciplined for scavengers—and Ada lifted their head like a deer catching wind.

"That's not snowmelt shifting," they said.

"No," Sefa said, already reaching for her sword. "It isn't."
