## 20. A City of Small Mappers

The bell above the schoolhouse door had a crack in it, so it rang in two notes instead of one—a bright tone and a slightly mournful one underneath, like someone laughing while trying not to cry. Ada had decided this was appropriate and refused every offer to mend it.

"Master Kavanagh!" called Tam Wexler from the doorway, snow melting off his shoulders. He was a baker with forearms like bread loaves and, as of three weeks ago, the ability to make his ovens hold heat for a full day on a single log. "The widow Ansel's cat is stuck in my chimney again."

"Is that a magic problem or a cat problem?"

"It's an *Ansel's cat* problem," said Tam gravely, which was fair. The cat in question had developed, along with the rest of Corvane, a faint and unexplained gift—its own being the uncanny talent of appearing exactly where it would cause maximum inconvenience.

"Then it goes on the list after lessons." Ada pointed to the slate by the door, where the morning's requests were chalked in their own handwriting: *Mrs. Oduya — well water tastes of tin. The Ferro twins — can't stop their hair from floating when they argue. Someone (unsigned) — made my mother's ghost-shaped shadow stop following me, please and thank you.*

Three weeks since the night the Spire went dark and the stars came home, and the city had woken up to find itself quietly, absurdly enchanted.

Not grandly. That was the thing Ada kept having to explain to visitors, to Wardens, to themselves at four in the morning when they lay awake listening to the absence of winter. No one could throw fireballs. No one could walk through walls. But Jem Ferro could coax a rusted lock open just by humming at it, and his sister could make bread rise twice as fast if she argued with the dough first, and old Mrs. Oduya down on Tanner's Row had discovered that when she sang her lullabies the sour milk went sweet again—or possibly the children just drank it faster to make her stop. Ada genuinely did not know, and found this delightful.

Power democratized was power diminished. They had known that going in, standing in the heart of the First Map with its whole singing vastness offering itself like a crown. What they hadn't fully understood until now was that diminished was another word for *livable*.

"Master Kavanagh," said Pell, one of the morning students, raising her hand before Ada had even finished setting out the day's materials. She was nine, sharp-eyed, and the daughter of a fishmonger; her gift was that knots untied themselves around her, which had already ruined two fishing nets and one very smug older brother's bootlaces. "My da says you used to be able to map whole countries with your mind. Whole countries that don't exist anymore."

"That's true," said Ada.

"And now you teach children to read?"

"I teach everyone to read," Ada said. "Reading isn't letters, Pell. Letters are just one alphabet. There's the alphabet of weather. The alphabet of a street—how it bends where people have walked ten thousand times, how it straightens where nobody wants to go. The alphabet of your own hands." They held theirs up, palms out, where the old burn-scar of the Spire's floor plan had faded to something like freckles arranged by a drunk architect. "Your da reads fish the way I read maps. He knows where the shoals are because he listens to the water. That's not so different from what I used to do."

Pell considered this with the ferocious seriousness of the young. "So you're teaching us to be cartographers."

"I'm teaching you to notice," said Ada. "Cartography is just noticing, written down."

* * *

The school occupied what had been the Warden garrison's records hall, which Brannoc had surrendered with an expression that on a less disciplined face might have been called sheepish. It still smelled faintly of ink and bureaucracy, which Ada had decided was better than the smell of fear that had lived in most other available buildings. The desks were mismatched—donated by half the city—and the great wall at the front, once hung with conscription rosters, now held the largest map anyone in Corvane had ever seen.

It was also the strangest map anyone in Corvane had ever seen, which was rather the point.

Ada drew it fresh each week, and it recorded nothing a conventional cartographer would care about. No borders. No territories. No strategic roads or defensive positions. Instead: *Here lives a woman whose soup never burns anymore.* Here, a street where all the lamps lit at dusk without being told. Here, the well that ran clear, here the bakery whose loaves rose like ambition, here the spot on the riverbank where a boy named Dov could stand and the ice would thin beneath him no matter how cold the night, so that everyone had learned, quickly, to keep him away from the river in February.

It was, Sefa had said upon first seeing it, the least useful military document ever produced. She had then stood looking at it for a long time, and Ada had pretended not to notice that her eyes were wet.

"The trouble," Ada announced to the room, tapping the map, "is that most of you think magic is something that happens *to* you. Like weather. You woke up three weeks ago and there it was, and you've been treating it like rain—sometimes it falls on you, sometimes it doesn't, nothing to be done either way."

A boy in the back—Hask, apprenticed to a farrier, whose gift was that horses told him things, mostly complaints—said, "Isn't it, though? Weather?"

"No. Because weather doesn't get better when you pay attention to it." Ada picked up a chipped teacup from the demonstration table. "Now. Who can tell me what's wrong with this cup?"

"Crack in the glaze," said Pell immediately.

"Right. Ordinary answer. Now the other answer." Ada turned the cup slowly. "Look at it the way you'd look at your da's nets, Pell. Or a horse's temper, Hask. Everything broken tells you how it broke. This cup was dropped"—they traced a fine spiral fracture—"from about this height, onto stone, handle-first, by someone who was embarrassed about it, because whoever glued it last did it in a hurry and hoped nobody would check."

Silence. Then Hask said, "How d'you know they were embarrassed?"

"Because the mending is under the handle, where you'd only look if you were checking whether the last repair held." Ada set the cup down. "Everything in the world is talking. Cracks talk. Knots talk. Bread talks—it talks *constantly*, ask Tam Wexler. Magic isn't shouting at the world. It's finally hearing what the world has been saying the whole time. Now pair up. Everyone brought something broken. Let's listen."

They bent over their tables—mended kettles, cracked spoons, one truly tragic hat belonging to Pell's grandmother that had been through, by the evidence, a war and at least one fire—and the schoolhouse filled with that particular hum of concentration that Ada had missed for twelve years without knowing it. Sunlight came through the high windows and caught the dust motes, and somewhere outside a cart rattled past, and Mrs. Oduya's lullaby drifted faintly from Tanner's Row, sweetening something.

Ada stood in the middle of it with chalk dust on their sleeves and thought: *I spent twelve years hiding a candle, and now the whole city glows, and none of it needs me.*

The thought should have hurt. Mostly it did. But underneath the ache was something else, something that fit strangely well, like a key they'd been carrying for years finally finding its lock. For twelve years their gift had been a secret, and secrets are heavy not because of what they weigh but because of what they cost. This—the chalk dust, the tragic hat, the children arguing about whether embarrassment leaves a residue in glue—this cost nothing. This was free.

*You're smaller now,* said a voice in their head that sounded uncomfortably like Master Orenne. *And look how much more of you there is.*

Ada was still recovering from this when the door opened on its cracked two-note bell and Sefa Ilan walked in with snow in her dark hair and an expression that meant trouble wearing its Sunday clothes.

* * *

Sefa Ilan, former rebel commander, current—well, current whatever-she-was, a title the two of them were still carefully not discussing—had taken to visiting the school under the pretext of delivering reports. The reports concerned the demobilization: rebel factions signing the Accord, Wardens trading their surveillance sigils for actual policing, the slow unclenching of a city that had been holding its breath for a century. The visits concerned none of these things, and both of them knew it, and both of them maintained the fiction with the dedication of professionals.

"Report from the north quarter," Sefa said, setting a paper on Ada's desk and leaning against it in a way that made the report entirely decorative. "Tensions low. Morale improving. One incident involving the Ferro twins' hair, which apparently floated into a chimney and caused a minor fire that put itself out, which the twins insist was intentional."

"Noted."

"One incident involving a horse telling Hask that his master was watering the ale, which turned out to be true, and the resulting brawl was settled by the horse kicking the ale-seller into the trough."

"Horses have strong opinions about honesty. Noted."

"And one matter," Sefa said, and her voice shifted registers, the way it did when the soldier came forward from behind the woman, "that I couldn't put in a report."

Ada looked up. Outside, the students had spilled into the yard for the midday break, and their laughter came through the walls muffled and bright.

"There's a man been asking questions in the taverns," Sefa said. "Not Wardens—they're accounted for, and Brannoc vouches for every one of them, which is a sentence I never expected to say. Not rebels either. A stranger. Asking who drew the new maps. Asking where the cartographer keeps the originals."

Ada felt the old cold move through them, familiar as an old scar. Twelve years of instinct didn't vanish in three weeks. "What kind of stranger?"

"Says he's from beyond the passes. Says—" Sefa hesitated, and Ada watched her decide whether to soften it, and watched her decide not to, because that was Sefa entirely. "He says the stars came back everywhere, but they came back *different*. Shifted. And he says there are people beyond the passes who read the sky the way your students read cracks in cups. And they noticed."

The schoolhouse was very quiet. In the yard, Pell was demonstrating knot-untangling to a crowd of admirers and a growing pile of undone boots.

"Different how?" Ada asked.

"Half a degree. Maybe less. Enough that a man who's spent his life reading stars would feel it like a splinter in the eye." Sefa crossed her arms. "Ada. When you dissolved the Map—when you gave it away—I assumed it stayed inside the walls. Inside the people who were here that night. But you redistributed *everything*. Did you check where everything went?"

Ada opened their mouth to say yes, of course, and discovered, with the vertiginous drop of a floor proving thinner than believed, that they hadn't. They had been a little busy at the time—holding back a war, unmaking a god, choosing love over omnipotence; one's attention wandered.

"The power went to *every living person*," Ada said slowly. "That's what I asked for. Every living person."

Sefa's eyes met theirs, and Ada saw the same realization arrive in her face like weather.

"Every living person," she repeated. "Not just in Corvane."

Somewhere in the yard, Pell's crowd burst into cheering—someone's bootlace had retied itself, or refused to, or some small miracle had occurred—and Ada Kavanagh, teacher of noticing, last cartographer of the Silent Spire, stood in their cracked-bell schoolhouse and understood that the world was considerably larger than the map on their wall.

"When does this star-reader arrive?" they asked.

"He's already here," said Sefa. "He's downstairs. He asked very politely if the cartographer might be willing to compare notes."
