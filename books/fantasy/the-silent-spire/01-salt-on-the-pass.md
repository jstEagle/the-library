## 1. Salt on the Pass

The wind came down off the Thornfell peaks like something with a grudge, and Orsen Nystrom walked into it the way she walked into everything: head down, shoulders set, counting her steps.

Forty-one paces behind her, the salt caravan creaked and groaned. Six mules, three wagons, and eleven men who had been complaining since Greyford about the cold, the mud, the price of grain, and the fact that their guide was a woman. She'd stopped listening somewhere around the second hour. The complaining was a kind of music, and like all music, it told you where the complainer's attention was — which was, at the moment, on their own misery and not on the narrow shelf of trail they were following along a cliff face that dropped four hundred feet into a river the color of dirty pewter.

Good. Let them complain. A frightened man paid attention, and a paying-attention man looked around, and a looking-around man noticed things like the fact that this trail did not appear on any map, or that the cairn at the fork was stacked wrong, or that their guide had stopped twice in the last mile to press her bare palm flat against the rock as though listening to it.

The trail did not appear on any map. The cairn was stacked wrong. And Orsen had been listening to the rock.

It had been singing wrong since dawn.

* * *

She had learned the Thornfells the way other children learned their letters — slowly, painfully, with a great deal of correction. Her father had walked her through these passes at an age when she still believed the world was larger than a valley. *Feel it,* he'd say, hand over hers on the stone. *Everything that stands, stands because it agreed to. Rock remembers the agreement. Your job is never to ask it to remember more than it can bear.*

She had not understood him then. She understood him now, standing at the lip of the Hollow Bridge with the wind trying to peel her off the mountainside, and understanding did not help.

The Hollow Bridge was not really a bridge. It was a spine of stone that had once connected the two walls of a gorge, before some ancient quake had chewed out its middle and left only a ragged arc of rock, thinner than a wagon axle at its waist, spanning a gap wide enough to swallow all three wagons whole. The smugglers who used the deep roads had a dozen stories about it. Most agreed that nothing heavier than a man and his sins had crossed it in living memory.

The salt had to cross. Salt was the whole point. Salt from the southern pans, bound for the freeholds beyond the mountains, where a sack of it bought what a sack of silver bought in the lowlands. And the low road — the honest road, the road with checkpoints and tolls and crown soldiers with their little brass scales and their little brass questions — was closed to this particular cargo, because this particular cargo belonged to a man named Doss Halloway, and Doss Halloway owed taxes in three provinces and existed in none of them.

So. The Hollow Bridge.

Orsen crouched at its near end and laid her palm against the stone, and let herself hear it.

People imagined the gift as a voice, because that was how it showed in the world — words that did what they said. But the hearing came first. The hearing was the true half of it. Rock spoke, if you were built to listen. Old rock spoke slowly, in syllables that took a season to finish. This bridge had been speaking the same sentence for two hundred years: *I hold. I hold. I hold.* And underneath it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, a second sentence had begun: *I am tired.*

She pressed harder. The cold of the stone climbed her arm like water.

*I am tired. I am tired. I am—*

The waist of the bridge, where the arc thinned to the width of her forearm, had a crack in it. Not new. Old, old, old — older than the smugglers' stories, older maybe than the road itself. But old things under strain do not stay old. They become new things very quickly, and then they become falling things.

Orsen sat back on her heels and did the arithmetic she hated.

Eleven men. Six mules. Three wagons, each loaded with forty sacks of salt, each sack sixty pounds, each wagon — wagon, mules, driver — the better part of a ton and a half of weight that wanted to be on the far side of a gorge, and the only way there was over a stone sentence that had been saying *I am tired* since before her grandmother was born.

She could make it hold. That was the obscene truth of her life: she could make almost anything hold, for a while. A word, breathed low, shaped right, bound into the stone — and the bridge would agree to bear them. Stone was honest. Stone kept its word once given. That was why her people had sworn on stone, in the old days, before the old days became the kind of thing you burned people for remembering.

The cost was the other half of the arithmetic. Every oath she spoke, she spoke with herself. That was the covenant under all the covenants, the one her father had finally explained the night before he sold — the night before he left, she corrected herself, *the night before he left*, because that was the story she still lived inside — every binding drew its strength from the binder. A word that held a bridge held it with her breath, her marrow, her own long agreement to be a thing that could be spent.

Small words cost small things. A shiver, a nosebleed, a night of dreams like falling. The bridge would not be a small word.

Behind her, boots on gravel. She stood and turned before the boots arrived, face arranged into the flat patience the caravaneers expected.

"Well?" Doss Halloway was a soft man gone hard at the edges, like bread left out in the rain. He looked at the bridge, then at her, then at the bridge again, and his face did the arithmetic she'd just finished and arrived at a smaller, more frightened number. "That's not a bridge. That's a dare."

"It's a crossing," Orsen said. "It's the only crossing. You want to turn around and explain to the men in Greyford why you're carrying three tons of untaxed salt back the way you came?"

Halloway's mouth worked. "I've seen goats cross that. Goats. I've never seen a mule do it, and I have *never* seen a wagon—"

"You're not going to see one now, either. Wagons stay here." She said it the way she said everything on the trail: without room for discussion. "We break the loads. Sacks across on the mules, two trips. Men cross light. Then we reload on the far side. It costs you half a day."

"It costs me half a *day*—"

"It costs you half a day," Orsen said, "instead of three wagons, six mules, and eleven men. I've seen what's at the bottom of that gorge. The river keeps what it takes. Nothing comes back down there, not even names."

He stared at her. She stared back, and let him see nothing, which was its own kind of skill, and after a long moment Halloway blew out his cheeks and turned to his men and started shouting, because that was how soft-hard men moved the world.

Orsen turned back to the bridge.

Under the shouting, under the wind, she put her palm on the stone one more time and listened to the crack at the waist, and it was a little louder than it had been a minute ago, and she did not let her face change at all.

* * *

They crossed the way she'd ordered: men first, light and single-file, because frightened men cross better than loaded mules and she wanted the far side manned before the animals came. The wind gusted. The bridge sang its tired song. Orsen walked it last of the men, testing each placement of her boot, and where the stone narrowed she stopped and crouched and pressed both palms flat and held them there for three long breaths, and if the men behind her thought it strange, they were polite or frightened enough to say nothing.

Then the mules.

Mules were smarter than men about bridges. The first one put a hoof on the span, felt the whole arc flex — a flex no human weight had ever been heavy enough to feel — and planted itself with the immovable conviction of its kind. It took two men, a rope, a hood over its eyes, and a quarter hour of the particular cruelty that patience requires, to get it moving. It crossed. The second mule, having watched, crossed worse. The third crossed best, because mules have no tradition of learning from each other's mistakes.

By the time the last mule was across, the light had gone from gold to the bruised white of mid-afternoon, and the wind had risen, and Orsen's hands had begun to ache in the old way — deep in the wrist bones, like the ache before a storm — and she had not yet spoken a single word of power. She was saving them. She was always saving them. Her whole life was a purse with a drawstring pulled tight.

The sacks went across on the mules' backs, two trips, exactly as planned. The men grumbled and hauled and slipped and swore and did not die. And then there was nothing left on the near side but Orsen, the wind, and the last crossing — hers — and the small problem of the crack at the waist of the bridge, which had been growing louder in her hearing all afternoon, syllable by syllable, like a word being spoken very slowly by something very old.

*I am tired. I am tired. I am tired, and the wind is high, and there is a weight coming, and—*

She knelt at the span's edge. The others were on the far side, repacking the loads, their voices shredded to nothing by the gorge. This was the part of the work no one saw. This was the part of the work that was the work.

She pressed her palms to the stone. She closed her eyes. And she listened, all the way down, past the surface sentence, down into the deep grain of the rock where the old agreement lived — the covenant the bridge had made with the mountain when the mountain made the bridge, *I hold, I hold, I hold* — and she found the place where the agreement had frayed, and she set her mouth close to the cold stone, so close her breath fogged it, and she spoke.

Not loudly. Never loudly. A swordspeaker's words were not for ears.

"*Hold.*"

One word. The oldest word in her people's language, the first word, the word under all the other words. She did not shout it; she *gave* it — breathed it into the stone with the shape of her whole intent behind it, the way a smith gives a shape to iron not with the hammer alone but with the knowledge of what the hammer is for. And the stone heard. The stone *remembered*. The old agreement woke along the whole ragged spine of the bridge like fire running down a fuse, and the crack at the waist — the tired, old, honest crack — drew itself tight and closed by the width of a hair, and the bridge agreed, one more time, to hold.

The cost arrived on schedule.

It always arrived on schedule. That was the covenant's cruelest courtesy: it never surprised you. Orsen felt it come up through her palms and down through her spine, a cold that was not cold, a draining that was not pain exactly but was pain's accountant — a precise, itemized withdrawal. Her vision went white at the edges. Her heartbeat stuttered, stumbled, found itself. Somewhere behind her eyes a candle she could not see guttered down by a finger's width, and she knew, the way she always knew, that the candle was not the kind of thing that grew back.

She knelt there on the stone with her forehead nearly touching it, breathing, counting. Ten. Twenty. The wind pushed at her back like a hand. Thirty. The white receded from her vision. Forty. Her heart settled into its ordinary rhythm, and the ache in her wrists dulled to the familiar background hum she had carried for thirty years, since the first word she'd ever spoken, at nine years old, holding a songbird's broken wing together with a whispered *heal* while her mother wept in the doorway for reasons Orsen hadn't understood until much, much later.

She stood. She crossed the bridge on her own two feet, and at the waist — where the crack lived, where her word now lived too, wound into the stone like a stitch — she did not look down. Looking down was for people with nothing waiting on the far side.

* * *

They made the far wall by dusk and camped in the lee of an overhang, a fire built small and screened, because Halloway's salt was not the only thing that preferred not to be seen. The men ate hard bread and salt pork and, in honor of their survival, a little of the honey one of them had been hoarding since Greyford. Someone produced a skin of something harsh. The mood softened the way mood does when the dying thing is safely behind you.

Orsen sat at the fire's edge with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of snowmelt, letting the heat work into her wrists, and let the talk wash over her.

"—telling you, the crier was at the market cross when we loaded. Stood right up on the steps of the assize hall with the crown's man beside him—"

"Criers are always saying something."

"Not like this." The speaker was Ferren, the youngest of Halloway's men, a thin boy with a chapped face who was too honest for smuggling and would learn or die. "It was a proclamation. Sealed. He read it twice, the way they do when they want it repeated right. All persons gifted — that's the word he used, *gifted*, like it's a blessing — all persons gifted are to present themselves at the assize in their shire for registration. By the first frost."

"Registration," Halloway said, around a mouthful of bread. "And then what?"

Ferren's voice dropped. The fire popped. "And then what," he agreed quietly. "That was the part nobody asked out loud. But my cousin's in the miller's trade down Greyford way, and he says the mill at Harrow's Bend — you know it, the one with the miller's wife who—"

"I know the one."

"Well. There's no mill at Harrow's Bend anymore. There's ash at Harrow's Bend. And a sigil nailed to the well post. Burned the whole place and the crown's criers haven't said one word about it, and the crown's men are buying up every drop of lamp oil between here and the river, and my cousin says—" the boy swallowed "—my cousin says they're not burning them for practicing. He says they're burning them slow, one at a time, village by village, like they're *looking* for something. Like it's a hunt."

The fire popped again. No one spoke. Somewhere up the dark mountainside, a stone came loose and clattered down into the gorge they'd crossed, and every man at that fire flinched, and every man pretended he hadn't.

Orsen drank her snowmelt.

The word *gifted* sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. Thirty years. Thirty years of small words, whispered words, words spent on bridges and broken wings and, twice, on men bleeding out on the trail when there was no one to see — thirty years of being so careful that careful had become the shape of her soul. She had a cover story the way other women had a maiden name. She had calluses on her palms from rope and rock, and not one mark on her that a stranger would know to read.

But a childhood friend had read them once. In Greyford. Three years ago, at the fish stall, and Marta Tull had seen the old oath-scars on Orsen's knuckles — the fine white lines, like writing, that no honest life explains — and had looked at her, and had looked *away*, and had not come to the stall again that day or any day since. Orsen had told herself it meant nothing. Marta was busy. Marta was careful too. Everyone in the Thornfells was careful; it was the regional religion.

*All persons gifted are to present themselves for registration. By the first frost.*

First frost was three weeks away.

"You're quiet, Nystrom," Halloway said. He'd come to sit near her, the way he did when he wanted something and hadn't decided how to ask.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always *listening*. There's a difference." He turned his cup in his hands. "That bridge. It shouldn't have held. I've crossed these passes twenty years, and I'm telling you, that span was done. It was finished. It was a dead thing waiting to fall. And then you knelt down and put your hands on it like a woman praying, and we all crossed it like it was the king's own highway."

"Stone's honest," Orsen said. "I know how to ask it politely."

Halloway looked at her. He was not a stupid man, which was the whole problem with him. "My grandmother," he said, "came from the high valleys. Before the purges. She used to say there were people once who could talk to iron and it would listen. She said they all died out." He paused. "She said it the way you say a thing you don't believe."

"Your grandmother was a storyteller."

"My grandmother was a liar," Halloway agreed, "except about the important things." He drank, and looked into the fire, and said, too casually, "First frost is three weeks away. You'll be wanting to get back over the passes before the snow, I expect. I've got cargo coming north in ten days. Pays double for a guide who knows the deep roads."

"I know the deep roads."

"I know you do." He stood, brushing off his knees, and paused beside her. "There's a checkpoint going up at Greyford, they're saying. Crown men with a bell. A *resonance* bell, Ferren's cousin called it. Rings different, near the gifted, they say. Rings different, and they take you."

He walked away to yell at someone about the mule pickets, because that was how soft-hard men carried fear.

Orsen sat alone at the fire's edge with her cup of cold snowmelt and her aching wrists and the candle behind her eyes burned a finger's width shorter, and she thought about a bridge that had agreed, one more time, to hold.

She thought about Marta Tull looking away.

She thought about ash at Harrow's Bend, and a sigil on a well post, and a bell that rang different, and a brother she had not let herself think about in twenty years, and a father's voice saying *your job is never to ask it to remember more than it can bear* — and underneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the same old sentence her whole life had been built on, the sentence she had been hearing since she was nine years old and had never once said aloud:

*I am tired. I am tired. I am tired, and the frost is coming, and there is a weight coming, and—*

She tipped the snowmelt out into the dirt and went to check the mule pickets, because there was always work, and work was the only prayer she had left.

Three weeks to first frost. Greyford lay a day and a half south, and the proclamation would be read at the market cross every morning until the frost came, and Marta Tull would hear it every morning, and Marta Tull knew what the marks on Orsen's knuckles meant.

Orsen looked south, into the dark where the pass dropped toward the lowlands, and began, silently, to plan.
