## 2. The Proclamation at Greyford

Greyford smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it all, the river — that flat grey scent of water moving slow over silt. Orsen had walked into the town a hundred times across fifteen years, and the smell never changed, and she had always found the sameness of it comforting. Today it felt like a noose being fitted.

She came in with the tail end of the salt caravan, hood up, driving the two lame mules because someone had to and no one else wanted them. The drovers called her Thorn-wife behind her back and nothing at all to her face; both were fine with her. The caravan would break apart in Greyford's market square by midmorning, salt sold or stolen, men paid or vanished into the taverns, and by noon she would be a woman buying lamp oil and boot wax, forgettable as rain.

The market cross stood where it always stood, at the meeting of three streets, a lichen-eaten post with an iron bracket for a lantern. This morning there was no lantern on the bracket.

There was a man.

He wore the crown's grey-and-gold, but not a soldier's cut — the coat was too clean, the boots too polished, and he stood too still. Around his neck, on a chain of dull iron, hung a bell no bigger than a fist, and Orsen's stomach dropped through the soles of her boots before her mind even caught up, because she knew what resonance bells were for, she'd known since she was nine years old and her mother had burned one in the hearth rather than let it hang in the house. *It sings when it's near a speaker,* her mother had said. *Like calls to like. That's how they find us.*

The man was not alone. Two soldiers flanked him, pikes grounded, faces bored the way only garrison men waiting for something to be over can be bored. A crier's board was propped against the cross, paper nailed to it, seals dangling.

The square was filling anyway. Market day waited for no proclamation. Farmers stacked their carts along the edges, wives argued about the price of onions, children threaded between legs, and all of it went on around the man with the bell as though he were a fencepost — until the town's own crier, old Hobben with his cracked voice and his felt hat, climbed the steps of the cross and unrolled the paper with hands that were not quite steady.

Orsen stopped at the edge of the square, half in shadow under a tanner's awning, and told herself she could simply walk away. She didn't need to hear it. She already knew what it said. Everyone knew what it said; the roads north had been thick with rumor since midsummer, rumors she had filed away with all the other things she refused to think about.

But her feet wouldn't move. There is a particular gravity to your own death warrant being read aloud. You have to stand and listen, if only to learn its exact weight.

Hobben cleared his throat. The square did not quiet — squares never do — but it dimmed, voices dropping by degrees the way light does behind cloud.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third, of that name, King of the Thornlands and the Low Reaches, Protector of the Faithful—"

The titles went on. They always went on. Orsen watched the man with the bell instead. He hadn't moved. He watched the crowd the way a fisherman watches water, without urgency, with the patience of someone who knows the net is wide.

"—let it be proclaimed throughout the shires: that all persons gifted beyond nature — speakers, binders, oath-makers, charmers, hedge-witches, and all who bear the marks or practice the arts — shall present themselves before the crown's assessors within forty days of this reading, at any garrison or appointed seat, there to be registered, examined, and—"

Hobben stumbled. Just slightly. A hitch in the breath, a wet blink. He read on.

"—and those found true shall be taken into the king's service, for the good of the realm." A pause. "And those who conceal their gift, or harbor the concealed, or aid them in flight—" Hobben's voice cracked clean this time, and he swallowed and forced it back down. "—shall be judged guilty of treason against the crown, and the penalty for treason is fire."

*There it was.* Not *death*. Not *the rope*. Fire, said plainly, in a market square full of farmers and children, in a town that had hanged a horse thief last spring and thought itself harshly used by the world.

The square made a sound Orsen had never heard a crowd make before — not a gasp, not a murmur, something lower than either, a collective exhale like wind moving through standing grain. Somewhere a baby started crying and was hushed too quickly. A woman near the well crossed herself twice and then, thinking better of it, crossed herself again, as though once might not be enough anymore.

Orsen stood very still under the awning and did her arithmetic.

Forty days. Assessors at every garrison. *Marks.* Her marks were on her knuckles, four small silvered lines on the right hand, faded now to the color of old scar tissue — which was exactly what they were, oath-scars, earned word by word over thirty years of keeping her mouth shut and her hand bare. Gloves in summer. Mitten-weather explained in autumn. A childhood of hiding a hand the way other children hid stolen fruit.

Thirty years of anonymity, and the crown had just spent one morning dissolving it into smoke. From today, every stranger who glanced at her hands was a potential assessor. Every neighbor was a witness. Every friend—

"Marta," said a voice beside her, low and shocked. "Marta Tull."

Orsen turned.

Marta Tull stood close enough to touch, a basket of late apples hooked over her arm, and she was not looking at Orsen's face. She was looking at Orsen's right hand, where it gripped the mule's lead, where the glove had ridden back half an inch in the cold and shown the first silver line across the second knuckle.

They had been girls together. That was the whole horror of it. Marta Tull had been the daughter of the ferryman at the Greyford crossing, gap-toothed and loud, and she had taught Orsen to swim in the mill pond the summer they were ten, and she had held Orsen's hair back the night they drank stolen cider at thirteen, and she had been there — she had been *there*, at the forge, the night the witch-hunters came the first time, twenty-five years ago, when Orsen's mother put her hands over Orsen's mouth and told her whatever you hear, whatever happens, you do not speak. Marta had hidden in the loft above the forge with Orsen while men with iron bells searched the ashes below.

Marta knew. Marta had always known. And for twenty-five years Marta had said nothing, and that silence had been the single most valuable thing Orsen owned, worth more than any smuggler's gold, and Orsen had never once thanked her for it because thanking her would have meant admitting the debt out loud.

Now Marta stood with apples on her arm and looked at the mark on Orsen's knuckle and then, slowly, deliberately, terribly, looked *away*.

Not just aside. Away — chin down, eyes fixed on the cobblestones, shoulders drawn in, the whole body language of a person making themselves small beside something dangerous. And her hands, Orsen saw, were trembling. The apples rattled faintly in the basket with a sound like teeth chattering.

"*Marta*," Orsen said again, softer this time, and reached out —

Marta stepped back. It wasn't dramatic. It was one step, small and quick, the step of a woman avoiding a puddle, and it was the worst thing anyone had ever done to Orsen Nystrom, and Orsen had been shot at, knifed, buried in avalanche snow, and once left for dead in a ravine with a broken leg.

"I'm sorry," Marta whispered. She still wasn't looking up. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I have children, Orsen, I have — Tom's in the garrison now, he's sixteen, they'd — if I'm seen talking to you—" Her breath hitched. "You shouldn't be here. Oh, saints, you shouldn't be here. Go north. Go anywhere. Don't come back to Greyford. Please don't ever come back to Greyford."

"Marta—"

"They're going door to door after the reading." Now Marta did look up, and her eyes were wet and frightened and, underneath the fear, grieving — grieving already, doing the arithmetic of survival the same way Orsen was, arriving at the same cold sums. "Assessors. With bells. They ask questions. *Has anyone new come to stay? Has anyone's neighbor ever seemed strange? Did your grandmother ever cure a cow with words?*" A bitter, terrified little laugh escaped her. "Half the valley will inform on half the other half by nightfall just to be left alone. You know how people are. You know."

Orsen knew. People were exactly like that. People had always been exactly like that; the Hunt hadn't created the fear, it had merely given the fear permission to stop pretending to be politeness.

"You taught me to swim," Orsen said. It came out stupidly, a child's sentence, and she couldn't take it back.

Marta's face crumpled for one raw instant. Then she mastered it, the way farmwives master everything, and lifted her chin, and when she spoke again her voice was low and fast and fierce, the voice of the loud gap-toothed girl from the mill pond, surfacing through thirty years like a body through ice.

"The Delling family," she said. "Up past the old mill, the west fork. The mother's a hedgewife — charms the cows' milk, eases birthing fever, nothing, *nothing* dangerous, but everyone knows, everyone's always known, and now everyone has to prove they *don't* know. If anyone warns them, it should be someone who owes them nothing and will be gone by dark. Not me. I can't. I can't, Orsen." The apples trembled again. "I've said too much already. Go. *Go.*"

And she turned and walked into the crowd with her basket of apples and her straight back, and did not look back, and Orsen stood under the tanner's awning holding a mule's lead and understood that the life she had built — the careful, patient, invisible life, thirty years of gloves and silence and small safe distances — had not been destroyed by the proclamation.

It had been destroyed by one step backward from a friend. Everything else was just paperwork.

* * *

She bought what she needed because a woman who didn't buy anything was more memorable than a woman who did: lamp oil, boot wax, a twist of dried plum, a new whetstone. She kept her glove on. She kept her head down. She counted the assessors.

There were four of them working the square by midday, and they worked in pairs, each pair led by a man with a resonance bell on an iron chain. The bells did not ring. Orsen passed within ten feet of one, heart slamming, hand curled tight around the mule's lead — and the bell stayed silent, swinging gently, dumb as a stone. Later, watching from the shadow of the tithe barn, she thought she understood: the bells rang for active speech, for the gift awake in the blood, and hers had been asleep so long, pressed down so hard and so long, that even her body had learned to hold its breath around them. Thirty years of silence had made her, in some sense the bells could read, almost innocent.

*Almost.* The word offered no comfort whatsoever.

What the bells couldn't find, questions could. She listened at the edge of the queue outside the tithe barn, where a line of the frightened and the calculating had formed — a few of the genuinely gifted, gray-faced, come to register and pray for the promised "king's service"; and far more of them the other kind, neighbors come to report neighbors, wearing the specific shameless blankness of people performing virtue. She heard a man describe, in careful detail, the hedge-charms of the Delling family. She heard him say the words *old mill, west fork*.

Her blood went cold and then hot and then cold again.

She should leave. Every instinct she owned, every rule she had lived by for three decades, said the same thing: leave tonight, leave now, go north past the checkpoints before they hardened into walls, save yourself, you cannot save anyone, saving yourself *is* the whole art of survival, that is the covenant, that is the price—

That was her father's voice saying it. That was her father's voice in her head, calm and reasonable and rotten to the center, and she realized with a strange floating clarity that she had obeyed that voice for thirty years without knowing whose it was.

Her mother's voice had sounded different. Her mother's voice had said *you do not speak* — but her mother's voice had also, the last night of her life, said something else, whispered into Orsen's hair while the forge still smoldered below: *One day, girl, you'll meet someone you can't leave behind. Pray you're strong enough to run anyway.*

No. Orsen stood in the tithe barn's shadow and corrected her, silently, with all the love and none of the obedience in her.

*Pray you're strong enough to stay.*

* * *

She left Greyford at dusk, alone, leading the two lame mules because they were slow and unremarkable and because a woman with mules was a woman with a reason to be on the road. Behind her the town glowed orange at the seams, windows lit against the coming frost, ordinary and warm and full of people rehearsing their testimony.

Ahead, the road split at the ford: north, toward the passes, toward safety, toward the deep roads and the plan she'd begun shaping three weeks ago in the high country. West, up the valley fork, toward the old mill.

Toward the Dellings, who charmed milk and eased fevers and had never hurt a soul. Toward a family who had perhaps a day, maybe two, before the assessors finished with Greyford's easy pickings and worked their way up the valley like frost working into a wall crack.

She stood at the fork for a long time. The mules shifted and blew. The first stars came out over the pass, hard and white, and somewhere far off a wolf began its winter song, thin as a wire.

Then she turned the mules' heads west, toward the mill, and did not let herself think about it any further, because thinking was how her father's voice got back in.

The moon rose as she climbed the west fork, and by the time the road bent around the last shoulder of the hill and the mill should have appeared — wheel, weir, the crooked chimney she remembered from childhood visits — Orsen smelled it before she saw it.

Smoke. Old smoke, days old, soaked into everything, the fat sweet reek of a burned building that has been rained on since.

The mill was gone. All of it — wheelhouse, drying sheds, the family house with its blue door — reduced to black foundations and standing chimneys, and nailed to the gatepost at the lane's head, driven in deep, was a sheet of iron stamped with a seal she recognized from twenty-five years ago and had prayed she'd never see again:

A bell, crossed by a sword, ringed in fire.

The sigil of the Hunt.

And beneath it, in fresh paint, red as an open wound, five words for everyone in the valley to read:

*JUDGED. FOUND GUILTY. RIGHTLY BURNED.*
