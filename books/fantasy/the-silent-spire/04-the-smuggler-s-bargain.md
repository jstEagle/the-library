## 4. The Smuggler's Bargain

The sign above the door of the Drowned Bell was a bell cast in green bronze, half-submerged in a painted wave, and it had never once rung in the thirty years Orsen had known the tavern to exist. That was the point. A smuggler's house announced nothing. It sat at the crook where the Greyford millrace met the river, half-swallowed by willow shadow, and it smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of ale that had been watered twice.

Orsen had dressed for the part she needed to play: a salt-factor's widow with more grief than coin, her oath-marks hidden under wool wraps wound tight from wrist to elbow, her hair scraped back under a plain coif. She had learned long ago that the best disguise was not beauty or ugliness but forgettability — a face the eye slid off like rain off oiled canvas. She had been practicing forgettability since she was nine years old, when she first understood what her voice could do and what men would do to own it.

She pushed open the door of the Drowned Bell and let the noise swallow her.

It was mid-afternoon, which meant the room held the day-drinkers and the schemers and no one honest. A one-eyed carter snored over his cup in the corner by the peat fire. Two women argued in low voices over a ledger, dividing up something that had probably once belonged to someone else. Behind the bar, Maren the keeper drew ale without being asked and pretended not to see anything at all, which was the most expensive thing she sold.

Vael Corrigan sat at the table furthest from the fire, because he had never trusted warmth he didn't control.

He looked older than he had three years ago, when their paths last crossed on the high road above Kettle Hollow — a little more silver at his temples, a new scar splitting his left eyebrow clean through, and the same unhurried stillness that had made him dangerous then. He was playing cards alone against himself, dealing hands to invisible opponents, and he did not look up when she crossed the room. He simply said, to no one, "Sit down before you fall down, Nystrom. You walk like a woman who hasn't slept since the solstice."

So much for forgettability. But then, with Vael, it had never worked anyway.

She sat. He gathered up his phantom cards with a flick of his fingers and finally raised his eyes, and there it was again, that look — the one that weighed her like a horse-trader weighing stock, except that somewhere underneath the appraisal was something less mercantile and considerably more inconvenient for them both.

"Three years," she said.

"Two years and eight months." He pushed a cup across the table and filled it himself from the jug at his elbow. "But who's counting."

"You are, apparently."

"I count everything." He smiled, and the scar pulled. "Occupational habit. Drink that. You've got the grey look of someone running on fear and trail-bread, and neither keeps."

She drank. The ale was bad and she was grateful for it. Around them the room went on with its small corruptions, and she kept her voice pitched under the argument of the ledger-women. "I need passage north. Past the tolls. All the way to Coldharbour ford, if it comes to that."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise; Vael didn't do surprise in public places, maybe not in any places — but interest, sharpening itself quietly like a knife on a strap.

"North," he repeated. "In autumn. When every checkpoint between here and the mountains has doubled its hunters and tripled its fees." He turned his cup slowly on the scarred wood. "And you come to me. Not to Hesk. Not to the Tallow Street runners. To me, whom you have cheated twice, insulted publicly once — and that speech about my parentage at the Saltmoot, I'm told, is still quoted at guild dinners."

"You were cheating first."

"I was *negotiating* first. You cheated." The smile stayed, but the voice beneath it had gone flat and precise, and she remembered why she'd never quite managed to hate him: he was one of the few people alive whose mind worked like hers, all angles and exits. "So either you're desperate, or you're carrying something worth the insult. Both, I'd guess. Desperate people don't plan routes this carefully — I watched you case the back door before you came in, and you checked the reflection in the copper kettle to see if anyone followed you to the millrace. Amateurs don't check kettles."

"Are you going to say yes, or are you going to keep demonstrating how clever you are?"

His laugh was low and genuine, and it did something warm and unwelcome in her chest. She smothered it. Warmth was a luxury item this season, priced beyond her means.

"I'll say yes," he said, "on terms. First term: full truth about the cargo."

"There is no cargo. Just me."

"No one pays what you're asking to move *just themselves* north in hunting season." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and dropped his voice to almost nothing. "Unless the cargo is themselves. Unless they're running from something specific. And here's what I know, Nystrom, free of charge, as a gesture of goodwill: two nights ago the witch-hunters burned a village called Millbrook to its foundation stones. Fourteen dead, maybe twenty. The criers in Greyford are reading a proclamation that says every gifted soul must present themselves for registration within the month or be taken. Registration." He said the word the way other men said *plague*. "And yesterday morning, a salt caravan came through Thornfell pass guided by nobody, crossing the Old Span after dark, which no living guide can do without—" he stopped, delicately, the way a man steps around a corpse in the road, "—without certain assistance."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly. Orsen kept her hands loose on the table and her breathing even, and thanked every god she'd ever half-believed in that she'd wrapped the marks under her sleeves.

"I don't know what you're suggesting."

"No. You know exactly what I'm suggesting. You just want me to unsay it so you can pretend I never said it, and then we can both go on lying comfortably." His eyes were steady and not unkind, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. "Here's my second term, then, and it's simpler. You don't have to tell me what you are. I'll take you north regardless. But you should know that I already suspect, and I am choosing — right now, sober, in daylight — not to care. Because whatever you are, you used it to bring mules across a bridge that should have killed them, and I've spent enough time around the court to know that the people they burn are rarely the people who deserve it."

Orsen looked at him for a long moment. The fire popped. The snoring carter shifted. Somewhere behind the bar Maren dropped a tankard and swore with real feeling.

"Why?" she asked. It came out quieter than she intended. "Why not care? Men like you sell everything. It's how you got the scar and the silver hair and the reputation. Why does my secret get to be the exception?"

He turned his cup again, and for the first time since she'd sat down, he looked away from her — out the fogged window toward the millrace, where the willows were letting go of their leaves one yellow finger at a time.

"Do you remember Kettle Hollow?" he said. "Two autumns back. The flood."

"The bridge went out. Half the village nearly went with it."

"A boy went with it. Eleven years old. Caught in the shallows, pinned against the weir. There were forty grown men on the bank and none of us could reach him — the current would have taken the strongest swimmer in the county." He spoke evenly, but she heard the effort of evenness, the sound of a story carried too long in a closed box. "And then an old woman stepped out of the crowd. Nobody knew her. She'd kept the shrine at the edge of the village, swept the stones, asked for bread now and then. She walked into the water like it owed her money, and she said a word — I was close enough to hear it, and I still couldn't tell you what it was, it went through me rather than past me — and the river parted. Just opened, like a hand unclenching. She brought the boy out. And three weeks later the hunters came through asking questions, and the old woman was gone, and the village let them believe whatever they wanted to believe because it was easier."

He looked back at her then, and his eyes were dry and hard and terribly clear.

"I've thought about that woman every day for two years. About how she saved a life with a word and lost hers for it, and forty of us stood on the bank and did nothing, and I have hated myself in a quiet, patient way ever since. So when a woman walks into my tavern walking like her — careful, watchful, carrying something heavy in her blood and pretending it's luggage — I find that I am not interested in selling her." He shrugged, one shoulder, as if dismissing his own confession. "Consider it a debt. The universe keeps books too. I'm just settling an entry."

Orsen discovered that her throat had gone tight. She took another drink to hide it, and the ale tasted worse and better than before.

"That's a stupid reason to trust someone," she managed.

"It's the only kind of reason that holds. Reasons based on profit renegotiate the moment the price changes." He straightened, and the merchant came back into his posture like armor buckling on. "Terms. One: you travel as my factor's widow, which conveniently you're already dressed as. Two: you speak when spoken to and not otherwise, because your accent has Thornfell written all over it and Thornfell guides are being questioned at every toll. Three: my route, my pace, my decisions on the road. If I say leave the cart and run, we leave the cart and run. And four—" he hesitated, and she saw genuine reluctance cross his face, "—four: we stop at the tollgate outside Harrowmere. It's unavoidable; the deep road surfaces there whether I like it or not. They've got a resonance bell."

The word landed in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

A resonance bell. She'd heard rumors from the caravans — a bronze bell, tuned wrong on purpose, that sang when gifted blood was near. The old tests had been crude: touch cold iron, recite the litany, watch your eyes. Cruel, but survivable with nerve. A bell didn't care about nerve. A bell cared about what you *were*, down in the marrow where no amount of practice reached.

"They ring it for everyone?" she asked.

"Everyone. Which means half the honest travelers in the county flinch and half the guilty ones don't, so mostly it's theater — but it's theater with teeth." He studied her face with that weighing look again. "Can you stand next to it while it rings?"

"I've stood next to worse things while they rang."

"I don't doubt it." Something gentled in him, briefly, before he shuttered it. "There's a trick to it. The bell reads resonance through proximity and panic — a calm ungifted body and a controlled gifted body read close enough together that a bored sergeant can't always tell the difference. If you stay close to me, breathe with me, keep your heart rate down, you may pass. It's not certain. Nothing on this road is certain anymore. But it's better odds than the open road alone, and the open road alone is what you'll get from every other runner in Greyford, so."

"So I take the devil I've insulted."

"The devil you've *negotiated with*," he corrected, and extended his hand across the table. "Half now, half at Coldharbour ford. And Nystrom — the fee includes discretion. Whatever you're carrying, whatever you're fleeing, it dies with me. Guild law, personal law, both. I don't sell passengers."

"You sold a route to the Duke of Marrow's men last spring."

"I sold a *road map* to the Duke of Marrow's men, and I pocketed the fee and gave them a map with a gorge on it that isn't there anymore. Discretion and honesty are different products." His grin flashed, quick and wolfish. "You'd know that better than anyone, I think."

She put her hand in his. His grip was warm and calloused and steady, and she held it perhaps a half-second longer than a bargain required, and felt him notice, and felt herself not particularly mind.

* * *

They settled the route over the dregs of the jug: out of Greyford by the tannery road before dusk, north along the river as far as the drowned chapel, then cutting inland through the beech woods to join the drovers' track below Harrowmere. Two days' travel, three if weather turned. Vael talked in distances and timings and the names of men who could be bribed versus men who could only be avoided, and Orsen listened with half her attention while the other half stood guard over the door and the windows and the whole trembling architecture of her secrecy.

Because that was the thing he hadn't said outright, the thing hanging unspoken between them like smoke: *he suspected.* Had suspected, maybe, since Kettle Hollow, since the Old Span, since longer ago than she dared calculate. Every year of her hiding had rested on the assumption that no one who mattered had ever truly looked at her. And here was a man who had looked — closely, patiently, across years — and instead of reaching for iron or flame had reached across a tavern table and offered her his hand.

She didn't know yet whether that made him safe or merely the most dangerous thing she'd encountered all season.

They left the Drowned Bell separately, as agreed — Vael first, whistling something tuneless, playing the idle trader; Orsen ten minutes later through the back, past the yard where Maren's boy was chopping wood with more enthusiasm than accuracy. The light had begun to fail, the sky over Greyford going the color of old pewter, and the first woodsmoke of evening lay flat on the town like a held breath.

Vael was waiting in the alley behind the tannery with two packhorses and a cart, exactly as arranged, and he handed her up onto the bench beside him without a word. For the first mile they rode in silence through streets that emptied early these days, shutters closing like eyes.

Then, quietly, not looking at her, he said: "The old woman at Kettle Hollow. Before the hunters came. Someone hid her in a salt wagon and got her over the border into the hill country. Never knew who. Always wondered."

Orsen kept her eyes on the road ahead, on the last orange smear of sunset dying behind the western hills, and said nothing at all.

He glanced sideways at her, and she felt his gaze travel slowly from her wrapped wrists to her face, and settle there — not accusing, not certain, but *counting*, because he counted everything — and then he faced forward again and clicked his tongue to the horses.

"Well," he said softly, to the horses, to the dusk, to her. "Whoever it was, they did good work. Let's hope they haven't gotten rusty."

Ahead of them, where the north road climbed out of the river valley toward the dark line of the beech woods, the first torches of the Harrowmere checkpoint were already visible — tiny, distant, patient points of fire, like stars that had fallen low to watch the road.

Two days, Vael had said.

Orsen watched those fires and silently began counting breaths.
