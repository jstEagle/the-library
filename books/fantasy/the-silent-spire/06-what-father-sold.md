## 6. What Father Sold

The house at Hollowmere had not changed so much as it had shrunk.

Orsen stood in the dooryard where she'd once been tall enough to see over the fence, and now the fence came to her hip, the gate swung on a rusted hinge, and the whole of her childhood fit inside a space she could cross in twenty strides. The thatch had gone gray and patchy. Someone — no one, probably; wind and weather — had left the door half-open, breathing out a smell of cold ash and mouse droppings and dried herbs that had lost their names to time.

She had told herself she was coming for practical reasons. That was how she had learned to speak to herself, in the flat grammar of survival: *You need coin. You need warm clothes for the north passes. You need to know if anyone followed you from Greyford.* All true. None of it the truth.

Her mother was buried on the rise behind the goat pen, under a rowan tree that Da had planted the week they put her down, because their mother had loved rowans and their father had loved the idea of giving her something even after there was no more of her left to give things to.

Orsen climbed the rise with her hood up and her breath steaming, and knelt in the frost-browned grass, and did not pray, because she had never learned how and because prayer felt like another kind of oath, and she had sworn too many of those already.

"Mam," she said instead. Just the word. It was enough to make her throat close.

She let the silence sit for a while. Somewhere downslope a crow complained about the morning. The mountains stood around the little valley like patient judges.

Then she took up the spade she'd carried three miles through the dark rather than be seen with it on the road, and began to dig.

* * *

Grave-robbing was a smuggler's trade, technically. She'd done it twice before for other people — recovering family seals, wedding rings, the small tin boxes of papers that mattered more than gold when the tax-men or the witch-hunters came asking questions. She had always told herself that when it was her own blood in the ground she'd find some other way.

But there was no other way. There hadn't been since the crier's voice in Greyford market, since Brenna's face going white and turning away, since the ash where the mill had stood and the sigil nailed to the post like a trophy hung by a man who thought he'd won something.

The crown knew a swordspeaker was moving through Thornfell. Sooner or later they would come to Hollowmere, because Hollowmere was where she had been born, and hunters were thorough the way rot was thorough. If there was anything in this house that named her — anything with her gift written into it, any record, any token — it needed to burn before they arrived.

That was the story. She dug anyway.

The earth came up soft and dark, thawed just enough at the surface to be cruel about it, frozen beneath like a held breath. She worked without magic. She always worked without magic when there was an honest way to do a thing, because each whispered word cost her — heat first, then strength, then something deeper she didn't have a name for, a thinning of the self, as though every oath-word spent was a page torn quietly out of the book of her. She had crossed a crumbling bridge two weeks ago on four syllables and paid for it with two days of shivering and a nosebleed that wouldn't quit.

So: spade, and shoulders, and the crow watching her like a creditor.

She hit the lid of the coffin a hand's width shallower than she'd expected. Her father had built it himself, she remembered suddenly, absurdly — remembered him planing the boards in the yard while Fen, twelve years old and already sickly, sat on the step and watched, and Orsen, ten, had hated the sound of the plane because it meant it was really happening.

The boards were soft with rot. They came apart under the spade edge like wet bread.

She made herself look. Her mother's bones lay quiet in the good dress, hands folded, and twenty years had been kind in the way the dead are kind, which is to say indifferent. The grave-goods were exactly where Mam would have wanted them: the copper kettle, the blue shawl folded beneath her head, a clay cup, a sprig of rowan berries long since turned to black beads.

And under her crossed hands, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed cord, a flat bundle the size of a psalter.

Orsen went still. Cold air moved across the back of her neck.

Mam had been buried quick, in the fever winter, when the priest couldn't be spared and the ground wouldn't soften. Whatever went into that coffin had gone in fast — Da kneeling at the graveside pressing things into the boards with the haste of a man hiding evidence, and she had thought, all these years, that she remembered him weeping.

Maybe he had been. Men could do both. Her father had been proof of that his whole life.

She lifted the bundle out, brushed the grave soil from it, and climbed out of the hole with her heart going hard and strange, and carried it to the ruined porch where there was light, and cut the cord with her knife.

* * *

Inside the oilcloth were three things.

The first was her mother's marriage-ring, which explained itself and broke her a little, because Da had never said where the ring went, and here it was, kept where he could visit it.

The second was a lock of hair tied with thread — fine, pale, unmistakably Fen's. Fen's hair before the sickness took the color out of it, before he went thin and gray-eyed and quiet, before the winters started taking more of him each year until the year they stopped taking anything because there wasn't enough left to take.

Fen had died the spring Orsen turned fourteen. Everyone in Hollowmere had said what people say. *He was always frail. It was a mercy. The gods loved him best.* And their father had aged ten years in a season and never spoken his son's name again, and Orsen had grown up believing that grief was simply a country her father emigrated to and could not be reached by letter.

The third thing was a document.

It was heavy paper, the kind used for guild charters and land deeds, and it had been folded in thirds and handled often — the creases were worn soft, the edges furred, as though someone had taken it out and looked at it many times over many years, the way you'd look at a wound to see whether it was healing. It was not healing. The ink had been pressed deep, in a formal hand, with a seal at the foot that Orsen did not recognize and yet recognized instantly, the way you know a face in a dream: a mountain split open, and within the split, a flame.

She read it standing on the porch of the house she'd been born in, with the frost melting off her boots, and the words went into her slowly, one at a time, like nails driven by a patient carpenter.

*A Contract of Tithe, entered between the Court Beneath the Mountain and Torvald Nystrom of Hollowmere, freely given.*

*The party of the first part conveys unto the Court one (1) soul entire, being the soul of Fenric Nystrom, aged eleven years, gifted in the speaking of oaths.*

*Payment to the party of the second part: remission of the wasting-sickness, and security of the household against all inquiry.*

*Let it further be entered that payment is deferred. The tithe shall be collected upon the finding of the last gifted heir of the Nystrom line. Until such time, the soul shall be held in service as surety.*

Signed, *Torvald Nystrom*. Signed, in a hand like frost forming on glass, a name that was less a name than a title: *First Magistrate, Undercourt of the Deep.*

Dated twenty-two years ago. Eleven years before Fen died.

Eleven years before everyone in Hollowmere had shaken their heads over the frail boy and called it the gods' will.

Orsen read it again. Then a third time, because the mind does that, returns to the thing it cannot hold, lifts it, sets it down, lifts it again, hoping it will weigh differently. It did not weigh differently.

Her father had sold her brother's soul when Fen was eleven years old.

Not after the death. Before it. There had been no death — or none that mattered. The sickness, the wasting, the gray decline that the whole valley had mourned gently and correctly for eleven years — that had been the *payment schedule*. Remission of illness in exchange for the soul. Fen had lain in his bed getting lighter and lighter because he was being drained by installments, sipped at year by year, and their father had signed the paper and taken the peace of mind and the safety of his household and sat at the table every night looking sorrowful, and Orsen had brought him broth, and he had wept, and she had believed the weeping.

And the last clause. She read it until the words stopped sliding away from her.

*Upon the finding of the last gifted heir.*

They weren't hunting the gifted of Thornfell generally. Or they were — the burnings were real, the bell in Greyford was real, Brenna's fear was real. But underneath the hunt, threaded through it like wire through rope, was a search. A specific search. *The last gifted heir of the Nystrom line.*

Her.

Every burned village, every checkpoint, every resonance bell rung at trembling travelers — somewhere behind all of it, the Court Beneath was sorting through the ashes for one particular name. Her father's contract hadn't bought her brother's freedom. It had made her the balance due. Payment deferred until the last heir was found — and the hunt was the finding.

The bundle slipped in her hands. She caught it against her chest, and stood there shaking, not crying, because the shaking was bigger than crying and had nowhere to go.

* * *

She did not remember walking into the house, but she was inside it, standing in the main room where the hearthstone still bore the scorch-mark of the bread she'd burned the day Mam died, because that had been her chore and grief had made her stupid with it, and Da had eaten the burnt loaf anyway, slice after slice, saying nothing.

The room reframed itself around her, memory by memory, the way a landscape reframes when clouds move.

The year Fen "recovered" from the worst fever — he'd been nine — and their father had come home with silver for the first time in living memory, and bought a new plow horse, and everyone had said Torvald must have found a good price for his wool. He had found a price for his wool.

The year the tax-assessor came sniffing around about unregistered craft-marks, because Orsen at eight had mended a neighbor's broken well-chain with a whispered word in front of witnesses — and their father had stood in the doorway with his arms crossed and lied with such calm, such *certainty*, that the assessor had apologized and left. She'd thought it was courage. It was a receipt. He was holding a piece of paper that made him untouchable.

The way he had flinched — actually flinched, she saw it now, replayed it frame by frame — whenever a stranger asked after Fen's health. Not grief. Accounting.

And the last winter, the winter before she ran, when he had gripped her wrist at the door harder than he'd ever gripped her, hard enough to leave marks, and said, *"Never use it where anyone can hear. Never. Promise me,"* and she had promised, thinking he was afraid for her — and he had been afraid, God help her, but not only for her. He was afraid the debt would be called early. He had spent his son to buy safety and then spent seventeen years making sure the collateral didn't wander into view.

He had taught her secrecy like scripture. Every rule of hiding, every trick of being small and dull and forgettable — he'd poured them into her from the time she could talk, and she had carried them like a religion, and the religion had kept her alive for thirty years, and its founding text was a bill of sale.

She sat down on the floor because her legs were done, right there in the dust and mouse-droppings, with her back against the cold hearthstone, and laughed once, a short terrible sound with nothing in it.

The crow outside said something rude.

"I know," she told it.

* * *

Here was the thing that frightened her most, when she finally let herself look at it straight: she understood him.

That was the horror under the horror. She sat in the ruin of her father's house holding his crime in both hands and she could trace every line of his reasoning as if she'd drafted it herself. A dying child. A voice out of the dark offering a trade no priest would sanction and no father should survive considering. The arithmetic of desperation — one soul against five, the boy already half-lost, the family marked by association with an unregistered gift, the Hunt's predecessors always circling. *Sign it and they live. Don't sign it and everyone burns.* She had made smaller versions of that bargain her whole life. She had left friends behind on the wrong side of checkpoints. She had watched a caravan guard take a whipping meant for her and said nothing, because saying something meant the whipping became two.

Her father's sin was her sin scaled up past recognition, and she could not hate him cleanly, and that was somehow worse than hatred. Hatred would have been simple. This was a splinter working inward.

"You should have told me," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the man thirty years dead. "You should have told me what I was carrying."

But of course telling her would have risked everything. Telling her would have put the knowledge in her eyes where a resonance bell or a careful question might find it. Their whole lives had been built on her ignorance, brick by brick, and she had called it love, and some of it *had* been love, and there was no way now to sort the love from the ledger. That was what betrayal did when it was patient. It didn't break the past. It poisoned it, so that every sweet memory tasted different going down.

She got up. Her knees cracked. Outside, the light had shifted; she'd been on the floor longer than she knew.

There were things to do. There were always things to do; that was the mercy of the shape her life had taken. She gathered the ring and the lock of hair and wrapped them back in the oilcloth — those she would keep, those were hers, those were Mam's — but the contract she held apart, because it was not a keepsake. It was a weapon, or a key, or a fuse. She didn't yet know which. She only knew that a document bearing the seal of the Court Beneath the Mountain, in her possession, was either the most dangerous thing she'd ever carried or the most useful, and the difference would be decided by what she did next.

She fed the rest to the hearth. Old letters, her mother's recipe book, the family Bible with its births and deaths recorded in her father's careful hand — *Fenric, born; Fenric, died,* the lie preserved in ink for twenty years — everything that named this house or its people. Let the hunters find a cold ruin and cold ash and learn nothing.

The contract she sewed into the lining of her coat, flat against her spine, where a knife would find it before any hand could.

Then she stood in the dooryard a last time and looked up at the mountains to the north, at the massif the maps called the Crownstones and the old songs called something else entirely — the songs her mother had hummed while kneading bread, songs about a fire under the world, songs she'd thought were nonsense for children.

*Mam knew.* The thought landed like a dropped stone. *Mam knew what he'd done. That's why the grave-goods. That's why she's holding it.* Her mother, dying fast in the fever winter, had made her husband bury the proof with her — hidden from the house, hidden from Orsen, but kept. Kept where a daughter who loved her would someday come digging. Whether it was a message or an accusation or just a mother refusing to let her son's soul stay a secret, Orsen would never know.

"Thank you," she said to the rowan tree, hoarsely. It seemed like the right address.

* * *

Vael was waiting in the hollow below the mill race, exactly where she'd told him, exactly on time, which was one of the several things about Vael Sund that made her distrust him pleasantly. He had a fire going small and smokeless, and he looked up when she dropped down the bank, and whatever he saw in her face made him set aside the flask he'd been holding out.

"That bad?" he said.

"I need to go north." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Past the checkpoints. Past Greyford, past the tolls, all the way to the Crownstones."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the merchant calculating, and under the merchant something quicker and less safe. "Nobody goes to the Crownstones. That's not a place, that's a wall with snow on it."

"There's a court under it."

He went very still. Firelight moved along the knife at his belt. When he spoke again his voice had dropped its drawl. "Where did you hear that name?"

She touched the coat lining, the paper lying flat against her spine like a second skeleton. Twenty-two years ago her father had signed her brother away to keep his remaining children breathing, and the debt had waited, patient as the mountain, and now the collectors were burning their way across Thornfell looking for her.

"The same place everyone hears it eventually," Orsen said. "From family."

Vael looked at her for a long moment — the look of a man deciding how much he's willing to know — and then he kicked dirt over the fire and stood.

"North, then," he said. "I know a road. But Orsen—" he paused, and for once didn't smile, "—the people who own that road don't sell passage. They trade. You'd better know what you're carrying before we're anywhere near their tollgates."

She thought of Fen, eleven years old, signing himself away in ink he couldn't read. She thought of her father's hand pressing the bundle down into the coffin, hiding the debt in the one place love would eventually dig.

"I'm starting to," she said.
