## 7. The Deep Road

**Orsen**

The entrance to the deep roads was a dead man's chimney, and Vael knocked on it the way you'd knock on a door.

Orsen crouched in the bracken above the ravine and watched him do it — three raps, a pause, two more, then the flat of his palm dragged once down wet stone. The cliff face gave no answer she could hear. But Vael waited with the patience of a man who had done this many times, rain running off the brim of his hat, and after a long minute a section of rock that had looked like every other section of rock swung inward on hinges she couldn't see.

"You're staring," he said without turning around. "I can feel it."

"I'm deciding whether to trust you."

"Decide faster. It's cold." He ducked through the gap, and after one last look at the grey sky over Thornfell — the last sky she'd see for a while, maybe ever — Orsen followed him into the mountain.

The passage behind the door was narrow enough that her shoulders brushed both walls, and it smelled of cold iron and old water. Somewhere ahead, faint as memory, was light.

"How far down?" she asked.

"Deep enough that witch-hunters don't bother looking. They think the gifted run *away* from things." Vael's voice came back to her out of the dark, half-amused. "Nobody runs deeper. That's the whole trick of this place."

The tunnel began to descend in earnest, spiraling like the inside of a shell. Stairs had been cut into the stone centuries ago by hands working without mortar or measurement, each step worn to a shallow bowl by feet. Lantern hooks studded the walls at intervals, empty now, but their brackets were ornate — worked iron shaped into leaves and wings, the craftsmanship of people who'd expected to live here a long time.

"Who built this?" she asked.

"Depends who you ask. Smugglers say smugglers. There's an old woman down there who says the first swordspeakers cut these roads when they were fleeing something worse than witch-hunters." He glanced back at her, lantern-light catching the edge of his jaw. "She'll want to look at your hands, you know."

Orsen's fingers curled inward before she could stop them. Thirty years of habit, thirty years of gloves and wrapped palms and never letting anyone see the fine silver tracery that climbed her knuckles like frost made permanent.

"I don't show my hands to strangers."

"She's not a stranger. Not to what you are." Vael stopped at a bend where the stair opened into a wider gallery, and turned to face her fully. In the low light his face lost its usual sardonic polish and became simply a man's face, tired and careful. "Orsen. I've moved contraband for twenty years. Salt, silk, unlicensed steel, fugitives. I know what people hide and I know how they carry it. You carry your hands like they're evidence."

"And if they are?"

"Then there's nowhere safer in Thornfell than where we're going. And nowhere more dangerous, if you're wrong about me." He held out his hand, palm up — an offer, not a demand. "So. Are you wrong about me?"

She looked at his hand for a long moment. Then she took it, and let him pull her down into the dark.

* * *

**Vael**

The cavern called Lamplight Hollow announced itself first by warmth, then by sound — water dripping somewhere vast, voices echoing soft and overlapping, and beneath it all a low hum Vael had never been able to explain, like the mountain itself murmuring in its sleep.

Then the light. Hundreds of lanterns, hundreds, hung on wires and chains and fishing line across a cavern so large its ceiling vanished into black. Beneath them: terraces of rough dwellings carved into the living rock, gardens under glass skylights that shouldn't have worked this deep, a market street of canvas stalls selling bread and boots and bottled fireflies. Children ran between the stalls. That was the part that always caught him. Children, here, laughing, as if the world above hadn't gone mad.

Orsen stopped dead at the top of the final stair.

"This is—" She didn't finish. Her eyes were moving the way they did when she read terrain, fast and cataloguing, but what she was reading now wasn't ground. It was people. A man mending a net whose forearms bore the same silver markings she hid under her sleeves. A girl of perhaps twelve juggling small stones that hung in the air between her palms a half-second longer than gravity allowed. An old man asleep in a chair, and even sleeping, his lips moved constantly, shaping silent words.

"You knew," Orsen said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I knew there were people down here. I've carried messages and medicine for years. I didn't ask what they were until recently." Vael watched her watch them. "They pay well and they don't talk to surface guilds. That's all a smuggler needs to know. Until it isn't."

A woman detached herself from a stall of dyed wool and approached — broad-shouldered, missing two fingers on her left hand, a scar across her throat old enough to have gone white. She looked at Orsen the way Vael had seen buyers look at a blade they intended to test.

"You brought a swordspeaker into my hall, Vael Ironhand." Her voice was gravel poured slow.

"I brought *Orsen Nystrom*, Maren. Which is either the same thing or worse, depending on the week."

Maren's eyes narrowed. "Nystrom." She said the name like she was tasting it. Then, louder, pitched to carry: "Greve! Greve, come see what walked in off the stair."

The old woman who emerged from the terraces moved like a much younger person pretending otherwise — quick, deliberate, wrapped in layers of grey shawls despite the cavern's warmth. Her eyes were milk-pale, clouded blind, and yet they fixed on Orsen with unnerving precision, stopping a hand's width short of her face.

"Give me your hands, child," the old woman said.

"Maren—" Vael started.

"Hush. This is none of yours yet." Greve's fingers found Orsen's wrists without waiting for permission, turned her palms up, and went still.

Vael had seen Orsen's oath-marks once, by accident, at a river crossing three years ago — a flash of silver when her glove slipped, gone before he could be sure. Now they were bared to a whole cavern: the fine luminous tracery running from her fingertips up past her wrists, branching like lightning frozen mid-strike, densest across the backs of her hands where the skin had gone faintly translucent with age and use.

Greve traced one branch with a fingertip, following it up, muttering. Then she went very quiet, and the quiet spread outward through the nearest stalls like oil on water, until the whole market seemed to be holding its breath.

"Ninth mark," the old woman said at last. "Nine founding oaths in the bloodline. Do you know how rare—no. Of course you don't. They burned the records." Her blind eyes lifted. "You are Nystrom of the Thorn line. Daughter of Aldric, who signed away more than he understood. Sister to Fen, who has been below longer than you've been hiding."

The world tilted.

Orsen didn't sway — Vael would remember that later, how she didn't sway, how she stood absolutely rigid while her face went the color of the ash at Greyford mill. But her voice, when it came, was barely air.

"My brother is dead. He died when we were children. Fever."

"Fever." Greve smiled, and it was the saddest thing Vael had seen in years. "Is that what they told you? Child, nothing dies down here that the court hasn't finished using. Come and sit. There is a great deal you were never meant to learn, and less time to learn it than any of us hoped."

* * *

**Orsen**

They sat in Greve's dwelling, a hollow room hung with dried herbs and old maps, and the old woman talked while Orsen's brother's name rang in her skull like a struck bell that wouldn't fade.

Fen. Alive. Below. The words refused to arrange themselves into sense.

"There are four hundred of us down here," Maren said from the doorway, arms crossed. "Maybe four hundred. Gifted, or descended from gifted, or married to both. We came down in waves. After the Purges under old King Maegar. After the Tithe Laws. After last spring." Her jaw tightened. "After the mill."

"The mill at Harrowbrook?" Orsen asked. "I heard it was grain fires."

"It was a family of ember-speakers," Maren said flatly. "Seven of them. The youngest was six." She let that sit. "We get survivors down the stair sometimes. Mostly we get names. People who almost made it."

Greve had taken Orsen's hands again, and was reading them aloud like a merchant inventorying stock — not the marks themselves, but what they meant. "First mark: the bridge-oath, sworn young, before you understood what swearing cost. Second: a warding, laid on someone you loved. Third and fourth: combat-oaths, used sparingly, paid for dearly." Her thumb pressed a cluster of dense silver at the heel of Orsen's palm. "These five, though. These five are strange. These are *inherited*. Sworn by others, generations back, still living in your blood. Your family has been paying interest on debts for nine generations, child."

"What debts?"

"The kind the Court Beneath collects." Greve released her hands and sat back. "You thought your gift came from you. From your voice, your will. It doesn't. No swordspeaker's does. Every word you've ever bound into steel was borrowed — drawn up from the dragon-fire under this mountain, through channels your ancestors dug with their oaths. You've been spending coin you never minted."

Orsen looked at her own marked hands. All those years she'd thought the exhaustion afterward was the price of power — her power, spent. Now it sounded different. Rent, paid on a house she'd never seen, owned by landlords she'd never met.

"And Fen? What does the court want with a boy of ten?"

"The same thing it wants with everyone it takes." It was Maren who answered, and her voice had gone careful in a way that made Orsen's skin prickle. "Fuel. The fire under the mountain isn't just old, it's *dying*, and the magistrates keep it fed. They've been feeding it for centuries. Souls. Years of life. Whole bloodlines, sold or seized." She paused. "Your father's contract bought your family twenty years of being overlooked. Payment was deferred, the paper said, until the last gifted heir was found."

"Found," Orsen repeated. The word tasted like iron. "Not born. Found. Like something being hunted."

"Now you understand why the Hunt worries us," Maren said. "It isn't zealotry. Zealots burn you and move on. The witch-hunters are *collecting*. They take the gifted alive when they can, and what they bring back to their masters—" She stopped herself, glancing at Greve.

But Greve only nodded slowly, her blind face turned toward some inner sight. "And they're close to finishing. We've counted. Eleven taken from the deep roads' edges this year alone. Our scouts, our runners, our children going to the surface for seed-grain and not coming back. They aren't hunting blindly anymore, child. Someone is giving them our map."

Silence filled the room. Above, distantly, the hum of the mountain seemed to shift pitch — or perhaps Orsen only imagined it, hearing everything differently now.

"They took a runner two nights ago," Maren said finally. "A girl named Sefa. Sixteen. Fire-gift, minor, could warm a kettle and light a lamp. The hunters took her off the north stair." Her eyes fixed on Orsen, hard and bright. "If they follow her trail home, they find four hundred people who trusted these tunnels to keep them safe. Four hundred."

Vael shifted beside Orsen — she felt it more than saw it, the way the whole chair seemed to gather itself. "You want me to go back up. Tonight. Find out which route the hunters used."

"I want you to do what you do," Maren said. "Move quietly, learn quickly, and tell no one."

"And her?" Vael tipped his head toward Orsen. "Because I'm guessing you didn't summon the ninth-mark heir of the Thorn line down here just to serve her soup."

Greve laughed — a dry rustle, like wind through winter reeds. "No. Not just for soup." The old woman leaned forward and took Orsen's marked hands one final time, cradling them like something breakable and priceless at once. "The fire under this mountain is guttering, child. I have heard it change since summer. And everything the court has stolen to feed it — including your brother — is sitting in a vault beneath the flame, waiting for someone with the right voice to open the wrong door."

Orsen's heart was beating somewhere high in her throat. "What door?"

"The archive of oaths," Greve said. "Where your family's contract is kept. Where the first covenant is kept. Where the unbinding word is written down — the one every swordspeaker since the beginning has been too afraid to say." Her blind eyes held Orsen's, and though they saw nothing, Orsen had never in her life been so thoroughly *seen*. "You didn't come down here for your brother, whatever you told yourself. Some part of you already knew what was under this mountain. So I will ask plainly, ninth-mark, and you may hate me for it:

"When the time comes — will you speak it?"

Before Orsen could answer, the lanterns overhead flickered — all of them, at once, hundreds of flames bowing in a wind that did not exist. The mountain's hum stuttered, caught, resumed a half-tone lower.

In the silence that followed, somewhere far above them, faint through miles of stone, Orsen heard — or dreamed she heard — a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat.

A bell, tolling at the surface. Witch-hunters, on the north road.

Coming down.
