## 8. The Bell in the Dark

The lanterns of the deep roads burned green.

Orsen had noticed it her first night below — a strange, swamp-colored light that came from the oil the exiles rendered from cave moss, and she had decided it suited them. A people who could not live in daylight ought not pretend to it. Now, three days into her stay in the cavern settlement they called Lanthorn, she had learned to read those lights the way she'd once read stars: which galleries were waking, which were sleeping, where the water dripped and where the silence pooled too thick.

She was learning names, too. Maren, who kept the moss-farms and hummed to the spore-beds in a voice that made them grow crooked and sweet. Dallit, a boy of maybe twelve whose gift was small and useless on the surface — he could warm his own hands, nothing more — but who here was treasured for it, because down here warmth was currency. And Sef, the old woman who had read Orsen's oath-marks by lantern-light and spoken the word *Nystrom* like a bell being struck.

*The last swordspeaker,* Sef had said. Not a question. A counting.

Orsen turned the phrase over as she walked the gallery toward the moss-farms. Last. She had spent thirty years trying to make the word untrue by simply refusing to be found, and now she walked among two hundred people who knew what she was and had not reached for torches. It should have felt like safety. Instead it felt like standing in a dry riverbed, watching clouds gather upstream.

She heard the humming before she saw Maren — a low, wandering tune, and beneath it the faint rustle of spore-fronds leaning toward the sound like wheat toward sun. Maren stood ankle-deep in the growing troughs, sleeves rolled, gray braid swinging, coaxing the morning's crop out of the dark.

"You're up early," Maren said without turning. "For someone who claims she's just passing through."

"I'm always up early."

"Mm. Runners are always up early." Maren glanced back at her, and her humor faded into something gentler. "Sef wants you at the hearth-gallery when the second bell rings. She won't say why. She's been grinding pigments since before I woke, and she grinds pigments when she's frightened."

"Frightened of what?"

"Of being right." Maren went back to her humming, but the tune had lost its ease.

Orsen stood a moment longer among the leaning fronds. The air in the farms was wet and alive and smelled of loam, and it struck her — not for the first time, but harder this time — how much care had been poured into this place. Terraced galleries. Filtered water. A schoolroom carved into a side chamber where Dallit was teaching smaller children letters by the light of glowstones. Two hundred people who had been hunted off the face of the earth and had answered by building a garden underground.

They were being picked off one by one. Sef had told her that the night before, in the flat voice of someone reporting weather. A tithe-collector here. A vanished family there. The roads between Lanthorn and the other settlements going quiet, one after another, like lamps blown out along a corridor.

And no one could say who was taking them, or why.

The second bell rang — a hollow bronze note that traveled the galleries like a fish through still water. Orsen made her way to the hearth-gallery, where Sef sat behind a table of ground ochre and ash, drawing lines on a slate: the deep roads rendered as a skeleton, branching and branching, thinning toward the north.

"You've been counting," Orsen said.

"I have been counting." Sef tapped the slate. "Forty-one gifted gone from the under-roads in two years. Not fled — gone. No bodies, no farewells. And in the last month, the disappearances have stopped." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible with certainty. "They stopped because they finished collecting what they needed."

"Needed for what?"

"I don't know. But I know what comes next." Sef set down the chalk. "When a hunter wants to find something hidden in stone, he doesn't search. He listens. And the best listening-stone in the world is a gifted soul, if you bend it far enough." She let that sit. "They're coming down. They're using our own people as divining rods."

Orsen felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs. "How long do we have?"

"If I knew, I'd be a richer woman." Sef began wiping the slate with her sleeve, erasing the skeleton of the roads. "We scatter tonight. The northern galleries first, then the rest. Tell no one the whole plan — each person gets their own road and no more. That's how we've survived thirty years, and it's how we'll survive this."

"And you?" Orsen asked. "You'll scatter too?"

Sef smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth as river stone. "I'm the one who counts, child. Counters stay until the end."

The scattering began at what passed for dusk — a scheduled dimming of the great lanterns, observed so faithfully that Orsen thought of it as sunset even though no sun had ever touched this place. Families moved through the galleries with bundles already packed; they had practiced this, she realized. They had drills. They had rendezvous-caves and fallback routes and hand-signals, all of it built by people who had learned, generation by generation, that safety was a thing you rehearsed.

She was assigned to the western road with Maren, Dallit, and eleven others. Vael was meant to take the southern route alone — his guild contacts gave him options the exiles didn't have — and she'd said goodbye to him an hour ago with a grip on the forearm that lasted a beat too long and neither of them commenting on it.

*"Come find me,"* he'd said. Not a question either. This place was full of things nobody would say outright.

The western road was a tall, narrow gallery that followed the bones of some ancient aqueduct, its vaulting cracked and patched with centuries of smuggler masonry. They moved in single file, boots wrapped in fleece, lanterns hooded to slits of green. Dallit walked ahead of Orsen, and every few minutes the boy would pause, hold up his warmed palm, and read the air temperature against the stone — his small useless gift, doing real work. Warmth meant living rock ahead. Cold meant a break, a shaft, an opening to somewhere else.

Two hours in, Dallit stopped so suddenly that Maren nearly walked into him.

"Cold," the boy whispered. "But wrong-cold. It's moving."

Orsen eased forward beside him. The air ahead did feel different — not the dead chill of a shaft but a draft with texture to it, carrying something she couldn't name. Then she named it.

Bell-metal. Faint, rhythmic, almost subliminal. And underneath it, threaded through the resonance like a hair through wax, something else. A vibration she knew in her teeth, in the old scars along her forearms where oath-marks lived.

A voice. Many voices. Gifted voices, singing — or being made to sing.

"They're close," she breathed. "Behind us. How far back is the junction?"

"A quarter hour," Maren whispered. Her face had gone the color of tallow. "Maybe less if we run."

"We can't run. They'll hear the feet." Orsen looked back down the gallery, into the layered darkness, and strained to separate the sounds. The bell tolled slow and patient, a hunter's instrument, and each toll pulled a thread of song out of the dark — and each thread of song bent slightly, like a compass needle, toward *them*. Toward the warmth of living gifts in a cold tunnel. She could feel it herself now, the way you feel a stare between your shoulder blades. Whatever the captured singers were doing, it wasn't tracking individuals. It was mapping resonance — and hers rang loudest of anyone in the file. Thirty years of silence, and in the presence of that bell she might as well have been shouting her name.

"Sef was right," Maren whispered. "Oh, gods. They really are using—"

"Move," Orsen said. "Quietly. Junction. Now."

They moved. Fifteen people gliding through the dark with the discipline of the long-hunted, and behind them the bell tolled on, and each toll came a little louder, a little clearer, and the singing threads tightened like a net being drawn.

At the junction — a vaulted crossing where four galleries met around a dry cistern — Dallit held up his palm again and shook his head. "All cold," he whispered. "Every direction. Something's open everywhere. That's not natural."

It wasn't. Orsen understood then, with a clarity that arrived like ice water, what the hunters had done. They hadn't followed one road. They'd opened the ways — breached the sealed galleries, cracked the doors the exiles had spent decades hiding — and now they were herding. Every cold draft was a gap, and gaps meant choices, and choices meant panic, and panicked people ran into the open.

The bell tolled again, and this time it was near enough that she could hear the overtone structure of the thing: a great bronze bell, rung not by clapper but by a resonant hum maintained by — she counted the threads — at least six gifted voices, stretched and flattened and bent until they barely sounded human anymore.

Her stomach turned over. She knew what bending did. She had felt the cost of her own small bindings — the nosebleeds, the tremors, the days of aching silence after a single word. What must it take to hold six souls in a chord for hours?

"There," Maren breathed, pointing east. "The cistern road. It's warm—"

"It's supposed to be warm," Dallit said miserably. "That's the trap road. Everyone knows the cistern road is the obvious one."

He was right, and everyone knew he was right, and yet the file was already drifting toward it because fear makes obvious roads irresistible. Orsen raised her hand to stop them —

And the bell tolled directly behind them.

Not near. *There.* At the mouth of the western gallery, fifty yards back, close enough that the green slit-lanterns threw trembling light on wet stone, and in that light she saw them.

Witch-hunters. Six of them in ash-gray mail, faces masked, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the dark they stood in. And behind them, carried in a lattice frame like a relic, hung the bell itself — taller than a man, black bronze covered in script Orsen's blood recognized before her mind did, because the same script curled through her own oath-marks. Swordspeaker script. Old covenant-work, stolen or inherited, put to this.

And beneath the bell, in cages of iron and wire, the singers.

Six of them. Orsen's eyes found them and she would have given anything not to see. They knelt with their heads bound in resonance-crowns — circlets of the same black bronze, wired back to the bell — and their mouths moved in unison, and their eyes were open and empty as winter ponds. One of them was young. One of them was Dallit's age, or near it, and her small hands pressed flat against the cage floor as though pushing against a weight only she could feel.

The boy made a sound. Small, involuntary, a hurt animal sound, and in the ringing aftermath of the bell it carried.

The lead hunter's masked face turned toward the junction.

"Toward the cistern road!" Maren hissed. "Go, go—"

"No." The word left Orsen before she knew she was speaking it, and it came out with the old weight in it, the weight she had spent thirty years strangling, and three people stumbled because her voice *pushed*. She swallowed hard. "No. It's a killing-ground. We go up."

"There's nothing up," Dallit whispered. "The shaft's sealed. It's been sealed for sixty years."

"The seal is a door, and doors are words." Orsen was already moving to the shaft-mouth in the junction's ceiling, hauling herself up the rusted rungs, pressing her palm to the stone plug that closed it. Old mortar. Old work. Beneath her hand she felt the faint grain of a binding laid into it decades ago by some exile mason — a small, careful holding-word, the kind her father's generation had used without shame.

The bell tolled again. Closer. Boots now, unhurried, echoing down the gallery.

"Orsen." Maren had come to the foot of the rungs, and her voice shook. "Whatever you're thinking — there are fifteen of us and six of them and we are *not fighters*—"

"I know." Orsen closed her eyes. She felt her heartbeat in her scars, in every mark her mother had inked and every mark life had added since. She thought about the bridge on Thornfell pass, the crumbling span she had whispered upright for a salt caravan, and the two days of silence that had cost her. She thought about a village called Millbrook that was now a smell of ash on the wind. She thought about six kneeling figures wearing crowns of stolen covenant-bronze.

Thirty years she had kept her voice folded away like a blade in oiled cloth, telling herself silence was survival, silence was wisdom, silence was the price of staying human. And the whole time the world had been getting darker anyway, one burned village at a time, because people like her kept paying silence instead of spending themselves.

Terror was still in her — she could taste it, copper and cold — but somewhere in the last minute it had changed shape. It had stopped being the terror of a hunted woman and become something with edges. Something she could hold.

Defiance, she thought. So this is what you feel like.

"Get everyone into the cistern basin," she said quietly. "All the way down. Cover your ears. Don't look up, whatever you hear."

"Orsen, what are you—"

"*Now*, Maren."

Something in her voice — that pushed quality again, the weight that moved stone — sent them scrambling. Orsen climbed the last rungs, wedged herself against the shaft wall, and laid both palms flat against the ancient seal.

She spoke aloud.

In the deep roads, in front of witnesses, for the first time in thirty years, she used the full voice — not a whisper, not a half-word mumbled into mortar, but the true speech, the speech that had once moved armies' worth of stone in the founding wars, the speech written into her bones before she was born.

"*Stone that sleeps, I name your binding. Stone that holds, I release your keeping.*"

The seal groaned. Mortar dust rained past her shoulders. Somewhere below, the exiles flinched at the sound of her voice — she heard Dallit gasp — because it didn't sound like a woman speaking. It sounded like the mountain remembering language.

But she wasn't done. Releasing the seal bought them a way up; it didn't buy them time. The hunters were two hundred yards away and closing, and the bell would sing their location to every gallery in reach. Unless there was no gallery in reach.

Unless the road itself ceased to be.

Orsen looked back down the western gallery, at the approaching lantern-light and the lattice-frame bell swaying above the hunters' heads, at the six bent souls singing inside their cages — and grief and fury rose in her together, indistinguishable, and she let them.

"*Road of my kin, road of the hidden,*" she said, and her voice filled the junction like water filling a hull, "*I speak your ending. Fall kindly. Fall complete. Let no foot follow.*"

The mountain answered.

It was not an explosion. It was worse than that — it was deliberate, obedient, vast. The western gallery drew a breath of absolute silence, and then the vaulted stones of its ceiling let go of each other, section by section, in a rolling cascade that marched away from her down the corridor like a wave closing over a wake. Dust erupted in a wall. The bell's toll shattered mid-note into a clangor of falling bronze. Somewhere in the roar she heard masked men shouting, heard iron scraping stone, heard — she would carry this sound to her grave — the singing stop. All six voices cut off at once, whether freed or buried she could not know, and the not-knowing was a knife she accepted because there had been no other path.

Then the dust took everything, and the world was white, and Orsen hung against the shaft wall coughing blood into her sleeve — the cost, always the cost, her body settling accounts — while below her fifteen exiles stood frozen in the cistern basin, staring up through the settling haze at the silhouette of a woman wreathed in stone-dust and lamplight, blood on her lips, oath-marks blazing along her forearms with a light none of them had ever seen outside of stories.

The seal above her crumbled away into open air. Cold surface-night poured down the shaft, smelling of rain and pine and a world she hadn't dared touch in years.

"Up," Orsen rasped, and her voice was ruined silk, barely hers at all. "Everyone up. Climb."

They climbed. Maren came last, hauling Dallit ahead of her, and as she passed Orsen in the shaft she caught her arm and looked at her — really looked, at the marks, at the blood, at all of it — and said nothing, which was its own kind of oath.

They came out of the earth into a night full of stars, into pine forest on a ridge Orsen half recognized from a lifetime ago, and behind them the mountainside showed a faint tremor of settling dust, a scar where a road had been. Fifteen exiles stood blinking in starlight like creatures surfacing from legend.

Dallit stared at her, and his voice cracked. "You collapsed a whole gallery. With a *word*." His eyes were huge. "Who *are* you?"

Orsen wiped the blood from her mouth and looked south, toward the deep roads, toward Lanthorn, toward Sef and her slate of vanishing names. The bell was silent. The hunters were buried or retreating. But they would come again — they always came again — and now they knew exactly what was walking around down there wearing a swordspeaker's skin.

She thought of the black script on the bell. Covenant-work. Court-work. Someone above or below had armed the hunt with her own inheritance, and that meant someone knew what she was long before tonight.

"Sef needs to see me," she said. "Before dawn. There's something the elders haven't told me about where that bell came from — and I think they know exactly what the court beneath the mountain is doing with the people it takes."

Maren exchanged a look with her. "How can you know that?"

"Because," Orsen said, tasting copper and starlight and thirty years of silence finally broken, "that bell was singing in my family's language."
