## 9. Oathbound

The elder's name was Sefa, and she poured tea like a woman who had outlived everyone she'd ever learned to pour for.

Orsen sat on a low stool in the lantern-lit hollow the exiles called the Warming Room, her hands still trembling from the tunnel collapse. The rock dust had settled into every crease of her skin; when she flexed her fingers, grit fell like snow. Around her, perhaps forty people moved through the cavern with the quiet economy of those who had learned to live small — mending nets over fish-farms lit by glowmoss, boiling water over vents of warm stone, teaching children letters scratched in slate because paper was too loud to burn.

Vael stood by the entrance with his arms folded, pretending to watch the passage. He'd barely spoken since the collapse. Since she'd spoken.

"You used it," he'd said once, on the walk down from the broken tunnel, his voice stripped of its usual silk. "Out loud. Where anyone could hear."

"There wasn't anyone left to hear," she'd said. "That was rather the point."

Now there were forty someones, and they kept glancing at her between tasks, quick and furtive, the way people looked at a fire that might warm them or eat them.

Sefa set a clay cup in Orsen's hands. The tea was bitter, root-heavy, the kind smugglers brewed to keep awake through long dark miles. Orsen drank it anyway. Her body ached in the old familiar places — behind the sternum where the oath-words lived, down both forearms where her oath-marks ran like veins of ink beneath the skin. Every use cost her. She'd known that since she was nine years old and spoke her first word to keep a falling beam from crushing Fen, and spent three days after unable to stand.

But today's cost sat differently in her. Today she had spoken at full voice, unmasked, before witnesses, and the toll had been more than muscle-deep. Something had *drained*. Something she couldn't name.

"Your marks are Thornfell stock," Sefa said, settling onto a bench across from her. The old woman's face was a map of fine creases, her eyes pale as river ice. "I can see it even under the ash. The double spiral at the wrist. The bridge-oath scar along the forearm." She reached out, and Orsen — against every instinct she'd honed over thirty years — let her take her hand and turn it palm-up. "And here." Her thumb traced the base of Orsen's ring finger, where a mark lay so faint most readers would miss it. "The founding line."

"What does that mean?" Vael asked from the doorway. He hadn't moved, but his voice had sharpened.

"It means," Sefa said, not looking at him, "that this woman descends from the first speaker. From the one who made the covenant." She released Orsen's hand and leaned back. "Which means you already know what I'm about to tell you. You just don't know you know it."

* * *

"They teach you that your gift comes from your blood," Sefa said. "That swordspeakers are born, the way smiths' sons are born to the forge. That oaths are a trick of lineage, passed mother to daughter like eye color." She snorted. "Convenient story. Keeps speakers proud and keeps them blind."

She gestured, and two of the exiles rolled back a woven screen at the far end of the chamber. Behind it, the cavern wall opened into a natural chimney of stone — a vertical shaft rising beyond the reach of the lanterns, its walls carved floor to darkness with spirals of script. Old script. Oath-script, but older than any dialect Orsen knew, the characters flowing into one another like water finding its level.

"The deep roads run under all of Thornfell," Sefa said. "Smugglers think they built them. Smugglers found them, is what happened. They were cut before there were kingdoms up top. And at the bottom of everything, deeper than any road we've mapped, there is a fire."

"A fire," Vael repeated flatly.

"Dragonfire. The last of it. A heart of flame older than the mountain around it, older than the crown, older than the names on your coinage. It burns in a sealed vault at the roots of the world, and it is the only thing standing between the living lands and what sleeps beneath it." Sefa's voice didn't rise or fall. She said it the way she might report the price of salt. "The dark below isn't weather or war or plague, boy. It doesn't conquer. It *unmakes*. It got loose once, in the first age, and it took the first dragons to cage it — their fire, poured out and bound into stone, burning without end."

"And the gift," Orsen said. Her throat was dry. The bitter tea did nothing for it.

"The gift flows from that fire. All of it. Every swordspeaker who ever lived drew their power up through the stone like water through a root. The oaths aren't yours, child. They're *borrowed*. When you speak a word and a bridge holds, it holds because somewhere far below, an ancient flame leans toward your voice and does what you ask. Your bloodline matters only because the first speaker wove a covenant into the fire itself — gave it shape, gave it law, taught it to answer human voices. Her descendants speak loudest, because her covenant knows their sound." Sefa smiled, and it was a terrible thing, gentle and grieving at once. "You've been praying your whole life, Orsen Nystrom. You just never knew to whom."

Orsen looked at her hands. The marks on her forearms seemed suddenly less like inheritance and more like *wiring* — channels cut into her by something vast and patient, running all the way down into the dark.

"And the court," she said. "The Court Beneath. What do they have to do with it?"

The room went quiet. Even the children had stopped scratching their slates.

"Ah," Sefa said softly. "So you've heard the name."

"I read it on a contract." Orsen kept her voice level. It took work. "My father sold my brother's soul to them twenty years ago. Payment deferred until—" She stopped.

"Until the last gifted heir was found." Sefa nodded slowly. "Yes. That's how they write them. They always write them that way."

"Tell me," Orsen said.

* * *

Sefa told her.

She told it in pieces, the way old women tell terrible things — circling, arriving sideways, letting the listener assemble the horror herself so it sits in the chest instead of washing past the ears.

The dragonfire, she said, was dying. Had been dying for centuries. Fire needs fuel, even sacred fire, even caged fire, and the dragons who made it were long dead. For a thousand years the mountain had held because the fire burned slow and deep, a coal the size of a cathedral. But coals go out. And the seals woven around the dark below were tied to the fire's strength — as the flame shrank, the seals thinned.

"So the first kings of the Undercourt faced a choice," Sefa said. "Let the world end slowly, honestly — or feed the fire something it could burn."

"Wood doesn't burn in dragonfire," Orsen said quietly. "I know that much. Nothing mortal does."

"No," Sefa agreed. "Nothing *mortal*. But souls aren't wood, child. Souls are what the fire was made *from*. Dragonhearts, given freely, in the first age. The fire remembers that shape. It will burn a soul the way a starving man will eat leather off his own boot — not well, not rightly, but it will burn, and it buys time." She met Orsen's eyes. "Every gifted soul the witch-hunters catch, every registration list, every village burned to flush the talented out of hiding — you thought that was fear? Fear is real enough, but it's the *tool*. The hunt is a harvest. The court has been buying souls of the gifted for four hundred years to shovel into a dying god's hearth."

The tea in Orsen's cup had gone cold. She didn't remember holding it. Somewhere behind her sternum, the drained feeling from the tunnel collapse had taken on a new and awful meaning: she was a straw stuck into the same reservoir the court was draining bucket by bucket. Her power, her whole life's secret economy of whispered words and aching days, drawn from a fire being fed her own kind.

"Fen," she said. "My brother. Why would they want his soul? He's not gifted. Our father—"

"Your father sold him *because* he wasn't gifted," Sefa said. "A gifted soul burns fast — too much channel already carved into it. An ordinary soul from a speaker's bloodline burns slow. Steady. Twenty years of slow." Her pale eyes didn't flinch. "If they took him whole, he'll be in the vault city. The Undercourt keeps its tithes alive as long as they can. A soul feeds better while the body still breathes."

Orsen heard Vael move behind her — a shift of weight, a half-step toward her that stopped short, because he didn't yet know what to do with what she was. She was glad, distantly, that he didn't touch her. If anyone touched her right now she would come apart like wet rope.

* * *

"But here is the part," Sefa said, and now her voice dropped, and the exiles in the room leaned in as if they'd heard this next part many times and it never lost its teeth, "here is the part that should break you open, founding-line."

She rose and walked to the carved shaft, laid her hand flat against the ancient script.

"The covenant the first speaker wrote into the fire — it has clauses. Laws. The fire obeys speech; therefore speech governs the fire. And the highest clause, the one written before all others, is this: *no binding made in grief shall hold forever, for the fire honors freedom above fuel.*" She turned. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," Orsen said.

"The souls in that fire were stolen. Bought. Taken by contract and coercion. They were never given freely, and the fire knows it — it chokes on them, it always has, and that is part of why it dies faster than it should. And the covenant says that a binding can be *unmade*. By the founding line. By the voice of the first speaker's blood, speaking the unbinding word." Sefa's hand slid down the stone. "One word. One word, spoken truly, breaks every stolen bond in the fire at once. Every soul the court has fed it for four hundred years tears free and goes home."

For one heartbeat — one bright, weightless heartbeat — Orsen felt it like sunrise. Fen. Not freed by blade or bargain but *unbound*, released by law older than the court's oldest lie, flying home through the roots of the mountain—

"And," Sefa said, "the fire goes out."

The word landed like a hammer.

"The souls are the only fuel it has left," Sefa went on, merciless as winter. "Take them away, and what remains cannot hold the seals. The dark comes up through the mountain within a season. Maybe within a month. Every soul saved, and everything else lost." She came back to her bench and sat, folding her hands. "Unless."

"Unless," Orsen echoed.

"Unless the fire is refueled. There is one other fuel the covenant permits. One gift the fire accepts freely, gladly, the way it accepted the dragonhearts in the first age." Sefa's voice went very quiet. "A swordspeaker's own oath. The founding bond itself — the covenant in your blood, the thing that lets you speak at all. Break your life-oath willingly, freely, in the fire's presence, and give it the breaking. It would burn steady for another age. Long past your grandchildren's grandchildren."

"And the cost," Vael said from the doorway. His voice was rough. "There's a cost. Name it."

"The gift," Orsen said.

She said it before Sefa could. She knew it the way you know a wound is deep before you see the blood — some things the body understands ahead of the mind.

"The gift," Sefa confirmed. "All of it. Forever. No words, no oaths, no marks. You'd be mute to the fire until you die. Just a woman. Just a voice like anyone's voice." She paused. "And the unbinding word and the life-oath are the same word, child. That's the cruelty of it. To free your brother, you must break yourself. To save the world, you must surrender the only tool that saves it. Both, together, or neither. The covenant does not bargain."

* * *

The Warming Room emptied slowly after that. Exiles drifted back to their nets and kettles, though several paused near Orsen on the way — a hand briefly on her shoulder, a bow, one young man pressing a strip of dried fish into her hand like an offering at a shrine. Word had spread through the cavern in the way word spreads among the hunted: *she's founding-line. She's the one.*

The weight of it settled on her like wet wool.

She stood at the carved shaft afterward, alone except for Vael, reading the old script without understanding it. Her reflection ghosted faintly in the polished stone — a thin woman with ash in her hair and oath-marks running down her arms like cracks in porcelain.

"My father," she said. "He knew. Didn't he. That's what the deferred payment meant. He wasn't selling Fen to save us. He was saving us *for* them — keeping the bloodline alive, keeping me hidden, until the court needed the last heir." Her laugh came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "Thirty years I thought I was hiding from the hunt. I was being *ripened*."

"Orsen." Vael's voice behind her. Close. He'd finally crossed the room.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "I mean it. If you're kind to me right now I will sit down on this floor and I will not get up."

He stopped. She heard him breathing. Then, instead of comfort, he said, in the flat merchant tone he used for numbers: "Then don't decide tonight. Decide tomorrow. But while you're deciding, know this — whatever you choose, I know a way into the Undercourt. Cost me everything I have and every friend I've got, but I know it. So the choosing isn't whether you can get to your brother. It's only whether you can live with what it costs."

She turned. He stood in the lantern light with his jaw set and his eyes giving him away entirely, and she understood that he had already done the arithmetic she couldn't face — had already decided, silently, in the dark of the walk down — that if she went into the mountain, he was going in with her.

"Sefa," she said instead, raising her voice toward the old woman across the room. "The unbinding word. Does anyone alive know it?"

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached into her robe and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, worn soft as skin with handling, and held it out.

"Not alive," she said. "But the first speaker left her archive under the mountain. And I have been keeping something for sixty years that I swore I'd only give to founding-line." Her pale eyes glittered. "It's a key, child. Whether it opens the door to salvation or the door to the end — that, no one has ever lived to tell."

Orsen took the bundle. Through the cloth, she felt the shape of a tablet — stone, old, humming faintly against her palms like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Somewhere far below her feet, deeper than the deep roads, a fire the size of a cathedral ate a stolen soul and guttered, and waited.
