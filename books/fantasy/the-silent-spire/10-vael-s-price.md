## 10. Vael's Price

The deep roads smelled of wet stone and tallow, and Vael had learned to read them the way other men read faces. Tonight the stone said *rain above*, and the tallow said *too many people burning too little light*, which meant the exiles were scared, which meant the elder woman had told them something worse than what she'd told Orsen.

He found Orsen sitting apart from the others, at the lip of a side gallery where an underground stream ran black and quick over stones the color of old teeth. She was turning her mother's grave-goods over in her hands — a ring, a comb, the contract that had undone twenty years of her life in the space of one page. She'd been holding that page for two days now. He'd watched her do it the way a man watches another man press on a wound to keep from bleeding out.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I've slept thirty years." She didn't look up. "It didn't help."

Fair enough. Vael sat down on the stream bank beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, far enough that they didn't. There was a grammar to sitting next to Orsen Nystrom, and he'd spent most of a decade learning it without ever being taught. You did not touch her uninvited. You did not ask twice. You stayed anyway.

Below them the water made its small relentless argument with the rocks. Above, somewhere back through three miles of tunnel, the lantern-lit caverns of the exiled gifted glowed like embers in a banked fire — which, he supposed, was exactly what they were.

"I talked to Sera," he said.

Orsen glanced at him. "The guildmaster?"

"The guildmaster." He picked up a flat stone and turned it in his fingers. "I sent word down the smugglers' line this morning. Coded, sealed, the whole liturgy. Asking about the Undercourt route."

Her whole body went still. Not the way a person goes still when they're listening — the way a deer goes still when it scents iron. "Vael."

"There's a way in," he said. "There always is. The court beneath buys things, and things have to be carried. Tithe-bearers, soul-wagons, whatever pretty name they use. Somebody drives those wagons down, and somebody drives them up, and that somebody has been paid by the Thornfell Combine for eleven years to look the other way while our salt goes past his lantern. His name is Doss Merrow. He drinks. He owes me. And he can walk a stranger into the Undercourt wearing a tithe-bearer's hood, if the price is right."

"What's the price?"

"That's not the price," Vael said quietly. "That's just money. Money I have."

Orsen set down her mother's things, all except the contract, which she folded once, twice, and closed her fist around. "Then what's the price?"

He'd rehearsed this. That was the humiliating part. He'd stood in front of a cracked mirror of polished tin in his cell back in the lantern-caverns and rehearsed it like a boy practicing a confession, and now that the moment came, every prepared word fell off him like water off waxed canvas.

"The price is me," he said. "Doss won't move for coin alone. Not for this. Not for a route into the Undercourt. He'll want insurance. He'll want my ledger — every contact I hold in the Combine, every guild oath I carry, every favor owed to me by name and date. My whole network, signed over. And when the Combine finds out I gave away the keys to the only door they don't know exists—" He shrugged. "They'll burn my name off every safehouse between here and Greyford. They'll put my face on the same posters as yours. Every road I ever walked becomes a road hunting me."

The stream argued on. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, faint as a pulse under floorboards, he could almost believe he felt it — the dragon-fire, the thing the elder had called the first oath, the heart that beat under everything.

"Vael." Orsen's voice had gone low and careful. "No."

"Yes."

"No. You don't know what you're—"

"I know exactly what I'm doing." He heard his own voice come out harder than he intended, and he let it. Some things deserved hardness. "I've been a traitor before, Orsen. Did you think the smugglers' guilds were founded by honest men? I've sold routes to people who used them for worse than you're going to use this one. The difference is that this time I'd be selling them for something that matters, and the people I betray will be men who knew exactly what the Undercourt does with souls and chose to keep driving wagons anyway." He looked at her. "Don't you dare protect me from my own arithmetic."

She stared at him, and in the thin light of the gallery her eyes were the color of the stream — dark, quick, going somewhere he couldn't follow. Thirty years she'd survived by being unreadable. He'd watched her lie to witch-hunters with a smile, watch caravans she'd guided get taxed into ruin without blinking, bury her gift so deep even her own body seemed to forget it. But she couldn't hide from him anymore; he'd seen her speak a bridge back together. Once a man has seen the actual shape of a person, there is no unreadable.

"They'll kill you," she said.

"Probably."

"Slowly, if they're smart. Guild justice isn't clean like the crown's. It's—"

"I know how it works. I've administered it." He flipped the stone out into the stream. It skipped once, twice, sank. "Three times, before I understood what I was administering it for. A man who cheats the routes endangers everyone who runs them. That was the law I enforced. So here's my question, and I want you to actually answer it: if your brother's soul is in that fire, and the way to him runs through me, and the cost of the way is everything I am — tell me why I should keep myself."

She didn't answer. Her jaw worked once, twice.

"You can't," he said gently. "And you know you can't, and that's why you're angry."

"I'm not angry."

"You're furious. It's very becoming."

Something cracked in her face — not a smile, but the place where a smile would live if she could afford one. She looked away, down at the fist around the contract. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"My father sold Fen for a wagon-load of safety. Twenty years of quiet, twenty years of the family name kept off lists. And I have hated him for it every single day since I read that page, because he put a price on a person and paid it without flinching." Her knuckles were white around the paper. "And now you sit here and offer to do the same thing, and dress it up in arithmetic, and expect me to take it."

"It's not the same."

"Why? Because you're choosing it?" Her voice rose, cracked, came back down. "Because he sold my brother and you're selling yourself, and somehow that makes it noble instead of obscene?"

"Yes," Vael said. "Exactly that. That's the entire difference, and it's the only difference that has ever mattered in the history of the world."

* * *

She got up and walked. Ten paces down the streambank, arms crossed, back to him — the posture of a woman arguing with a river. He let her have the silence. He'd learned that much about her, at least: Orsen processed grief the way ore processes in a furnace, slowly, at heat, and rushing it only made slag.

When she came back, she sat closer than before. Their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away.

"When I was sixteen," she said, "there was a boy in Greyford. Millwright's son. He used to leave bread on the fence post outside our house because he'd figured out we were poorer than we pretended to be, and he was too shy to hand it to me directly." A breath. "When the Hunt came through the first time — the small one, before the criers, before any of this — my father made me watch from the loft while they took him. The millwright's son. He was gifted too. Nobody ever talks about the gifted ones who aren't swordspeakers, who can't hide behind useful oaths. He could make flowers open early. That was the whole of it. Flowers." Her voice stayed flat, which was how Vael knew it was costing her everything. "My father watched with me. And he said, *that's why we're quiet, Orsen. Quiet keeps you alive.* And then, twenty years later, quiet wasn't enough, and he sold my brother, and I keep thinking — the bread was still on the fence post when I came back this year. Rotting. Nobody takes it down."

Vael reached over, slowly, giving her the whole length of a heartbeat to pull away, and took the contract out of her fist. She let him. He unfolded it and read it again, though he knew every clause by heart now — the deferral, the condition, *until the last gifted heir be found*, his signature at the bottom in a hand that trembled only slightly.

"You're not your father," he said.

"I'm about to ask you to be worse than him."

"No." He folded the contract and handed it back. "You're about to accept that someone offered. That's different. He never asked Fen. He never asked anyone. He decided in the dark and called it love after." Vael turned to face her fully. "So I'm asking you. Here, in the light, with the water making noise so nobody hears. Orsen Nystrom, last swordspeaker, terror of tollgates, woman who skips stones badly — will you let me buy my brother-in-law's soul with my own worthless reputation?"

The word was out before he could weigh it. *Brother-in-law.* He felt it land between them like a dropped knife.

Orsen went very still again. Then, slowly, terribly, she laughed — one short, broken sound, half sob, the laugh of a woman who had not laughed in weeks and whose body resented being asked.

"That was not a proposal," Vael said. "That was a clerical error."

"It was the worst proposal in the history of the Thornfells."

"The bar is underground, to be fair."

And now she did smile, brief and ruined, and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes, and Vael sat in the wreckage of his own slip and found he didn't regret it. There was a freedom in saying the true thing by accident. All the careful words he'd rehearsed had built a wall; the clumsy word had knocked it down.

"Say it properly, then," she said, hand still over her eyes. "If you're going to be an idiot. Say the whole idiotic thing."

He took a breath that hurt on the way in.

"I love you," he said. "I've loved you since the Salt Pass, since I watched you whisper a bridge back into standing and then pretend you hadn't, since I realized you were spending pieces of yourself to keep strangers alive and calling it a job. I loved you at the tollgate when I lied to a witch-hunter with a bell in his hand and didn't know until afterward what I'd done or why. I'll love you tomorrow, when I sign my name over to Doss Merrow and every friend I have becomes a stranger with a knife. I'm not promising anything, because neither of us is stupid enough to promise. I expect to die down there. I expect you might too. But if there's a version of any world where we walk out of that mountain — I want it to be a world where I said this. Out loud. Where you heard it."

The stream ran. Far above, impossibly far, rain would be falling on the Thornfells, and no one up there would know that under their feet a smuggler was confessing to a swordspeaker in the dark.

Orsen lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than he had ever seen them.

"I don't love you," she said.

His stomach dropped.

"I don't *dare* love you," she corrected, and her voice shook, and she grabbed the front of his shirt with the fist that wasn't holding the contract, and pulled him in until their foreheads touched. "Do you understand the difference? If I say the word, it becomes a thing the magistrates can take. It becomes a hostage. Everything I love, they find, Vael. That's what they *do*. That's how they found the millwright's son — someone loved him loudly." Her grip tightened. "So I'm telling you without telling you. I'm drawing the map and leaving the country blank. Read it however you want."

"I read it fine," Vael said. His heart was going hard enough that she could probably feel it through his chest. "You love me. You just refuse to file the paperwork."

"I hate you."

"Noted for the record."

She kissed him then — or he kissed her; afterward neither of them could reconstruct the sequence, and neither tried. It was not gentle. It was the kiss of two people standing on the edge of a long fall, gripping each other not for tenderness but for ballast. Her hand slid from his collar to the back of his neck. His found the small of her back, the oath-marks there, the ridged scar-tissue of a lifetime of words spoken in secret. When they broke apart, she kept her forehead against his and breathed, and he counted her breaths the way a drowning man counts waves.

"One condition," she said finally.

"Doss gets the ledger, not the routes east. Those are other people's livelihoods. I'll burn them first."

"Not that." She pulled back and looked at him, and the light from the distant lanterns caught the wet on her cheeks. "When we go down there — when I speak whatever word breaks the fire loose — you run. Whatever happens, whatever you see, you take Fen if you can and you run, and you don't wait for me, and you don't come back for me. Swear it. A real oath, smuggler's kind, the kind you can't wriggle out of."

"No."

"You haven't heard the terms—"

"I don't need to hear the terms. No." He cupped her face, rough palms against sharp cheekbones. "You've spent thirty years alone because everyone who might have stood next to you either died or looked away. Your father sold your brother. Your friend at the market saw your marks and trembled and said nothing. Everyone in your life has had a price, and you've paid every one of them." His thumb traced the edge of an oath-mark. "I don't have a price, Orsen. I have a direction, and it's wherever you're standing. You can order me to run all night. I'm going to stand there."

"That's not noble. That's just stubborn."

"We contain multitudes."

She made the broken-laugh sound again, and then she leaned into him, all the way, letting her weight rest against his shoulder for the first time since he'd known her — and Vael understood that this, more than the kiss, more than the words, was the confession she couldn't speak aloud. He wrapped his arm around her and watched the black water run and did not promise anything, because promises were for people who expected futures.

What he did instead was plan.

* * *

Doss Merrow met him four hours later, in a disused ore-gallery two turns below the exile caverns, by the light of a single shielded lamp. Doss was a wide, damp man with a drinker's bloom across his nose and the shrewd, tired eyes of someone who had driven wagons past terrible doors for so long he'd stopped seeing them.

"You want in," Doss said. Not a question. Word traveled fast down here; Vael suspected Sera had already sold him out, or saved him, depending on how the guild voted. "Through the tithe-door. You understand what's behind it."

"I understand enough."

"Nobody understands enough. That's how they keep it." Doss sucked his teeth. "Next tithe-train goes down in six days. Two bearers short. Hooded, silent, hands bound with cord — ceremonial, mostly, but the hoods are real, because the court doesn't like to be seen seeing you. I can put two names on the manifest. Yours and whoever travels with you. After that, they're inside, and I'm outside, and what happens inside isn't mine to know or care about." He held out a broad palm. "Ledger. Signed. Every contact, every route, every debt owed to Vael of the Combine. And you never run these roads again."

Vael took out the ledger. He'd written the transfer deed already, hours ago, before he'd even asked her — because some part of him had known she'd say yes, and a smaller, truer part had known he would offer regardless. He set it in Doss's palm.

"Six days," he said.

Doss weighed the book like a man weighing a heart, nodded once, and shuffled off into the dark with the lamp, taking Vael's thirty years of work with him. It should have felt like dying. It felt, instead, like setting down something heavy at the top of a hill before the long climb down.

When Vael came back to the streambank, Orsen was asleep sitting up, the contract still clenched in her fist, her head tipped against the stone. He eased down beside her, and after a moment — uninvited, breaking his own grammar, because the grammar was dead now along with everything else — he let her head rest on his shoulder.

Six days, and then the bone gates.

Above them, far above, through miles of rock and root, he thought he felt the mountain shudder — just once, faintly, like a sleeper turning toward a nightmare.

Or maybe he imagined it. He hoped he imagined it.
