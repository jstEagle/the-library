## 11. The Court Beneath

The tithe-bearer's robe smelled of tallow and old smoke, and it fit Orsen the way a coffin fits.

Vael had stolen it from a dead man — he hadn't said the man was dead, but he hadn't said otherwise either, and there was ash ground so deep into the weave that no living man would have worn it long. She pulled the hood low over her oath-marks and let the wool scratch her chin and told herself that disguise was just another kind of oath: *I am no one. I carry nothing. I belong to the fire.*

The words sat wrong in her mouth. Every word she spoke these days sat wrong, because she had spent thirty years speaking as few of them as possible, and now she was walking into the one place in the world where speech was currency and flesh was coin.

"Last chance to turn around," Vael murmured beside her.

They stood at the mouth of the Bone Gate, where the smugglers' road ended and the court's territory began. Behind them, the deep roads wound back through Thornfell's roots toward lantern-light and exile and the old woman who had read Orsen's marks and wept. Ahead, the gate itself: two pillars of femurs lashed with iron, tall as watchtowers, and between them a darkness that was not empty but *attentive*.

"I've turned around my whole life," Orsen said. "I'm out of practice."

She felt him almost reach for her hand. Almost. In the Undercourt, even tenderness would be contraband. He adjusted his own hood instead — he wore the gray of a tithe-warden, one rank above bearer, enough authority to walk her through the checkpoints without explanation and not enough to be questioned closely — and said, "Count the doors. Count the guards. If I raise my left hand to my belt, you run for the sump tunnel and you don't look back."

"And if I raise mine?"

"You won't." He smiled, and it didn't reach his eyes. "You never do."

The darkness between the pillars let them through the way water lets through a swallowed stone.

* * *

Inside, the mountain opened like a throat.

Orsen had imagined the Undercourt as a cave — damp, cramped, the kind of hole rats and smugglers fought over. What she walked into was a cathedral. The cavern rose higher than Greyford's bell tower, ribbed with stone columns that might have been carved or might have been grown, and every surface that wasn't living rock was bone. Femurs laid in herringbone across the floor. Ribs arched into vaulting overhead. Skulls — thousands upon thousands of skulls — were mortared into the walls in patterns too regular to be decoration and too deliberate to be accident. A script. She realized with a cold slide down her spine that the walls were *written*, whole generations of the dead arranged into sentences she could not read but could, somehow, almost hear.

And at the center, banked low in a basin of black iron, burned the fire.

It was smaller than she expected. That was the horror of it. All this — the cathedral, the magistrates, twenty years of her family's ruin, the Hunt burning villages on the surface — all of it served a flame no bigger than a cookfire. It burned a color she had no name for, gold shot through with veins of white, and it did not flicker. It *breathed*. In and out, slow as a sleeping thing, and each time it drew breath the temperature of the whole cavern dropped a degree, and each time it exhaled, warmth rolled out over the congregation below like a blessing.

Around the fire-basin, tier upon tier of bone benches descended, and on the benches sat the congregation: hundreds of hooded figures, silent, still, their faces lost in shadow. Tithe-bearers. Wardens. Magistrates in deeper hoods, their robes trimmed with silver thread that caught the fire's breathing light. And before them, on a platform of fused vertebrae, the auction was already underway.

A woman stood on the platform. She was naked to the waist, and her skin was marked — not with oath-marks like Orsen's but with *ledger-lines*, fine silver scars ruled across her arms and chest in columns, numbers written in a hand that had used her body as vellum. A magistrate held her wrist up to the firelight the way a merchant holds a lamp to a gemstone.

"—fourteen years remaining, sound constitution, minor resonance gift, untrained," the magistrate intoned. His voice carried without effort, filling the cathedral the way the fire's warmth did, and Orsen understood that his voice, too, was fed by the flame. "The tithe will be taken at the next drawing of breath. Opening bid: one writ of succession."

From the benches, a single hand rose. A sealed writ changed custody. The woman on the platform did not struggle. That was the worst part, Orsen thought. Not the selling. The stillness. The way the woman looked at the fire with something that was not fear and was not worship but the exhausted peace of someone who has been told, so many times and by people with authority, that her burning is necessary.

Fourteen years. Fourteen years of mornings, of bread and rain and someone's laugh, weighed against a writ of paper.

Orsen's hands curled inside her sleeves, and she made herself open them again. Made herself be furniture. She was carrying a tithe-satchel on her hip, heavy with the sand-and-ash mixture Vael had prepared, heavy with the seals that said its contents had already been rendered down and accounted for. No one questioned a bearer whose work was done. She only had to walk. Walk the long aisle, deliver nothing, and find the ledger.

Find Fen.

* * *

She found him because everything else pointed at him.

The living ledger kept a dais of his own, set apart from the auction platform, near the fire-basin's edge — close enough to the heat that sweat shone on his face, far enough that the cold reached him between the flame's exhales. He sat at a desk of black iron, writing. Around him, stacked in towers that leaned like drunk men, were the books. Ledgers. Hundreds of them, spines cracked, pages furred at the edges, and every page she could see from the aisle was filled with names.

He had been seventeen when their father signed him away. He was thirty-seven now, and he looked seventy. Not aged exactly — *emptied*, the way a house looks emptied, the shape of habitation still visible but the life moved out of it. His hair had gone white at the temples. His hands, moving down the ledger lines with a penmanship too perfect to be human, were translucent at the fingertips, the firelight passing faintly through them.

But he was alive.

Orsen stopped walking. Somewhere behind her a magistrate droned a figure — nine years, strong bones, latent gift — and somewhere ahead of her the fire breathed its slow gold breath, and Orsen stood in the aisle of a cathedral built from the dead and felt hope hit her like a physical blow, staggering, humiliating, enormous. *Alive.* Twenty years of assuming a grave, a nameless one, a small marker if any — and he was here, three hundred feet away, writing names.

She forced her feet to move. One step. Another. A bearer does not stop in the aisles. A bearer walks her circuit and keeps her eyes down, and Orsen had spent thirty years practicing invisibility, and none of it helped, because her heart was slamming so hard against her ribs she was certain the whole congregation could hear it, certain the fire itself could hear it, the fire that fed on gifted blood and knew its own when it breathed it.

Ten feet from the ledger-dais, a warden stepped into her path.

"Bearer." The warden's hood tilted. "Your seal."

Orsen's mouth went dry. She had rehearsed this. Vael had drilled her until midnight — *you are Maret of the Low Roads, your route closed at the second checkpoint, you carry rendered tithe from the eastern cells* — and she gave the rehearsed answer in a voice pitched flat and dull, and held out the satchel's seal-tag with a hand that did not shake because she ordered it not to, the same way she once ordered a bridge to stand.

The warden turned the tag over. Ran a thumb across the wax.

"The eastern cells closed a season past."

"The last of them closed yesterday," Orsen said. "I carried what remained."

A silence. Beyond the warden's shoulder, Fen wrote on, line after line, and Orsen watched her brother's pen move and thought: *I came through fire and ash and the graves of everyone Father sold to keep us safe, and I will not be stopped ten feet away by a man with a thumb.*

Then the warden grunted and waved her through, and Orsen learned in that moment that terror and relief feel identical in the body — the same flooding heat, the same loose knees — and differ only afterward, in the story you tell yourself about surviving.

She passed the dais close. Close enough to smell the ink, close enough to see that the ledgers' pages weren't paper at all but something thinner, paler, veined —

Don't look. Don't think about what the pages are.

Close enough to see his eyes.

Fen looked up. It was nothing — the glance of a clerk interrupted by a passing footstep, half a second of attention, already returning to his line. But in that half second Orsen saw her mother's grey eyes looking out of a hollowed face, saw recognition arrive and *drown*, deliberately, consciously drowned, as Fen's gaze slid off hers like water off oiled steel and fixed itself back on the ledger as though she were no more remarkable than a draft through the hall.

Only his pen stopped. Just for the length of one breath — the fire's breath, the long slow draw that pulled the warmth out of the air — and then resumed, and the resumed strokes were flawless, and no one watching would have seen anything at all.

But Orsen had spent her whole life reading the language of things unsaid. She knew a message when she saw one, and the message was:

*I know you. Do not know me.*

* * *

She completed the circuit. Delivered the satchel to a receiving alcove where a faceless functionary logged it without opening it — rendered tithe, accounted, done — and took up a station along the wall where bearers waited for reassignment, heads bowed, invisible as furniture. From there she could watch the ledger-dais, and she watched it for an hour, and in that hour she counted.

Fen wrote without pause except when the fire drew breath. On each drawn breath — and the rhythm was steady, perhaps forty to the hour — his pen lifted, and in the lift she began to read a second script, the one his body was writing over the top of his servitude. The tilt of his head toward the auction platform. The flex of his fingers, once, when a magistrate's bid went unanswered and the woman with fourteen years was led away through a side door, still calm, still peaceful, still *still*. The way he turned each finished ledger to face the wall, always the wall, so that whatever he recorded last was never seen.

He was alive, and he was hollow, and he was — impossibly, against every law of what the court did to the souls it bought — *still in there*. Some part of Fen had survived twenty years of being a book, and that part was leaving her messages in the pauses between breaths.

*Do not know me,* the first message said.

The second came near the end of the hour, and it undid her.

A magistrate mounted the auction platform leading a child.

Small. Seven, maybe eight years old, wrapped in a bearer's smock cut down to size, walking with that same terrible stillness, and when the magistrate lifted the child's wrist to the light, Orsen saw the first ledger-line already ruled across the small forearm — a single silver stroke, a beginning, an account opened.

"—sixty-one years remaining," the magistrate said. "Unawakened gift, lineage uncertain but promising. The court values potential."

On the ledger-dais, Fen set down his pen.

He rose. The motion was wrong, jerky, a puppet testing strings, and every hooded head in the nearest tiers turned toward him, and Orsen's heart climbed into her throat because they were going to kill him, surely, they were going to strike him down where he stood — but he only walked to the edge of his dais and knelt there, facing the fire, forehead to the iron rail, in an attitude of perfect devotion. Worship. The court relaxed. The auction continued. Sixty-one years, a voice said somewhere, opening bid.

But kneeling there, hidden by the angle of his own body, Fen's right hand lay flat on the dais floor where no one could see it — no one but a sister standing in the bearer's line with her head bowed and her eyes angled down through thirty years of practiced sight.

Two fingers tapped the iron. Twice. Then pointed — a small, precise gesture, barely a movement at all — toward the fire-basin, and swept downward, beneath it.

*Below the fire,* the gesture said. *Go down.*

Then he folded his hands, and rose, and returned to his ledgers, and became a book again.

* * *

Orsen waited through two more auctions. It cost her something she didn't have a name for, sitting on that cold bench while years of human life were weighed against writs of paper, while the fire breathed and breathed and the walls full of skulls seemed to lean closer, listening. Waiting was its own kind of oath: *not yet, not yet, not yet.* She broke it at last when the magistrates withdrew for the interval between sessions and the congregation stirred and thinned, and she slipped from the bearers' bench into the shadow of a rib-vaulted colonnade, and followed the architecture down.

There were stairs behind the colonnade. Of course there were; a cathedral is built around its crypt. They spiraled into the rock, lit at intervals by lamps that burned without oil, and the colder she went the more clearly she heard it — not the fire's breathing anymore but something else, something under it, a sound like wind through a keyhole, like whispering heard through a wall, like a vast dark patience pressing its mouth to the other side of the world and murmuring.

The seals. Fen had pointed below the fire, and below the fire the mountain was full of that murmuring, and every stair-step took her closer to the thing the fire existed to hold back.

At the bottom, a door of black iron stood ajar — ajar, in the Undercourt, where nothing was ever careless — and through the gap came firelight, real firelight, gold-shot-white, and the murmur was loud here, and beneath the murmur she could hear voices. Magistrates. Arguing in low, urgent tones about *the coal* and *the shortfall* and *the Nystrom girl* —

Her own name. Her father's name, spoken in the dark beneath a mountain.

Orsen put her eye to the gap, and her blood went to ice.

Beyond the door, in a chamber ringed with iron and carved script, the dragon-fire burned at its true heart — and it was dying. The great breathing flame above, the one the whole cathedral worshipped, was a reflection, a projection, a promise. This, here, was all there actually was: a coal the size of a fist, pulsing weakly in a basin the size of a field, and around it the frost was climbing the walls in feathered patterns, and around the frost the whispering pressed, and around the whispering the magistrates stood in a circle, hooded heads bent together, and the tallest of them was saying:

"—one soul remains of sufficient lineage. The last swordspeaker. Find her, and the fire holds another century. Fail, and everything below comes up to meet us."

Orsen stepped back from the door into the dark, her heart a hammer, her mind racing through the arithmetic of it. They were hunting her already. They had been hunting her since Greyford, since the mill, maybe since before she was born — and Fen had sent her down here, straight to them, straight to the one place in the world she must never be found.

Unless.

Unless her brother, hollow-eyed and half-transparent, enslaved for twenty years, had seen something in the ledgers that they hadn't. Something about the coal. Something about what it was actually hungry for.

Above her, muffled through miles of bone and rock, the great false fire drew its next long breath — and this time, for the first time, it stuttered.

The whispering below rose like applause.
