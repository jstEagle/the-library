## 12. A Brother Made Ledger

The tithe-bearer came through the Bone Gate at the wrong hour.

Fen knew the hours of the Undercourt the way a blind man knows a room — by sound, by temperature, by the weight of footsteps on bone. The magistrates kept their own clock down here. There was the hour of ledger-call, when the hooded clerks filed past his desk and he read out the day's accounts in a voice that no longer felt like his own. There was the hour of feeding, when the soul-wagons rolled in from the surface and the fire drank. And there was the long hour between, when the cathedral went quiet enough that Fen could hear the seals breathing — that slow, wet flex of ancient wards straining against whatever pressed on them from below.

No one crossed the Bone Gate in the long hour. No one was permitted to.

But someone had, and she was walking toward him now in a tithe-bearer's grey robe, hood up, hands folded with the careful stillness of someone who had rehearsed this walk a hundred times in her head. Fen's quill stopped mid-stroke. A drop of ink fell onto the ledger page and bloomed there like a wound.

He knew her walk before he knew anything else about her. He had taught it to her, in a way — the flat-footed, rolling gait of a girl who'd grown up carrying salt sacks over mountain scree, who had learned early that loud steps on loose stone got people found. Orsen Nystrom moved like a woman who did not want to be found. She always had.

She was not moving that way now. She was moving like a woman walking into a trap she had built for herself, and Fen's whole body went cold in a way the Undercourt's chill could never manage.

*No,* he thought. *No, no, no.*

He looked back down at the ledger. That was the first rule they had ground into him in those first terrible months: the ledger does not look up. The ledger reads. The ledger is read to. A living ledger who looked at things too long became curious, and curiosity in the Undercourt was a debt, and all debts were collected.

But he looked anyway. He was already damned; twenty years of looking down had bought him nothing but a straighter spine and a quieter heart.

She reached the great counting-table where he sat, third from the end, beneath the rib-vaulted dark where the dragon-fire's glow came up through the floor grates like light through deep water. The other ledgers — there were four of them now, though once there had been nine — bent over their columns without raising their heads. Fen set down his quill with exaggerated care, as if his hand were not shaking.

"Tithe-bearer," he said, and heard his own voice come out level, bored, official. Twenty years of practice. "You're late for your intake. The intake hour closed at second bell."

The hood tilted slightly. Beneath it, half-shadowed, he saw her jaw tighten — that small stubborn clench he'd last seen when she was eleven years old and their father told her she couldn't come on the pass run because girls slowed the mules. She had gone anyway. She had beaten the caravan by half a day and come home smug as a cat, and their father had been so furious he hadn't spoken to her for a week, and Fen had laughed until he cried because someone in that house had to.

"Forgive me," the tithe-bearer said. Her voice was roughened, pitched low, sanded down to something anonymous. But underneath the gravel of it, Fen heard the melody. He would have known it drowned, burned, buried. He had spent twenty years listening for it in every footstep in this place, and hating himself for hoping. "I was delayed at the Gate. The wardens questioned my papers."

"The wardens question everyone's papers." He gestured to the intake ledger without looking at it. "Name."

A pause. Long enough to be dangerous.

"You know I can't give you that here," she said quietly.

And there it was. Not the words themselves — anyone might say those words, any frightened tithe-bearer trying to keep her name off a court document — but the way she said them. The wry edge under the fear. The refusal to be small even while being small.

Fen closed his eyes for one heartbeat. When he opened them, he made himself look at her fully, and let her see him look, and watched her hood tilt back just far enough for lamplight to catch her face.

Orsen. Older. Harder around the eyes, a scar he didn't know cutting through one eyebrow, thirty years of hidden living written across her like marginalia. But her eyes were the same. They were the same eyes that had watched him from the doorway the night their father sold him, the same eyes that had filled with a fury too large for an eight-year-old's face, the same eyes he had dreamed about every night for two decades and then trained himself to stop dreaming about because hope was a currency the court taxed.

"Sister," he said.

Her breath caught. In the vast bone silence of the cathedral, it was a gunshot.

* * *

They gave him a quarter-hour. That was the arrangement — the magistrates allowed the ledgers small mercies, calibrated mercies, the kind that bound tighter than chains. Family visits were encouraged, actually. Family visits reminded the ledgers what they were protecting. Fen had understood that arithmetic within his first year, and it had never once stopped hurting.

There was a alcove off the counting-floor, curtained in grey wool gone stiff with age, with two stools and a brazier that gave more smoke than heat. A warden stood outside the curtain. The warden always stood outside the curtain. Fen sat down across from his sister and put his hands flat on his knees so she couldn't see them tremble, and she pulled her hood back, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

"You're tall," she finally managed, and her voice cracked on the second word.

"You're old," he said, and immediately hated himself for it, because it was true and cruel and she flinched. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I — you have to understand, I've imagined this—" He stopped. Started again. "I've imagined this ten thousand times and in none of them did I open with an insult."

"In most of mine you opened with crying," Orsen said. "So we're both embarrassing ourselves." Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Fen. Look at you. You're—"

"A ledger," he said. "Yes. I know what I am. Do you?"

"I know what they've done to you—"

"Do you?" He leaned forward, and the words came out of him fast and low, the way water comes through a cracked dam. "Because you walked in here wearing a stolen robe with forged papers and a plan, I can see the plan sitting behind your eyes like a stone behind ice, and I need you to listen to me before the plan gets either of us killed. This isn't a prison, Orsen. Prisons hold people against their will. This is a load-bearing wall. Do you understand? I am *structural.*"

"That's what they told you."

"It's what's *true.*" He said it hard, harder than he meant to, and the warden's shadow shifted beyond the curtain, and he dropped his voice to almost nothing. "You don't know what's below us. You can't. Nobody on the surface knows — maybe nobody above the elder circles has ever known. The fire isn't a furnace, sister. It's a seal. It's the only seal. Everything the court takes — every soul, every year of life, every tithe from every gifted child sold off by desperate fathers — it doesn't feed cruelty. It feeds the flame. And the flame holds shut a door that has been trying to open since before this mountain had a name."

"Fen—"

"There are things down there." His hands had curled into fists on his knees without his permission. "I've heard them. Everyone down here hears them, eventually, on the bad nights, when the fire gutters low and the wards go thin. They whisper. They *count.* They count louder than the ledgers do, and they are patient in a way nothing alive is patient, and the only reason you grew up breathing free air on the surface is that this fire burns. And the fire burns because I sit at that table and I read the accounts and I keep the tally true. That's what a living ledger is, Orsen. Not a slave. A keystone. Pull me out and the arch comes down."

She was silent. He watched her take it in — really take it in, watch it land behind her eyes — and he thought, briefly, foolishly, that it might be enough. That she would stand up, straighten her stolen robe, and walk back out through the Bone Gate and up the deep roads and away, and live, and hate him, and live.

Then she said, "How much of that did they teach you, and how much did you check?"

And Fen felt the floor tilt under him, metaphorically, the way it always did when his sister asked the one question he couldn't afford.

"What?"

"You were eight years old when they took you." Her voice was gentle now, which was worse than hard. Gentle was how she'd talked to him when he'd broken his arm falling out of the rowan tree, right before she set it herself and made him bite down on a leather strap. "Eight. They raised you. They wrote the world for you and handed it over finished, and you've had no one to check it against for twenty years. Fen — I love you. I have loved you every single day since the night Father signed that contract, and I am telling you, as the person in this family whose entire adult life has been learning how lies are built and sold — ask yourself who benefits from you believing you're a keystone."

"The surface benefits," he whispered. "Everyone benefits. That's the point—"

"Who *specifically,*" she said, "benefits from the last swordspeaker's brother believing his captivity is holy?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it.

Because he had asked himself. Of course he had asked himself, in the long hour, in the dark, in the twenty years of nights when the whispers came up through the grates and he lay on his narrow bed and stared at the ceiling and turned the question over like a stone with something soft underneath. Who benefits. The magistrates benefit. The magistrates who lived in warmth and power while he sat in a cold alcove reading numbers. The magistrates who had taken an eight-year-old boy and told him he was sacred, which is the oldest trick of every jailer since the first one.

But —

"But the whispers are real," he said aloud. "That's the thing you can't forge, Orsen. The whispers are real. I've heard them. The frost on the walls some winters, climbing higher than it should. The nights the fire goes brown at the edges. Whatever is down there is real, and hungry, and the wards are real, and whether the magistrates are monsters or saints or both, the arithmetic doesn't change. The fire needs fuel. The fuel is souls. If the tally stops—"

"Then maybe the tally was never the answer."

He stared at her.

She leaned in, and her voice dropped so low he had to strain for it, and her eyes had that look — the look from the rowan tree, from the bridge, from every impossible thing she had ever decided to do while everyone else was still arguing about whether it could be done. "Something happened on my way down here. Something the exiles showed me. Something about what the fire was *for,* originally, before the court got its hands on it. Fen, I can't say it here, the walls in this place eat words — but I need you to trust me for one hour. One hour, and then I'll tell you everything, and if I'm wrong, if it's all exactly what they said, then I'll walk out of here myself and you'll never see me again. I swear it on Mother's grave."

"Your oaths bind," he said automatically. "Yours especially. Everyone knows what you are now, don't they? The last swordspeaker. The whole court's been whispering about it for a month — the hunt on the surface, the villages burning, all of it to flush you out. Do you know what they'll pay for you? Do you know what your soul alone would buy the fire? Years. Centuries of margin. You could walk into the feeding chamber right now and buy the world another hundred years, and they'd call you a saint in every chapel from here to Greyford—"

"And what would they call you?"

The question landed like a blade laid flat against skin.

"They'd call you the brother who brought her in," Fen said. His voice had gone very quiet. "They'd unbind me. That's the offer they've dangled since I was twelve. Bring in a swordspeaker and the ledger walks free. Twenty years I've carried that offer around like a stone in my chest, and I have never once picked it up, and do you know why? Because I did the math, Orsen. I do the math every day. My freedom against the seal. My freedom against the whispers. My freedom against the door." He shook his head slowly. "It's not even close. It was never close. So don't you dare come down here and offer me rescue like it's a gift, because I am telling you — I am *begging* you — the price of me is a world."

"No," Orsen said. "The price of you is a lie. And I think somewhere under twenty years of their arithmetic, you already know it."

* * *

The quarter-hour ended the way it always ended: the warden's hand on the curtain, the cough that wasn't a cough, the mechanical mercy of the court asserting itself. Time in the Undercourt belonged to the magistrates, and they lent it out in measured doses so that everyone remembered the lender.

Fen stood. His legs felt like borrowed things.

"One hour," Orsen whispered, rising with him. Her hand found his wrist — quick, fierce, gone again before the warden could mark it. Her grip left a ghost of warmth on his skin that he knew would still be there tomorrow. "The archive gallery, after ledger-call. There's a door behind the third reliquary that the wardens think is bricked. It isn't. I'll be there. Come and hear me out, and if you still believe afterward — truly believe, with your own mind and not theirs — then I'll go. I swear it."

"You shouldn't swear things in this place," Fen said hoarsely. "Everything sworn here gets collected."

"Then I'll pay," she said. "I've been paying my whole life. One more debt won't break me."

She pulled her hood up. For a moment, standing there in the grey robe with the lamplight catching the scar on her brow, she looked so much like their mother that Fen's throat closed entirely — the same set of the shoulders, the same chin lifted slightly as if braced against weather. Their mother, who had died not knowing where her son was. Their mother, whose grave-goods had apparently held the truth all along, because of course Orsen had found it, because Orsen found everything, because she was the one who went ahead on the pass while everyone else said it couldn't be done.

"Don't," he said, as she turned to leave. It came out cracked, young, eight years old. "Orsen. Don't do whatever you're planning. Please. I need you to walk away. I need you to be the one Nystrom who survives."

She paused at the curtain's edge. She didn't turn around.

"You were the one who survived," she said softly. "Twenty years in this place, and you're still in there — I can see him, Fen, I can see my brother behind the ledger. You didn't survive this place. You just haven't gotten out yet."

Then she was gone, grey robe swallowed by the bone-dark of the cathedral, and Fen stood alone in the alcove with the dead brazier smoking beside him and his heart hammering out a rhythm he hadn't felt since childhood.

Hope. That was the rhythm. He'd forgotten its tempo.

He went back to his table. He sat down. He took up his quill, and he made himself read the next account line, and the next, and the ink flowed and the numbers marched, and outwardly he was the ledger again, obedient and structural and true.

But inwardly, for the first time in twenty years, Fen Nystrom was counting down.

One hour. Behind the third reliquary. A door the wardens believed was brick.

And below his feet, faint and patient and endless, the whispers counted along with him — as if they, too, knew that something was coming. As if they, too, were waiting to see whether the keystone would hold.

Or whether, at last, someone had come to pull it free.
