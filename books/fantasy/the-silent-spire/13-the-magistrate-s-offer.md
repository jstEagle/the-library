## 13. The Magistrate's Offer

They took her cloak at the Bone Gate.

Not roughly — that was the worst of it. A warden with soft hands and softer eyes folded Orsen's salt-stained traveling cloak over his arm the way a servant handles a guest's coat at some great house above the sun, and he said, "You won't be needing it where you're going," in a tone of genuine courtesy, and Orsen understood that she had walked out of one world and into another, and that in this one even theft wore white gloves.

The tithe-bearer's disguise had gotten her this far: the gray robe, the ledger-sigil stamped in wax on her shoulder, the yoke of empty soul-jars that clinked faintly with every step like a wind chime made of teeth. It had gotten her past three checkpoints, through a corridor of hooded auditors who never once looked up from their counting, and into the antechamber of the Chief Magistrate herself — because the court beneath the mountain was a machine, and machines do not question the parts that fit them.

But now she stood alone in a room without doors, and the machine had stopped moving around her, and she could hear her own heartbeat against the silence like a fist knocking on wood.

The chamber was round. That seemed wrong; everything else in the Undercourt had been angles and columns and ledgers, straight lines laid down by straight minds. But this room curved, its walls of pale bone-smoothed stone sweeping up into a vaulted ceiling where veins of ember-light ran like blood through marble. The light came from below. Everything here came from below. Orsen could feel it through the soles of her feet — a warmth, a pulse, slow as a sleeping heart, and beneath it something colder, older, patient as frost.

The Chief Magistrate stood at the room's center with her back turned.

She was smaller than Orsen had imagined. The magistrates she'd passed in the corridors had been tall, robed, faceless behind their hoods, moving like pieces on a board. This woman was slight, gray-haired, her hands clasped behind her, and when she turned, Orsen saw a face that might have belonged to any grandmother in Greyford market — except for the eyes. The eyes were the color of ash after the fire has gone through. Not dark. *Empty.* Burned all the way down to something smooth and heat-cracked, and Orsen knew that color. She had seen it once before, in a polished shield in her father's hall, the day she'd first spoken an oath into steel and watched the metal sing back to her.

She had seen it in her own reflection, afterward, for just a moment. Before the marks faded.

"You're not a tithe-bearer," the Chief Magistrate said. Her voice was dry and level, a page turning. "The jars are empty. Tithe-bearers carry full ones down and empty ones up. You carried nothing." She tilted her head, birdlike. "And you walk like someone listening to the floor."

Orsen said nothing. Her hand ached to rise to her throat, where the oath-marks lay hidden beneath the gray collar — the old scars, the spiraling sigils burned into her skin at birth, the inheritance she'd spent thirty years burying under salt dust and silence.

"There is no point in denying it," the Magistrate went on. "I have been what you are. I know the way you stand when there's a word caught behind your teeth." Something moved across the ash-gray eyes — not warmth, but the memory of warmth, the way a hearthstone remembers fire long after the logs have gone to powder. "Swordspeaker. Last of the Nystrom line. Do you know how long I have waited for you to come down?"

*How does she—* But Orsen knew how. Fen. The living ledger, hollow-eyed at his table, every secret of the surface world flowing through his quill into the court's accounts. Had he given her up? No — she thought of his face in that instant their eyes had met across the counting-hall, the raw flash of terror, and then the small, deliberate turn of his head away. *Go,* that look had said. He hadn't given her up. He'd tried to save her.

Which meant the Magistrate had known anyway. Which meant it didn't matter what Fen did or didn't do. The court had simply been waiting, patient as the dark beneath the seals, for the last gifted heir to walk in on her own feet.

"The contract," Orsen said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Fen's contract. My father signed it twenty years ago. Payment deferred until the last gifted heir was found." She stepped forward, and the floor's pulse beat up through her boots. "I'm found. So we settle the account."

Something flickered at the corner of the Magistrate's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, worn smooth by decades of disuse.

"Direct," she said. "That's the gift in you. Oath-speakers were always direct. Words are too expensive to waste." She gestured, and a section of the curved wall slid open with a sound like a held breath released. Beyond it: a small table, two chairs, a carafe of water catching the ember-light. An ordinary room tucked inside the impossible one. "Sit. We will speak of accounts."

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes," the Magistrate said. "I remember that too." She sat herself, unhurried, and poured water into two cups anyway, and pushed one toward the chair across from her, and waited, and after a moment Orsen realized she was waiting the way a fisherman waits — still, patient, letting the line do the work — and she hated herself for sitting down.

* * *

The Magistrate let the silence stretch until it sang.

Then she said, "Do you know what the fire is?"

"I know what your ledgers say it eats."

"The ledgers lie." The old woman said it flatly, without shame, as if reporting the weather. "Not in the numbers — the numbers are true, every soul weighed to the grain. They lie in the arithmetic. In what the feeding is *for*." She turned one cup slowly on the table, a quarter-turn, back again. "There is a thing beneath us. Older than the mountain. Older than the first oath ever spoken into steel. The dragon-fire holds it sealed — has held it since before your people had names. And the fire is dying."

"So feed it more souls."

"We have fed it souls for four hundred years." For the first time, something cracked in the dry voice — not emotion, exactly, but the sound of a wall settling. "Four hundred years of tithes, of contracts, of fathers selling sons and courts buying daughters, and do you know what it has bought us? Nothing. The fire shrinks regardless. Every year it is smaller. Every year the frost creeps a little higher up the seal-walls. Souls are not fuel, swordspeaker. Souls are *kindling* thrown onto a coal. They flare, and they gutter, and they leave ash, and the ash chokes the flame a little worse than before." She looked up, and those ash-colored eyes held Orsen's without mercy. "We are smothering the very thing we are trying to keep alive. We have been doing it for centuries. Because sacrifice is easy to organize, and no one downstairs has ever asked a harder question."

Orsen's mouth had gone dry. "What question?"

The Magistrate leaned forward. Up close, in the ember-veined light, Orsen could see the faded ghosts of marks on the old woman's throat — spirals, sigils, the same architecture as her own oath-marks, burned down to almost-nothing. Scars where a voice used to live.

"*What was the fire before it needed feeding?*" the Magistrate whispered. "It was spoken. That is the truth my predecessors buried under four hundred years of convenient accounting. The fire answers to a speaker's oath. It always has. The first swordspeakers bound their voices into it, and it blazed, and the dark stayed down. And when the last of them died, some clever frightened magistrate discovered that souls would make the flame *flicker higher*, briefly, and mistook flickering for burning, and built a religion of bookkeeping on top of the mistake." She spread her small hands. "I found the truth thirty years ago. I was young. I brought it to my elders. Do you know what they did?"

"They burned it out of you," Orsen said quietly.

"They offered me a choice, and I chose wrong, and they took my voice-gift for my insolence and left me the office." The Magistrate smiled then, and it was the most terrible thing Orsen had seen yet, because it was genuine. "So now I keep the ledgers of a lie, and wait for someone to come down who can end it. And here you are. Salt-dust on your boots. Your brother's face in your eyes. Asking to settle accounts."

"Then settle them," Orsen said. "Give me Fen."

"And I will," the Magistrate said. "Freely. Tonight. His contract torn, his name struck from the rolls, his body walking up the deep road beside yours by morning." She paused, and let it land, and Orsen felt the words go through her like a blade sliding between ribs — because it was everything, it was the whole of why she had come down into the dark, handed across a table with a cup of water, no fight, no price—

"No price?" Orsen heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away.

"Oh, there is a price." The Magistrate's ash-eyes did not waver. "But it is not the one you fear. I am not asking for your brother's freedom in exchange for your soul. The court has enough souls. Souls are ash." She turned the cup a quarter-turn. Back again. "I am asking for your *oath*. One word. Spoken freely, here, now, into the oldest binding-stone in the world." She reached beneath the table and set something on it: a stone the size of a heart, black as cooled iron, veined with hairline threads of gold. It hummed. Orsen felt it in her teeth, in the marks on her throat, in the marrow the gift lived in. "Your founding-oath. The one every swordspeaker swears and spends a lifetime keeping. Break it — willingly, knowingly, with your whole voice behind the breaking — and the shock of it will tear loose every bond the court has woven. Every stolen soul in these archives comes free. Including his."

The chamber was very quiet. Below them, faintly, the fire pulsed. Below *that*, patient and endless, the whispers counted.

"And me?" Orsen asked. "When the oath breaks?"

"You told me yourself what breaking costs," the Magistrate said gently. "You've felt the edge of it every time you've spent a word. A bridge held. A tunnel dropped. Each use takes a little." She folded her hands. "This will take all of it. The gift. The voice that carries it. Permanently. You will walk out of this mountain mute, swordspeaker, or near enough — and you will never speak an oath into anything again, steel or stone or fire. You will be only a woman. Free, and ordinary, and finished."

*Only a woman.*

Orsen stared at the black stone humming on the table between them, and thirty years rose up in her like floodwater.

Thirty years of biting words back behind her teeth while bridges groaned and travelers prayed. Thirty years of watching witch-fires on the ridgelines and saying nothing, doing nothing, being *no one*. Thirty years since her father had looked at her across the supper-table and said *never use it where anyone can see, Orsen, never, or they'll take you like they took him* — and she had obeyed, obedient even in exile, and the obedience had cost her a village, a friend's trembling gaze in a market square, a family's bones under ash. And here it was at last, the trade laid bare: her gift, the burden she had cursed every day of her hidden life, exchanged for her brother's freedom and every other stolen soul besides. It wasn't a bargain. It was a *reprieve*. It was the door out of the prison she'd been locked in since birth, dressed in duty's clothing so she could walk through it with her chin up.

*Say yes,* something in her sang, low and sweet as a lullaby. *Say yes and be done. Be small. Be safe. Be only a woman, and let the fire be someone else's war.*

Her lips parted. The word was already forming — she could feel it gathering in her chest the way all her oaths gathered, that pressure behind the breastbone, the sense of language leaning its weight against the cage of her ribs. One syllable. Freely given. Fen walking up the deep road beside her by morning, blinking in a light he hadn't seen in twenty years. Vael waiting at the top with his crooked smile and his traitor's route-map. Done. Finished. *Safe.*

"Yes," Orsen began —

—and stopped.

Because the Magistrate had closed her eyes.

It was a small thing. A flicker, barely a heartbeat long, there and gone like a fish under ice. But Orsen had spent thirty years reading faces in market crowds, learning which merchants lied about weight and which guards lied about pay, and she knew what she had just seen. Relief. Not satisfaction. Not triumph. *Relief* — the bone-deep sag of a woman setting down something she has carried too long.

The Magistrate wanted her to say yes.

Wanted it desperately. And that was the crack in the floor.

Orsen looked at the ash-eyed woman across the table — at the faded scars on her throat, at the office she'd accepted instead of rebellion, at the four hundred years of ledgers she kept and called truth — and the lullaby in Orsen's chest went suddenly cold.

"You said the fire answers to a broken oath," Orsen said slowly. "*Any* broken oath? Or mine specifically?"

The Magistrate's eyes opened. For an instant the grandmother's face was gone and something older looked out — something tired beyond measuring, standing watch over a mistake it had inherited and could not put down.

"Yours," she admitted. "The last of the founding line. The fire was *spoken by your blood*, child. Only your blood can speak it whole again." She hesitated — the first hesitation Orsen had seen from her. "Breaking your oath releases what is bound. But a release is not a reforging. Freed souls tear loose and go home, and the flame flares on their passage, and then—" her dry voice frayed, very slightly, "—and then it gutters. Unless a new oath is spoken into it. A living one. Freely given. Forever."

"And I couldn't give it," Orsen said. "Voiceless. You know that. You'd be trading my brother's freedom and every soul in these archives for a fire that dies within the year anyway — unless someone else stands where I stood and speaks what I cannot." She stood. Her chair scraped back, absurdly loud in the hush. "Who? Who's left to inherit it?"

Below their feet, the whispers stopped counting.

And in the sudden silence, Orsen heard — distantly, through leagues of stone, through four hundred years of lies — the dragon-fire falter.

Once. Like a heartbeat skipping.

The Magistrate's head came up sharply. The ember-veins in the vaulted ceiling dimmed, brightened, dimmed again, and somewhere far below, frost began — for the first time in living memory — to climb.

"It's early," the old woman breathed. She was on her feet now, ash-eyes wide, all her patience burned away in an instant. "It isn't due for weeks. What did you *do*—"

But Orsen was already thinking of Fen. Of the third reliquary. Of the door the wardens believed was brick, and the hour he had been counting down behind hollow eyes — and of whatever he had just done, or failed to do, in the dark below the ledgers.

She ran.
