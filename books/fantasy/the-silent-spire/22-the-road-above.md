## 22. The Road Above

The first thing the sun did was hurt.

Orsen had spent eleven days beneath the mountain, where light was something rationed — lantern-oil and banked fire and the red pulse of the heart-flame through her closed eyelids. When she and Vael finally climbed the last switchback of the Thornfell road and stepped out onto the high shoulder of the pass, dawn broke over the eastern ridgeline like a held breath released, and it struck her full in the face with no mercy at all.

She stopped. She put up a hand. She let it hurt.

Vael walked three steps before he noticed she wasn't beside him. He turned, saw her standing there squinting into the gold like a woman meeting someone she'd only ever heard described, and waited without comment. He'd gotten good at that in eleven days — waiting, watching her hands, reading the shape of what she couldn't say. His silence wasn't empty. It had furniture in it now. Room made for her.

The morning was cold enough that their breath hung in ribbons. Below them the Thornfell passes unspooled southward — grey stone, white pine, the thin thread of the smugglers' road stitched along the mountainside where honest people never looked. Somewhere down there was a crumbling bridge over the Glaivewater, and a caravan of salt-mules whose drivers still thought their guide had been a quiet woman with a limp and an unremarkable face. Somewhere down there was the life she'd built out of whispering, one oath-word at a time, each one costing her a little more blood-warmth than she could spare.

She couldn't go back to it. Not because of the Hunt — the Hunt was dying, its witch-hunters scattering like ash off a cold grate now that the court beneath had nothing left to pay them with — but because there was nothing left in her throat to go back *with*. She'd felt it happen at the fire-heart: the gift tearing loose, not ripped away so much as poured out, every word she'd ever bound into steel and stone and oath flowing out of her in a single luminous exhalation. She remembered thinking, in the last instant before silence took her, that it sounded like her mother's voice.

Then it was gone, and she had been a woman standing in a cathedral of bone with smoke in her lungs and nothing behind her teeth but air.

* * *

They came down off the high shoulder by midmorning and joined the surface road where it bent along the river valley. It was strange, walking the open way. Thirty years of habit pulled at her like a current — the instinct to hug the treeline, to keep hedgerows between herself and sightlines, to read every distant rider's gait for the particular arrogance of witch-hunters. Twice she caught herself doing it, shoulders creeping up toward her ears, and twice she made herself stop.

The second time, Vael's hand found hers. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. The road was full of ordinary traffic — a wool merchant's cart, two children driving geese with more confidence than skill, a tinker whose pots chimed with every step. Nobody looked at her twice. Nobody would look at her twice again, she realized, for as long as she lived. Whatever she was now, it was the same thing everyone else was: a woman on a road, going somewhere or nowhere.

It should have felt like freedom. Some of it did. But grief is greedy, and it takes the whole body hostage, and so what she felt most of all was the absence — the phantom weight of words she'd carried since she was six years old, when her father had first pressed her small hand against a knife-blade and taught her the word that made steel remember. She kept reaching for it the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. *If I wanted that bridge to hold,* she thought, watching a mason across the river set stones into a wall, *if I wanted that wall to stand a hundred years —*

Nothing. Just the thought, and the ache under it, clean and constant as a scar.

Her stomach growled. Vael heard it, grinned, and steered them toward a roadside inn whose sign — a painted goose with a suspiciously human expression — creaked on its bracket.

* * *

The innkeeper's wife talked enough for both of them.

Orsen sat with her back to the wall — some habits were load-bearing — and ate bread and hard cheese and a stew of root vegetables and mystery meat while the woman refilled their cups four times and told them everything. About how the tithe-collectors had come through a week past and then come back the other way, moving fast, looking over their shoulders. About how the proclamations nailed to the church doors in Greyford and Millbrook had been torn down by persons unknown, and how the crier hadn't come around since. About how old Marta Hollis, who everyone had always said was touched, had walked out of her cottage two days ago singing, after thirty years of not speaking above a whisper.

"They say whatever it was under the mountain," the innkeeper's wife confided, leaning close, "it's done. Over. My cousin's boy works the tollgate at Ironbridge and he swears the hunters burned their own papers in a barrel and just — left. Walked off down the king's road like men released from service." She straightened, wiping her hands on her apron, suddenly uncertain. "You'll have heard more, I expect. Travelers always do."

Orsen shook her head and smiled, and touched her throat, and spread her hands in the universal gesture.

The woman's face went soft with sympathy. "Oh, love. Since when?"

Vael answered for her, smooth as river-polished stone. "Fever, years back. She manages fine."

"She'd want to be managing better," the woman declared, and disappeared into her kitchen, and returned ten minutes later with a jar of honey that she refused payment for, pressing it into Orsen's hands and closing her fingers over it as if sealing a covenant. "For the voice, if it ever comes back. And if it doesn't" — she patted Orsen's cheek, callused palm warm against skin still roughened by mountain wind — "then for the tea, which you'll take with honey anyway, because life's short."

Life's short. Orsen held the jar all through the rest of the meal, turning it in her hands, feeling the weight of a kindness given freely by a woman who had no idea she was pressing honey into the palms of the person who had ended a war. There was a version of this moment where she laughed until she cried. What she actually did was blink a lot and eat her stew, and when they rose to leave she squeezed the innkeeper's wife's forearm once, hard, and hoped it translated.

Outside, Vael glanced at the jar under her arm. "We're going to be eating honey until we're eighty."

She tapped the jar. Then pointed at him. Then made an exaggerated face of stern warning, brows down, mouth grim.

"That's fair," he said solemnly. "I accept my fate."

* * *

They walked north.

Not because north meant anything — Vael had guild debts scattered across half the map and enemies in the other half, and Orsen had nothing anywhere anymore except a mother's grave outside Greyford and a brother under the mountain who could never leave it — but because the road forked and Vael had looked at both branches, and then at her, and shrugged with his whole soul. So: north. North had the river on their left and the mountains retreating on their right and, according to the tinker they'd passed, a town three days on with a famous pie shop and a bridge-builder's festival that nobody could adequately explain.

Orsen found she liked having no destination the way a drowning woman likes air. For thirty years every step had been weighted — away from hunters, toward coin, along routes planned three moves ahead with contingency and counter-contingency. Now the road simply went, and she went with it, and the only thing she had to carry was the jar of honey and the grief, and the grief was lighter than it had been under the mountain. Not gone. Lighter. Grief, she was learning, got lighter exactly in proportion to how much of it you let yourself feel instead of swallow.

That night, camped under a leaning rowan with a fire small enough to hide from no one, she wrote in the dirt with a stick. It was easier than the slate; the slate reminded her of Fen's ledger-boards, and she wasn't ready for that yet, might not be ready for years.

*Do you regret it?* she wrote. *The guilds. Your route. Everything you traded.*

Vael lay with his hands laced behind his head, boots off, studying the stars through the rowan's bare branches like they owed him money. "The guilds will survive me," he said. "They survived worse. They survived me *joining*, if you can believe it." A pause. The fire popped. "My contact sold us out, you know. Doss. Twenty years I ran his routes, covered his mistakes, pulled him out of a debtor's cell in Karrowmere with my own coin. And the magistrates' silver sang sweeter." He turned his head, and in the firelight his eyes were steady and a little rueful. "So here's what I learned, spending eleven days in a cell waiting to be fed to a fire: loyalty isn't a debt you pay. It's a thing you spend. You spend it where it buys something true. I spent twenty years buying Doss's survival and it bought nothing. I spent one bell at a tollgate lying about a tuning fork, and it bought—" he gestured at her, at the fire, at the whole dark valley, "—this. Best trade I ever made."

Orsen looked at him for a long time.

Then she reached over, took his hand, and pressed it flat against her throat — against the silence there, the whole ruined country of it — and held it while he understood. *This cost me everything I was,* the gesture said. *And I would do it again. That's the terrible arithmetic of it. I would do it again.*

His thumb moved once against her jaw. He leaned over and kissed her forehead, and neither of them said anything, and the fire burned down, and somewhere far below them under miles of stone a dragon-fire burned steady and gold because her brother had given it his living oath, and the ancient dark stayed sealed, and none of it needed her anymore.

She slept without dreaming for the first time in thirty years.

* * *

On the third day they came to the bridge.

Orsen saw it from a quarter mile off and her feet slowed without asking her permission. It shouldn't have mattered. It was nothing like the Glaivewater crossing — that one had been smuggler-built, rotten-planked, sagging over black water with its bones showing, a structure she'd sworn upright twice a year for a decade with whispered words that tasted like copper and cost her a night of shivering afterward. This was a proper king's-road bridge, stone-arched, wide enough for two carts, built by masons who got paid and went home to their dinners.

But it was a bridge, and it was old, and she could see — even at a quarter mile, even with no gift left to see with, just thirty years of a stonemason's daughter's eye — the crack in the third pier. A long seam running diagonally through the mortar, hairline-thin, the kind of flaw that meant nothing for fifty years and everything on the fifty-first. Flood water had been working at it. Winter would finish the job.

The old her would have stood at the near end, laid her palm on the cold stone, and spoken it sound. One word. Ten seconds of warmth draining out of her chest, and then a hundred years of travelers crossing safe without ever knowing a woman had paid for their passage in something no coin could buy.

She stood at the near end now, and the word wasn't there, and the crack was there, and the river ran loud and indifferent underneath.

Vael stopped beside her. He'd gotten good at noticing when she stopped. "What is it?"

She pointed. He followed her finger to the pier, squinted, and frowned. "That?"

She nodded.

"It looks solid to me."

She looked at him. He looked at her. Something moved through his expression — the slow dawning of a man realizing what he was being asked, not to do, but to understand. "You want me to tell someone."

She nodded again, and felt her mouth pull sideways, and had to look away at the river because her eyes had gone hot and ridiculous. Because that was it. That was the whole of it. Thirty years of carrying every failing wall and groaning beam and cracking pier alone in her own throat, secret after secret after secret, the world's hidden damage stacked inside her like stones — and now all she could do was point, and trust that someone else would act. A deaf woman in a burning house. Except the house wasn't burning. Except the world, it turned out, was full of people who would listen, if you could find a way to ask.

Vael was already waving down a cart coming the other way. The driver — a broad, sunburnt man hauling timber — reined in, wary, until Vael pointed at the pier and said, in the easy authoritative tone of a man who had definitely been a bridge inspector in a former life, "Friend, you see that seam? Third pier. That's shear, that is. You'll want the warden at the next town to know before the spring melt. There's a girl coming behind us with her father's tools, or so they told us at the last inn — bridge-mender's family. Someone'll fix it proper, but only if somebody says something."

The timber-hauler leaned out, looked, whistled low. "Well, blow me down. That *is* a seam. Right — I'll tell the warden, and I'll tell the mender's girl, and I'll tell anyone else who'll stand still long enough." He tipped his hat to Orsen. "Good eye, miss."

He rattled off across the bridge, wood clattering, the arches taking his weight without complaint.

Orsen watched him go. Then she stepped onto the bridge.

The stone was cool through her boot soles, worn smooth in the center by a century of feet. She walked slowly, one hand trailing the parapet, feeling for the deep hum she used to feel — the faint vibration of mass and age and stress that her gift had translated into language, *I am tired, I am cracked, I am holding.* There was nothing. Just stone. Just cold, dumb, silent matter doing its work without witness, the way it had done for a century before any swordspeaker ever drew breath.

Halfway across she stopped, in the exact center, over the deepest water, and gripped the parapet with both hands, and bowed her head, and let herself feel the whole size of the loss — the gift, the voice, the brother, the father, the thirty years of hiding, all of it rising in her chest like floodwater finding the crack.

And the bridge held.

Of course it held. It was stone, and stone is patient, and somewhere downstream a timber-hauler was already telling a warden about a seam, and somewhere ahead a bridge-mender's daughter was walking toward it with her father's tools, and the world — the loud, indifferent, unbound, unmagical world — was going to catch its own falling things now, because that was what the world did, when you gave it half a chance and told it the truth about where it hurt.

Vael came and stood beside her, shoulder against hers, saying nothing, needing to say nothing.

Orsen lifted her head. The river ran bright under the arches. Ahead the road climbed away north through green country toward a town with a pie shop and an inexplicable festival, and beyond that toward every nowhere at all, and she was mute, and ungifted, and alive, and free, and the price had been everything, and it was worth paying — she knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, wordlessly, in the body, where all the truest oaths live anyway.

She took Vael's hand. They crossed.

The bridge held.
