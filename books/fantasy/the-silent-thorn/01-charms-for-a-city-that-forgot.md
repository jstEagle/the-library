## 1. Charms for a City That Forgot

The night market of Vessengard woke the way a cat wakes: one eye at a time, pretending it hadn't been listening the whole while.

Wendell Oyelaran had learned to read its moods from behind her little folding table, wedged between a fishmonger who sang off-key and a candle-seller who never sold candles but always smelled of tallow. Her own wares were honest enough on their faces — knotted cords, pressed flowers in wax, small clay beads painted with spirals. Nothing illegal about knots. Nothing illegal about flowers. The Inquisition's lists of proscribed objects ran eleven pages long, and Wendell had memorized every entry just so she could sit comfortably beside them.

"Three coppers for the blue one," she told the child.

The boy couldn't have been more than eight, all elbows and worry, his mother's shawl wrapped around him like armor. His eyes kept darting toward the mouth of Copperlane Street, where the lanterns burned a shade too white for comfort. White lanterns meant inquisitors. Everyone in the market knew that arithmetic without being taught it.

"My sister's having a baby tonight," he said. "Da says the baby won't live. He says it plain, like weather."

Wendell looked at the luck-knot in his grimy fist. She'd tied it that morning with genuine care — three cords, nine turns, a whisper of intent folded into the center like a seed into soil. *Hold fast. Hold warm.* Small magic. The smallest kind. The kind that wouldn't so much as ripple a candle flame if you weren't looking for it.

But the Inquisition was always looking.

"They charge two for those," said the fishmonger loudly, not looking at either of them, mending a net that did not need mending. "Two coppers. Terrible business, overcharging children."

"Prices vary," Wendell said, "by the urgency of the need." She took the two coppers the boy held out — because taking three would have been robbery and taking none would have drawn questions — and watched him run off into the crowd, clutching the knot like a talisman, which, in fairness, it was.

She felt the little working settle somewhere beyond her hands, faint as warmth through a wall. Whether it would be enough, she couldn't say. Knots didn't promise miracles. They promised *inclination* — a midwife's hands steadier by a hair, a fever breaking an hour sooner, a door that stuck until the right person came along. The Thornwright Guild had once called this "the gentle craft," and had taught it in halls with singing stones in the ceilings, and had graduated apprentices by candlelight ceremonies that lasted till dawn.

Now it was taught nowhere, practiced in whispers, and punishable by burning.

Wendell rearranged her beads and thought about supper.

* * *

The patrol came at the change of the watch, as patrols did, moving through the market with the unhurried menace of people paid by the hour. Two gray-cloaked wardens first, then the inquisitor himself: tall, dry-voiced, with a detector-rod of black iron carried across his palms like a sleeping snake.

Wendell knew him by sight. Most of Vessengard did. Dain Corvath — though she only knew the name from other vendors' mutterings — had a face that seemed assembled from the same material as his rod, and a habit of pausing at stalls at random, letting the iron do its slow work while the vendor sweated.

Tonight the iron swung past the fishmonger. Past the candle-man. Past the woman who sold charms that were *only* charms, wood and wire and nothing else, who nevertheless crossed herself twice.

It swung toward Wendell's table.

There was nothing to find. That was the whole discipline of it — there was *nothing to find*. The knot was sold; the residual warmth of her workings faded within hours; her tools were a needle, thread, and beeswax, innocent as bread. But Corvath's rod had a reputation for finding what wasn't there, and men with reputations needed to keep feeding them.

She began packing. Slowly. A closing vendor packs; a fleeing vendor runs, and running is a confession written large enough for anyone to read.

The rod drifted nearer. Wendell could see now the fine etchings along its length, sigils she recognized from a stolen page of guild instruction she'd read at fourteen by the light of a shuttered lamp — *residue-seeking, truth-bending, hungry*. It passed over her table and paused.

Paused.

On the clay beads.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world. The spiral patterns — she'd painted them with pigment mixed, out of habit, out of *carelessness*, with a drop of binder that had once been part of a working. Old magic, months cold. But old magic was still magic, still a scent, and the rod was a nose.

"You sell beads," Corvath said. Not a question. His voice was exactly as dry as advertised, like paper left too long in sun.

"Beads, cords, blessings of a strictly decorative nature," Wendell said. She smiled the smile of a woman profoundly bored. Inside, her heart was performing maneuvers no heart should attempt.

He lifted one bead with two fingers, turning it. The iron rod hovered at his shoulder, quivering faintly, the way a dog quivers before deciding whether to bark.

"The paint," he said. "Where is it sourced?"

"A chandler on Tallow Row." True. "With linseed oil." Also true. "And a great deal of patience." Truest of all.

Somewhere down the lane, a cart overturned with a magnificent crash, and shouting bloomed — a drunk, a dispute, something ordinary and human and gloriously distracting. Every head in the vicinity turned, including, after a moment's visible irritation, the inquisitor's. When he looked back, he set the bead down.

"Vessengard forgets things easily," he said, almost to himself. "That is a mercy. For some." And he moved on, the white lanterns bobbing away into the dark, the rod swaying like a dowsing stick that had found nothing — or chosen, for reasons of its own, to find nothing.

Wendell sat very still for a long moment, then packed the rest of her goods with hands that shook only after they were safely done.

* * *

The guildhouse stood on Thistle Wynd behind a gate that hadn't latched properly in six years, its sign long pried off but its ghost still legible in the pale rectangle of unpainted stone: **THORNWRIGHT GUILD — CHARTERED BY ROYAL GRACE**.

Royal grace had withdrawn rather abruptly, along with royal soldiers, royal fire, and everyone Wendell had ever loved.

Inside, the entrance hall still bore the scorch-shadows of the purge — dark silhouettes on the flagstones where the singing stones had been pulled from the walls and smashed. She'd swept this floor a thousand times. She swept it again now, out of habit, out of something that wasn't quite hope and wasn't quite grief but lived in the same room as both.

Supper was barley and a heel of bread, eaten in the old refectory at the apprentices' table, which seated forty and seated one. She'd tried sitting elsewhere — the masters' high table, the window bench — but the acoustics of the hall seemed to carry loneliness differently depending on where you put it, and the apprentices' table was where hers sounded least like a bell.

"Good news," she announced to the empty benches, because talking to yourself was either madness or survival and she'd decided which. "I nearly got arrested today. Again. The man has excellent timing; I'll give him that."

Silence offered its usual commentary.

She climbed afterward to the scriptorium, where the last of the guild's books hid inside covers disguised as ledgers, almanacs, and — her personal favorite — *A Complete Treatise on Municipal Drainage, Vol. III*. Nine volumes of knotwork theory, herb-craft, and the gentle craft, wearing the most boring costumes Serah— no. Not Serah. The archivist who'd smuggled them out had worn different faces than the one Wendell remembered. She'd been gone ten years, like everyone.

Wendell took down the drainage treatise and read a chapter on binding-knots by candlelight, mouthing the old words, keeping the craft alive in the only way left: one reader, one candle, one night at a time.

It was near midnight when the knock came.

Not the gate — the *side door*, the delivery entrance, the one only guild members used. Nobody living knew that door existed except her, and the dead, and the dead didn't knock.

She froze, candle guttering. Then came a sound worse than knocking: a wet, dragging shuffle, and a voice slurred thin with pain, saying words she would remember for the rest of her life, however long or short the Inquisition allowed it to be.

"Thornwright. Please. I need — a Thornwright."

Wendell blew out the candle.
