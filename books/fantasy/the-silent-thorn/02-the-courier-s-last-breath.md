## 2. The Courier's Last Breath

The trouble with selling luck-knots, Wendell had learned over ten years of it, was that the luck never seemed to apply to the seller.

She'd woven this one at dawn—a simple thing, three strands of river-grass twisted around a chip of amber, knotted in the old Thornwright pattern that was supposed to turn a bad road kind. It had taken her an hour. The knot itself was worth perhaps two coppers in honest materials and nothing at all in honest terms, because honest terms said magic didn't exist, magic had never existed, and anyone who claimed otherwise would be invited to have a very short conversation with the Inquisition.

"Two coppers," she told the boy.

He couldn't have been more than eight, all elbows and worry, his mother's shopping basket clutched like a shield. He'd been hovering at the edge of her stall in the night market for a quarter hour, working up courage the way other children worked up appetites.

"For my da," he said. "He drives the lime carts. Up Harrow Hill." He said *Harrow Hill* the way grown men said the names of battles. "The cart tipped last month. Old Tam's cart tipped, and Tam—"

"I know what happened to Tam." Everyone knew what happened to Tam. The city had needed a closed casket for him. "So your da needs a knot."

The boy nodded, solemn as a magistrate.

Wendell glanced down the row of stalls—tallow-candles, pickled eels, a fortune-teller whose fortunes were accurate only by coincidence—and lowered her voice to the pitch she saved for real work. "Now listen. This isn't just string. You tie it round his wrist, left hand, and you tell him: *the road remembers kindness*. Can you say that?"

"*The road remembers kindness,*" the boy repeated, frowning. "What's it mean?"

"It means if he sees a beggar on the hill road, he stops. Even when he's late. Especially when he's late." She pressed the knot into his grimy palm and folded his fingers over it. "That's what wakes it up. Magic's shy, see. It only comes out for kind people."

This was, technically, a lie. The knot worked on geometry and intention and a whisper of the old guild-words, regardless of who wore it. But the lie cost her nothing and made the world slightly kinder, and Master Edevane had always said that was two-thirds of what charms were actually *for*.

The boy dug out two coppers, then a third, held it up hopefully. "Can I get one for my ma? She burns the bread."

"Burning bread is a talent," Wendell said gravely. "Some people are born to it. But—" she took the third copper, because rent was rent, "—I'll see what I can do."

She was reaching under the counter for a spool of thread when the stranger fell into her stall.

Not *near* her stall. *Into* it. He came through the crowd like a ship taking on water, listing hard to starboard, and then he simply stopped being vertical and crashed across her table, scattering candle-stubs and sending her luck-knot display into undignified exile among the pickled eels.

The market went quiet around them in that particular Vessengard way—not alarmed, not helpful, just collectively deciding how much of this was their problem. (Answer: none of it.)

"Sir?" Wendell crouched beside him. He was young, maybe twenty-five, dressed as a tanner's laborer except that tanners' laborers did not have soft hands or boots worth more than her yearly earnings. His side was soaked dark. Even in the lantern-light she could see it was blood, too much blood, coming from a wound low in his ribs that someone had bound badly and recently.

His eyes found hers. They were fever-bright and terribly clear.

"You're—" he coughed. "Guild. I can smell it on you. The knots. Thornwright."

Her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. Ten years of hiding, of being nobody, of selling trinkets to frightened children, and this bleeding stranger had picked her out of a crowded market in the space of a heartbeat.

"You're mistaken," she said, loudly enough for the neighbors. Quietly: "You need a surgeon. There's one on Tanner's Row, if you can walk—"

"No time." His hand shot out and seized her wrist with shocking strength. From inside his coat he pulled something folded small and sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and pressed it into her hands, folding her fingers over it the way she'd folded the boy's. "The Emberhold. Follow the fire. It gutters, do you understand? It *gutters*—"

"I don't understand anything," Wendell whispered. "I'm a charm-seller. I sell *knots*."

"You're Oyelaran's girl." His mouth twitched toward something like a smile. "I know the guild seal when I see it stitched. She marked every piece she ever made. Said a maker should sign her work even if no one's allowed to read it." His grip tightened. "The king still breathes."

Then the light went out of him between one word and the next, like a snuffed candle, and he was heavy in her arms, and the night market of Vessengard went on haggling around them as though a man hadn't just died holding her hands.

*The king still breathes.*

The words sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. Because everyone knew the king breathed—the Hollow King, the pale beautiful thing in Ashcrown Palace, the one whose face appeared on coins and proclamations and whose eyes, people whispered, were the exact same shade in every portrait ever painted since the Change, as if no artist dared give them variation. Everyone knew the king breathed.

Nobody said he *still* breathed. As though breathing had once stopped. As though the man on the throne were a second draft.

Wendell looked down at the folded thing in her hands. Blood-sealed wax. Royal blood-magic, though she wouldn't learn that word until tomorrow; tonight it was just wrongness, a hum against her palms like a trapped wasp.

And then the whistles started.

Every market vendor in Vessengard knew the Inquisitor's whistle—three rising notes, like a bird that had learned about cruelty. The crowd didn't scatter so much as *dissolve*, flowing away from her stall with practiced smoothness, leaving her alone in a circle of empty ground with a corpse and a package.

They emerged from the alley between the eel-stall and the chandler's: four of them, grey coats, iron masks shaped like serene faces, and the leader carrying a long silver rod that Wendell knew by reputation. A truth-lash. It didn't hurt, exactly. It *unmade*. It unpicked concealments, lies, glamours, and—if applied with enthusiasm—the occasional soul.

"Charm-seller," the leader said. Her voice was muffled and pleasant, which was somehow worse. "We're looking for a courier. Tall, young, wounded."

Wendell's mind, which had spent ten years training for exactly this moment, offered her nothing whatsoever. Her mouth said, "Dead men don't buy knots."

A pause. The serene iron mask tilted. "No," the Inquisitor agreed. "They don't."

Behind her mask, Wendell could feel the woman smiling. Behind her own face, Wendell could feel herself making a decision she would spend the rest of her life either justifying or regretting, and possibly both.

She threw the eel-brine.

The whole jar of it, two days ripe, straight into the lead Inquisitor's serene face. Then she snatched the packet, vaulted her own stall—which she had never once managed in practice—and ran.

The alleys behind the night market were a maze she'd mapped in childhood out of pure paranoia, and paranoia, it turned out, was simply foresight that hadn't happened yet. She took the cut behind the chandler's, ducked the low arch where the tanners hung their worst hides, splashed through the drain-channel that she'd swear on any oath you liked she would *never* run through again and had now sworn on twice this year alone.

Whistles behind her. Boots. Once, the flat crackle of a truth-lash discharging against a wall where her head had been, and the stones there wept a thin grey dust for a moment before forgetting they'd ever been stone.

She ran until her lungs burned and her legs shook and the sounds faded, and then she kept running anyway, because stopping felt like dying, and finally she collapsed in the doorway of a derelict smokehouse and let herself shake properly for a full minute, which she considered a reasonable allotment.

Then she opened the packet.

It was a map. Of course it was a map—folded small, drawn on vellum gone yellow-brown with age, inked in a hand so fine it might have been written with a single hair. It showed the city, then the mountains east of it, then—she turned it toward the moonlight—tunnels. A whole hidden country beneath the mountain, spiraling down and down to a chamber marked with a symbol she knew.

Every apprentice of the Thornwright Guild knew that symbol. It was burned into the lintel of the old chapterhouse door. It was stitched into the hem of her master's coat, the one they'd taken away with her body. A circle, and within it a flame, and within the flame an eye, open.

*The Emberhold.*

The seal of red wax held the map's center shut. As she stared at it, the seal pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"That's not for me," Wendell told it firmly. "Whatever you are, you've got the wrong girl. I sell luck-knots to children. I hide. That's my whole strategy. Hide, and sell, and hide."

The seal pulsed again, and split.

There was no pain at first—only warmth, spreading through her right palm like sunlight through water. She cried out and dropped the map, but the warmth didn't drop; it climbed. When she forced her fingers open, she saw it: the circle, the flame, the open eye, sinking into her skin like ink into wet paper, settling there, glowing faint gold in the dark of the smokehouse doorway.

Branded. Signed. *Marked*, in the way her master used to mark her practice-work: *good, but not yet finished.*

"Oh no," Wendell said, to the brand, to the dead courier, to the universe generally. "Oh no, no, no. Find someone else. Find someone *brave*."

The brand flared once—warm, almost gentle, almost amused—and dimmed to a quiet ember-glow beneath her skin.

Somewhere across the rooftops, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm-bells. The curfew bells, rolling out their nightly sentence: *everyone indoors, everyone asleep, everyone pretending.* And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the Inquisitors' whistles, moving outward from the market in a widening spiral. Searching. Methodical. Patient. They knew a charm-seller had run. They would find the stall by morning. They would find the boy who bought the luck-knot by noon.

Wendell looked at her glowing palm, and at the map lying in the dust, its inked tunnels winding down and down toward a flame with an open eye, toward whatever *it gutters* meant, toward a king who still breathed.

She thought about Master Edevane, signing her work where no one was allowed to read it.

She thought about a boy buying a knot for his father on Harrow Hill.

"Right," she said aloud, voice cracking in the middle, and picked up the map.

The brand pulsed, warm against her palm, like a hand closing around hers—and pulled, gently but unmistakably, eastward. Toward the mountains. Toward the fire.

Toward the one person in Vessengard who might know what royal blood-magic looked like when it decided to choose you—and who hated her nearly as much as she'd earned.

Serah Vance was going to be insufferable about this.
