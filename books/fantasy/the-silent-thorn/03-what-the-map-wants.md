## 3. What the Map Wants

The map did not let Wendell sleep.

She had expected many things from the blood-sealed thing burning on her palm—pain, perhaps, or a slow poison, or the inquisitors kicking down her door before dawn. What she had not expected was that it would have *opinions*.

It woke her at midnight with a tug behind her ribs, gentle and insistent, like a fisherman testing a line. She sat up in her narrow cot in the guildhouse's ruined dormitory, heart hammering, and held her hand to the moonlight. The map had spread since evening. Where it had been a single crimson seal pressed into her skin, thin veins of color now branched outward, tracing roads she didn't recognize through mountains she half-knew from her master's old lessons. And all the lines bent, eventually, eastward. Toward the Emberhold.

"No," she told it.

The tug came again, patient as tide.

"I am not going anywhere," she said, with the particular firmness of someone arguing with parchment embedded in their own flesh. "I have survived ten years by being *unremarkable*. By selling luck-knots and never once looking at an inquisitor for longer than politeness requires. You are the least unremarkable thing that has ever happened to me."

The lines on her palm shimmered, almost smugly, she thought.

She lay back down. She got up again within the hour, because the pulling had grown from suggestion to ache, and because—and this was the part that frightened her most—it wasn't entirely unpleasant. It felt like standing at the top of a hill on a windy day, when something in your chest wants very badly to run downhill with your arms out.

By dawn she had packed a bag she refused to admit was packed. By midmorning she had walked three streets in the wrong direction out of sheer spite, and her palm had throbbed so sullenly that a passing grandmother asked if she needed a healer.

"There is one person," Wendell said aloud, to the empty street, "who might know what you are."

The tugging eased, just slightly. Approving. Which was somehow worse.

* * *

Serah Vance lived above a shop that sold broken clocks, which Serah maintained was a deliberate statement about the passage of time and everyone else suspected was simply what happened when you stopped paying rent.

Finding her had taken Wendell two days of careful asking—not asking after Serah directly, which no one in Vessengard would admit knowledge of, but asking after "the archivist who used to correct Master Aldren's footnotes." That description produced three shrugs and one terrified silence before a candle-seller had jerked his chin toward Clockmaker's Row without meeting her eyes.

Wendell climbed the stairs behind the clock shop, each step announcing her arrival to anyone listening, which she supposed was the point. The door at the top bore no name. It did bear a small chalk mark—a spiral with a line through it—that made her breath catch. An old Thornwright signal. *Knowledge kept under lock.*

She knocked.

"Go away," said a voice inside, dry as autumn paper. "Unless you're the landlord, in which case I've paid, and unless you're an inquisitor, in which case I haven't and I'll die before I do."

"It's about the guild," Wendell said. "It's about the seal of the First Binders."

Silence. Then shuffling, the scrape of a bolt, and the door opened on a woman perhaps fifty years old, sharp-featured, wrapped in a dressing gown that had once been magnificent and now mostly remembered being so. Her eyes were quick and gray and they went straight past Wendell's face to her hand.

"Oh," said Serah Vance. "Oh, you *fool*. Come in before the whole street sees it."

* * *

The room above the clocks was crammed floor to rafters with books, scrolls, jars of ink gone to varnish, and at least one stuffed owl that watched them both with taxidermied judgment. Serah lit three candles, pulled Wendell's hand beneath the light, and went very still.

"The courier," she said. "Describe him."

"Young. Guild tattoo, burned off but I could see the ghost of it. He was bleeding from—" Wendell's throat tightened at the memory. "From everywhere. He said the king still breathes. Then he died, and this—" she turned her palm, and the map shifted, roads glinting like embers, "—happened."

"And you didn't burn it off."

"I tried. Well." Wendell hesitated. "I thought about trying. It hurts less than it should and more than it makes sense to, and every time I get near flame it just... settles deeper."

Serah made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter had been wrung through grief first. "Of course it does. It's royal blood-magic. Old law. Older than the crown that outlawed it." She released Wendell's hand and began pulling books from a shelf with the speed of long habit. "The seal on your palm is the Embermark—the bearer's writ. There were only ever two ways to carry it: be born to it, or be given it by dying hands that chose you."

"Chose me? He was a stranger. He was *dying*. He probably grabbed the nearest person wearing a guild charm—"

"He grabbed the person the map wanted him to grab," Serah said flatly. She opened a massive folio, its pages soft as cloth, and turned it toward Wendell. Inked there, faded but unmistakable, was the same branching pattern now living in her skin. Beneath it, in a dead archivist's careful hand: *The Embermark seeks the Thornwright line. It has always sought us.*

Wendell stared until the words blurred. "That can't be right. My master never—"

"Your master never told you much of anything, I'd wager, because telling apprentices things got them hanged." Serah's voice gentled despite itself. "The Thornwrights weren't charm-sellers who got too bold, girl. We were *keepers*. Before magic was outlawed, the guild tended the dragon-fire directly. Every warden of the ember was Thornwright-trained. When the crown sealed the Emberhold, we became inconvenient. When the king changed—" she paused, choosing her words like steps across ice, "—we became evidence."

"The king changed." Wendell said it carefully.

Serah looked at her for a long moment over the candlelight. "You felt it too, then. Everyone did, those of us who could feel anything at all. Nine years ago the warmth went out of the world's magic like a hearth going cold. Spells still work—barely—but they work the way a dying man walks." She closed the folio. "And now a map has chosen you, and it wants to go east, and I would bet every broken clock below us that it wants to go to the Emberhold."

The tug behind Wendell's ribs pulsed, agreeable.

"I'm not going," Wendell said. "I've spent ten years learning how to be small. Small keeps you alive."

"Yes," Serah said. "It does. And it also means that when you die, you'll die small, having done nothing, having saved nothing, and the fire will gutter out and everything your master bled for will have been for nothing at all." She said it without cruelty, the way one reports weather. Then she sighed, rubbed her eyes, and Wendell saw suddenly how tired she was, how many years of hiding sat in the lines of her face. "I know exactly how that argument sounds. I've been making it to myself for fifteen years."

"Then come with me," Wendell said, before she could think better of it.

Serah blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"You're an archivist. This is—" Wendell gestured helplessly at her own burning palm, at the folio, at the stuffed owl, at everything. "This is an archive question. A really enormous, terrifying archive question. And I don't know anything except knots and hiding, and if I walk into whatever this is alone I will die in a ditch somewhere and the map will pick some other poor fool." She swallowed. "Please. I don't even know what questions to ask."

For a moment Serah only looked at her, and Wendell braced for refusal—for the sensible, survivable refusal she herself would have given a year ago.

But Serah was already reaching for a traveling coat.

"I want it noted," she said severely, shrugging into it, "that I am coming because the Embermark cannot be studied from a distance, and not because a young woman with her master's eyes asked me nicely."

"Noted," Wendell said, and discovered she was smiling, and that her palm had stopped hurting altogether.

* * *

They left Vessengard two days later, at dawn, through the Salt Gate where the guards were bored and the toll was cheap and nobody looked twice at a charm-seller and her fussy aunt. Serah had insisted on the disguise. "Fussy aunts are invisible," she explained, adjusting a gray shawl over her magnificent gown-coat with visible mourning. "It's the one gift of age. Use it."

They were a day out from the city, following the eastern road through country gone gold and brown, when Serah finally asked the question Wendell had been dreading.

"The courier. Before he died. Did he say anything else? Anything at all?"

Wendell watched the road ahead, where the mountains were beginning to rise like a dark thought on the horizon. "He said the king still breathes. And he said—" she closed her eyes, hearing it again, the wet rasp of it, "—he said *she'll know the way, tell her the thorn remembers*."

Serah stopped walking.

"Serah?"

"That's not possible," the archivist said quietly. "That phrase was guild-master's cipher. Sealed. It hasn't been spoken aloud since—" Her face had gone pale beneath its wind-reddening. "Wendell. Who trained you? Tell me your master's name."

"Aldren," Wendell said. "Master Aldren Vess. Why? What does the thorn remember?"

But Serah wasn't looking at her anymore. She was looking past her, up the road, at the figure standing in the middle of it—a rider, dismounted, armored in black lacquer with the crown's white eye blazoned across the breastplate. And behind the rider, cresting the hill, came four more.

"Inquisitors," Serah breathed. "Down, girl—"

Too late. The lead rider raised one gauntleted hand, and though they were fifty yards apart, her voice carried clear and cold across the empty road.

"Wendell Oyelaran," the inquisitor called. "You are carrying stolen property of the Crown. Kneel, and perhaps the fire takes you quickly."

Behind Wendell's ribs, the map pulsed once—eastward, urgent, alive—and the first of the hunters began to run.
