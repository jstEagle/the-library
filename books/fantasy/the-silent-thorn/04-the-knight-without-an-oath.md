## 4. The Knight Without an Oath

Serah Vance had catalogued forty-one varieties of forest silence in her years as a guild archivist, and the one pressing against them now was not on any list.

She walked behind Wendell with her satchel clutched to her chest like a shield made of paper and regret, listening to the road. The pines should have been loud this time of year — jays quarrelling over cones, squirrels scolding, the whole rude machinery of the woods going about its business. Instead there was nothing. The birds had packed up and left without leaving so much as a forwarding note.

"That's wrong," she said for the third time that morning.

"You've mentioned," Wendell said. She had the map out again, or rather her palm out, because the map no longer troubled itself to be a map; it simply burned faintly beneath her skin like a coal wrapped in flesh, tugging her northward whenever she let her hand hang loose. "You said it at dawn. You said it when we stopped to eat the bread that tastes like a punishment. You're saying it now."

"Repetition is the foundation of scholarship."

"Repetition is the foundation of nagging." But Wendell's mouth was tight, and her eyes kept flicking to the treeline, so Serah knew the lesson had landed. The girl had grown up in a guildhouse full of adults who spoke in riddles and warnings; she recognized danger the way other people recognized weather.

They crested a rise where the old king's road dipped through a cutting of grey stone, and Serah felt the silence change texture. Not absence anymore. *Attention.*

"Wendell," she whispered. "Down."

Wendell dropped. An arrow buried itself in the post where her head had been, quivering, fletched with white goose-feather and a sliver of red lacquer. Crown work. Inquisitor issue. Serah knew the specification from a monograph she had personally annotated at nineteen, which was the sort of knowledge that seemed impressive until the moment someone tried to put an arrow through you because of it.

"Hunters!" she shouted, uselessly, because obviously they were hunters, and then did the only scholarly thing available: she threw herself into the ditch and began cataloguing her regrets by order of severity.

There were six of them, maybe seven, dropping out of the trees with the practiced grace of men paid by the capture. Grey cloaks, half-masks, crossbows. They spread across the road in a crescent, closing like a hand around the two of them crouched in the ditch, and their leader called out in a voice polished smooth by repetition:

"For the girl bearing the mark! Take her breathing! The other one — the crown has no interest in archivists."

"How flattering," Serah muttered. "I've been demoted to collateral."

Wendell grabbed her arm. Her hand was glowing. Not the map-glow, not the soft coal-light of the brand — something brighter, something with teeth in it, and Serah realized with a lurch that the last apprentice of the Thornwright Guild was about to do exactly the thing she'd spent ten years not doing in front of witnesses.

"Wendell, don't—"

A horn sounded.

Not a hunter's horn. A hunter's horn was a short bark, two notes. This was long and low and rolling, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and every man in the crescent flinched as though the note had passed physically through them.

Then the trees on the far side of the cutting began, one after another, to fall down.

That was Serah's first impression, anyway — trees toppling like dominoes, splinters flying — until she understood that what was actually happening was that something very large and very fast was *removing* the obstacles between itself and the hunters, and doing so with what could only be described as professional courtesy.

The horse came through the wreckage first: a warhorse the colour of wet slate, ears pinned, moving like a landslide that had learned manners. The rider came second, and Serah's scholar's brain, even mid-terror, reached automatically for its files.

Armour: mismatched but well-kept, the black-and-silver of the King's Own scraped and re-blacked so the royal sigil no longer showed. Sword: drawn, guild-forged steel by the ripple pattern, pre-Ban, probably looted from a chapterhouse armoury — possibly *their* chapterhouse, which would be either a coincidence or a story. Bearing: terrible. Magnificent. The bearing of a woman who had once been reviewed by marshals and had since decided reviews were for other people.

Dame Ilsa Reyne. Oathbreaker. The Knight Without an Oath. Serah had read three conflicting accounts of her disgrace and had privately suspected all three were written by men she'd humiliated.

The reality was simpler and worse. Ilsa Reyne hit the hunters' line like a verdict.

It lasted less than a minute. It was not elegant — Serah had read enough campaign histories to know elegance was mostly invented afterward by poets — but it was decisive. Two hunters went down to the flat of the blade before they finished turning. One fired his crossbow at her and might as well have thrown it. The leader, to his credit, actually managed to engage her for four full exchanges before she caught his wrist, turned him neatly, and introduced him face-first to a tree.

The remaining two looked at each other, arrived at the same conclusion, and ran.

The knight sat her horse in the sudden, ringing quiet, sword resting against her pauldron, and looked down into the ditch where two fugitives were huddled like a pair of guilty hedgehogs.

"Well," she said. Her voice was gravel soaked in honey, or honey soaked in gravel; Serah couldn't decide. "Which of you is carrying royal blood-magic on your skin?"

* * *

They sat around a fire an hour later, because apparently being rescued by an oathbreaker came with catering.

The knight had led them off the road to a hollow beneath a rock shelf, watered the horses, produced bread that tasted considerably better than theirs (a low bar), and now watched Wendell with an expression Serah slowly identified as *grief wearing patience as a disguise*.

"You know my name," Ilsa said. "Everyone does. Oathbreaker Reyne. Traitor of the Vellum Bridge. Ask me why I broke it."

"I didn't ask anything," Serah pointed out.

"You were composing the question. I could see it. You have the look — you're an archivist." She said the word the way some people said *heretic*, and others said *darling*. "So ask."

Serah glanced at Wendell, who was holding her branded palm near the fire as though warming it, though the brand needed no warmth. The girl nodded, small and wary.

"Why did you break your oath?" Serah asked.

Ilsa turned her hands over. There was a scar across both palms, old and white, twin lines — the oath-cut, self-inflicted at knighting, the same on every knight of the King's Own. Except hers had been reopened, roughly, recently, deliberately.

"Because eight years ago I stood guard outside the king's chambers and watched him eat a memory," she said. "Out of the air. Out of a serving girl. He leaned close, breathed in, and everything she was went dim — her name, her mother's face, the tune she used to hum. She just... stood there. Smiling. Empty as a cup."

The fire popped. Nobody moved.

"I reported it," Ilsa went on, conversational, awful. "To my marshal. Three days later the marshal gave a speech at court, praised the king's vigour, and could not remember her own daughter's birthday. So I understood how reports were handled. I cut my oath out of my hands and I left, and I have spent eight years following rumours, and graveyards, and the trail of everyone else who noticed and didn't survive noticing." She looked at Wendell. "Until a courier named Tam Wicker got a blood-sealed map out of the Emberhold and started looking for someone the seal would answer to. He wrote to me. He said if anything happened to him, find whoever carries it."

"He died in my stall," Wendell said quietly. "He said the king still breathes."

"The man breathes," Ilsa said. "What wears him is another matter." She fed a stick to the fire. "Your guild archives will have the word, archivist, if you go back far enough. Pre-Ban shelves. The sealed folios nobody's allowed to touch."

Serah's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution, because she *had* gone back that far, illegally, twice, and she had seen the word, and she had told herself it was myth, taxonomy, folklore. She heard herself say it anyway, in the dry voice she used for catalogue entries:

"*Hollow.*"

Ilsa nodded. "A creature that eats memory. Not flesh — memory. It doesn't kill you; that would be wasteful. It drinks you until there's nothing left worth keeping, and then it keeps the shell smiling. And eight years ago, something Hollow found our king alone, and drank him down to the dregs, and put on his body like a coat." She looked into the fire. "I served that king. I held his standard at Coldmarch. I know the difference between the man and the thing wearing his smile. The thing doesn't love anyone. It only remembers loving, the way a parrot remembers words."

"And the dragon-fire," Serah said. Her mind was running ahead of her mouth now, pages turning somewhere behind her eyes. "If the fire gutters while the Hollow sits the throne —"

"Magic dies," Wendell said softly. "And it never comes back."

"Not never," Ilsa said. "But the world forgets how. Like the serving girl. That's what it wants. A world with nothing left to remember is a world it can wear forever." She looked up, and her gaze went from Wendell to Serah and back again, weighing. "Tam's letter said the seal chose its bearer. Guild-blooded. Last apprentice. I've spent eight years looking for a weapon, and instead I've found a girl with luck-knots in her pockets and a librarian who argues with birds."

"I wasn't arguing with birds," Serah said with dignity. "I was *noting* them."

"There it is," said Ilsa, and something happened to her face — a crack, brief, that might have been the ancestor of a smile. "Fine. Here's the shape of it. You can't outrun Corvath's hunters, and you can't out-hide a thing that eats memories, because it will drink your name out of every innkeeper between here and the capital. What you can do is get to the Emberhold before the fire fails, and unbind the king, and burn the Hollow out of him. Which requires a bearer, an archive, and a sword. Or," she added, leaning back against her saddle, "you can refuse my help, walk north alone, and die stupidly in the snow. I've seen it done. It's very popular."

Wendell was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "Why? Why spend eight years? You had a life. You could have gone anywhere."

Ilsa looked at the scarred palms in her lap.

"Because he was my king," she said simply. "The real one. And somebody has to remember him properly, or the Hollow wins twice." She shrugged, armour clinking. "Also I'm extremely good at hitting people with swords, and it seemed a waste."

Serah laughed — a short, startled, undignified sound she hadn't made in eleven years, and she caught Wendell glancing at her with an expression of scholarly interest, filing it away, the little wretch.

"We'll need supplies," Serah said, retreating into competence. "Maps. The northern passes are —"

"I know the passes. I've been hiding in them for eight years." Ilsa was already unbuckling a rolled bundle from her saddle, spreading it open: maps, real ones, hand-corrected, inks in three colours, marginal notes in a soldier's blunt hand. Serah leaned over them despite herself and felt something in her chest unknot slightly. Someone else who corrected margins. Someone else who couldn't leave a wrong elevation standing.

"Dawn start," Ilsa said. "Sleep in turns. And the girl—" she paused, and gentled her voice in a way that was clearly not native to it, "—the girl sleeps in the middle. Hunters take edges."

Wendell blinked at her. "You've known us for one hour."

"One hour and one ambush," Ilsa said. "In my experience that's faster than most families form."

Later, in the dark, with Wendell asleep between them and the brand pulsing faintly under her bandage like a second heartbeat, Serah lay awake listening to the woods come back to life — jays, squirrels, the rude machinery of the world resuming — and thought about the word *family*, and decided the archives could wait a day or two before she filed anything.

She was nearly asleep when Ilsa spoke quietly from the edge of the firelight.

"Archivist. Your guild — the Thornwrights. Before the Ban. Tell me what they did with the fire."

Serah opened her eyes. Somewhere north, under a mountain, under a stolen throne, something ancient was guttering, and she found she already knew the beginning of the answer, and dreaded the rest.

"They tended it," she said. "They were its keepers."

Ilsa was silent a moment. "Then the fire sent for you," she said. "Both of you. And we'd best not be late."

Far to the north, Serah would think later, the ember must have felt it too — three sleepers around a small fire, keeping faith with a larger one. But that night she only dreamed of shelves, and in the dream, every book was burning gently, and none of them were afraid.
