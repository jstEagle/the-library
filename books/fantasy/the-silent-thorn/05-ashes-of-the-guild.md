## 5. Ashes of the Guild

The chapterhouse looked like a dead thing that had forgotten to lie down.

Wendell stood at the edge of the overgrown courtyard, the blood-mark burning faintly on her palm, and let the sight of it wash over her. The Thornwright sigil—a bramble-wrapped hand—still clung above the great doors, though moss had claimed half its fingers and weather the rest. Ivy strangled the archways. A rowan tree had pushed up through the flagstones of what used to be the apprentice garden and now stood taller than the roofline, as if the ground itself had decided to keep something alive here.

"You grew up *here*?" Ilsa asked, reining her horse beside Wendell's mule. The knight's voice was careful, the way people are careful around graves.

"Seven years." Wendell swung down. Her legs ached from four days of hard riding, but she didn't feel it properly yet. She wasn't sure she'd feel anything properly for a while. "Master Aldous said a guildhouse should look like it was loved even when it's empty. He'd be furious at the state of it."

"Or pleased," Serah said quietly, from the other side. "Rowans grow where grief was planted. That's not neglect. That's mourning with roots."

Wendell looked at her. "You've been reading the wrong books again."

"I read all the books. That's the problem with me."

They left the horses in the courtyard, where grass had grown knee-high and soft as a bed, and Wendell pressed her marked palm against the great door. She hadn't expected anything. Ten years of hiding meant expecting nothing was a survival skill. But the old warding woke under her touch anyway—a shiver through the wood, a click like an arthritic knuckle—and the door swung open onto dust and dimness and the smell of ash that had never fully left.

Inside, the entrance hall still bore the scorch marks.

She had known they would. She had seen them made, from across the square, held between two market women who pretended to be examining ribbon while a fourteen-year-old girl watched the Thornwright Guild burn in miniature—chairs, ledgers, banners, and then Master Aldous Okafor himself, dragged out in chains while the fire they'd set in the hall licked at the windows behind him.

The execution had been three days later. Public. They always were.

"Wendell." Ilsa's voice came from somewhere near her elbow. "You've gone white as bone."

"I'm fine."

"Liar," said Serah, not unkindly. "Sit down before you fall down. There's a bench. Guildhouses always have benches; it's in the founding charter, probably. *Thou shalt provide seating for weary travelers and nervous apprentices.*"

Despite everything, something in Wendell's chest unknotted half a degree. She sat on the bench—which was exactly where she remembered it being, which felt like the cruelest kindness the building could have offered—and put her face in her hands.

* * *

They made camp in the refectory, because the roof there was mostly intact and because Ilsa refused, with the immovable logic of a woman who had slept in ditches for two years, to sleep anywhere without two exits. Serah built a fire in the old hearth, using broken chair legs for kindling, apologizing to each one under her breath like a madwoman.

"Sorry. Sorry. You were good chairs. Someone carved you." She glanced up, caught Wendell watching. "What? Guilt is free. It's the only thing in Vessengard that is."

Ilsa was checking the windows, counting exits, doing whatever it was knights did instead of resting. Wendell wandered.

She told herself she was looking for supplies—there might be stores in the pantry, salves in the stillroom, anything useful. But her feet took her where her feet had always wanted to go: down the west corridor, past the dormitories with their collapsed bunks, to the master's study.

The door was swollen shut. She shouldered it the way he'd taught her to shoulder stubborn things—"with your whole self, girl, hesitation is just fear wearing a polite coat"—and it gave with a groan.

Dust lay over everything like snow. His desk. His shelves. The little brass orrery he'd let her wind on festival mornings, frozen mid-turn. And on the wall behind the desk, where a map of the guild's territories had once hung, a pale rectangle of unfaded plaster.

Someone had taken the map. Inquisitors, probably. Or looters. It didn't matter. What mattered was that Aldous Okafor's study was a wound shaped exactly like him, and Wendell stood in the middle of it and finally, ten years late, began to cry.

She cried the ugly way, the private way, folded down onto the floor with her back against his desk, and she cried until her ribs hurt, and no one came to find her, because she'd chosen a room with one door and her found family, bless them, knew how to count to one.

* * *

It was full dark when Serah found her. Wendell heard the archivist's uneven footsteps in the corridor—Serah walked like a woman permanently apologizing to the floor—and didn't bother wiping her face.

"The knight's asleep," Serah said, easing herself down onto the dusty rug beside Wendell with a grunt. "Or pretending to be. With her it's the same thing." She peered into the dark. "This was his study?"

"Yes."

"He taught you here."

"He taught me everywhere. But yes. Mostly here." Wendell's voice came out scraped raw. "He used to say magic wasn't a weapon. Everyone said that, after the Purge—it was practically a prayer. But he meant it differently. He said magic was a *promise*. Something the world made once and never took back, no matter how many men with hammers tried."

Serah was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone she used for footnotes—the gentlest part of her voice—"There's something I should show you. I found it in the records vault before I was disgraced. I've been carrying it since."

She produced a leather folio, water-stained, its seal long broken. Inside were pages copied in an old, careful hand. She held one toward the hearth-light she'd brought in a lantern, and Wendell leaned close.

It was a diagram. A spiral of knots—not thread-knots like the luck-charms Wendell sold in night markets, but vast ones, drawn in lines that wrapped around mountains. At the center of the spiral was a symbol she knew intimately, because it was branded into her palm: a flame within a ring.

"What is this?" she whispered.

"That," Serah said, "is the reason the Thornwrights existed. Not charms, Wendell. Not luck-knots and fever-wards and love tokens sold at fairs—though those mattered too, don't misunderstand me. Those were the *edges* of the work. The center was this." Her finger traced the spiral. "Every knot the guild ever tied drew its strength from one source. The Emberhold. The dragon-fire beneath the mountain. We weren't hedge-witches hiding from kings. We were keepers. Tenders of a fire older than the crown. Every charm you've ever tied has been fed by it, whether you knew it or not."

The blood-mark pulsed on Wendell's palm, warm as a sleeping bird.

"When the fire gutters," Serah went on softly, "the knots fail. All of them. Everywhere. And when it dies—" she closed the folio "—magic doesn't get outlawed, Wendell. It gets *unmade*. Every gift, every ward, every small bright thing. Gone so completely the world won't remember it had them. That's what's coming. That's why the map chose you."

"It didn't choose me. It grabbed me because a dying man fell on my stall."

"Mm." Serah smiled the small, infuriating smile of a woman holding a footnote in reserve. "And how many bearers do you suppose it burned through before it reached a Thornwright? Blood calls to blood. The guild bound itself to that fire eight hundred years ago. You're not carrying the map, child. You're *answering* it."

Wendell stared at the diagram—at the mountain-wrapping spiral, at the little flame in its ring—and felt something shift in her chest, the way a lock shifts when the right key finally turns.

All those years of hiding. Of selling luck-knots with her sleeves pulled low, of flinching from every grey cloak, of burying her gifts so deep she'd nearly buried herself. She had thought she was surviving. She had thought survival was the whole of it.

But Master Aldous hadn't hidden because he was afraid. He had hidden the way an ember hides in ash—because the fire was more important than the flame, and someone had to keep it, and keeping was quiet work.

"*A promise*," she said aloud. "That's what he called it. I thought he was being comforting."

"He was being accurate. Comforting and accurate aren't enemies, whatever the inquisitors think." Serah gathered her pages with tender hands. "Come sleep by the fire. Dame Doom-and-Two-Exits snores, so pick your spot carefully."

* * *

Wendell did not sleep. Instead she sat in the refectory doorway with her marked palm open in her lap, watching it glow like a coal breathing, and spoke to the darkness in general and one dead man in particular.

"I'm going to do it," she murmured. "Whatever it wants. Whatever it costs. You kept the promise your whole life and they burned you for it, and I ran, and running was right—I know it was right, I survived—but I'm done running now." Her thumb traced the brand. "Teach me the last lesson. The one you never got to."

Behind her, Ilsa's voice came soft from the dark of the refectory, wide awake after all. "*Keepers don't run.*"

Wendell turned. The knight sat propped against her saddle, sword across her knees, eyes open.

"How long—"

"Since Serah went to find you. I don't sleep well in dead buildings." Ilsa paused. "My commander died protecting the king's secret. I ran instead. So believe me when I say this: the running ends badly either way. Better to end it on purpose, walking toward something."

From her bedroll, muffled and indignant, Serah: "Are we having a moving moment without me? Unacceptable. I'm sitting up. Ow. Floor."

Wendell laughed—wet, cracked, real—and the sound echoed strangely in the ruined hall, and the blood-mark flared so bright it threw shadows across the walls.

Not a flare. A pulse. Rhythmic. Patient.

Pointing east, toward the mountain.

Serah sat bolt upright, lantern-light catching her face. "That's new."

"No," Wendell said slowly, rising, watching the light swing like a compass needle finding true north. "I think it's been waiting for us to stop grieving long enough to listen."
