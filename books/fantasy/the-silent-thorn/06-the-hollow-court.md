## 6. The Hollow Court

Ilsa Reyne had worn many uniforms in her life: the white-and-gold of the King's Vanguard, the mud-brown of a border garrison, once, memorably, a chicken-seller's apron during the Siege of Kelmoor. She had never worn a dress with this much embroidery. It itched like penance.

"You're pulling at the sleeve," Serah murmured beside her, not looking at her. They stood in the slow river of nobility flowing toward the palace gates, all of them wrapped in furs against the capital's grey drizzle.

"I'm adjusting the sleeve."

"You've adjusted it nine times."

"I'm counting differently."

Ahead of them, Wendell snorted—a small, undignified sound quickly smothered behind a lace fan she'd been taught to hold that afternoon. The girl was disguised as Serah's niece, a merchant-family ward come to the capital for the winter presentation. She looked, Ilsa thought, terrified and furious about it, which was at least honest. The rest of the crowd wore their lies like powder on the face.

The gates swallowed them in twos and threes. Guards in black lacquered armor checked invitations, checked faces, checked hands—checked hands?—and Ilsa felt Wendell go rigid as a gloved inquisitor took the girl's fingers briefly in his own, turning them palm-up, reading them like a ledger.

"New to court," the inquisitor said. It wasn't a question.

"First season," Serah said smoothly. "Her mother insisted she see the King's Radiance before the year turns."

"Everyone insists." He let go. "Mind you don't stare."

"Of course not," said Serah.

Everyone stared. That was what court *was*, Ilsa remembered—an arena where staring was done sideways and knives came from the front, smiling.

* * *

The throne room had been beautiful once. Ilsa had knelt on those flagstones at seventeen, sworn her oath beneath windows of blue glass, and believed every word of it. Now the blue glass was gone, replaced by panes of something milky and faintly luminous, and the light that fell through them did not warm the floor so much as pool on it, reluctant, like a cat forced to sit still.

And there, on the dais, sat the king.

He was beautiful. That was the first wrongness. Ilsa had known Aldric Vane when he was forty and weathered, a man with a soldier's sunburn and a scholar's stoop, who laughed too loud and apologized for it. The thing on the throne had kept only the bones of him—the jaw, the brow—and smoothed away everything else, until he looked less like a man than a statue of one, carved by someone who had heard descriptions secondhand.

"He doesn't blink," Wendell whispered. "Serah. He hasn't blinked since we came in."

"Don't look directly at him," Serah breathed back.

"Why?"

"Because he'll notice."

The court flowed around the dais in its rituals—presentations, petitions, a duke's daughter reciting a poem about spring while snow tapped the milky windows. And through all of it, courtiers approached the throne in ones and twos, knelt, and offered up their hands.

That was when Ilsa saw the feeding.

A young baron knelt and kissed the king's signet ring. For a heartbeat—no longer—the light in the room bent toward the throne, like candleflames leaning in a draft. The baron rose looking pleased, glowing, honored. But his eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere behind himself, and as he walked away he passed a woman he clearly knew and greeted her with the wrong name. Twice.

"He ate his memory of her," Wendell whispered. Her hand had found Ilsa's sleeve under the fan. "Ilsa. He just—he *ate* it. Like bread."

"Yes."

"That's—" The girl swallowed. "How long has he been doing this?"

"Years," Ilsa said quietly. "Since before I ran. Perhaps since the beginning." She watched another courtier rise from the dais, radiant and hollow-eyed, and felt an old cold move down her spine. "They come back, you know. Week after week. They give more. They think they love him."

"Do they?" Serah asked.

"I don't know. That's the worst of it."

* * *

They drifted, as courtiers do, toward the wine. Serah worked the crowd with the ease of a woman who had spent thirty years in archives learning exactly which gossip mattered, and within the hour Ilsa had learned three useful things: the inquisitors' barracks had doubled its night watch; the eastern stair to the royal apartments had been sealed since midsummer; and no one, not one soul in this glittering hall, could remember the king's voice saying anything longer than a blessing.

"He speaks," a marquise confided to her wineglass, "so rarely now. And when he does, it's like listening to your own thoughts echo. Comforting. Like being *known*." She shivered pleasantly. "Isn't it wonderful?"

"It's wonderful," Ilsa agreed, and smiled with teeth she hoped looked like admiration rather than nausea.

It was Wendell who went pale first. Ilsa saw it happen across the room—the fan lowering, the color draining—and followed her gaze to the dais, where a tall figure in the King's colors had just finished kneeling.

Where the king had just risen, and begun to walk.

Toward them.

"Positions," Serah said, very softly, and the word moved through the three of them like a current. Ilsa turned her body into the crowd, angled her face away—but there was nowhere truly to go. The hall was a bowl. The king descended into it step by step, courtiers parting before him like water, bowing, beaming, offering their hands as he passed, and the light bending toward him each time, and—

And Ilsa made the mistake.

She looked.

Not directly; she wasn't a fool even in terror. But enough. Enough to see the face properly as it drew near, past the beauty, past the sculpted calm, and to see what was underneath the smoothing, the way grief shows through fresh paint on an old wall.

She knew that face.

Not the king's. Not Aldric Vane's.

Captain Maerin Solhaut. Commander of the Third Vanguard. The woman who had taught Ilsa how to hold a shield wall and how to lose a soldier without losing herself—who had died at Greyford Ford holding a bridge for eleven hours so that four hundred civilians could cross, who had been buried with full honors while Ilsa held the corner of the pall with numb fingers, whose last order to Ilsa had been *go home, Reyne, you've done enough*—

The Hollow stopped three feet away.

Up close, the wrongness was worse. Maerin's face fit the king's frame the way a stolen coat fits—not quite, seams straining, the eyes a half-inch wrong, the smile arriving late like a messenger who'd forgotten why he'd come.

"Dame Reyne."

Her name. In Maerin's voice. Slightly flattened, slightly sweetened, like honey poured over stone.

Around them, the nearest courtiers had gone still in that particular way of people pretending very hard not to witness something. Ilsa felt Wendell press close at her shoulder, felt Serah arrive at her other side like a wall of calm, felt her own heart hammering against the ribs of her ridiculous embroidered bodice.

"Your Radiance honors me." Her voice came out steady. Ten years of practice at steadiness, spent on smaller things.

"The court is brighter tonight." The Hollow tilted its head, and the gesture was Maerin's—the little tilt she'd used when weighing a subordinate's report—and Ilsa's chest seized around the sight of it. "You have carried something heavy for a long time, Dame Reyne. Guilt makes good shoulders. You should set it down." A pause. The milky eyes caught the strange light. "Greyford was not your failure. You were not fast enough, but you were faithful. Isn't that what you tell yourself?"

The hall was silent except for snow against glass.

Every instinct Ilsa had screamed at her to kneel, to weep, to offer her hands—to become one of the radiant hollow-eyed things drifting through this room. Because it would be easy. Because it knew exactly where the wound was, and how to make the hurting stop, and it was *offering*.

Instead she thought of Maerin—real Maerin, mud on her cheekbones, laughing at her own terrible jokes—and what she would have said if some pretty thing wearing her face had tried this trick in front of her soldiers.

"She never called me faithful," Ilsa said. "She called me stubborn. She'd have hated you for borrowing her."

Something flickered across the Hollow's face—hunger, or curiosity, or the ghost of surprise. The light in the room leaned harder toward it.

"How interesting," it said. "You keep your memories *sharp*. Most people let them soften. It hurts less." It smiled then, and it was the king's smile and Maerin's smile and neither, and Ilsa understood suddenly, completely, that this creature had known she would be here. Had perhaps arranged it. "Keep them sharp, Dame Reyne. We will speak again. Sharp things are the most nourishing."

It moved on. The court exhaled and resumed its music and its murmuring, and Ilsa discovered that Serah had a grip on her elbow firm enough to leave marks, and that Wendell had positioned herself squarely between Ilsa and the throne like a small furious hedgehog defending a larger, dumber animal.

"Breathe," Wendell said.

"I am breathing."

"You're breathing like a bellows in a house fire."

"I'm fine."

"You're crying," Wendell said gently, "just a bit."

Ilsa touched her own cheek and found it true. She hadn't noticed. She wiped it away with the heel of her hand, rough, angry at the tears for arriving without permission. "It wears her face," she said. "It's been wearing her face. How long—"

"Long enough to learn everything she knew," Serah said quietly. "Including you. Including what you loved. Including—" she hesitated, "—including what you blame yourself for. It didn't guess, Ilsa. It *remembered*. Which means somewhere in that thing is everything Maerin ever was, being digested a memory at a time."

Wendell's hand closed over hers. Small, calloused from knot-work, warm.

"Then we get her back," the girl said. "Whatever's left of her. Whatever's left of *all* of them in there. That's what the unbinding does, isn't it? Serah—you said it releases what the fire holds."

Serah's expression did something complicated. "I said the texts suggest—"

"*Suggest,*" Wendell said, "is a very archivist way of saying yes."

Above them, unnoticed by the resuming court, the milky windows flickered—once, twice, like a lamp running low on oil. Somewhere far below the palace, Ilsa thought, the dragon-fire was guttering, and every light in this hall was secretly a countdown.

And then, across the chamber, a figure detached itself from the shadows near the eastern stair. Black lacquer. Inquisitor's sash. Moving toward them through the crowd with unhurried certainty, and Ilsa recognized the walk before she recognized the face.

Dain Corvath. And he was not looking at the king.

He was looking at Wendell's gloved left hand.
