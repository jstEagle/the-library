## 7. Inquisitor's Mercy

Serah Vance had spent thirty years learning that most cages were built of paper. Ledgers, writs, confessions—men built their prisons out of documents, and so men could be unmade by them, if you knew which page to pull.

The cage they'd put her in now, however, was iron, and it rattled horribly as the prisoner-wagon lurched over the mountain road. Across from her, Wendell sat with her wrists bound and her chin high, doing a very poor impression of someone who wasn't terrified. Ilsa was not bound at all, because the inquisitors had taken one look at her shoulders and decided she was probably a knight, and probably best handled carefully. They hadn't taken her sword. That was either arrogance or an invitation to disaster, and Serah intended to find out which.

The wagon's rear bench held a man in grey-and-silver: Inquisitor Dain Corvath himself, riding backward-facing so he could watch them, which Serah considered rude and also useful. Rude because no one liked being watched while they thought. Useful because she had spent the last hour watching him back, and she had noticed something.

Inquisitors of the Crown did not carry mourning rings.

Corvath wore one on his right hand—a plain band of black iron, old-fashioned, the kind that had gone out of style eleven years ago. The kind that had been fashionable precisely when a true king still sat his throne and people still bothered to mourn things properly.

She said nothing. Not yet. Paper first, always paper. You learned what a cage was made of before you burned it down.

"You're staring," Corvath said.

"I'm cataloguing," Serah said. "It's an archivist's habit. You have very interesting hands."

His jaw tightened. "You'll have less to say after questioning."

"Undoubtedly. Questioning tends to do that." She shifted her bound wrists into her lap, deliberately, like a woman settling in for a lecture. "Though I wonder, Inquisitor—when you question us, will you ask about the map? Or will you already know? Because there's a difference between asking questions and performing them, and I've watched enough interrogations to know the performance when I see one."

Wendell shot her a look that clearly meant *what are you doing*. Serah ignored it. This was, after all, the family business now: Wendell made the small magics, Ilsa broke the large obstacles, and Serah talked until something gave way. Every household had its chores.

"The map," Corvath said quietly, "was destroyed with the courier."

"It was *carried* by the courier." Sera let the silence stretch. "And couriers are hired to die, Inquisitor, not to succeed. Whoever sent him expected him to be caught. Expected the map to fall into your hands. Ask yourself why."

Something flickered behind Corvath's eyes—not doubt exactly. Doubt was a door; this was more like a draft through a door he'd thought long nailed shut.

"You're lying."

"I'm an archivist of the Thornwright Guild. Lying is beneath me. Misleading, however—" she smiled thinly, "—is simply indexing."

* * *

They stopped at dusk at a waystation that had once been a tollhouse and was now mostly roof and regret. The inquisitors—there were six, plus Corvath—put them in the cellar, which Serah noted with professional appreciation had excellent acoustics. Everything spoken above drifted down through the floorboards like weather.

"He knows," Wendell whispered, pressing close. "About the map. He has to. The courier—"

"The courier told him nothing, or Corvath would already have it. No." Serah worked at her bindings with a hairpin she'd liberated from her own collar during the arrest, an act of theft she considered the finest thing she'd done all year. "He suspects. And suspicion is a wound, dear girl. You don't attack a wounded man. You offer him a bandage and see where he bleeds."

"That's horrible."

"That's *archival*."

Ilsa, sitting against the wall with the serenity of a woman who had decided violence was inevitable and therefore not worth worrying about, said, "If your bandage fails, I'll take the stairs."

"If my bandage fails," Serah said, "we will all be dead, and you can take whatever stairs you like in the afterlife."

Above them, boots crossed the floor. Voices, low. Once—just once—she heard Corvath say, in a voice stripped of its office, "Eleven years. Eleven years and nobody asks what happened at the coronation." And another voice answering with the flat recitation of doctrine: *the king is the king*, and then footsteps moving away.

Serah closed her eyes and let herself smile. There it was. The bleeding place.

"They're going to burn us," Wendell whispered. "That's what happens. Trials and pyres. Master Aldous—"

"I know what happens," Serah said, gentler than she meant to. "I attended. I indexed it, too. Which is why we are not going to be burned. We are going to be *useful*, and usefulness is the only mercy this crown understands." She freed her wrists at last and began on Wendell's bindings. "But listen to me, Wendell Oyelaran. What I'm about to do will feel like betrayal. It is not. Trust is a tool like any other—you can build with it, and you can cut with it, and tonight I intend to do both."

Ilsa snorted softly. "You're going to tell him about the map."

"I'm going to tell him a version of the map. The truth is a wardrobe, Dame Reyne. Tonight we show him one garment and keep the rest hung in the dark."

* * *

They brought her up when she demanded to be brought up—pounded on the door until an irritated inquisitor fetched Corvath, who came down with the weary expression of a man whose prisoners would not behave like prisoners.

"A word," Serah said. "Alone. Or I scream guild secrets loud enough for your six colleagues to memorize them."

"You have no leverage."

"I have information and a big mouth. That's more leverage than most armies start with."

He studied her for a long moment. Then he waved his men upstairs and descended into the cellar alone, lantern in hand, and set it on a barrel between them like a border marker.

"Speak."

"The courier carried a blood-sealed map to the Emberhold. Royal work. King's-blood binding—the real thing, not the theater they teach inquisitors these days. It marked itself onto the bearer's palm." She turned her own empty hands outward, palms up, honest as a ledger. "Not mine. Hers." A tilt of her chin toward Wendell. "Which means the map chose, and maps sealed in royal blood choose by lineage, and if you know anything at all about the Thornwright Guild's founding charters, you know why that should keep you awake."

Corvath's hand—the one with the black iron ring—had curled into a fist without his permission. Serah watched it happen and catalogued it.

"There is no Emberhold fire," he said. "There is no map. There is a traitor's fantasy."

"There is a Hollow on the throne," Serah said, very softly, "and you know it. You've known since the coronation. Eleven years, Inquisitor. Who were you mourning when everyone else was cheering?"

The lantern flame stood perfectly still. So did Corvath.

Then, slowly, he drew off his glove, and the black iron ring caught the light. "My brother," he said. "Captain of the king's own guard. He was at the ceremony. He wrote me the night before—one line. *Whatever you see tomorrow, remember the man, not the shape.* By morning he was dead. Officially: fever. Unofficially, I dug up his grave and found it full of stones." He looked at his bare hand as though it belonged to a stranger. "I have served the thing that killed him for eleven years. Do not speak to me of what I know."

"Then serve him instead," Serah said. "The true king breathes. Under the mountain, in the dark, half-eaten by the thing wearing his face—and there is a girl downstairs who can unbind him. But only if she reaches the ember alive. Only if certain inquisitors find themselves mysteriously slow to report, quick to misfile, and generous with detours."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow you deliver three heretics to the capital, and you spend the rest of your life filing reports to a creature that eats memories, wondering which of yours it took last." She let that sit. "You've already lost a brother to stones and lies, Dain Corvath. Don't lose the rest of yourself to paperwork."

For a long time he said nothing. The lantern guttered, steadied. Somewhere overhead, a floorboard creaked under Ilsa's patient weight.

"The map," he said at last. "Show me her hand."

They brought Wendell up, and she held out her palm, and the brand flared at his nearness—gold veins crawling across her skin like frost in reverse, the shape of roads and mountains and a small burning heart. Corvath stared at it the way starving men stare through bakery windows.

"It's real," he whispered. "Gods help me, it's real."

"Gods help all of us," Serah agreed. "Now. Here is my bargain. You escort us to the mountain pass—as prisoners, officially. Your reports say you're tracking the heretics to their nest. Your men believe every word, because men believe what pays their wages. And at the pass, you let us walk, and you ride home and misfile everything."

"And if the crown sends others after you?"

"Then others come," Serah said. "But they won't have you. And you—" she tapped the black ring where it lay on the barrel beside his glove, "—will finally have stopped digging your brother's grave deeper."

Corvath looked at Wendell. Wendell, to her credit, looked back—frightened, exhausted, and utterly unflinching, the way she'd looked selling luck-knots to frightened children in a market that wanted her dead.

"Why does it choose you?" he asked her quietly.

"Because somebody has to fix it," Wendell said. "Apparently that's me."

He laughed then—a short, broken sound, like a hinge that hadn't moved in years. He picked up his glove, put it back on over the ring, and became an inquisitor again from the boots upward.

"At dawn," he said. "Prisoners ride in front, where I can see you. And Vance—" his eyes were flat and cold and, Serah thought, not entirely without warmth underneath, like a lake in winter, "—if this is a betrayal, I will bury you myself."

"How flattering," Serah said. "Most men just file a report."

* * *

At dawn they rode, and the mountain grew ahead of them like a held breath. Serah sat her horse beside Wendell's and felt, rather than saw, Corvath's gaze moving over them—counting them, perhaps, or praying over them, she couldn't tell.

"You lied to him," Wendell murmured. "Part of it. About the map."

"Of course."

"Will he forgive you? When he finds out?"

"When he finds out," Serah said, "he'll be too busy forgiving himself for eleven years of serving a monster. We're doing him a kindness, really. Guilt shared is guilt halved—it's in all the better proverbs." She squinted up at the peaks, where snow smoked off the ridgeline in long white banners, and felt the map's pull like a hook behind her ribs even secondhand, through Wendell. "Besides, dear girl, I didn't lie. I curated."

Ahead, Corvath raised a fist, and the column halted. Beyond the last bend of the road, where the trees thinned and the pass began, something was wrong with the morning. Smoke, maybe. Or ash. Or the particular grayness of air where magic was dying by inches and the world had begun to notice.

"Riders," one of the inquisitors called from the point. "Crown livery. A full hunting company—they're fanning across the pass."

Corvath's face went carefully blank. Too carefully. Serah read it instantly, and her stomach dropped: orders he hadn't given. Orders he couldn't counter without revealing himself.

"Well," she said softly, to no one in particular, "it seems our truce is about to be tested rather sooner than advertised."
