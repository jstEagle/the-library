## 8. The Road Beneath the Mountain

The map had stopped being paper three days ago.

It lived in Wendell's palm now, a lattice of ember-colored lines that woke when the light failed and dimmed when they rested. She'd tried to explain it to Serah twice and given up both times. It was like explaining a heartbeat to someone who'd never been afraid of losing one.

"Left at the split," Wendell said. "No—sorry. Right. It changed its mind."

"The map has opinions," Ilsa said flatly.

"It's royal blood-magic. Of course it has opinions."

Serah, walking behind them with her pack straps crossed like a man expecting arrest at any moment, made a small scholarly noise. "Blood-magic is directional, not decisive. If it's changing its mind, it's because *we're* running out of time." He adjusted his spectacles, which were cracked down one lens and which he refused to replace on the grounds that he could still see out of the other. "The fire is guttering faster than the archives predicted."

"How much faster?"

"Think of it this way. A candle in a windstorm versus a candle in—" He gestured vaguely at the mountain's throat around them, the black stone sweating cold. "Well. Versus whatever this is."

Wendell looked down at her palm. The lines were fainter than yesterday. Not gone—she didn't think they could be gone while she carried them—but thin, the way a voice goes thin when the speaker is tired of shouting.

She felt it too. That was the part nobody warned you about: that magic failing wouldn't feel like absence. It felt like *muffling*. Like the whole world had been wrapped in wool. Her little charms—the luck-knots, the warmth-spells she wove into their bedrolls at night—still worked, but each one cost more, as though she were drawing water from a well with a thimble. Last night she'd warmed Ilsa's hands after the knight fell asleep on watch in the frost, and the effort had left her shaking for an hour.

Ilsa had pretended not to notice. Ilsa was very good at pretending not to notice things, in the way of people who noticed everything.

* * *

They made camp that evening in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Wendell did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

Their food would last four days if Serah ate like a scholar (he didn't; he ate like a man who'd once been starved and never trusted abundance again). Five if Ilsa gave up half her share, which she would do quietly and lie about afterward. The Emberhold's inner gates were somewhere below them—a day's walk through the tunnels, maybe two, if the map's opinions held.

And behind them, two days back or less, came Corvath.

"You're doing the face," Ilsa said. She sat sharpening her sword with the patience of a woman who believed all problems were ultimately nail-shaped. "The face where your eyebrows are having a private meeting."

"I'm calculating."

"You're worrying. Calculating is when Serah does it and writes it down."

"I heard my name," Serah said, without looking up from the journal he was writing by the light of Wendell's palm, which he insisted was better than any lamp. "For the record, the pursuit math favors us by roughly half a day, assuming Corvath doesn't know the old miners' road."

"He knows it," Ilsa said. "He wrote the report on it. I read his reports for six years. The man annotates."

"Then we have until tomorrow night," Wendell said, "before he finds us."

Silence took its seat among them, uninvited but familiar now. Four days ago it would have been unbearable. Now it was just something they shared, like the cold, like the bread.

It was Serah who broke it, gently, the way he broke most things. "There's a passage ahead. The map shows a junction—the Withered Stair, the old guild maps called it. One wide road and one narrow. The narrow one drops straight toward the inner gates."

"And the wide one?"

"Comes from behind us. From the miners' road." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a habit Wendell had learned meant he was about to say something everyone already knew. "If someone stood at that junction, the wide road could be... discouraged. Collapsed. Delayed."

"No," Wendell said.

"You don't even know what I was going to—"

"You were going to say that Ilsa should hold it alone, and I was going to say no before you finished, because we've all read the same story and nobody likes how it goes."

Ilsa set down her whetstone. For a moment Wendell thought she'd argue—and some tired, frightened part of her wanted her to argue, wanted the clean arithmetic of it, *one life against three, one life against the world.*

Instead Ilsa said, "She's right. Nobody holds a pass alone unless the story requires it, and I refuse to be required."

But she said it looking at the dark mouth of the tunnel, and Wendell saw what she was really saying, which was: *if it comes to it, I will be required, and I need you to let me.*

Wendell put another knot of warmth into the air between them and said nothing, because love, she was learning, sometimes meant letting people carry the weight they insisted on carrying, and being there when the carrying broke them.

* * *

The Withered Stair earned its name.

The narrow road descended in switchbacks cut by hands long dead, the walls veined with quartz that should have caught their light and thrown it back in glittering answer. That was how it used to work—Serah told them so, voice hushed in the way scholars get hushed in old places, which was louder than anyone else's normal voice. "The Thornwrights built these roads to sing. Light, sound, heat—all of it moved along the stone like water in a channel. There are carvings here older than Vessengard. Older than the crown."

"Can you feel them?" Wendell asked.

Serah paused, laid his palm flat against the wall, and his face did something complicated. "Barely. It's like pressing your hand against a door and feeling music through it. Everything's still *there*. Just—" he searched, "—unplayed."

That was the word for what she'd been feeling for days. Unplayed. The world, muffled. Her own magic, a song hummed through closed lips.

They descended through the night and into a gray false-morning that existed nowhere but underground, and somewhere in the small hours Wendell realized her palm had gone dark.

She stopped so suddenly that Ilsa nearly walked into her back.

"Wendell?"

"It's out." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "The map. It's—it went dark."

Serah was beside her instantly, journal open, spectacles abandoned entirely. "Show me. When did you last check? What were you—"

"I checked an hour ago. It was faint but it was *on*, and now it's—" She turned her palm up, empty, ordinary, a hand like anyone's hand. "Oh gods. Oh gods, it's dead, we're lost, the fire's—"

"Stop." Ilsa's hand landed on her shoulder, heavy and steady. "Look again."

Wendell looked. And saw—not nothing. In the deepest black of her palm, so faint she might have invented it, a single line glimmered. Not a road. Not directions.

A thread. Straight down.

"It's not showing us paths anymore," Serah whispered. "There aren't choices left to show. There's only *down*. Only the fire." His voice cracked in the middle. "It's pulling us. It's begging."

They went down.

* * *

They heard the pursuers before dawn—or what passed for dawn, forty fathoms under the mountain.

Boots. Many boots, echoing up the switchbacks behind them, and with them the particular ring of inquisitor steel, which was tuned, Serah had once explained mournfully, to disrupt minor workings. Which meant Wendell's charms would fail within thirty feet of it. Which meant no hiding, no misdirection, no luck-knots.

Which meant the junction.

It rose up to meet them minutes later: the place where the narrow stair met the wide miners' road, a chamber tall enough to lose a cathedral in, its ceiling lost in darkness, its far mouth breathing cold air from the depths. And behind them, growing louder, the boots.

"There's a shelf of rock above the wide road," Ilsa said. All business, all calm, and Wendell hated how calm. "Old pillar, cracked—I've been watching it since we arrived. Bring it down across the road and it buys you a day. Maybe two."

"With what?" Serah demanded. "We don't have blasting powder, we don't have—"

"We have me."

"No," Wendell said again, and this time it came out fierce. "Not alone. We go together or—"

"If we all stay, Corvath's men come down that road behind us and none of us reach the gates." Isla was already moving to the pillar, testing it with the heel of her hand, reading stone the way she read men. "This isn't the story where the knight dies beautifully, Wendell. This is the story where the knight buys time and *catches up later*, because I am extremely difficult to kill and I owe you both a proper meal in a city where magic is legal."

"That's not a real promise," Wendell said, and her eyes were burning, traitorous, useless.

"It's the best kind." Ilsa smiled at her—that crooked, unhandsome smile that had taken weeks to earn and that Wendell would have crossed this whole black country to keep. "Hold my sword. I'll want it back."

Wendell took it. It weighed nothing and everything.

Isla pressed her shoulder to the pillar, found her footing, and looked back at them once. "Down the stair. Don't stop. Don't wait. And Wendell—" the boots were close now, torchlight bleeding into the chamber, "—when you meet that fire of yours, tell it about us. Tell it we came all this way together. Things like that matter to fires."

Then she shoved, and ten thousand pounds of mountain came down across the wide road in a roar that swallowed the world, and Wendell ran, clutching a borrowed sword, down into the dark, while above and behind her Dame Ilsa Reyne turned alone to face the boots.

* * *

They ran until running became stumbling and stumbling became falling, and then they crawled, and then the thread in Wendell's palm began—slowly, mercifully—to brighten.

By the time they stopped to breathe, it wasn't a thread anymore. It was a handprint of light, blazing, urgent, and through the stone beneath them Wendell felt something she had no name for.

Warmth. Faint as memory. Faint as a candle seen from a hillside at dusk.

But warm.

"It knows we're coming," she whispered.

Beside her, Serah stared at her glowing palm, and then at her face, and Wendell watched him arrive at the thought she'd been refusing all week—the one about why the map chose *her*, about what the guild's oldest records said bearers were *for*.

"Wendell," he said carefully. "When we reach the heart. Whatever the ritual asks—"

"Don't."

"—promise me you'll ask us first. Before you decide anything alone. Promise me that much."

Behind them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of fallen rock, steel rang against steel—one clear note, then another. Still fighting. Still holding.

Still catching up.

Wendell closed her burning hand into a fist and gave him the only promise she had left.
