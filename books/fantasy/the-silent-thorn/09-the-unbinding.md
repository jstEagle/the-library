## 9. The Unbinding

The tunnel opened like a held breath finally released.

Wendell felt it before she saw it—a warmth against her branded palm, steady as a heartbeat that had been waiting for her all her life. The passage widened into a cavern the size of Vessengard's great cathedral, and there, in its center, burned the last dragon-fire.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. That was the cruelty of it. Ten years of whispered legends, of Serah's trembling archivist voice reciting what the guild had guarded, and here it was: a flame no bigger than a cookfire, guttering on a bed of blackened stone, casting light the color of late autumn. Around it, the walls of the Emberhold's deep heart glittered with veins of dead crystal—veins that had once, Wendell understood, blazed like captured suns.

"Oh," Serah said softly, behind her. "Oh, it's *starving*."

"I know," Wendell said. The map on her palm had gone quiet now, its burning lines faded to a faint silver scar. It had brought her home, and homes did not need maps.

Ilsa stood at the cavern's mouth, sword drawn, watching the tunnel behind them. Blood streaked her hair from the pass—she'd held it alone for an hour while they ran, and she walked with a limp she was pretending not to have. "How long?" she asked.

Wendell knelt by the fire and held her hand near it. The warmth answered her, leaned into her like a cat recognizing its person. "Days," she said. "Maybe less. It's been keeping the king alive instead of itself."

"The king," Ilsa repeated. "You're certain he's down here?"

"He never left," said a voice from the far side of the fire.

* * *

He was sitting against the wall as if he'd been sitting there for years, which perhaps he had. A man in rotted finery, gray-bearded, hollow-cheeked—but his eyes were his own. That was the first thing Wendell noticed, and the most important. She'd seen the Hollow's eyes, through Ilsa's shaking account of court. These eyes were tired and human and *afraid*, and they were looking at the map-scar on her palm with something like hope.

"King Aldric," Serah breathed, dropping to a curtsy so abrupt she nearly overbalanced. Ten years in exile hadn't worn the reflex out of her.

"Don't." He raised a hand. "Please. I've had ten years of bowing from things that wanted to eat me. Just—" His voice cracked. "Just tell me who you are. Tell me something true. I can't always tell anymore."

Wendell sat down across the fire from him, cross-legged, the way you'd sit with a frightened child. "My name is Wendell Oyelaran. Last apprentice of the Thornwright Guild. My master was hanged in Vessengard square when I was thirteen, and I've spent ten years selling luck-knots to children who needed them, and I am very tired, and I would very much like this to be over."

The king laughed. It was a broken sound, but it was real. "The Thornwrights. Yes. They came to warn me, near the end. I didn't listen." He looked at his hands—half of them weren't quite hands anymore, the fingers going translucent where they caught the firelight, like smoke pretending to be flesh. "It started eating me slowly. A memory at a time. My mother's face first. Then my coronation. Then..." He gestured vaguely at himself. "Then everything soft. What's left of me is mostly duty. And duty, as it turns out, burns poorly."

"Can you walk?" Ilsa asked from the mouth of the cave. Practical to the bone, even now.

"I can walk. Whether what walks out is still me—" He smiled ruefully. "We'll see."

Serah had already unpacked her satchel, spreading pages across the stone: fragments of guild ritual, copied in her cramped, furious handwriting over years of exile. "The unbinding," she said. Her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed not saying. "Wendell. There's a component we discussed on the road. I said the texts were ambiguous."

"You said," Wendell agreed, watching her.

"They're not ambiguous." Serah wouldn't look up from the page. "A binding of this depth—the Hollow isn't a creature, it's a *wound*, a hole torn in the world when the fire was first chained to serve a crown. To unbind it, the fire has to be given freely back. Not fed. Given. All of it." She finally raised her eyes. "It demands a life. Freely given. And the bearer of the seal is the one it will accept."

The cavern went very quiet. Even the little flame seemed to lean in and listen.

"Well," Wendell said, after a moment. "That's convenient, then. Because I'm the bearer."

"No," Ilsa said.

"I haven't finished negotiating yet," Wendell said. "Let me at least hear the terms."

"*No.*" Ilsa crossed the cavern in four limping strides and planted herself between Wendell and the fire like a wall. "I watched my commander die for this secret. I threw away my oath, my name, everything, because I refused to let one more good person burn for a crown. You don't get to volunteer."

"It's not a crown, it's magic itself," Wendell said gently. "Every charm in the world dies with that fire. Every child who'll ever need a luck-knot. I'm the only one it will take. Those are the terms, Ilsa. I've read enough of Serah's terrible handwriting to know when a text is being blunt with me."

"She's right about the text," Serah said miserably. "She's right about the terms. And she's still not doing it."

"I'm standing right here," Wendell pointed out.

"We noticed," said both of them, in unison, and despite everything, despite the dying king and the dying fire, something in Wendell's chest went warm that had nothing to do with dragons.

* * *

They argued for an hour. It was, Wendell thought later, the strangest hour of her life: three people shouting themselves hoarse around a guttering miracle while the last true King of Vessengard dozed against the wall, occasionally offering unhelpful commentary like "*in my day we settled these things via heraldry*" and "*the small one with the map has the right of it, historically speaking*."

Serah tried scholarship. She laid out six alternate readings, three mistranslations, and one outright forgery she'd uncovered in the archives, each a thread by which a clever woman might escape the price. Wendell followed every one of them down and found the same dead end: the fire required a willing soul bound to the seal, and the seal lived in her palm, and there was no version of the ritual where someone else's blood would take.

Ilsa tried force. She tried carrying Wendell bodily toward the tunnel, which ended with both of them on the floor, laughing breathlessly—Ilsa because her leg had buckled, Wendell because Ilsa's expression of betrayed dignity was the funniest thing she'd seen in a decade. Then Ilsa stopped laughing and said, quietly, "I buried too many people who volunteered. Please. Not you. We just found you."

And that was the knife of it. That was the thing Wendell kept turning over in her chest like a coin she couldn't spend. For ten years she had been alone by design—alone was *safe*, alone meant no one to lose and no one to grieve her. She had built her whole life out of small magics and smaller distances. And now, at the exact moment the world demanded her death, she had finally, *finally* gotten a family, and they were loud and stubborn and bleeding and *hers*, and the ritual wanted her to leave them at the door.

"Duty says yes," she told them, when the shouting had worn itself out. She stood by the fire, her palm glowing faintly silver. "Duty's been saying yes since I was thirteen. Master Elowen died for the guild; her master died for the fire; it goes back and back like that, a long line of people walking willingly into ovens because the alternative was unthinkable." She looked at her hand. "And I used to think that was noble. Now I think it's just loneliness wearing armor."

"Wendell—" Serah started.

"Let me finish. I've earned a finish." Wendell turned to face them both, and she was crying a little, and she found she didn't mind them seeing it. "I'm not refusing to die because I'm afraid. Well. I'm also refusing because I'm afraid, obviously, I'm not a hero in a ballad. But mostly—" She took a breath. "Mostly because you two taught me something the guild never did. That love isn't a distraction from duty. Sometimes it's the *better instruction*. And I refuse—do you hear me, I *refuse*—to believe the fire was ever meant to run on orphaned apprentices. Something that beautiful can't want to be fed grief forever. There has to be another way. There has to be a way where I keep you."

The flame flickered. And—Wendell would swear this until her dying day, whichever day that turned out to be—it flickered *toward* her. Like a nod.

From the wall, King Aldric spoke, and his voice had changed. Some of the fog had lifted from it. "Apprentice Oyelaran. When the Hollow takes a memory, it doesn't destroy it. Nothing is destroyed. It's only... moved. Held. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Wendell looked at him. At the translucent fingers. At the fire that had spent ten years pouring itself into a man instead of itself.

"You've been holding him," she whispered. "All this time. The fire's been holding *you*."

"A binding runs on what is given," the king said. "It was given a king. Perhaps it can be given—" he gestured at her palm, at her, at the two battered women flanking her like honor guards, "—something else entirely. I was a scholar before I was a crown. Check the third fragment. The one your archivist called a forgery."

Serah snatched up the page. Her eyes raced down it. Then her hand rose slowly to her mouth.

"What?" Ilsa demanded. "What does it say?"

"It's not a forgery," Serah whispered. "It's the *first* rite. The original. Before the guilds chained it to kings. The fire doesn't demand death. Death is just—" she laughed, wet and disbelieving, "—death is just what people assumed, because everyone who wrote about it was grieving. It asks for a keeper. A life *lived into it*. Freely given, ongoing, whole. Not a sacrifice. A *marriage*."

The cavern was silent except for the small, stubborn crackle of the last fire in the world.

Wendell looked at her palm. The silver scar pulsed once, twice, like a question.

Behind them, far up the tunnel, boots struck stone—many boots, inquisitor rhythm, closing fast. Corvath's men had found the pass. Ilsa drew her sword one-handed, jaw setting. "Whatever you're going to decide," she said, "decide it running."

But Wendell wasn't listening anymore. She was kneeling at the edge of the fire, and it was rising to meet her, small and hungry and hopeful, and she was beginning—with the particular clarity of a girl who had spent ten years hiding her light in a city that outlawed it—to suspect exactly what kind of life, freely given, she was about to offer.

The Hollow knew she was coming. She could feel it now, high above, wearing its stolen crown—and it was afraid.
