## 2. Prayers in the Pass

The road down from the pass was the kind of road Zephyrine had spent ten years learning to love: narrow, unremarkable, and utterly forgettable. It wound through pine and scree toward the valley settlements where nobody asked questions, where a woman traveling alone was poor rather than strange, and where the gods were spoken of the way one spoke of weather—distantly, without expectation.

She walked it now at the pace of a peddler's wife, which is what her papers said she was. Her sword rode under a tarp of dried fish on the mule cart she'd bought off Dax for an insulting price, because a woman hauling fish needed no reason to be armed and armed women hauling fish were simply prudent. The disguise had held through nine years of roads like this one. She had built it the way masons build walls—one careful stone at a time, no mortar of anything true.

Except the rockslide yesterday. Except the prayer.

She had not stopped thinking about it since. Somewhere above the high switchbacks, someone had knelt—she could see the flattened grass still in her mind's eye, the small cairn of offering-stones, the wax stubble of a burned candle—and *asked*. Not the polite, rote asking that filled Thornfell's shrines, words worn smooth as river pebbles by generations of mouths that expected nothing. This had been raw. Desperate. A voice cracked open against the mountain's silence.

And something had answered.

Zephyrine knew what answered prayers felt like the way a deaf man knows thunder—not by hearing but by the trembling in the ground. Swordspeech ran in her blood like a second pulse; she had spent a decade strangling that pulse, and yet when she'd passed the place of kneeling grass, every scar on her palms had ached at once, and the air itself had seemed to lean, as if listening for more requests.

Old gods did not answer prayers. That was the first truth of her childhood and the last lesson of her order: the gods were dead, or bound so deep beneath death that their dreams couldn't reach the surface. The Silent Veil, the scholars called it—that great quiet after the Purification, when the last executioner-swordspeakers had done their work and hung up their blades and let themselves be forgotten. Her family had been the knife. The forgetting had been the sheath.

So either the old truths were wrong, or something new wore the shape of an answer.

Both possibilities made her want to turn around and walk back into the mountains until she found a valley with no name at all.

She didn't turn around. She told herself it was because answers were dangerous only when unexamined—a swordspeaker's discipline, applied to a life that no longer wanted any part of swordspeaking—but there was a smaller, uglier truth underneath, and she let it ride in silence at the back of the cart: if something was awake in Thornfell, then her decade of erasure might already have failed. And a woman who has burned her own name would walk into fire to learn whether the ashes were disturbed.

The village appeared in the late afternoon: Ashenmere, said the leaning signpost, though the paint had given up most of its letters. Twenty-odd houses of grey timber clustered around a stream, terraced gardens climbing the slopes behind them, and at the village's heart—the reason she'd chosen this route over the safer lowland road—a shrine.

It was older than the village. That was common enough; settlers built their homes in the shadow of whatever holy places they found, the way moths gathered at lamps. But this shrine had been *kept*. Someone had re-thatched its roof recently. Fresh garlands of rowan and pine hung from its lintel. The stone basin before it held clean water scattered with coins—copper, mostly, but Zephyrine's eye caught two silver pieces glinting at the bottom, and silver was harvest-money here, silver was a season's labor.

Villages did not spend like that unless they were grateful.

Or afraid.

* * *

She watered the mule at the stream and let the village come to her, which villages always did. A girl of perhaps eight approached first, bold as sparrows are, and studied the fish-cart with open suspicion.

"You're not from the tax-man."

"No," Zephyrine said. "I'm selling fish and buying nothing. Worst merchant in Thornfell."

The girl considered this with grave seriousness. "You can't buy my brother anyway. He got better."

"Did he."

"He was dying." She said it flatly, the way children recite things too large for them, facts carried like stones too heavy to set down. "Fever. Three days burning and the sweat-smell, and Mother had the priest come and he said make her ready. And Grandmother went up to the shrine in the night even though it was snowing and she prayed, and in the morning Tam was cold—" Zephyrine's breath caught— "cold like normal people, not fever-cold. Sweating the fever out. He ate porridge. He's annoying again."

"Your grandmother prayed," Zephyrine said carefully, "and your brother healed."

"The Veiled One answered." The girl said it easily, a phrase learned whole, its syllables worn smooth already. "Everyone says. We leave the silver so it keeps answering."

The name went through her like a splinter working toward bone. The Veiled One. There had been a god of veils once, in the oldest of the order's records—records she had read at fourteen, memorized at fifteen, and burned page by page at twenty-two when she'd begun her vanishing. A god whose domain was not war or harvest or hearth but *concealment*, who was worshipped by those with things to hide. The executioners had never caught its cults, because its cults left no altars, kept no names, and its faithful were everyone who had ever lied.

A god of hidden things, awake and answering prayers in a mountain pass.

"And your grandmother," Zephyrine said, keeping her voice at the level of market-chat, "does she pray often? At the shrine?"

"Every night since." The girl shrugged. "She says you don't stop paying a debt just because it's paid. Then she makes me say thank-you too." She wrinkled her nose. "I don't know what I'm thanking."

Neither, Zephyrine thought, do I. But I intend to find out before dark.

* * *

The grandmother was easier to find than she expected. Old women who pray nightly at shrines are predictable as tides, and sure enough, as the light went copper and long, a bent figure climbed the terrace path with a basket of offerings—bread, a twist of salt, a handful of rowan berries—and Zephyrine fell into step beside her as naturally as if she'd been sent by the priest.

"Mira," the grandmother said, when asked. "Mira of the Ashen line. And you'll be the fish-woman everyone's talking about." Her eyes were quick despite the stoop, and they took in the cart, the mule, the calluses on Zephyrine's hands, and dismissed all of it in the space of a breath. "You'll want to ask about Tam. Everyone wants to ask about Tam."

"I heard he was ill."

"Dying." Mira said it without flinching. "I've buried three children and a husband; I know the look of dying. So I went up to the shrine and I did what my mother taught me and hers before that—we keep the old prayers, girl, the ones from before the priests came, passed mouth to mouth like seeds. And I prayed them. Properly. With the blood-prick and everything."

Zephyrine's stomach tightened. "Blood-prick?"

"How else does a prayer get through?" Mira said, as if explaining rain to a fool. "Words are just wind. Blood is a promise." She held up her thumb, showing an old brown scar across the pad. "My grandmother showed me. Said the day would come when we'd need it again. Called it keeping the door oiled."

They had reached the shrine. Up close it was humbler than it looked from the village—rough-hewn stone, no idol inside, only an empty alcove where a statue might once have stood. But the alcove's back wall was covered in markings, and Zephyrine's heart stumbled.

Not runes. Not the script of any living faith. Oath-sigils—the formal notation of binding, the grammar her order had used to chain gods into flesh, cut into this wall in lines fine as spider silk and old as the mountain.

Only a swordspeaker could read them. Only a swordspeaker could have written them.

Her hands wanted to shake. She kept them folded, peasant-calm, while Mira set out her bread and salt and pricked her thumb on a thorn kept in a fold of her shawl for exactly this purpose, letting three drops fall into the basin water.

"Grandmother," Zephyrine said, very quietly, "who taught your grandmother these prayers? What did she call the thing she prayed to?"

Mira smiled, and there was something in the smile that did not belong on a farmwife's face—something patient and pleased, like a trap that had waited a long time and did not mind waiting longer.

"She called it the Veiled One," the old woman said. "But it always signs its letters the same way." She nodded toward the basin. "Read yours. You've the eyes for it. I've watched you looking at the wall since we came up the path—most folk see scratches. You saw *writing*."

The world narrowed to the sound of water dripping from Zephyrine's own cupped hand.

Because she had dipped her fingers in the basin without meaning to—some reflex deeper than caution, some muscle of her inheritance flexing after ten years asleep—and when she lifted them, wet, the water on her fingertips had arranged itself. Beads of it stood on her skin in deliberate patterns, sigils small as grains of barley, and she read them the way she read her own heartbeat.

*I know you, daughter of Eriksen.*

The basin water went still. The evening birds went still. Somewhere below, the village dogs began to howl, all of them at once, all of them facing the shrine.

"You see?" Mira said softly, gathering her empty basket, entirely unafraid. "It knows your name. It's known since you came over the pass." She patted Zephyrine's arm with a hand dry as bark. "It said you'd come. It said to tell you—" the old woman's voice dropped into a cadence that was not her own, that had the weight of mountains and the hush of closed doors— "*the veil lifts for those who lift it.*"

Then she shuffled away down the path toward supper, leaving Zephyrine alone with the shrine, the sigils crawling the walls like frost given intent, and the message drying on her fingertips.

Behind her, far down the valley road, hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast.

Someone else had read the same signs she had—and they were riding straight for Ashenmere.
