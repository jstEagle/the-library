## 4. Court of Borrowed Faces

The capital of Thornfell announced itself the way a predator announces itself: not with sound, but with silence.

Zephyrine felt it from the crest of the last hill, where the king's road bent down toward Caelmere and the sea of slate roofs spread out below her, glittering in the late autumn light. Ten years ago she had walked this road as something other than herself — as the last Eriksen, sword on her hip, the old words humming in her blood like a second heartbeat. Now she walked it as Meris Callow, scribe to a salt merchant named Hobb Fenwick, with ink-stained fingers, a ledger case strapped across her back, and a sword left behind in Dax's smuggler cache three valleys east.

She had hated leaving it. That was the first warning sign she chose to ignore.

*You are nobody,* she reminded herself as the caravan wound down through the terraced vineyards, past the toll archers in their wine-dark livery. *Nobodies pass through gates without a second glance. Nobodies are invisible.* She had spent a decade perfecting the art of being unseen — forged papers, invented histories, a face trained into pleasant forgettability. In every town between here and the northern passes, there were ledgers that did not contain her name, priests who could not recall her face, innkeepers who would swear to the wrong color of her hair. The erasure was complete. It had to be.

And yet.

Three days ago, in the pass below the shrine village, a rockslide had quieted at the sound of her voice — not because she had commanded it, but because something else had answered *for* her, eager as a dog hearing its master's step. Something had prayed over that road. Something had answered. And whatever had answered had known her name before she spoke it.

So here she was, doing the one thing she had sworn never to do again: walking willingly into the heart of the court where her family had been unmade.

"Meris." Hobb Fenwick reined his mule alongside hers, a broad man with salt-cracked hands and the shrewd, incurious eyes Zephyrine paid him to have. "You've gone quiet."

"I'm counting gate fees," she said. "Your margins."

Hobb grunted approvingly. Fictional scribes who worried about margins made the best cover; no one suspected a woman who complained about tariffs.

The gate guards waved them through with barely a glance. Inside the walls, Caelmere swallowed them whole — the smell of fish and lamp oil and roasting chestnuts, the press of bodies in the Merchant's Spiral, the great white bulk of the palace rising above the terraces like a jaw full of teeth. Banners hung from every balcony: silver and black, the crowned stag of House Aldric. But beneath each banner, Zephyrine noticed, hung smaller pennants of deep blue bearing no sigil at all — just a plain circle of thread, empty at its center.

She stopped a fruit-seller's boy with a copper. "The blue banners. A festival?"

He looked at her as if she'd asked about the sun. "The Veiled Season, mistress. His Majesty's devotion month. Everyone hangs the circle so the prayers find their way home." He grinned, gap-toothed. "My gran says the king hears every word spoken under one."

*Every word.* Zephyrine smiled, thanked him, and let the cold settle deeper into her spine as she walked on.

In ten years, the faith of Thornfell had changed its clothes. When she'd fled, the old shrines stood empty and the Swordspeaker Order was a scattered memory, its citadel burned, its members dead or dispersed. The gods were silent; everyone agreed on that. Silence was safe. Silence meant no oaths, no executions, no families like hers marked for service or slaughter.

Now the city wore empty circles over its doors like eyes kept deliberately blank. And people *prayed*. Openly. Joyfully. As if the silence had ended and no one had thought to ask what had come back in the gods' place.

* * *

Hobb's trade contact was a wool factor named Ostrey who kept offices near the Chancery Row, and Ostrey, being prosperous and pious, held a seat among the merchants invited to the Season's first Devotion Audience — a public blessing in the Great Hall, followed by a feast. He required a scribe to record his observations of rival factors' dealings. Zephyrine volunteered before Hobb could open his mouth.

"You hate courts," Hobb muttered later, watching her pack her ledger case with unusual care.

"I hate paying for wine," she said. "Free feast is free feast."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true. What she didn't say was that she had spent two days learning the rhythm of the palace: when servants changed shifts, which postern gate took deliveries of candle wax, how the chapel bells staggered their ringing so that no single bell ever tolled alone — a detail so small and strange she'd turned it over all night. Bells that never rang solo. Voices that never sounded singly.

On the evening of the audience, she dressed in her best borrowed grey, pinned her hair under a scribe's coif, and entered the palace through the flow of merchants and minor clergy, her invitation copied in Ostrey's own hand, her ink-stained fingers folded demurely around a ledger.

The Great Hall had been rebuilt since she'd last seen it. She remembered it as a place of hard stone and harder silences, built by kings who feared assassination more than they loved display. Now it blazed with a thousand candles, their light caught and multiplied by mirrors of polished silver set into the pillars, so that the whole chamber seemed to swim in reflected flame. The blue circles hung everywhere — embroidered into banners, inlaid in the marble floor, worked into the great rose window above the dais, where an empty ring of glass glowed amber against the darkening sky.

At the center of it all stood the throne.

King Aldric sat upon it, and the hall loved him.

Zephyrine saw it immediately, the way one sees a current beneath still water. The courtiers pressed forward in waves whenever he glanced their way. Ladies wept openly at his benedictions. Old men knelt with the unselfconscious abandon of children. He was young — younger than she'd expected, perhaps five-and-twenty, with his mother's fair coloring and a face built for coinage portraits — and he moved through the ritual of blessing with a grace that seemed almost tender. He touched foreheads. He whispered over bowed heads. When a farmer's daughter was lifted up to him, lame since birth, he laid both hands on her twisted ankle and the hall went silent as a held breath.

The girl walked. Unsteadily, laughing, weeping — but she walked, crossing thirty feet of marble on legs that hadn't borne her weight in six years, while the court erupted around her like a struck bell.

Zephyrine did not cheer. She was watching the king's face.

There was a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — after the girl was carried away rejoicing, when Aldric's expression went somewhere else. Not triumph. Not compassion. Something flatter, wearier, older than his twenty-five years, like a man setting down a heavy load only to be told he must pick it up again. Then the light returned to his features, warm and golden, and he raised his hand for the next petitioner.

*Miracles with a price,* she thought. *Everything has a price. Who pays?*

She edged along the wall, letting the crowd's attention carry her closer to the dais, and took up a station beside a pillar whose mirror threw the king's reflection almost directly to her. Scribes were furniture; furniture could stand anywhere. She opened her ledger and began, ostentatiously, to write.

Aldric rose to deliver the Season's address.

His voice was beautiful. That was the first thing. Trained, resonant, pitched to fill the hall without effort, the voice of a man born to be listened to. He spoke of harvests blessed, of plagues stayed at the border, of the realm's enemies confounded by fortune rather than arms. He spoke of gratitude owed, and of debts gladly rendered, and the hall drank every word like wine.

Then, halfway through a sentence, he paused.

"—and I say to you, as my grandfather said before me, and his father before *him*, that the crown of Thornfell is not—"

The pause lasted less than a second. To anyone else it would have been breath, rhetoric, nothing. But Zephyrine had spent her childhood listening to voices the way other children listened to music, and she heard what happened inside that pause.

Two voices. For the space of a syllable, the king's voice doubled — not echoed, *doubled*, one tone laid precisely atop another, the way a chord is two notes pretending to be one sound. One voice was young. The other came from underneath it, from some depth the young voice was stretched across like skin over a drumhead, and it was patient, and vast, and utterly without warmth.

"—is not mine," Aldric finished smoothly, "but the realm's. I hold it only as steward. As servant. As vessel."

*Vessel.*

The word dropped through Zephyrine like ice water finding every vein at once. Around her, the court sighed with devotion. Several nobles sank to their knees. An elderly duchess pressed her hands together and mouthed the word back — *vessel* — with an expression of rapture that made Zephyrine's stomach turn.

Because she knew that word. Every child raised in the old order knew it. It was not courtly humility. It was liturgy — the liturgy of binding, the formula carved above the doors of her family's crypt, spoken over the swords that drank gods. *The vessel is not the wine. The vessel serves the vintage.* Her grandmother had made her recite it until she understood what it meant: that a bound thing wears its host, and speaks with its host's mouth, and the host's own voice becomes a mask hung over something else entirely.

The king continued his address. Zephyrine watched his reflection in the pillar's mirror and forced herself to look — truly look — past the golden surface.

His lips moved correctly. His gestures flowed beautifully. But there were small errors, tiny desynchronies, visible only if you knew to hunt for them: a blink that arrived a fraction late, a hand that began rising a heartbeat before the words it accompanied, a smile assembled rather than felt. And his eyes — when the doubled voice surfaced, his eyes changed. Not in color. In *focus*. They went from looking *at* the crowd to looking *through* it, the way a speaker looks when the words are coming from somewhere behind and beneath him, and he is only the instrument through which they pass.

Three generations, Maerin Voss had claimed in the pass, before Zephyrine had run from her. Three generations of kings, worn like coats.

She watched Aldric bless a line of petitioners, and she thought: *he was a child when they put it in him.* Whatever coronation meant in this court — whatever oath was sworn on that throne — it had happened to a boy too young to understand what he was agreeing to carry. And now he stood in candlelight performing tenderness with a stranger's patience behind his eyes, and ten thousand people loved him for it, and none of them knew they were loving the thing that wore him.

The hall erupted in applause as the address ended. Petitioners withdrew. Courtiers surged forward to be seen near the dais. And Zephyrine, closing her ledger with steady hands, allowed herself to be swept backward into the crowd, her mind running cold and fast.

*Prayers answered in the pass. Oath-sigils on healed children. A city wearing empty circles. A king speaking in two voices.* The picture assembled itself with terrible clarity, and every piece pointed to the same conclusion: the silence of the gods had not been broken by a god's return. Something else had woken. Something that ate oaths, that fed on devotion, that had learned to farm the faithful like a shepherd farms sheep — answering a prayer here, healing a child there, always in exchange for the one currency it craved.

Sworn words. Given freely. Forever.

* * *

The feast that followed was held in the Long Gallery, and Zephyrine worked it the way she'd once worked smugglers' dens: ears open, glass untouched, positioned always within range of interesting conversation. She learned that the Chancellor's faction resented the Bishop of the Circle's growing influence; that grain shipments from the south had been requisitioned for mysterious purposes; that a noble house had recently been elevated for reasons no one could name, though everyone repeated, with the careful flatness of rehearsed phrases, that Lord Cassel was *"devoted beyond measure."*

Devoted. The word recurred like a refrain. Devoted houses prospered. Devoted houses received blessings. Devoted houses, Zephyrine noticed as she charted the room's alliances in her head, were uniformly wealthy, obedient, and — this was the detail that chilled her — increasingly interchangeable. They spoke in the same cadences. They laughed at the same moments. Their eyes, when talk turned to the king, took on the same soft, unfocused shine.

Oaths given freely, forever. How many of these smiling courtiers had already given theirs?

She was contemplating the exit when the gallery's far doors opened and a hush rolled down its length like wind flattening grass. The king himself had come to walk among his guests.

Up close, away from the mirrors, Aldric was worse.

The doubling was subtler here — masked by proximity, by the noise, by the sheer human unwillingness to see what stood in front of you — but Zephyrine stood close enough to hear him exchange courtesies with the wool factor Ostrey, and twice, *twice*, she caught it: the second voice surfacing for a fraction of a second beneath the first, like something rising in a well. Once when Ostrey mentioned his daughter's illness. Once when a lady begged the king's blessing on her unborn child.

Each time, the king granted the request with radiant kindness. Each time, the shadow-voice beneath his own murmured something too low for any ear but hers — and each time, the petitioner's face softened afterward into that same glazed devotion, as if a small door inside them had quietly closed.

The king moved on. Zephyrine stood very still among the candlelight and the laughter, and found that her hands had begun to shake.

It wasn't fear. Or not only. It was recognition — the awful, intimate recognition of a craft. Because what moved through that gallery wearing a young king's body moved the way the old texts described the bound ones moving: gracefully, patiently, *economically*, wasting nothing, harvesting everything. Her ancestors had hunted such things across three kingdoms. Her family's swords had been forged for exactly this purpose. And the last of the Eriksen line stood disguised as a merchant's clerk, holding a ledger full of nothing, while the thing her blood was made to fight toasted the health of its own flock ten feet away.

*You should draw steel,* whispered the old training, the old hunger. *You should speak the words your grandmother taught you and burn it out of him here, tonight, before it roots deeper—*

But she had buried those words a decade ago, along with her name, along with her sister's body, along with the smoking ruin of everything the gift had cost her. Speaking a sword against a bound god in the heart of its own court, surrounded by its devoted, with no Order behind her and no plan ahead of her, was not courage. It was suicide with extra steps.

*Observe. Learn. Find the flaw.* Her grandmother's voice, cool as well water. *Even a god in chains has a chain.*

The king drifted nearer. Whether chance or something else, Zephyrine couldn't say — but suddenly he was three feet away, exchanging pleasantries with Ostrey, and then, unbearably, his gaze slid sideways and landed on her.

For one suspended moment, the King of Thornfell looked directly at the merchant scribe Meris Callow, and the hall's noise fell away from her like water off glass.

His face was kind. His face was perfect. And beneath it, behind it, through the gold of his eyes, something *looked back* — slowly, deliberately, the way a man turns over stones in a streambed — and Zephyrine felt it brush across her mind with the delicacy of a finger trailing through dust, searching for tracks.

She did the only thing a scribe would do. She dipped into a bow, murmured, "Your Majesty," and fixed her gaze respectfully on his shoes.

The attention lingered. One heartbeat. Two. She kept her breathing slow, her mind shallow, offering it nothing but ink-stained fingers and thoughts of tariffs. Somewhere beneath her sternum, in the sealed vault where she had locked her gift ten years ago, she felt — distantly, horribly — a faint stirring, like a sleeper turning over. Like her own buried power recognizing what stood before it, and beginning, traitorously, to wake.

Then the king's gaze moved on, drawn away by a marquis's joke, and the hall's warmth closed over her again as though nothing had happened.

Zephyrine excused herself from Ostrey, walked with unhurried steps to the gallery doors, and did not stop walking until she reached the servants' stair, where the candle-smoke couldn't reach and the cold stone pressed against her back.

Her hands were shaking badly now. She pressed them flat against the wall and breathed, and in the silence of the stairwell she admitted what she had known since the pass, since the shrine village, since the moment the rockslide had quieted for her:

The invisibility was over. Whatever slept beneath that crown had felt her the way a spider feels a fly land at the edge of its web — and it had looked, and it had nearly found her, and next time it would not be distracted by a marquis.

She had come to Caelmere believing she might watch from the shadows for weeks, gathering proof, planning her escape. Standing in the dark of that stairwell, she understood the timeline had just collapsed. Days, perhaps. Maybe hours.

Above her, muffled by floors of stone, the chapel bells began to ring the midnight hour — and true to pattern, no bell sounded alone. Twelve bells, twelve voices, perfectly braided into one sound.

Zephyrine pushed off the wall and started down the stairs, toward the servants' quarters and the long night ahead.

Tomorrow, she would find the royal chapel. Tomorrow, she would touch the throne itself, if she had to.

But tonight, walking down through the sleeping palace, one thought rang in her louder than all the bells: *the king had looked at me, and something behind his eyes had hesitated — as if it recognized me too.*

Not Meris Callow. Not a scribe.

Something older. Something with a name it shouldn't have known.

And in the morning, when she passed the notice board by the Chancery Row, she would see the new proclamation posted in fresh ink — an announcement that His Majesty would receive, in private audience, one further guest during the Veiled Season: a pilgrim of no house, summoned by name.

*Zephyrine Eriksen.*
