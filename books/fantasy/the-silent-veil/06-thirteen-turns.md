## 6. Thirteen Turns

Sellen had catalogued eleven thousand four hundred and six documents in the undercroft of the Vault of Hours, and he could tell you the provenance of every one of them. He could not, however, tell you why the woman who had slipped through his door three nights past moved like a rumor—present only when you weren't looking directly at her.

She called herself Marrow. She paid in old coin, the good silver from before the third king's debasement, and she asked questions no merchant scribe should ask.

Tonight she stood at his worktable with a folio open before her, and her finger rested on a line of ink so faded it might have been breath on glass.

"Read it again," she said.

"I've read it four times."

"Read it again anyway."

He sighed the sigh of a man whose authority lived entirely in other people's belief in it, and bent over the page. The document was a fragment—a single leaf rescued from the burning of the Abbey of Candlemere two centuries gone, its edges scorched to lace. The script was pre-Reform liturgical, the kind written by monks who still remembered when the gods answered.

*And when the wheel of heaven turns past twelve,* Sellen recited, *the thirteenth moon shall rise over Thornfell, and the Veiled One shall gather to itself every oath sworn beneath that light—kneeling or standing, whispered or shouted, given freely or wrung from fear—and the realm shall belong to it as the body belongs to the name.*

Silence pooled in the undercroft like water in a cellar.

"That's the whole prophecy?" Zephyrine—though he knew her only as Marrow—asked quietly.

"It's the whole *fragment*," Sellen said. "There were supposedly nine leaves. The Abbey burned. What survived is what someone thought to carry out in their shirt." He tapped the table with one knuckle. "You understand what this says. You read it the same way I do."

"I want to hear you say it."

"The thirteenth moon isn't a metaphor." He straightened, and his knees cracked in protest; he was sixty-one years old and had spent forty of them underground. "It's an astronomical event. Every two hundred and seventeen years, the lunar calendar falls out of alignment with the solar—one extra lunation wedged into the turning of the year. The astronomers call it the intercalated moon. The common folk call it the Widow's Moon, because it rises thin and late and out of season, and because nothing good has ever happened under it." He met her eyes. "The next one rises in thirty-one days."

* * *

Zephyrine did not move for a long moment. Sellen had learned to watch people in that first stillness after bad news—it told you more than anything they said afterward. Some went pale. Some laughed. This woman simply went *quieter*, as if she were drawing herself down inside her own skin, a lamp being shuttered room by room.

"Thirty-one days," she repeated.

"Twenty-nine, now. It's near midnight." Sellen shuffled to the shelf where he kept the astronomical ephemerides, the ones the crown's astrologers pretended they didn't consult because consulting them admitted the calendar could be *wrong*. "The court knows, incidentally. The Office of Tidings published a revised festival schedule last month—they've folded the extra moon into the Feast of the King's Grace. Extended the celebrations. Very festive. Very deliberate." He pulled the ephemeris down and opened it to a page dense with columns of numbers. "They're not hiding it. They're decorating it."

"A feast."

"A *consecration*, if the fragment is honest. Look—" he turned the leaf toward her again, careful not to touch the brittle margins— "*every oath sworn beneath that light.* Not during the moon's passage. Beneath it. Under its rise. Which suggests a moment, not a season. A single night when every vow spoken in the realm becomes... harvestable." He grimaced at his own word. "That's not how the monks would have put it."

"How would they have put it?"

"*Gathered home.*" He said it flatly, but the words left a taste in his mouth anyway, like coins held too long between the teeth. "Binding-language. Old binding-language. You find it in the earliest covenant-stones—the ones predating the Order." He caught himself, watched her face for a flicker. She gave him nothing, which was itself informative. Most people flinched at the word *Order* these days; it had been ten years since the Swordspeakers were disbanded, and the disbanding had been done loudly, with burnings and proscriptions and a great deal of royal rhetoric about superstition.

This woman didn't flinch. She leaned *in*.

"Tell me about the Order's records," she said.

"Ah." Sellen lowered himself onto his stool with the care of a man whose bones had opinions. "Now we arrive at why you're really here. Three nights of polite questions about trade ledgers, and finally the true cargo surfaces." He wasn't angry. He was, if anything, relieved—he disliked pretending, and had spent a career surrounded by documents that lied for a living. "Yes. The Order kept archives. Oaths, mostly. Registries of every binding ever performed, every god ever named, every sword ever made to speak. When the Order fell, the crown seized the archives. And then—"

"They burned them?"

"Worse." He let the pause stretch, savoring it the way a lean man savors his one luxury. "They *catalogued* them. Moved everything into the royal vault beneath the chapel, sealed it under triple writ, and assigned it a custodian." He spread his hands. "Me."

Zephyrine went very still. "You've read them."

"I've dusted them. There's a difference, and the difference is my continued employment." But even as he said it, his eyes flicked to the locked cabinet against the far wall, iron-banded, plain, deliberately unremarkable. She followed the glance. Of course she did. "I will confess," he added, "to certain irregularities of dusting."

* * *

The cabinet opened with a key Sellen wore on a cord around his neck, beneath his shirt, where it had hung for nineteen years like a small cold sin.

Inside were folios. Dozens of them. Leather gone stiff as bark, corners rounded by handling, spines labeled in the cramped shorthand of the Order's scriveners. Sellen lifted one out with both hands, the way one lifts a sleeping animal, and set it on the table between them.

"Registries of Binding, Volume One," he said. "The founding records. Everything the Order was before it became knights and politics—back when it was scholars and swordsmiths trying to solve a problem."

"What problem?"

"The problem of gods who won't stay dead." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "You've heard the nursery version. The Order slew the old gods, heroism, trumpets, etcetera. The truth is in here. You cannot kill a thing that exists because people believe in it. Cut it down and it grows back from the root of the next prayer. So the founders tried something else. They bound the gods into mortal vessels—hosts. Living prisons. A god wearing a man's life the way a hand wears a glove, unable to reach beyond the glove's edge." He turned pages, careful as breathing. "The first binding took nine swordspeakers working in concert, and it cost—well. The record is frank about the cost. Two of the nine died. One went mad. And the binding itself required something the later generations seem to have forgotten."

Zephyrine's voice came out low. "What?"

"A freely given crown." Sellen looked up. "The host must consent. That's the flaw in the whole architecture, the hinge everything swings on. A binding forced on an unwilling vessel fails. Always. The god burns the vessel out from inside within a generation. But a vessel that *chooses*—that offers itself—that holds. For a long time. Not forever." He closed the folio gently. "Nothing holds forever. The bindings decay. And when they decay..."

"The god wakes up hungry."

"You say that like you've heard it eat."

She didn't answer that. Instead she reached across the table, hesitated with her fingers an inch above the folio, and said, "May I?"

"Volume One stays on the table. My neck, my rules." He pushed a second folio toward her instead. "But this one you may hold. Volume Seven. Succession of Hosts."

She opened it. Sellen watched her read—or rather, watched her *hunt*, her eyes moving not line by line but the way a tracker reads ground, looking for sign. Her finger stopped a third of the way down the first page.

"Three hosts," she said. "In three hundred years. King Maren the Pious. King Aldous the Just. And now..." Her finger pressed flat against the page. "Aldric."

"Crowned at fourteen," Sellen said quietly. "Eleven years ago."

"He was a child."

"He was a *consenting* child, according to the coronation writ—which, I'll note, was signed by his mother the queen regent and countersigned by the High Provost of the Order's remnant, back when the remnant still had official standing. Whether a fourteen-year-old can meaningfully consent to spend his life as a prison cell is a question the record does not ask." Sellen's bitterness surprised even him; it had been fermenting a long time in the dark. "The record asks nothing. Records never do. That's why they're safe."

Zephyrine turned pages faster now. "The bindings decay. Where does it say how fast? Is there a schedule, a—"

"Leaf forty-one."

She found it. Read it. And Sellen saw the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

*Each binding endureth according to the strength of the oath that founded it,* the text ran. *A binding sworn in love may hold three hundred years. A binding sworn in fear, less than one hundred. In the final turnings, the host dreams the god's dreams, and the god reaches through the host's mouth, and the oaths of the realm begin to answer prayers that are not prayers.*

"The miracles," Zephyrine said. Her voice had gone strange. Flat and precise, the voice of someone holding something heavy very carefully so it doesn't fall. "Shrines. Healing. Answered prayers along the mountain passes. It's not waking up. It's already reaching. It's been reaching for—"

"Years, by the look of the petition-records I'm not supposed to cross-reference." Sellen laced his fingers together on the tabletop. "Here is what I know, Marrow-or-whatever-your-name-is, and you may weigh it however you like. Thirty-one days from now, the thirteenth moon rises, and the Feast of the King's Grace will put half the capital into the streets swearing loyalty-oaths under it—feudal renewals, guild vows, wedding vows, the army's annual oath of service, all of it scheduled, all of it *moved* onto that night by royal decree. If the fragment means what it says, every one of those oaths goes into the god's keeping. Not just the king's oaths. Everyone's. Forever." He unfolded his hands. "And there is one more item. The item I suspect you actually came for."

"Say it."

"Volume Nine. The final folio. The one the Order's founders sealed separately from the rest, under their own cipher—not the Order's cipher, a private one, a family cipher." He watched her carefully. "The binding-flaw is documented in Volumes One through Eight: a freely given crown founds the binding. But Volume Nine concerns the *unbinding*. How to take a god back out of a host without killing the host. And the seal on Volume Nine bears a name."

The undercroft was silent except for the drip of the condensation pipe, counting seconds into the dark.

"Eriksen," Sellen said.

* * *

She stood so suddenly the stool scraped, and for a heartbeat Sellen thought she would run—she had the coiled readiness of a woman who had run many times and gotten good at it. But she didn't run. She planted both hands on the table and leaned over Volume Nine's iron clasp like a starving woman over a door.

"Open it."

"No."

"You just said—"

"I said the seal bears the name Eriksen." Sellen rose, slowly, joints complaining, and put his palm flat on the clasp. "I also said the cipher is a family cipher. I have stared at that lock for nineteen years. It is not a mechanism. It's a *word-lock*—old craft, pre-Reform, keyed to speech. A specific phrase, in a specific voice, spoken by blood of the founding line." He met her eyes, and let every year of his patience show in his face. "I cannot open it. Believe me, I have tried. I have recited every formula in the registries at that clasp until the guards thought I'd gone religious. The lock doesn't want scholarship. It wants *kin*."

"And you think I—"

"I think nobody else in Thornfell went white at the name Eriksen. I think you knew what the Order was before I told you. I think you move like a woman who taught herself to be invisible, and there is exactly one family in this kingdom's history famous for vanishing." He stepped back from the table, leaving the folio between them like a border crossing. "The Eriksens were the ninth line. The founders. The ones who wrote the binding and sealed its remedy. And then, ten years ago, right on schedule with the Order's fall, they simply—stopped existing. No bodies. No trials. No graves. They erased themselves so thoroughly that the crown's own genealogists list them as extinct." He tilted his head. "Extinct things don't come asking after prophecies in the middle of the night."

For a long moment the only sound was the condensation pipe, dripping its patient count.

Then Zephyrine Eriksen—for he was certain enough now to think the name, though he'd be hanged before he spoke it aloud—straightened, and something in her face shifted. Not softening. *Decision.* It came over her like weather.

"Thirty-one days," she said.

"Twenty-nine."

"Then I need three things from you, archivist. Everything Volume Eight says about how a decaying binding behaves in its final month. Everything the petition-records show about where the answered prayers cluster. And a way into the royal chapel that doesn't go through the front doors." She laid her hand on Volume Nine's cold clasp, and Sellen watched her fingers flex as if testing a grip. "I'll deal with the lock myself."

"You realize," Sellen said, "that helping you is treason. Not metaphorically. The actual statute. Section nine."

"I realize."

"And you realize that if the fragment is true, doing *nothing* is also treason—against everyone who'll be kneeling in those streets." He snorted at himself, a dry sound in the wet dark. "Forty years underground, and I finally get to commit an interesting crime. Very well. But there's one condition, and it isn't negotiable."

She waited.

"When this is finished—whatever *finished* means—you sit down and you tell me the whole truth of the Eriksen line, from beginning to end, for the archive. Unsealed, unciphered, in plain words." His mouth twisted. "I've spent my life guarding other people's secrets, Mistress Eriksen. I'd like to spend whatever remains of it knowing at least one of them properly."

Above them, muffled by six floors of stone, the Vault of Hours' great clock began to strike midnight—twelve slow bronze tones rolling down through the stacks like a countdown that had already begun.

Zephyrine listened to all twelve. Then she said, "Deal," and reached for Volume Nine, and somewhere far above them, under a sky already leaning toward its impossible moon, something ancient in the royal chapel turned over in its sleep—and smiled.
