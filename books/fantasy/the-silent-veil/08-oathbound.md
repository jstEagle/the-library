## 8. Oathbound

Maerin Voss had killed men for less than what she was about to do. She had certainly never told one the truth.

The safehouse was an old tithe-barn two miles south of the shrine village, its rafters black with a century of candle smoke, its floorboards scarred by the knees of farmers who had once prayed here before the gods went quiet and the praying stopped being worth the walk. Now it smelled of dust and mouse droppings and the tallow Maerin had bought from a woman in Harrowgate who asked no questions about who was buying candles by the crate.

Zephyrine Eriksen sat across the fire from her, sharpening a sword that did not need sharpening.

Maerin had watched her do this three times now. It wasn't maintenance. It was conversation—the way a man might stroke his beard while thinking, or worry a coin between his fingers. The whetstone moved in slow, patient strokes, and every so often Zephyrine's lips parted slightly, as if listening to something only she could hear, and Maerin's skin crawled every single time because she knew exactly what Zephyrine was hearing.

*She speaks to it,* Maerin thought. *Ten years of silence and she still talks to it like a lover.*

"You're stalling," Zephyrine said without looking up.

"I'm deciding how much of your night to ruin."

"All of it, probably. You have that face."

Maerin almost smiled. She didn't. Instead she reached into the satchel at her hip and drew out the thing she had carried for eleven years, through seven false leads and two excommunications and the slow, rotting collapse of everything the Swordspeaker Order had once been: a book bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked, its pages furred at the edges with damp.

She set it on the floor between them and pushed it into the firelight with her boot.

Zephyrine stopped sharpening.

"What is that?"

"The Voss Ledger," Maerin said. "My family kept it. Before your family kept theirs." She let that land. "It's the Order's founding record. Not the pretty version we recited at investiture—the real one. What we actually were. What we actually did." She paused. "What you actually are."

"Say it plainly."

"No," Maerin said. "I'm going to show you, because if I say it you'll call me a liar, and I've earned too many scars to be called a liar by an Eriksen." She leaned forward and opened the ledger to a page marked with a fraying ribbon of red silk. "Read the first line aloud. Aloud matters. Some things don't hold their shape inside a skull."

Zephyrine hesitated—Maerin saw the hesitation, catalogued it, filed it away—and then she read:

"*On the binding of the Ninefold Choir to vessels of clay and crown.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl called and got no answer.

"That's not the litany," Zephyrine said quietly.

"No. The litany says *slain*. *We slew the old gods and gave the world to men.* Every initiate swears it with a hand on steel." Maerin turned the page herself, because Zephyrine had gone still in a way she recognized—not refusal but dread, the particular paralysis of a person standing at the edge of a hole they can feel under their feet. "Here. Read the margin note."

Zephyrine bent closer. Her eyes moved. Maerin watched them stop, and widen, and come back to the same line twice, the way eyes do when the mind refuses delivery of what they're reporting.

"'A god cannot be killed,'" Zephyrine read, her voice flattening into something toneless. "'Only persuaded to wear flesh. A body gives it borders. A name gives it weight. A crown—'"

"A crown gives it hunger," Maerin finished. "Keep going."

"'For a god in flesh feeds on what mortals feed on, only more so. Feed it bread and it grows fat. Feed it worship and it grows strong. But feed it oath—sworn word, given freely, binding soul to promise—and it grows *rooted*.'" Zephyrine sat back. Her hand had found her sword's hilt without her noticing, Maerin saw. Gripping it like a railing over a long drop. "This is a forgery."

"It's not."

"It has to be. We were executioners. My grandmother's grandmother—"

"—was a binder," Maerin said. "They all were. Every swordspeaker in history. That's what the gift is, Zephyrine. It was never a weapon. It was a *tool*. A key cut to a very specific lock." She tapped the ledger's open page. "Swordspeech doesn't kill gods. Nothing kills gods. Swordspeech *binds* them—speaks a promise so heavy, so absolute, that a god caught in it cannot move without breaking itself. The blade is just the pen. The oath is the ink. Your family wrote the first binding nine hundred years ago, and my family held the pen steady while they did it, and together we built the Order around one job: keeping the bindings intact."

Zephyrine stood up. It wasn't anger; Maerin had expected anger and would have preferred it. This was worse. This was a woman walking to the far wall because the room had gotten small.

"My family hid," Zephyrine said, her back to Maerin. "Ten years ago I burned every record, every sigil, every trace of the name Eriksen. Because the Order fell apart and the gift became a death sentence and I wanted—" She stopped. Started again. "I wanted to be nobody. That was the whole point. Nobody hunts nobody."

"And instead you spent ten years learning exactly what it feels like to be unbound," Maerin said softly. "Which is the one lesson they needed you to learn. Look at the last entry. Page ninety-one. Read who drafted the First Binding."

Silence. Then the sound of pages turning, quick and rough, and then no sound at all for a long time.

When Zephyrine finally spoke, her voice came out strange, stripped of everything, a voice reading its own autopsy. "*Drafted and sworn by Serane Eriksen, Voice of the Ninth Choir-Binding, in the presence of the assembled Order.*"

"Serane Eriksen," Maerin said. "Your ancestor. The first swordspeaker. The one who figured out that a god could be talked into chains if you used words heavy enough." She rose, slowly, keeping her hands visible the way you do with a spooked animal—or a drawn blade. "Every binding since was written by an Eriksen or corrected by one. Your bloodline isn't just part of the Order's history. It's the Order's *foundation*. And here's the part that's been keeping me awake since the day I found the ledger."

Zephyrine turned. Her face in the firelight was pale as bone and perfectly composed, which was its own kind of terrible.

"The bindings decay," Maerin said. "That's in there too—a god wears at its chains, year by year, century by century, and eventually the oath thins enough for it to slip. When that happens, someone has to renew the binding. Someone with the voice. Someone with the blood." She took one step closer. "There are four hundred years of renewal records in that book. Four hundred years of Eriksens walking into throne rooms and chapels and battlefields to re-swear gods back into their cages. And then, sixty years ago, the entries stop. No renewals. No corrections. Just a final note in my great-grandfather's hand saying the Eriksen line had withdrawn from the Order's service and taken the voice with them."

"We didn't withdraw. We were purged. The Grandmaster declared us heretics—"

"He declared you *gone*, which was worse, because he knew what your absence meant. He knew the bindings were starving. And rather than admit the Order couldn't function without your family, he buried the ledger, rewrote the litany, and taught six generations of children—including me—that we were god-slayers." Maerin's laugh came out bitter as ash. "We weren't slayers. We were jailers. Understaffed jailers. And the prisoners have been picking their locks ever since."

* * *

The candle had burned down two fingers' width before either of them spoke again.

Zephyrine had taken her sword off her belt and laid it across her knees, and now she sat looking down at it with an expression Maerin had seen once before, years ago, on the face of a knight the Order had sent to burn a village that sheltered a renegade. He'd done it. He'd done it well. And afterward he had looked at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger.

"So the Veiled One," Zephyrine said at last. "Three generations wearing the crown. Feeding on oaths. Growing rooted." She said the last word like it tasted of iron. "That's not possession. That's an old binding decaying. Something we chained centuries ago worked its way loose and found the biggest vessel in the kingdom."

"Or," Maerin said, "something we chained centuries ago was always meant to end up there." She crouched by the ledger again, turning to the middle pages, where diagrams crowded the margins—circles within circles, names written in a script that hurt to look at directly, and at the center of each diagram, a human figure crowned. "Read the theory section. The part about vessels."

Zephyrine read in silence. Maerin watched her jaw tighten, line by line.

"'A vessel must be given, not seized,'" she recited. "'A god forced into flesh will tear it asunder; a god invited will dwell in patience, waiting for the flesh to weaken. Therefore the binder must secure a vessel that consents—and a kingdom that consents for him. The strongest cage is one the prisoner believes is home.'"

"There it is," Maerin whispered. "You see it now. The Veiled One didn't conquer Thornfell. Thornfell *invited* it. Coronation oaths—freely spoken, generation after generation. The acclamation of the court. The loyalty-oaths of every lord and knight and guildmaster. Three generations of kings who swore themselves to the realm and thereby swore the realm to whatever was listening." She straightened. "It's not a parasite that wore the crown. It's a prisoner our ancestors locked in a cell made of promises, and every coronation adds another bar, and somewhere along the way it figured out that the bars go both ways—that a cage of oaths binds the keeper to the kept. It's been *feeding* on its own prison walls. Growing fat on the kingdom's faithfulness."

"Then why answer prayers? Why heal a dying child in a shrine village?"

"Because a rooted god wants the roots deeper. Every answered prayer buys another oath. Every miracle converts doubt into devotion. It's not generosity—it's agriculture." Maerin met her eyes. "And it knows the harvest is coming. Sellen's moon-count puts the thirteenth turn weeks away. On that night, if the old texts are right, the Veiled One completes the circuit—every oath in the realm, sworn and resworn and woven tight, until the binding inverts and the kingdom becomes the vessel and the god becomes the king in truth. No more Aldric. No more court. Just a god wearing a country."

Zephyrine's hands had gone white on the sword hilt.

"You said the bindings decay. You said someone with the voice has to renew them." Her voice was very controlled, very quiet, and Maerin recognized the control for what it was: a dam holding. "So that's what I am. That's what the gift is. Not execution. Not protection. Maintenance." A breath. "I spent ten years running from a legacy of slaughter, and it turns out I was running from a legacy of *carpentry*."

"Worse than carpentry." Maerin knelt across the ledger from her, close enough that the fire's heat pressed against both their faces. "Look at the diagrams again. The binding needs two anchors. The voice that speaks the oath—and the blood that carries the original covenant. Voice and blood, Eriksen and Voss, lock and key." She swallowed. Everything after this would change everything, and she had carried the knowledge alone for eleven years, and some part of her had hoped she'd never have to say it. "But there's a third element. The flaw in the design. Serane wrote it into the First Binding herself—deliberately, I think, because even she couldn't stomach a perfect cage."

"The flaw," Zephyrine repeated.

"*An unbinding requires a freely given crown.*" Maerin let the words fall into the silence like stones into a well. "The binding can't simply be broken from outside—it's woven too deep, anchored in every oath in the kingdom. But it can be *unmade* from within, if the vessel itself releases the god. Freely. Knowingly. A crown given back instead of worn. That's the one door Serane left unlocked. The one mercy in nine hundred years of chains."

Zephyrine stared at her. "Then the solution is simple. Tell Aldric. Have him abdicate."

"Do you hear yourself?" Maerin's voice cracked upward despite her. "The king hasn't drawn a free breath in thirty years. There may be nothing left of him to convince. And even if there is—even if some shard of Aldric survives in there—the Veiled One will know the moment anyone gets close. It reads oaths, Zephyrine. Intentions. It tastes a lie the moment it's spoken and a plan the moment it's formed. Everyone who's tried to reach the king has vanished, or worse—come back *sworn*, walking and talking and smiling with someone else's patience behind their eyes." She counted them off on her fingers. "Chancellor Beren. Captain Ilsette. The entire delegation from Callowmere, eleven souls, gone in a single night. And now the Order remnant knows you're asking questions, and the god knows your name—I felt it when you spoke your sword at the pass, the way a spider feels a fly touch the web—and the thirteenth moon is coming whether we're ready or not."

"So we're lost."

"No." Maerin closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way you close a coffin. "We're not lost. We're *outnumbered*, which is different. Because the flaw is real, and the flaw lives in you. Serane Eriksen wrote the First Binding, which means an Eriksen's voice can write its ending. You're not just the key, Zephyrine. You're the lock. The whole cage. Nine hundred years of your family's words are holding that god in place right now, tonight, while it eats, and the only person alive who can unravel them is sitting in a tithe-barn sharpening a sword she talks to in her sleep."

Zephyrine looked down at the blade across her knees. For a moment her lips moved, soundlessly, and Maerin—despite herself, despite everything—leaned in to catch the shape of the words.

Nothing. Or nothing she could hear. Whatever passed between the woman and the steel stayed between them.

"If I'm the lock," Zephyrine said finally, "then the Order remnant won't want the Veiled One destroyed. They'll want it *renewed*. Bound tighter. A god in a crown, forever, and the Order relevant forever, with an Eriksen dragged back to do the writing." Her eyes came up, and they were cold and clear and more frightening than any rage. "How many of your brothers and sisters know I'm alive?"

Maerin didn't answer fast enough.

"That many." Zephyrine nodded slowly, and began, with unhurried precision, to sheathe her sword. "Then we have a problem, Maerin. Because you just told me I'm the most valuable prisoner in Thornfell—and you told me in a barn with one door."

"I told you because you need to hear it," Maerin said. "Whatever else happens. Whatever you decide I am."

"And what I decide," Zephyrine said, rising, pulling her cloak around her shoulders, "is that tomorrow we ride for the capital. Tonight—" she paused at the barn door, silhouetted against the starless dark, and Maerin heard her draw one long breath, "—tonight I need to know everything else in that ledger. Every name. Every binding. Every secret your family buried under mine."

She stepped out into the dark. Maerin sat alone with the guttering fire and the grey book and the weight of what she'd unleashed.

It was only later, banking the coals, that she noticed the ledger's ribbon had slipped, and the page beneath the last one lay open, and across its bottom margin—in a hand she had never seen, brown and faded, older than the book itself—ran a single line of script that was not ink at all but something burned into the vellum from within:

*The ninth binding remembers its author. When the thirteenth moon rises, the Voice will be called home.*

Maerin Voss sat very still in the cold, and understood that she had not chosen this night to tell the truth.

The truth had chosen her.
