## 9. The Queen Who Knew

Isolde had learned to measure her son's absences the way other women measured rain.

Some days Aldric was present in his own body—laughing at the hounds, quarreling with the Master of Coin over grain tariffs, complaining that the mutton was over-salted. Those were the good days, and they had grown rarer as the years wore on, like coins slipping through a torn purse. On the bad days, the king sat at council with a stillness too perfect for any living man, and when he spoke, his voice came out at half a breath's delay from his lips, as though the words had to travel a great distance to reach him.

She had stopped telling anyone about the delay eleven years ago.

Now she stood at the window of her dowager apartments, watching the courtyard below where a merchant scribe—a woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that moved too carefully across the assembled court—was being escorted toward the royal chapel by one of the Veiled Guard. Isolde had noticed the scribe three days ago. She had noticed her because the woman watched the king the way Isolde herself once had: not with ambition, but with grief.

*Someone else sees it,* she thought, and the thought frightened her more than discovery ever had. Because if someone else could see it, then the silence she had built her life upon—the silence she had told herself was mercy, was duty, was love—might be nothing but what it had always whispered to her in the dark hours:

*Cowardice.*

A knock at her door. Not the chamberlain's triple rap. A single knock, soft, almost apologetic.

"Come," she said, and did not turn from the window.

The door opened and closed. The footsteps that crossed her carpet were light, deliberate, and stopped precisely where a servant would stop and an equal would not. Isolde turned.

The scribe stood in the middle of her sitting room, holding her cap in her hands, and up close there was no mistaking her for anything but what she was—not a merchant's clerk, not a spy of any house Isolde knew. Her eyes were the grey of old sword-blades, and they went first to the queen's hands, then to her throat, then to her face, reading her the way scholars read scripture.

"You sent for me," the woman said. "Or rather, you sent for a scribe to copy correspondence, and I answered."

"I asked for a woman who could keep her mouth shut and her eyes open," Isolde said. "The steward recommended you. He said you'd been in the capital a fortnight and no one remembered your name." She let that sit between them. "No one remembers your name because you have made certain of it. I know the craft. My husband's grandmother practiced a gentler version of it."

Something flickered in the woman's expression—not fear exactly. Recognition, perhaps. The weary kinship of one hunted creature sighting another.

"Who are you?" Isolde asked.

"Zephyrine." The name came out rough, as if it had been buried a long time and dug up only reluctantly. "And before you summon the guard, Majesty, consider that I could have killed you already, and instead I came when you called."

"I didn't call you here to threaten me."

"No," Zephyrine said. "You called me here because you're dying inside your own palace, and you wanted to look at someone else who is."

Isolde laughed—a short, ugly sound she hadn't made in years. "Bold, for a clerk."

"For a swordspeaker," Zephyrine said quietly, "it's hardly bold at all."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass. Isolde felt her whole body go still, the stillness she had perfected over eleven years of listening to her son speak in two voices. *Swordspeaker.* The order her husband's grandfather had disbanded. The executioners of the old gods, erased from every chronicle so thoroughly that Isolde herself had believed them legend until—

Until the night she had knelt beside her son's coronation bed and heard something answer his prayer that was not Aldric.

"Sit down," Isolde said. Her voice did not shake. She was proud of that, in a hollow way. "And tell me how long you've known."

* * *

They sat on opposite sides of the cold hearth, two women keeping a fire neither wished to light, because light drew attention and attention was death.

"My whole life," Zephyrine said. "I was born to it. My family served the old gods before the Order existed—we heard the swords speak, we carried out their judgments. When the gods went silent, we went silent with them. I spent ten years making sure no ledger in Thornfell held my name." She turned her hands over, palms up, as though showing the queen empty hands proved something. "Then prayers started being answered again. Real ones. Healings. Crops saved. A rockslide stilled on the mountain road. And every miracle carries a mark—a sigil in oath-script that only my kind can read."

"And the sigil says?"

"It says the god is awake. It says the god is *here*. And it knows my bloodline." Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "It's been leaving me messages, Majesty. In healed children. In shrine stones. It wants me found."

"Why?" Isolde asked, though she suspected she already knew, in the way she had always known things about the thing wearing her son's crown—knowledge arriving sideways, unbidden, like weather through a cracked window.

"Because I'm useful. Or dangerous. With gods, those are usually the same thing." Zephyrine leaned forward. "But I didn't come here to talk about me. You sent for me. So ask your question, Majesty. The real one. The one you've been carrying so long it's grown into your bones."

Isolde looked at her hands. They were her mother's hands, broad-knuckled, ringed in widow's gold. They had signed pardons and death warrants, had smoothed her son's hair, had once gripped a bedpost so hard the night Aldric was crowned that two knuckles had cracked and never healed right.

"The night before his coronation," she said, "Aldric prayed alone in the chapel. He was nineteen. He was terrified—he'd never wanted the crown, you know. His father died too soon, and Aldric would have been content to be a second son all his life, hunting and reading and disappointing no one important." Her voice stayed level. She had rehearsed this confession in her head for eleven years without ever speaking it aloud; the rehearsal held. "I came to bring him supper. I heard him praying. And I heard something answer."

"What did it say?"

"It said—" and here, for the first time, her voice did shake, because the words had waited so long to be spoken that they came out with the force of a dam breaking, "—it said, *at last, a vessel worth the wearing.*"

Zephyrine closed her eyes. "Yes," she said softly. "That's how they talk."

"I ran to him. I threw open the chapel doors, and he was standing at the altar with his arms out, and he was *smiling*, and it wasn't his smile. It fit his face the way a stolen coat fits—close enough to pass, wrong in every seam. And he turned to me and said, *Mother, I feel wonderful.*" Isolde's hands had become fists in her lap. "Eleven years. Eleven years I have watched that smile eat my son from the inside, and I have said *nothing*."

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. It was not an accusation. That was somehow worse. It was the question of a physician asking where it hurt.

"Because I tested it." Isolde rose and walked to the window, needing not to be looked at while she said this. Below, the courtyard had emptied; the scribe's escort had done its work and gone. "The first year, I told myself I was mistaken. Grief does strange things—I had just buried my husband, and kings are strange creatures even unpossessed. But by the second year I could not lie to myself anymore, so I began to test. I would say things to Aldric only he and I knew—childhood things, private things—and watch whether the thing wearing him flinched. Sometimes it did. Sometimes it used them against me, gently, the way you'd soothe a dog you didn't want biting. I learned its habits. It keeps him close to the surface on feast days, when the oaths of celebration are thick in the air—it *feeds* on feast days, did you know that? Every toast sworn to the king's health, every knee bent, every *long live His Majesty*—it drinks them. He comes back afterward, a little. Pale and blinking, like a man surfacing from deep water. Those are the days I get my son for an hour. Maybe two."

"And the rest of the time?"

"The rest of the time I get the king." She said the word *king* like something spat. "And here is my sin, swordspeaker, and you may judge it however you like, because I have judged it every way there is and none of them acquits me." She turned back to face Zephyrine, and let her see everything, because that was why she had summoned her—because after eleven years, someone needed to witness it or Isolde would go into her grave having confessed to no one but God, and God, she had begun to suspect, was not listening in Thornfell. "I said nothing because saying something would have broken the kingdom. Do you understand? The realm runs on the crown the way a mill runs on its wheel. Three provinces had already risen in revolt during the succession crisis. The border lords were sharpening knives. The Church of the Nine was split over my son's legitimacy. If I had stood up in the coronation hall and cried *that is not my son on that throne*, there would have been civil war within the month, and the war would have ground ten thousand children into the mud—and for what? To replace a parasite-king with a dozen ambitious men carving up the corpse of Thornfell? I chose peace. I told myself I chose peace. I told myself a kingdom fed and quiet under a false king was better than a kingdom burning under a true one."

"And now?"

"Now I watch the miracles being sold." Isolde's voice hardened. "You think I don't know? The Lord Chamberlain arranges audiences for nobles with sick daughters and failing harvests. They pray in the royal chapel. Their prayers are answered. And in exchange, they swear oaths—oaths of service, of loyalty, of *gratitude*—oaths that taste sweeter than any sworn under duress, because the swearer believes them freely given. Each one tightens the binding. Each one feeds it. I have watched the strongest houses in Thornfell kneel in that chapel and rise again as something slightly less than themselves, smiling, grateful, *hollowed*, and I have sat at table with them and drunk their wine." She laughed again, that ugly sound. "Peace. Yes. I bought peace. I bought it with my son's soul and I am still paying the installments."

* * *

For a long moment neither woman spoke. The fire remained unlit. Somewhere below, a bell rang the hour, and both of them flinched at it—Isolde from habit, Zephyrine, Isolde realized, from something older.

"You could have fled," Zephyrine said at last. "Widows flee. No one questions it. You could have taken a convent in the southern marches and let the thing wear the crown in peace."

"To what end? It would still wear him. Fleeing would only have meant I didn't have to watch." Isolde returned to her chair but did not sit; she stood behind it, hands on its back, like a captain at a helm that steered nothing. "Besides. There was one other reason. One I'm less proud of than all the others combined."

"Tell me."

"If I exposed it—if I broke the kingdom open looking for a cure—there might not *be* a cure. What if the thing cannot be driven out? What if driving it out kills him? Then I would have traded my son's life for a war." Her knuckles whitened on the chairback. "So I told myself I was waiting. Watching. Hoping some remedy would present itself. Eleven years of waiting, swordspeaker, and do you know what waiting looks like from the inside? It looks exactly like doing nothing. It has the same shape. It weighs the same."

Zephyrine was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, careful, the voice of someone handling a blade they respected.

"There may be a remedy."

Isolde's heart, which had beaten steadily through eleven years of horror, stumbled. "Speak plainly."

"The binding that holds it—an oath-binding, the oldest kind—can be undone. But not by force. Force feeds it. Every act of violence against the host, every treason, every rebellion, every assassination attempt—those are all just oaths of hatred, and it drinks those too. It can only be released if the crown is *freely given back*. Freely, Majesty. Not seized, not renounced under threat. Given." Zephyrine met her eyes. "Which means the unbinding has to come from the king himself. From whatever's left of Aldric underneath."

"Aldric is nineteen years old and buried alive beneath that thing. How would he free a crown he doesn't know he wears?"

"That," Zephyrine said, "is what I intend to find out. There are ways into a dreaming mind. Old ways. My family knew them." She paused. "They may kill me. I want you to understand that going in. If the god catches me in his host's dreams, it will learn everything I am, and it will use me. It may already be hunting me. So before I try, I need to know what you'll do, Majesty. If I fail. If I succeed and the kingdom learns its king was a puppet for three generations—that his grandfather was, and his father, and that every miracle, every sanction, every divine right this dynasty ever claimed was a parasite feeding at the root—what will you do?"

Isolde walked slowly to the window. The afternoon light lay flat and silver on the capital, on the smoke and the spires and the thousand small lives going about their business under a crown that ate oaths. Somewhere in the palace, her son—or the thing that had been her son for eleven years—was holding audience, dispensing justice with borrowed wisdom, accepting homage with a stolen smile.

Three generations. Not one. *Three.* Her father-in-law. Her husband. All of them worn. All of them eaten. And she had thought her grief was singular, her horror unique, when in truth she was only the latest in a line of widows and mothers who had knelt beside coronation beds and heard something answer.

"I will tell the truth," Isolde said.

She heard Zephyrine shift behind her.

"When the moment comes—whatever moment that is—I will stand in front of the court, the Church, the lords, and I will say what I know. Not before. Before would only feed it a war. But when you give me the moment, swordspeaker, I will spend everything I have left. My name, my title, my safety, my place in history—they can write *traitor* on my tomb if it buys my son back and breaks that thing's hold." She turned. "I have been loyal for eleven years, and I have finally understood that my loyalty was the lock on his cage. Loyalty to the crown. To the peace. To the lie. Well. I am an old woman, and I find I would rather die honest than live useful to a god."

Zephyrine studied her for a long moment, and Isolde saw something move behind the grey eyes—not pity, which she would have resented, but respect, offered cautiously, like a hand extended to a dog that had been kicked too often.

"They'll execute you," Zephyrine said simply. "If it comes to that. The god won't allow a public denunciation to survive the week."

"I know."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I raised a boy who hated the sight of blood," Isolde said. "He used to cry when the falconers wrung pigeons. Whatever is left of him, down there in the dark, is that boy. And I have spent eleven years abandoning him to protect the kingdom. I will not abandon him twice." She lifted her chin. "Give me your moment, swordspeaker. I am done choosing silence."

Zephyrine bowed her head—shallow, soldierly, nothing like a courtier's obeisance. "Then there's something you should hear before I go into his dreams. Something I learned from an archivist, about a prophecy concerning the thirteenth moon—"

The door burst open.

Isolde spun. In the doorway stood Captain Veyra of the Veiled Guard, armor gleaming, face arranged in perfect blank courtesy—and behind her, four guardsmen, and behind *them*, filling the corridor, the sound of many boots.

"Your Majesty." Veyra's bow was flawless. Her eyes never touched Isolde; they fixed on Zephyrine with the flat patience of a cat watching a hole. "Forgive the intrusion. His Majesty requests the presence of the scribe who has been attending the archives. Immediately." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "He seems to believe she has something to confess."

Isolde's blood turned to ice water. *He knows. He has known since she walked through my door.*

Zephyrine's hand had drifted, very casually, to the plain merchant's blade at her hip—and Isolde saw, with a clarity that would stay with her the rest of her life, the exact moment the woman decided not to draw it. Saw her choose the court, the witnesses, the game. Saw her smile a merchant's small, stupid smile and say:

"Of course, Captain. His Majesty honors me."

As they led her away, Zephyrine glanced back once—not at the guards, not at the door, but at Isolde. And her lips moved, shaping two silent words in the language of the old courts, the tongue every noblewoman learned at her mother's knee:

*Thirteenth moon.*

Then the door closed, and Widow-Queen Isolde stood alone in her cold room, understanding at last that the clock she had ignored for eleven years had begun, at long and terrible last, to tick.
