## 10. Miracles for Sale

The invitation arrived on vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, sealed in green wax with the sigil of the Chancellery of Devotions—a sunburst pierced by a quill. Zephyrine turned it over twice before she understood what she was holding.

*The Lord Chancellor requests the presence of Mistress Veyra Solenne, scribe in service to House Marrowgate, at the Autumn Presentation of Graces. Attendance is compulsory for all contracted scriveners of the third rank.*

She had been in the capital nine days. Nine days of copying trade ledgers and marriage contracts in a merchant's script she'd built from nothing, of wearing gray wool and ink-stained fingers, of being nobody so thoroughly that servants looked through her and guards forgot her face between one corridor and the next. It was the best disguise she had ever worn, because it wasn't one at all. Veyra Solenne existed on paper, in the tax rolls, in the memory of a hiring clerk who had been paid to remember—and that was enough.

But someone had noticed her anyway. Or perhaps the summons meant nothing at all, and every scrivener in Thornfell's capital had received the same green seal.

She decided to believe that, right up until the moment she walked into the Hall of Graces and saw what the court did with its prayers.

* * *

They had converted the old tournament grounds into something between a marketplace and a cathedral. Canopies of white silk stretched in rows like sails, each one hung with lantern-glass that caught the afternoon light and scattered it into halos. Beneath them sat the nobility of half the realm—dukes and margraves, dowager countesses and ambitious second sons—all of them waiting in an atmosphere of hushed, greedy reverence that Zephyrine recognized from temples she had no intention of remembering.

At the far end of the grounds, elevated on a dais of pale stone, stood a shrine.

It was new. That was the first thing wrong with it. The stone hadn't weathered; the gold leaf on the altar still smelled of fresh sizing; even the moss that should have crept up a shrine's northern face after a single winter was absent, scrubbed away or never permitted. Someone had built this thing within the last year and aged it by committee—a shrine designed by men who had read about holiness but never felt it.

And kneeling at its altar, robed in silver-gray, was a priest Zephyrine did not know, conducting what could only be called a demonstration.

"Bring the child forward," the priest said, and his voice carried with an ease that made the hairs on Zephyrine's neck rise. Not amplification. *Resonance.* She had heard that quality once before, long ago, in a voice that came out of her grandmother's sword on the night the Order died.

A noblewoman in dove-blue silk led a small boy up the steps. The boy walked stiffly, oddly, and when he turned to face the crowd Zephyrine saw why: his left arm ended at the wrist, the stump bound in linen.

"He fell from the stable loft in spring," the mother announced, loud enough for the front rows. "The physicians said the bone was too shattered. They said nothing could grow where nothing remained."

"And what did you offer?" the priest asked gently.

The woman's chin lifted. Whatever shame might once have attended this transaction had been polished away like tarnish from silver. "Three oaths. Sworn upon my house's name and witnessed under the crown's authority."

"Then let us see if the crown keeps faith with you."

The priest laid his hands over the boy's stump. He murmured—Zephyrine strained forward on her bench, heart hammering, trying to catch the words—and what she heard made her blood go cold. Not a prayer in any tongue she knew. Not the formal liturgies of the Nine, not the folk-whispers offered at roadside shrines. This was older, rawer, structured like a *contract*. Terms stated. Consideration named. A signature implied.

The language of oaths. Spoken fluently, by a man who had no gift for it, because he didn't need one.

He only needed to pass it along.

Light gathered—not the warm gold of temple frescoes but a thin, silvery pallor, moon-colored, and Zephyrine felt it the way a deaf man feels thunder through the floor. The air pressure changed. Every lantern in the hall guttered at once, though there was no wind.

When the light faded, the boy was screaming—but not in pain. His left hand was whole. Fingers flexing, bewildered, nails still soft as a newborn's. The crowd rose in a rustle of silk, and somewhere near the front a countess began to weep openly, and Zephyrine sat very still among the scriveners' benches with her pen frozen above her attendance ledger and thought:

*I know what that cost.*

Not in coin. Oaths were never paid in coin. Three oaths sworn on a house's name meant three bindings laid into the marrow of that family's future—their marriages, their inheritances, their loyalties, all of it now threaded with silver light and signed over to whatever waited beneath the throne. She had spent ten years learning how vows worked, how they braided themselves into people, how they could be spoken and kept and broken and *harvested*. Her family had been executioners of gods, yes—but more than that, they had been readers of the fine print of souls.

And the fine print here was written in a hand she recognized. The same sigil-work she'd seen branded into a healed child's skin in the mountain village. The same architecture of binding, scaled up from a peasant shrine to a national industry.

The Veiled One wasn't answering prayers out of mercy or mischief.

It was *selling* them.

* * *

After the presentation, the scriveners were set to work.

That was the part that nearly undid her. Rows of desks had been arranged in a side gallery, and each contracted scribe received a stack of documents to copy: Certificates of Grace, they were called, bearing the Chancellery's sunburst-and-quill seal. Each certificate recorded a petitioner's name, house, the favor granted, and—rendered in careful legal prose designed to sound like mere formality—the oaths tendered in payment.

*Magnus of House Delvere, having received restoration of his daughter's sight, does solemnly swear fealty unto the crown and unto the crown's appointed instruments, in perpetuity, binding himself and his heirs...*

*Ysolde of House Crane, having received favorable winds for her husband's fleet, swears her firstborn's service...*

*Bertrand of House Vail, having received remission of his debts, swears silence concerning matters seen within the palace walls...*

Zephyrine copied them all with steady hands while her mind screamed. Dozens. Hundreds. And these were only today's—the certificates stacked in archive chests along the gallery wall went back years, back past Aldric's coronation, past his father, a sedimentary record of a kingdom selling itself one answered prayer at a time. Dukes who could not disobey the crown because their grandfathers' healing had purchased their obedience. Admirals whose winds blew only at the Veiled One's pleasure. Commoners too, though their certificates were simpler, cruder—one oath of loyalty for a cured fever, a sworn tithe of devotion for rain on a dying field.

Every prayer answered was a hook set. Every miracle a mortgage.

She thought of the shrine village in the mountains, the healed child with the oath-sigil glowing faintly beneath his skin, and understood at last what she'd been looking at. Not a god waking in generosity. A god waking *hungry*, and discovering that the world had grown fat with desperate, faithful people who would sign anything for a miracle.

Her pen pressed so hard against the forty-third certificate that the nib tore the vellum.

"Mistress Solenne." A shadow fell across her desk. One of the Chancellery overseers, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that catalogued everything. "You've damaged a document."

"Forgive me, sir. The ink was slow."

"The ink," he repeated, and looked at her a moment too long. Then he held out his hand. "Give me the ruined sheet. You'll transcribe a replacement—and note, for your own guidance, that errors in Grace-certificates are treated as acts of impiety under the Revised Concordance. The crown takes its covenants seriously."

*Its* covenants. Not the realm's. She heard it, the little slippage, the truth wearing a clerk's grammar.

She surrendered the torn page and watched him carry it away, wondering if it would be burned or filed, whether somewhere a functionary would wonder why a third-rank scrivener's hand trembled on the word *perpetuity*.

Nine days of invisibility. She had perhaps nine hours left of it now.

Good, some cold voice inside her said. She was tired of being no one.

* * *

She found the archives by following the certificates backward.

The Chancellery of Devotions occupied a wing of the palace that had once housed, according to the architectural records she'd memorized during three nights of quiet research, the Royal Chapel of the Nine. The chapel itself still existed—she had glimpsed its spire from the courtyard—but its functions had migrated, its priests reassigned, its congregation thinned to ceremonial rump until the building stood mostly empty except for state funerals. Meanwhile the Devotions wing hummed with clerks and petitioners and armed stewards wearing livery she now understood: silver thread on gray, the exact color of the priest's robes, the exact color of the light that had regrown a child's hand.

The color of moonlight.

She got into the records vault the way invisible people get everywhere—by carrying the right documents, moving with purpose, and never once looking as though she didn't belong. The night archivist accepted her forged requisition slip without reading it closely, because the slip bore the seal of an under-secretary whose name she had lifted from a discarded memorandum, and because no one, anywhere in this palace, questioned paperwork. Paperwork was the true religion here. Even the miracles had been reduced to it.

The vault was cold and smelled of dust and lamp oil. She found what she needed in under an hour, working by shielded candlelight through the Chancellery's internal ledgers: annual tallies, going back thirty-one years.

Thirty-one years. Longer than Aldric had lived. She ran her finger down the columns and watched the numbers climb like a fever chart—oaths received per annum, categorized by type: fealty, service, silence, succession, devotion-in-perpetuity. In the early years, modest dozens. By Aldric's grandfather's reign's end, hundreds. Under Aldric's father, thousands. The current year's column was only two-thirds complete and already exceeded any prior record entirely.

And beside each annual total, in the margin, a smaller notation in a different hand—an older hand, recurring across decades like a refrain:

*Tithe rendered. Accepted. The account remains open.*

No name. No title. Just those words, generation after generation, in handwriting that changed with each king's reign but always wrote the same phrase. As though the kings themselves had served as bookkeepers for something that considered the entire realm a running balance.

Zephyrine closed the ledger with hands that were not quite steady.

*The account remains open.* Of course it did. Accounts opened in blood and miracle did not close; they compounded. Three generations of a kingdom's most powerful houses, bound oath by oath, feeding on their own desperation and gratitude, until the day the whole debt came due—and she knew when that day was, because Maerin Voss had said it in a snowbound inn with her scarred hands flat on the table: *the thirteenth moon, when the cycle completes and everything owed is collected at once.*

The Veiled One wasn't preparing for conquest. Conquest implied resistance. It was preparing for *collection*—and the collection notice had already been sent, in the only language it trusted, written in silver light across the bodies of everyone who had ever knelt at a new-built shrine and asked for a miracle.

* * *

She was three corridors from the vault when the bells began.

Not alarm bells—she knew those, had heard them once in a border town, all dissonance and panic. These were different: a slow, sweet, deliberate peal rolling out from the palace spires, and beneath it, spreading through the evening streets like spilled wine, the murmur of gathering crowds. She reached a window overlooking the Processional Way and saw them streaming toward the Devotions wing by torchlight. Hundreds. Thousands. Merchants and laborers and minor nobility, all converging with the same expression she'd seen on the faces in the Hall of Graces—that hushed, hungry reverence.

An announcement was being posted. She could read it even at distance, because the letters were painted large on a banner unfurled from the balcony: **BY GRACE OF THE CROWN: THE WINTER LISTING OF DEVOTIONS. ALL PETITIONERS OF FAITH PRESENT THEIR OFFERINGS. THE CROWN HEARS AND ANSWERS.**

A public market. They were opening the miracle trade to the general public—no longer just nobles with houses to pledge, but anyone, everyone, the whole city invited to come and kneel and swear. Zephyrine did the arithmetic with numb precision: the annual tally she'd just read would be surpassed in a single season. Perhaps in a single week. The thirteenth moon was weeks away, and the Veiled One was gorging itself, fattening its ledger, calling in advance on every ounce of devotion the capital could produce.

Below her window, a young woman in a fishmonger's apron clutched a sick infant to her chest, pushing toward the front of the crowd with the terrible fixed hope of a mother who has tried everything else. She would swear anything. They all would. That was the genius of it, the obscene elegance: the Veiled One needed no armies, no inquisitions, no forced conversions. It had merely to heal a few children, save a few ships, and desperation would deliver the rest.

Anger came up in Zephyrine like floodwater, sudden and total, washing away ten years of careful neutrality. She had hidden to survive. She had told herself that silence was wisdom, that erasure was safety, that a swordspeaker who spoke nothing harmed no one and no one harmed her. Standing at that window, watching a city line up to sell itself, she finally admitted what she had known since the mountain shrine: silence was not neutral. Silence was complicity with interest accruing.

Her family had bound gods once. They had written the first binding, Maerin claimed, and she had hated them for it—for the arrogance, the presumption, the chains passed down through her blood like a birthright of cages. But at least they had *acted*. At least they had looked at something vast and hungry and said *no further*.

The crowd below began to sing. Some official had distributed hymnals, or perhaps the words were simply known, and a thousand voices rose together in a melody Zephyrine almost recognized—an old devotional tune, she thought, from before, twisted slightly, its cadences bent toward a rhythm she felt in her teeth. Silver light kindled along the Processional Way, gentle as frost forming, and the fishmonger woman reached the banner at last and sank to her knees with her baby raised overhead, her mouth shaping words of offering.

Zephyrine stepped back from the window into shadow.

Resolve settled over her the way her old armor once had—heavier than she remembered, colder, but familiar. She could not unbind a god alone. She could barely walk through this palace without a forged slip. But she knew now where the power lived, and where the weakness: in the ledger. In the oaths. Every binding had terms, and every contract had flaws, and somewhere in thirty-one years of silver-scripted paper was the seam that would tear the whole garment—if she could find it before the thirteenth moon.

She turned down the darkened corridor toward her quarters, mind already composing the message to Maerin Voss that she would write tonight and burn after memorizing.

She had taken six steps when the lamps ahead of her lit—not by any lamplighter's hand, but all at once, in sequence, silver-white and utterly silent.

Standing beneath them, seven strong, in armor the color of moonlight on steel, waited the crown's own knights. Their leader removed his helm, and Zephyrine saw the oath-sigil burning faintly on his brow like a brand seen through skin.

"Mistress Solenne," he said, in a voice that resonated the way the priest's had resonated, layered, doubled, *shared*. "The crown would have a word with you about the books."
