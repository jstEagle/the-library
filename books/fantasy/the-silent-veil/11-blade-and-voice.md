## 11. Blade and Voice

The bell above the dyer's shop had not rung in years. Zephyrine noticed its silence the way she noticed everything now—too carefully, too completely—as she slipped through the alley behind the tannery with Sellen's stolen map pressed flat against her ribs.

The archivist's warning rang in her skull like a struck coin: *Twelve turns remain. When the thirteenth moon rises, every oath in Thornfell will belong to it.*

She had meant to leave the city that night. She had a horse stabled past the Saltgate, papers forged well enough to pass a bored customs officer, and a route south through the fen country where no knight of the crown would think to look for a dead woman. That was the plan. It was a good plan.

Then the lamps went out.

Not all of them—the whole street, only the three that mattered, the ones bracketing the mouth of Tanner's Row. The darkness that poured into the gap between them did not behave like darkness. It pooled. It waited.

Zephyrine stopped walking.

*They know,* said the sword at her hip, in the small voice only she could hear—or had been able to hear, once, before she'd starved the gift down to embers. Ten years she had kept it muzzled. Ten years of biting back answers when blades whispered their opinions, of letting iron rust silent on her hip while lesser soldiers fumbled and bled.

*They know,* the sword said again, and this time she heard something beneath it. Fear.

"I know," she murmured, and drew.

The steel came free with a sound like a held breath released. Moonlight caught the fuller—that thin channel of darker metal running the blade's spine—and for the first time in a decade, Zephyrine let herself *listen* all the way down.

The blade sang.

It was not music. It was closer to a name being spoken very slowly by something that had no mouth—a vibration in the metal older than the forge that shaped it, older than the smith's grandfather, a thread of the old voice that lived in all true-forged steel and that her family alone had been bred to hear. Swordspeech, the Order had called it. The executioner's gift. The reason the Eriksens' name had been scrubbed from every ledger in Thornfell, including the ledgers of heaven.

*Left,* the sword breathed. *Three. Roof. One more behind you. They carry oaths like lanterns.*

Zephyrine turned her head a fraction and saw them: shapes detaching from the shadows of the tannery roofline, dropping without sound, armor black as spilled ink. Oath-sworn knights—the Veiled One's own. She'd read about them in Sellen's restricted folios, described in a hand that shook: *men who have sworn so much of themselves away that nothing remains but obedience and edge.*

Four of them. In the narrow street. Against a woman who hadn't spoken a full word of command in ten years.

"Fair odds," she told the sword, and meant it as a joke, and the sword did not laugh.

*You are rusty,* it said. *And they will not tire, and they will not flee, and if one of them touches you with bare steel you will hear what he has sworn, and it will unmake your sleep for a year.* A pause, the metal equivalent of a swallow. *Speak to me, Eriksen. All the way. Or die polite.*

The first knight came off the roof like a dropped stone.

---

Later—much later, in the hours when her hands still trembled and she sat in a drainage culvert outside the city walls watching the moon climb—Zephyrine would try to remember the fight in order, and fail. Memory broke apart under what happened next the way ice breaks under a boot: cleanly, all at once, along lines no one chose.

But some pieces stayed whole.

She remembered the first knight's halberd coming down and her body answering before her mind caught up, ten years of buried instinct rising like a drowned man. She remembered parrying rather than dodging—*stupid, rusty, stupid*—and feeling the impact travel up her arm and into her chest, and hearing, through the contact, exactly what the sword had promised she would hear.

The knight's oath.

It poured through the joined steel into her bones: *I swear my breath to the throne, my sleep to the throne, my name to the throne, I am the Veiled One's until the last moon wanes, I swear, I swear, I swear—*

No man's voice. A thousand men's voices braided into one, and underneath them something vast turning over in its sleep, attentive, curious, *hungry*. The knight's eyes above his visor were the color of milk left out overnight. He felt nothing. He had sworn away the part of him that felt.

Zephyrine understood, in one white-hot instant, why her ancestors had been made executioners. Because only a swordspeaker could hear an oath. And only a swordspeaker could—

*Unmake it,* the sword finished for her, and there was awe in the metal now, and terror. *Girl. You remember how.*

She did. Her grandmother's hands over hers on a practice yard at dusk, guiding a blade through a pattern called the Opening of the Mouth. *An oath is a knot tied around a soul,* old Maren had said. *A swordspeaker doesn't cut flesh. We cut knots.*

She had not spoken since she was nineteen years old. Since the night she burned the Order's rolls and ran, she had swallowed every syllable, kept the gift leashed so tight it had become a kind of self-mutilation. Speaking meant being heard. Being heard meant being found.

But the second knight was already inside her guard, and the third was circling toward the alley mouth to cut off retreat, and the fourth—she could feel him now through the ground, through the wet cobbles, through every piece of metal within thirty feet, because her sense was blooming open like a wound—was kneeling in the dark with his palm against the earth.

Praying.

*She is here,* he was saying, to something below the city, below the world. *The swordspeaker is here, in Tanner's Row. I give You her location. I ask Your will.*

The answer came up through the stones.

It was not words. It was pressure—the same pressure Zephyrine had felt in the royal chapel when she touched the throne, multiplied, focused, *aimed*. The Veiled One was awake, and it was looking at her through the kneeling knight the way a man looks through a keyhole.

*There you are,* said the pressure, without language. *Little lock. Little key. I have wondered for so long which you were.*

Zephyrine made her choice the way she made most choices: fast, angry, and certain to cost her.

"Come," she said to the sword.

Not aloud. Not in any tongue that had a writing system or a homeland. She spoke downward, into the steel, into the old voice coiled there like a spring compressed for centuries, and she spoke *everything*—not the fragments, not the half-commands she'd permitted herself in the rockslide pass a lifetime ago, but the full grammar of her blood. The naming-tongue. The one her family had used to execute gods.

The street lit up.

Not firelight—*wordlight*, every syllable she spoke made visible in the air as pale silver script that hung and drifted like ash falling upward. The knights froze mid-motion. Even oath-sworn things, even hollowed-out things, recognized the sound of the old authority. Somewhere in their ruined depths, the parts of them that had once been men remembered kneeling.

*Bind and break,* Zephyrine said, and moved.

What followed was not swordsmanship as any drillmaster in Thornfell taught it. She cut the first knight's halberd shaft and the *oath in the halberd shaft*—severed the binding that made the weapon an extension of the throne's will, and the wood simply forgot how to be wielded, sagging in the knight's grip like a rotten branch. He stared at it with his milky eyes, and in that heartbeat of confusion she took his legs out and put her blade against his gorget—not to kill.

To speak.

"Loose," she commanded the knot of him. "Loose, loose, loose."

The oath tore. She felt it go—felt it as a shudder through her arm, a soundless snap like a mast cable parting, and the knight collapsed sideways, breathing, blinking, his eyes flooding back to brown and then overflowing with tears as ten years of suppressed horror arrived in him all at once. He began to sob into the mud. She had no time to envy or pity him.

The second knight's sword met hers, and through the crossing she heard his oath trying to *take hers*—the Veiled One reaching through him, through the joined steel, groping for the bright new voice it had just heard. Its attention was fully on her now. The pressure of it wrapped around her skull like a band of cold iron.

*Say it again,* the god purred through the contact. *Your true name. Say it and be mine. Every speaker breaks eventually. Every speaker kneels. Your grandmother knelt. Did she never tell you?*

*Lie,* said the sword, fiercely. *She lies even in truth, girl. Keep cutting.*

But the damage was done. Because to fight at full voice, Zephyrine had to keep speaking—and everything she spoke, she spoke *in her own name*. The naming-tongue required it. Authority flowed from identity; that was the whole architecture of the gift. Every command she gave signed itself.

*I, Zephyrine Eriksen, daughter of Corvan, daughter of Maren, of the line of the First Binder—I loose this knot—*

The third knight didn't attack. He simply stood at the end of the street and *listened*, palms open, head tilted, a living ear for his master. And far beneath the city, in the chapel-dark beneath the throne room, something ancient and patient gathered each syllable of her name the way a miser gathers coins.

The second knight fell with half his oaths unraveled, weeping like the first. The fourth rose from his knees, drawn sword in hand, and now he was not praying—he was *singing*, a low monotone, feeding her every spoken word upward in real time.

Zephyrine killed him. Properly, cleanly, through the throat before his song reached its second verse, and she hated it, hated the waste, because he had been someone's son before the throne ate him. The other two she left alive, gutted of their bindings, useless to the god and broken open to their own grief.

And then she stood alone in Tanner's Row among drifting silver script, and understood what she had done.

The silence after the fight was not empty.

*He knows,* the sword whispered. It was shaking. Steel should not shake. *He knows everything. Your line. Your blood. The First Binding is written in you, and you just recited yourself to him from the marrow out.*

"I had no choice."

*There is always a choice. You chose to win.* The blade's voice gentled, which was worse. *Put me away. Run. And Zeph—when you sleep tonight, bar the door of your mind as best you can. He will come visiting. He knows your name now, and names are doors.*

---

She made the Saltgate as the fourth bell rang, hood up, blood on her sleeve that she'd passed off to a drunk watchman as butcher's spill. The horse was where she'd left it. The forged papers were in her saddlebag. The road south lay open and dark and safe.

She did not take it.

Because as she tightened the girth, she felt it happen—felt it the way you feel a change in weather before you can name it. The pressure returned. Not around her. *Beneath* her. Beneath the city, beneath the fens, everywhere at once, a tide turning in some ocean deeper than geography. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that she knew its texture, was her own voice played back to her.

Her name, repeated. Learned. Practiced.

*Zephyrine. Zephyrine Eriksen. Daughter of Corvan. Key and lock and door.*

And beneath the repetition, faint but growing clearer, another voice. Young. Male. Fractured, like light through cracked glass.

*Help—no. No, not help. Listen. Listen, whoever you are, whoever he's hunting—you have to understand what I am before you come. Please. Before you do anything at all—understand me first.*

The boy-king. Aldric, somewhere down in the dark under the crown, had felt her voice tear through the city's oaths like lightning through a roof—and he was reaching for her the way a drowning man reaches for the one lit window on a dark shore.

Zephyrine stood in the stable doorway with her hand on her horse's bridle and the road south waiting, and the sword spoke one last time, quiet as a confession:

*Well, Eriksen? The road's right there. He'll hunt you either way now. But only one direction leads back to him.*

She looked south. Then she looked north, toward the palace hill, where the thirteenth moon's shadow had already begun to gather.

She turned the horse around.
