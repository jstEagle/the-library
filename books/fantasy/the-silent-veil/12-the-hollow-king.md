## 12. The Hollow King

Aldric had been dreaming of the sea again.

It was always the sea. Not the grey chop of Thornfell's northern coast, which he had seen perhaps six times in his life and always from behind a wall of honor guard, but a sea from before—vast and green-gold, warm as bathwater, stretching to a horizon that belonged to no kingdom. In this dream he was nine years old, barefoot on wet sand, and no one had ever told him he would be king.

He knew, even here, that it wasn't real. That was the cruelest part of the dream: he knew. Nine-year-olds were not supposed to know things like that. But the boy on the sand knew, the way a man drowning knows the surface is above him, and he kept running along the shoreline anyway because running was better than standing still, and standing still was better than listening for the voice beneath the water.

The voice was there tonight.

*You are loved,* it said, in the tone of a mother, of a priest, of a whole kneeling nation. *You are chosen. You are the crown and the crown is you.*

"I'm nine," Aldric said to the sea. His voice came out small and cracked, older than it should have been. "I've been nine for eleven years."

The water did not answer that. The water never answered that.

Then the tide went out.

Not slowly, not the way tides should—the whole green-gold sea pulled back from the shore at once, draining away like water through fingers, and the sand under his feet turned to pale flagstone, and the sky turned to vaulted stone, and Aldric stood alone in the great chapel of the Citadel, the one with the thirteen moon-windows, except every window showed the sea draining into darkness.

Someone else was in the chapel.

She stood near the altar where he had been crowned—or where *something* had been crowned wearing his body—and she did not look like anyone who belonged in dreams. She was thin and road-worn, dressed in a smuggler's oilskin over plain wool, dark hair cut short and practical, and at her hip hung a sword wrapped so thoroughly in cloth and cord that it looked less like a weapon than a wound someone had bandaged. Her eyes were the color of wet slate, and they moved across the chapel with the flat, cataloging attention of someone counting exits.

Zephyrine Eriksen. He didn't know how he knew her name. He knew it the way he knew the sea.

"You're not supposed to be able to come here," Aldric said.

Her hand went to the wrapped sword—not drawing, just resting—and she turned, and when she saw him she made a sound low in her throat, half breath, half grief, quickly swallowed.

"Your Majesty," she said.

"Don't." The word came out harder than he meant it, and he heard the other voice underneath it for a moment, the doubled resonance that made courtiers bow lower and priests weep, and he hated it, hated hearing it in his own mouth even here, even in the one room left that was supposed to be only his. "Don't kneel. Don't call me that. You came all this way—" He stopped. "How *did* you come all this way?"

"Swordspeech opens doors," she said. "Even doors inside a god."

"It's not asleep," he warned, urgent now, glancing at the drained windows. "When you speak it, it hears. It hears everything I hear, everything I—"

"I know what it hears." She crossed the flagstones toward him, and up close he could see she was younger than her eyes, maybe thirty, with a scar through one eyebrow and sun-cracks at the corners of her mouth that said she used to smile more. "That's why I'm speaking quietly. And why I'm here instead of anywhere with walls thinner than your skull."

He laughed. It startled him—he couldn't remember the last time laughing hadn't felt like something borrowed. "My skull has very thick walls. It built them itself. It wanted somewhere to keep me."

Something moved across her face. Pity, he thought, and then anger, though not at him. "How long?"

"How long since the coronation?" He looked down at his own hands. They were nine years old in the dream, small and uncallused, and he flexed them because it was the only part of himself he trusted to be true. "Eleven years, four months. I remember the anointing oil. It smelled like rotting lilies, but everyone said it smelled like sanctity, so I learned to say it too. Then I was walking backward up the steps of my own mind, and something very old sat down in the front of me and started waving."

Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "And before? What do you remember before?"

"The sea." He gestured at the impossible windows. "I was a ward at Greywatch Abbey until I was eight. My mother sent me there after my father died—I think she was afraid. I never understood of what until later. I used to run down to the water when the brothers weren't looking." He smiled, and it hurt. "I was fast. It's the only thing I was ever good at, being fast. Then the regency council found me and said I was the last of the line and the realm needed a king, and my mother knelt to me in front of everyone, and I understood that I had been caught."

"She knew," Zephyrine said softly. "Your mother knew what would happen."

"My mother is dead." He said it evenly, because he had practiced saying it evenly for eleven years, alone, in the dark of himself, while his mouth gave speeches and his hands signed treaties and his body fathered nothing because even that the Veiled One kept locked. "She died two winters ago. It wept at her funeral. Everyone said what a devoted son. I was screaming the whole time. Nobody heard."

The silence between them filled with the sound of the absent sea.

"They don't know," Aldric said. "Any of them. Chancellor Veyne thinks I'm cold. The priests think I'm holy. My aunt Isolde—" he hesitated on the name, watching Zephyrine's face sharpen— "my aunt thinks I am exactly the king my father was, which is to say distant, and she grieves it, and I watch her grieve across dinner tables three times a week while *it* eats her loyalty out of the air like a man eating oysters." His voice shook. He let it. There was no one here to perform for except a stranger with a bandaged sword, and somehow that made honesty possible for the first time in eleven years. "Do you know what it's like? Watching your own life from the back row? It lets me see. That's its mercy, or its cruelty, I've never decided. It lets me sit in the dark behind my own eyes and watch myself be adored."

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. "Why let you see at all? A prisoner kept blind is easier to manage."

"Because seeing is the leash." He hugged his arms around himself, suddenly aware of how small the dream-body was, how much smaller than the king's body it wore outside. "It says the same thing every night, when it settles down to feed. *This is what you agreed to.* Because I did agree. That's the worst of it. At the coronation, when the archpriest asked if I would take the crown and all its burdens—" he recited it in the doubled voice without meaning to, and flinched— "*all its burdens, seen and unseen*, I said yes. I was eight days past my ninth birthday and I said yes, and it has never once let me forget that the door was opened from the inside."

Zephyrine had gone very still. He watched her arrive at something, some piece of knowledge clicking into place, and her hand tightened on the wrapped hilt until the knuckles whitened.

"That's why it needs the consent," she murmured. "That's why it can't just take. Every binding wants a freely given oath, even the oldest ones. Even *him*."

"You know what it is." It wasn't a question. "The thing wearing me. You know its name."

"The Veiled One. And I know how it got into your family, and I know how it gets out." She paused, and he watched her decide how much truth he could bear, and something in her expression told him the answer she'd reached was: all of it, because he'd already borne worse. "But first tell me what you want. Not what the realm wants. Not what prophecy wants. You."

Aldric opened his mouth and the easy answers came crowding up—freedom, restoration, vengeance, justice, every word a coin minted for him by other people's hopes—and he pushed them all aside and looked at what was underneath, and what was underneath terrified him.

"I want to be believed," he said.

Zephyrine blinked.

"Not rescued. Not freed, not yet, maybe not ever—I've done the arithmetic, I know what happens to the realm if the crown empties suddenly, I've had eleven years to do the arithmetic." He stepped closer, and the dream-chapel dimmed around them, the drained windows bleeding darkness inward, as if the place itself leaned in to listen. "Everyone who might save me will want proof first, and the proof is *me*, and no one can reach me to check. So they'll trust the signs—the two voices, the miracles, whatever trail you followed here. But someday, someone will stand in front of me and say *are you in there, is it really you*, and I will need them to believe my answer. Not because I prove it. Because they choose to." His small hands were fists at his sides. "It lies to everyone. It lies *beautifully*. It can wear my face and my voice and my tears—it wore them at my mother's funeral, it wept real salt water, I felt it cry and I could not stop it. So when I tell you the true things, you will have no way of knowing they're true except deciding that I am worth believing. That's what I want. Someone to decide."

The silence stretched long enough that he began, quietly, in the cellar of himself, to prepare for the answer *no*.

Zephyrine unwound the cord from her sword.

Not all of it—just enough to free the grip, enough to lay her bare palm against the leather wrapping, and when she spoke it was in a language he had never heard and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own name shouted across a crowded square. The syllables were old and sharp-edged, made for oaths and executions, and the chapel windows flared green-gold, the dead sea briefly refilling with light.

She was swearing something. He could not translate it, but he could feel its shape, the way you feel the shape of a key through a coat pocket: *I will not lie to you. I will not leave you in the dark. What I learn, you will learn. Whether it saves you or damns you, you will hear it first from me and not from the thing that wears your face.*

When she finished, the light sank back down and the sword went quiet, and Aldric realized his cheeks were wet.

"That's swordspeech," he managed. "My family used to—"

"Yes." Her voice was rough. "Yes, they did. We'll get to that. It's worse than you think and stranger than you're imagining, and I need you to hold on to being angry at me for about a day before I tell you, because right now you're grateful and gratitude makes people stupid." She wiped her own eyes with the heel of her hand, annoyed at them. "But understand this much: my blood wrote the first binding. Whatever was done to you, my family started it, generations before either of us was born. So when I say I'll tell you the truth, know that the truth includes my guilt."

"I don't care whose fault it is," Aldric said, and heard how it sounded, and made himself say plainer: "No—that's a lie, I care enormously, I'll care about nothing else once you tell me. But right now, tonight, there is one thing I need to give you before it wakes, because it sleeps shallowly and it's been stirring for an hour, and I may not get another chance."

"Tell me."

"The thirteenth moon." He glanced up at the highest window, where the drained sea met a sky that held, faintly, the ghost of a moon that cast no shadow. "It's been feeding faster. You've seen the answered prayers, the kneeling crowds, the nobles buying blessings—it's gorging because the turn is close. When the thirteenth moon rises full, it completes something. A great binding. Every oath sworn in this realm, from coronation vows to a farmer's promise to his neighbor, gathered into one knot, and the knot tied around—" he tapped his own chest, around the heart of the nine-year-old body— "around the anchor. Me. After that, unbinding isn't hard. It's impossible. There won't be a seam left to cut. It will have sewn itself into the fabric of every promise anyone ever makes, forever."

"How close?"

"One turn of the moon. Perhaps less. It's begun rehearsing." His voice dropped. "Last week it made me—made *the king*—swear a small thing before the full court. A road tax remission, nothing. But I felt it taste the oath afterward, savor it, the way you'd test a rope before climbing. It's checking its knots, Zephyrine. All of them."

She nodded slowly, and he watched the strategist wake behind her eyes even as the mourner stayed in her face. "Then here's what I need from you, and it may be the hardest thing I ask. I need you to keep being the hollow king. Keep performing. Keep the mask perfect while I find the way to break the binding—because there *is* a way, I've read the original oath, and it has a flaw, and the flaw is the reason I'm in your head instead of driving a sword through the throne."

"There's a flaw?" Hope rose in him, huge and dangerous, and he crushed it down with practice. Eleven years had taught him that hope was the thing the Veiled One fed on best; despair it merely tolerated. "What kind of flaw?"

"A freely given crown," she said. "Unbinding requires one. Which means at some point, someone is going to have to offer you the crown freely—and you're going to have to be awake to receive it." She met his eyes, slate-grey meeting grey, prisoner meeting lock-maker's heir. "Can you stay? In there, in the dark, watching, for one more turn of the moon? Knowing it ends or doesn't end soon?"

Aldric thought about the sea. About the boy who ran on the sand because running was better than standing still.

"I've stayed for eleven years," he said. "I can stay for thirty days. But Zephyrine—" and here his composure finally cracked, the boy showing through the king showing through the prisoner, all three of them at once, "—when it's time, whatever you have to do, however it has to be done—if freeing me costs anything you cannot pay, then leave me. Swear it. Swear it in your sharp old language. I would rather be the anchor of a living kingdom than the freed corpse at the center of a ruined one. Promise me you'll weigh it honestly. Promise me you won't spend the realm to buy me."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then her palm flattened against the wrapped blade again, and the green-gold light came up, and she swore.

He didn't understand a word. He believed every one.

Far above the chapel, in the highest window, the ghost-moon shivered—like the surface of water disturbed by something rising from far below—and Aldric felt, in the deep cellar of himself, the vast attention begin to turn toward them like a whale hearing a heartbeat through a hundred fathoms of dark.

"It's waking," he whispered. "Go. Go *now*—"

"Thirty days," Zephyrine said, already fading at the edges, the dream reclaiming her. "Hold the mask. Count the knots. And Aldric—" her voice came from very far away and very close, both at once, the last thing before the sea rushed back in— "you are not the crown. You never were. Remember that when it tells you otherwise."

The tide returned.

He woke in the dark behind his own eyes, in the body that waved and blessed and lied, and felt the ancient attention settle over him like a wet cloak, and somewhere below the throne room, faint through stone and blood and bone, a bell was ringing for an hour that had no business being rung—

—and the thing wearing his crown sat up, and smiled, and said, in his voice, to the empty chamber:

*"Well. Now I know your face too, little swordspeaker."*

* * *

Outside the Citadel walls, in a rented room above a dyer's shop, Zephyrine woke with the taste of oath-iron on her tongue and her unwrapped sword humming against the floorboards, and knew that the price of mercy paid in dreams was always collected in waking.

Maerin Voss knocked twice—her signal—and said through the door, low and urgent:

"Get dressed. The Order's citadel just lit its beacon. They know you're in the city."
