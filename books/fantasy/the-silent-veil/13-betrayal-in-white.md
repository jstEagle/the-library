## 13. Betrayal in White

Maerin Voss had broken oaths before. She had broken them the way a soldier breaks bread—quickly, without ceremony, because hunger demanded it and tomorrow would demand it again. She had abandoned her post at the Saltmarch garrison when the Order dissolved. She had let Commander Halleth die rather than speak his name into her blade one last time, because speaking it would have cost her the only weapon she had left.

But she had never betrayed anyone she trusted.

She sat in the white-walled chamber beneath the Citadel of the Sundered Blade, watching candlelight gutter against stone that had been scrubbed of every sigil, every sword-mark, every trace of what the Order used to be. Nine years since the disbanding. Nine years since the Grandmaster's seal was cracked in half and scattered into the sea, and still the walls smelled of ash and lye, as if the cleansing had happened yesterday.

The remnant had kept this room. Of course they had. The Order had never truly died; it had only gone quiet, the way a held breath goes quiet, waiting for the lungs to remember their purpose.

Zephyrine Eriksen was in the cells below.

*She trusts me,* Maerin thought, and the thought was a knife turning slowly in a wound that would not close. *That is the worst part. That is the unforgivable part.*

The door opened without a sound. High Remnant Corvane entered the way he entered everything—like frost spreading across a window. He wore white, as they all did now. Not the grey of the old Order, not the black of mourning. White, the color of unwritten pages, of blank vows, of things erased so thoroughly that even memory could find no purchase on them.

"Sister Voss." His voice was soft. It was always soft. Men who shouted wanted to be obeyed; men like Corvane wanted to be believed. "You've had three days. I trust you've reached your decision."

"I have."

He waited. He was very good at waiting. Maerin had watched him hold silence for entire hours in council, letting the other Remnants fill the void with their fears until their fears became his arguments.

"She found the flaw," Maerin said. "In the First Binding. There's a way to unmake the Veiled One—a freely given crown, spoken in swordspeech. The girl means to use it."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes. Not fear. Something colder. Satisfaction, maybe, or the particular pleasure of a man confirming arithmetic he had already done.

"And you told her where the flaw could be read."

"The archives are open to any sworn blade. She asked. I—" Maerin stopped. Started again. "I answered honestly. That was my error. Or my sin. Whichever word the Order prefers these days."

"Neither." Corvane drew a chair from the wall and sat, unhurried, arranging his white sleeves with the care of a man dressing an altar. "Honesty is not a sin, Sister. Honesty is simply information. What we do with information—that is where judgment lives." He folded his hands. "Tell me what troubles you. Truly. Not the doctrine. You have never cared much for doctrine."

The observation stung because it was true, and because it proved how well he knew her, and because being known was its own kind of chain.

"What troubles me," Maerin said slowly, "is that I can't decide whether I'm about to save the kingdom or gut it."

"Ah." He nodded, gentle as a confessor. "Then let me ask you something. When you stood over Zephyrine Eriksen in the pass—when you offered your alliance, blade to blade—what did you feel?"

Maerin looked at her hands. They were a swordspeaker's hands, scarred across the knuckles where the words lived just under the skin, itching to be spoken into steel. Ten years she had carried them. Ten years of silence that felt less like discipline and more like suffocation.

"Purpose," she admitted. "For the first time since the disbanding. Someone was finally doing the work. Someone finally saw what I see—the king is hollow, the court is rotten, the prayers are being eaten by something wearing a crown. And she was going to stop it. With her bloodline, no less. The Eriksens wrote the first binding. It was—it felt like fate correcting itself."

"Fate," Corvane murmured. "Yes. And then?"

"And then she started talking about unbinding it. About freeing the god." Maerin's jaw tightened. "She thinks the Veiled One is a parasite and Aldric is its prisoner. She thinks killing the binding kills the sickness. But she doesn't understand what happens after, Corvane. None of us do. Three generations of kings bound to the thing. Every oath sworn beneath that crown—every knight's vow, every coronation, every marriage contract sealed in the royal chapel—all of it runs through the Veiled One like blood through a heart. Cut that heart out and what bleeds?"

"The kingdom," Corvane said quietly. "The kingdom bleeds."

"The kingdom *dies.*" Maerin stood, unable to sit under the weight of it. "Every loyalty-oath in Thornfell unravels the moment the anchor breaks. The border lords will tear each other apart within a season. The eastern marches will go to the Kethrin clans before the snow melts. We spent nine hundred years building a realm on the bones of the old gods, and the only reason it holds together—the *only* reason—is the thing in the throne room keeping every sworn word taut as bowstring." She turned to face him. "Zephyrine calls that slavery. Maybe it is. But I've seen free kingdoms, Corvane. I've walked through what's left of them."

Corvane listened with his head slightly bowed, and Maerin hated how much comfort she took from being heard. Nine years of silence made a feast of any listening ear.

"You believe she will succeed," he said.

"I believe she might. Her gift is stronger than anything the old records describe. When she spoke her blade in the chapel courtyard, I felt it in my teeth from forty paces. The god learned her name, Corvane. Do you understand what that means? It means she can reach it. It means the First Binding answers to her blood." Maerin's voice roughened. "And she means to use it. Within days. Before the thirteenth moon."

"So the choice is upon us." Corvane rose and crossed to the narrow window slit, where grey light fell across his face and made him look carved rather than born. "You know why the Order bound the gods in the first place. Not ambition. Not hunger for power, whatever the histories written by our enemies claim. Mercy. The old gods warred among themselves, and mortals were the field they fought upon. The first Swordspeakers looked at a burning world and chose chains over extinction. Your ancestors chose order over freedom, and they paid for that choice with their names, their graves, their children's children hunted down like dogs." He turned. "We are their heirs, Sister. Not the disbanded rabble who took pensions and married farmers. *Us.* The ones who kept the white. Who kept the purpose."

"The Order is dead," Maerin said. "You keep saying otherwise, but—"

"The Order is a body, and bodies die. The Order's *purpose* is a soul, and souls do not." Corvane stepped closer, and his voice dropped to the register he saved for the deepest truths, the ones that rewrote people. "Think clearly, Sister Voss. If the Eriksen girl unbinds the Veiled One, what remains for us? A realm of ordinary oaths, kept or broken by ordinary men, with no power above them but weather. And what remains for *you*? The last trained swordspeaker of the Sundered Blade, guarding a kingdom that has forgotten blades need voices. You have spent nine years starving, Maerin. Don't tell me you haven't noticed the famine."

"I've noticed," she said flatly.

"Then recognize the shape of the meal set before you." He gestured toward the floor, toward the cells. "The Veiled One endures, and the Order endures with it. Our vigil continues. Our purpose breathes. And you—you return to a brotherhood that remembers what you are, that needs what you are, that will give you back the words you've been choking on for a decade." He smiled thinly. "Or you stand beside a smuggler's pet ghost and watch the world you swore to protect dissolve into chaos, and spend the rest of your life explaining to yourself why you chose a stranger's mercy over a kingdom's survival."

"It isn't that simple."

"No," Corvane agreed. "It's simpler than that. It always is."

* * *

They brought her down to see Zephyrine at dusk.

This was Corvane's design—Maerin understood that even as she descended the spiral stair, lantern in hand, hating herself for understanding and complying anyway. Let the traitor look her victim in the eye. Let the victim's face become the price tag. Corvane believed all conversions were transactions dressed as epiphanies.

The cell was clean, dry, even comfortable, which somehow made it worse. The Order had always been civilized in its cruelties; cruelty was merely administration performed on flesh.

Zephyrine sat cross-legged on the cot, and when the door opened she looked up with an expression of such uncomplicated relief that Maerin had to grip the iron frame of the doorway to keep her knees steady.

"Maerin. Thank the silent gods." Zephyrine rose. Up close, in the lantern light, the decade of hiding showed plainly—the hollows under her eyes, the careful nothingness of her posture, the way she moved like someone perpetually ready to become furniture. "I wasn't sure anyone would come. They took my sword, obviously, and my notes, but I got far enough into the First Binding to see the structure. Listen—the flaw is in the third clause, the one about consideration. The original binding required the crown to be *taken*, because Torvald Eriksen didn't dare ask the first king to surrender it willingly. Fear was the mortar. But fear cuts both ways. If a crown is given freely—"

"Zephyrine."

"—freely, spoken in swordspeech, witnessed by the bloodline, the whole architecture fails. The god can't counterfeit it because counterfeiting requires hunger and a free gift has none. I need to get to the capital. The moon turns in four nights. Is Dax—"

"*Zephyrine.*" Maerin's voice came out wrong, strangled, and Zephyrine finally stopped. Finally *heard* what was underneath the words.

The silence between them stretched long enough for the lantern flame to stutter.

"You're not here to help me," Zephyrine said quietly.

"I'm here because there's something you need to hear before you decide what kind of death you want." Maerin made herself say it plainly. She owed her at least that. "There are people in this citadel who will not kill you. They'll bind you instead—there are rites, older than the disbanding, rites for exactly this contingency. An Eriksen who turns against the Order's purpose. You'd live out your life as a locked archive, your gift warded shut, your bloodline preserved because your bloodline is the lock they can't afford to lose. You'd be safe. Fed. Alive."

Zephyrine stared at her. Then, horribly, she laughed—a short, exhausted sound with no humor in it anywhere.

"That's the offer? Imprisonment with meals?" She shook her head. "Maerin, I hid for ten years. I am *very* good at being locked away. It changes nothing. The moon turns. The flaw exists. Someone will speak the unbinding if I don't."

"No one else can. You know that. The bloodline clause—"

"Then I'll speak it or die trying, and either way your masters' problem remains." Zephyrine stepped closer, and her voice dropped, urgent, pleading. "Maerin. I know what you're afraid of. I've seen it since the pass. You think the world after the god is a wilderness. You think every oath in Thornfell snaps like dry thread. Maybe you're right. Maybe it *is* a wilderness. But listen to me—I touched the throne. I heard it dreaming. Three generations of kings, Maerin, screaming inside their own skulls while something wears their face and eats their people's prayers. Aldric was crowned at *nine years old*. Whatever's left of him has been in that dark since he was a child." Her eyes were wet and furious. "You want to preserve the thing that does that. Because it gives your life meaning. I understand—I do—but meaning bought with a boy's mind is not meaning. It's just a prettier cage."

The words went in like splinters driven deep, and Maerin knew she would carry them for the rest of her life, whatever length that proved to be.

"You didn't hear the border lords," she whispered. "You weren't at Saltmarch when the garrison oaths failed and half the men deserted in a night. You didn't bury the villages that burned because no sworn sword stayed to hold the line. Freedom is beautiful from inside a story, Zephyrine. From outside it's just teeth."

"So you'll hand me to them."

Maerin couldn't answer. Her silence was the answer.

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment—not with hatred, which would have been bearable, but with grief, precise and personal, the grief of someone cataloguing exactly what had been lost and finding it irreplaceable.

"I trusted you," Zephyrine said. "In the pass, when you could have taken me, you chose alliance. I built on that. Four nights ago I would have called you the first honest thing the Order ever gave me." She stepped back, and her voice hardened into something ancient, something that sounded like her ancestors speaking through her throat. "Whatever you tell yourself tonight, Voss, know this: I'm not the one betraying the Order. *It* betrayed itself the day it decided its purpose mattered more than the people it was meant to protect. You're just the hands it's using."

The lantern flickered. Somewhere above them, the citadel bell rang the hour, and Maerin felt the sound move through her chest like a verdict.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and knew it changed nothing.

"So am I," said Zephyrine. "Because when I walk out of here—and I will—you're going to have to choose again. And next time, Maerin, there won't be a middle ground to hide in."

* * *

The rite was scheduled for moonrise. Corvane had been explicit: the binding must be complete before the thirteenth moon, before the girl's gift crested, before she became dangerous rather than merely inconvenient. Wards would be cut into her skin in the old script. Her voice would be folded away behind seven seals. She would remain, breathing and fed and utterly silenced, for as long as the Order endured—which, with the Veiled One preserved, would be forever.

Maerin stood watch outside the ritual chamber because she had volunteered, because refusing would raise questions, and because some part of her—some part she despised—needed to see it done. Needed the finality. Needed the wound cauterized so it would stop bleeding.

The Remnants gathered in their white robes. The old script was mixed in bronze bowls. Two acolytes led Zephyrine in, wrists bound not with rope but with oath-cord woven from the hair of nine generations of sworn blades, and Zephyrine came with her head high and her eyes scanning—counting guards, marking exits, doing the mathematics of escape she had no chance of solving.

Her gaze found Maerin's across the chamber. Held it. Did not plead. Did not curse.

Just held it, the way you hold a mirror up to someone.

Corvane raised his arms and began the opening litany, and the white-robed circle took up the response, and the candles bent in a wind that had no source, and Maerin felt the first seal begin to form in the air above Zephyrine's bowed head—a ring of pale fire, hungry and patient—

—and that was when the bells began.

Not the citadel's hour-bells. The *warning* bells, wild and arrhythmic, and beneath them shouting, and beneath the shouting a sound Maerin had not heard in ten years and knew instantly, bone-deep, the way you know your mother's voice:

A sword, speaking.

Down in the lower galleries, where they had stored the confiscated armory—where they had locked Zephyrine's own blade behind three wards and a door of blessed iron—something had begun to sing. The wards shrieked. The door groaned. And every oath-sworn blade in the chamber rattled in its sheath like a dog straining at a leash, answering a call older than the Order, older than the binding, older than the white robes that suddenly seemed very thin indeed.

Corvane's litany faltered. "Impossible. The wards—"

The door of the ritual chamber exploded inward in a storm of splinters and smoke, and through it came Dax the smuggler—grinning, bleeding, holding a torch in one hand and Zephyrine's singing sword in the other, with half a dozen armed smugglers at his back and madness in his eyes.

"Told her you lot would be terrible hosts," Dax announced. "Nobody listens to me either."

And in the chaos—in the smoke and the screaming and the scattering of white robes—Maerin Voss stood frozen with her hand on her own hilt, hearing her blade whisper her name in a voice she had buried for a decade, feeling the choice she thought she had already made crack open like river ice in spring.

Zephyrine caught the sword Dax threw her. It sang the moment it touched her palm.

She looked at Maerin one last time.

"Now," she said. "Choose."
