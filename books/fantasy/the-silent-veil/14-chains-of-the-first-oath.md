## 14. Chains of the First Oath

The cell beneath the Order's citadel had no door.

That was the first cruelty of it. There was simply an archway, open to any who cared to look in, and beyond it the binding-wards that made looking in feel like drowning. Zephyrine sat against the far wall with her wrists crossed in her lap—not chained, because chains were unnecessary when your own blood was the lock—and watched the light change through a slit window no wider than her hand.

Three days, or what she judged to be three days by the angle of that pale blade of sky. They fed her well. That was the second cruelty: bread still warm from the oven, broth with actual marrow in it, water clear as mountain snowmelt. The Order wanted her healthy. The Order wanted her *whole*. Whatever they meant to do with a swordspeaker whose gift had been heard aloud for the first time in a decade, they could not do it with a broken woman.

Or so she assumed. She was beginning to suspect she assumed too much about them.

She tested the wards twice a day, the way Dax tested a lock—gently, patiently, learning its temper. She pressed one fingertip against the archway's threshold and let the smallest thread of awareness flow through it, the way she'd once quieted rockslides and hushed frightened horses. The wards drank the touch and gave back nothing but cold. Not pain, exactly. More like speaking into a room where every surface was a mouth that swallowed sound.

Her sword lay across her knees. They hadn't taken it.

*They don't need to,* she reminded herself. But she kept it close anyway, the way a drowning woman keeps a plank even when she knows the sea is deeper than any plank is long.

On the fourth day—she thought—the footsteps came.

Not Maerin's. Maerin had not come back since the betrayal, since she'd stood at the top of the stairs with her white cloak immaculate and her eyes fixed on some middle distance where guilt could not reach her, and said only: *"I'm sorry. It's better this way."* Better for whom, Maerin hadn't said, and Zephyrine had shouted after her until her voice cracked on stone.

These footsteps were older. Slower. Deliberate in the way of someone who had walked these corridors for forty years and saw no reason to hurry now.

The woman who stopped at the archway was small and grey as ash, dressed in the plain undyed wool of an Order scribe rather than a hunter's whites. Her hands were stained with ink to the wrist. She carried a wooden case under one arm like a midwife carrying a newborn, and she looked at Zephyrine the way scholars look at a text they have spent their lives trying to read.

"You're the Eriksen girl," she said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody."

"Mm." The old woman lowered herself onto the stool that stood outside the ward-line—it had been there all along, Zephyrine realized; they'd been expecting this visit—and set the case on her lap. "Sister Anthea. First Archivist of the Order, which is a grand title for the last person willing to read the old scripts." She unlatched the case. "Maerin tells me you touched the throne. Heard it dreaming."

Zephyrine said nothing.

"She also tells me you went into the king's sleep and met the boy underneath." Anthea's eyes came up, sharp and black as beetle shells. "You can keep your silence, child. I've read three hundred years of silence. I know every dialect of it. But you should know that I'm not here for the Order."

"No?"

"I'm here because you're the first Eriksen to sit in that cell in four generations, and there is something in my archive that has been waiting for an Eriksen since before I was born." She drew out a scroll case of blackened silver, worked with a sigil Zephyrine knew in her bones before her mind caught up to it—the oath-mark, the same mark burned into the healed child at the shrine village, the same mark she'd seen ghosting over King Aldric's sleeping face.

Her family's mark.

"What is it," Zephyrine said, and hated how much it wasn't a question either.

"The First Binding." Anthea held it up between two fingers, delicate as a relic, which it was. "Written in the hand of Ysolde Eriksen, swordspeaker of the old covenant, eleven days before she bound the entity you call the Veiled One into the body of King Osric the First. Eleven hundred years ago." She smiled without warmth. "Every swordspeaker for a millennium has been taught the liturgy of the slaying—that our order executed gods for their crimes. It is a beautiful story. It is also a lie we told ourselves so we could sleep."

"I know," Zephyrine said quietly. "Maerin told me. You didn't slay them. You bound them."

"We bound one." Anthea's smile died entirely. "The others were already gone. Dead or fled or faded, we don't know—the gods were leaving this world long before men learned to write their names. Only the Veiled One refused to go. And so Ysolde did the only thing left. She wrote it into flesh." The archivist turned the silver case over in her ink-stained fingers. "And then she wrote this. The record of how. And the record of why she believed it could never be undone."

"Could never be—" Zephyrine leaned forward despite herself. "You're saying it can't be undone."

"I'm saying Ysolde believed it couldn't. Beliefs have flaws, child. Even beliefs written in blood." Anthea rose, stool and all, and carried it to the edge of the ward-line. "I am going to pass this through the wards. The moment it crosses, the binding will know it, and know *you*, and it may hurt. Ysolde wrote it keyed to her bloodline deliberately—so that only an Eriksen could ever read the whole of it, and so that reading it would cost. Nothing true comes free. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Good girl. Catch."

She tossed it.

* * *

Catching it was like catching a falling star.

The moment the case touched her palms, the wards woke—and not the cold swallowing silence of the threshold, but a roar, a choir, a thousand voices layered over each other like sediment in stone. Every oath ever sworn inside this citadel. Every vow whispered by every hunter over every whetted blade. Eleven centuries of spoken devotion pressing down on her skull like deep water.

And beneath all of it, threaded through it, one voice. Dry as papyrus. Female. Unhurried.

Ysolde Eriksen, speaking across a thousand years directly into her descendant's blood.

Zephyrine bit through the inside of her cheek and did not scream. She unrolled the scroll across her knees with shaking hands, and the script rose off the vellum in letters of faint gold, and she read.

It began as prayer and ended as engineering.

*I write this in the eleventh hour, when the moon stands thirteenth in the sky and the thing that calls itself the Veiled One has eaten the oaths of three kingdoms and wears the crown of the fourth as a feeding-mask.* The handwriting shifted as she read, growing cramped, hurried, as though the writer felt time running out even then. *My sisters are dead. My brothers are dead. The Order believes I go to die gloriously. I do not intend to die at all. I intend to do something worse.*

Zephyrine's breath caught.

*I have studied it for nine years. It is not a god. Gods are born of worship; this thing is born of agreement. It cannot take what is not given. Every miracle it grants, it grants as payment—and the price is always an oath, freely spoken, freely surrendered. This is its hunger and its law and, praise every silent heaven, its flaw.*

*A freely given oath binds. Therefore—a freely given crown must unbind.*

There it was. Plain as a knife on a table.

Zephyrine read the passage three times, certain she had misunderstood, certain the words would rearrange themselves into despair if she looked away. They did not.

*The parasite sits upon the throne of Osric because Osric swore himself to it—body, voice, and crown—in exchange for victory over the northern host. The binding I have devised does not imprison it. Imprisonment implies walls, and walls imply an outside, and this thing *is* an outside; it is the space between promises. No, my binding makes the throne itself the vessel. As long as the crown remains given—remains *owed*—it holds. The day the crown is freely returned, freely relinquished, spoken back into the god's hands with no debt attached, the vessel empties.*

*But hear me, whoever reads this, whoever carries my blood: no king will ever do it. Understand this. A man who wears a crown believes the crown is his. Even the good ones. Even the kind ones. Possession is the first lie power tells. To give back a throne freely—to stand before the god and say, without coercion, without bargain, without fear, "take it back, it was always yours"—that is a renunciation no crowned head in history has managed. We are not built for it. We are built to hold.*

*So I bind it not for a lifetime but for an age, and I leave this flaw like a door in a wall, and I pray to gods who no longer answer that someday, somewhere, a king will be born who wants nothing.*

*Until then, the Order watches. Until then, we wait.*

*If you are an Eriksen, know this last thing: the binding runs through us. Our blood is the hinge. Our voice is the key. And the god knows it. It has had a thousand years to learn our name.*

*Do not let it speak yours first.*

The gold letters guttered out. The scroll went dark and ordinary in her lap, just vellum and iron-gall ink, and Zephyrine sat in the roaring aftermath of her ancestor's voice and understood, at last, the shape of the trap she had been standing in since the road through Thornfell.

A freely given crown.

She laughed—one short, ugly bark of sound that echoed wrong in the ward-lined cell. Because she had already seen it. In the boy-king's dreamscape, Aldric's caged mind had begged not for rescue but for truth. *Tell me what I am,* he'd said. *Tell me what's real.* He was a prisoner who didn't know he was a prisoner, a king who had never once been asked whether he wanted the crown.

He'd been coronated at nine years old. Had anyone asked him? Had anyone, in twenty years of velvet and ritual and the Veiled One's patient feeding, ever once put the question plainly: *do you want this?*

No. Of course not. Kings aren't asked.

But the binding didn't need a king's refusal. It needed a king's *renunciation*. Freely given. Spoken in swordspeech, Ysolde's notes specified further down the scroll in a section Zephyrine had skimmed past in her shock—spoken in the tongue of the old covenant, witnessed by a bearer of the binding-blood, with no debt, no bargain, no fear shaping the words.

No fear.

Zephyrine looked down at her wrists. At the sword across her knees. At the cell with no door that had no need of one.

The Order wouldn't allow it. She'd seen enough in Maerin's face, in the hunters' careful courtesy, to understand what the remnant wanted: not the god destroyed, but the god *kept*. The Veiled One gave them purpose, gave them prayers to police and miracles to ration and a reason to exist a thousand years after their gods had fled the world. A free kingdom was a kingdom that no longer needed swordspeakers. They would never permit a freely given crown. They would sooner crown the god openly.

Which meant the strategy was simple, and impossible, and had to happen within—she counted on her fingers, counting moons as Sellen the archivist had counted them in the capital's library—within two turns of the moon. Before the thirteenth moon rose and completed the great binding, before every oath in Thornfell curdled into the Veiled One's belly.

Escape the Order's citadel. Reach the capital. Get past the oath-sworn knights who knew her true name now, thanks to her own blade and her own arrogance. Get into the king's dream again—or wake him, or whatever the truth required—and ask a boy who had been a puppet for twenty years to surrender the only thing he had ever been allowed to hold.

And do it all while her blood sang with a binding that made her, she now understood, not the weapon against the god but part of its machinery. The lock. Ysolde had said it herself: *our blood is the hinge.* If the Veiled One took her—if it wore her the way it wore Aldric—it wouldn't need a freely given crown at all. It would hold both sides of the door.

*Do not let it speak your name first.*

Too late. Blade and voice, on the mountain road. It had her now: *Zephyrine Eriksen, last of the binding line.*

She pressed her forehead against the cold of the scroll case and breathed until the panic became arithmetic.

Two turns of the moon. One impossible renunciation. One order of hunters who would kill to prevent it. One god who knew her name and her bloodline and her location.

One smuggler's crew, she remembered, that owed her more than they owed the Order—and a crew leader named Dax who had once picked a lock in Saltmarsh that three guildsmen had declared unpickable, purely because someone told him it couldn't be done.

Her hand found her sword hilt. Beneath her palm, faint as a heartbeat under snow, the steel hummed.

It had sung for her on the mountain. It would sing again. The wards around this cell were woven from oaths—and oaths, her ancestor had proven with a thousand years of terrible patience, were things with seams.

Zephyrine lifted her head. Beyond the archway, the corridor was empty, the ash-grey archivist long gone, the torches burning steady in their brackets.

"Thank you, Grandmother," she murmured to the dark scroll. "You built the cage. Now show me the door."

Somewhere above her, boots began to move—many boots, hurrying, and beneath them a bell she had not heard ring in four days, tolling fast and urgent through the citadel's bones.

Something had changed. Something was coming down those stairs.

And from the direction of the surface, thin as a thread, she heard the first voices of the alarm cry a word that made her blood run colder than the wards ever had:

*"The thirteenth moon—it's early—"*
