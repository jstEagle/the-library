## 15. The Moon's Twelfth Turn

Dax Merrow had smuggled salt past tax collectors, silk past warlords, and once — a night he still didn't like talking about — a live wyvern cub in a fish barrel. He had never smuggled a woman out of the Order's citadel with the sky coming apart behind him.

"Harder!" Zephyrine shouted from the back of the wagon, one arm braced against the tilting crates of salted herring as they careened down the mountain switchbacks.

"You want harder or you want alive?" Dax yelled back over the groan of the axles. "Pick one!"

The wheels hit a rut and the whole cart leapt like a kicked dog. Behind them, the Order's citadel burned — not all of it, just the eastern tower where they'd kept her chained, flames pouring from the windows in a color that wasn't quite orange, that leaned toward violet whenever the wind shifted. Dax had seen enough wrong-colored fire in his years on the roads to know it when he saw it. That fire had been lit by something that wasn't fire at all.

He cracked the reins again. The mules screamed and obeyed, because mules were honest creatures who only wanted two things: forward and away. Dax respected that. He shared the philosophy.

"They'll follow," Zephyrine said. She'd climbed up beside him on the driver's bench, and up close she looked worse than when they'd pulled her out of the cells — thinner, gray around the mouth, hands shaking in a way she was clearly pretending they weren't. Ten years of hiding herself and three days in an Order dungeon had ground something essential out of her. But her eyes were still doing the thing they did, the thing that had made Dax trust her back before he knew what she was: they looked at the road ahead like they were reading it.

"Who's following?" he asked. "The Order? Your white-cloaked friend who sold you?"

"Everyone." She turned and looked back down the valley, and Dax followed her gaze despite every instinct telling him not to look at whatever she was looking at.

Below them, spread along the river road toward the capital, the towns were lighting their evening lamps. Normal. Peaceful. Except for the shrine at Harlow's Bend, where a knot of people knelt in the square, motionless, faces tipped up to a moon that wouldn't be full for another day. And beyond them, at Millhaven, more of them — a whole street's worth, kneeling between the market stalls, carts abandoned mid-lane, a child standing alone in the middle of it all holding a dropped loaf of bread and crying.

Dax felt the hair rise on his arms. "What are they doing?"

"Paying." Zephyrine's voice came out flat and quiet, which was how Dax knew it was bad. Zeph got loud when things were merely dangerous. "Every oath anyone ever swore in this kingdom — to the crown, to each other, to the gods they thought were sleeping — it all runs through the throne now. It's drawing it all in. Every loyalty, every promise. It's drinking."

"It's eating *people's promises*?"

"It's eating the part of people that keeps promises." She finally looked at him, and Dax wished she hadn't. "By tomorrow there won't be a soul in Thornfell who can choose anything. They'll kneel because kneeling is all that's left inside them."

Dax stared down the road at the kneeling towns, at the little boy with the bread, and made a decision he would later describe, in the taverns, as obvious.

"We're going to the city," he said.

Zephyrine blinked. "We're going the opposite way from anywhere safe—"

"I heard myself fine. We're going to the city." He flicked the reins. "You don't leave a crew member's kid standing in the road crying if you can help it. And that goes double for a whole kingdom."

* * *

They made the river ford by midnight, and that was where Dax learned what the Veiled One's hunger looked like up close.

The ferryman's family ran the crossing at Tarnwater — old Bren and his wife and two grown sons, and Dax had known them eleven years, long enough that Bren always charged him half-rate and pretended it was a favor rather than friendship. As the wagon rattled down to the waterline, Dax raised a hand to hail them and stopped.

All four of them stood at the edge of the dock in a perfect line, facing east. Toward the capital. Toward the moon, low and swollen and one turn shy of full, hanging over the black hills like a coin someone had nailed to the sky.

"Bren!" Dax called, climbing down. "Bren, it's Merrow! Wake up, you old river rat, I need a crossing!"

Nothing. The four figures swayed slightly, like reeds, breath moving through them but nothing else. Up close Dax could see their eyes were open, and that was the worst part — open and fixed and shining faintly, a silver film across the iris like the first skin of ice on a pond. Bren's lips moved. No sound came out. His lips moved anyway, shaping words over and over, and Dax leaned in close enough to catch them.

*I serve. I serve. I serve.*

"He's still in there," Zephyrine said quietly. She'd come to stand beside Dax, and she didn't touch the old man, though her hand hovered near his shoulder like she wanted to. "That's the oath he swore at his coronation-day feast, probably, or his wedding, or when he named his sons. The god isn't puppeting his body. It's pulling the thread of the promise itself, and the rest of him is being dragged along after it."

"Can you cut it?" Dax asked. "Your sword-talk thing?"

Her face did something complicated. "My gift is spent. I unbound Aldric's binding-words when I read my ancestor's cipher under the citadel — I paid most of what I had to do it. What's left is barely enough to whisper." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it heavier. "And even at full strength, I couldn't have cut this. These oaths are freely given. That's what makes them food. You can't unsay a man's own yes."

Dax looked at Bren, at the old man's lips moving their silent liturgy, and felt something in his chest go hard and cold. Dax Merrow had built his whole life on a simple creed: nobody owns anybody. Not kings, not creditors, not love, not gods. It was why he smuggled. Every road he ran was a road somebody else had tried to fence.

"Then we get to the city fast," he said, "and we stop it at the source. Where's the source?"

"The throne room. The thirteenth moon rises tomorrow night. When it crests the palace spire, the binding completes, and everything sworn in Thornfell belongs to it forever." She paused. "There might be one loose thread left. Isolde."

"The widow-queen? Thought she was locked up in the west tower playing ghost."

"She's the only person alive whose oath to the crown outranks everyone else's. She swore it as regent, publicly, before ten thousand witnesses. If she renounced it — publicly — it would tear a hole in the web big enough for us to drive a wagon through." Zephyrine almost smiled. "Metaphorically."

"No such thing," Dax said. "Everything's literal if you've got enough mules."

* * *

They crossed at dawn using the ferry flatboat, poled themselves, because Bren and his sons stood on the dock watching them go with those iced-over eyes and made no move to help or hinder. Dax's youngest crewman, Pell, a wiry ex-soldier with a crossbow he talked to like a pet, kept staring back at the shore until the mist took it.

"That could be any of us," Pell said. "Anybody who ever bowed to a lord. Anybody who said 'long live the king' at a festival."

"Anybody who got married," said Sefa, the crew's quartermaster, from beside the cargo. She said it very evenly, the way people say things when they're holding something down. Her wife lived in the capital. Had lived there. Dax watched Sefa check the straps on a crate that didn't need checking and decided not to say anything kind, because kindness right then would crack her open and they needed her whole.

The road east filled up as morning came — refugees, mostly, flowing west away from the capital in ox-carts and on foot, and Dax wove the wagon through them while Zephyrine leaned out and questioned everyone who'd stop. Their stories arrived in fragments and fit together into a picture none of them wanted.

*"It started last night. My husband just — stopped. Mid-sentence. Knelt down in the yard facing east and stayed there till sunrise, and when I shook him he looked at me like I was a stranger wearing his mother's face—"*

*"Half the garrison at Eastgate is kneeling. The other half deserted. You can't give orders to men who can't choose anymore, and the ones who can choose are running—"*

*"The priests are saying it's penance. Saying the gods have come to collect and the faithful should kneel gladly. Father Ondrel knelt first. He knelt first and then he told everyone to."*

At that last one, Zephyrine went very still. "Ondrel," she repeated. "The high priest of the Silent Court?"

"You know him?" Dax asked.

"I know what he is." Her voice had gone quiet and terrible. "I found his name in the Order's records. He was one of the first. A swordspeaker who turned coat three generations ago — who helped the Veiled One take the first king instead of fighting it. He's been feeding it court oaths ever since, dressed up as liturgy." She looked east, toward the towers of the capital, visible now as a gray suggestion on the horizon beneath that too-large moon. "It's not just consuming. It's organizing. It's building a clergy."

"A god with a HR department," Dax muttered.

"A god with *banners*." Zephyrine pointed. Ahead, where the refugee road bent around a hillside shrine, a dozen riders in white-and-silver sat their horses in perfect formation — the Order's remnant colors, except the silver had been changed to a pale, moonlit gray, and where the crossed swords used to be, the new sigil showed a veiled face. "Maerin's order. Or what Maerin's order became the moment it realized which side fed it better."

Pell's crossbow came up. "Want me to—"

"No shooting," Dax snapped. "Look at their horses' tack. Look at their faces."

Up close, as they rolled carefully past the mounted knights, Dax saw it: the same ice-film eyes. The Order hadn't chosen anything. The Order had sworn oaths too — oaths to preserve the Veiled One, oaths sworn decades ago in cellars by frightened men who thought they were protecting the world. The god had simply come to collect, and the hunters had become the hunted, and now they patrolled the roads in perfect silent ranks, guarding a prison they no longer understood they were also inside.

One knight turned his head as the wagon passed. His lips moved. *I serve.* Nothing else.

Nobody breathed until the bend took them out of sight.

* * *

They reached the capital's outer wall at dusk, and Dax understood, looking at it, why the histories called Thornfell's seat the Silent Court. The walls rose white limestone gone honey-gray with age, crowned with banners — and every banner hung dead still. No wind touched them. No wind had touched them since noon, Zephyrine said; the air for a mile around the palace moved only when the god permitted it, drawn inward, slow and constant, like a lung.

The gates were open.

That was the detail that turned Dax's blood to river-water. The great western gate, wide enough for six wagons, stood open and unguarded, torches burning in their brackets with flames that leaned steadily inward toward the city's heart. Beyond it, the grand avenue climbed toward the palace hill, lined with lanterns and statues and ten thousand people —

— kneeling.

Not all of them. That was the horror of it, the precision of it. The avenue was a tapestry of the kneeling and the walking both: families pressing east against the current of deserters fleeing west, and scattered among them, every twenty paces or so, a citizen on their knees on the cobbles, facing the palace, eyes filmed silver, lips shaping their silent syllables. A baker in his apron. Two city guardsmen, spears laid down neatly beside them, kneeling shoulder to shoulder. An old woman who had clearly been on her way somewhere with a basket of onions, spilled and rolling in the gutter, her hands folded in her lap like a woman at prayer, except her face was empty as a shut house.

A young man stumbled out of a doorway near the gate, wild-eyed, alive, free — and Dax watched him grab a stranger's sleeve and scream, *"Don't swear anything! Don't bow, don't promise, don't say yes to ANYONE—"*

The stranger nodded, terrified, agreeing — and Dax saw it happen. Saw the moment the nod curdled. The stranger's knees folded. His face went smooth. He turned, gently, like a leaf finding water, and knelt down next to a lamppost, and began to speak his silent word to the palace on the hill.

"Oh no," Pell whispered. "Oh no, no, no—"

"Agreement counts," Zephyrine said. Her voice was ice over deep water. "Consent counts. Even consent given in panic. Especially then." She gripped the bench rail. "Dax. The west tower. Isolde. If she kneels — if anyone gets her to agree to anything tonight — the realm's last unbroken oath snaps, and there's no hole in the web for us to use. There's no web left to tear. We lose everything before the moon even crests."

"How do we get to a locked tower in a city that's turning into a congregation?"

Zephyrine was already digging through the false bottom of the salt crate, pulling out the Order robes they'd stolen during the escape — the old colors, crossed swords, not the new veiled face. She threw one to Dax, one to Pell.

"Badly," she said. "We go in as prisoners being escorted. Nobody questions guards dragging a hooded prisoner toward the towers — fear makes people polite. And the kneeling can't report us." She pulled her hood up, and in its shadow her face was all sharp planes and banked fury. "Sefa stays with the wagon and the mules. If we're not back by moonrise—"

"I'm coming with you," Sefa said instantly.

"Someone has to hold the escape route, and you're the best liar among us, which means you're the best at talking your way past whatever's left of the gate watch—"

"Sefa," Dax said quietly. "She's in there. Your Marla. Third street off the avenue, the chandler's row." He held his friend's gaze. "If the web tears — if the queen does what Zeph thinks she might — everyone kneeling gets let go. Everyone. Including her. But only if we get to Isolde first."

Sefa's jaw worked. Tears stood in her eyes and she refused, absolutely refused, to let them fall. Finally she nodded once, sharp as a blade being sheathed, and turned to the mule team.

Dax shrugged into the stolen robe, took up the wagon whip as a prop, and looked east up the avenue at the palace rising against the swollen moon — one turn from full, hanging so low and heavy now it seemed to lean on the spires, seeming to grow as he watched, drinking the light of the whole sky.

Somewhere up there, a boy who had been king since he was nine years old was trapped inside his own crowned head, and a widow-queen who had chosen silence for twenty years was about to learn whether silence could be unsaid.

"Walk slow," Zephyrine murmured. "Kneeling people make crowds nervous, but nervous crowds make mistakes. We're just guards. We're just tired. We belong here."

They stepped through the open gate into the silent city, and above the palace hill, behind the veil of the dying day, the twelfth moon began, slowly, to climb.
