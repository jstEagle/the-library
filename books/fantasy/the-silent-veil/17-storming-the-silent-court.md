## 17. Storming the Silent Court

Maerin Voss had sworn her last oath at dawn, and it was to no one but herself.

She stood in the shadow of the palace's western gate, where the Order's white cloaks had gathered like frost on stone—nine of them, remnant of a brotherhood that had once numbered in the hundreds, and every one of them waiting for her signal. Behind them, the city of Thornfell knelt in the streets. Not praying. Just kneeling, thousands upon thousands, faces slack with a peace that had been poured into them like wine into a cup. The Veiled One was drinking the realm dry one oath at a time, and the moon above was already swelling past its twelfth turn, a silver wound in the sky.

*Hold the gate,* Zephyrine Eriksen had said. *That's all I ask. Hold it until we're inside.*

Maerin had spent twenty years learning to hear what oaths cost. She could taste this one on her own tongue: bitter as ash, heavy as a coffin lid. But she had given it anyway, because there was no one left to give it to who mattered more.

"Voss." Brother Cassian's voice, low and certain, at her shoulder. He wore the Order's old sigil over his heart—the crossed blade and open mouth—and he wore it like a man wearing a flag over a wound. "The breach teams are ready. When the thirteenth moon crests the spire, we go in. We cut the girl down before she can unmake what our founders built."

"What they built," Maerin said quietly, "was a cage. And we've been feeding the thing inside it for three generations."

Cassian's face did not change. That was the horror of him, she thought. She had trained beside him for fifteen years, bled beside him, buried students beside him—and his face did not change. "The Veiled One is the last living covenant between this world and the divine. Without it, we are nothing. The kingdom is nothing. You would trade the soul of Thornfell for the pride of one traitor?"

"The kingdom's soul is being drunk right now, Cassian. Look at the streets." She nodded toward the boulevard below, where a fruit seller had dropped her basket and knelt mid-stride, oranges rolling into gutters, her eyes fixed on nothing with terrible contentment. A child knelt beside her, copying her mother's posture the way children copy everything, not understanding. "You call that covenant. I call it a tapeworm."

For a moment—one long, dangerous moment—Cassian said nothing. Then: "Your doubts were noted in the citadel. That's why you lead the rear guard, Voss. Not the vanguard." His hand settled briefly, almost gently, on her shoulder. "When this is done, you'll be given the chance to confess. The Order forgives its own. It always has."

He walked away to inspect the breach ladders, and Maerin stood alone with her heartbeat and her lie, which had grown so large inside her that she could feel it pressing against her ribs.

The lie was this: there was no rear-guard plan. There never had been. The message she'd carried to Zephyrine through Dax's smugglers—*western gate, third bell, I'll hold it open*—had been written in her own hand, sealed with her own wax, and if the Order discovered it before the third bell, she would die here among people who had once been her family, killed by the family she had chosen instead.

She found she could not decide which loss frightened her more. That, too, was an answer of sorts.

* * *

The third bell came wrapped in fog off the river.

Zephyrine came out of it first—a shape resolving from gray, sword already drawn, flanked by Dax's smugglers moving with the loose-hipped silence of people who had spent their lives not being where guards looked. Maerin counted them: eleven, plus the big one, Brannic, carrying a sapper's charge meant for the chapel doors. And behind them, half-hidden in a hooded cloak that did nothing to disguise the set of her shoulders, the widow-queen Isolde walked like a woman attending her own funeral and finding the eulogy satisfactory.

"You came," Maerin breathed.

"You sound surprised." Zephyrine's eyes were the color of old iron, and there was something new in them—or rather, something newly missing. Maerin had seen swordspeakers look at their blades like lovers. Zephyrine looked at hers like a debt nearly paid. "Is the gate yours?"

"For now. Cassian has nine on the inner wall and thirty more coming up from the citadel barracks by the fourth bell. After that—" She drew a breath. "After that I can't promise anything, including my own conduct. So don't stand near me when it breaks."

Dax snorted softly. "Comforting woman."

"I'm not paid to comfort."

"You're not paid at all," Dax said. "Which is why I trust you more than anyone I've ever met."

There was no time for more than that. Maerin gave them the gatehouse rotation, the blind spot beneath the arrow slit, the count of heartbeats between watch turns. And then, because she could not help herself—because some part of her still belonged to the Order even now, even tonight—she said: "The throne room will be guarded by the Hollow Knights. Oath-sworn, all of them. They won't tire, won't fear, won't stop while the god feeds them. Don't fight them fairly. There's no such thing against men who've sold their wills."

"And you?" Zephyrine asked. Her gaze went to the white cloaks massing on the inner wall, then back. "What have you sold, Maerin?"

"Myself," Maerin said. "A long time ago. Tonight I'm buying it back." She pulled her cloak tight. "Go. Third bell is ringing out. I'll hold the gate."

Zephyrine held her eyes for one moment longer—an exchange without words, though Maerin felt it pass between them like a drawn blade offered hilt-first. Then the smugglers melted into the fog, and the queen followed them, and Maerin Voss turned to face her brothers and sisters of the Order with murder in her heart and duty on her tongue.

"Cassian!" she called, pitching her voice to carry. "Movement at the river stairs. Smugglers—I counted eight. Coming for the postern."

It was a lie within a lie, a false report aimed true. Cassian took the bait exactly as she'd known he would, taking six of the nine down toward the water, leaving the gate thin. Thin enough.

She just hadn't expected how thin she herself would feel, standing in the gap she'd made.

* * *

The alarm came at the half-bell, sooner than she'd hoped—someone on the wall had seen the real breach, the one at the chapel side, where Zephyrine's team had gone over the roofs like ghosts. Bells began to scream across the palace grounds, and Maerin watched Cassian stop dead at the foot of the river stairs, turn slowly, and look up at the gatehouse where she stood.

Even at forty paces, through fog and torchlight, she saw him understand.

"VOSS!" His voice cracked across the courtyard like a whip. "The gate! She bought the gate!"

Maerin did not run. Running was for people with somewhere to hide. She walked down the stair with her hand on her sword and her heart going like a war drum, and when she reached the courtyard, the seven remaining brothers and sisters of the Swordspeaker Order had formed their crescent around her, blades bare, faces lit orange by torches that made all of them look like figures out of an old, bad painting.

"Sister," Cassian said, stepping forward. He sounded genuinely grieved, which was somehow worse than anger. "Tell me it isn't true. Tell me you didn't sell the Order's last purpose for the promises of a traitor's bloodline."

"The Eriksens weren't traitors." Her voice surprised her with its steadiness. "They were the only ones honest enough to stop. We bound gods into kings, Cassian, and called it holiness. Three generations of boys have screamed inside their own skulls so the Order could feel important. I read the first binding. I know what we are."

"We are the shield between this world and the dark."

"We're the lock on the door," she said, "and the thing behind it taught us to love the sound of the key."

He sighed, and it was the saddest sound she'd heard in years. "Then kneel and confess, sister. Or stand and be struck down. Those are the only two roads left to a Voss."

"I stopped being a Voss the day I chose the truth over the Order." She drew her sword. The steel sang faintly—not swordspeech, nothing so grand, just good metal remembering it had a purpose. "I choose the truth."

What followed was not a battle. Battles have movement, rhythm, space to breathe. This was a slaughter conducted in both directions, seven against one in a courtyard barely wide enough for the crescent to swing. Maerin fought the way the Order had trained her to fight—which was to say she fought like someone who expected to die and found the expectation liberating. She broke Brother Hale's guard and put him down hard, ribs cracking under her pommel. Sister Anwen's blade opened her arm from elbow to wrist, and Maerin felt the hot sheeting of her own blood and laughed, actually laughed, because the pain was simple and clean compared to twenty years of complicity.

They were better than her, most of them. It didn't matter. She only had to hold the gate until the fourth bell, and the fourth bell was ringing now, great bronze voices tumbling over each other across the city, and somewhere beyond the chapel walls Zephyrine Eriksen was running toward a boy in a crown, and every second Maerin stayed upright was a second bought with the only coin she had left.

Cassian cut her leg out from under her in the seventh minute. She went down on one knee in her own blood, and the crescent closed.

"Last chance," he said, standing over her, torchlight shivering along his edge. "Kneel properly, at least. Die facing the Order."

"I've been kneeling to the Order my whole life." She looked up at him and found she was smiling. "I'd rather die standing."

She got halfway up. Cassian's blade came down—

—and stopped, deflected, by a thrown hatchet that buried itself in his forearm with a sound like an axe in wet wood. Maerin knew that throw. She knew the follow-up boot that put Brother Tamms into the fountain, and the knife work that opened the crescent's flank, and the voice cursing fluently in three dockside dialects as Dax's smugglers poured through the gate she'd held.

"No," Maerin gasped, hauling herself up on her sword. "No, you idiots, the palace—you're supposed to be at the palace—"

"Palace is handled," Dax grunted, catching a blade on her bracer and headbutting its owner into oblivion. "Queen's orders. Said, quote, 'no one dies alone holding a door for me.' End quote." She spat. "Bloody royalty. Always making things complicated."

The fight went out of the Order all at once, the way fights do when the arithmetic changes. Cassian knelt cradling his ruined arm, staring at the smugglers, at the kneeling city visible through the open gate, at Maerin swaying in a lake of her own blood.

"You've killed us," he whispered. "Don't you see? If the god falls, the Order falls. Everything we were—"

"Then be something new," Maerin said. Her voice was going thin; she could hear it fraying. "It's what I'm trying."

Sister Anwen lowered her sword first. Then Brother Hale, groaning, sat up and did not reach for his weapon. One by one, the white cloaks of the Swordspeaker Order looked at the thirteenth moon cresting black-rimmed over the palace spires, and Maerin watched something break in their faces—not defeat, exactly, but the terrible beginning of doubt, which is the first stage of every rebirth.

Cassian was the last. He stared at her for a long time, blood dripping from his arm onto the stones, and finally said, very quietly: "You always were the best of us, Voss. That was the problem."

Then he knelt—not to her, not to the Order, just knelt, like the city outside, empty-handed under the swollen moon.

Maerin tried to say something worthy of the moment. What came out was mostly blood. Dax caught her before the ground did.

"Hey. Hey. Eyes open, gatekeeper." The smuggler's voice was rough, and underneath the roughness, afraid. "You did it. Gate's held. Now hold something else."

"Holding," Maerin managed. The world was tilting. Through the open gates she could see the palace rising against the moon, and high in the tallest tower, a light had begun to burn that was not any fire she knew—white and vast and hungry, the color of an oath being kept or broken.

"That'll be her," Dax said, following her gaze. "That'll be your Zephyrine."

"She's not mine," Maerin whispered. "None of us are anybody's anymore. That's the whole point."

The light in the tower flickered—dimmed—flared again, and with it came a sound that rolled across the city like the first word spoken after a long silence: a voice in two parts, one young and human and terrified, one ancient and vast and *losing*, tangled together in the air above the Silent Court.

And Maerin, bleeding out in a smuggler's arms beneath the thirteenth moon, understood that whatever happened next, the age of gods wearing crowns was ending tonight.

"Stay awake," Dax begged her. "Stay awake and see it."

Maerin Voss, executioner, huntress, traitor twice over, fixed her fading eyes on that impossible light and held on—as she had held the gate, as she had held the truth—as if letting go were simply no longer among her options.
