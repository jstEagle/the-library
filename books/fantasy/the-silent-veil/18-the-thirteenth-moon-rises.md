## 18. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The doors of the throne room stood open.

That was the first wrongness. Zephyrine had fought her way through six halls of kneeling courtiers and oath-drained knights to reach this place, expecting siege doors, expecting the Veiled One to bar its sanctum like any cornered animal. Instead the great bronze leaves yawned wide as a host's welcome, and candlelight poured out across the marble like spilled honey.

Maerin leaned against the doorframe, one arm pressed to a gash along her ribs that had soaked through the white of her Order coat. Her sword was still drawn. Behind her, down the corridor, the bodies of three remnant knights lay in attitudes of sudden apology.

"I held the gate," Maerin said. Her voice was scraped raw. "I don't— I don't expect that counts for anything."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. The betrayal in the Order's citadel still lived between them like a blade neither had finished pulling out. But the thirteenth moon was climbing past the eastern towers now, swollen and silvered and wrong—a moon with no name in any almanac, hanging where no moon should be—and there was no time left for debts.

"It counts," Zephyrine said. "Stay here. Don't follow me in unless I scream."

"And if you don't come out?"

"Then it doesn't matter what anyone does."

She stepped through the doors alone.

* * *

The throne room of Thornfell had been built to awe: vaulted ceiling painted with the ascension of the Nine, pillars carved into a forest of petitioning saints, windows of colored glass that turned ordinary daylight into scripture. Tonight there was no daylight. The thirteenth moon filled every window, and its light came through the glass colorless, bleaching the saints to bone, so that the whole chamber seemed to be underwater at the bottom of some drowned cathedral.

And on the dais, beneath a canopy of black silk that had not been there yesterday, sat King Aldric.

He sat very straight, hands folded on his knees, wearing the crown of his grandfather's grandfather as if it weighed nothing. His face was composed, courteous, terrible. When he saw Zephyrine cross the threshold, he smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile reached Aldric's eyes, and it was the eyes that told her nothing behind them was home.

"Zephyrine Eriksen." The voice came doubled, as she'd heard it in the court—the king's tenor and beneath it something vast and patient, the sound a glacier makes when it decides to move. "Daughter of the Eriksen line. Speaker to swords. Last of the executioners." He rose from the throne, unhurried, gracious as a host greeting an expected guest. "You've made such an effort to arrive. Through my knights, through my court, through your own sisterhood's knives. I would have sent a carriage."

"You know what I've come to do."

"I know what you *believe* you've come to do." He descended the dais steps, one at a time, the crown catching moonlight. "Belief is such a heavy thing to carry up all those stairs. Set it down. Let us talk, you and I, as the last two old things left in this kingdom."

Zephyrine did not draw her sword. She had learned, in the long decade of erasing herself, that the moment you reached for a weapon was the moment you admitted you had nothing else. She walked forward instead, until ten paces separated them, close enough to see the fine tremor in the king's left hand that did not belong to the god at all.

"Aldric," she said quietly. To the boy beneath. A stone dropped down a well.

The tremor stopped. For half a heartbeat the king's face flickered—younger, lost, mouth opening around a sound that never escaped—and then the composure closed over it like ice over water.

"He can't hear you," the Veiled One said gently. "I keep him very safe. That is what I have done for thirty years, Zephyrine. Kept them all safe. Your order bound me into a mortal crown because they could not destroy me, and I have honored the bargain better than any of my jailers ever honored theirs. Three kings have worn me, and under my keeping Thornfell has known no famine, no civil war, no plague worth the name. I answer prayers. I keep oaths. I am the most honest thing in this kingdom—I have never once broken my word."

"No," Zephyrine said. "You just eat everyone who gives you theirs."

"Eat." The god tilted its borrowed head, considering the word as though tasting it. "A farmer eats his field and calls it harvest. A mother feeds her child from her own body and calls it love. I take what is freely given—that is the law written into my binding, older than your ancestors' script, and you know it. Every oath in this realm has been *offered*. Kneeling looks like kneeling from the outside. From inside, it is rest."

* * *

It stopped five paces away. Close enough now that Zephyrine could smell the throne room's true perfume beneath the candlewax—old iron, and something sweeter, like fruit going soft.

"Let me show you what I brought you," the Veiled One said.

It raised Aldric's hand, palm up, and the moonlight gathered in it—not reflected, but *gathered*, drawn through the glass windows in threads—and in the cup of that hand images formed the way frost forms on glass.

Zephyrine saw a hall of banners. The hall of her childhood, before the purge: the Swordspeaker Order in its full glory, two hundred blades sworn to the old gods, and at their head her father in his grey mantle, and beside him a girl of nine years with her hand already resting on the flat of her first sword, learning to hear it sing.

"I know what was taken from you," the god said. "Your name struck from every ledger. Your family's honor burned into ash by frightened men. Your gift buried so deep you flinch even now when a blade speaks in your dreams. Ten years, Zephyrine. Ten years of being no one." The image changed: the same hall, restored, banners mended, and the girl grown, standing where her father had stood, and every face below the dais turned toward her with something that had been absent from her life since childhood—recognition. Belonging. *Home.*

"I can give it back," said the Veiled One. "All of it. The name, the hall, the honor. Not a counterfeit—look closely, you who read oaths the way priests read scripture. I cannot lie about what I offer; my binding forbids it. Kneel, and swear yourself to my keeping, and by tomorrow's dusk the Eriksen name will stand in Thornfell brighter than it ever stood before. The Order will be re-founded with you at its head. Every sword in this kingdom will know your voice."

The images held. They were perfect. That was the poison of them—they were not lies, they were truths arranged like a snare, and Zephyrine felt the hook of them sink past her reason into the raw place underneath, the place where a nine-year-old girl still waited in a burned hall for someone to say *you are ours, you belong, come home.*

Her knees ached to bend. It was the strangest thing—to feel her own body leaning toward treachery, gravity itself complicit.

"That's what you offered Isolde," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it. "Peace for silence. And she gave you thirty years and it bought her a son who isn't her son."

"Isolde chose poorly. She chose fear." The god let the images gutter out. In the dark of its palm the moonlight kept pooling, silver and hungry. "You needn't choose fear. You above all people know what the world is like without gods to answer it. Silent heavens. Prayers falling like snow on stone. Your whole life has been lived in that silence, Zephyrine, and look what it made you—a smuggler's ghost, a woman who signs other people's names. With me, you will never be silent again. Neither will the sky. The thirteenth moon rises tonight, and when it sets, the binding completes, and the realm's every oath flows to me forever—and I will spend them *well*. Better than the Nine ever spent theirs, hoarding miracles while children died of fevers. Ask the village in the pass. Ask the child I healed. Ask anyone whose prayer was answered this season whether the price was too high."

"The child was marked." Zephyrine's hand finally found her sword hilt—not drawing, only holding, the way you hold a friend's wrist. "Your sigil, burned into a baby's skin. I read it. It wasn't a blessing, it was a *claim*. You don't answer prayers. You write contracts in flesh and call the ink mercy."

"And your ancestors wrote contracts in gods." The doubled voice hardened, the glacier grinding. "Do not preach to me about claims, daughter of binders. Your bloodline forged the first chain ever laid on my kind. Your family made me *this*—" the hand gestured at itself, at the crown, at the prison of kingflesh, "—and then fled into anonymity when the bill came due. Everything that has befallen this kingdom, every oath devoured, every generation hollowed, began with an Eriksen hammer striking an Eriksen chain. So consider, before you refuse me: perhaps this is not temptation. Perhaps it is restitution."

* * *

There it was. The second hook, sunk deeper than the first, and Zephyrine felt it twist.

Because it was true. She had read the original binding in the citadel's depths—her ancestor's hand, elegant and ruthless, the First Oath written in swordspeech across a god's living skin. *We bind you to serve the realm.* She had told herself the flaw she found in those lines was her weapon. She had not let herself feel, until this moment, standing in the god's own light, that it was also her inheritance. Guilt passed down like an heirloom. The lock and the blood that forged it.

"You want me to kneel," Zephyrine said slowly, "because a kneeling Eriksen completes it. Don't you. The binding needs a speaker of the line on both ends. My ancestor chained you; my knee closes the circuit. My family's honor isn't the prize—it's the *keyhole*."

For the first time, the composure in the king's face moved. Not a flicker this time—something larger, something almost like respect, surfacing through the god's control like a whale through black water.

"Yes," the Veiled One said simply. "Three generations I have worn these kings, and never once could I finish the work, because the last speakers hid too well and died too quiet. Then you came. You came *to me*, Zephyrine. Do you understand how rare that is? Everyone else in this kingdom spends their lives trying to get something from the throne. Only you came to give."

"Only because you started answering prayers. You rang the bell. I followed the sound."

"I rang the bell because I was starving." And there—beneath the grandeur, beneath the glacier voice—something cracked open, and what came through it was not majesty but hunger, vast and cold and terribly old, the hunger of a thing that had been chewing on the same small kingdom for ninety years and was still empty. "Do you know what it is to feed on oaths? Each one a grain. Each grain less than the last, as your kind grows faithless and forgetful. Ninety years, and I am *thin*, Zephyrine. The thirteenth moon comes once an age, and when it wanes I go back to the dark between the words with a kingdom's worth of crumbs in my belly. I do not want to devour you. I want to be *full*. Is that so monstrous?"

Behind the king's eyes, something screamed.

Zephyrine heard it the way she heard swords—not with her ears, but in the marrow-deep register where her gift had always lived. It was thin and wordless and young, a boy's voice worn down to a single note, and it came from so far beneath the surface of that composed face that she understood with sickening clarity how deep the drowning went. Thirty years. Crowned at seven. He had been screaming down there since he was seven years old, and the god wore his throat to smile with.

*Aldric.* She pushed the thought down the well again, harder this time, a shout rather than a stone. *Hold on. I'm coming down.*

The scream stuttered. Changed. Found, for one impossible instant, something like a word—

And the Veiled One closed its hand, and the connection snapped like cut rope, and the king's face smoothed over, and the smile returned, colder now, the courtesy of a thing done pretending.

"You should not speak to him," the god said. "It is unkind. He hears you and hopes, and hope in the vessel is like water in oil—it makes everything burn unpredictably." It stepped back, up the first dais step, and spread Aldric's arms wide, an invitation, a benediction, a trap with its jaws politely open. "So. We are at the last turn of it, you and I. The moon is nearly at zenith. On one side: your name restored, your family's honor returned, your gift unburied, a kingdom of answered prayers, and an end to hunger—yours and mine alike. All of it real. All of it freely offered, as my binding requires. On the other side: silence, exile, a boy who may or may not survive what happens next, and a realm of ordinary oaths that no power will ever keep again." The doubled voice softened to something almost tender. "Kneel, Zephyrine Eriksen. You have been kneeling your whole life—to fear, to necessity, to the memory of fire. Kneel to me instead, and rise honored."

The moonlight stood in the windows like a wall of silver water.

Zephyrine drew her sword.

Not fast—slowly, deliberately, the whisper of steel loud in the vast hush, and she held it point-down before her the way her father had taught her to hold it in the old rites: not a weapon's grip but a speaker's, the blade a throat waiting for a voice.

"You're right about one thing," she said. "My family wrote the First Oath. Which means my family also wrote the way it ends." She let her gift rise for the second time in a decade, letting the god hear it fully this time, letting it taste what it thought it was buying. "You want my knee. You want my oath. Freely given—that's your law, the law older than my ancestors' chain. So here is mine, freely given, spoken true."

She lifted the blade until it caught the thirteenth moon, and the whole throne room seemed to lean in around the line of light, and somewhere far beneath the king's face, a boy stopped screaming to listen.

"But I choose *which* oath," said Zephyrine, "and I choose *who* receives it—and the god learned, in that final silver instant before she spoke, that it had invited the one voice in Thornfell that could unmake it to make a wish."
