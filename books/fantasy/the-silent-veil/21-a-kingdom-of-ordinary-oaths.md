## 21. A Kingdom of Ordinary Oaths

The question hung in the air between them longer than any answer could have.

Zephyrine didn't speak at once. She stood in the wrecked throne room with the thirteenth moon's last light pooling around her boots like spilled water, and Aldric watched her decide—not quickly, not carelessly, but the way she seemed to do everything, as though every motion were a word being chosen for a sentence that had to be exactly right.

"No," she said.

He had known she would say it. He had known since the dreamscape, since the boy who had been him for ten years pressed his face against the inside of his own skull and asked her only for truth. And still the word went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor—clean, expected, devastating.

"May I ask why?" His voice came out smaller than he intended. Three days king again, and already he was learning how much of kingship was performance. "Not to argue. I just—" He laughed, a dry, cracked sound. "I've never actually heard someone refuse me before. The Veiled One made sure no one could."

"That's exactly why." She looked at him then, fully, and there was something in her gaze he had never seen directed at a king: plainness. No awe, no fear, no calculation. Just one person regarding another. "If I stay, they'll say you kept an executioner's blade at your side because you couldn't rule without one. They'll be right, some of them. And I'd let you." Her hand drifted to the sword at her hip—an old gesture, unconscious—and stopped halfway. "I spent ten years learning what it is to be needed by something that eats oaths. I'm not going to become the thing you need instead."

"The god is gone," he said.

"The *wanting* isn't." For a moment her composure flickered, and beneath it he saw the woman from the shrine village, the one who had touched a dying child's brow and read her own family's crime written there in silver. "You're twenty-three years old, Aldric. You've been a prisoner longer than you've been anything else. You don't know yet who you are when no one is holding your leash or offering to. Neither do I. We'd be building on the same broken ground."

"And if I ordered you?" It came out before he could stop it, and he hated himself instantly—but also, in some buried honest place, he didn't. That was the terrible new freedom of it. The Veiled One had never permitted him cruelty or pettiness; those impulses had been locked away with everything else. Now they were his, along with everything else that was his.

Zephyrine smiled. It transformed her face, and it was not entirely kind.

"You won't," she said. "And if you did, I'd walk out anyway, and you'd learn more from watching yourself fail to stop me than you would from my staying. That's the first lesson of ruling without a god in your mouth, Your Majesty: nobody's loyalty is real unless its refusal is possible."

*Your Majesty.* She said it like a joke they were both in on. He found, to his surprise, that he could bear it.

Dawn found him where it always found him now: in the small council chamber, listening to men and women tell him things he did not want to hear.

Chancellor Berrow had served three generations of kings. He was old enough to have knelt before Aldric's grandfather, and his hands trembled slightly as he laid out the ledger of the realm's wounds. "The northern lords are calling it the Kneeling Sickness," he said. "Half of Harrowmere spent two days on their knees in the streets while the thing fed. Some of them can't remember their own marriage vows. There's a man in the Tanner's Quarter who swore himself to a neighbor's dog during the consumption—he remembers doing it and doesn't know why. The temples are full. The temples are *empty*. Both at once. People pray and nothing answers, and half of them are grateful and half are furious, and both halves blame the crown."

"Let them," Aldric said.

Berrow blinked. "Sire?"

"Blame the crown. Blame me. It's accurate enough—I sat under it for ten years and felt nothing, said nothing, noticed nothing worth noticing." He spread his hands across the table, palms down, steadying himself against the grain of the wood. Real wood. Real table. His hands, which obeyed him now, all of him obeying him, and the weight of that obedience heavier than any possession had ever been. "My grandfather ruled with miracles. My father ruled with miracles. Every harvest blessing, every coronation, every wedding vow spoken in the royal chapel carried the god's signature underneath, and everyone knew it even if no one said it. The realm's faith wasn't in the crown, Chancellor. It was in the *thing wearing* the crown. Now they get me." He looked up. "A young man with no divine sanction, no prophecy, no voice of two tones. Just a title and a great deal of guilt."

Berrow was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—the courtier's polish thinning to show something older and plainer beneath. "My wife died in the winter of the Red Fever. I prayed every night in the royal chapel. Nothing answered." His trembling hands went still on the ledger. "I used to think the silence meant I hadn't prayed hard enough. Or that the god was testing me. Or that my faith was insufficient and her death was somehow the price of it." He met Aldric's eyes. "Now I know the truth was simpler and worse. Something was eating prayers like a man eats olives. Pitting them out when it finished." He straightened. "So. A young man with no divine sanction. I've had worse masters, sire. At least this one knows he owes us."

After the chancellor left, Aldric sat alone for a long time, watching dust turn in the light through the high windows.

That was the shape of it, then. That was the whole of the kingdom he had inherited: a country full of people who had prayed into a hungry mouth and been answered with theft, now looking at their king and asking—with fury, with hope, with the particular bitterness of the defrauded—*what are you, if not that?*

He did not know. That was the honest answer, and he was done lying, even to himself. He was a boy who had gone into a chapel at thirteen to be crowned and woken, ten years later, in the ruins of a binding, aged past his years by a decade lived secondhand. He remembered his mother's funeral through another mind's attention. He remembered falling in love with a foreign ambassador's daughter through a parasite's bored observation, remembered the girl leaving court because the prince never looked at anyone, really looked, and understanding only now why. Ten years of a life watched rather than lived. Griefs he had witnessed but never grieved. Joys he had catalogued but never felt.

The court called him grave beyond his years. They mistook the damage for wisdom. Perhaps, in time, he could make it true.

She was in the training yard on the third morning, and he almost didn't recognize her without the weight of purpose bending her shoulders.

She stood among the palace guard's practice dummies with her sleeves rolled, correcting a sergeant's grip on a blade the way she must have corrected a hundred soldiers in another life, before the order fell, before the hiding began. The guardsmen clustered around her with the wary hunger of men who had heard rumors—*the Swordspeaker, the one who freed the king*—and didn't dare ask.

Aldric stood in the shadow of the archway and watched her work.

This, he thought, was what he had never had. Not the power—though gods knew he'd dreamed of it, in the dreamscape, in the long gray prison of his own head—but the *certainty of the body*. Zephyrine moved like a sentence that knew its own meaning. Even stripped of her gift, even walking away from the hollow where the swordspeech had lived in her, she was wholly present in her skin in a way he might never manage. He had spent ten years as a passenger. She had spent ten years erasing herself, and somehow both of them arrived here unfinished.

But she had chosen her unfinishedness. That was the difference, and it mattered, and he was beginning to understand that it might matter more than anything else in the world.

"Sire." Captain Ondrey materialized at his shoulder, armor creaking. "Shall I clear the yard?"

"No." He stepped into the sunlight. Conversations died around the perimeter; guardsmen bowed in ragged sequence, uncertain whether the bow was still owed, whether the king still deserved it. He felt each hesitation like a stone dropped down a well. Let them hesitate. Hesitation was honest.

Zephyrine saw him and set down the practice blade. "Your Majesty."

"I'm told you're leaving today."

"I'm told that too."

"With the smuggler. Dax." He had met the man briefly—a broad, scarred creature with a laugh like gravel and eyes that tracked exits. Dax had knelt to no one during the whole ceremony of thanks, and Aldric had liked him immediately for it. "He tells me there's work on the southern coast. Legitimate work, mostly."

"Mostly," Zephyrine agreed. A ghost of the smile again.

"My council wants me to offer you a pardon. Formal, public, heralds in every market square. Erasing the last of Thornfell's memory of the Eriksen crimes." He paused. "I want to offer you something else instead. Stay. Be my sword. Not the Order's, not the god's, not your bloodline's. Mine. Chosen, both directions, every day." He heard the echo of his own words from the throne room and pressed on before shame could close his throat. "I know what you said. I'm asking again anyway. Not because I didn't hear you. Because the council will spend the next week telling me I can't rebuild trust without a symbol of force, and I'd rather lose the argument with your refusal standing in front of me than win it alone."

For a long moment the yard was silent. Even the wind seemed to hold off.

Then Zephyrine unbuckled her sword.

She did it slowly, deliberately, holding his eyes, and when the belt came free she crossed the distance between them and held it out—not offering it up, not presenting it, just handing it over, one person to another, hilt first.

"Then take this," she said. "Hang it in whatever room you give the worst news in. Not as a symbol of force. As a reminder that the last time a king of Thornfell was asked to kneel to something that ate oaths, somebody walked in and said no." Her fingers opened. "I can't be your sword, Aldric. But you don't need one. You need to be the kind of king who makes people forget they ever wanted one."

His hands closed around the belt. The leather was worn soft, scarred, ordinary. It weighed almost nothing and it nearly brought him to his knees.

"Will I see you again?"

"Probably somewhere inconvenient." She stepped back, and the morning light caught the gray at her temples that hadn't been there a month ago—grief does that, or freedom does, or both. "When you least want me. That's usually how I arrive."

He laughed, and it hurt, and it was clean.

Behind her, the gates of the lower courtyard stood open, and a smuggler's wagon waited with Dax on the bench and the road going south into green country, into a kingdom that no longer had a god in it anywhere, only people—frightened, furious, faithful, free—beginning the slow unglamorous work of trusting each other again.

Zephyrine Eriksen turned and walked toward it, unnamed and unburdened, and Aldric stood in the sun with a dead woman's blade in his hands and watched her go until the road took her.

Then he turned back toward the palace, toward the council chamber, toward the country waiting to find out what a king without a god was worth—and found, to his astonishment, that his hands had stopped shaking.

He had work to do. Ordinary work. Human work.

It was, he thought, climbing the steps alone, the finest inheritance he had ever received.
