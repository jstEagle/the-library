## 1. Charms and Cinders

The trick to surviving in Vhalder was to be forgettable, and Rafael Marchetti had spent ten years perfecting the art of being no one at all.

His stall in the night market occupied the worst corner of Copperlane Row—too close to the tannery drains, too far from the lantern-string that hung over the better merchants' wares. The canvas above him sagged where rain had pooled and dried and pooled again, leaving maps of mold like continents on a dying world. He sold charms: knotted red thread against night-terrors, copper discs stamped with a sun so crude it could have been a coin with ambitions, bundles of dried lavender tied with waxed string for mothers whose children coughed through the winter.

None of them worked. That was the point. None of them *had* to work, because none of them carried anything but thread and copper and lavender and his own careful, practiced ignorance. A woman bought a knot-charm for her son's nightmares and slept better because she believed, and belief was free, and free things kept you alive in Vhalder.

Real magic got you burned. Small magic got you fined. No magic at all—that was the only currency Rafael dealt in, and he had grown rich in it, if rich meant breathing.

"You're charging four bits for this?" A woman with flour to her elbows held the lavender bundle at arm's length like something dead. "I can grow this myself."

"And yet you haven't." Rafael smiled the smile he kept for haggling, warm but slightly tired, a man mildly put-upon by fortune. "Grown lavender wants sun and patience. This grew in the Ashlands without either. Stubborn stuff. Holds its scent longer."

It had grown in a farmer's field two miles east. But the lie was small and pleasant and did no one harm, and the woman snorted, and paid five bits instead of four because he'd let her feel she'd won something. Rafael made change from a box with a false bottom that contained nothing but more change. Ten years ago that compartment had held a charm that could quiet a wolf's heart mid-howl. Now it held coppers. It was better this way. He told himself it was better this way most nights, and most nights he almost believed it.

The market moved around him in its usual river: fishwives arguing over ice, a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking against some poor man's cleaver, two apprentices from the dyers' guild walking too close together and pretending they weren't. Somewhere a fiddler played the same eight bars over and over because the ninth bar was hard and nobody had ever paid him enough to learn it. Vhalder at night smelled of tar and frying onions and the green rot of the canal, and Rafael loved it the way a prisoner loves the exact dimensions of his cell—precisely, helplessly, because they were his.

"Marchetti." Old Tam Hesk, three stalls down, selling lamp-oil and rumors in equal measure. "Heard the Inquisition took a charm-seller off Tanner's Walk last week. Fined him forty crowns."

"Heard that too," Rafael said, not looking up from re-knotting a display of red thread. "Fool must have sold something that actually worked."

Tam laughed. "There's wisdom. Sell them nothing, eh? Nothing's the safest stock in the city."

"The very safest." Rafael wound the thread tight, tighter than it needed to be. "Though I hear nothing's getting harder to move. Market's flooded with it."

Tam laughed again and went back to his oil. Rafael finished the knot and hated how steady his hands were. Steady hands were a skill, same as lying. You practiced until the fear lived somewhere your fingers couldn't reach.

* * *

He closed the stall at half past eleven, as he always did, folding the canvas with the economy of a man who had performed every motion ten thousand times. He was halfway down Cooper's Stair—the cut-through between the market terrace and the tanners' lanes—when he heard the boots.

Inquisitor patrols didn't march; they strolled. That was the horror of them. Six men in grey who walked like gentlemen out for evening air, unhurried, conversational, their white sashes bright under the street-lamps. One of them laughed at something. They were in no rush because there was never any rush. The guilty ran; the innocent strolled; and the patrol simply drifted through the city like cold water finding cracks, patient as erosion.

Rafael did not run. Running was a confession written on the body. Instead he stopped at the landing where a shuttered lantern sat on an iron bracket, and he bent over it with the fussing attention of a shopkeeper who has noticed his merchandise is crooked, and he turned the shutter a quarter-inch, and studied the flame inside as though its alignment were a matter of civic importance.

The boots came down the stair behind him. Paused.

"Evening."

"Evening, sergeant." He straightened, gave the man the smile—the other smile now, the bland one, the one that said *I am exactly what I appear to be and less*. "Lamp's been guttering. Thought I'd save someone a fall."

"Saw you at the market. Charms, is it?"

"Charms. Knots and copper and lavender. My wife says I sell superstitions at criminal prices." He let the word *wife* sit there, warm and fictional. There was no wife. There had been no wife for ten years, and before that there had been no wife either, only a sister who used to say he'd marry his work, which had turned out to be prophecy of the cruelest kind.

The sergeant looked at him. Inquisitor patrols always had one who looked, while the others looked away, and Rafael had learned to survive the looking by having nothing in his face worth finding. He thought about his ledger, which was clean. His hands, which were clean. The cellar under his shop, which was empty, swept daily, containing nothing but a barrel of nails and a chair.

"Lavender," the sergeant said. "Against what?"

"Coughs. Bad dreams. It's mostly smell, sergeant. Smell does half of medicine anyway."

Something almost like approval crossed the man's broad face. "My mother swore by it." He touched the brim of his helmet—a gesture so ordinary it was obscene—and strolled on after his fellows, laughing again at whatever joke had survived the stairs.

Rafael stood in the dark a long moment, listening to the boots fade, feeling his heartbeat return from wherever he sent it during such conversations. Then he finished turning the lantern shutter—he might as well—and continued down into the tanners' lanes, where the light didn't reach and the rules loosened just enough to breathe.

* * *

The dog found him at the mouth of Gibbet Alley, as it always did.

It was a big ragged thing, some shepherd cross gone yellow at the muzzle, ribs showing under a coat the color of wet cardboard. It had claimed the alleys behind the tanneries the way old soldiers claim a bench, and it had adopted Rafael with the unreasonable loyalty of its kind. Every night it appeared at the edge of the lamplight, tail moving in that cautious half-wag dogs use when hope and experience are at war, and waited.

Rafael crouched and produced the heel of yesterday's bread from his coat pocket. This was also ritual. He fed stray dogs. He told himself it was because he was a soft-hearted fool and a bad liar even to himself, and that explanation was true, and it was also a locked door with something standing behind it.

"There you are," he said. "You're late."

The dog took the bread with surgical gentleness, all the gentleness its jaws owned going into that single act, and then sat and looked at him with amber eyes and the terrible open patience of its species.

And here was the thing about Gibbet Alley at midnight, with the tannery drains muttering and the city's noise worn down to a whisper: it was empty. It had been empty for years. And Rafael Marchetti, who sold knots and copper and lavender and nothing else, who had not spoken one true sentence of power since the night his family's smoke rose over the Ashlands—who had starved that part of himself so thoroughly it had become a rumor even to him—looked at the dog, and opened his mouth, and heard himself say:

"*Hva sker der, gamle ven?*"

The words came out rusted and soft, in a language with no living name that scholars dared write down. The First Tongue. The language the old gods had given the beasts, and the bloodlines they trusted to speak it.

The dog's ears came forward. Its whole body changed—not dramatically, nothing that would read as strange to a passerby, but Rafael saw it the way you see a candle flame lean when a door opens elsewhere in the house. The dog tilted its head, and made a sound low in its chest, and answered.

"*Kald nat. Godt til at gemme sig i,*" it said, or something like it—dog-thought rendered in an ancient grammar, the way a child paraphrases scripture. *Cold night. Good for hiding.*

"I know it," Rafael said. "I know it well."

"*Du er ensom.*"

*I am,* he agreed silently, because saying it aloud was more than he could afford. "Eat your bread."

The dog ate its bread. And then it did the thing it sometimes did, the thing that had started maybe a year ago, the hairline crack across the porcelain of his life: it pressed its head against his knee and held it there, and looked up at him with those amber eyes, and asked a question it had no words for but which needed no translation. *Why do you speak to me only here, in the dark, in whispers, when the whole world is full of my kind and yours? Why do we hide this? What are you afraid of?*

"I'm afraid," Rafael said quietly, "of fires."

The dog didn't understand fire the way he meant it. Dogs understood torches and hearths and the burning end of a soldier's brand, and none of those were the fire he meant—the particular fire, the sanctioned one, the pyre built by lawful men with white sashes who strolled rather than marched. His family had died in that fire. Not all of them. His mother had died in the *other* way first, the way that came before the fire, when the bond was ripped out of her by men who knew how, and what was left of her had been given to the flames as a formality.

He scratched the dog behind its ears, where the fur was thin over old scars. "Go home," he said, in plain trade-tongue now, sealing the door shut again. "Stay out of the streets tomorrow. There'll be patrols."

The dog looked at him a moment longer with its unbearable eyes. Then it loped off down the alley, silent as spilled water, and Rafael stood alone in the dark with bread crumbs on his fingers and the taste of the First Tongue still in his mouth like a coin held too long.

* * *

His shop sat at the end of Candlemakers' Row, wedged between a chandler who hated him and a widow who merely pitied him, and he unlocked it with the three locks he'd installed himself, and lit one candle, and went through the closing rituals. Ledger balanced. Shutters barred. Floor swept. Cellar checked—empty, swept, a barrel of nails, a chair. Everything in its place. A life with everything in its place, packed so tight there was no room for anything to happen in it, which was the design.

He was blowing out the candle when he heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. More a change in the quality of the silence, the way the air changes pressure before weather. The dog's voice had left something behind in his ear, the way a bell keeps humming after the strike. And beneath it, fainter, farther, coming from nowhere he could name—from the walls, from the stones, from the dark past the edge of hearing—there was something that was almost a voice, and it said a word.

It said his name.

Rafael Marchetti stood in his dark shop with his hand still raised toward the dead candle, and listened, and heard nothing more. Rats, probably. Wind in the flue. The city settling its weight onto its foundations, groaning like any old thing does in the night.

But his hand, when he finally lowered it, was not quite steady. And when he slept, he dreamed of smoke rising over green hills, and a child's voice calling a word he had sworn never to answer—

and somewhere far off in the sleeping city, small feet were already walking toward his door.
