## 2. The Child at the Door

The last hour before closing was Rafael's favorite and his most dangerous. Favorite because the night market's noise thinned to a murmur, the lanterns in Copperlane guttered low, and he could count his coins in peace. Dangerous because tired men made mistakes, and Rafael Marchetti had survived ten years in Vhalder by never being tired enough to make one.

He was counting now—seventeen coppers, two silvers, and a bent half-bit some drunk had paid him for a luck-charm that was, honestly, just a knotted thread and a good story—when the bell above his door rang.

He didn't look up immediately. That was a technique. Eager shopkeepers invited questions; bored ones invited nothing. He finished the stack of coins, slid them into the drawer with the soft click of a man who had all the time in the world, and only then raised his eyes.

A girl stood in the doorway.

She was small—seven, maybe eight years old—and she was filthy in the way that meant days rather than hours. Mud had dried in layers on her bare feet, cracked like old paint. Her hair was a dark tangle with twigs in it, and her dress had once been fine wool, now torn at the hem and stained with something he chose not to identify. She stood the way animals stand at the edge of firelight: weight back, ready to run, eyes doing all the work.

Rafael's hands went still on the counter.

*Not your problem.* The thought arrived on schedule, as it always did, wearing the voice of his own survival. *Street children come to shops like this every week. Give her bread or chase her off. Either way, close the door.*

"Shop's closing," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended, so he softened it. "Come back tomorrow if you're after a charm. Luck ones are a copper."

The girl didn't move. She looked at him—not at the charms strung along the wall behind him, not at the coin drawer, not at anything a hungry child should be looking at. She looked at *him*, the way you'd look at a door in a house you'd been walking toward for a very long time.

Then she spoke.

"*Vael su marroth. Vael su Marchetti-kaen.*"

Rafael's blood went cold in a way he hadn't felt since the night his brother died.

It wasn't the words themselves—though he knew them, knew them the way you know a lullaby you've spent a decade trying to forget. It was the shape of them. The low vowels rolling like water over stone. The grammar that put the name first and the person second, because in the First Tongue, the name was older than the person and would outlast them too.

*I have come far. I have come to Marchetti.*

No one spoke that language. Not in Vhalder. Not anywhere south of the Greyfell wastes. The Inquisition had burned the last fluent speakers forty years ago, and their children had learned to pray quietly in Common, and their grandchildren had learned nothing at all. That was the point of burning. Fire was patient. Fire did its work down the generations.

"You shouldn't say things like that," he said quietly. "Not here. Not anywhere."

The girl tilted her head. Mud flaked from her cheek. "*Serra veth?*" she asked—the rising tone of a question, the word for *understand*—and there was no fear in it, no cunning, nothing but the simple hope of a child checking whether a stranger could hear her.

That was the worst part. She didn't know it was forbidden. She didn't know it was anything at all. To her it was just her voice, just words, the same way breathing was breath.

Rafael came around the counter slowly, crossed to the door, and turned the lock. The bolt slid home with a sound like a bone setting. He drew the shade. Copperlane outside dimmed to a smear of orange light through the fabric, and the little shop became a box, and the girl became the only other thing in the world inside it.

"Where is your mother?" he asked.

She considered this. "*Mara veth su kaelen-roth.*"

*I don't understand your soft-words.* Or possibly *I don't speak your quiet-tongue*—his grammar was rusted, ten years out of practice, and some part of him hated how quickly it was coming back, like a dog answering a whistle it had sworn it couldn't hear.

"You do understand," he said. "You understand me right now. You just won't answer in it."

"*Vael su kaelen-roth,*" she agreed solemnly, and touched her own throat. *This tongue.*

"And who taught it to you?"

"No one." She said it in Common, suddenly, the word landing in the middle of the foreign sentence like a stone dropped in still water. Then, seeing his face, she tried again, halting: "Mother says—mother *said*—" Her brow furrowed. The past tense cost her something; he watched it land somewhere behind her eyes and stay there. "Mother said the tooth would know the man. The tooth knows names."

"The tooth."

She reached into the collar of her ruined dress and drew out a cord. On it hung a pendant: a wolf's tooth, long as his thumb, capped in tarnished silver, with three notches cut into the root.

Rafael stopped breathing.

He knew that tooth. He had held it once, when he was nine years old and his grandfather had sat him down by the hearth and shown him the family relics one by one—the ledger with its beast-script marginalia, the iron key to a house that no longer existed, and the tooth. *Three notches,* his grandfather had said, *for three promises kept. When you're grown, boy, you'll learn what we promised, and to whom, and why the kingdoms let us live.* And then the kingdoms had decided otherwise, and the tooth had gone into the fire with everything else, and Rafael had watched it burn—or believed he had.

Ten years ago. The Ashwood house. He had buried what was left of them himself, with a spade borrowed from a neighbor who asked no questions because Rafael paid him not to. There had been nothing left to bury of the tooth but slag.

And yet here it was. Whole. Cold silver. Three notches catching the lamplight.

"That's not possible," he said aloud, before he could stop himself.

The girl looked at the tooth, then at him, then back at the tooth, as though the impossibility were self-evidently his problem and not hers. "*Su kaen-veth roth,*" she said. *It remembers.* And then, in careful, accented Common, the way a child repeats a lesson drilled into her: "It said your name. It said Rafael Marchetti, in the city of bridges, behind the blue door. I walked twenty-and-more days."

Twenty days. Through the passes, alone, at seven years old, following a dead wolf's tooth that whispered directions. His hands wanted to shake and he made them not, the way he'd trained them, the way a man trains any part of himself that might betray him.

"What's your name?"

"Wren." She pronounced it carefully, like a foreign word. Maybe it was. Maybe she'd named herself after something. Birds nested in strange places when they had to.

"Wren. Who else knows where you are? Did anyone follow you? A woman in grey robes—a tall man with a ledger—anyone?"

She frowned at the flood of Common, caught perhaps half of it, and answered the question underneath it instead, which was somehow worse. "*Vael su roth-kellen.* No one sees Wren. Wren walks quiet. The crows tell Wren where the grey-cloaks sleep."

The crows. Of course the crows told her. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye socket and breathed.

Outside, boots on cobblestone—two pairs, unhurried, patrol-weight. Rafael froze, one hand drifting to the lamp, ready to kill the light. The footsteps passed. Faded toward the fountain square. He counted to sixty before he exhaled.

Every instinct he owned, every rule carved into him across ten careful years, said the same thing: *put her out. Now. Tonight. Before dawn. Before someone saw her come in, before the neighbor's shutter moved, before the wrong rat carried the wrong whisper to the wrong ear. She is a lit torch standing in a room full of oil, and she walked through your door on her own legs, which means the universe has finally found the crack in you and is pouring itself through it.*

But she was swaying on her feet now. He saw it happen—the long walk arriving all at once, twenty days presenting their bill. Her knees dipped. She caught herself on the edge of his counter, leaving a small brown handprint on wood he'd varnished himself, and looked up at him with an expression of such complete, unguarded trust that he felt it physically, like a hook set behind the sternum.

"*Kaelen-roth?*" she asked. *May I?* She pointed at the floor. At the space beside his stove, where the coals still held a dull heat.

Terror disguised as hospitality. That was what his father would have called it, if his father had lived long enough to be glib about such things. Every animal instinct said the stranger at the door was death wearing mud and a child's face. And every cold, calculating voice said the same. But the calculation had a hole in it, and the hole was shaped like a girl who had walked twenty days on bare feet because a burnt tooth told her to.

"One night," he heard himself say. "You eat, you sleep, at dawn we talk about where you actually go. Not here. Not with me. One night, Wren, and then—"

She was already moving. She curled up beside the stove with the efficiency of a creature that had slept in worse places recently, pulled her knees to her chest, and was asleep in under a minute. Truly asleep—not the performance of it, but the real collapse, breath going long and slow, one hand still closed around the tooth's cord.

Rafael stood over her for a long time.

The lamp threw his shadow huge against the charm-wall: knotted threads, river-stones, dried herbs, all of it harmless, all of it deliberate, a decade of carefully cultivated nothing. He had built his life the way a mason builds a dry-stone wall—no mortar, no signature, each piece placed so it could be taken away without a trace. One night. He could survive one night. In the morning he'd find Odile, buy the girl passage north with whatever he had, and go back to counting coppers in the last quiet hour, and this would become the strangest week of his life and nothing more.

He reached down—slowly, telling himself he was only going to cover her with the blanket from the back room—and his fingers brushed the tooth where it lay against her collarbone.

Cold. Winter-river cold, though it had been sitting against a sleeping child's skin for an hour.

And as his skin touched the silver cap, something turned over in the dark beneath his ribs. Something old, and patient, and glad.

*Rafael,* it seemed to say, in the First Tongue, in his grandfather's voice, in a voice that belonged to none of them and all of them. *You kept the door shut for ten years. We only needed you to open it once.*

He snatched his hand back. The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The girl slept on. Outside, somewhere over Copperlane, a dog began to bark—not the ragged argument of strays, but one long, sustained note, like an alarm, like a summons, and it did not stop until dawn.

Rafael did not sleep at all.

* * *

In the grey hour before market-bell, he sat at his counter with the ledger open in front of him and wrote nothing. Across the page, in his neat merchant's hand, he had written the day's date, and below it, twice, and struck through both times:

*Send her to Odile.*
*Send her to Odile and forget her name.*

Under the strikes, without deciding to, his pen had added a third line in letters smaller than the rest, as if written by a hand trying not to be seen:

*Three notches for three promises. What did we promise?*

From the floor by the stove, Wren stirred. She didn't wake, exactly—but her lips moved, shaping words in the old language, soft and looping, and the tooth at her throat caught the first light coming under the shade and threw it back silver.

And Rafael understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own warrant, that whatever had chosen her path through the mountains had not sent her to Vhalder by accident.

It had sent her to him. By name.

The market-bell rang. Somewhere in the city, doors were opening.
