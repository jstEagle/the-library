## 3. What the Tooth Remembers

The tooth talks in the dark.

Not with a mouth. Not with words like Mama's words, or the miller's, or the priest's when he stood on the steps and said the beasts were gone and the gods were gone and the world was quiet now, so hush, children, hush. The tooth talks the other way. The way water talks to roots. The way the wind talks to wheat until all of it bends together.

Wren has no word for that way except *true*.

She is walking again. Her feet hurt. Her feet have hurt since the second day and now it is the—she counts on her fingers, loses count, starts over—it is many days. Nine? Eleven? The sun has come up more times than she can hold in her hands. She walks along the edge of a road because the tooth says roads remember feet, and if her feet are on a road, the road will remember her too, and remembering is how you get found.

But being found by the wrong ones is dying. That part the tooth says sharp, sharp as frost on a window: *hide from the men who smell of ink.*

Ink. She knows what ink smells like now. She smelled it three nights ago, or four, in a town with a bell tower where she almost asked a baker woman for bread. Two men at a table near the door had leather satchels and paper edges showing and their coats carried that dry bitter ghost-smell, and before Wren even understood why her stomach turned over, the tooth went cold against her chest, cold as a stone pulled out of a river, and she walked past the bread and out of the town and did not stop shaking until the trees came back.

*Men who smell of ink,* the tooth says. *They write names down, and what they write down, they come for.*

So Wren does not let anyone write her name down. She does not even say her name out loud anymore. In her head she is still Wren, small and quick, the bird that lives in the hedge, the one nobody catches. But to the world she is nobody, no-name, a mud-colored girl walking the ditch-line south, eating berries that make her mouth itch, drinking from ditches when she must and from streams when she can find them.

At night she curls up under things—fallen logs, bramble tangles, once the hollow belly of a dead cart horse she could not bear to climb inside after all—and she holds the tooth in both fists under her chin, and the voice comes, and she sleeps.

The voice is old. That is the only way she can think of it. Old like the hills are old, but not sleepy like the hills. Old like something that has been waiting so long the waiting has worn grooves into it, and down those grooves the voice runs smooth and patient, saying the same things over and over, and every time it says them Wren understands a little better, the way you understand a song after hearing it your whole life without knowing you were learning it.

It speaks the First Tongue. She didn't know that was its name until the tooth named it. It is just *speaking*, to her—the way speaking works, the way meaning arrives. It was only when she tried to say one of its words with her mouth, alone in a beet field, testing, that she felt how strange it was: how her tongue made shapes no one else would know, how the word for *wolf* was also the word for *brother* was also the word for *the-one-who-runs-beside-you-in-the-dark-so-you-are-not-alone.* One word. All of that. She had sat down in the dirt among the beets and cried a little, not from sadness, just from how big the word was, how it didn't fit inside her mouth, how it fit anyway.

Then she got up and kept walking, because the voice said *south*, and said a name.

*Marchetti,* the voice says. *Rafael Marchetti. South, along the river-road, to the city with the crooked crown. Find the shop with the charms in the window. Knock.*

*He won't open it,* the voice adds, and here the grooves in the old waiting go deep and sad, and the tooth grows warm against her sternum, warm like a hand pressed there. *He buried us. He will not want to dig us up. But knock anyway, little wren. Knock and knock and do not leave. He is the door. He has always been the door.*

Wren doesn't understand doors that are people. But she understands knocking. Knocking is easy.

* * *

The village burns behind her even now, some nights, when she closes her eyes.

She tries not to close her eyes all the way. She keeps a crack open, a slit, so the fire has somewhere else to be. But sometimes sleep takes her anyway, and then it is that night again, whole:

Mama shaking her awake, no lamp lit, which was wrong, Mama always lit the lamp first. Mama's hands cold. *Out the back, love, out through the bean rows, don't look back, whatever you hear, don't look back.* And pressing something into her palms—warm, smooth, curved—a thing on a cord, and folding Wren's fingers closed over it and saying words Wren had never heard her say, low and fast, words that made the dark lean closer to listen: *run to the tooth, the tooth will carry you, run.*

And Wren ran. Through the beans wet with night-dew, through the gap in the stone wall where the fox went, into the wood, and behind her the village began to scream in orange light, and she did not look back, she didn't, she only looked forward, forward at the dark trees, and the tooth against her chest began to speak for the very first time, and its first words were:

*This way, daughter-of-the-line. I am sorry. This way.*

Sorry. The first thing her inheritance ever said to her was sorry.

She ran all that night. Things moved in the wood alongside her—she saw eyes, pairs of eyes, green and gold, keeping pace at the edge of her seeing—but nothing touched her, and the tooth said, *they know whose blood you carry; walk on, walk on,* and so she walked on while her home became smoke behind her, and by morning her feet were bleeding inside her shoes and she had learned the shape of the voice the way you learn a road.

She never learned what happened to Mama. The tooth knows. She can feel it knowing, the way you can feel bread rising through a closed oven door. But when she asks—when she curls up in the bracken and presses the tooth to her lips and whispers *is she—is Mama*—the voice goes quiet in a way that isn't no and isn't yes, a silence shaped exactly like grief, and Wren has decided that means: *later. When you're safe. Some things are too heavy to hand a child on a road.*

She is almost nine. She is tired of people deciding things are too heavy for her. But she lets the silence stand, because arguing with the tooth is like arguing with winter.

* * *

Cities, it turns out, are louder than burning.

Wren reaches Vhalder on an evening when the sky over it glows brown-yellow, not with fire but with ten thousand lamps and cookfires and forge-fires, all the little fires of ten thousand lives stacked on top of each other. The walls rise up out of the plain like the jawbone of something enormous, and gates yawn in it, and guards stand in the gates with pikes, and everyone streaming in and out smells of sweat and onions and horse and iron, and Wren stands in the ditch outside for a long time, because the tooth has gone very still.

Still is worse than talking. Still is the tooth holding its breath.

*What is it?* she asks it, inside, the asking that needs no mouth.

And the tooth says, slow, like a man standing at the edge of a grave he dug himself long ago: *I was born in a place beneath this city. I was carried through these gates on the neck of a Marchetti who was singing. The last time I came home, they burned my bearer in the square while the bells rang noon.*

Wren's throat goes tight. *Is Rafael Marchetti going to burn too?*

A long pause. The longest pause yet. Down in the grooves of the old waiting, something turns over, heavy as a plow.

*No,* the tooth says at last, and the word has teeth of its own. *Not this time. This time, if the ink-men come, we will not be the ones who burn. Knock on his door, little wren. Knock, and tell him the line is not ended. Tell him the bond chose him before he was born, and hiding was never the same as refusing. Tell him—*

The voice falters. Wren waits. The gate-guards wave people through, bored, checking carts for smuggled brandy and not for smuggled girls.

*Tell him his grandmother's name,* the tooth says quietly. *Tell him Serafina. He will pretend not to flinch. Watch for the flinch.*

* * *

Finding the shop takes two days, because Wren is nine and the city is a maze built by a mad god, and because she has four coppers stolen—no, *found*, found fair, dropped by a fat man who didn't deserve them—which buys her rolls and a corner of an inn stable to sleep in among the straw, where the stable cat stares at her for a long time and then, slowly, deliberately, blinks and dips its head.

*Little sister of the old speech,* the cat says, in the way cats say anything: like it is doing you a favor. *You smell of tooth and wolf. There is a charm-seller off Tanner's Row whose mice talk in their sleep about him. The mice say he listens. The mice say he pretends not to.*

*Thank you,* Wren says, and offers to scratch its ears, and the cat permits this as though granting a kingdom.

So: Tanner's Row. A street that stinks of lime and hides, where the buildings lean together overhead like conspirators, and halfway down, wedged between a dyer's and a shuttered chandlery, a narrow shop with a bow window and, in the window, on little velvet shelves, charms. Knots of thread. Bits of bone. Coins with holes drilled in them. Small dull harmless-looking things, each one tagged with a tiny paper label in a cramped careful hand.

Wren stands in front of the window with her heart going like a bird in a box.

Through the glass, deep in the dimness behind the shelves, she can see a man. Dark hair going grey at the temples. Sleeves rolled. Hands moving over a workbench, doing something fine and precise with a needle and wire. He does not look up. He looks like a man who has practiced not looking up for a very long time.

*Rafael Marchetti,* the tooth says, and the warmth of it floods her whole chest, and she feels the voice straining toward the man behind the glass the way a dog strains at a rope toward home. *There he is. There is the last of the line. Look how small he has made himself. Look how well he hides. Ten years, and he has hidden so well he has forgotten there was ever anything to hide from besides everything.*

*I'm scared,* Wren admits, to the tooth, to herself, to the evening.

*I know,* the tooth says gently. *Be scared and knock anyway. Courage is fear that kept walking. Someone told me that once. He had your chin.*

The shop door has a bell above it, a small cracked thing that makes a sound like a spoon dropped in an empty hall.

Inside smells of dust and beeswax and, underneath, faintly, of dog—old dog, ghost dog, a smell soaked into the floorboards from some animal long gone. Wren's nose, which has been getting better lately, better than noses should get, tells her the man himself carries the same ghost-smell on his skin, so faint you'd need her new nose to find it.

The man sets down his needle. Looks at her. His face does the thing faces do when they decide what to show you: settles into polite blankness, shopkeeper's blankness, *nothing-to-see-here* blankness. But before it settles, for half a heartbeat, Wren sees underneath it, sees the flinch the tooth promised, and she hasn't even said the name yet.

"We're closed," Rafael Marchetti says. "Come back tomorrow."

Wren reaches up and pulls the cord over her head. The tooth swings free, catches the lamplight, yellow and curved and old.

"I need to give this back," she says.

And then, because the tooth rises in her throat like a tide, because ten years of waiting pour out of her small mouth all at once, she says the rest—in the First Tongue, the big words, the words that don't fit and fit anyway:

*Wolf-brother, son-of-Serafina, the line is not ended, the bond chose you before you were born, hiding was never refusing, and I ran a hundred miles to knock on your door, so you have to open it, because I don't have anywhere else, and the tooth says you were always the door.*

The needle in his hand slips. She hears it tick against the bench.

The man goes white—not angry-white, not frightened-for-himself white, but the terrible white of a man hearing a voice he buried come up through the floorboards of the world. His eyes fix on the pendant swinging from her fist, and Wren watches ten years happen to his face all at once: the grief arriving late, the terror arriving on time, and underneath both, faint as the ghost-dog smell, something waking up that he has spent a decade convincing the dark it should leave asleep.

"Where," he says, and his voice comes out cracked down the middle, "did you get that?"

"It was my mama's," Wren says. "Before that it was everybody's. Before that it was yours." She holds it out to him across the counter, arm straight, the way you return a borrowed thing. "It remembers you. It called me here by name. It wouldn't stop saying your name the whole way south."

Behind him, in the back of the shop, a floorboard creaks though no one is there.

Outside on Tanner's Row, a rat pauses in the gutter beneath the bow window, whiskers twitching, listening to a language older than the city—then turns and flows away through the dark, toward a door with a lantern burning behind it, toward a man with a ledger, to report, in the only way rats report anything, that the silence of Vhalder has just cracked wide open.
