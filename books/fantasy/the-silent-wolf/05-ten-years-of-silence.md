## 5. Ten Years of Silence

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Rafael finally spoke.

Wren sat on the floor beside the cold stove, knees drawn up, the wolf-tooth pendant cupped in both hands like something that might fly away if she loosened her grip. She hadn't spoken since the door closed and bolted. She didn't need to. The silence she kept was the First Tongue itself—old, patient, filling the room the way water fills a hull.

"You know what this is," he said. Not a question. Her eyes flicked up, amber in the candlelight, and she nodded once.

He turned his chair to face her. His hands were shaking. He folded them flat against his thighs so she wouldn't see, though he suspected she could hear it—the way her head tilted slightly, tracking some sound beneath the sound, the small animal music of his pulse. Ten years of practice at being unreadable, undone by a child of perhaps eight winters sitting on his floorboards.

"Then you should know why I'm going to say no."

* * *

The night it happened, Rafael was fourteen, and the thing he remembers first—not the fire, not the screaming, not even his mother's voice—is the smell of the bread.

His mother had baked late because his father came home from the eastern pastures with frost in his beard and a hunger like a hole in him, and Marchetti men were always hungriest in autumn, when the bond ran close under the skin like a second bloodstream. The loaf sat on the windowsill to cool, and the whole house smelled of crust and wheat and woodsmoke, ordinary as any house in Vhalder's outer ring, because that was the entire art of their family: to be so ordinary that the extraordinary in them went unwritten.

His father called the beasts by no name anyone else could hear. That was the law of the bond. You did not speak where walls had ears, and in Vhalder every wall had ears. For six generations the Marchettis had carried the wolf-bond quietly—a charm here, a whispered word to a carthorse there, nothing the Inquisition's ledgers could catch. His grandmother used to say the bond was a debt you paid in silence.

The debt came due on the third of Frostmonth.

It was a neighbor. Rafael never learned which one. Someone saw his father standing in the frozen turnip field at dusk with his arms open, and saw what came to him—not summoned, just *answered*—three grey wolves out of the treeline, moving around him like dogs around a beloved master, and someone understood that this was not luck, not training, not a trick of hungry animals. Someone wrote a letter. Someone took coin for it.

They came at midnight. Not soldiers first—inquisitors first, four of them in grey coats, with an iron cage between them that Rafael thought at first must hold a prisoner. It held coals. Blessed coals, taken from the cathedral pyre, that burned without light.

His father opened the door before they knocked. Of course he did. He'd heard them three streets off; the bond gave him their heartbeats like drums. He could have run. Later, Rafael would spend years understanding why he didn't—why he stood in the doorway in his nightshirt with flour still on his hands from helping with the bread, and said, in a voice pitched to carry to whatever neighbor was watching through shutters:

"You have the wrong house."

But the lead inquisitor held up a sheet of paper, and on it was drawn a wolf's head, and beneath it a word in a dead language, and Rafael's father looked at it and stopped lying, because the paper was *theirs*. It was a page of the treaty. It had been in the family for two hundred years, hidden inside the binding of a hymnal, and someone had sold that too.

"Marchetti," the inquisitor said. "You are accused of carrying the old inheritance. Confess and be cleansed."

His mother pulled Rafael behind her. His little sister Lia, seven years old, slept upstairs. He would think about that later—how his parents' first act was not to fight but to position themselves between the door and the stairs, as if bodies could be a wall.

"I renounce it," his father said. "Take it. I give it freely." Because there was a procedure, everyone knew there was a procedure—you confessed, they burned the bond out of you with the blessed coals, and if your body survived you lived the rest of your life hollow, deaf to the world's old voices, but alive. His father knelt. His father, who could call wolves across a valley, knelt on his own floor and bowed his head.

The inquisitor smiled. That was the part Rafael's memory refused to soften: the smile. "The writ says cleanse the line," he said. "Not the man."

What happened next happened fast and then slow. It always does, in memory. Rafael remembered his mother's hands shoving him toward the back window. He remembered the coals coming out of the cage glowing black-red, and the inquisitor pressing them—not to skin, worse, *into* skin, into the place below the sternum where the bond sat—and his father's scream going up through registers no human throat should find, and the wolves outside howling in answer, a sound like the treeline itself grieving. He remembered his mother turning to face the men with a kitchen knife and no magic at all in her hands, because she'd married into the bloodline and carried none of it, only the love of it.

He remembered the window frame splintering under his palms. He remembered looking back once. Once was enough for a lifetime.

The house burned. They let the fire do what the coals began. In the morning the parish record would say *accidental blaze, family perished*, because the Inquisition preferred its cleansing quiet, and Vhalder preferred not to know what it preferred.

Rafael ran east through the frozen fields with his lungs full of blood and smoke, and behind him the wolves' howling rose and rose and then—cut off. All at once, mid-note, like a door closing. And he knew, in the wordless way the bonded know things, that the last of his father's blood had burned out of the world, and the beasts felt it go.

Except it hadn't. Except here, ten years later, a mud-streaked child sat on his floor holding the tooth that proved the ledger wrong.

* * *

Wren was watching him with that terrible stillness children have, the kind adults mistake for patience and is actually total attention.

"The last man who carried it," Rafael said, and had to stop, and start again. "My father. Do you understand? The tooth passed to me after—or it should have. My grandmother hid it. I think she hid it from *me*, so I'd never answer when it called." He nodded at the pendant. "I was supposed to be the end of the line. A line ends, the debt dies. That was the plan. That was the whole point of everything I've built since."

Wren said something then—soft syllables in the First Tongue, two words, and he hated that he understood them without wanting to, the way you hate knowing the meaning of your own name in a language the law has outlawed.

*I waited.*

"Yes," he said roughly. "I know you waited. I know it called you across half the country. I know you're brave and I know you're tired and I know—" his voice cracked and he pressed it flat "—I know what they did to your village because of what you can do. So listen to me now, girl, because I'm only saying this once."

He crouched in front of her, close enough that the candlelight filled the space between them, and made himself hold her gaze.

"There was a war. Before either of us, before my grandmother, two hundred years ago. The kingdoms called it the Beast Wars and the priests call it the Cleansing and the old texts call it nothing at all, because the people who won got to burn the words. The bonded lines served the old gods' beasts—we were the treaty made flesh, the bridge between what the world was and what men wanted it to be. And the last bearer of our bond—" his jaw tightened "—was not a keeper. He was a breaker. He took the war-magic inside the inheritance and he *used* it. Called the great beasts down on cities. Turned rivers to red ice. Killed forty thousand people in the Sack of Corvane alone, and when the kingdoms finally broke him, they swore it would never happen again, and the Inquisition was born from that oath, and every bonded family in every kingdom was hunted to ash so the oath would hold."

He touched the tooth, lightly, one finger, the way you touch a snake.

"That is what answers you when you speak to it. Not a friend. Not a guide. A weapon that has been waiting two hundred years for a hand stupid enough to pick it up again. Your village burned because someone learned what you are. Mine burned because someone learned what my father was. If I take that inheritance—if I wake what sleeps in it—then everything they died hiding becomes true. Do you understand? I wouldn't be saving you. I'd be proving them right."

Wren's chin lifted. Stubbornness, he'd learn, was bedrock in her. She spoke again, and this time the First Tongue came slower, weighted, and he translated it in his chest before it reached his mind:

*The tooth chose you anyway.*

"It chose wrong," he said.

But the words came out wrong themselves—too loud, too fast, the overcorrection of a man arguing with a truth. Because the tooth *had* chosen. It had crossed mountains to find the last Marchetti, had dragged a feral child through winter to his door, had put its ancient weight into his shop, his life, his carefully constructed nothing. Bonds did not make mistakes. His grandmother had taught him that much before she taught him silence: *the bond is older than want, boy. It doesn't ask what you prefer.*

He stood abruptly and turned his back on her, busying his hands with the shutter latch, checking it twice, thrice. Cowardice, dressed as caution. He knew the difference. He had studied the difference for ten years the way scholars study scripture.

For ten years he had been a ghost with excellent bookkeeping. Charms too small to prosecute—hearth-luck knots that might be superstition, travel-salves that might be good herbs, blessings muttered low enough to be breathing. He had learned the exact weight a magic could bear before the scales tipped. He had learned which patrols walked which streets, which informants drank at which taverns, how to look bored when the inquisitors swept the night market. He had built a life the size of a coffin and lain down in it every night and called it survival.

And it had worked. Ten years. Long enough that he'd almost believed the debt was paid, that the silence was a life instead of a sentence.

Then a child knocked on his door speaking the language of the old gods, wearing his family's death around her neck, and every wall he'd built turned out to be exactly as thick as a wish.

Behind him, Wren shifted. He heard the small sounds of her settling against the wall beside the stove, pulling her ragged cloak around herself, making a nest of nothing because she'd slept in worse places and expected to sleep in worse ones again. That expectation was its own indictment. Somewhere out there, men had burned a village to get to a little girl, and she had run alone through a winter country following a voice in a tooth, and the voice had led her to him because he was the last name left on a list of the dead.

He should send her away tonight. Every rational instinct said so. Put silver in her hand, point her south, bar the door behind her, and by morning return to the coffin-life that had kept him breathing while better people burned.

He looked at the shutter. He looked at his own hands, scarred from ten years of harmless craft, steady now only because he forced them to be.

In the morning, he told himself. In the morning he would find Odile. Not to buy the girl passage—no, he'd already decided that too, in the coward's arithmetic of *one more night*. Just to ask prices. Just to know his options. Knowing wasn't committing. Knowing was what careful men did.

He blew out the candle and lay down on his pallet in the back room, and listened in the dark to a child breathe in his shop, and hated himself for how much quieter the silence was with her in it.

Sleep took him slowly, and dreams took him faster. Fire, bread, his sister's name in his mouth. And beneath the dreaming, faint but unmistakable, rising through the floorboards of his sleep like heat through stone: the tooth, awake now, patient now, speaking in the First Tongue to the child across the dark.

And Wren answering it softly, in a whisper meant to be private, in words he understood whether he wanted to or not:

*He'll say yes. He just needs to remember why it matters.*

Rafael lay still in the black, heart hammering, and did not sleep again. By the time the grey light came, he had made a decision that was not the decision he'd planned, and he pulled his coat from its peg and set out through the waking streets of Vhalder to find the one woman in the city who moved people the way others moved contraband—before whoever had burned Wren's village finished following the same trail he had.
