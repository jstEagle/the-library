## 6. The Old Market Bargain

The Old Market woke after midnight, the way all of Vhalder's honest business slept and its dishonest kind stirred. Rafael went down through the tannery district with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, past walls still sweating dye, past the shuttered stalls where by day merchants sold candle-wax and rope and other blameless things. By night the same stalls changed their inventory. Rope became a way out of the city folded into a coil. Candle-wax became names written on slips and sealed inside.

He had not come here in four years.

Four years, he told himself, was not cowardice. Four years was discipline. There was a difference, and he had spent a decade learning to feel it in his bones: the coward ran when running might save him; the disciplined man stayed put and kept his head down until staying put stopped working. Tonight it had stopped working. A child slept on the floor of his back room on a pallet made from an old coat, her fist closed around a wolf's tooth, and every hour she remained in Vhalder was an hour the city had to notice her before he could make it forget her again.

So: the Old Market. So: Odile.

* * *

She kept her office above a fishmonger's stall that hadn't sold fish in six years, up a staircase that smelled of brine and lamp oil, behind a door with no lock because locks invited questions about what needed locking. Rafael knocked twice, paused, knocked once. The pattern was old enough that his knuckles remembered it without consulting him.

"Come in before someone sees you standing there like a man visiting his own funeral."

Odile sat behind a desk that had been a ship's door in some earlier life, propped on two barrels. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a barge, with iron-grey hair cropped close to her skull and hands that looked like they had personally strangled most of the century's problems. A single lamp burned low. Two cups waited on the desk. She had known he would come. That was the first price, though no one ever counted it: with Odile, you paid in being known.

"Madame Odile." He sat across from her, keeping his knees angled toward the door out of habit. "You look well."

"I look rich, which is better." She pushed a cup toward him. Tea, dark and bitter, cut with something that stung the back of the throat. "Ten years since you darkened my stairs. I'd begun telling people you were dead."

"That would have been convenient for both of us."

"It would have been convenient for one of us and profitable for me, so I didn't." She drank, watching him over the rim with small, quick eyes the color of wet slate. "But dead men don't climb my stairs at half past midnight looking like a man who hasn't slept in a week. Alive men do. What is it?"

"A passage. Out of the city. North road, or river—whichever's cleaner this month."

"For you?"

"No."

Something shifted in her face—not surprise, exactly, but the recalibration of a woman who had priced a hundred conversations in advance and now had to reprice one. "No," she repeated. "For whom, then? You have no family. You have no friends. You have nothing but a shop full of trinkets and a reputation for minding your own affairs so scrupulously that the Inquisitors use you as an example of what a licensed charm-seller ought to be." She set the cup down. "Whoever this is, they're new. Newer than a week. And you came to me at night, alone, without haggling first, which means you're frightened, and frightened men pay badly and talk too much. So we'll do this properly. Sit there. Be quiet. Let me ask the questions."

Rafael sat there. He was quiet. It cost him more than talking would have; every second of silence was a second Wren spent alone in his shop, and the tooth around her neck, and the First Tongue sitting in her mouth like a lit coal.

"The passenger," Odile said. "Age."

"Young."

"Young is a range, Marchetti, not an answer. Six? Ten? Fifteen?"

"Eight. Perhaps nine. She doesn't know."

"A girl." Odile leaned back, and the barrels under the desk groaned. "Alone?"

"With me. For now."

"And where does she need to go that you don't?"

"Anywhere that isn't here."

"Mm." She laced her fingers over her stomach. Her hands, up close, were a map of old work: burn scars along the left thumb, a finger gone crooked at the second knuckle, ink stains at the cuticles that never washed out entirely because the ink she used wasn't the kind sold in shops. "Let me tell you how this looks from my side of the desk. A man who has spent ten years being the most invisible man in Vhalder walks into my market at midnight and buys passage for a child he won't travel with. Either the child is worth more to him than his own skin—which makes her valuable to other people too—or she's trouble he can't keep, which makes her dangerous to move. Both answers raise my price. Do you want to know which answer I'm leaning toward?"

"The second," Rafael said quietly. "Because the first would mean asking why."

"Good. You remember how this works." She opened a drawer and took out a ledger—not hers, something older, water-stained, its spine held together with tarred cord—and laid it flat between them without opening it. Her hand rested on the cover like a woman resting her hand on a dog that hadn't yet decided whether to bite. "Passage lists," she said. "Every runner on the north road, every bargemaster on the Greywater, every caravan master who takes a body out of this city without papers. I buy their silence monthly. It is the most expensive thing I own. More than the boats. More than the bribes. Because a list is only worth something while nobody else has read it."

Rafael felt the floor tilt slightly, though the floor was fine. "And has somebody else read it?"

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned the ledger around, opened it to a marker three pages in, and rotated it so the lamplight fell across the columns.

There, in her own cramped hand, was the record of the last month's payments. And beside each name, in a different ink—a colder, neater, official hand—small checkmarks.

"She came to me eleven days ago," Odile said. "An Inquisitor. Young. Woman. Vance—the name means anything to you?"

It meant everything. It meant the file he'd never seen, the decade of small deniable charms, the attention he'd spent ten years believing he'd escaped. Rafael kept his face very still, the way he did when a patrol passed the stall, and heard himself say, "Should it?"

"Probably not. Probably she's one of forty clerks with a warrant book and a taste for paperwork." Odile tapped the nearest checkmark. "But she didn't come with a warrant. She came with coin. City coin, drawn on the Inquisition's disbursement fund—I checked the seal twice, because I am careful, and then I checked it a third time, because I am also greedy and wanted to be sure the money was real. She bought the passage lists. All of them. Every route, every name, every date going back eight months. Paid double what they're worth and asked no questions, which is how I know she already knows whatever the questions would have found."

The tea had gone cold in Rafael's cup. He looked at it rather than at her. Eleven days ago. Eleven days ago Wren had been—where? On the road. Fleeing a burned village, following a voice in a tooth, walking toward a man whose name she shouldn't have known. And Sera Vance had been buying lists, patient as winter, sweeping every road out of Vhalder into a ledger she could read at her leisure.

"She's not hunting anyone specific," Odile said, watching him. "If she were, she'd have come with soldiers and saved herself the money. She's fishing. She bought the whole river because she thinks something will swim downstream eventually, and she wants to know the shape of every net before she decides where to stand." Odile closed the ledger. "Now. My turn to tell you how this works, since you've apparently forgotten. Those checkmarks mean every route I just described is warm. Warm routes cost triple. They cost more than triple, actually, because the people I'd normally use are the same people whose names are in that book, and right now none of them will take a passenger north for love or money, mostly money, mostly mine. Which leaves me using people who are either very good or very stupid, and the stupid ones are cheaper and die faster, which creates its own problems."

"How much?" Rafael said.

"For a child, alone, unregistered, on a warm route, in the next—" Odile glanced at the lamp, as if the flame kept the calendar— "let's say week, before your Inquisitor finishes reading her fishing book and starts pulling nets. Forty crowns. Half now."

"I don't have forty crowns."

"Nobody has forty crowns. Forty crowns is a number I invented to make you understand what you're asking." She folded her hands. "What you're really asking is for me to spend a runner I trust, on a route that's burned, carrying cargo that's hot, for a man who's been off my books for four years and won't even tell me the girl's name."

"Wren."

It came out before he decided to say it. Odile's eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch, and Rafael understood, with the particular horror of a careful man, that he had just done the one thing he never did: he had given a piece of the truth away for free, because he was tired, and frightened, and because somewhere under ten years of discipline the part of him that had once been a Marchetti still believed that names were given in trust.

"Wren," Odile repeated, tasting it. "Not a Vhalder name."

"Northern. Village name."

"Burning villages produce a lot of northern refugees lately. None of them come with wolf-tooth pendants." She said it flatly, watching him, and Rafael's blood went cold in a slow, deliberate wave. "Yes," she said, reading him. "I saw it. She'll have been holding it in her fist the whole time—that's how children carry things that matter—and when your runner lifts her onto the barge tomorrow or whenever, she'll still be holding it, and my runner will see it, and runners talk. That's not a flaw in the plan, Marchetti. That's the plan failing at the exact moment I stop being able to control it."

"Then the tooth stays with me."

"And she goes north screaming? Or goes north quiet, like a child who's learned that the things she loves get taken?" Odile shook her head slowly. "I've moved a lot of children out of this city. The ones who arrive clutching something always arrive clutching something, or they arrive broken. I don't ship broken goods. Bad karma. Worse business." She pushed the ledger aside and stood, crossing to the window—or what served for one, a gap between boards stuffed with rag—and stood looking down at the black lane below. "I'll take her."

Rafael's breath went out of him.

"But not for forty crowns," Odile continued, "and not for eighty, and not for anything you're currently carrying in that coat pocket, so stop checking it. For something else." She turned. The lamplight caught her face from below and made her look, briefly, like something carved on the prow of a ship. "A favor. Open-ended. Undated. Mine to call in when I choose, however I choose, and you don't get to hear the terms until I need the favor done. That's the price. That's the only price, because everything else you could pay me would run out, and favors don't."

"No."

It surprised him, hearing it aloud. Odile smiled, thin and genuine, like a knife being appreciated.

"There he is," she said. "Ten years ago you'd have said yes. Ten years ago you'd have said yes to anything, because you were twenty-two and your family had just taught you what the world does to people who say no. But you're not that boy anymore, are you? You're the invisible man. And the invisible man knows that owing me a favor is worse than owing me money, because money spends once and a favor spends forever." She came back to the desk and sat. "So let's be honest with each other, because honesty is cheaper than either of our reputations. You're going to say yes eventually. The question is whether you say it tonight, when it's worth forty crowns' worth of favor, or in three days, when it's worth eighty, or next week, when it's worth a runner's life and I'll want a different favor entirely. Prices rise, Marchetti. That's the whole of economics. That's the whole of everything."

Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog began to bark—short, sharp, rhythmic, the sound of an animal announcing something it couldn't name. Rafael knew the voice. One of the alley dogs, the grey bitch with the torn ear who took his scraps on Thursdays. He listened to it for three full seconds and understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with magic, that it was barking at footsteps. Someone was walking the fishmonger's lane at half past one in the morning, unhurried, taking their time.

Or someone was simply walking. Cities were full of footsteps. That was the disease of his whole life: he could never again hear a footstep and believe it was nothing.

"Time's passing," Odile said softly. "And I think we both hear the same dog."

* * *

He walked home the long way, doubling back twice through the tannery rows, and the footsteps—if they were footsteps—did not follow, or followed well enough that he couldn't tell, which amounted to the same thing. The rain started somewhere over the harbor and caught him at the corner of Chandler's Row, fine and cold, beading on his collar.

Forty crowns. He had eleven, sewn into the lining where he'd kept them since the year he'd buried the last of his family, because grief was the only thing he'd ever trusted himself to hold onto. He could sell the shop—not quickly, not cleanly, and selling a charm-seller's license drew the Inquisition's eye the way a wound draws flies. He could empty the strongbox under the floorboards and find maybe six more. Twenty crowns against forty, and the gap widening every day he hesitated, because Odile had been right about the one thing she'd been cruel about: prices rise.

And beneath the arithmetic, quieter, the other thing. The tooth. The way Wren held it even in sleep. The way the words came out of her mouth in the First Tongue, easy as water, as if the language had been waiting ten years for a mouth young enough to speak it without shame. *The bond chose him,* she'd said—or the tooth had said, or whatever spoke through the tooth had said—and Rafael had spent three days refusing to think about what that meant, the way a man refuses to look directly at a bright fire.

The favor. An open-ended debt to Odile, called in whenever she chose. He had watched people owe her favors. He had seen what she asked. Nothing monstrous—she wasn't a monster—but things that chipped at a man, piece by piece, until the man who came out the far side of her ledger bore only a passing resemblance to the man who'd signed.

Behind him, three streets back, the dog had stopped barking.

That was worse.

He reached his own door a little after three, and stood in the rain a moment before unlocking it, listening to the building breathe. Inside, the shop smelled of wax and dried herbs and, faintly, of woodsmoke from a village he'd never seen burning. He climbed the stairs, crossed the dark workshop, and eased open the back-room door.

Wren was awake. Of course she was awake. She lay curled on the coat-pallet with the tooth in her fist and her eyes open and fixed on him in the dark, luminous, unblinking, the way animals watch.

"You smell like the sea," she said in the First Tongue, and then, catching his expression, repeated it in trade-talk, clumsy and careful. "You smell like the sea. And like fear."

"Markets smell like fear," Rafael said. "It's the main export."

"Did she say yes?"

He stopped in the doorway. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, at the end of the lane, a cart rattled past at an hour when carts had no honest reason to be moving.

"She'll take you north," he said. "Three days. Maybe less."

Wren considered this with the grave, total attention of a child deciding something enormous. Then she held up the tooth, and in the dark it seemed to hold light the way a stone holds the day's heat, faintly, stubbornly.

"She said yes," Wren said, "because the tooth told her to. It tells everyone. They just don't listen yet."

Rafael stood in the doorway and felt, settling over him with the patience of rising water, the certainty that the price he'd been haggling over tonight was not the real one—and that somewhere in the city a woman with a neat, cold hand was turning the pages of a ledger, finding the blank space where his name should be, and beginning, at last, to wonder about it.
