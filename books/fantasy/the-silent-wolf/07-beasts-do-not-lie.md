## 7. Beasts Do Not Lie

The shop closed around Wren like a mouth.

Rafael had gone out through the back, the way he always went out—fast, quiet, one hand pressed flat to the door as if he were saying goodbye to it—and he had told her three things before he left. *Do not open for anyone. Do not touch the jars with wax seals. Do not speak where windows can hear.*

She had promised all three. She was good at promising. Promises were easy; they were just words shaped a certain way and given away.

The hard thing was being alone in a room that smelled of him.

The shop was small and full of small things. Dried herbs hanging from the beams like sleeping bats. Drawers within drawers within drawers, each labeled in a cramped hand that slanted downhill, as if even his writing wanted to be unnoticed. Charms on strings, charms in boxes, charms so tiny they could be swallowed by accident, which she suspected was the point of them. A city where magic was death made people very interested in magic the size of an eyelash.

Wren sat on a stool behind the counter with her knees pulled up and her chin on them, and she counted the sounds of the building the way other children might count sheep.

*Tap.* Water somewhere in the walls, going from high to low.

*Creak.* The floor above, breathing when the wind pushed at the roof.

*Skkrrrk.* That last one she knew. That one had a name.

"Sable," she said softly, "you're scratching again."

The wall answered with a longer scratch, almost embarrassed.

He came out through the gap behind the flour shelf—a rat the color of wet slate, whiskers bent on one side from some old argument with a trap. He was not afraid of her. None of the rats here were, once they'd heard her speak. Fear was a language too, and she had learned to leave it out of her voice entirely, the way you'd take salt out of bread.

"You're hungry," she said.

*I am always hungry,* said Sable, in the way of rats, which is less words than a shape of wanting. *You are still here. The thin man said you would be gone by moonrise.*

"He says a lot of things." She dug in her pocket for the heel of bread she'd saved, broke off a piece smaller than a coin, and set it on the floorboards. "There's cheese in it. Don't tell the others."

*Too late,* said Sable, and two more shapes poured out of the dark seam under the skirting board—one fat, one young and jittery, both moving with the particular dignity of rats who have decided, temporarily, not to be vermin.

Wren smiled. It felt strange on her face, like wearing someone else's coat. She hadn't smiled much since her village stopped being a village.

The tooth hung against her chest on its leather cord, warm as always, warmer than skin should be. It didn't speak tonight. Some nights it murmured in that old old voice—the one that had called her south through the hills, calling a name over and over until the name became hers to answer: *Marchetti, Marchetti, find Marchetti.* Tonight it only warmed, like a hand resting on her collarbone, patient. Listening, maybe. The tooth listened more than it talked, which she respected. In her experience, most people talked far too much and listened hardly at all, and that was why villages burned.

She fed the rats and let them eat in silence for a while, because beasts do not like to be hurried, and because their eating-silence was comfortable in a way human silence never was.

It was the young one, the jittery one she'd named Pip because he moved like a seed popping, who said the wrong thing first.

*The man with the ledger asks about you again.*

Wren went very still. Stillness, in her body, was total—breath shallow, hands flat, eyes unfocused. It was how you kept birds on your arm. It was how you kept from being seen.

"What man," she said, "with what ledger."

*The man across the street,* said Pip, matter-of-fact, crumbs on his whiskers. *In the room above the dye shop. He has been there twenty-nine days. I count in days because days are when the bakery throws out its ends. He pays us in sausage.*

The word landed in her stomach like a stone.

"Pays you," she repeated. "In sausage."

*Rind and gristle,* admitted Pip, unashamed. *But sausage. There is a man who comes up the back stairs every third night. Heavy boots. He leaves meat on the sill and papers on the table, and the papers have lines on them, many lines, and the man with the ledger reads the lines aloud sometimes when he can't sleep. We sit in the walls and listen because listening is free, and then he gives us meat for telling him things.*

"Telling him what things?"

Pip's whiskers trembled. Rats could not shrug, but this was as close as a rat got.

*What we see. What we hear. Where the smells go. He asks about the shop. About the thin man. About whether anyone comes and goes, and when, and carrying what. And now he asks about you.*

Sable had stopped eating. The older rat's black eyes were fixed on Wren with an expression she could only call pity, though she hadn't known until recently that rats could wear it.

*We did not know, at first,* Sable said slowly, *that telling him was a betrayal. Meat is meat. Walls are walls. But then he asked questions about your smell, little speaker. Your specific smell. Woodsmoke and river-mud and the tooth-thing you wear. And the heavy-boots man wrote it down, and the man with the ledger said—" Sable paused, and when a rat pauses, the pause means something terrible, "—he said, 'So the rumor has feet after all.'*

Wren's hands had gone cold. Not cold the way night was cold. Cold the way the well-water in her village had been cold, the day after the fires, when she'd lowered the bucket past the floating ash.

"Twenty-nine days," she whispered. "Before I even came here."

*Twenty-nine days,* agreed Pip cheerfully, having no understanding whatsoever of what he was saying, *and the ledger man is cross lately, because the thin man buys almost nothing interesting. Flour, lamp-oil, thread, candle-wax. The ledger man says no one lives on flour and thread unless they are hiding, and hiding men are worth money, and children are worth more.*

The tooth flared hot against her chest.

Not speaking-hot. *Warning*-hot. It had done this once on the road, the night before she'd found hoofprints and a burned cart and decided, without knowing why, to walk wide around the main road. The tooth knew things. The tooth remembered things. Right now it remembered something about ledgers, maybe, or about men who paid in meat, and it was pressing its memory into her skin like a brand.

She looked down at it. The wolf-tooth hung yellow-white in the lamplight, cracked along one side, old beyond old. Sometimes, when she held it up to her eye, she saw snow. She didn't know why. Snow and pine trees and a darkness moving between the trees that was not a bear and not a wind but something that loved her, somehow, distantly, the way a mountain loves the valley it stands over.

"Not yet," she told it quietly. "I'm thinking."

The heat faded to warmth. It obeyed her, mostly. She had the feeling it obeyed her the way a river obeys a stone—going around, going along, but never actually stopping.

*Little speaker,* said Sable. *You have gone strange and quiet. Should we not have said?*

"No." Her voice came out rougher than she meant. She softened it. Beasts did not deserve her fear; they had enough of their own. "No, you should have said. You should always say. You did right." She broke another crumb of bread, larger this time, and gave it to each of them in turn, a queen paying her soldiers. "I need to ask you things, and I need you to listen for me now, the way you've been listening for him. Can you do that? Can you listen for *me*?"

Three rat-faces looked up at her. In the lamplight their eyes were drops of tar, and inside the tar there was something old and clever and loyal in the narrow way that beasts are loyal—to whoever feeds them, yes, but also to whoever *speaks*. Nobody else in Vhalder spoke. For twenty-nine days they had taken a stranger's sausage because no better offer existed.

Now a better offer existed.

*Ask,* said Sable.

"The man with the ledger. What does he look like? Truly look—not how humans look to you. How he *is*."

This was the important question. Humans described faces; Wren needed weather. Every person had a weather. Rafael's was gray slate and banked coals. The soldiers who'd come through her village had been iron and wet canvas and fear under the fear. Weather did not lie. Faces lied all the time. Weather never bothered.

Pip answered first, because Pip answered everything first.

*Dry,* he said. *Paper-dry. Like a room nobody lives in.*

*Patient,* said Sable. *Cold-patient. Like a heron standing in water. He does not fidget even when the fleas bite him. Once he sat without moving from dusk to dawn and we thought he had died, and then he turned a page.*

The third rat, the fat one, whose name was Barrel because naming things was a pleasure Wren refused to give up, offered his contribution around a mouthful of bread:

*He smells of ink and silver. Real silver. Under the ink.*

Silver. Wren chewed on that. Silver was church-money. Silver was the Inquisition's color—she'd seen their badges glinting in the firelight, the night her world ended, tall figures in gray with silver sunbursts at their throats, asking questions in voices like closing doors.

"And the heavy-boots man? The one who brings the meat?"

*A hired nothing,* said Sable dismissively. *Beer and sweat and a bad conscience. But he takes orders well and he counts stairs carefully, and he has started counting OUR stairs too. Yours. The shop's.*

The shop's stairs. Their stairs.

Wren slid off the stool and crossed to the window without touching it, standing beside it the way Rafael had taught her in four short days—beside, never in front, letting your eyes pass across the glass at an angle instead of straight through it, because straight-through eyes could be caught by other straight-through eyes.

Across the crooked lane, above the dye shop with its stained awnings, a window glowed dull orange. Candlelight. From outside, it would look like nothing. A worker's room. A tired man.

She could feel him anyway.

That was new. That was the tooth, she supposed, or whatever slept in the tooth and stretched a little further every night. She could feel him the way she could feel weather coming in her teeth—a pressure, a direction, a *there*-ness. One person in that room, motionless. A heron in water. Twenty-nine days of standing in water, waiting for a fish.

Waiting for *her*.

*He watches the door,* said Pip helpfully. *And the back door. And the roofline. He is very thorough. He will be watching until the candle burns out, and then he sleeps two hours and lights a new one. He bought twelve candles on the ninth day.*

Twelve candles. Twelve nights minimum, planned ahead. This was not a curious neighbor. This was not luck.

Someone had sent him.

* * *

Wren sat back down on the stool and put her face in her hands and thought.

Thinking was harder than listening. Listening was just opening doors in yourself. Thinking was building things, and she'd never built anything except snares and lies, and both of those skills felt wrong for this.

Here was what she knew, laid out like beads on a string:

A man had watched this shop for twenty-nine days. She had arrived four days ago. Therefore the man was not watching for her—or rather, he had not *begun* watching for her. He'd been watching for Rafael Marchetti since before she ever knocked on his door with mud on her feet and a dead man's tooth around her neck.

Which meant the tooth had brought her to a man who was already hunted.

Or—

Her breath caught.

Or the tooth had known that too. Had known everything. Had carried her through the hills, whispering *Marchetti, Marchetti*, not because Rafael was safe, but because he was *necessary*. The voice in the tooth had never promised safety. She realized that now, sitting in the amber light of a stranger's shop while a stranger's enemy counted candles across the street. The voice had promised *finding*. Nothing else. Children heard what they wanted to hear, even in the First Tongue. Even especially in the First Tongue.

"You used me," she said to the tooth, very quietly, without anger, the way you'd note that rain was falling.

The tooth warmed, and did not apologize, because mountains do not apologize for standing where they stand.

Down in the walls, the rats waited. They could wait a long time; waiting was a rat's whole profession.

*The thin man,* said Sable at last, gently, in the tone beasts use for broken things, *will be back soon. The baker's boy said he goes to the river district on closing nights. He will walk home through the Tanner's Walk. He walks it the same way every time. Same turns. Same pace. He thinks sameness keeps him safe.*

"Do you think it doesn't?"

*I think,* said Sable, *that the heavy-boots man has walked the Tanner's Walk three times this week, learning it.*

The cold came back into Wren's hands and stayed there.

They weren't watching the shop to learn about the shop. They were watching the shop to learn *him*—his hours, his routes, his habits—so that someday soon, on some ordinary closing-night, a man with beer-and-sweat weather and orders he half-regretted could step out of an alley on the Tanner's Walk and do whatever men with ledgers paid to have done.

Except they didn't want Rafael dead. Dead men couldn't be questioned. Dead men couldn't be *ledgered*.

Dead men couldn't tell an Inquisitor why a child had walked out of the northern hills and knocked on his door speaking a language ten years forbidden.

Wren stood up. Her whole body had decided before her mind caught up—that happened to her a lot; her body was faster and braver and much worse at consequences. She went to the counter, to the drawer where she'd seen Rafael keep his matches, hesitated over the wax-sealed jars (she had promised), and settled instead on the stub of tallow candle and the chipped lamp.

If he came home the same way every time, and the heavy-boots man knew the same way every time, then the only kind thing left in the world tonight was to break his sameness for him.

She couldn't go out. He'd said not to open for anyone, and the door opened onto the lane where the heron watched, and she was four feet of mud-streaked child with a price on her head she'd only just learned the size of.

But she wasn't helpless. She had never once in her life been helpless. Helpless was a word made up by people who had never asked a rat for anything.

"Pip," she said. "How fast can you run to the Tanner's Walk?"

*Fast,* said Pip, delighted, already vibrating.

"Faster than a man with heavy boots walking careful?"

*Much faster. Heavy boots are loud and slow and they step ON things.*

"Then run to the Tanner's Walk and find the thin man. Gray coat, walks soft, smells of lamp-oil and worry. Tell him—" She stopped. How did you say this in rat? How did you compress *a man has been buying our loyalty with sausage and an inquisitor is counting your footsteps and you must come home a different way or not at all* into a shape a rat could carry?

Beasts did not lie. That was the whole gift and the whole burden of it. Whatever she gave Pip to carry would arrive exactly as she packed it.

So she packed it true.

"Tell him: the walls have ears that were sold. Tell him: the man across the street knows his steps. Tell him—" and here her voice dropped into the First Tongue without her meaning it to, the old syllables rising up out of her chest like water finding its level, filling the little shop with a sound that made all three rats go still and made the tooth blaze against her ribs like a coal wrapped in cloth, "*tell him the pack-scents circle, and the den is marked, and the moon is wrong for staying.*"

Silence. Even the water in the walls seemed to hold its breath.

Then Sable, hushed, reverent in the manner of a creature hearing a hymn from a religion older than rats:

*You spoke the Old Words.*

"I speak lots of words." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "Go on, Pip. Run the rooftops, not the gutters. And if a heron-shaped man asks you questions—"

*We don't talk to strangers,* said Pip, deeply offended at the suggestion.

"We don't talk to strangers," she agreed, "unless they pay in sausage," and Pip had the grace to look ashamed for nearly a whole second before joy took him and he poured himself up through the wall-gap and was gone, a ripple in the plaster, a scrabble overhead, then nothing.

Wren stood alone in the middle of the shop with her fists clenched and the tooth burning steady against her heart.

Outside, across the lane, the candle in the dye-shop window flickered once—as if a man had leaned forward suddenly, as if something he'd heard through the glass, some old syllable leaking under a door and up through a wall, had made the heron lift its head at last.

The candle steadied.

The watcher began, very quietly, to write.
