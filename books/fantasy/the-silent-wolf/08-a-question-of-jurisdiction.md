## 8. A Question of Jurisdiction

The file arrived on a Tuesday, which Sera Vance considered an insult. Files like this should arrive in the dead hours, when the archive clerks were drunk or asleep and a woman with the right seal could pull what she wanted without leaving fingerprints. Instead it came by daylight, hand-delivered by a junior clerk who smelled of ink and fear, wrapped in brown paper tied with the grey cord of routine business.

She waited until evening anyway. Patience was the first tool of her trade and she kept it sharper than any of the others.

Her office above the Inquisitorial Annex had one window, and she had chosen the room for that reason. The glass was old, wavy, and gave her a distorted view of Tanner's Row: the crooked shopfronts, the gutter-lamps being lit one by one by a boy on a ladder, and at the end of the row, wedged between a dyer's and a chandler's like a bad tooth between two healthy ones, Marchetti Charms & Minor Repairs.

The lamp inside it had just come on.

Sera poured herself tea she did not drink and opened the file.

Ten years of paper. That was the first thing wrong with Rafael Marchetti—there was too much paper for such a small man. Petty convictions, all of them dismissed, all of them *dismissed*, stacked year upon year like sediment at the bottom of a river. Selling charms without a guild license, 4th Bell District. Unlicensed charm, again. Unlicensed charm, again. A fine here. A warning there. Once, memorably, a charge of *unauthorized thaumaturgical commerce* that had made it all the way to a magistrate before dying of embarrassment.

Every conviction involved charms. Every charm was small. Every case collapsed.

Small was the trick. Small was deniable. A love-charm that didn't work could be chalked up to a fool's hope; a fever-knot that eased a child's night sweats could be folk medicine, grandmother's craft, nothing that would survive scrutiny in a courtroom and everyone knew it, so no one brought it to a courtroom twice. The Inquisition's writ covered great workings—the summoning circles, the beast-speech, the blood-pacts that had started the wars. It did not have teeth for a woman selling luck-knots at three coppers in the night market.

But the pattern. The pattern had teeth.

Sera laid the incident reports out across her desk in chronological order and looked at them the way a hunter looks at ground. She had marked each location where a charm had been seized from a buyer rather than a seller—buyers talked more freely than sellers, and sellers near Marchetti's street never seemed to be found at all. Eleven points of ink on eleven streets. She drew the circle they made with a grease pencil, lightly, so it could be wiped away.

The circle closed around his shop like a hand around a throat.

Either Vhalder's smallest criminals had developed a taste for operating exclusively within forty paces of one particular door, or Rafael Marchetti was the most careful practitioner of illegal magic the city had produced in a decade, and someone—or something—had finally taught him to be careless.

It was not the charms that had caught her attention in the first place. She would admit that, privately, in the ledger she kept in her own head where no clerk could transcribe it. It was the child.

Three weeks ago, a river-warden out past the Marsh Gate had reported footprints on the towpath: small feet, bare, moving fast, heading cityward. Two days after that, a toll-collector swore he'd seen a mud-covered girl slip through the gate crowd "like water through fingers." Then a beggar on Candle Lane told a patrolman, for the price of a bun, that there was a child in the old quarter who *talked to rats*, and the patrolman had laughed and written it down because writing things down was his job, and reports go where reports go.

Reports went to Sera Vance.

Beast-speech was not petty magic. Beast-speech was the First Tongue, or a fragment of it, and fragments were how the whole had always begun. She had read every surviving account of the war years. She knew what the old bonds had grown from—a word spoken to an animal, an animal that answered, a bond that answered back. No one woke up commanding armies. They woke up talking to dogs.

So she had spent three weeks doing what she did best, which was nothing at all, very loudly. She had let her patrols sweep Tanner's Row on their ordinary rounds. She had let the licensing bureau send its ordinary notices. And when the quarterly licensing audit generated its ordinary list of shops due for inspection, and Rafael Marchetti's name sat on it in its proper alphabetical place, she had done the most extraordinary thing available to her.

She had signed the inspection order herself, and put on her plain coat instead of her whites.

* * *

He answered the door before she knocked.

That was the first data point, and she filed it where she filed everything. She had approached quietly—not sneaking, never sneaking, but with a professional softness of foot—and yet the door swung open while she was still three steps away, and there he stood in the lamplight of his own threshold, a lean man of perhaps thirty-five with ink-stained fingers and the carefully blank face of a shopkeeper interrupted at his accounts.

"Inspector," he said, reading the folded paper in her hand before she offered it. His eyes moved over the seal, the letterhead, and back to her face, and she watched him do the arithmetic of it: *licensing bureau, but not a licensing man.* "You're early. Your notice said next week."

"Notice said within the fortnight." She stepped past him into the shop without asking, because asking was for people who expected to hear no. "Inspector Vance. This is a routine audit of licensed practitioners in the ward. Your last inspection is noted as—" she consulted the paper she did not need to consult, "—four years overdue."

"That would be because I'm not a practitioner." He closed the door behind her. She heard the latch click and noted that he left the key in it, facing inward, ready to turn. Another data point. "I sell souvenirs. Knots and tokens. Guild charter 44-B exempts decorative goods."

"Decorative." She walked the narrow aisles slowly, trailing one finger along the shelf edge, not touching anything. The shop smelled of cedar shavings, tallow, and underneath it something faintly animal—wet fur, almost, gone in a blink. "And this?" She lifted a braided cord hung near the door, knotted in a pattern she recognized from her reading as a warding-braid, if you believed the books, which she did.

"Door-hanging. Keeps the rain off the lintel."

"Does it?"

"It's very good at that."

His voice was flat and easy and perfectly bored, and it was all excellent work, and none of it mattered, because she had stopped listening to his answers and started listening to the pauses between them. He paused exactly half a breath before every answer. Not long enough to seem evasive. Just long enough to choose. Innocent men chose too—chose among the true things—but guilty men chose among versions, and the difference lived in the eyes, and his eyes never quite met hers while choosing.

"You keep late hours," she said, turning to face him across the counter. He had drifted behind it, naturally, the way a man takes cover behind furniture he doesn't want to admit is furniture. "Your neighbors say the lamp burns past midnight."

"My neighbors say a lot of things. Half of them are trying to get my lease revoked so the dyer can expand."

"And the other half?"

"The other half buy my knots and don't want their wives knowing what they paid."

There it was—one flash of wit, quick and self-deprecating, a man inviting her to like him. She did not smile. She set the inspection paper on the counter, smoothed it flat, and produced from her coat a second document, thinner, unsealed, the kind of thing that existed nowhere in any record.

"A separate matter. Unrelated to the audit." She let the silence stretch until he reached for it, the way men always did. "In the past month, four separate witnesses in this quarter have reported a child. Unregistered. Traveling alone. One description places her on Tanner's Row itself, as recently as nine days ago."

The pause before his answer was half a breath, same as all the others. But his hands—his hands, resting on the counter in the posture of a bored tradesman—went still. Perfectly still, both at once, the way a body goes still when the mind has shouted an order down every limb at once.

"I run a shop," he said. "Children come in. They steal things, mostly."

"This child wouldn't need to steal. According to one witness, animals bring her things." Sera watched his face with the patience of a woman who had once sat through six hours of testimony from a forger without blinking. "Birds, specifically. A crow left a button on a windowsill where she slept. Do you know many children crows give gifts to, Mr. Marchetti?"

"Crows give gifts to anyone who feeds them. It's in every almanac."

"It is." She nodded pleasantly. "You read almanacs?"

"When business is slow."

Everything about him was slow and reasonable, a pond with no ripples, and everything beneath the pond was moving. She had interrogated smugglers who sweated, fanatics who sermonized, murderers who wept, and none of them frightened her the way competence did. Rafael Marchetti lied the way a mason lays brick—each one checked, each one seated, no mortar showing. But even masons misjudge a line now and then, and she had seen the flaw already: he was answering questions she hadn't asked with details she hadn't supplied. *Nine days ago,* she had said, and his hands had stilled. *Crows,* she had said, and he'd reached for almanacs. A man with nothing to hide matched his ignorance to hers. This man kept correcting the record toward innocence, and only the guilty bothered.

"Here's my difficulty, Mr. Marchetti." She turned the thin paper around and slid it toward him. On it, in her own handwriting, was a list: dates, locations, the eleven points of ink from her desk upstairs, though he would see only a scattering of them. "Unlicensed charms recovered from purchasers in this ward. All small. All deniable. All purchased within"—she tapped the page once—"a short walk of your door. Now, a cynic might suggest that a licensed-exempt dealer in *decorative goods* was supplying working charms and letting his customers absorb the risk."

"Or," Rafael said, "your buyers got swindled by traveling peddlers and are embarrassed to admit where they really bought them."

"Possible."

"Likely, even."

"Possibly likely." She took the paper back, folded it along its original crease, and returned it to her coat, and saw—oh, it was beautiful, it was a gift—saw his shoulders drop a fraction of an inch at the disappearance of the evidence. Relief. A man with clean hands does not feel relief when a list of crimes leaves the room. He feels curiosity. He feels indignation, maybe. He does not feel *relief.*

"I'll note the audit as passed," she said, standing, "pending follow-up. There will be a follow-up, Mr. Marchetti. The bureau is reorganizing. New oversight." All true, and all of it hers, arranged like furniture in a room she wanted him to walk through. "In times of change, it's the quiet shops that get examined most closely. Everyone watches the loud ones. Nobody trusts the quiet ones."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a courtesy." At the door she paused with her hand on the latch, and delivered the last item on her list as though it were an afterthought, tossed over her shoulder, weightless. "If you do happen to see the child—mud on her, wild-looking, perhaps ten years old—you're required by statute to report her to the ward office within one day. Unregistered minors of unknown origin are taken into protective custody pending inquiry into their parentage." She smiled then, briefly, a clerk's smile. "Some of the inquiries take years. The children usually stay with us throughout. It's kinder than the street, most would agree."

Behind her, in the little shop that smelled of cedar and wet fur, Rafael Marchetti said nothing at all.

That was the loudest answer she'd gotten all evening.

* * *

She did not go straight home. Home, for Sera Vance, was a room with a bed in it, and beds were for later. She walked the long way, down through Tanner's Row's back lanes with her collar up and her pace that of a tired civil servant going to supper, and she counted.

Lamp outside the chandler's: lit. Lamp outside the dyer's: lit. Lamp outside the charm-shop: dark.

Dark, at barely the eighth bell, on a trading night, in a shop whose lamp she had been told burned past midnight.

She stood in the mouth of the alley across the lane for the better part of an hour, cold settling into her boots, and was rewarded twice. First, a shape crossed the shop's back window—a second person, small, moving low and quick, gone before the eye could fix on it. Second, and better: the shop's cat, a scarred grey tom she'd noticed on her earlier rounds, came trotting down the alley toward the charm-shop's rear door, and stopped.

Stopped dead, ten feet from the door, ears forward, tail still. Stared at the door the way a dog stares at a stranger's hand. Then it turned around, deliberately, and walked away, and would not come back even when she clicked her tongue at it, though it glanced over its shoulder once—at the door, not at her—with what she could only call, in the privacy of her own skull, *wariness.*

Animals did not avoid Rafael Marchetti's shop. Animals avoided predators, and fires, and—

And things that spoke their language.

Sera walked home through the falling dark with her breath steaming and her mind running clean and cold as a whetted blade. The child was in the shop. The child had been in the shop for days, likely since before the river-warden's report, and Marchetti had hidden her through a licensing inspection while lying to a senior inquisitor with a steadiness most sworn officers couldn't manage under oath. Which meant either the child meant nothing to him—which the stillness of his hands said was false—or she meant everything, and a man who sold three-copper luck-knots had put his entire careful, invisible, ten-year life on the scale against the Inquisition for her sake.

People did not do that for strangers. People barely did it for family.

Which raised the question she had been circling for three weeks, the question she had not written anywhere, the question that would cost her her post if she spoke it aloud in the wrong room: why did Rafael Marchetti care? Why him, of all the doors in Vhalder? A child fleeing something bad enough to leave bare footprints on a winter towpath, guided somehow to one specific shopkeeper on one specific row—and the tooth-shaped pendant the toll-collector swore he'd glimpsed at her throat, glinting like an old fang—

She stopped walking.

She had not written the pendant in any report. She had heard it from the toll-collector herself, in a tavern, off the record, because she had wanted it off the record. *A tooth on a string,* the man had said, *big one, like off a wolf.* And she had carried that detail home and locked it in the ledger in her head, and she had not shared it with her superiors, and she had not asked herself why, and now, standing alone in a freezing lane with the lamps of Tanner's Row glowing behind her, she asked it.

Because a wolf-tooth pendant on a feral child who spoke to beasts, appearing at the door of a shopkeeper whose family name had been struck from three registries ten years ago, was not a licensing matter.

It was a war matter. And the last time the Inquisition had handled a war matter, they had burned a city block and called it mercy, and Sera Vance had read those reports too, all of them, including the ones that weren't in the archives anymore except in the memory of people like her.

Above the rooftops, the charm-shop's lamp flickered back to life—late now, guilty-late, a man pretending he'd been home all evening.

Sera watched it burn and made her decision the way she made all of them: alone, in writing that existed nowhere, in a ledger no clerk would ever see.

Tomorrow she would request the sealed files on the Marchetti family. Tomorrow she would walk past the shop twice, openly, letting him see her seeing him. Let him sweat. Men who hid children made mistakes, and mistakes left tracks, and tracks led somewhere, and she would be there when they did.

But tonight, walking home past the ward office with its statue of the Lawgiver glaring blind-eyed at the square, she permitted herself one honest thought, the kind her training called contamination and her conscience called the truth:

She had looked Rafael Marchetti in the eye tonight and told him the Inquisition took unregistered children into custody for years, and she had watched him understand exactly what she was threatening, and she had felt—for one unprofessional moment, one hairline crack in twenty years of perfect service—ashamed of the weapon she had chosen to be.

Then she went home to her room with a bed in it, and slept badly, and dreamed of wolves, which she had never done before, and would do many times again.
