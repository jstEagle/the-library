## 9. The Bond Beneath the Skin

It began with the smell of rain.

Not rain that had fallen, or rain that was falling—rain that *would* fall, three days hence, over the eastern hills where the river bent toward Greyfell. Rafael knew this the way he knew his own heartbeat: without wanting to, without asking. He stood in his shop at dawn with a bolt of charm-cord in his hands and understood, suddenly and completely, the water content of a sky he could not see.

He set down the cord carefully, the way a man sets down a knife when his hand has begun to shake.

*No,* he thought, in the direction of the tooth, which sat wrapped in three layers of oilcloth inside a locked box beneath his floorboards. *I did not agree to this.*

The tooth said nothing. Teeth do not talk. But somewhere behind his sternum, something old and patient shifted its weight, like a wolf settling by a fire it had no intention of leaving.

***

By midday he had catalogued the damage.

His hearing had sharpened until the market's noise came to him in layers—first the shouting, then the individual voices beneath it, then the pulse of each voice, fast or slow, calm or afraid. He could hear Wren upstairs counting under her breath in the First Tongue, and beneath her counting, her heart, steady as a drum. He could hear the rats in the walls, dozens of them, their small claws ticking through the plaster like rain on a roof.

He could smell fear. That was the worst of it. A customer came in at noon—a fat man with a merchant's smile buying a luck-charm for his wife—and Rafael smelled the lie on him before he spoke it: sour and metallic, fear folded under sweat. The man's wife was not sick. The man intended to sell charms to someone he shouldn't, and wanted Rafael's stock cheap, and Rafael watched himself smile and haggle and take the coin while every animal instinct in him screamed *predator, careful, predator.*

Ten years of practice kept his face still. But his hands wanted to do something else. His hands remembered things he had never learned.

When the man reached across the counter, Rafael's body read the movement before his mind did—the weight shift, the shoulder drop, the angle of approach—and something beneath his skin answered it, a coiled readiness so old and so fluent that Rafael had to grip the counter's edge to keep from moving the way that thing wanted him to move. Not flinching. *Answering.* Stepping off-line, turning his hip, putting the counter between them, his weight dropping to his heels the way a fighter stands who has killed men before.

He had never fought anyone in his life.

"Something wrong?" the merchant asked, and Rafael realized his nostrils were flared, his lips parted slightly, showing teeth.

"Bad egg," Rafael said. "Breakfast." He sold the man his charm at full price just to get him out of the shop faster.

Afterward he stood in the empty room, breathing hard, listening to his own blood move.

This was how it started. He knew the stories—every child of Vhalder knew the stories, though they were told as warnings and never as history. The bond does not ask. The bond *seeps.* It takes the shape of whoever carries it, but it always takes more than it gives, and what it gives is never what you want. His grandfather had been a gentle man, they said, right up until the bond woke in him and he burned a patrol of forty Inquisitors' men to ash in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and then knelt down among them and wept and let them take him away without a fight.

Rafael looked at his hands. They were steady now. Too steady. They had the particular stillness of an animal that has scented danger and chosen, for the moment, not to run.

"I buried you," he said aloud, to the floorboards, to the box beneath them. "I wrapped you in iron and oilcloth and I put you under six inches of oak and you are still doing this."

From upstairs, faintly, Wren laughed at something. And beneath his ribs, the old thing turned toward the sound like a flower toward sun.

***

He tried to starve it first.

Three days of nothing—not a charm sold, not a word spoken to anything with fur or feathers, meals taken standing up like a man expecting bad news. He told himself the senses would dull if he gave them nothing to feed on. He told himself a great many things.

On the fourth day he caught himself tracking a fly across the shop by sound alone, mapping its path behind him, left shoulder, right ear, the precise moment it landed on the window frame—while his conscious mind was elsewhere entirely, weighing herbs. He had not known he was doing it until it was done. That was the horror of it, he decided. Not the sharpness. The fact that none of it required him. Some other tenant had moved into his body and was paying rent in reflexes.

That night he took the box out from under the floorboards.

Wren was asleep. He had checked twice, standing in her doorway longer than a man should stand in a child's doorway, watching her breathe, telling himself this was for her good. The tooth hung around her neck most days; she treated it the way other children treated a mother's locket, touching it without thinking, turning it when she was nervous. But she slept deeply, and she had let him keep it downstairs tonight—"so it can be near you," she'd said, in her careful trade-tongue, with the terrible matter-of-factness of children, "it gets lonely"—and so it was in his hands, and the oilcloth felt warm, which oilcloth should not feel.

The streets were empty. The fog off the river had come up the hill and swallowed the lamplights whole, and Rafael walked through it with the box under his arm and the sensation—unshakeable, absurd—of being escorted. Every shadow at the alley mouths felt attended. Twice he heard dogs shift in doorways, and both times they rose as he passed and stood watchful and silent until he was gone, and he hated them a little for it, and hated himself more for understanding exactly why they rose.

The river was high with snowmelt. It ran black and muscular under the fog, and it made no sound that ordinary ears would catch beyond its general roar—but to Rafael's traitorous new hearing it was a crowd, a thousand small voices, water speaking to water.

Iron first. He had bought it that afternoon from a smith who asked no questions because Rafael paid triple. Two horseshoes' worth, hammered flat. He unwrapped the tooth, and the sight of it stopped him the way it always stopped him: yellowed, cracked, heavy-looking for its size, threaded on a leather cord gone dark with decades of neck-sweat and grief. His mother had worn one just like it. His father too. He had burned two of them himself, in the cellar, the night after, because leaving them intact felt like leaving doors open.

This one he had not managed to burn. Ten years, and he had not managed it.

"Not tonight either," he said, and dropped it in the box with the iron, and nailed the lid shut with four nails, and walked to the middle of the bridge, and threw the whole thing over the rail.

The splash was enormous. To his new ears it sounded almost like a scream.

He stood at the rail afterward, gripping cold wood, breathing fog, waiting to feel lighter. Men in stories felt lighter. Men in stories cut the past loose and watched it sink and walked home clean.

What he felt instead was a silence opening behind his sternum like a wound. And then, filling it, slow and total, a grief that was not his.

It rolled through him in waves—the river's cold, the dark of the deep water, the indignity of iron, and underneath all of it something older and vaster: loneliness. Centuries of it. A patience worn down to its last thread. *We held the terms,* the grief said, though no words crossed the air. *We held them alone, and our bearers died, and we waited, and we waited, and you were given to us and you threw us in a river.*

"I'm not my grandfather," Rafael said through his teeth. Passersby would have seen a madman talking to the water. There were no passersby. "I'm not a treaty. I'm a charm-seller. I make things small. That's all I've ever done, and it kept me alive, and it will keep *her* alive, and you will drown quietly and leave us both alone."

The grief did not argue. That was somehow worse. It simply receded, the way a tide goes out—completely, without rancor, taking everything with it. His senses dimmed. The fog thickened back into mere weather. The city's sounds collapsed into their ordinary distances, and Rafael stood on the bridge feeling suddenly deaf, suddenly poor, suddenly the narrow man he had spent ten years building.

*Good,* he told himself. *Good.*

He was halfway home before he noticed the dog.

It was the stray from the alleys—the one he'd spoken to once, guilty words in the dark, months ago. It fell into step beside him without a sound, and when he glanced down it was looking up at him with an expression he had no business recognizing and recognized anyway: apology.

"What," Rafael said.

The dog looked at the river. Looked at him. Then it did something no ordinary stray should have done—it turned and trotted back toward the bridge, stopped at the rail, and looked at him again. Waiting.

"No."

The dog waited. Fog curled around its legs. Somewhere behind its eyes, something patient and old was looking out, and Rafael felt the hair rise on his arms, because he understood—he understood with total, sickening clarity—that the river had not drowned anything. You cannot drown the sea by pouring it into the sea. The tooth was down there in the dark wearing its iron like a coat it found amusing, and the bond had simply stepped out of it and into the nearest living thing that had ever, even once, listened.

"It's not consent if there's nowhere else to go," Rafael said.

The dog wagged its tail, once. Which was, he suspected, the point.

***

Wren was awake when he got home. She sat on the stairs in her nightshirt, hugging her knees, and she didn't ask where he'd gone, because she already knew. Children like her didn't need the bond for that. They needed only to watch a man's face.

"You tried to give it back," she said.

"The river isn't giving anything back. It's—"

"It doesn't live in the tooth." She said it gently, the way you tell someone their house has burned. "Grandmother's voice told me. The tooth is just... a door that's closed. You're the house." She unfolded herself and came down two steps, small and grave in the lamplight. "You can nail a door shut. Houses don't work like that."

"Wren—"

"It's angry," she said. "Not at you. It's been angry for a long time. It's been *waiting* a long time. And now it's scared, because you keep trying to throw it away and it thinks—" Her voice caught, and she pressed on with the stubbornness of a child determined to deliver a message exactly as received. "It thinks if you throw it away enough times, it'll lose you too. Like it lost the others."

Rafael crouched down to her level. Up close he could smell the river on his own clothes, the iron, the fog—and beneath that, faint as a memory, the smell of pine and blood that belonged to a night ten years gone.

"Listen to me," he said. "Whatever the tooth tells you, whatever voice is in it—there was a man who carried this before me. My family carried it for generations. And the last time it woke fully, it did not protect us. It *marked* us. Forty people died in one night because of what I'm carrying under my skin right now. I am not refusing it because I'm a coward, little one. I'm refusing it because everyone who ever accepted it is dead."

Wren considered this with terrible seriousness. Then she reached out and touched his wrist, right over the pulse.

"That's not what it says," she said. "It says you're the last term of the treaty. And treaties don't get refused, Rafael. They get *kept*, or they get broken." Her hand was very warm. "And it says somebody's coming to break it."

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off in the direction of the Inquisitors' quarter, a bell rang once—the night watch changing—and Rafael heard, beneath the bell, beneath the fog, beneath the whole sleeping weight of Vhalder, the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving in step. Still distant. Still patient.

But not stopping.

He looked down at the dog sitting in his doorway, dripping river-fog, watching the dark street with its ears pricked. He looked at his own hands, steady with a steadiness that wasn't his.

"How long," he said to the night, to the bond, to whatever was listening, "do I have?"

The answer arrived not as words but as knowledge, settling into him like silt:

*Less time than you think. More than you fear. Now sleep, bearer. You will need your teeth.*

Rafael Marchetti stood in his doorway for a long moment, a man deciding—or discovering that the decision had been made for him years ago, in a cellar, by fire.

Then he went inside, and barred the door, and did not sleep at all.
