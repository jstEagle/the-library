## 10. Odile's Price

Odile kept her office above a dye-house in Saltrow, where the air stank of alum and rot and the walls were stained the color of old bruises. Rafael climbed the stairs with Wren's hand locked in his, feeling every board groan under his weight like a man walking into a trap he'd built himself. He had not come here in six years. He had promised himself he never would again.

The door at the top was painted a green that had once been fashionable. It opened before he knocked.

"Marchetti." Odile Vasseur stood framed in lamplight, small and square and iron-gray, wearing an apron over a merchant's coat as if she couldn't decide whether she was a grandmother or a smuggler and had stopped caring which. Her eyes went past him to the girl, lingered a half-second too long, and came back flat as river stones. "You've got nerve, coming to me."

"I need passage out of the city. Two people."

"Do you." She stepped back from the doorway — not an invitation so much as a concession that it was colder in the hall than in her rooms — and let them in.

The office was cluttered the way other people's homes were cozy: charts pinned three deep, ledgers stacked on chairs, a dead stove, a living cat asleep on the one clean surface. The cat opened one eye at Wren, considered her, and did not flee. Rafael noted that. Most animals fled Wren, or crowded her; indifference was new.

He also noted the shutters. All of them closed, all of them barred, and a second door at the back that hadn't been there six years ago.

"You're frightened," he said.

"I'm *careful*," Odile said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm still alive. Sit down. Both of you. And you—" she pointed a thick finger at Wren, "—don't touch my papers. Children who touch my papers end up in them."

Wren sat on the edge of a crate, spine straight, hands folded, watching everything with the stillness Rafael was coming to recognize as her particular kind of listening. He took the stool across from Odile and kept his voice low even though there was no one to hear.

"A woman and a child. North road or river, I don't care which. You move people. That's what I'm told."

"Who told you?"

"The same people who told me you'd charge me more than I have."

Something moved at the corner of Odile's mouth — not quite approval, but adjacent to it. "Honesty. Rare in this trade." She pulled a ledger toward herself but didn't open it. "Here's what honesty costs you, Marchetti. Two berths out of Vhalder, false papers, a guide who doesn't ask names: forty crowns. Cash. Half now."

Rafael felt the number land in him like a stone in still water. He had eleven crowns in a tin beneath his floorboards, and the tin was the whole of ten years' careful, invisible life. "And if I can't pay all of it now?"

"Then we discuss terms." Odile laced her fingers over the ledger. "I know you, boy. Not well — you've spent a decade making sure nobody knows you — but I knew your mother, and I know your family didn't leave you money. So either you've found some way to make coin appear, which interests me professionally, or you want credit, which interests me differently."

"I'll work it off."

"On *what*? Charms?" Her laugh was short and without warmth. "I don't deal in charms. I deal in quiet. In routes. In knowing which gates have bribable sergeants and which have true believers." She leaned forward. "I deal in information. And right now, information is the only currency worth anything in this city, because something has changed."

Rafael said nothing. Beside him, Wren had gone very still.

"Inquisitor Vance," Odile said, as if the name were a card laid face-up. "You've heard of her. Everyone's heard of her since the night market raids started. What nobody's saying out loud is that she's not fishing anymore. She's bought things. Passage lists. Harbor manifests. The innkeeper rolls at the Drowned Bell, the ferry logs at Cinderslip." She tapped the ledger once. "*My* lists are for sale too, Marchetti, if the price is right. Everything is for sale, including the fact that everything is for sale. And a woman and a child traveling together, buying two berths north—"

"That's not what it looks like."

"It looks exactly like what it is," Odile said. "Which is why the price just changed."

* * *

She rose, went to the shuttered window, and put her eye to a gap in the slats out of pure habit before speaking again, back turned.

"There's a herd," she said.

Rafael's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the room. "A what?"

"Don't play simple with me, it wastes both our lives." She turned around. "North past Greyfell, up where the maps go blank because the surveyors stopped coming back. Beasts. Free ones. Not the broken things they keep in the capital menagerie, not the half-starved packs in the border woods. A real herd. Old blood. The last one in the world that never knelt." She sat back down, heavily, folding her hands. "My business is moving people through places the law can't follow. Do you understand what a free beast-herd means to a woman in my trade? Safe roads. Corridors no patrol will ride. The beasts keep their own borders, and the kingdom keeps out of beast country because the last time they didn't, it cost them a generation of soldiers."

"You want me to find it for you."

"No." Her eyes were steady and pitiless. "I want you to tell me how to find it. Because there's exactly one way left in this world to locate that herd, and your family held it, and everyone knows the Marchettis burned, and yet here you sit with a wolf-tooth around a child's neck and a look on your face like a man standing in front of a grave he dug himself."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them the dye-house vats churned, a slow industrial heartbeat.

"Who told you about the tooth?" Rafael asked quietly.

"Nobody had to." Odile spread her hands. "The girl arrived in this city three weeks ago, mud to the eyebrows, alone, asking after a charm-seller by name. Children don't cross four hundred miles of wild country alone. Something brought her. And then she shows up at *your* door, and within a fortnight an Inquisitor starts pulling a decade of files on a shop that has never once given anyone cause." She shook her head slowly. "I don't need to be told things, Marchetti. I need only to count."

Wren spoke for the first time. "You could take us anyway."

Both adults looked at her. She hadn't raised her voice; she rarely did. But there was something in it now that made the cat lift its head.

"Could I," Odile said.

"You help people disappear. We need to disappear. The rest is—" Wren searched for the word, found it, discarded it as beneath the argument. "Details."

Odile stared at her for a long moment, and then laughed — a real laugh this time, startled out of her. "Gods preserve me. Your mother talk like that?"

"She died when I was small."

"Mine too." The humor drained out of Odile's face, leaving the merchant underneath, the woman who had buried a husband, two sons, and whatever softness she'd been born with, and kept working. "No, little one. Details are the whole trade. Here are mine: I don't run cargo I can't pay for. Right now, the most valuable thing in this city isn't gold or silk. It's the location of Greyfell. There are men — powerful men, the kind who fund Inquisitors and buy passage lists — who would empty their vaults for it. If I give you your escape for free and someone learns I had that location within reach and let it walk away, I'm not a smuggler anymore. I'm a liability. Liabilities get reported." She tapped her own chest. "So. The price stands. The herd's location, and you ride north tonight on the best berth I own. Refuse, and I wish you luck, and I forget you were ever in this room — except that forgetting costs money too, these days, so consider that included in what you're already costing me."

Rafael stood. His legs felt distant, like borrowed things. "Then I refuse."

"Rafael." Odile's voice dropped, and for a moment the iron cracked and he saw the woman who had known his mother, who had maybe loved her a little, the way hard women love. "Boy. Look at me. They will catch you. Not because you're careless — because everyone is caught eventually, and the only question is what they catch you *with*. Walk out of here with nothing and you're carrying the herd's secret anyway, locked in that head of yours, and they'll crack it out of you in a room with hooks. Give it to me and it stops being yours to lose."

"That's why I won't give it," Rafael said. "Not because I don't trust you, Odile. Because I trusted someone once with less than that, and a war came out of it."

He put his hand on Wren's shoulder. "We'll find another way."

"There is no other way," Odile said, not unkindly. "That's the tragedy of it. But go on. Go be noble somewhere I can't watch." She turned away, already opening her ledger, already writing him out of her day. At the door, without looking up, she added: "For what it's worth — the ferry logs at Cinderslip were sold Tuesday. Whatever you do next, do it fast."

* * *

The stairs down seemed longer than the stairs up. Wren matched his pace without being told, which somehow made it worse; she understood the shape of the failure better than he could bear to say aloud. Out on the Saltrow street the evening fog was coming off the river, wrapping the lamp-posts in gray gauze, and the smell of dye gave way to the smell of wet stone and fish.

They walked. Rafael didn't choose a direction; his feet chose, taking them down the narrow lanes toward the water, away from the main streets, along the route he'd walked a thousand nights when the world was simpler and his only problem was staying small.

"We can't pay forty crowns," Wren said at last. It wasn't a question. She'd listened to everything, done the arithmetic of a child raised lean, and arrived where he'd arrived.

"No."

"But you could give the woman what she asked."

"Yes."

"And you won't. Because of the war." She said the word carefully, like handling something hot. "The one from before I was born. The one the tooth remembers."

Rafael stopped walking. Fog curled between them. Down at the quay a bell buoy clanked, patient and idiotic, keeping time for no one.

"Wren." He crouched to her level, which was getting harder to do — she'd grown in the weeks she'd been with him, or he'd only just noticed. "What I carry — what the tooth carries — it isn't a map to a herd. It's the key to something the whole world agreed to bury. Ten years ago men came to my family's house for it. My mother burned. My father burned. My uncle lasted longest, and I remember his face when they finally broke him, and I remember that what was in his eyes wasn't pain." He swallowed. "It was relief. Because holding that thing is worse than losing it. And you want me to hand it to a smuggler because she offered us a boat."

"I want us to not die," Wren said simply. "But that isn't what I was going to say."

"What, then?"

She reached up and touched the wolf-tooth at her throat, the way she did when she was gathering courage, and looked at him with those dark, unchildlike eyes.

"You told her only one way left in the world to find the herd," Wren said. "Only the bond can find it. That's what she believes. That's what everyone believes, because everyone thinks beasts are animals that ran away." Her fingers tightened on the tooth. "But beasts aren't lost, Rafael. Beasts *remember*. The crows know every road. The rats know every wall. The fish know every current. Nobody needs magic to find the herd." She lifted her chin. "Somebody just needs to *ask*."

The fog seemed to thicken around the words. Somewhere behind them, up the hill, a whistle sounded — a patrol changing shift, probably, probably nothing — and Rafael found himself listening to it with ears that weren't entirely his own, sorting the sound into harmless and harmful before his mind had decided to try.

"No," he said.

"They'd answer me."

"*No*, Wren." He kept his voice level with an effort that cost him. "Do you understand what you're proposing? Every time you speak to them, you spend yourself. The First Tongue isn't a trick; it's a debt. And there are people listening for exactly that sound — a child's voice carrying further than a child's voice should. Vance doesn't have proof of you yet. Every word you say to the beasts is another thread for her ledger."

"The woman said Vance already bought the ferry logs."

"I know what she said."

"Then we're hunted either way." Wren's voice didn't rise; it *settled*, the way water settles into a channel it has cut over years. "You taught me to stay small. Small in the shop. Small in the market. Don't speak where windows can see. I learned it. I'm good at it." She stepped closer, and the fog parted around her small frame like it was reluctant to touch her. "But small didn't save your family. Small didn't save my village. Small is just dying slowly instead of quickly, and I've done slow already, walking four hundred miles alone with a dead man's tooth telling me your name." She held out her free hand. "I'm not asking permission to start a war. I'm asking to ask a crow a question. There's a difference, and you know it, because you've talked to the alley dogs for ten years and the city hasn't burned."

Rafael stared at her. The buoy clanked. The fog breathed.

She was right, and she knew she was right, and the worst part — the part that made his chest ache like a bruise — was that he recognized the expression on her face. He'd worn it himself, once, at nine years old, explaining to his father that the rules were wrong. His father had laughed and ruffled his hair and died three years later in fire, and the rules had turned out to be right after all.

Except rules made by frightened men, Wren would say, are just fears wearing uniforms.

"Tonight," he heard himself say, and hated how easily he surrendered, and loved her for winning. "One question. To one bird. Away from the shop, away from any window, down by the breakwater where the fog is thickest. And if the crows answer wrong — if anyone hears, if anything goes sideways — we abandon the north road entirely and you let me take you to the Temple quarter and surrender you to the Sisters, and you live small forever. Swear it."

Wren considered the terms with the gravity of a magistrate. "Sworn," she said. "But Rafael?"

"What."

"They won't answer wrong." She turned toward the water, toward the gray dark where the gulls hung like scraps of torn paper, and the tooth at her throat caught the lamplight and gleamed like something newly sharpened. "They've been waiting a long time for someone to ask."
