## 13. The Herd at Greyfell

The road north did not begin anywhere. It simply stopped pretending to be something else.

Wren felt it the morning they left Odile's people behind—a change in the dirt under her bare feet, like a word held half-spoken in a dry throat. The cart track they had followed out of Vhalder's shadow thinned to a footpath, and the footpath thinned to nothing, and still the land pulled them, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.

*This way,* said the earth. Not in words. Words were Rafael's thing, heavy and careful, stacked like the stones of a wall. The land spoke the way she had spoken before anyone taught her to be ashamed of it: in pressure, in lean, in the small tilt of a slope that meant *yes* rather than *no*.

"The map says there's no road here," Rafael said behind her. He had said it three times now, each time a little quieter, like a man arguing with a door he already knew was open.

"There is," Wren said. "It's just asleep."

She crouched and pressed both palms flat against the ground. Under the grass, under the frost-stiffened loam, something old ran through the hills like a vein through a wrist—a road made when the world was younger and roads were promises instead of conveniences. It remembered wheels. It remembered hooves. It remembered feet much larger than hers, four-footed and heavy, walking in procession with banners snapping overhead, and beneath all of it, older than memory: the drum-deep vibration of beasts moving together, many together, toward Greyfell.

"It remembers wagons," she told Rafael, standing. "And before wagons, it remembers them going home."

Rafael looked at the empty hillside for a long moment. He had been doing that more—looking at things she pointed to and not arguing, which was its own kind of miracle, like watching ice decide to become water. Ten days out from Vhalder and his face had changed. The shopkeeper's careful blankness was cracking, and underneath it was something startled and hungry, a boy who had once known how to listen and had buried that boy under ten years of small charms and smaller words.

"Home," he repeated. His voice did something strange on the second syllable.

Wren touched the wolf-tooth at her throat. It was warm. It had been warm since the river, since the night Rafael had thrown it into the black water and it had come back to him anyway, dripping, patient, landing at his boots like a dog that would not be shooed. Since then it had stopped speaking only to her. She could feel it reaching past her now, thread-thin and insistent, plucking at the man beside her the way she plucked at harp strings she wasn't supposed to touch.

He heard it. She knew he heard it, because sometimes when the tooth hummed, his step faltered—half a heartbeat, no more—and his eyes went distant, fixed on some horizon only he could see. And because at night, when he thought she slept, he lay with his hand curled near the pendant where it hung from the tent-pole, not touching it, never touching it, the way you keep your hand near a fire you've sworn off but can't stop warming yourself by.

The tooth had chosen them both. She had understood it for weeks. He still thought it was her burden, her gift, her danger, and that he was merely the escort. Wren was nine years old and had watched adults lie to themselves her whole short life, and this was the biggest one she'd ever seen, and it was happening right in front of her, every day, wearing Rafael Marchetti's tired face.

She didn't tell him. Some things you let a person find on their own, the way you let bread rise.

* * *

The crows found them on the fourth day.

They came down out of a grey sky in ones and twos and then in dozens, settling along the fence posts of an abandoned croft, and Wren knew before they opened their beaks that this was warning, not greeting. Crows always led with the noise they wanted you to hear. These ones wanted silence.

"They're saying *flat-down, flat-down,*" she whispered, dropping to her belly in the wet grass without waiting for Rafael to understand. "Horsemen. Coming up the river valley. Many."

Rafael went down beside her, smooth and fast, and she realized—not for the first time—that under the shopkeeper there was a soldier's body, trained long ago and never fully untrained. His family had been bond-bearers; bond-bearers learned war even when they refused it.

"How many is many?"

Wren listened. The crows' speech was all edges, all counting: *six wings, twelve wings, twenty.* "Ten riders. Maybe more behind the ridge." She frowned, sorting the pictures the birds sent, which came tangled with images of carrion and bright things and the specific pleasure of mobbing an owl. "Grey cloaks. The kind from the city. They have dogs."

Something moved through Rafael then. She saw it happen—a tightening around the eyes, a stillness in the hands—and then, unmistakably, the air *changed*. It thickened. The hair on her arms lifted. Somewhere very far away and very deep, something old turned over in its sleep, and the nearest crow screamed and flapped upward as if scalded.

"Rafael," she breathed. "*Rafael.*"

"I know." His voice was strained, thin. "I didn't—I'm not doing it."

But he was. Or it was doing itself, through him. The tooth burned against her collarbone, and through the link she felt what he was feeling: the riders' positions unfolding in his mind like a map drawn by smell—their horses' sweat, the dogs' excitement, the iron of their weapons—all of it arriving without eyes, carried on currents he had no business sensing. The bond was waking in him whether he consented or not, and it had just done the thing he feared most: it had reached outward, hunting.

"Make them go," Wren said. "The riders. You can—you can push at the dogs, I think. Make them smell wrong."

"I don't know how."

"You do. You just haven't forgiven yourself for knowing."

That landed. She saw it land, saw his jaw work. Then he closed his eyes and did the thing she had described, or tried to—she felt him grope along the bond's new threads the way a blind man feels along a wall, embarrassed, furious at himself, desperate.

Down in the valley, the dogs began to scream.

Not attack-screaming. *Wrong*-screaming. Wren felt Rafael pour a single idea down the invisible threads: *wolf, here, old, huge*—a lie shaped like a nightmare, dropped into the dogs' noses. The hounds bolted, hauling their riders' horses sideways, and the grey-cloaked line dissolved into shouting and cursing and the chaos of animals that had smelled something out of the oldest stories.

When the last rider had fought his horse back down the valley and away, Rafael opened his eyes. He looked sick.

"That was war-magic," he said hoarsely. "What I just did. That's how it starts. That's how it started for—" He stopped. Swallowed the name.

"No," Wren said, with the certainty of a child correcting a foolish adult. "That was a *door* opening. What you do next is yours." She took his big scarred hand in both of hers. "My grandmother said the First Tongue isn't commands. Everyone thinks it's commands. It's not. It's asking so honestly that lying becomes rude. The old gods gave the beasts ears for honesty. That's all the magic is."

"And the war?"

"The war was people who forgot to ask." She squeezed his hand. "Come on. The crows say the ridge path is clear until dusk, and dusk comes early in these hills."

They walked. Behind them, the crows lifted off the fence posts one by one and followed, riding the wind above their shoulders like a ragged black blessing.

* * *

They crossed into the high country on the ninth day, and the land began to speak so loudly that Wren could hardly sort one voice from another.

The stones remembered processions. The rivers remembered oaths—actual oaths, sworn at fords, the words still caught in the water like leaves in a weir, repeating themselves to any ear that cared to listen. The wind over the moors carried fragments of songs in the First Tongue, worn smooth as sea glass, and Wren sang them back half-understood, and the wind bent closer to hear itself in a child's mouth.

Rafael walked half a step behind her now. Always half a step. She had stopped telling him to walk beside her because she'd finally worked out why he wouldn't: he was listening. All day, every day, his head tilted slightly, his eyes unfocused, catching the edges of what came to her whole. He was learning the language the way drowning men learn water—not by study but by necessity—and every mile north, more of it settled into him.

At night he dreamed loudly. She could feel his dreams through the bond, shapeless and hot: burning houses, a woman's voice singing a lullaby in the First Tongue, the smell of smoke and wet ash, a wolf the size of a pony standing over a cradle. His family. The bond-bearers the kingdom had burned. In his dreams the wolf always turned its head at the last moment, and its eyes were amber, and they were *his* eyes, and he always woke before anything else happened.

"You dream about her," Wren said one night, watching the fire. "The wolf."

Rafael fed a stick to the flames. For a long time she thought he wouldn't answer.

"My grandmother," he said finally. "Before. Before everything, she used to sit me on her knee and talk to the kitchen mice. Just talk, nothing useful—weather, gossip, complaints about my grandfather. I thought everyone's grandmother did that. I was six when I learned otherwise." A dry, terrible almost-smile. "I spent thirty years building a life so small that nothing would ever look at it twice. Charms too petty to prosecute. Silence in the streets. And now I can smell a patrol two valleys off, and the ground tells me where the hard road runs, and last week I scared a dozen soldiers' dogs without lifting a finger." He shook his head slowly. "I keep waiting to wake up back in the shop."

"The tooth waited ten years," Wren said. "It can wait a bit longer for you to stop being surprised."

He laughed—short, startled, real. It was the first time she'd made him laugh, and she filed it away carefully, the way she filed all the good things, because good things in her life had always been rationed.

"The matriarch," he said after a while. "At Greyfell. The smugglers' stories call her *the Old Law*. If she asks me what I think she'll ask me—"

"She will ask you to choose."

"—then I don't know what I'll say."

"Yes, you do," Wren said gently. "You just don't know it yet."

* * *

Greyfell announced itself three days before they arrived.

First the game vanished—no deer, no hares, no fox-sign, as if the whole mountainside had agreed to be empty. Then the weather turned theatrically beautiful, which Wren had learned to distrust: the herd kept its approaches swept clear, the way a housekeeper sweeps a step. And on the last morning, the crows left them. All at once, mid-flight, wheeling south as one, their job done, their voices fading with a final chorus that was unmistakably *farewell* and unmistakably *hurry*.

The valley opened before them in the late afternoon light: a bowl of green ringed by grey fells, threaded with a river that ran silver-bright, dotted with white stones that were not stones.

Wren counted forty before she stopped counting.

Beasts such as the kingdoms had sworn were extinct. Great elk-antlered shapes grazing the far slopes. Long-bodied hunters flowing through the bracken like spilled ink. And at the valley's heart, by the river, the herd itself—creatures she had no name for except the ones the First Tongue supplied, names like *long-walking* and *deep-remembering*, vast grey forms with eyes like dark lanterns, standing in a loose ring around a single figure larger than all the rest.

The matriarch raised her head.

Her eyes found Wren first—found the tooth—and held there for three heartbeats with a weight that made Wren's knees soften. Then those ancient eyes moved to Rafael, standing half a step behind, and Wren felt the whole valley hold its breath.

The matriarch crossed the meadow toward them. She did not hurry. Nothing that old hurries. Each step landed like a verdict, and the grass bent beneath her feet not from weight but from attention, and when she stopped before them, close enough that Wren could feel the heat rising off her grey hide, she breathed in—one long, deliberate inhalation, drawing them in through her nose the way a reader draws in the first line of a letter long awaited.

Then she spoke, and her voice was the voice of the land itself, the voice Wren had been hearing all journey long, gathered at last into one throat:

"*Little tongue-speaker. Son of the burned line.*"

Rafael's breath went out of him.

"*You are eleven days late,*" the matriarch said, and her dark-lantern eyes did not blink. "*We have been keeping your seat warm for ten years. Come and see what patience costs.*"

Behind her, the whole herd turned its head as one—forty faces, eighty lantern-eyes, all fixed on Rafael Marchetti—and far to the south, so far that only the tooth felt it first, a cold thread tightened: riders on the move, grey cloaks, and among them a scent Wren had never smelled but knew instantly from Rafael's nightmares.

Iron. Ledger-paper. A woman who believed in her work.

The matriarch's nostrils flared. She smelled it too.

"*Come inside quickly,*" she said. "*Your hunters have found the road that remembers.*"
