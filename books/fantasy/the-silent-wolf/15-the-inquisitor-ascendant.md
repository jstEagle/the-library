## 15. The Inquisitor Ascendant

The summons came at dawn, and it was not written on ordinary paper.

Sera Vance knew this before she touched it. The courier who delivered it to her lodgings in the Inquisition's quarter would not meet her eyes—a junior acolyte with ink-stained fingers and the particular pallor of a boy who had carried something he didn't understand. The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, sealed with wax the color of dried blood and pressed with the sigil of the High Tribunal: a sword through an open book.

Not the Office of Inquiry. Not the provincial magistracy that had funded her decade of patient work in Vhalder. The Tribunal itself. The Conclave of Ashes, as the older inquisitors called it when they thought no one was listening—the inner circle that had been assembled after the Beast Wars to make certain nothing like them ever happened again.

She broke the seal standing in her doorway, still in her nightclothes, and read it twice because the first reading made no sense.

*Inquisitor Vance. You will present yourself before the Tribunal at the ninth bell. Come alone. Come prepared to account for your ledger.*

*Account for your ledger.* Four words that could mean promotion or a cell. Sera had spent ten years learning what fear looked like on other people's faces—she'd built a career out of reading the small betrayals of the mouth and hands—and she recognized it now in the mirror, hers, wearing her own features like a borrowed coat.

She dressed carefully. Grey wool, high collar, the silver pin of her office at her throat. She packed her satchel the way she always did: ledgers, ink, the thin knife she was licensed to carry and had never once needed. Then, after a moment's hesitation, she added the file. Rafael Marchetti's file. Ten years of charm-peddler's convictions, cross-referenced, annotated, annotated again. If they meant to ask her about him, she would not be caught unprepared.

If they meant to take her file from her, she wanted to know what it felt like when they tried.

* * *

The Tribunal met in the old assize courts beneath the Inquisition's citadel, in a chamber built before Vhalder had walls, back when the city had been a market town trading in wool and river salt. Sera had testified there twice in her career, both times from the witness box, both times looking up at the tiered benches where the masked assessors sat. Today she was brought through a different door entirely—up, not down—into the gallery above, where the light came slantwise through windows of smoked glass and everything smelled of candle tallow and cold stone.

There were seven of them. She had expected three.

Six wore the grey masks of senior assessors, featureless porcelain that turned their faces into blanks. The seventh sat apart, at the chamber's head, and wore no mask at all: Lord Chancellor Aurel Denne, who had signed the Edict of Silence thirty-one years ago and whose name appeared in every text she had ever studied as the man who ended the Beast Wars by ending magic itself—or rather, by ending *people*, which was how the wars had actually been fought, though the histories preferred the cleaner phrasing.

He was older than his portrait in the tribunal hall. Thinner. His hands, folded on the table before him, were spotted and precise, a scholar's hands, and Sera found herself unnerved by them more than by all six masks together.

"Inquisitor Vance." He did not rise. "You've been in Vhalder eleven years."

"Ten years and four months, my lord."

"Your conviction rate is extraordinary." He said it without warmth, the way one might note a fever. "Ninety-four percent of your cases end in confession without trial. Your methods have never been formally censured. And your caseload—" he lifted a single sheet, though Sera suspected he had memorized it long ago, "—is dominated by a category we might call *residual*. Small magics. Charms. Hedge-craft. The kind of thing the provinces used to ignore."

"The kind of thing that grows," Sera said. "My lord. Small magics are how large ones hide."

Something moved behind the masks. A shifting, perhaps. Or perhaps only candlelight.

"That," said Denne, "is precisely what concerns us."

* * *

They told her about the rumors the way one tells a physician about a symptom—clinically, with the expectation that she would already suspect.

A shepherd in the northern passes, three weeks dead now, who had sworn before witnesses that he'd seen wolves walking a road in daylight, unafraid, moving aside for a cart as if the cart were the wilder creature. A carter near Greyfell whose mules had refused a ford and then, when whipped, had spoken—not words, the carter insisted, drunk or mad or both, but something *understanding*, a sound that made the whip fall from his hand. A crow that had landed on a magistrate's window ledge in Vhalder itself and stared at him for an hour, and when he'd shooed it, had said—in a voice like gravel and bells—*the tooth is awake.*

The magistrate had reported himself. That was the detail Sera found herself turning over later, the one that lodged under her skin like a splinter. He had walked into the Inquisition's offices and confessed to hearing a bird speak, knowing full well what such a confession meant, because the alternative—to carry it silently, alone, wondering—had been worse.

"And you believe these accounts?" she asked.

"I believe," Denne said, "that for twenty-nine years, the beasts of this country have behaved as beasts behave. Stupidly. Fearfully. At a distance. And in the last month they have stopped." He unfolded his hands and refolded them. "There is an old phrase in the pre-Edict texts, Inquisitor. *When the wolf walks the road, the treaty walks with him.* Do you know it?"

"I know the Edict burned the texts."

"Some things survive burning." He gestured, and an acolyte Sera hadn't noticed in the shadowed corner stepped forward bearing a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth. "Recovered from a smuggler's cache two months ago. Pre-Edict. It describes the bonds—the beast-bonds the old bloodlines carried. It describes what happens when one awakens."

The room went very quiet. Even the candles seemed to lean in.

"It says the land knows first," Denne continued. "The animals know first. The birds, the rats, the wolves—they feel the bond kindle before any man does. What we are hearing, Inquisitor Vance, scattered across four provinces, consistent in every detail, is the sound of something waking up. Something that has been asleep since my generation put it down."

"You believe a wolf-bond exists. A living bearer."

"I believe," said Denne, "that I am too old to believe in coincidences, and too honest to believe in ghosts. Which leaves one possibility, and it is currently walking around my country with a child beside it."

The word *child* dropped through the chamber like a stone through water. Six blank masks watched her. Sera kept her face still, but behind it her mind was moving fast and cold: *he knows about Wren.* Of course he did. The warrant she'd filed—the unregistered minor, the night market raid, the girl who had vanished into the sewers with a charm-seller—reports traveled upward. Everything traveled upward. That was the architecture of the institution; she had trusted it her whole life.

"The child," she said carefully, "was last seen fleeing with a Vhalder shopkeeper. Rafael Marchetti."

"We know." Denne's voice didn't change at all, and somehow that was worse. "We have read your file on him. All forty pages of it. Charm licenses queried and denied. Convictions clustered within a quarter-mile of his shop, every one of them petty, every one deniable, every one—what was your own notation?—*'consistent with concealment of capacity.'*" He looked at her directly, and his eyes were pale and very tired. "You built a case against a wolf-bond bearer over ten years, Inquisitor, without ever once writing the word *wolf*. Why?"

Because if she had written it, someone would have taken the file from her. Because a claim that large, unproven, would have ended her career and started a panic, and panics burn innocent people along with guilty ones. Because she had wanted to be right *before* anyone listened.

"Because the evidence wasn't sufficient," she said. "I don't file what I can't prove."

"No," Denne agreed. "That's why you're here."

* * *

What they gave her arrived in three parts, and each part frightened her more than the last.

The first was a writ. Extraordinary commission, sealed by the Tribunal, countersigned by the Crown's own chancery: authority to command any garrison, search any property, detain any person, in any province, without provincial consent. Authority, in effect, to act as the law rather than under it. Sera held the parchment and understood that she was holding more power than most magistrates saw in a lifetime, and that it had been given to her not because she was trusted but because she was *useful*—a blade doesn't need to be trusted, only sharp and pointed the right direction.

The second was men. Twelve of them, drawn from the Tribunal's own retinue, arriving in Vhalder within the week. They would report to her. They would also, she noted, reading the roster with a professional eye, include three assessors-in-training whose presence served no operational purpose she could see except observation. They were watching her as much as hunting for Marchetti. She had expected that. It stung anyway, somewhere old and unexamined.

The third was the whisper.

It came not from Denne but afterward, in the corridor outside the chamber, from a man she had never seen before—grey-robed, unbadged, the kind of official existence that institutions maintain precisely so they can deny it. He fell into step beside her as she descended toward the exit, matching her pace, speaking low, without preamble.

"Inquisitor Vance. A word about the child."

She stopped. "I don't take words in corridors."

"You'll want this one." He smiled without warmth. "The Tribunal's writ says find the bond and burn it. But there are those among the Conclave—serious people, people with the Chancellor's ear—who question whether destruction is the wisest course. The bond is a weapon, Inquisitor. A weapon that commands every beast in the kingdom. The Edict gave us silence. Imagine what obedience would give us."

Her stomach turned, slow and cold. "The Edict exists because the last war killed a hundred thousand people."

"The last war happened because the bond was *contested*," the man said mildly. "Uncontested, it's simply... administration. Think of it. No more cavalry lost to spooked horses. No more granaries emptied by rats. Armies that move through wilderness untouched because the wilderness has been *spoken to*. One voice, properly trained, properly loyal—" his eyes flicked to her, gauging, "—and the child is young enough to be properly anything."

"She's a person."

"She's a vector," he said, with the patience of a tutor correcting a slow pupil. "So is the man carrying her. The difference between us, Inquisitor, is that you've spent ten years learning to hate magic, and some of us have spent ten years learning to want it." He produced a card—plain, unmarked, a single symbol pressed into the wax of its seal—and set it on the stone balustrade between them. "When you find them, you'll have a choice. Burn them and be celebrated. Or send word first, and be *remembered*. The Chancellor signs your writ. Others sign your future."

Then he was gone, absorbed into the citadel's traffic of grey robes, and Sera stood alone on the stair with the card in front of her and the writ heavy in her satchel and the strange sensation of having been offered a bribe by her own government.

She picked up the card. Turned it over. Blank on both sides but for the seal—a stylized wolf's head, jaws open, rendered in a style she recognized from the pre-Edict woodcuts reproduced in her academy textbooks.

They had chosen the wolf deliberately. Of course they had.

She dropped it into the brazier by the door and watched it curl and blacken, and told herself the feeling in her chest was satisfaction.

It wasn't.

* * *

She rode for Vhalder that afternoon, and the road gave her time, and time gave her doubt, and she had not budgeted for either.

Eleven years—ten and four months—she had believed in the work absolutely. Not the cruelty of it; she had never confused the Edict with justice, exactly. But necessity. Her grandmother had died in the Beast Wars, in the second wave, when the bonded lords had turned the beasts on the countryside and the countryside had answered with fire. Sera had grown up on that arithmetic: whatever the Edict cost, the war had cost more. Magic was a loaded weapon lying in every village square, and the Inquisition existed to pick it up and melt it down before a child found it. That was the story. That was the whole story, clean as scripture, and she had told it to herself through every interrogation, every pyre, every family she had dismantled on suspicion alone, because the alternative to believing the story was to believe she had done those things for nothing.

Now she rode north with a writ in her satchel that let her do anything at all, and a faction's whisper in her ear that wanted the weapon *kept*, polished, aimed—and she found the story wouldn't sit right anymore.

Because here was the thing she could not stop thinking about, the thing that circled her mind like the crow had circled the magistrate's window: they had given her the extraordinary commission *before* confirming the bond existed. On rumor. On birdsong and drunk carters. Either the Tribunal knew something they hadn't told her—which meant she was a tool being aimed by people who lied to her—or they didn't know, which meant they were willing to grant unlimited power on a guess.

And the man in the corridor had known things too. Had known about the child before Denne mentioned her publicly. Had spoken of *training* and *loyalty* with the ease of a man describing a plan already in motion, not a proposal awaiting approval. As if the choice she'd been told she would make had already been made for her, and her only remaining role was to deliver the merchandise.

*Merchandise.* A child. A mud-streaked girl who spoke to rats.

Sera had interviewed the survivors of the night market raid. She remembered one witness—an old woman selling roasted chestnuts—describing the girl: *"Small thing. Wild-looking. Held onto the charm-seller's sleeve like it was the only rope in a flood."* The woman had wept, and Sera had assumed grief or fear. Now she wondered. Now she turned the sentence over and found another reading underneath it: not fear. Recognition. The look of someone watching a child cling to the last decent thing in reach.

Was Marchetti decent? Her file said: careful, disciplined, invisible, possibly monstrous. Forty pages of circumstantial dread. But dread was not evidence, and she had built her career on knowing the difference, and lately everyone around her seemed determined to blur it.

At the third milestone out of the capital, she stopped at a waystation inn, and while her escort watered the horses she stood in the yard and watched the crows on the fence rail.

They watched her back. That was normal; crows watch everything. But they didn't leave when she approached, and they didn't beg, and when she stood there long enough—one breath, two, three—the nearest cocked its head and made a sound. Not speech. Not quite. A low, rattling croak with a cadence to it, almost conversational, as though remarking on the weather, or on her.

*The tooth is awake,* the magistrate's crow had said.

Sera took her hand off her knife, which she had not realized had moved toward it, and walked back to the horses, and did not tell her escort what had happened, because she suddenly understood—with the clarity of a woman stepping off a cliff—that there were things she would now be reporting to the Tribunal selectively, and that this was the first day of the rest of her corruption, and that it had not begun with a bribe or a threat but with a bird making conversation.

* * *

Vhalder received her at dusk, wet with early spring rain, its lamps doubled in the flooded streets. The garrison commander met her at the gate with the writ already read—he'd been sent advance notice—and a map of the city spread on a table in his office, pins marking the charm-seller's district, the sewer mouths, the night market, the last confirmed sighting.

"He's gone to ground," the commander said. "Or gone north. Caravan masters say a woman named Odile bought out three runs northbound this month, all paid in coin, all manifest-light."

"Odile." Sera marked the name. "Find me her people. Quietly. And pull every watcher you had on Marchetti's street—every report since the raid, unedited."

"There's... one item." The commander hesitated, and Sera, watching his hands rather than his face—as she had learned to—saw them go still in a way that meant trouble. "One of my watchers. A rat-catcher we retain on the south quarter. He reports he hasn't had rats in Marchetti's block for three weeks."

"Rats leave buildings all the time."

"Not all of them. Not at once. He says the walls went quiet, like something asked them to." The commander rubbed his jaw. "I logged it as superstition. But you said unedited, so."

Sera stood over the map a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere north, beyond the pins and the ink, a herd of beasts had exhaled in the dark, and a man who sold charms too small to notice was deciding what to answer.

Three weeks of silence in the walls. A child who could ask the vermin to leave. Which meant the girl had known about the watchers before anyone in this room had—had felt the eyes through the plaster, and had simply, quietly, closed them.

They were not hiding from her. They had been *managing* her. A shopkeeper and a child, outthinking twelve years of the Inquisition's machinery.

And the Tribunal's answer to being managed was to hand her a sword with no hilt and a whisper offering to buy the child alive.

She pinned the map's northern edge—the passes, Greyfell, the wild country where the shepherd had seen wolves walking roads—and said, "We ride north at first light."

The commander blinked. "North? The city's the prize—"

"The city is where they were," Sera said. "The crows are going north. The rats went quiet so they could leave. Everything that can't lie in this kingdom is pointing the same direction, Commander, and I have finally learned to trust it more than I trust my own institution."

She rolled up the map herself, and in doing so her eye caught the writ's seal where it lay half-out of her satchel—the Tribunal's sword-through-book, gleaming dully in the lamplight—and she thought of the man in the corridor, and his wolf's-head card, and the easy way he had said *vector*.

She had come to Vhalder believing she hunted monsters. She was beginning to suspect she worked for them.

Outside, the rain thickened. On the garrison roof, unnoticed by anyone but the woman at the window, a single crow shook the water from its wings, faced north, and waited—as if reporting—that she would follow.
