## 16. Betrayal at Greyfell

The crows stopped coming on the third morning.

Wren noticed first. She had taken to sitting on the flat stone above the camp each dawn, murmuring to the birds that gathered there—greyfell crows, bold and ragged, who brought her scraps of the valley's news in their croaking, half-remembered tongue. *Riders on the south road. A fox den robbed. The river running high.* Small things. But on the third morning the stone was empty, and Wren came back down with her jaw set in a way that made her look far older than her nine years.

"They've gone quiet," she said. "Birds don't go quiet unless something makes them."

Rafael looked up from the fire he was banking low, the way Odile's people had taught him—smokeless, starved of light. Around them, the smugglers' camp clung to the shoulder of Greyfell like lichen: four canvas shelters tucked into a fold of rock, a cold-meat cache, a lookout posted at the crag. Eleven people who moved other people for coin, and had moved them now for three days without moving them at all.

Odile stood at the edge of the overhang, her grey braid whipping in the wind, staring down at the treeline as if she could read it.

"Brannick hasn't reported," she said, without turning. "He took the south watch at moonset. He should have come back an hour ago."

"He could be—"

"Don't." She said it softly, and it was worse than shouting. "Don't give me reasons, Marchetti. I've been doing this work twenty years. Reasons are what you tell yourself while you're dying."

The wind came up the valley carrying pine and snowmelt and, beneath it, faintly—a smell Rafael's nose had no business catching from this distance. Iron. Horse sweat. Oiled leather. His heart began to hammer before his mind caught up to why, and when he turned toward Wren he found her already looking at him, wide-eyed, one hand pressed flat against the wolf-tooth at her throat.

"It smells wrong here," she whispered.

"I know," he said. And hated that he knew. For days now the bond had been leaking into him through every crack he'd left open—the sharpened senses, the dreams of running, the way his hand drifted to a blade he hadn't carried since Vhalder. He had told himself he was only listening. Listening was not accepting. Listening could be walked back.

The mountain chose that moment to prove him a liar.

* * *

It started with the mule.

One of Odile's pack animals screamed from the picket line—a high, human sound—and then the world came apart into noise. A whistle from the north crag cut short mid-breath. A crossbow bolt punched through the canvas of the nearest shelter and buried itself in a supply crate with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Then voices, many voices, disciplined and cold, calling out in the cadence Rafael knew from ten years of nightmares:

"Inquisition! By warrant of the Holy Office of Vhalder! Surrender the child!"

They poured out of the trees below in white-and-grey, torches despite the daylight because torches were theater, and theater was Sera Vance's native language even when she wasn't there. Rafael counted twelve, then fifteen, fanning through the camp with the smooth economy of men who had done this before. Two fell on Odile's crew. One went down under a smuggler's hatchet and did not get up. The rest kept coming.

"Wren." He grabbed her wrist. "Behind me. Don't run unless I say run. Running is what they want—they'll loose on anything that moves."

"But the rats—" Her eyes were huge, wet, furious. "There are rats under the snow, they can tell us where—"

"Not now." He shoved her behind the supply crates and drew the only weapon he had ever allowed himself: a skinning knife, four inches of honest steel, legal in any market in Vhalder. It looked like a joke against fifteen soldiers. It was the joke his whole life had been built on.

Odile fought like a woman settling old accounts. He saw her put a knife through a soldier's thigh, saw her take a pike butt across the shoulder and keep her feet, roaring at her people to scatter into the rocks. Three of them made the treeline. One didn't.

And still they came for the crates where Wren hid, unhurried, because they had a ledger and a map and a traitor, and none of those things could be fought with a skinning knife.

A soldier rounded the crates. Young. Visor up. He saw Wren and his face did something complicated—pity and duty wrestling—and Rafael didn't remember crossing the space between them, only that his knife was suddenly in the man's arm and the man was screaming and Rafael was screaming too, wordlessly, a sound that tore out of his chest from somewhere older than language.

The bond answered.

* * *

Later, he would try to describe it and fail. The nearest he ever came was this: imagine a door you have leaned against your entire life, and then imagine stepping aside.

It didn't flood. It *arrived*—the way winter arrives, total and without negotiation. Every scent in the valley sorted itself into meaning: blood, fear, horse, iron, the sour spike of adrenaline on every soldier within a quarter mile, Wren's terror-sweat sharp as vinegar beside him. Sound became geography; he could hear the heartbeat of the man he'd stabbed, fast and stumbling. His night-vision eyes burned gold in the daylight—he felt them change, felt the world sharpen until the trees resolved into individual leaves—and beneath all of it, rising like water finding its level, came something vast and patient and *old*: a pressure of attention from the mountain itself, from the stones and the roots and the things that ran beneath the snow, all of it turning toward him with the terrible tenderness of a god remembering its servant.

*There you are,* said everything at once. *We waited.*

"No," Rafael heard himself whisper. "No—not like this—"

But the soldier was raising his crossbow toward the crates, toward Wren, and there was no room left for refusal.

He moved. That was the only honest word for it. He did not decide to move; the distance simply stopped applying. One moment he was standing, the next he was inside the soldier's guard, hand closing on the crossbow's stock, and the wood *crushed* like bread in his grip. The man stared at him—at the golden eyes, at the too-fast hands—and did the sensible thing, which was to faint.

Another came. Rafael took the pike from him and bent it double. Another, swinging a sword; Rafael caught the blade on his forearm and heard—or imagined he heard—his own bone ring like a struck bell, unbroken, the bond pouring strength into the wound faster than steel could make it. He threw the man into two others. He was aware, distantly, with a horror that sat very calm and very cold at the center of him, that he was not killing anyone. The bond was holding him back from killing, reining him like a trained thing, and that restraint frightened him more than slaughter would have, because it meant this power had *intentions*, had rules, had a shape it wanted to pour him into—and he was fitting that shape perfectly.

"RAFAEL!" Wren's voice, thin with panic. "You're growling—you're growling like—"

He clamped his jaw shut. The sound died. Around him the camp had gone still: surviving soldiers backing away in a ragged half-circle, weapons up but arms shaking, their discipline cracked clean through. One of them made a sound he'd heard once before, years ago, from a man watching wolves burn—a small involuntary noise of animal recognition.

"*Wolf-bonded*," someone breathed. "Gods preserve—it's real, it's actually—"

"Fall back!" the sergeant shouted. "Fall back and mark him! We don't engage a bonded—"

They withdrew to the treeline, dragging their wounded, and Rafael stood alone in the smoking wreckage of the camp with blood on his forearms that was mostly not his own and a silence in his head where, for thirty seconds, a god's patience had been.

It was worse than the fighting. The fighting had been simple.

* * *

Wren reached him before anyone else dared. She crossed the churned snow, took his bloody hand in both of hers, and held it the way you hold something you're afraid will blow away.

"You accepted it," she said. Not accusation. Something closer to grief.

"I didn't—" He stopped. Looked at his own hands. They had already healed; the cuts from the sword-catch were gone, smoothed away, and somehow that was the most obscene detail of all. "I didn't choose. There wasn't time. There's never time, that's how it works, that's how it *got* everyone, Wren—my father didn't choose it either, he just failed to refuse it one day at a time until refusing wasn't possible anymore—"

"The matriarch said acceptance would come," Wren said quietly. "She said it always comes at the moment you'd rather die than lose someone. That's the door. Love is the door." Her chin trembled. "I'm sorry I'm the door."

He crouched and pulled her against him, hard, ignoring Odile limping toward them through the wreckage and the dead lying quiet under the trampled snow. Above the treeline, the Inquisition soldiers were still there—he could smell them, could count their heartbeats if he let himself, which he refused to do, which was itself becoming a full-time labor—and somewhere among them, or coming toward them along the south road, was Sera Vance herself.

Because Brannick had sold them. He understood that now without needing proof; the ambush had known exactly where the shelters were, exactly where the watch was posted, exactly which morning the camp would be fattest and slowest. Odile had read it too. When Rafael found her, she was binding her shoulder with her teeth in the knot, and her face was the face of a woman performing arithmetic she despised.

"Three of my people dead or scattered," she said. "My route burned. My name worth nothing from the Greywater to the coast." She spat. "Brannick got paid in advance, I'd wager. Vance buys good information."

"Odile—"

"Save it." She looked at him—really looked, at the gold still fading from his eyes—and something in her expression shifted from anger to a kind of bleak respect. "Twenty years I've moved hunted people over borders, Marchetti. Never once moved one of *those*. Didn't think I'd live to see it." She jerked her chin at the treeline. "They'll be back by dark with twice the number and fire-pikes. You need to be at Greyfell proper by then, up where the herd waits, because down here you're just a monster in a hole. Up there—" she shrugged, wincing, "—up there you're apparently somebody's treaty. Go. Take the girl. Take my north trail; it's not watched, because until today I didn't know I needed a trail my own people couldn't sell."

"And you?"

"Someone has to bury my dead and find Brannick." Her smile had no warmth in it at all. "I want to have a conversation with him before Vance's people do."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, Wren ahead, translating the mountain as she went—which paths remembered feet kindly, which hollows held hares who had seen white cloaks moving in the dark. Rafael followed in a body that no longer felt entirely like his own. Every stride was effortless. Every breath tasted of information. And beneath it all, patient as bedrock, the bond hummed—not demanding, not yet, just *present*, the way a scar is present, the way a debt is present.

At a bend in the trail, Wren stopped and looked back at him, and the last light off the snow made her squint like an old woman's.

"You're afraid of yourself now," she said.

"Yes."

"Good," said the feral child who spoke to rats. "Grandmother says the ones who aren't afraid are the ones the war uses." She turned and climbed on. "Come on. The herd is waiting, and the tooth says we're almost out of time."

Far below, faint on the wind, he smelled smoke—and beneath the smoke, riding up the valley road, horses. Many more horses.

Coming fast.
