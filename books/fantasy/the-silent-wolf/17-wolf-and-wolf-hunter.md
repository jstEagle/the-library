## 17. Wolf and Wolf-Hunter

**RAFAEL**

They came at dusk, and the mountain told him first.

Rafael felt it through the bond the way he'd once felt rain through an old scar—a pressure, a wrongness, a hundred boots moving up the switchback trails below Greyfell with the deliberate patience of men who had all night and no mercy. Crows lifted off the pines in ragged black sheets, screaming warnings Wren translated with a child's terrifying fluency: *soldiers, soldiers, fire-oil, nets, the small ones in front who smell of iron and paper.*

"The small ones," Rafael said. "Trackers. Vance's."

Wren stood in the mouth of the cave, wind pulling her hair sideways, the wolf-tooth pendant bright against her throat. She didn't look afraid. That was the worst part. Ten days north, and she had stopped being afraid of things that should have frightened her.

"They're not coming for me," she said.

"Wren—"

"They're coming for *you*." She turned, and her eyes were old in a way no eight-year-old's eyes should be. "The crows can taste it. Iron for you. Rope for you. For me they brought—" she frowned, searching the crow-speech for the right shape, "—a box. A soft box. With blankets."

Rafael's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

A soft box. Blankets. They hadn't come to burn a witch-child. They'd come to *collect* one. Alive, unmarked, deliverable. Somewhere in Vhalder a man with a ledger had decided that the First Tongue was too valuable to feed to a pyre, and Sera Vance—Sera Vance, who had hunted him across half a kingdom with the patient relentlessness of winter—had been given new orders.

He looked down the trail. Torches now, threading through the blue dark like embers blown uphill. An hour, maybe less. The bond surged under his skin, offering him things: the exact number of men (forty-one), their fatigue (three days hard march), which of them feared the dark (nine), which feared *him* (all of them, though only three knew why). It offered him their throats like a merchant offering fruit.

"No," he said aloud, to himself, to it.

Behind him, deeper in the cave, Odile's people were already moving—muffled curses, crates dragged, the old smuggler herself barking orders in three languages. But Rafael barely heard them. Because there, at the edge of the torchlight far below, walking ahead of her trackers like a woman out for an evening stroll, was a figure in grey, and even at this distance the bond read her heartbeat as clearly as if she stood beside him: steady. Controlled. And underneath it, so faint he might have imagined it—

*Grief.*

---

**SERA**

Sera Vance climbed the mountain with forty-one men and a lie in her pocket, and she hated every step of both.

The orders had come three days ago, sealed in black wax, signed by Chancellor's warrant rather than Inquisition writ. *Secure the vessel intact. The bearer may be terminated with prejudice.* She had read the word *vessel* four times before the meaning finished arriving, and when it did, she had gone to the window of her quarters and been quietly, thoroughly sick.

She was not a woman who lied to herself. It was the first discipline of her office: see what is, name what is. So she named it. Her order—the order she had given thirty years to, whose ledgers she had kept with the devotion other women gave to prayer—intended to take a child and use her. Not burn her. Worse than burning. Burning was at least a theology; this was logistics.

And Rafael Marchetti—witch, beast-speaker, the last of a slaughtered line—was to die for the crime of standing between them.

"Vance." Captain Hessler fell into step beside her, breathing hard. Young, competent, ambitious. He glanced back at the six figures trailing them in plain clothes—not soldiers, but Chancellery men, soft-handed, with their blank boxes strapped to mules. "Those civilians. What's their function?"

"Observers," Sera said.

"Observers don't carry chains."

"No," she agreed. "They don't."

Hessler waited for more. She gave him nothing. Let him work it out himself, or not. She found she could no longer tell which outcome she wanted.

Ahead, the cave mouth showed black against the last grey light. Smoke had drifted from it all afternoon—deliberately, she suspected; Marchetti was many things but never careless—and the wind carried woodsmoke and something else beneath it, something animal and cold that made the horses stamp and the trackers murmur.

Sera had grown up on the borderlands. She had heard the old stories at her grandmother's knee, before the Inquisition school scrubbed them out of her: stories about the treaty, about the bloodlines who held the beasts' peace in trust, about what happened the last time men tried to take that peace by force. She had believed those stories were ash. All her career, she had believed she was sweeping up ash.

Now she climbed toward a living ember, carrying orders she meant to follow exactly as far as her conscience allowed and not one step further, and she understood—with the clarity that comes only at the edge of treason—that she had no idea what she would do when she reached the top.

That frightened her more than any wolf ever had.

"Hold here," she told Hessler at the last bend. "I go forward alone."

"Alone? Vance, the man burned out a patrol's minds at the river crossing—"

"He did no such thing, because no such thing happened, and if you say otherwise I'll have you flogged for superstition." She unbuckled her sword, considered, and left it on. A truce offered unarmed was a plea. A truce offered armed was a negotiation. She intended, tonight, to negotiate. "Keep the men back. *All* the men. Especially the observers."

"And if he kills you?"

"Then you'll have a very clear picture of what we're dealing with," Sera said, and walked into the dark.

---

**RAFAEL**

She came alone. Of course she came alone. It was exactly the kind of move he would have made, ten years ago, if he had still been the sort of man who made moves instead of hiding from them.

"Sera Vance." He stood at the cave mouth with the bond singing under his skin, and he made himself keep his hands loose and visible. "You've lost weight. The job doesn't suit you."

"Marchetti." Her voice was flat as slate. Torchlight from below caught the edge of her face—sharper than he remembered, greyer. "Where is the child?"

"Somewhere you won't find her."

"I don't have time for theater." She stopped ten paces out—close enough to talk, far enough to dodge, and he noted with grim professional respect that her hand wasn't near her sword at all. "Listen to me carefully, because I will only say this once. My orders changed. There are men behind me who answer to the Chancellor, not the Inquisition. They came with boxes and blankets and sedatives, Marchetti. They aren't going to kill the girl. They're going to *keep* her."

The words landed in the silence like stones in a well.

"I know," Rafael said.

Her eyes narrowed. "How."

"She told me. The birds told her. The rats in the scree told her." He let a little bitterness into it. "Your kingdom is very loud, Inquisitor. You just never learned the language."

Something moved behind her eyes—not doubt; he'd seen her doubt nothing in ten years—but *recalculation*. The recalculation of a careful mind discovering its ledger has a second column.

"You're going to fight," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to keep her free."

"With what?" And then, softly, with terrible precision: "With the bond. I know what you are now. I've known since the river. Forty men down that hill, Marchetti, and you could tear through them like paper—I've read the war-records, I know what your grandfather did at Callow Ford. But the moment you do it, every faction in Vhalder stops arguing about whether you exist and starts arguing about who gets to own what's left of you after. You'll confirm everything. You'll make her the most valuable prisoner in the kingdom's history." She paused. "Is that freedom?"

Rafael said nothing, because the bond inside him was saying *yes, yes, tear them apart, yes*, and he could not let that thing speak for him.

"You came up here alone," he said finally. "Forty-one men, and you told them to wait. Why?"

For the first time, Sera Vance hesitated. He watched her weigh the truth against the habit of a lifetime, watched the scales tip.

"Because my order sent a child-catcher to do an executioner's job," she said quietly, "and I needed to see your face before I decided which of them I am tonight."

* * *

**SERA**

He looked older than his file photograph, and younger than his crimes. That was the thing the ledgers never captured: the files described what a man had done, never what it cost him to do it—or to refuse to. Marchetti stood in the cave light with exhaustion carved into him like weather into stone, and every instinct Sera had honed over thirty years told her two contradictory things at once: *this man is the most dangerous creature on this mountain,* and *this man will die before he lets anything happen to that child.*

Both were true. That was the horror of it. Both were true, and her order's response to both truths was a box with blankets in it.

"The girl trusts you," she said.

"She trusts the tooth. I'm just the address it was delivered to."

"That's not what the smuggler's people say. That's not what the pattern says." She took a breath, and committed herself to the ledge, knowing there was no climbing back. "Ten years, Marchetti. Charms too small to prosecute, sold within fifty yards of your shop, always deniable, always *small*. I built a case on that pattern. I requested your file. I interrogated you in your own shop and you lied to my face badly enough to confirm everything." She stepped closer. "And then I found out what your family was. What the bond is. And I went back through every conviction I ever secured, and I asked myself one question: in ten years of watching you, did Rafael Marchetti ever harm one living soul?"

His jaw tightened. "You burned my family."

"*They* burned your family. I was nineteen years old, signing supply requisitions in Vhalder Central." Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and pushed through. "I have spent my whole career cleaning up what they did, and believing I was. And now my own order hands me a warrant that reads like a slave contract, and you want to know why I climbed this hill alone? Because someone has to decide where the line is, and apparently tonight it's going to be me."

From deep in the cave, a small voice spoke a word in a language that predated every ledger she'd ever kept—and every horse below shied at once, and the night itself seemed to lean in and listen.

Wren stepped out of the dark beside Rafael, small and mud-streaked and utterly unafraid, and looked up at Sera Vance with the frank assessment of a child meeting a large dog.

"You're the wolf-hunter," Wren said—in trade speech, accented, careful. Then her head tilted, and Sera saw her lips move silently, reading something in the air, in the dark, in Sera herself. The child's expression shifted. Surprise. Then something almost like pity.

"You're sad," Wren said. "The beasts thought you'd be cruel. You're just sad."

Sera Vance, who had faced down riots and heretics and her own dying mother without flinching, found she had absolutely nothing to say.

---

**RAFAEL**

He watched the Inquisitor take the blow of a child's pity, and he understood—suddenly, completely, with the bond amplifying every flicker of her pulse—that Sera Vance had just crossed her own Rubicon, and knew it, and was not going back.

Below them, torches began to move. Hessler's voice, tight with nerves, ordering formations. And another voice, silkier, answering—the leader of the Chancellery men, overriding him.

"They're coming up without you," Rafael said.

"They were always coming up without me." Her hand went to her sword hilt now, deliberately, and she drew it—not toward him, but point-down, planted in the dirt like a standard. "Marchetti. I cannot fight my own order. Not openly. Not yet. But I can control the terms of engagement, and I'm about to abuse that power shamelessly."

"What are you proposing?"

"A truce. Ugly, temporary, and deniable on both sides." Her eyes flicked to Wren, then back. "My warrant specifies capture of the vessel—of the *child*—and termination of the bearer. So here is what happens. We go down. I announce that the fugitives fled north ahead of us and scattered the tracks. I arrest *no one*. But if my superiors ask why the man wasn't taken—" her voice hardened, and for one moment he saw the true steel under the tiredness, "—I tell them the wolf-bonded man could not be subdued without unacceptable losses, and the priority target was secured or escaped per circumstances. I buy you the night. Maybe two. After that, I can't protect you from what's coming, and neither of us should pretend otherwise."

"And what do you get?" Rafael asked. Because there was always a price. There was always a price.

Sera Vance looked at him for a long moment, and the torchlight from below moved across her face like water.

"Proof," she said. "That the institution I served my whole life is worth serving. Or proof that it isn't. Either way, I stop having to guess." She picked up her sword, sheathed it. Held out her bare hand. "Truce?"

The bond roared in Rafael's skull—*trap, trap, wolf-hunter, trap*—and he quieted it the way he was learning to, the way Wren had taught him without meaning to: by listening past it. Beneath the roar, the woman's heartbeat stayed steady. Her palms were dry. Beasts did not lie, and the bond was, whatever else, a beast.

He took her hand.

"One condition," he said. "When this ends—however it ends—you look me in the eye and tell me what you decide about us. No ledgers. No reports. Your actual face, saying your actual truth."

"That's a terrible condition," Sera said. "It's the only honest one anyone's offered me in thirty years. Done."

* * *

**SERA**

Going down was worse than going up.

She marched ahead of forty-one men with her heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a door, and delivered the performance of her life: the fugitives fled north at moonrise, the trail split at the scree field, the child was carried—witnesses among the crows' roosts confirmed movement, and yes, sir, the tracks headed for the pass. The Chancellery man, one Aldous Crane, soft-handed and soft-voiced, listened with a smile that never reached his eyes and asked, gently, whether the Inquisitor was quite certain the fugitives hadn't doubled back.

"Certain," Sera said, and met his gaze, and added a weapon she'd never used on a colleague: "Unless you'd care to search this mountain yourself, Master Crane. Tonight. In the dark. With the wolves."

Something flickered behind his smile—calculation, not fear—and she filed it away as the single most alarming data point of her career. This man did not believe her either. He was simply deciding, for his own reasons, to pretend.

"Tomorrow," Crane said pleasantly. "We'll sweep at first light. The vessel cannot have gone far."

*The vessel.* She bowed, precisely, and walked away, and did not let her hands shake until she was alone in her tent with the lamp out.

In the dark she took out her personal journal—unofficial, unfiled, the only honest document she owned—and wrote a single line:

*Tonight I chose a witch and a child over my order, and it is the first night in thirty years I have not been ashamed.*

Then, after a long pause, she added:

*Crane knows. God help us all, Crane knows—and he's smiling. Which means the truce is already dying, and whatever comes at dawn will not be a search.*

She closed the book. Outside, far up the mountain, something howled—one long note, high and clear and unmistakably deliberate, and every soldier in camp sat bolt awake, and no one slept again.

Sera listened to it fade and understood it perfectly. It wasn't a threat.

It was a message, sent through her, to Vhalder:

*We are awake. And we are done running.*
