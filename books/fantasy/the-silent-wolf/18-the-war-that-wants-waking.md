## 18. The War That Wants Waking

The tooth does not sleep anymore.

Wren knows this the way she knows when rain is coming—not with her eyes, not with her ears, but in her back teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the small animal place behind her ribs where all true knowing lives. The tooth is awake, and it has been waiting for a quiet moment to show her what it carries, and tonight, on the third night at Greyfell, with Rafael asleep by the fire and the herd breathing its great slow breaths across the valley, the quiet moment comes.

She should wake him.

That is the first thought. *Wake Rafael.* He has rules about things like this. He has rules about everything—rules about touching the pendant, rules about speaking to the crows, rules even about how loudly she may speak the First Tongue indoors, as though words themselves could break windows. He would want to be here for this. He would want to stand between her and whatever the tooth wants to show.

But the tooth is already showing her.

It starts small, the way all big things start. A warmth against her chest, like a stone left too long in the sun. Then the fire goes strange—the flames lean toward her instead of away, and their crackling turns into something almost like syllables, almost like the old language, and Wren understands that she is not looking at the fire anymore. She is looking through it.

*Little voice,* says the tooth, and it has no mouth, and it speaks anyway. It sounds like her grandmother sounded, if her grandmother had been a mountain. *You asked once why your village burned.*

Wren's hands go still in her lap. She has not asked that question out loud since they fled north. But she has asked it every night inside herself, in the dark behind her eyes, in the place where her mother's face lives now—her mother's face, and the smoke over it, and the way the sky turned orange and stayed orange until the soldiers came and made it black instead.

*I remember,* she tells the tooth. *I remember enough.*

*You remember the ending,* the tooth says gently, so gently, and Wren hates it a little for being gentle. Gentleness from something that old is like gentleness from an avalanche. *Let me show you the beginning.*

* * *

The fire becomes a field.

Not a real field—a memory-field, vast and green, under a sky with two suns in it, which is wrong, which is impossible, except that nothing is impossible here because this is before. This is so long before that the word "before" barely works. The grass is taller than houses. The beasts move through it like weather: wolves the size of hills, stags whose antlers hold actual stars in their branches, serpents swimming through the air the way fish swim through water, leaving ripples in the light.

And standing among them, small as she is now, are people.

People with the First Tongue in their mouths. People with bonds running from their chests into the beasts' chests like ropes of golden light, hundreds of them, thousands of them, a whole civilization braided together man-to-beast and beast-to-man, and Wren's throat closes up because this is what the world was. This is what it was *for*. Not kingdoms. Not walls. Not ledgers full of names to burn. This.

The old gods are there too. She cannot see them properly—they are too large, the way the sea is too large—but she feels them the way you feel thunder through the floor of a house. They are the ones who made the bonds. She understands this without being told. They poured themselves out into the treaty-threads, gave pieces of their own divinity to the bloodlines, said: *hold this. Hold this in trust. As long as the bonds are held, there is peace between my children and yours.*

Then the vision moves, and the peace ends.

* * *

The war comes fast, the way wars do in memories—no years of buildup, just a tearing, a sound like the whole sky ripping along a seam. Kingdoms rise where the tribes were. Kings who were never bond-bearers decide that the bonds should belong to *them*, that divinity held in trust can be seized like grain or gold. They take bond-bearers and they bend them. They learn to force a bonded voice to command what it was never meant to command.

The first beast-god dies screaming, and Wren screams with it, because she is in the memory now, not watching it, and its death goes through her like a spear of cold.

After that, the beasts stop distinguishing between kings and everyone else.

She sees cities fall. She sees the tall grass burn for a hundred miles. She sees wolves the size of hills wading through rivers of their own blood, and men riding them down with weapons forged from a stolen god's bone, and everywhere, everywhere, the bond-bearers—the innocent ones, the trust-holders—being killed by both sides, because both sides understood the same terrible truth:

A bond-bearer can end the war. If one will only say the words.

The tooth shows her those words.

They rise up in the vision like a black sun, coiled and waiting, and Wren recognizes them instantly, the way you recognize your own name shouted across a crowded square. It is the First Tongue. All of it. Every word she has ever spoken to rats and crows and stray dogs, every word the tooth ever whispered to guide her through the dark—all of it is a single sentence, one enormous sentence, and these words are the part at the end. The last clause. The teeth of the sentence.

*Unmake the bonds,* say the words. *Or unmake the world that broke them.*

The old gods built the war-magic into the inheritance itself. Not as a weapon for armies—armies cannot use it, no soldier's throat could shape it, no king's will could carry it. The magic needs a child. It needs a voice that has not yet learned to lie, a heart that has not yet learned to doubt, a speaker pure enough that the First Tongue moves through them without snagging. The gods designed it that way deliberately, in their grief, in their rage, so that no ruler could ever wield the ending—and so that any ruler who pushed the world far enough would find that a child, some child, somewhere, could always pull the thread loose.

A child carrying an apocalypse. That was the design. That was always the design.

Wren sits very still by the dying fire, and the tooth rests warm against her sternum, and somewhere above them on the ridge an old matriarch wolf lifts her head and looks down at the small human shape, and does not come closer, because even the beasts do not know what to do with what she now knows.

*Why show me this?* she asks. Her voice comes out smaller than she means it to.

*Because you will be asked,* the tooth says. *Soon. And you must understand what you are being asked to do—or refuse.*

* * *

She wakes Rafael.

She means to wait for morning. She truly does. But the words sit in her like swallowed stones, and stones want to come up, and besides—Rafael sleeps badly, the way he does everything, carefully and half-braced for disaster, so his eyes open the moment her hand touches his shoulder, and he reads her face in one heartbeat the way he reads patrol routes and market crowds.

"What," he says quietly. "What did it show you."

So she tells him. She tells him in the First Tongue first because some of it doesn't exist in human words, then translates, stumbling, into the trade-speech, and she watches his face go through the same journey hers went through: wonder, horror, understanding, and then the thing underneath all of them, the gray flat thing that is guilt wearing grief's clothes.

"My village," she finishes. "The soldiers didn't burn it because I spoke to animals. They burned it because someone found out what I might become. What any child of the old lines might—" Her voice cracks. "They weren't hunting me, Rafael. They were hunting the *sentence*. The end of it. Before anyone could say it."

"I know." His voice is rough. "I've known for ten years why they came for us. I just never knew—" He stops. Rubs his face with both hands. In the firelight he looks older than ten years older than he did in Vhalder. "I never knew the gods built it that way. That they made it *need* someone like you."

"Someone like me."

"A child. An innocent. A—" He gestures helplessly, and the gesture takes in her whole self, mud-streaked and small and sitting cross-legged by the embers. "The most dangerous thing in the world, and it wears your size."

Wren hugs her knees. For a long time neither of them speaks. Across the valley, the herd shifts in its sleep, a sound like wind moving through a forest that has decided to walk.

"Rafael," she says finally. "The words showed me how they work. The war-magic." She swallows. "It isn't a sword. It's more like... a knot. Everything the bonds are, tied into one knot, and the words untie it. Untie it all the way." She looks up at him. "I don't think it only unmakes the world. I think it could be untied *carefully*. Partway. Like picking one thread out of a braid instead of cutting the braid off."

He goes very still.

"You're saying—"

"I'm saying maybe it doesn't have to be used or buried. Maybe there's a third thing." She reaches up and touches the tooth through her shirt, and it pulses warm under her fingers, almost hopefully. "But I need you to hear the rest first. Because the tooth showed me one more thing, and it wasn't the past."

His eyes narrow. "The future?"

"No. The present." She leans close, and drops her voice, though there is no one for a mile who could hear them except beasts—and beasts do not lie, and beasts have been telling her things all evening, things she hasn't said aloud yet because she hoped she was wrong.

"The crows came in from the south an hour ago," she whispers. "There are riders on the road. Six of them. And they aren't kingdom soldiers, Rafael—they're wearing no colors at all, and one of them is holding something metal that catches starlight, and the crows say they stopped at the crossroads and looked at a piece of paper." Her mouth goes dry. "And Rafael—one of them is a woman, and the others keep calling her *Inquisitor*."

Rafael is on his feet before she finishes, hand already reaching for his pack, and Wren stands too, the tooth burning against her chest like a second heart—and deep inside it, coiled and patient, the sentence waits to see what they will do.
