## 20. Unmaking

**Rafael**

The stairs beneath Vhalder went down farther than the city admitted to.

Rafael had known about the under-ruins the way everyone in Vhalder knew—rumor, mostly, and a drunk mason's story about foundations laid on older foundations. But walking them was different. The stones here were not Vhalder's stones. They were cut too large, fitted without mortar, and carved with lines that might have been decoration until you looked too long and realized they were script. First Tongue, running down the walls like water stains, like veins.

Wren's hand was small and cold in his. She read the walls the way other children read shop signs.

*"It says welcome,"* she said. *"It says it's been waiting a long time."*

"It can wait a little longer," Rafael said, and hated how his voice shook.

Behind them, far above, the night had come apart. He could still hear it if he listened—the bond made his hearing obscene now, sharp enough to pick out individual boots on individual flagstones three streets up. Sera Vance shouting orders in a voice stripped of all its careful patience. The crack of a ward-lance against stone. His name, spoken twice by men who had never learned it politely.

He didn't listen. Listening was how the bond fed him, and he needed to be empty for what came next.

The chamber at the bottom of the stairs was round, and its ceiling was lost in darkness, and in its center stood something that had no business surviving ten centuries of Vhalder being built on top of it: an altar of black stone, uncarved, and above it, hanging in the air like a held breath, a knot of light the color of old blood.

The war-magic.

It did not look like power. It looked like a wound that had never been allowed to close—a tangle of light and hunger coiled around itself, waiting for a voice. Wren's voice. A child's voice, because the old gods had built their last weapon with a lock only innocence could open, and then died before they could feel ashamed of it.

"Tell me again," Rafael said. "All of it. Slowly."

Wren looked up at him. Mud-streaked, exhausted, a wolf-tooth pendant at her throat that had been singing since Greyfell and would not stop. Ten days ago he'd have given anything for silence. Now silence was the thing they were buying.

*"'The bearer may carry,'"* she recited, her child's cadence going strange and formal on the old words, *"'or the bearer may break. What is carried is owed. What is broken is forgiven.'"*

"That's it? That's the whole rite?"

"The tooth says there's one more line." Wren's grip tightened on his hand. "It doesn't want to say it."

"Wren."

Her chin trembled, but she was a girl who had walked out of a burned village alone and crossed half a kingdom guided by a dead woman's tooth, and her chin stopped trembling almost as fast as it started. *"It says,"* she whispered, *"'the breaking cannot be taken back, and the bearer will hear the beasts go quiet forever.'"*

There it was. The price, itemized at last. He had known it since Greyfell, really—the matriarch had shown him with the flat honesty of a creature who had watched empires rot. Break the war-magic and the treaty died with it. The beasts would owe him nothing. No warning, no shelter, no herd at his back when Vhalder came collecting. And the bond itself—

"And me?" he asked. "What happens to the man?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment, listening to something he couldn't hear anymore without opening himself to the bond, which he refused to do. When she spoke, it wasn't translation. It was just a kid telling the truth.

"You'll still be you. Just—" she searched for it, "—quieter. Like a room after the fire goes out."

Above them, faint through a hundred feet of ancient stone, something exploded.

* * *

**Sera**

Sera Vance had spent eleven years learning exactly how much force the law permitted, and tonight she was spending all of it at once.

"Hold this door," she said, "and I will personally see every one of you decorated, promoted, or buried with honors, depending on how the next hour goes."

The six Inquisitors on the stair looked at her. They were hers—or had been hers, forty minutes ago, before the dispatch from the Chancellery arrived with the seal of the faction that called itself the Custodians and orders that used the word *acquisition* four times. Acquisition of assets. Acquisition of the subject. Acquisition, where feasible, of the child.

She had burned the dispatch. Then she had locked the door at the top of these stairs and put herself between it and everyone who wanted through.

"Inquisitor Vance." Deverell, senior of the six, young enough to still believe the Chancellery was the law. "You are instructed to stand aside."

"I am instructed to acquire a child," Sera said. "Read your own orders aloud, Deverell. Slowly. In front of witnesses. See how it sounds."

His jaw worked. Behind him, the corridor rang with distant fighting—she'd triggered every alarm in the district on their way down, and the city watch, bless their corrupt, indifferent hearts, were responding to the noise rather than the warrant. It bought time. It did not buy enough.

"They'll take the bond," Deverell said, quieter now, appealing to the believer in her. "A weapon like that, in the kingdom's hands instead of a fugitive's—that's not evil, that's duty."

"A weapon like that," Sera said, "is why the Beast Wars killed a hundred thousand people, and the only reason it ever stopped is that someone agreed to hold it instead of use it. We are not the ones holding it. Think about what that makes us."

Boots on the upper stair. Many boots. Her former colleagues from the night market detail, arriving late and heavily armed.

Sera drew her ward-lance and set her feet.

"Door stays shut," she said.

* * *

**Rafael**

The altar was warm under his palms, which was wrong, because everything else down here was cold as a grave, and Rafael understood with a lurch that the warmth was the magic recognizing him. Ten years of hiding and it had known him the whole time. The tooth had sung to Wren, but the walls sang to him—had always sung, faintly, in the marrow, in the dreams he'd told himself were just dreams. *Welcome home,* said the blood-colored knot of light. *We waited.*

"Don't listen to it," Wren said sharply. "It talks nice first. The matriarch said so."

"I know. I'm a merchant. I know how nice sounds right before the price." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Gods, Wren. Ten years I built a life small enough to hide in. Charms nobody noticed. Words to stray dogs in alleys, quiet enough that even the dogs pretended not to hear. And the whole time—"

"The whole time you were already carrying it," she finished. "That's what the tooth kept saying. That's why it called me to you. Not to give you something." Her small hand pressed flat against the black stone beside his. "To take something away."

The bond roared up through his arms the moment he truly reached for it—not the trickle he'd allowed himself in the sewers, sharpening senses, bending fear, but the whole drowning depth of it. Ten generations of Marchettis. The treaty-terms written into his family's blood by gods who no longer existed to enforce them. And underneath all of it, coiled tight as a fist, the war-magic: a spell so old it predated language, designed to call every beast within a thousand miles into a single killing purpose. He felt what it would do. He felt how easy. One word from Wren's mouth, channeled through his blood, and Vhalder's streets would run with more than soldiers' blood tonight.

*This is yours,* said the knot of light, tender as a mother. *Take what is yours.*

"No," Rafael said aloud.

And began, with Wren translating line by line, to take it apart.

* * *

**Sera**

They came at her in pairs, the way doctrine taught, ward-lances high, and Sera gave ground the way doctrine taught, trading space for time, and none of it mattered because there were twelve of them and one of her and stairs are the worst place in the world to make a stand unless you're the one standing.

She broke Kessler's lance across her knee and put him down the stairwell with a shoulder-check she'd learned from dockworkers, not doctrine. She took a graze across the ribs that would need real healing and ignored it. She shouted "Vhalder Watch! Raid in progress!" at intervals, because lies cost nothing and confusion cost her enemies everything.

Deverell got past her once. She caught him by the collar at the bottom landing and slammed him into the doorframe hard enough to rearrange his priorities, and for a moment they were face to face, both breathing hard, both bleeding somewhere.

"Why?" he rasped. "You believe. I've *watched* you believe for six years."

"I believe in the law," Sera said. "Not in whoever wrote tonight's orders." She shoved him back up two steps. "Go home, Deverell. Write whatever report keeps you clean. I'll write mine."

From below, through the door she was holding with her body, she heard a sound she had no category for. Not an explosion. Not screaming, exactly—though something in it screamed. It was the sound of a very large thing being unmade very carefully, and every ward-lance in her hand flickered, and every beast in Vhalder went silent at once.

She had grown up outside the walls. She knew what night sounded like: dogs, cats, rats, the constant rustling argument of the city's animals. 

Now: nothing. A silence so total it had texture.

Sera stood in the doorway with her broken lance and her bleeding ribs and felt the silence settle over the world like snow, and thought, with a grief that surprised her by being real: *it worked. They actually did it.*

Then she turned back to the stairs, because the twelve were regrouping, and the job wasn't done.

* * *

**Wren**

The words fought him.

That was the thing nobody said about unmaking. Wren had imagined it would be like pulling a thread, like undoing a braid. It wasn't. The war-magic *knew* it was dying, and it did what any living thing does—it grabbed. It sank hooks of light into Rafael's arms, his chest, the place behind his eyes where the bond lived, and every word Wren translated gave him another piece to tear free and every tear cost blood. Not from wounds. From everywhere. His nose bled. His ears. The corners of his eyes, so that he wept red while he worked, and never stopped working.

*"Say it back to me,"* she said, and her voice only shook a little. *"'What is carried is owed.'"*

"What is carried," Rafael gasped, "is owed—"

*"The debt ends tonight. The bearer forgives it. The bearer is forgiven."*

"The bearer forgives it. The bearer—" He coughed. Kept going. "Is forgiven."

Piece by piece. The calling-word, torn loose and crumbling into ash-light. The obedience-knot that would have bent a million beasts to one purpose, unwound thread by screaming thread. The oldest part, the core, the thing the old gods had whispered their last breath into—Rafael gripped it with both hands on the black stone and *pulled*, and the whole chamber lit the color of dawn, and Wren felt the tooth at her throat go hot, then warm, then cool, then—

Quiet.

For the first time since her village burned, the voice in the wolf-tooth was gone. Not sleeping. Gone, the way a person is gone, the way her grandmother was gone: leaving only the shape of where they'd been.

The light collapsed inward, folded itself smaller and smaller and smaller, and went out with a sound like a sigh, like the last page of a very long book.

Rafael stayed standing for three heartbeats. Then his knees hit the stone floor, and Wren caught his shoulders before his head did, and he was so light, suddenly—heavier than a man should weigh and lighter than a bonded one, all at once.

"Is it—" His voice was a ruin. "Did it work?"

Wren listened. With her ears. With her skin. With the tooth that no longer spoke.

The beasts were silent. All of them. The rats in the walls, the crows above the ruins, the great slow minds of the herd at Greyfell a hundred miles north—all silent, all released, all *free*, which was the same word and a different word. Somewhere out in the dark, animals were waking up to a world with no treaty in it, no terms, no voices. Free, and gone, and never coming when called again.

"Yes," she said. And then, because it was true and he deserved truth: "It's quiet. Everything's quiet."

Rafael started to laugh, or cry, or both, and got as far as making a sound like a snapped bowstring before the door at the top of the chamber burst open and Sera Vance fell through it, bleeding, lance-less, alive.

* * *

**Rafael**

She looked worse than he felt, which was a genuine achievement.

"Twelve of them," she said, leaning on the doorframe. "I've got maybe twenty minutes before they decide I'm more trouble than the door. So." She looked at Rafael—white-faced, blood-crusted, kneeling on an ancient altar floor—and at Wren, whose pendant hung dull and ordinary as a pebble. "Report says the wolf-bond is extinguished. Beasts silent across the entire district. Is that accurate?"

"It's accurate," Rafael said.

Something moved across her face too fast to name—satisfaction, or mourning, or both wearing the same coat. "Then I have a lie to file and a fugitive to lose track of, and you have approximately fifteen minutes to be somewhere I can plausibly claim never saw you." She straightened, wincing. "Odile's people are moving a cargo run out of the Tanner's Gate at midnight. Don't ask how I know. Asking how I know things is my job, and I'd hate to do it."

"Sera." Rafael got upright by way of the altar, which was cold now, finally cold. "Why?"

She considered the question the way she considered everything—thoroughly, and from several angles at once.

"Because I spent six years believing the law was the same thing as the right," she said, "and tonight I read my own orders carefully, slowly, in front of witnesses, and found out which one I'd been serving." She turned back toward the stairs. "Fifteen minutes, Marchetti. The city's still Vhalder. It doesn't forgive. It just forgets—if you're good at helping it forget."

She climbed. The door boomed shut behind her, and a bar scraped into place, and her boot-steps faded upward toward the noise of the world.

Rafael looked down at Wren. Wren looked up at Rafael. Between them, on the black stone, lay the wolf-tooth pendant—cool, silent, and, for the first time in a thousand years, just a tooth.

"We should bring it," Wren said quietly. "So somebody remembers."

Rafael picked it up. It weighed nothing. He put it around his own neck, under his shirt, against his skin, where for ten years he had carried a bond he refused and tonight had finally, at terrible cost, set down.

"Come on," he said, and took her hand. "Midnight's in fourteen minutes, and I've never once been late for Odile."

They climbed together, out of the silence, toward the road.
