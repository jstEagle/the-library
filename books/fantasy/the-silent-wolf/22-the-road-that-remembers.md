## 22. The Road That Remembers

Wren dreamed in the First Tongue again that night, but the dreams had changed.

Before—before the ruins beneath Vhalder, before the screaming death of the bond—they had been full of voices. The tooth's voice, the old gods' voices, the war-magic coiled and patient in its deep dark place, whispering terms. Now the dreams were quieter. A field of tall grass moving without wind. A river she didn't know saying its own name over and over. Her mother's kitchen, which had burned when she was four, rebuilt out of nothing but smell: barley, smoke, wet wool.

She woke on the cart-bed with dawn coming gray through the canvas and Rafael asleep sitting up beside her, his back against the grain sacks, his chin on his chest. He slept like a man who had finally been given permission to. Ten years he hadn't, she understood now. Ten years of listening through walls for boots on stairs.

The cart swayed. Odile was singing under her breath—one of those road songs with no ending, just verses that circled like crows. Somewhere behind them Vhalder was waking to Inquisitors and ledgers and laws about what a person was allowed to be. Wren thought about it the way you think about a bruise: touching it lightly, not pressing.

The dogs said the city still stood. The dogs said a lot of things.

She climbed down over the tailboard while the cart was still rolling—Odile had stopped objecting around the third day—and walked a little way behind it in the dust, because walking was better. Walking meant you could hear the land.

That was the strange thing. The bond was gone. Rafael had broken it apart in the dark beneath the city, and Wren had translated the last of the First Tongue's terrible grammar while the magic died screaming in his hands, and afterward there had been silence—the real kind, the kind that goes all the way down. She'd expected the beasts to forget them after that. To go back to being animals, dumb and separate, the way they were for everyone else.

They hadn't.

The crow found her an hour past sunrise. It came out of a birch stand at the road's edge, dropped onto a fencepost ahead of the cart, and dipped its head once—formal, deliberate, the way a herald bows to someone whose title has been taken but whose name is remembered. *Walked-with,* it said, in the tongue that wasn't a language anymore, only an echo of one. *Unbound. Walked-with.*

"I know," Wren said, and the crow ruffled its feathers, satisfied, and lifted away into the pale morning.

Rafael had seen it too. He didn't say anything—he'd mostly stopped asking how much of it she could still hear—but his hand found her shoulder as she climbed back onto the cart, brief and warm, and stayed there until the next bend in the road.

* * *

They left Odile at the ferry crossing on the fourth day.

"This is where I get paid," Odile said, hauling the cart off the ferry ramp at a muddy village called Brast's Landing, all smoke and fish-rot and geese. "And where you two stop being my problem." But she pressed a cloth bundle into Wren's arms—bread, hard cheese, dried apples wrapped in waxed linen—and cuffed Rafael gently on the ear, which was a thing nobody else in the world would have dared. "You walk north," she told him, "and you don't sell charms to children anymore. I'll know. The dogs will tell me."

"The dogs tell you everything?" Wren asked.

"Everything worth knowing." Odile's face did something complicated—grief and relief wrestling behind a smuggler's flat stare. "Go on, then. Before I decide to keep you."

So they walked.

North, because Greyfell was north, though neither of them said they were going there. The herd would be gathering—Wren felt it sometimes at night, a pressure in the air like distant thunder, the old patience of beasts converging on some purpose no living creature could name. They might go see it. They might not. For the first time in either of their lives, no one was making them do anything, and they were both learning the weight of that the way you learn to carry water: carefully, spilling some, getting stronger.

The road remembered them. That was the other thing.

Wren had known roads were old even before the tooth—had known it the way children know things, without proof. But now she walked on roads that murmured. This stretch had carried pilgrims once, heading south to a shrine that no longer existed; the stones still held the rhythm of their footsteps like a song half-forgotten. That ford remembered a battle so old the names of both armies had worn away entirely, leaving only grief soaked into the clay, and the horses refused to drink there, and Rafael touched Wren's arm and said, "Here?" and she nodded, and they crossed upstream where the water was quiet.

"You hear all of it?" he asked once, that first week, as they climbed out of the river valley into country of gorse and sheep.

"Some. It's louder than it was." She considered how to explain. Talking about it was still new; for years the rule had been *never speak to beasts where anyone can see*, and the rule had lived in Rafael so long it had grown into him like a splinter. "It's like—" She hunted for it. "Like the bond was a shout. And this is people talking in another room. You can't make out the words unless you lean close. But you know someone's there."

"And the language? The First Tongue?"

"Dreams," she said. "Mostly dreams."

He was quiet for a long time after that. Then: "My grandmother spoke it. When she thought no one listened." His voice did the thing it always did around his family—a door opening onto a cold room, then shutting fast. "I used to think it was just her being strange. Singing to the horses in words that weren't words."

"She was holding it," Wren said. "All your family held it. Someone had to, or—" She didn't finish. *Or the treaty dies.* They both knew how that sentence ended now. They'd watched the world hold its breath over it for three weeks in autumn, and then they'd ended the sentence themselves, in the dark, at a cost that was still being counted.

* * *

They fell into a pattern without deciding to.

Mornings, they walked. Rafael bartered small work when they passed through villages—mending, sharpening, once repairing a loom with clever fingers that had spent ten years tying charms too small to notice. He never sold magic now. He never quite explained why, and Wren never asked, but she noticed that when a charm-seller's stall appeared in a market square, Rafael found something fascinating to look at across the street.

Afternoons, Wren talked to whatever talked back. There was more of it than she'd expected. Not speech, exactly—not anymore—but acknowledgment. A fox watching them from a stone wall and dipping its head, *walked-with*, before flowing away. A herd of red deer in a misty clearing going still, then parting to let them pass through the middle, which Rafael called impossible and the deer called courtesy. An ancient dog, grizzled and half-blind, tied outside an inn, who told Wren long rambling stories about his boy and asked her to scratch behind his left ear where he couldn't reach, and she did, and the innkeeper's wife gave them supper free and said the child had a way with animals, didn't she, and Rafael smiled and said yes, she does, and did not flinch.

That was new too. Rafael, not flinching.

One evening in the third week, camped in a hollow under a rowan tree with rain just beginning, Wren heard him speaking low by the fire. She lay still in the bracken and listened, thinking maybe he was praying, which would have been funny given everything.

He was talking to a hedgehog.

"—no, that side's mine, you take the mushrooms, I'm not arguing with you—" A pause. "Fine. Fine. Take the mushrooms."

The hedgehog trundled off into the dark with its winnings, and Rafael sat looking at the fire with an expression Wren couldn't read—something between shame and wonder, like a man discovering a room in his own house he'd boarded up so long ago he'd forgotten it existed.

"You could have just let it eat," she said, from the bracken.

Rafael jumped a full hand-span. "I *knew* you were awake—"

"You knew I might be. You spoke anyway." She crawled out and sat next to him, shoulder against his arm. The rain ticked on the rowan leaves. "You used to never talk where anyone could see."

"No one can see," he said. "That's rather the point of a hollow in the middle of nowhere." But his voice had gone quiet and honest, the way it did late at night. "Ten years, Wren. You spend ten years teaching yourself that the smallest thing you are is the safest thing you are, and then—" He turned his hands over, looked at the palms, at the faint pale scarring where the bond had burned out of him. "Then someone breaks the biggest thing you had, and it turns out the small things were the point all along. Talking to a hedgehog. Scratching a dog's ears. Being—" He stopped.

"Being visible," Wren said.

"Being visible," he agreed, and laughed, short and startled, like the laugh surprised him.

They were visible now. That had been the whole bargain, in the end, though neither of them had signed it knowingly. Sera Vance's report had declared the wolf-bond extinguished and the child dead; somewhere in Vhalder, in some ledger with a leather spine, Wren Marchetti-adjacent was a closed file. But Rafael had walked out of an Inquisition raid alive. Rafael had been seen in the sewers, on the north road, at the ferry crossing, doing small work in villages with his face bare to the world. Sooner or later—months, a year, Wren guessed less—someone would match the dead child's description to the living one walking beside him, or match the fugitive's face to the man mending a loom.

"Not safe," she said aloud, testing it.

"Not safe," Rafael said. "Not hidden." He poked the fire up. "I keep waiting for it to feel worse than hiding did. It doesn't. Isn't that the maddest thing?"

Wren didn't answer, because it wasn't mad at all. Hiding had a taste. She'd tasted it in him every day since the night at his shop door—fear ground fine, like ash in bread. This had no taste yet. This was just open country and rain and a fire, and two people whom the whole architecture of a kingdom had tried to erase, sitting visibly in the middle of it, eating dried apples.

* * *

The dream came back near the end of the month, and this time it wasn't quiet.

She stood on the moor above Greyfell—it was Greyfell, she knew it by the split crag shaped like a wolf's jaw—and the herd was there, spread across the valley floor in the hundreds: wolves and elk and shaggy hill-cattle and things with antlers like winter branches, all of them standing very still, all of them facing the same direction, toward the crag, toward her. And the matriarch—the great gray wolf with frost in her muzzle, the one who had shown Rafael the war as the beasts remembered it—lifted her head and looked straight at Wren across all that distance, which should have been impossible, and said, in the First Tongue, in the true old grammar that made the dream-air ring:

*Treaty-bearer's heir. Unmaker's kin. Come when the snows come. There is a thing we must show the last of you before it is forgotten entirely.*

"What thing?" Wren asked, and her voice in the dream was her own small voice, and it carried anyway.

But the matriarch only dipped her head—the herald's bow, the crow's bow, the bow that meant *remembered*—and the valley began to fill with snow, soft and silent, erasing everything except the shape of the crag and the direction of the herd's attention, and Wren woke with frost on the blanket and Rafael already sitting up, watching her.

"You dreamed about Greyfell," he said. Not a question. He'd learned to read her sleep the way sailors read weather.

"There's something they want to show us. Before it's forgotten." She hugged her knees. "The snows, Rafe. They said come when the snows come."

He looked east, where the sky was going the color of pewter, where the wind had started carrying the smell that meant winter was walking down out of the high passes toward them. He was quiet for a long moment. Wren watched him weigh it—she could almost see the scales, the old instinct to hide pulling one way, the new open country pulling the other.

"They're still gathering?" he asked.

"All autumn. More every week. The crows count them for me." She hesitated, then gave him the rest of it. "There's a white stag with them now. From the far coast. The beasts say the stags haven't crossed the water in two hundred years."

Rafael rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he was smiling—tiredly, crookedly, but really smiling, the expression of a man who has decided to stop pretending he isn't curious about the world.

"Well," he said. "We're not doing anything else this winter."

He began kicking dirt over the fire, and Wren stood and shook out the blanket and shouted good-morning at a pair of jays, who shouted back, unbound and unhurried, and together, visible on the open road, they turned north toward the snows.

The road remembered them going. And this time, so did everyone else.
