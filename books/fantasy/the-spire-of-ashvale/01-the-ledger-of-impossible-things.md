## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things, and I wrote two of them myself.

The first: never write down what you can remember.

The second: never remember what you've written down.

The third — the guildmaster's contribution, delivered over brandy and a cough that had been dying to become something worse for a decade — is that a ledger which cannot lie must at least be able to hold its tongue. Master Corvane said it better than that, of course. Everything sounds better after the third glass of brandy. But the principle stands: our books record miracles, and miracles survive on discretion the way fish survive on water. You can take one out to look at it. Leave it there too long and it dies.

So every morning I climb the stairs to the counting room of the guildhall at Ashvale, light the lamp with a match like an ordinary man — *person*, Wren would correct me, and she'd be right, though "ordinary" has never once applied in either direction — and I balance the impossible against the improbable until the columns agree.

Today's entries, for those keeping score at home:

One bottled sunset, vintage unspecified, sold to Lady Amareth of the Hill Houses so that her dying husband might see the sky turn gold one last time without requiring the weather's cooperation. Price: four hundred crowns, paid in advance, no refunds, as the sunset in question was procured from a summer eleven years dead. The bottle sits on my desk awaiting delivery. It hums faintly when I'm not looking at it, which is either sentiment or static. With sunsets, it's usually both.

Three borrowed years, returned by the widow Sable from her late husband's estate. He'd bought them young, spent them never, and they came back to us slightly moldy, the way years do when left in a cellar. We'll resell them at a discount to someone desperate enough not to ask why they smell of damp and disappointment.

And, under seal, in the locked drawer that only opens for my hand and Corvane's key together: one sleeping god, subletting the crypt beneath the chapel of Saint Verene, rent paid quarterly in dreams. Don't ask. The entry runs to forty pages and half of them are apologies.

This is my life. Ink, arithmetic, and the occasional apocalypse kept in escrow. Seven years I have done this work, and in seven years I have not once called lightning out of a clear sky, not once split a thunderhead to see what it was hiding, not once done any of the thousand things my blood itches to do when the storms come rolling off the western sea like old friends who don't know we've fallen out of correspondence.

I keep ledgers instead. Numbers are honest in a way weather isn't. A number does exactly what you tell it, goes precisely where you put it, and never — *never* — answers back with thunder.

* * *

Wren arrives at midday, as she always does, by the window rather than the door, because doors imply permission and Wren has never in her life asked for any.

"Your sunset's leaking," she says, dropping onto the corner of my desk with the particular grace of someone who has stolen things off higher surfaces than this.

"It isn't leaking. It's *breathing*."

"It's dripping on your amortization schedule."

I look down. There is, in fact, a small golden stain spreading across the column where I'd been reconciling the borrowed years. I sigh and move the bottle onto the stone sill, where it settles into a sulk. Outside the window, Ashvale goes about its business in the gray light — cart wheels, gull cries, the bell of Saint Verene tolling the hour across rooftops the color of wet slate. From up here, four floors above the counting floor, the city looks almost peaceful. Almost lawful. Almost a place where a person could live their whole life without anyone asking what they are.

"You're doing the thing," Wren says.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your eyes go all distant and tragic, like a portrait of somebody's dead grandmother. You're thinking about the west coast."

"I'm thinking about interest rates."

"You're thinking about the west coast *at* interest rates." She plucks the pen from behind my ear — I didn't know it was there; she always knows where everything is, that's her gift, if you want to call larceny a gift, and in the Guild of Impossible Things we're generous with terminology — and twirls it between her fingers. "Rafael. Look at me."

I look at her. This is not a hardship, whatever my dignity claims. Wren has a face like a locked door that keeps getting more interesting the longer you wonder what's behind it. Dark hair cut short and practical, a scar through one eyebrow she refuses to explain — I've heard four versions, all mutually exclusive, all delivered with total conviction — and eyes the brown of strong tea, which is to say they will wake you up whether you asked for it or not.

"The proclamation," she says, and my stomach drops before she says another word, because I know her, and she doesn't lead with a subject unless the subject has teeth. "It went up on the Hall of Records at dawn. And the market square. And the temple doors. And — well. Everywhere."

"Proclamation of what? The levy was settled. I settled it. I have the receipts."

"Not the levy." She sets the pen down, aligning it with my blotter's edge, which is how I know she's choosing her next words carefully, and Wren choosing words carefully is like the sea choosing to be careful — technically possible, deeply unnatural. "A hunt. For the gifted. Crown-sanctioned. Wardens with white cords, registration edicts, the whole architecture." She pauses. "There's a list. Categories. Ranked."

"Ranked," I repeat. My voice comes out very level. Levelness is a skill; I've had seven years of practice. "Ranked by what?"

"By danger." She meets my eyes and doesn't flinch away, which I love her for, and which also means it's bad. "Storm-callers first."

The lamp gutters. Not dramatically — I'm better than dramatic, seven years of practice — but the flame leans, just slightly, toward the window, toward the west, like a dog hearing its name.

"That's superstition," I say. "Storm-calling hasn't been seen since the Sundering. They're ranking us against ghosts."

"They're ranking a list, Rafael. Lists need names to be useful." She slides off the desk and stands close, close enough that I can smell the river-smell the undercity leaves on her, tar and cold water and something underneath that's just Wren. "So here's my question, and think hard before you answer, because I already know the answer and I'm asking anyway. Are you on anybody's list?"

Seven years ago, I walked into this guildhall with forged papers, a new name, and a talent I swore on everything holy and several things profane to bury so deep even I wouldn't know where the grave was. Seven years of matches instead of sparks. Seven years of standing in doorways during thunderstorms with my hands flat against my thighs, feeling the pressure build behind my ribs like a tide that has forgotten the shore exists.

"No," I say. "No list. No name. I'm a bookkeeper, Wren. I'm the least interesting person in this building, and I include the rat in the cellar who may or may not be the sleeping god's accountant."

Something crosses her face then — quick, gone before I can name it, a flicker like heat-lightning, there and not there. Later I will lie awake turning that expression over, wondering what she knew and when she knew it and why she never said. But that's later. In the moment, she just nods, and takes my hand, ink-stained and steady, and holds it the way you hold something you're afraid might blow away.

"Good," she says. "Then let's keep it that way."

* * *

Corvane summons me at dusk. Summons is a strong word; he sends his boy with a note reading *Come down when convenient, bring the god's rent ledger,* which in Corvane's dialect is a summons. I go down through the guildhall as the lamps are being lit, past the great room where the fence-goods sit under canvas — dragon-scale gloves (probably), a compass that points at your regrets (verified), six crates of absolutely nothing that we sell as *mystery cargo* at a markup that would embarrass a bishop — and into the master's office, which smells of brandy, pipe smoke, and old paper pretending to be older than it is.

He's at his desk, and on the desk, face-down, lies a printed broadsheet. I recognize the format. Royal proclamations come to the guildhall twice a year, usually about tariffs. This one arrived today. Wren told me about it hours ago, which means Corvane has known since dawn and spent the day deciding how to tell me, or whether to.

"Sit, Rafael."

"I'll stand, thank you. The god's rent—"

"Can wait. Gods are patient; it's practically their whole profession." He turns the broadsheet over and slides it across the desk. I already know what it says. I read it anyway, because that's the job: you reconcile what is against what should be, line by line, and you don't flinch at the totals.

*A hunt for practitioners of unlicensed art. All persons possessing or suspected of possessing gifts shall present themselves for assessment. Categories ranked by hazard.* And there, at the top of the list, in print blacker than anything I've ever set in a ledger:

*Storm-callers.*

"Old magic," Corvane says quietly. "They haven't used that word in a century. Do you know why a crown resurrects a century-dead word, Rafael?"

Because words are hooks, and hooks are for catching things that think themselves too clever to be caught. Because somewhere in a palace office, someone with good handwriting looked at a map of the Sundering and drew a circle around the west coast, around the storm-country, around the places where the old blood pooled when the rest of it drained away. Because a list is never finished until it has names on it.

"No idea," I say.

He studies me for a long moment. Corvane has eyes like a pawnbroker — everything gets appraised — and I feel the appraisal settle on me, weigh me, find me, apparently, worth the risk. "The guild protects its own," he says finally. "That's the whole reason we exist. Whatever comes of this — assessments, wardens, lists — the guildhall's roof holds. Remember that."

"I will," I say, and it isn't even a lie, yet.

That night I climb back to the counting room and work late by lamplight, because numbers are honest and honesty is a kind of shelter. Below me, Ashvale sleeps. Above me, clouds are gathering off the western sea, fat and electric and full of voices I stopped answering seven years ago.

I am balancing the account of one bottled sunset when the pressure starts behind my ribs — that old tidal ache, patient as debt — and outside the window, far off over the dark water, lightning stitches the horizon once, twice, three times.

Like a knock.

Like something knocking, gently, on the other side of the sky, asking if I'm still in residence.

I put my hand flat on the desk, breathe, and wait for it to pass.

In the morning, the hunt begins in earnest.
