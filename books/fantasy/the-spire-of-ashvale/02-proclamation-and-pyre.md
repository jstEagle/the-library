## 2. Proclamation and Pyre

The bells woke me before dawn, which was the first wrong thing. Ashvale's bells are punctual the way a drunk is punctual — they ring when they feel like it, usually to announce a wedding, a funeral, or a guildmaster's birthday nobody asked to celebrate. But this ringing had teeth in it. Every bell in every spire, hammered over and over, no rhythm, no mercy.

I sat up in my narrow bed above the counting room and listened, and my hands — my careful, ink-stained, unremarkable hands — went cold before my head caught up.

Wren would have said I felt it in my bones. Wren says things like that. She believes bodies know things before minds do, and she is usually right, which is insufferable.

I dressed. I did not run. Running draws eyes, and I have spent seven years being the least interesting man in any room: Rafael Coldharbour, keeper of ledgers, a man whose greatest vice is an attachment to expensive paper. I walked down the stairs, past the sleeping god in his jar — he murmurs when the world tilts, and this morning he was murmuring like a kettle — and out into the street where the proclamation had already been nailed to the guildhall door with four nails, as if one nail might be argued with.

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN*, it began, in letters fat enough to read from horseback. *THE HUNT FOR THE GIFTED IS DECLARED THROUGHOUT THE REALM. ALL PERSONS POSSESSING UNNATURAL POWER SHALL SURRENDER THEMSELVES FOR JUDGMENT. THOSE WHO HARBOR THE GIFTED SHALL SHARE THEIR FATE.*

And beneath that, in smaller script, the part that made my stomach drop through the floorboards of the world:

*STORM-CALLERS TO BE SOUGHT FIRST.*

First. Not witches, not whisperers, not the hedge-healers and luck-benders who'd been quietly ruining dice games since before Aldric wore the crown. Storm-callers. There were perhaps nine of us in Ashvale that I knew of, because I kept the ledger, and keeping the ledger meant knowing. That was the whole point of knowing things: so that no one else had to.

I stood there in the grey light reading my own death warrant, and do you know what I thought? I thought about ink. Specifically, I thought about the fact that iron-gall ink fades if you treat it right, and that the ledger pages bearing nine names could be made to look like honest accounting errors by noon, provided nobody had looked at them yet.

Provided nobody had looked at them yet.

I went inside at a pace I allowed myself to call brisk.

* * *

The counting room smelled of tallow and dust, as sanctuaries should. I lit no lamp — daylight through the high window was enough, and lamplight invites witnesses — and I unlocked the great cabinet where the Ledger of Impossible Things slept under three locks and a blessing from a priest we paid not to ask questions.

The ledger itself is a monster. Half a century of miracles, entered in my predecessor's crabbed hand and then mine: bottled sunsets, debited and credited. Borrowed years, with interest schedules I invented myself and rather pride myself on. The sleeping god, carried as a long-term asset with depreciation I refuse to discuss. And in the back third, unmarked, interleaved between grain contracts for the winter fair — the names.

Not just mine. Everyone's. The hedge-witch Maren who cured fevers for a copper. The twins on Tanner's Row who could speak to rats, uselessly but sweetly. Old Corvin, who could make a candle burn blue and swore it was a trick until the night he made a whole chandelier burn blue. Nine names. Nine small lives I had promised, silently, in the privacy of my own skull, to keep.

I turned to the pages.

They were gone.

Not the pages themselves — those were still there, still sewn into the binding, still carrying my neat entries. But the *names*. Gone. Cut out with a blade so sharp it had taken the margins too, leaving windows in the paper where people used to be. My own name, Rafael Coldharbour, storm-caller, exile, accountant — excised like a stain.

For a long moment I simply stood there holding a book full of holes, doing the arithmetic I am apparently constitutionally incapable of avoiding. Nine names. One knife. One person with keys to all three locks besides me.

One guildmaster.

Master Oren Vail. Seventy years old, half-blind, fond of honeyed wine and saying things like "the Guild endures, child" in a voice like a warm bath. He had taken me in when I crossed the mountains with frostbite and a secret. He had taught me double-entry bookkeeping and told me that the guild existed precisely so that the impossible could happen *safely* — off the books, out of sight, beneath the notice of kings.

And sometime between the crown's decree and this grey morning, he had opened the cabinet I trusted him with and cut out the people I had sworn to keep.

I want to tell you I raged. I want to report some grand and cinematic fury. What I actually did was sit down on the stool, very carefully, the way you do when standing has become unreliable, and think: *he sold them already, or he will by sundown, and either way the market square will fill up today.*

Because that was what the bells meant. Bells like that mean an execution, and executions need an example, and examples need a name, and names need a seller.

I took out my penknife and began destroying everything else. My hand was steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Page by page, I fed seven years of my own careful life into the stove — the entries in my hand, the marginal notes only I could read, the little code marks that mapped which client owed what miracle. If the ledger was going to become a kill-list, it would do so missing its index. It felt less like burning evidence and more like burning a diary, which is to say it felt like burning myself, and I kept doing it anyway, because sentiment is a luxury for people who aren't first on a list.

"You're awake early."

Wren stood in the doorway with two cups of chicory and the particular stillness she gets when she's been listening longer than she admits. She set the cups down, looked at the stove, at the ash, at the ledger with its ragged wounds, and her face did something complicated and then settled into the expression she wears for bad news received in advance.

"How much did you hear?" I asked.

"The bells." She came closer. "And now this." She touched the cut edges of a page, gentle as a nurse. "Rafael. Who?"

"Who do you think? How many people can open this cabinet?"

"Two," she said. Then, after a pause that lasted approximately one betrayal: "Three. He gave me a key last spring. Said the guild couldn't afford to have its secrets die with one man." She laughed, short and ugly. "He was grooming successors. Or accomplices."

"Wren—"

"I didn't look. I never looked." Her jaw tightened. "I'm telling you that because it matters to me that you believe it, not because it matters to anything else."

It mattered to me. I filed that away to examine later, in a safer life, along with several other things I felt about Wren that I had spent seven years not examining, on the theory that a man hiding a storm in his chest shouldn't also carry weather in his heart. Very disciplined of me. It had worked beautifully right up until this exact morning.

"We have to warn them," I said. "Maren. The twins. Corvin. Before the wardens get their names."

"Rafael." Wren put her hand flat on the mutilated page. "Listen."

And underneath our voices, underneath the dying bells, I heard it: the crowd. A crowd doesn't sound like anything until it sounds like *this* — a low animal hum with a hunger in it, coming from the direction of the market square. Coming, I understood with a clarity I would gladly have returned, from exactly where a proclamation had promised the realm its first example.

We ran. I'll admit it; discretion died somewhere around the second stair.

* * *

The market square had been built for commerce and repurposed, that morning, for theater. A pyre stood where the fishmongers usually did, stacked neat and professional — someone had done this before, elsewhere, and brought their craft north with them. Wardens in crown grey held the perimeter. And on the pyre, tied to a post much too large for him, stood a boy.

He couldn't have been more than twelve. Skinny. Barefoot, though it was autumn. His hair had been shaved off — wardens do that, I learned later; they say gifted hair holds power, which tells you what passes for scholarship in the hunt's command. He wasn't crying. He was looking out over the crowd with the bewildered patience of a child waiting for an adult to fix something, and every adult in that square was looking anywhere else.

I knew his face. Of course I knew his face. Tam. Tanner's Row. Rat-speaker. He used to bring messages to the guildhall from the rats — actual messages, rat gossip, mostly about food and cats, delivered with tremendous gravity — because he thought it made him important. Because we let him think it. Because I entered him in the ledger under *minor assets, non-monetized* and thought that was kindness.

A crier read the charges. The words were the usual: unnatural power, corruption of the commonweal, defiance of the crown's mercy. The charge that undid me was *concealment within the guild of Impossible Things.* They said it plainly, publicly. The crowd heard *guild* and muttered, and I watched seventy years of shelter curdle into suspicion in real time. Oren hadn't just sold names. He'd bought absolution with the guild itself as the coin.

"They copied the pages," Wren said beside me. Her voice was flat, which is how she sounds when she is keeping herself together by main force. "That's why he cut them out. Clean copy first, destroy the original. No trail back to his desk."

Tam saw us. I don't know how, in all those faces, but he did — maybe the rats told him, maybe children simply know — and for one horrible moment his eyes found mine across the square and brightened, because he recognized a friend. Because he thought we were here to fix something.

Then they lit the pitch.

I am going to skip what happened next. Not because I don't remember it — I remember every second with the awful fidelity of a man who keeps accounts — but because some things should not be written down twice. I will say only this: the crowd made a sound I had never heard a crowd make, and would give a great deal never to hear again. And I stood there in the smoke with my gift clawing up my throat like a caged animal, the sky above Ashvale going bruise-colored without my permission, thunder muttering somewhere over the mountains as if the weather itself had opinions — and I did *nothing.* I hid. In front of a burning child, I chose my skin, because that is what I have trained myself to choose every day for seven years, and I discovered in that moment that training is not the same as peace.

Wren's hand closed on my wrist. Hard. "Not here," she breathed. "Not like this. He wouldn't want you to burn too."

She pulled me back into the press of bodies, away from the smoke, and I let her, and I will answer for that someday, in whatever ledger exists for men like me.

By the time we reached the guildhall, the sky had begun to spit rain — my rain, I suspected, leaking out of me sideways. The doors stood open. Inside, Master Oren sat at the long table with a cup of honeyed wine and four grey-clad wardens, and on the table between them lay a fresh copy of the ledger's stolen pages, and he was pointing, with a steady hand, at the line where my name had been.

He looked up. Our eyes met. The oldest man I loved gave me a small, apologetic shrug — *the Guild endures, child* — and said, in a voice pitched perfectly to carry:

"There. That one. The tall fellow by the door. He's the storm-caller."

Wren grabbed my sleeve, and we ran, out the back, into the ash-choked streets of the only home I had left — with the hunt's horns waking behind us like the world's most punctual bells.
