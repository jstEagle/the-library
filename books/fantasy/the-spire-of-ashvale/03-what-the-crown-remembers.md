## 3. What the Crown Remembers

There are three ways out of the guildhall, and Rafael knew only two of them.

I knew the third because I'd built it — a crawl-space behind the wine racks that let out into a cooper's cellar on Tallow Lane, then down through the cisterns where the city kept its oldest secrets drowned. I'd used it twice before, both times for other people's escapes. Fences learn the architecture of leaving the way priests learn prayer: not because we expect to need it, but because the day we don't know it is the day we die of our ignorance.

So when Rafael came through my door with soot on their cuffs and betrayal in their face, holding a ledger page like it had bitten them, I didn't ask questions. I took their wrist, said "wine cellar," and pulled.

They followed. That was the first miracle of the night, and the Guild of Impossible Things keeps no account of those.

* * *

The undercity smells the way old grief smells — wet stone, lamp oil, the ghost of everything that ever rotted above it. We went down through the cisterns by lamplight shielded with my coat, and Rafael walked like a person being carried rather than walking, which worried me more than any warden could have.

"Say something," I whispered, when we'd put three archways between us and Tallow Lane.

"They copied my pages." Their voice came out flat as coin. "Every name. Every entry in my hand. The guildmaster has been selling us by weight, Wren. Like flour."

"I know."

That stopped them cold in the ankle-deep water. The lamp swung. In its light Rafael looked older than seven years of ledgers should have allowed.

"You *know*?"

"I've known for months. A buyer from the palace came to the back door in spring. I was moving goods for Halvard — nothing anyone would hang for, or so I thought. The guildmaster met him in the counting room. I heard money change hands. I heard your name."

The silence between us got very wide, the way it does when someone you love discovers you've been keeping a room in your heart locked, and they realize they were never given the key.

"How long," Rafael said carefully, in the voice they used for auditing, "have you been keeping things from me?"

I laughed. It wasn't a kind sound, even to my own ears. "Rafael. Sweetheart. Light of my thoroughly criminal life. Let's do the arithmetic honestly, since that's your language. You hid a storm behind double-entry bookkeeping for seven years. You think I didn't notice? You think *nobody* noticed? You flinch every time thunder rolls over Ashvale. You balance accounts for bottled sunsets and you've never once touched one. When the lightning came off the harbor last autumn and every gifted soul in the district felt it in their teeth, you dropped an inkwell and apologized to the floor."

"That doesn't mean—"

"It means everyone who mattered already knew, and loved you anyway, and said nothing, because saying something gets people burned." I held up the lamp between us. "So yes. I knew. And yes, I kept it. And if you want to stand in a sewer and call that betrayal while the man who sold your ledger sends wardens to your bed, then stand there. But I'd rather you walk."

* * *

We walked.

The cisterns gave onto the Warrens — the undercity proper, a second Ashvale stacked beneath the first, all rope bridges and stolen light, where the hunt's writ ran thin because the hunt's men feared to go. Word travels fast below. By the time we reached the lantern-market, half the stalls were shuttering, and a woman selling eel pies pressed two into my hands without charge and said, "For the road north," though we hadn't told anyone we meant to go anywhere at all.

Everyone was doing arithmetic that night. Everyone was counting exits.

We ate in the hollow beneath a collapsed granary, sharing one pie because Rafael still couldn't eat, and that was where I finally paid my own debt. Not because Rafael demanded it. Because the proclamation had come that morning, nailed to every post in the upper city, and I had read it four times before the ink dried, and I knew what it was really for. Someone had to say it aloud. It might as well be the woman whose mouth still remembered the taste.

"Do you know what I did," I said, "before I was a fence?"

Rafael looked up. Pie crumbs on their lip. Eyes red-rimmed and steady. "You never talk about before."

"No. Well. Here's the before." I wiped my hands on my knees, buying time the way I always do, with small useless motions. "Twelve years ago I was taken into the palace household. Orphan girl, quick palate, no family to complain when I vanished. They made me a taster. You know what that means?"

"For the king's food—"

"For the crown's food. For the crown itself." I watched them not understand, and I envied them the not-understanding, briefly, the way you envy anyone standing outside a burning house. "The Ashen Crown isn't just metal, Rafe. It drinks. Dragon-fire, mostly — that's its proper meal, the thing it was forged from. But a bearer wears it on a mortal head, and mortal heads have mortal hungers, and sometimes the crown wants to know whether the food set before its king is clean. Whether it's drugged. Whether some clever poisoner means to loosen the bearer's leash. So they keep a taster. Someone small and expendable who eats first and reports what dies."

The granary dust hung in the lamplight between us. Somewhere above, boots crossed a bridge — two sets, unhurried, gone again.

"They fed me the poisons too," I went on, because once you open this door there's no closing it. "Small doses. Building tolerance. That's the craft of it — a taster who can't survive her own work is useless, so they seasoned me for years. Wolfsbane in the honey. Thinned rotroot in the wine. You stop tasting bitterness after a while. You stop tasting much of anything. What you get instead is obedience. That's the design. The same discipline that lets the body hold poison lets the mind hold orders. I could stand in front of the king with a tray and feel nothing about anything, and I was *proud* of it, Rafe. Proud of my emptiness. It took me three years after I ran to taste salt properly again."

Rafael reached across the space between us and took my hand. Said nothing. Which was right. There are moments when words are just noise trying to interrupt a fact.

"The proclamation," I said. "You read it as a hunt for the gifted. Storm-callers listed first, yes. But listen to how it's written. Not 'capture.' Not 'question.' The list isn't for prisoners. It's an inventory." My voice cracked on the word and I hated it, but I pushed through. "The crown is running low on dragon-fire. There's one dragon left in the world, up in the northern reach, and it's dying — everyone below a certain rank knows it, everyone above a certain rank is terrified of it. When that fire goes out, the crown will starve unless it finds new fuel. And what burns hotter than dragon-blood?" I turned my palm up toward the ceiling, toward the city, toward the smoke that had been rising off the market square since dawn. "People like you. The hunt isn't a purge, love. It's provisioning."

* * *

Rafael was quiet for a long time after that. Long enough that I finished the second pie mostly to have something to do with my hands.

Then they said, "Eleven minutes."

"What?"

"Aldric. The king. There's a story the smugglers tell — that he's lucid eleven minutes a day. Some hour when the crown sleeps and the man underneath wakes up. The rest of the time it's the crown wearing him, signing proclamations, feeding." They rubbed their face with both hands. "If you're right — if the hunt is fuel — then the man who ordered it may be the only person in Ashvale who hates it more than we do."

"That's a generous reading of a tyrant."

"That's an *audit*," Rafael said, and there — finally — was a ghost of the dry smile I'd fallen for over invoices and contraband candlesticks. "A tyrant signs his own name. A prisoner signs whatever's put in front of him. I've spent seven years learning the difference between what a ledger says and what happened. I'm not going to stop now."

"And if you're wrong? If you walk into that palace and the thing on his head takes one look at you and sees a walking meal?"

"Then I'll have finally done something impossible enough to earn my guild card." They stood, brushed granary dust from their coat, and offered me their hand — the same gesture I'd made pulling them into the wine cellar, turned around and handed back. "Wren. You knew what I was and stayed anyway. You ate poison for years and walked out of the palace alive. Of everyone in this kingdom, you're the only person qualified for what comes next."

"What comes next being?"

"Getting an accountant into the royal household before the hunt does."

I looked at that hand. At the person attached to it — liar, coward, keeper of impossible books, the bravest fraud I had ever loved. Twelve years of tasting other people's dangers, and here was mine, warm-palmed and terrified, asking me to walk back into the house that had eaten my childhood.

Somewhere far above, a bell began to toll the hour, and beneath it, fainter, another bell answering — the palace, ringing on its own schedule, keeping its own time.

Eleven minutes, I thought. Somewhere up in that gilded cage, a king was waking up right now, lucid and drowning, with perhaps a quarter-hour of being himself left before the crown swallowed him whole again.

I took Rafael's hand.

"All right," I said. "But I'm picking our disguise. Nobody audits a war levy in that coat."
