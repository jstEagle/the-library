## 4. The King in the Gilded Cage

The thing you must understand about ledgers is that they are honest in a way nothing else is. A ledger does not flatter. It does not weep. It simply says: *here is what was taken, here is what was given, and here — this thin column of red — is what was stolen.*

I had spent seven years learning to love that honesty. So when Wren pressed two sets of forged credentials into my hands and told me we were entering the palace as auditors of the war levy, I confess I felt something adjacent to comfort. Numbers. Columns. A task with edges. The crown could burn every gifted soul in Ashvale, but it would never think to fear an accountant.

"They'll search us," I said, as we joined the queue at the River Gate, wedged between a wool merchant sweating through his velvet and a priest who smelled of incense and cheaper wine.

"Of course they'll search us." Wren adjusted the spectacles perched on her nose — plain glass, purely costume, though she wore them with the conviction of a woman who had suffered for her eyesight. "That's why you're carrying nothing but arithmetic. You *are* arithmetic, Rafael. It's the most invisible thing about you."

There was a compliment buried in there somewhere. I chose not to excavate it.

The wardens at the gate were thorough in the manner of men who have been given power recently and intend to enjoy it. One turned my satchel inside out, frowned at the reams of levy schedules, tally-sheets, and tax rolls, and looked at me with genuine pity.

"Guild work?" he said.

"Someone has to count it," I said, "or someone steals it."

He waved us through with the weariness of a man who knew exactly how the second option worked.

And so we walked into the heart of the machine that was hunting my kind, armed with nothing but a legitimate grievance and excellent penmanship.

* * *

The palace of Aldric the First of His Name was not what the proclamations promised. Proclamations, in my experience, describe palaces the way eulogies describe the deceased: taller, kinder, better lit than anything reality ever managed. This one was vast, yes, but vast the way a tomb is vast. Every corridor we were escorted down — by a chamberlain whose smile had been starched into place sometime before the war — was swept, polished, and utterly unoccupied. No courtiers. No laughter. Servants moved like fish beneath ice, appearing when needed and vanishing before the eye could fix on them.

"Where is everyone?" I murmured to Wren.

"Gone or frightened," she said. "Look at the sconces."

I looked. The candles were burned down to stubs and never replaced. In a palace. Where money was no object. Either the treasury had failed or the spending had gone elsewhere, and I had a professional suspicion which.

Our audit was assigned a room in the eastern wing: a counting-house with a view of the inner courtyard, three desks, and a lock on the outside of the door. That last detail I noted without comment. Prisons always keep good records; it's one of their few virtues.

For six days we counted. Genuinely counted — I insist on this. The war levy was a horror of creative mathematics, funds multiplying and evaporating between ministries like rain on hot stone, and I did honest work reconstructing where it went. Most of it went north. To the hunt. To Lady Vess's wardens, to pyre-wood, to chains rated for things stronger than men. I copied the figures in my neatest hand and felt each number settle into my chest like a swallowed stone.

It was on the sixth night that Wren put down her pen, listened to something I couldn't hear, and said: "Eleven minutes."

"Eleven minutes for what?"

"The king." She was already gathering her shawl. "The servants talk when they think no one's listening. They say he's mad — raving, screaming, burning things with his mind, all the rest. But there's a window. An hour past midnight, eleven minutes long, when he's *himself*. The chamberlain calls it his affliction's mercy. The guards call it nothing at all, because they've been ordered not to speak of it." She looked at me over the spectacles. "You wanted to know why a king who fears magic hunts the gifted. I want to see his eyes when we ask him."

"And if the stories are true? If he's exactly what the proclamations say?"

"Then we've lost nothing but sleep." She smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "You know I'm very good at losing things, Rafael. I fenced half the impossible objects in that guildhall. Losing is a discipline."

* * *

Getting to the royal apartments required neither skill nor daring, which unsettled me more than any lockpick ever had. We simply walked. The corridors at midnight belonged to us and to the mice, and twice we passed guards who looked through us as though we were a draft. Fear, I thought, makes better locks than iron ever will. Nobody goes where everyone dreads.

The doors to the king's chambers were oak and iron, and they were unlocked.

Inside, the air was wrong. I felt it before I understood it — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of struck flint on the tongue, the particular static hum that I had spent seven years teaching my own blood to forget. Magic, old and enormous, soaked the room like water in a wall. My skin prickled with recognition and terror in equal measure, because I knew this feeling. I had been born knowing it.

Storm-magic. Or its corpse.

King Aldric sat in a chair by the cold hearth, dressed in a nightshirt that had once been fine, his hands bound in his lap with silk cords — silk, I noted, because rope chafes and he would be wearing them a long time. He was perhaps fifty, gaunt in the way of men being eaten slowly from within, and his head was bowed as though in prayer.

Then he raised it, and I saw his eyes.

One was a man's eye: brown, exhausted, human, and so full of terror that I felt it like cold water down my spine. The other was ember-orange, lit from within, and it did not blink. It did not need to. It was not entirely his.

"Eleven minutes," he rasped, before we'd said a word. "You've come in my eleven minutes. Clever. Or doomed. Possibly both." He laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through into something that wasn't laughter at all. "Who are you? Not assassins — assassins knock. Not priests — they come at dawn when I'm screaming. Let me guess. Ledgers. You have ink on your fingers and pity on your faces. Auditors?"

Wren stepped forward. "We came to understand the hunt, Majesty."

"*Don't* call me that." The word came out of him like a splinter. "Majesty. Your Grace. Highness. Do you know who says those words to me? *It* does. Through my mouth. Do you know who signed the proclamation burning children in the market square?" He lifted his bound hands slightly. "*I* did. These hands. While I watched from inside my own skull, screaming, and it smiled with my face and called it justice." His breath hitched. "Eleven minutes a day it sleeps. Eleven minutes I get to remember my own name."

"What is it?" I asked quietly. "What sleeps?"

His human eye fixed on me — really fixed, the way a drowning man fixes on a rope — and then dropped to my hands, my ink-stained, storm-humming, carefully ordinary hands.

"You're one of them," he whispered. "Gifted. I can smell it — *it* can smell it, even sleeping, even now—" He shook his head violently. "Run. Whatever you came for, forget it. If it wakes and finds you here—"

"We're not leaving," Wren said, "until you tell us how to kill it."

The king stared at her. And then, horribly, he began to weep — silently, efficiently, the crying of a man who has learned to ration even his grief.

"That's the cruelty of it," he said. "Everyone who sees me wants to *save* me. My physicians bleed me. My confessor prays over me. A maid last year tried to cut the cords off my wrists and it woke early and — " He stopped. Swallowed. "Do not save me. Do you hear me? Rescue is a door it can use. As long as I live, it lives, rooted in me, wearing me. But destroy the crown itself — the artifact, the thing that was forged, not the man it infests — destroy *that*, and I might yet be only a man."

"The Ashen Crown," I said. "It's real."

"It's on my head eleven minutes from now," he said simply. "It drinks dragon-fire. Has drunk it for thirty years — there was a hoard once, embers harvested from the last great wyrms, enough fuel to bind a king for a lifetime. The hoard is nearly spent. Can you feel it?" He leaned forward, and the ember eye brightened. "It's *hungry*. When the last ember dies, it will not die with it. It will seek new fuel. What burns hotter than dragon-fire? What does it hate, and hunger for, and hunt across my kingdom even now?"

The answer arrived in me like a stone dropping through still water.

"The gifted," I breathed.

"It will wear me until it finds a better vessel," Aldric said. "Or it will feed on them directly — every storm-caller, every flame-speaker, every child with light in their palms. A kingdom of kindling, auditor, and it holds the match." He straightened as much as his bonds allowed, and for one moment the exhaustion fell away and something regal and ruined looked out of him. "So I am begging you. Not as your king. Kings beg for thrones. I am begging you as the only man in this palace who is also its prisoner. Find the fire that made it. Unmake it. And whatever else you do — whatever mercy moves you — do not try to take it off my head. Promise me."

I looked at Wren. She was pale, but her jaw was set in the line I had learned meant *she had already decided*.

"We promise," she said.

"Liar," the king said gently. "Good. Honest people couldn't do what's needed."

Somewhere beyond the door, a bell began to toll the quarter hour. Aldric's face changed — slackened, smoothed, like a bed being made over a sleeping body. His spine straightened. The ember eye brightened from coal to forge.

"You should go," he said, in a voice already becoming someone else's. "And auditor — when you find what you need — " the human eye fought up through the orange, one last time — "look north. Fire remembers where it was born."

The doors were heavy behind us, and the corridor was dark, and we did not stop walking until we reached the counting-house and locked ourselves in with our honest columns and our stolen knowledge.

Wren sat down slowly at her desk. "North," she said. "Fire remembers where it was born. That's not a riddle, Rafael. That's a direction."

"I know where dragons were born," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, distant, like someone else's ledger entry. "Everyone does. The northern reach. Where the calderas are."

"And where the last dragon is dying," Wren finished softly. "If it isn't dead already."

Outside, far off toward the river, a warden's horn sounded — one note, then two, then a third that meant *found*. Not for us. For someone. Somewhere in Ashvale, another name off another list was learning what the crown's hunger tasted like.

I opened my satchel, took out a fresh sheet, and wrote at the top, in the careful hand of a man who had spent seven years pretending numbers were all he was:

*Item: one crown. Fuel: failing. Intent: everything.*

Underneath, after a moment, I added:

*Item: eleven minutes. Value: immeasurable. Debtor: me.*
