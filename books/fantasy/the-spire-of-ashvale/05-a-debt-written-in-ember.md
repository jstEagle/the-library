## 5. A Debt Written in Ember

There is a particular smell to a safehouse that has been lived in by two frightened people for nine days: tallow, damp wool, and the ghost of onions. Wren and I had made a home of it anyway, the way you make a bed of nails comfortable by refusing to move. She slept in the loft above the counting room; I slept at the desk, because I had discovered that I could no longer tell the difference between vigilance and habit.

I was balancing the accounts of our flight when the knock came.

Three knocks, a pause, then two — the guild's old signal for *a debt collector calls*. Nobody had used it since the purge began, because nobody wanted to advertise that the Guild of Impossible Things still existed anywhere but on Lady Vess's kill-lists. My hand went to the ledger first — old instinct — before my brain caught up and reminded me that the ledger was ash now, burned page by page in the guildhall's cold hearth while Wren held the candle and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

The second set of knocks was impatient.

I opened the door with a poker in my left hand and an apology ready in my mouth, and there stood Master Corvane, guildmaster of the Impossible Things, looking like a man who had been ironed badly. His coat was fine — finer than any guild coat should be, silver frogging, boots that had never seen a gutter — and his eyes were the eyes of a man who has been doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Rafael," he said. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"Survival is expensive." He glanced past me into the dark of the counting room with the ease of a man who had signed the lease. "May I come in? I'd rather not discuss treason on the doorstep."

"It isn't your doorstep anymore?"

"Nothing is anyone's anymore." And there it was, the old weariness under the new finery, and I hated that I recognized it, hated that some traitorous part of me wanted to sit him down by the dead fire the way I had sat across from him for seven years, passing miracles back and forth over a ledger line.

Wren came down the ladder without being called. She had that look she got — arms crossed, chin level — the look that meant she had already counted the exits and found them all wanting.

"Corvane," she said. It was not a greeting.

"Wren." He inclined his head with genuine courtesy, which somehow made it worse. "Still keeping company above your station, I see. A fence and a storm-caller. The guild always did have eclectic taste."

"We're not guild anything anymore," I said. "We burned out."

"No," Corvane said gently, stepping past us both to warm his hands at the unlit hearth as though fire were a courtesy we owed him, "you burned *pages*. There's a difference, Rafael, and it's the difference between what you think you did and what actually happened. Sit down. Both of you. What I have to say will take eleven minutes, and I'd rather not spend them watching you stand there looking righteous."

*Eleven minutes.* The number snagged in me like a fishhook, though I couldn't have said why.

We sat. Corvane remained standing, which told me everything about how he meant to conduct this.

"I sold names," he said.

Wren made a sound low in her throat. I put a hand on her wrist.

"Not yours," Corvane continued. "Not yet. I want you to understand the order of operations here, because you're both romantics and romantics always assume betrayal comes with malice attached. It doesn't. Betrayal comes with *arithmetic* attached. Vess had forty-one names from my ledgers. In exchange she gave me forty-one exemptions. Forty-one guild members who will sleep in their own beds tonight because I wrote other people's names in a different book. That's not malice. That's a balance sheet."

"That's exactly what it is," I said. "A balance sheet. With people in the debit column."

"People were always going to be in the debit column, Rafael. The only question was whose handwriting decided which people." He turned from the hearth and looked at me directly, and I saw that underneath the ironed-and-badly-pressed exterior he was terrified — properly terrified, the way I had been every night for nine days. "Here is why I came. Here is the offer, and I'll make it once. Vess wants the storm-caller who audited the war levy. She knows someone inside the palace moved numbers that shouldn't have been movable. She knows a name was scratched from a requisition roll in a hand that matches no clerk in the chancery. She does not know it was you. Bring her proof of who holds the king's secret — the true nature of the Ashen Crown, what it is, what it eats, what it's dying of — and your exemption becomes a pardon. Full. Sealed. Signed. You and the fence both. You could open a shop. You could grow cabbages. You could stop smelling of onions."

Silence. The tallow candle leaned and righted itself.

"And if I don't?" I asked.

"Then I give Vess your name tomorrow, because the alternative is that she takes mine, and Rafael—" his voice cracked, just slightly, just enough to show the mortar between the stones, "—I am sixty-two years old and I have spent my life keeping impossible things alive and I do not want to die in a market square to the sound of drums."

*To the sound of drums.* The child had burned to drums. I had heard them from three streets away and done nothing, and I would do nothing now, and the arithmetic of that was very simple and very ugly.

"The king's secret," I said slowly. "You know about Aldric."

"I know what everyone who's ever been inside the palace knows, which is nothing, which is precisely the point. But I know the crown is failing. Everyone knows. The king's proclamations have gone strange this year — repetitive, circular, a man arguing with himself in public. Vess thinks it's madness. Madness is convenient for her; mad kings get replaced. But you've been inside. You've seen him lucid." He paused. "Haven't you."

It wasn't a question. Corvane had always known things he shouldn't. Seven years of miracles taught a man to read the gaps in a story.

"Eleven minutes a day," I heard myself say. God help me, I answered him. Eleven minutes a day, and the rest of the hours a puppet with excellent posture, wearing a crown that whispers."

Something crossed Corvane's face that I had never seen there before — not greed, not fear. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own nightmare described by a stranger.

"So you know," he said quietly. "Then you know more than Vess. And that, my dear impossible boy, is worth more than a pardon. It's worth a kingdom."

He laid it out then, and I will write it here exactly as he laid it out, because someone must keep the account even if the account is monstrous.

The Ashen Crown was forged in the last age from the heart-fire of a dragon — not slain, Corvane said, but *given*, or so the oldest stories went, though the stories disagreed on whether the giving had been voluntary. Dragon-fire does not burn out the way mortal flame does. It burns the way memory burns: slowly, in circles, feeding on what fed it. For three hundred years the crown had drawn on its original ember, dimming by fractions so small that no reign noticed. But embers are embers. The last dragon in the world — one dragon, Corvane said, in the northern reach, in the caldera country where the glass still crunches underfoot — was dying. Old, wound-gutted, alone. When it died, the crown's fuel source ended.

And the crown, being an artifact of hunger rather than malice, would do what hungry things do.

"It will seek new fire," Corvane said. "And where does fire live now, Rafael? Where has it gone to hide? In bottled sunsets and borrowed years. In hedge-witches and storm-callers and children who light candles with their minds. The gifted are walking kindling. When Ysolde gutters out — and she is guttering now, the northern traders speak of the sky over the caldera turning the wrong colors — the crown will begin to feed on *them*. On people like you. Vess thinks she's hunting the gifted to destroy them. She's herding them. Whether she knows it or not, she's building the crown a larder."

The room was very quiet. Wren had gone white. Not, I thought, with fear. With the particular horror of a woman rearranging everything she knew around a new fact, the way you rearrange a room after learning the floor is rotten.

"The taster," I said to her. "Is that why—"

"Don't," she said. One word, flat as a coin. Later, then. We would have it later, the conversation we kept deferring, the one about what she'd swallowed in that palace and what it had been *for*.

Corvane watched us with the weary patience of a man who has already grieved this scene and is waiting for it to finish.

"The pardon stands until tomorrow's dusk," he said. "Bring Vess the truth, or I bring her your name. I'm sorry, Rafael. Truly. If there were another arithmetic, I'd use it." He straightened his beautiful, terrible coat. "For what it's worth — and it's worth nothing — I hope you run."

And then he was gone, and the door clicked behind him like a full stop.

* * *

Wren lit the candle properly after that. Neither of us spoke for a long time. Down in the street, somewhere far off, a patrol drummed past, and we both flinched at the rhythm without admitting it.

"He'll do it," she said finally. "You know he will. He sold forty-one names. He'll sell a forty-second."

"Yes."

"So we run north."

I looked up. "North."

"To the dragon." She said it the way she said everything — as though it were merely a delivery route, a fence's itinerary, a thing to be scheduled. "If the crown needs fire, and the last fire in the world is dying, then the answer is standing in front of us wearing scales. Either we find out what a dying dragon wants, or we spend the rest of our lives being larder."

"It's a caldera, Wren. It's hunted country. It's a *dragon*, singular, the last of a species that ate kingdoms, and you want to walk up and negotiate."

"I want to walk up and *count*," she said. "Everything has a price. Everything can be bargained with. That's the whole of my profession and most of my life. A pardon bought with a king's secret is cheap, Rafael. Cheap things are always traps. So we find the expensive thing instead." She crouched in front of me, took my face in her hands, and her thumbs smelled of candle wax. "You called a storm off a rooftop in the rain quarter when you were fourteen and nobody died except the lightning rods. You've spent seven years pretending you're a ledger. I'm telling you: the ledger's closed. Open something else."

Outside, the drums faded toward the river. Somewhere north, past the wards and the patrols and the whole armed machinery of Lady Vess's tidy tyranny, an old fire was going out, and a crown made of its ashes was learning to be hungry.

I looked at the blank ledger on my desk — the new one, the empty one, the one I'd bought the day after the burning because my hands needed something to do — and I opened it to the first page, and I wrote, in the neat accounting hand that had hidden me for seven years:

*Debt owed: one kingdom. Creditor: pending.*

"We leave at dusk," I said. "Before his."

Wren smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a woman opening a lock she probably shouldn't.

"Pack the ink," she said. "We're going to need to keep records."
