## 6. Northward, Where the Sky Sings

Here is what they don't tell you about fleeing for your life: it is mostly walking.

Walking through mud. Walking in rain that can't decide whether to be rain or sleet. Walking past the burned-out shell of a tollhouse where a warden's notice has been nailed to the door at three different heights, as if the hunt wanted to make sure even children could read their own death warrants. *By decree of the Crown: all persons of gifted nature to surrender themselves for assessment. Assessment is mandatory. Resistance is heresy.*

"Assessment," Rafael said, reading over my shoulder. "That's a lovely word for it. Very administrative."

"You would like it," I said. "It's got numbers in it somewhere."

"I'm choosing to take that as a compliment."

We walked on. That was the whole of our strategy, most days: walk north, stay small, stay quiet. Rafael had cut their hair and traded their guildmaster's coat for a drover's oilskin two sizes too big, and I had learned to stop flinching every time a rider appeared on the road behind us. Mostly. I'd like it noted for the record that I only screamed once, and it was at a goose.

The villages were the worst part. Not because they were hostile — because they were empty. We passed through six of them in the first week, and each one was the same: doors barred from the inside by people who had left anyway, gardens gone to seed mid-row, a doll in a doorway with its face turned toward the road as if waiting for someone to come back and pick it up. In one, someone had chalked names on the well-house wall. A list. Dozens of them. Some crossed out — taken, assessed — and some not, which meant either fled or hidden, and there was no way to know which hope was kinder.

Rafael stood in front of that wall for a long time. Long enough that I started counting the seconds out of habit, the way you do when you're afraid of how long a silence is going.

"They're names," Rafael said finally. "Someone wrote them down so they wouldn't vanish."

"That's your whole religion, isn't it."

"It's not a religion. It's bookkeeping." But they touched the wall, just once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't want to wake, and we left.

*

The wardens came through on the fourth day, riding in pairs along the north road with ash-grey cloaks and long hooked poles I pretended not to look at too carefully. Detection rods, Wren, I told myself. Just detection rods. They sweep them over travelers and if you're carrying anything the crown doesn't like — charms, relics, a gift in your blood — the rod sings.

Rafael's gift wasn't something you carried. It was something you *were*. Nobody knew what a rod did when it passed over a storm-caller, because storm-callers hadn't existed openly since before either of us was born, and the ones who had been found since had not been in any condition to file reports.

So when a pair of wardens reined up beside us at a crossroads and said, "Travelers. Names and business," I did what I do best. I lied.

"Wren Ashby, factor's agent. This is my clerk, Marlow. We're auditing grain shipments for the millers' consortium out of Dunmere."

I have never been prouder of the word *audit* than I was in that moment. Wardens are brave men facing down sorcery, but show a warden a ledger and mention tariffs, and something dies behind his eyes. The older one actually sighed.

"Papers."

I produced papers. Forged, obviously — I'd made them myself in a barn outside Ashvale, and they were good, because forgery is just accounting with worse ethics. He read them with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own eulogy while the younger one swept his rod lazily over Rafael.

The rod hummed. A low note, uncertain, like a tuning fork deciding whether it cared.

My heart stopped. I mean that almost literally; there was a moment where I genuinely believe my body filed the incident under *pending* and moved on without me. Rafael stood perfectly still, hands loose, face arranged into the profound boredom of a man who has audited grain for twenty years and has opinions about none of it.

"Hm," said the young warden.

"Rod's damp," said the old one. "Been raining for three days. Everything hums when it's damp. Move along."

They moved us along. We walked until the road bent and the wardens were out of sight, and then another hour after that, because neither of us wanted to be the first to speak.

Finally, in a stand of birches with the leaves coming down around us like small yellow surrender flags, Rafael sat down on a fallen log and put their head in their hands.

"The rod sang."

"The rod *hmm*-ed."

"Wren."

"I heard it." I sat down next to them. Close enough that our shoulders touched, which was as much comfort as either of us knew how to ask for out loud. "You need to learn to dampen it. Whatever you do — the thing you do inside yourself when you're holding still — you need to do it harder."

"I've spent seven years doing it harder. It's why I get headaches. It's why my handwriting went bad." They laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? I used to have beautiful handwriting. Now look at me. Exiled twice over by penmanship."

"Rafael—"

"If they catch me, they don't burn me, Wren. You've seen the lists. Storm-callers don't get assessed. They get *transported*." Transported was the other lovely administrative word. It meant north, to the reach, where the crown kept its fires. "And now they'll have felt me once. The next rod won't hmm. It'll sing, and it'll remember."

I didn't say anything, because there was nothing true and comforting available. So instead I took their hand, which was cold the way stone is cold, and we sat among the birches until the light went, two liars pretending very hard that lying was going to be enough.

*

It wasn't enough. I want that on the record too, in my own handwriting, which is excellent.

We heard the refugee column before we saw it — a sound like a village learning to whisper all at once. They came down the river road at dusk, maybe two hundred souls with carts and bundles and children asleep against their mothers' backs, moving north because north was the only direction nobody claimed. And behind them, maybe a mile back and closing, rode thirty wardens with the hooked poles and the grey cloaks and the particular unhurried confidence of men who know the quarry cannot outrun horses.

There was a bridge ahead. Old, stone, narrow. A column of two hundred funnels onto a bridge like wine into a bottle-neck, and thirty riders behind them with fire and iron, and the orders we both knew by heart: the gifted are to be culled from the columns, and anyone who shelters them shares their sentence.

Rafael stopped walking.

"No," I said. I said it fast, before they'd even turned around, because I already knew their face. Seven years I had known that face, and I had loved it in lamplight and in danger and once, memorably, upside down, but I had never seen this particular expression on it except once before — the night the proclamation came, and they'd looked at the pyre smoke over Ashvale and asked me, quietly, *how many names were in my ledger*.

"Rafael. No. There are thirty of them. There's one of you, and you're rusty, and the last time you called anything down you were nineteen and it leveled a watchtower and half a hillside."

"Yes," Rafael said. "I remember. I was there. I wrote the apology letter. It ran to eleven pages."

"Eleven pages of apology doesn't unlevel a hillside!"

"No," they agreed. "But it does suggest I've gotten better at precision since." And then they did the thing that broke me, the thing I still carry: they handed me their pack. Calmly. Like a man handing his coat to a friend before stepping into a ring. "Hold this. If it goes wrong, run north, find Ysolde, tell the dragon I sent you. She'll hate that. Do it anyway."

"There is no dragon, that's a story—"

"All the best things are stories until someone keeps the books on them." They smiled at me, and it was the smile from the guildhall, the one over the arithmetic of miracles, and I understood suddenly that I had loved them longest not despite the concealment but inside it — that the careful, ink-stained, headache-prone clerk I adored had always been a held breath, and here was the exhale.

Then Rafael walked out into the open field between the column and the road, raised both arms, and called the sky down.

*

I have decided I am not going to describe it the way poets would. Poets weren't there. I was, and I had my eyes shut for half of it, and I will report faithfully that even through my eyelids it was white, and that the thunder did not crack so much as *arrive*, everywhere at once, the way bad news arrives.

When I opened my eyes, lightning stood on the field in five places like fence posts, ringing the refugee column in a cage of light wide enough for carts and children and none for riders. The wardens' horses screamed and refused the line — animals are sensible about storms — and the rain came down sideways and the wind picked up the wardens' grey cloaks like a mother hauling children home by the collars. Not one bolt struck flesh. Not one. Five fences of lightning, precise as ruled lines, and above them Rafael stood in the downpour with their arms up and their hair standing in a dark corona, and they were *laughing*. I could see it even at distance. Seven years of held breath, laughing in the rain.

The column poured across the bridge. The wardens regrouped, conferred, and — being brave men facing down something far beyond their pay grade — withdrew to report to someone whose pay grade this was. The rain eased. The lightning posts guttered out one by one like candles after a vigil.

And every soul in those two hundred refugees had seen. Every one.

An old woman was the first to come back across the bridge. She walked straight up to Rafael, who stood there dripping and mortal and terrified under the performance, and pressed something into their hands — a heel of bread, wrapped in cloth. Then she bowed. Actually bowed. And behind her, hesitantly, in twos and threes, others came: bread, a blanket, a copper coin, a child's wooden bird with one wing. An offering pile built itself at Rafael's feet, and Rafael stood in the middle of it looking like a man who has accidentally founded a religion.

"I killed the audit," they said to me hoarsely, when the crowd thinned. "Marlow the grain clerk is dead. There's no coming back from lightning."

"No," I said. "There isn't."

And here is where I confess what changed in me, because I have kept honest books ever since and I refuse to fudge this entry: I had spent seven days asking Rafael to be small. Hide it. Dampen it. Be less. And I had done it out of love, but love had curdled somewhere on the north road into its coward cousin, and watching them stand in that field — watching them choose to be enormous, precisely, on purpose, for strangers — I understood that I had been asking them to survive rather than to live, and that those are not the same account.

So I picked up the wooden bird, and I handed it back to them, and I said: "Next time, don't hand me the pack. Teach me the signal for *wider*. If you're going to burn bright, you shouldn't have to aim alone."

Rafael stared at me. Rain, or possibly not rain, on their face.

"Wren—"

"Don't make it a moment. Moments are inefficient." I shouldered both packs. "Come on. The dragon story starts somewhere north of here, and I want to meet the thing that's been keeping my beloved in exile since before I knew them. And Rafael—" I turned back, one last time. "When Vess comes for us, and she will, she's already coming — we don't run. We *invoice*."

Far to the south, on the dark horizon, watch-fires answered one another along the ridgeline. Thirty riders had become three hundred. Word of the lightning was traveling faster than we could.

Northward, then. Where the sky sings, and the last dragon waits to hear whether we're worth the tune.
