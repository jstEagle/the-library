## 7. The Last Dragon's Bargain

The maps had lied to us, which was only fair, since we'd stolen them.

The northern reach was supposed to be three days of hard riding through pine country and then a valley of hot springs where the snow never settled. What we found instead was a wound in the world: a caldera the size of a city, its rim black glass, its floor a lake of cooling stone that glowed like a banked hearth. The air tasted of copper and old lightning. Every hair on my arms stood up and stayed there, like soldiers at attention who'd been told the war would last forever.

"Charming," Wren said beside me. "Remind me why we didn't retire to the coast."

"Because the coast has wardens."

"The coast also has wine."

We picked our way down the glass slope in silence after that, because the mountain was doing something I'd only ever felt once before — in the throne room of Ashvale, standing eleven minutes' distance from King Aldric and his crown. A hunger. Old and vast and patient as arithmetic. Something down there was burning out, and it knew it, and it had opinions about that.

The dragon was smaller than the stories promised.

I don't know what I expected. Cathedrals of scale, wings that blotted out the sun, the whole illuminated-manuscript production. But Ysolde lay curled in the center of her caldera like an old woman by a dying fire, and she was — I want to say *modest*. The size of a granary barn, perhaps. Her scales had gone the color of ash with veins of ember running through them, brightening and dimming in rhythm with her breath, and each breath came further apart than the one before. Where her belly pressed against the cooling stone, the rock had gone soft again, weeping threads of glass.

She opened one eye when we were still twenty paces out. It was molten gold, and it was tired, and it looked at me the way Wren looked at ledgers: seeing everything, forgiving little.

"A storm-caller," she said. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "I smelled you coming for two days. Rain that walks on two legs. How novel."

"We're told the crown needs fire," I said. "Yours."

"Direct. Good. I haven't the strength left for riddles, and you clearly lack the wit for them." The eye slid to Wren, who stood very straight and very pale and did not flinch, which I have always admired about her even when it terrified me. "And you brought your heart. Also good. You will need someone to carry home whatever is left of you."

*If anything,* went unspoken, hanging between us like the smell of sulfur.

Wren's hand found mine. Hers was cold; mine had been shaking since the pass, since I'd felt the crown's hunger through forty miles of stone and understood exactly what Lady Vess intended to feed it if we failed. We had crossed hunted country for this. We had watched Rafael Coldharbour, ledger-keeper, become a rumor with a price. There was no version of this story where I turned around now.

"Tell us what you want," I said.

"What I *want*," Ysolde said, "is another thousand years and a sky without smoke in it. What I can offer is somewhat narrower." She shifted, and the movement cost her — I heard it in the grinding of her joints, saw the ember-veins flare and gutter. Glass ran from under her tail in bright ropes. "Come closer, storm-caller. Let me look at what I'm buying."

"I wasn't aware I was for sale."

"Everything is. The only question is the currency." She exhaled, and the breath washed over us warm as a baker's oven, carrying the smell of every fire that had ever burned. "The Ashen Crown was forged from my sister's heart. Do you know that? Not her hoard, not her flame — her *heart*, cut living from her chest while she slept, because a sleeping thing cannot refuse. Your kings have worn her death on their brows for three hundred years and called it majesty." The gold eye narrowed. "It is nearly spent. Ember-fire does not renew itself. It consumes. And when it has finished consuming itself—"

"It looks for new fuel," I said. "The gifted."

"You've done your reading. Yes. It will drink them the way it drinks him, but faster, and it will not stop until it has emptied the realm of everything that burns bright. Your Lady Vess thinks she means to wear it. The crown will wear *her*. And then it will wear everyone she loves, and everyone she fears, and eventually the whole wretched kingdom, orderly and obedient and hollow." Another breath. Further apart now. "Unless it is unmade. Which requires fire older than it. Fire given freely, by the last creature alive who remembers how it was made."

"And you'll give it," Wren said. Not a question. Her voice had that flat steadiness she used across fences' tables, the one that meant she already knew the shape of the deal and was simply waiting for the other party to admit it.

"I will give it," Ysolde agreed. "But fire does not travel well in buckets. It must be carried. By a vessel. By a bearer who takes it *willingly* — that part matters, storm-caller, more than you know. Fire taken is a curse. Fire accepted is a covenant. My sister's heart was *taken*, and look what three centuries of that have wrought." The ember-veins along her flank pulsed, dimmed. "You would carry my fire in mortal flesh. Flesh that was not built for it. Some of you would burn away. Perhaps all of you. Perhaps you would burn away everything within a mile when it leaves you. I cannot promise otherwise. I am dying, and dying things make honest merchants but poor guarantors."

* * *

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the theme of the rest of my life: for about ten seconds, I tried to do the math.

That's what I am, underneath everything. Seven years of balancing accounts for miracles teaches you a certain reflex. One vessel, expendable, versus a kingdom full of the gifted. One storm-caller who has already lost everything worth keeping except the woman currently crushing his fingers, versus a realm of children on pyres and a king in a gilded cage. The sums were clean. The sums were almost insultingly clean.

But I have also kept enough ledgers to know that the numbers are never the whole entry. Somewhere in the margins there is always a note in a different hand.

"Rafael." Wren turned me to face her. She was crying, I realized, without any change in her expression at all, tears just quietly going about their business on her cheeks. "Don't. Find another way. There's always another way, you're the one who taught me that — every impossible thing has a workaround, you said, that's the entire point of the guild—"

"The guild sold names to save itself," I said gently. "I'm not sure I'm its best advertisement anymore."

"Rafael."

"Wren." I took both her hands. Behind us the dragon waited with the patience of mountains, and I understood suddenly that this was the true bargain, the one no one had named yet: not fire for flesh, but this moment, this choice, witnessed. "Seven years I hid. Seven years I balanced other people's miracles and told myself ink was braver than lightning. Then they burned a child in the market square for a gift smaller than mine, and I learned what hiding costs. It doesn't cost nothing. It costs *everyone else*." I swallowed. "I'm done paying in other people's coin."

"That's a terrible reason to die."

"It's the best one I've got."

"It's not a reason to die, it's a reason to be brave, and those are different things, and you know they're different things because you correct my grammar when I confuse them—"

A sound came out of me that might have been a laugh or might have been something rawer. "Then let's call it bravery, and you can correct me later."

"When?"

"When I'm back. That's the deal I'm making. With you, with the mountain, with whatever gods audit this sort of transaction. I come back, and you get to be insufferable about it for the rest of our lives." I leaned my forehead against hers. Her tears had gone hot, or mine had. Hard to say. "I love you. That's not in the ledger. That's the margin note."

"Don't make jokes."

"Never do."

Behind me, Ysolde said, softly, for something so large: "Storm-caller. The offer stands until my next breath. Choose."

* * *

Here is what taking dragon-fire feels like, for the record, for whoever keeps the next ledger:

It feels like being read.

Not judged — read. Every page at once. Every name I'd hidden, every miracle I'd balanced, every night I'd spent loving Wren in silence and calling it prudence, every time I'd called lightning up out of my bones and hated myself for how much I loved it. The fire went through all of it the way light goes through glass, and where it passed it left something burning that had always been there, banked low, waiting for permission.

Ysolde pressed her brow to mine — a skull the size of a rowboat, gentle as a moth — and said, "Freely?"

"Freely."

"Then take what I cannot keep, and spend it better than we did."

And she *poured*.

I have called storms. I have held lightning in cupped hands like water and known it for a cousin. This was not that. This was the first morning of the world arriving in my chest and finding it furnished. I felt my heartbeat change key. I felt my blood go luminous behind my eyes. I felt — distantly, as though reported by a clerk in another building — Wren screaming my name, and my own knees hitting the glass, and somewhere far above the clouds tearing open in answer, rain beginning to fall into the caldera and hissing to steam a hundred feet before it touched stone.

The fire did not consume me. I want that written plainly, because everyone assumes it must have. It *considered* consuming me. It weighed me, the way I weigh entries, and it found — I will spend the rest of my life trying to deserve this — it found me sufficient.

When I could see again, Ysolde was smaller. Dimmer. The ember-veins had gone grey, and the great gold eye had become merely amber, merely mortal-eyed, merely tired.

"There," she whispered. "My sister's death, returned to the family. Spend it well, bearer. Unmake what was made wrongly." A pause, long as a season. "And tell your king... tell Aldric... that Vessa sends her regards."

The name landed like ice water.

Vessa. *Vess.*

The eye closed. The breathing slowed. Around us the caldera began, very quietly, to cool — and in my chest, beneath my sternum, something ancient and patient and *awake* settled in beside my heart like a guest who intended to stay.

Wren caught me before I fell. She was saying my name over and over, and the rain was coming down harder now, real rain, cold rain, and I was laughing, or sobbing, or both.

"Wren," I managed. "The commander. The hunt's commander. She's — the crown was forged from the heart of a dragon named Vessa, and Lady Vess wants to wear it, and I don't think that's coincidence, I don't think any of this has been coincidence—"

Wren went very still.

"Rafael," she said slowly, "what did you just put inside yourself?"
