## 8. Vess Ascendant

I have been many things in my life: orphan, pickpocket, fence, taster of kings' poisons, and — for one humiliating quarter-hour in a smuggler's tunnel beneath Ashvale — a person who cried on Rafael Coldharbour's shoulder and meant it. But I had never yet been a spy. I mention this because it matters to what follows, and because if you're going to be stupid, you should at least be stupid with your eyes open.

Rafael slept. That was the whole of the situation, laid bare. Three days since Ysolde's caldera, three days since they walked out of that glass-bright hollow with dragon-fire banked behind their ribs like a second heart, and Rafael had done nothing but sleep and burn. Not visibly. Internally. The blankets steamed. The healer we'd paid in Vell's Crossing kept touching their forehead, frowning, and touching it again as though the answer might be different on the third attempt.

"They'll wake," the healer said, in the tone of a woman who wanted very much to believe her own invoice.

"Of course they will," I said, in the tone of a woman who had decided that believing things made them true, which is a skill every fence learns before she learns to count.

And then, while Rafael dreamed whatever dragon-vessels dream — I suspect ledger columns; I suspect the columns were balanced — I did the arithmetic myself. The hunt was moving north. Lady Vess's banners were two valleys over, camped at the mouth of the pass like a hand closing around a throat. And somewhere between here and there was the only thing standing between my sleeping storm-caller and a pyre: information.

So I went to get some. Alone. Without telling anyone, mostly because telling anyone would have required admitting aloud how bad an idea it was.

* * *

Vess's camp was not what the songs would have you expect. No drunken soldiery, no rape-and-pillage sprawl. It was clean. Tents pitched in ranks so precise you could have measured them with a carpenter's rule. Wardens drilled in silence. Cook-fires smoked without crackling. Even the horses seemed to stand at attention.

I slipped through the outer perimeter wearing the grey cloak of a laundress and carrying a basket of other people's linens, because there are two great truths about infiltration: nobody looks at a servant's face, and everybody looks at what she's carrying, which means you carry something boring enough to make them stop looking altogether.

The center of the camp held a command tent larger than any church I'd been thrown out of, and around its edges, on iron stands, burned braziers that gave off no heat I could feel from ten paces away. They didn't burn wood. They burned *embers* — little red hearts of light that pulsed slow as sleeping breath.

Ashen fire. Splits of the crown itself, or something kin to it. My stomach remembered being a girl of nine, kneeling before King Aldric's throne, sipping from the taster's cup while a man in ember-red robes watched my pupils dilate. Seven years of small doses. Seven years of learning to smile while my blood learned obedience. I know that light the way you know the smell of a house you grew up in.

I got close enough to hear voices through the tent canvas, which is where I learned that Lady Vess did not intend to serve the crown at all.

"—the vessel fails, the crown seeks new fuel," a man was saying. I placed him later: Corwin Hale, the hunt's chief inquisitor, a person I imagine was born middle-aged. "The last dragon gutters. When it dies—"

"When it dies," Vess said, "the crown does not starve. It *chooses*. Do you understand the difference, Hale? A starving beast devours what stumbles into its den. A choosing one selects." A pause, the sound of someone pouring wine. "It will select the gifted. Every storm-caller, every flame-speaker, every child who ever lit a candle with a wish. It will drink them down to keep its own ember alive. And whoever wears it will hold the leash."

"And you mean to wear it."

"I mean to *save* us all with it," Vess said, and here was the terrible thing — she said it gently. Not mad. Not hungry. Reasonable, the way frost is reasonable. "You've seen the realm these seven years, Hale. Guilds selling miracles to merchants while children starve beside bottled sunsets. Storm-callers hiding in ledgers. Magic hoarded, magic feared, magic leaking through every crack in the kingdom like water through a rotten roof. Chaos dressed up as freedom." Another pause. "Ordered tyranny is kinder than chaotic freedom. Someone must hold the reins. I am simply volunteering to be the hand."

"You'd be bound," Hale said. "Aldric was bound. Eleven minutes a day of himself, and the rest—"

"Aldric was *weak*. He fought it. Fighting it is what leaves you eleven minutes." I heard her set down the cup. "You don't fight a fire, Inquisitor. You *aim* it."

I stood there in my borrowed grey with my basket of strangers' laundry and understood, with the particular clarity that only arrives when it's too late to do anything useful, two things.

First: everything Rafael had suffered — Ysolde's bargain, the fire in their blood, the plan to shatter the crown on coronation day — all of it assumed the crown would fall into no one's hands. Vess intended to catch it.

Second: I had been standing still too long, and the laundress whose cloak I wore had just come around the corner of the supply tent carrying her own basket, looking at me the way people look at themselves.

She opened her mouth. I did the arithmetic of mercy versus speed, chose speed, and regretted it within four seconds, because her scream brought six wardens around the tents like crows to a dropped coin.

* * *

They put me in a command tent after all — just not the big one. A smaller one, with a folding table, a lamp, and Lady Vess herself, who turned out to be a compact woman of fifty-odd with iron-grey hair cut short for helmeting and eyes the color of a frozen pond. She dismissed her guards, which flattered me until I realized she didn't need them.

"You're not a laundress," she said. "Laundresses don't listen at canvas with their mouths slightly open. That's a courtier's habit."

"Fence," I said. There seemed little profit in lying, and some small pleasure in honesty. "Wren, of the Guild of Impossible Things. Formerly of the palace kitchens. Formerly of worse places."

"The taster." Something moved behind the pond-ice. Recognition, or memory. "I wondered what happened to you. You vanished the same year Aldric stopped being a king and started being a proclamation."

"You know about that."

"I know everything the crown knows. It remembers faces. It remembered yours." She leaned back. "Which brings us to the interesting question. Why does a retired poison-taster sneak into my camp the week before coronation?"

Here is where I should tell you I had a plan. I want you to know that I searched for one, thoroughly, the way you search pockets for a coin you're fairly sure isn't there. What I found instead was the truth, which has the advantage of being memorable.

"I came to learn whether you meant to destroy the crown or wear it. Now I know. So I suppose I came to fail."

Vess smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "Hale wants you broken on the rack by morning. He believes pain produces truth. I believe it produces *noise*." She slid a paper across the table. A warrant. My description, accurate down to the scar on my left thumb, and beneath it a second name in fresher ink: *the vessel known to harbor dragon-fire.* "We took a wounded storm-caller out of the caldera country three days ago. Carried by companions to a healer in Vell's Crossing. My riders reached the healer an hour ago. The companions were slower than you."

The lamp flickered. Or maybe that was me.

"Rafael can barely stand," I said.

"Yes. Which makes now the ideal time to collect them. Dragon-fire in mortal flesh — do you know what that's worth? The crown could drink it for a century. A vessel that carries fuel *and* wears the crown." She let that sit. "Unless, of course, something intervenes."

"What kind of thing."

"You tell me. You're famous for bargains, Wren of the Guild. The guildmaster sold you all out and you still speak well of the trade." She folded her hands. "So. Bargain."

I looked at the warrant. At the pond-ice eyes. At the brazier-light leaking under the tent flap, pulsing like something breathing, and I thought: *this is what it wants. This is what it has always wanted. Not obedience — appetite. And she thinks she can aim it.*

"Your riders don't know what they're riding toward," I said. "Dragon-fire burns what reaches for it. Your wardens will arrive at that healer's hut and find ash, and Rafael awake, and a sky full of weather. Or—" I made my voice steady, made it the voice I used across fences' tables, the voice that says *this is worth more than you think and less than you fear* "—or you recall them, and I give you the palace."

Vess went very still. "Go on."

"Eleven minutes a day. Everyone assumes that's when Aldric is weakest. It isn't. It's when the crown sleeps deepest — and there are ways into the king's lucidity that no warden knows. The old service corridors. The taster's door. I poured wine down Aldric's throat for seven years, my lady. I know which key opens which silence, and I know the hour the crown's attention thins to a thread. Without me, you take the palace by storm and the crown feels you coming a mile off — and a crown that feels threatened wakes its bearer screaming. Ask yourself what an ensorcelled king's guard does to intruders."

I hadn't verified half of that. Some of it I invented on the spot, in the grand tradition of my profession. But it was built on true bones, and true bones carry lies a long way.

Vess studied me for a long moment. Outside, the camp made its precise, silent noises. Inside, the lamp burned steady, and I burned steadier, and hated every second of it, because I knew exactly what I was doing. I was putting myself between the hunt and Rafael, and pricing my love in secrets, and calling it strategy.

"You realize," Vess said at last, almost softly, "that if you're lying, I will wear the crown, and I will find you, and I will spend a hundred years finding you."

"Lady," I said, "if you're wrong, you won't live long enough to gloat either. We'll call it mutual collateral."

She laughed — one short note, genuine, and I understood why soldiers followed her. Then she rang a small bell, and told the warden who appeared to send riders to recall the detachment from Vell's Crossing, and told another to give me a tent, dry clothes, and the illusion of freedom.

That night I sat in my borrowed tent listening to the embers pulse in their braziers, and I wrote — in my head, on no paper, because paper gets confiscated — the letter I would never send.

*Rafael. I have sold the palace doors to buy you one more day. I have put our names in the same ledger Vess keeps, and I don't know yet which column we land in. If I'm not beside you when you face the throne, it isn't betrayal. It's the price. Burn bright anyway.*

Then I heard the horns at the edge of camp, and the shout going tent to tent, and I stepped out into the ember-light to see prisoners being marched up the pass road under guard — three of them, limping, defiant — and the tall one in the middle, wrapped in a stolen cloak with frost still melting off their shoulders, lifted their head, found my face across forty yards of Vess's perfect orderly camp, and said, loudly enough for everyone:

"Wren. You have *got* to stop leaving notes."
