## 9. The Unbinding

There is a particular quality to light that falls through throne room windows on the morning a tyrant is crowned. It is gold, of course. Coronations are always gold — the banners, the plate, the anointing oil, the lies. But this gold had a color under it, the way old varnish has wood beneath, and the color was ember-orange, and it came from the crown itself.

I saw it from the doorway, at the end of a procession of accountants and clerks summoned to witness the realm's great balancing, and I thought: *there it is. The worst arithmetic in the world.* An artifact that eats fire, wearing the head of a woman who believes she is its master.

Wren stood beside me in her borrowed clerk's grey, hands folded, spine straight, the very portrait of a person who has never stolen anything in her life. Only I could see the tension in her wrists, the way her thumbs pressed together like a woman holding a door shut from the inside.

"Eleven minutes," she murmured, not moving her lips.

"I know."

"He's been lucid since dawn. I bribed the under-chamberlain to dose his wine early."

"I know," I said. "You told me. Twice."

"I'm nervous."

"You're nervous," I said, "because in about four minutes you're going to commit high treason in front of four hundred witnesses, and the only person standing between you and the headsman is a storm-caller with dragon-fire in their veins who has never done this before."

Her eyes flicked to mine. Grey eyes, the color of Ashvale's sky in the weeks before rain. "That's the part that frightens you," she said. "Not the dying."

"The dying is straightforward," I said. "It's the *after* that worries me. I've spent seven years being no one. I'm about to be extremely someone, and I haven't the wardrobe for it."

The herald's voice rang out — *Lady Vess of the Northern Reach, Commander of the King's Hunt, anointed protector of the realm* — and the crowd's murmur rose like water coming to a boil. Vess walked the length of the hall in white and steel, unhurried, and I will grant her this: she did not look like a usurper. She looked like a woman arriving at a destination she had mapped years ago, down to the last step.

Aldric knelt at the altar-step. They had dressed him in mourning white, which I suspect was Vess's idea of a joke, and the chains — I saw them now, thin as wire, threaded through the sleeves of his robe, running up under the collar to the crown's rim. Not holding him down. Holding him *together*. Without them he was a king. With them he was a puppet with excellent posture.

His eyes found mine across the hall.

Eleven minutes of lucidity, and he spent the first of them finding the accountant in the back row and *knowing* me. I don't know what he saw. Perhaps the dragon-fire, banked in my chest, answering the crown's ember like a bell answers a struck bell. Perhaps just a person who had come a long way to keep a promise.

His mouth moved. No sound. But I have spent seven years reading ledgers, and I read it anyway.

*Now.*

* * *

The anointing oil went on. The scepter was placed in Vess's hands. The Archbishop of the Ashen Rite — a man whose sermons I had once balanced the guild's donations against and found wanting — lifted the crown from its velvet cushion with both hands, reverent as a man lifting a sleeping infant.

The ember at its heart brightened.

And the crown *woke*.

I felt it the way you feel a hand close around your wrist. Across the hall, every gifted person in that crowd — the ones hiding, the ones who had come to watch their hunter crowned, the ones who didn't yet know what they were — felt it too. A great inhale. The artifact tasting the room, finding the small fires of the small talents, and finding, above them all, the enormous banked furnace it had been carried north to meet.

Vess's head bowed under the weight. Her hands came up — not to receive it, but to *steady* it, the way you steady a horse that has decided something you haven't agreed to yet.

"No," she said, quietly, to the crown, and only I was close enough and listening hard enough to hear it. "You'll take what I give you. Nothing more."

Brave, stupid woman. She thought she was wearing it. She had been wearing it for weeks, in a sense, in her dreams, and it had been measuring her the whole time.

The crown came down.

And I walked out of the row.

* * *

I will not pretend the walk was dignified. My legs shook. The hall was very long, and every step echoed, and by the third step the guards had noticed, and by the fifth the Archbishop had stopped mid-liturgy, and by the seventh Vess had turned her crowned head — her *crowned* head, the fire already crawling down from the rim into her hair like ivy in fast bloom — and looked at me with the flat assessment of a commander reading a battlefield.

"Stop that man," she said. Or tried to say. The crown was in her voice now, a second speaker underneath, and what came out was layered, harmonized, *wrong*: **"STOP THAT MAN."**

Guards moved. Wren moved faster.

I heard it behind me — the crack of the wire chains parting under her shears, the ones she'd spent three days sharpening against a whetstone while I pretended to sleep, the ones she'd hidden in her sleeve the way she'd once hidden stolen signet rings. Aldric came up off his knees like a man surfacing from deep water, and the sound he made was not a shout. It was a *breath*. The first unforced breath in eleven years, and four hundred people heard it and did not understand what they were hearing.

I understood. It was the most beautiful sound I have ever heard, and I was too busy to enjoy it.

Because the crown had finished tasting Vess and found her wanting — found her *mortal* — and it did what hungry things do when the meal in front of them is insufficient. It began to eat her anyway. She screamed, and the scream came out as fire, and the banners at the hall's far end caught light that had not touched them.

"RAFAEL COLDHARBOUR," I said, to the hall, to the guards frozen mid-stride, to the Archbishop with his oil and his silence, to Wren, to Aldric, to the realm entire. "Keeper of ledgers for the Guild of Impossible Things. Storm-caller. Gifted. *Here.*"

My name in my own mouth, spoken aloud, for the first time in seven years. It fit better than I remembered. It fit like something I'd outgrown and then grown back into.

"You wanted a hunt," I said, and the storm in my chest, the dragon-fire Ysolde had poured into me in a caldera of cooling glass, rose up my throat like a tide deciding. "Here is the quarry."

I did not call the storm *down*. That was always the mistake in the old stories, the storm-callers of legend hurling lightning from the sky like angry gods, and look how the legends end. Ysolde had taught me better in her last hour, with her heart guttering: *The crown is a fire that eats fire, little ledger-keeper. You do not fight a fire with lightning. You fight it with a door.*

So I opened a door.

Through my own veins.

* * *

Here is what it is like to channel dragon-fire through mortal flesh while standing in a throne room: it is like being a riverbed. Everything that has ever fallen as rain on the whole watershed of your life comes down at once — every storm you ever swallowed, every year of ink and arithmetic, every night you lay awake counting the names in the ledger so you wouldn't have to count your own cowardice, every hour of Wren's laughter, Aldric's eleven minutes, Ysolde's last ember, the child burned in the market square whose name I had cut out of the ledger with my own hands and carried north like a stone.

It all came down the riverbed at once, and it went *through* me, and it went *into* the crown.

The crown drank. That was its nature; it could not help itself. It drank the fire I fed it the way it had drunk dragons for three hundred years, and for one glorious moment Vess's screaming stopped, because the thing on her head was sated, was *distracted*, was mine.

"Now," I said, through my teeth, to the hall, to Wren, to anyone.

Wren did not need telling twice. I heard her running. And Aldric — mortal Aldric, unchained Aldric, a king with nothing left but his own name — Aldric took the crown off Lady Vess's head with his bare hands.

It fought him. Of course it fought him. But it was mid-draught, drinking from a door it did not know was a door, and a crown cannot guard its rim and its heart at once, and his hands were the hands of a man who had been *wearing* it for eleven years and knew exactly where it gripped.

He lifted it. He held it up, trembling, in the gold morning light, an offering and an accusation.

And I let go of the door.

* * *

You have seen a forge. You know what happens when the smith quenches white-hot iron — the hiss, the steam, the violence of heat meeting cold. Now imagine the reverse. Imagine white-hot fire meeting a fire that eats, and the eating going *wrong*, the hunger turning inward on itself, the crown consuming its own ember because I had filled it past its capacity with a fire older and larger and angrier than any it was built to hold.

It shattered.

Not all at once. First the rim, cracking like river ice in spring, a sound that went through the hall and through the walls and through, I am told, the whole city of Ashvale, every bell ringing without being struck. Then the ember at its heart — three hundred years of stolen dragon-fire, the last of Ysolde's line, the leash of kings — flaring once, bright as a small sun, and going out.

Not extinguished. *Released.* I felt it go — felt it pass through me on its way out of the world, a warmth like a hand on the back of my neck, like forgiveness, like Ysolde saying *well done, little ledger-keeper* — and then it was ash on the morning air, and the throne room was very quiet, and very bright, and full of people on their knees who were not praying.

Vess fell. I will say this for her: she fell fighting, even unconscious, even burned, even emptied — her hand still reaching for the place where the crown had been, her mouth still shaping orders no one would ever follow. Whether she lived, the surgeons would decide. Whether she *ruled*, that question had just been answered in front of four hundred witnesses.

I fell too.

* * *

I remember the floor. Cold stone, which was a mercy, because I was fairly sure I was on fire and the stone disagreed with me, and I have always trusted audited numbers over feelings. I remember hands — Wren's hands, on my face, on my chest, checking for a heartbeat with the same brisk competence she used for checking a mark's purse.

"Stay," she was saying. "Stay, stay, *stay* — you absolute ledger-keeping fool, you *stayed down* in that caldera, you can stay down here—"

"I'm balancing," I told her. My voice came out as smoke. "The accounts. Someone has to—"

"Someone has to shut *up*," said Wren, who was crying, which she would deny to her grave, and pressed her forehead to mine in front of four hundred witnesses, the guild's fence and the realm's most wanted storm-caller, concealing nothing, hiding nothing, both of us extremely someone at last.

Above us, King Aldric — mortal, unbound, a man holding eleven years of stolen breath in his lungs — stood over the ashes of the Ashen Crown, and when he spoke, his voice was only his own, and it carried to the back of the hall.

"Let it be known," he said, "what was done here, and by whom."

And then he looked down at me — at the storm-caller, the liar, the keeper of impossible accounts, lying scorched and smoking on his throne room floor — and said the words that would follow me out of this hall and into everything that came after:

"Bring me the guild's ledger. *All* of it. Every name — and every name kept safe."

Wren's hand found mine in the ash.

The hunt was over. The accounting, I suspected, had only just begun.
