## 10. Ashvale, After

They tell me I died on the throne room floor for the better part of an hour. Wren disputes this. She says it was forty minutes, and that she counted, because counting is what you do when there is nothing else in your hands but a man's cooling fingers and the whole of your stubbornness.

I have learned not to argue with Wren about numbers. She is, after all, the one who taught me most of them.

What I remember of that hour — or forty minutes — is mostly fire. Not the crown's fire, which had spent seven chapters of my life trying to eat me from the inside out, but Ysolde's: slow and old and patient, banked in my chest like a hearth that would never quite go cold. It held me together while everything else came apart. When I woke, the first thing I saw was the shattered Ashen Crown lying in pieces on the marble, each fragment dark as a burnt-out lamp. The last ember of dragon-fire, finally allowed to be dead.

The second thing I saw was Wren, asleep in a chair dragged to my bedside, still wearing the guard's uniform she'd stolen three days earlier, one hand curled around mine like she'd signed a contract with no exit clause.

"Your penmanship is terrible," she said, without opening her eyes.

"I'm unconscious."

"You're awake now." She opened her eyes then, and looked at me the way she looks at ledgers she intends to audit thoroughly. "Welcome back, keeper."

I did not yet know what she meant by that. Give me a moment. I'm getting there.

* * *

King Aldric repealed the hunt nine days later, standing on the same balcony where the proclamations had been nailed up in iron and thunder. He was a different man than the one who'd issued them — thinner, greyer, moving like someone learning to walk again in his own body after years of being a passenger in it. Eleven minutes of lucidity a day will do that to a person. So will carrying the memory of every order you gave while something else spoke through your mouth.

He read the repeal himself. No heralds, no velvet voice. Just a king with a paper and a voice gone hoarse from saying things he actually meant:

"The hunt for the gifted is ended. Their names struck from every list. Their crimes — those invented by the crown — forgiven. And let it be recorded that the realm owes them a debt, which this crown intends to pay."

Then he did something no one expected. He rolled up the proclamation, walked down into the square, and handed it to a woman in the crowd who flinched away from him before she realized who was offering. A storm-caller. One of ours. One of the names I had kept.

She took the paper. She didn't smile. Neither did he. But something passed between them that no ledger I've ever kept has a column for, and I watched from the edge of the crowd with Wren's shoulder against mine, and I thought: *this*. This is what all of it was for. Not the shattering. Not the fire. This small, unglamorous moment of a frightened woman being handed her own name back.

The gifted came out of hiding slowly, the way frost comes off a window — patchy, reluctant, melting first where the sun hit. Some never came out at all; I don't blame them. Trust is a currency, and the realm had spent its entire reserve buying tyranny. But they came. They came to Ashvale, to the guildhall steps, because word travels in the impossible trade faster than anywhere else, and the word was simple:

*The ledger-keeper keeps.*

* * *

Here is the trouble with becoming famous for surviving an unmaking: everyone wants a piece of the survivor.

For a month I tried to go back to my old life. Ink, arithmetic, the quiet corner desk by the north window where I had balanced accounts for bottled sunsets and borrowed years and one sleeping god (who, incidentally, woke during the coronation, yawned, assessed the political situation, and went back to sleep — even gods know when a story isn't theirs). For a month I sat at that desk and told myself I was still anonymous Rafael Coldharbour, exiled storm-caller, nobody of no consequence.

It didn't hold. It couldn't. My hands were marked now — fine lines of silver tracing my forearms like veins of ore, visible whenever I called so much as a breeze. Dragon-fire leaves a signature. You cannot hide a hearth in your chest behind a clerk's collar forever, and every child in the guildhall could see me glowing faintly through my sleeves when it rained.

"You're the worst anonymous person in history," Wren observed over dinner. "You unmade a crown in front of four hundred witnesses. There are ballads. They're *bad*, but they exist."

"One of them rhymes 'Coldharbour' with 'sorrowful harbour.'"

"I wrote that one."

"You're a treasure, Wren."

"I'm a fence. I know good merchandise when I move it." She pushed a document across the table. "Speaking of which."

I looked down. It was the guild's charter, the original, older than the guildhall itself. And pinned beneath it, the guildmaster's seal — broken, formally, in front of two witnesses whose names I will keep out of this account, because some debts are settled privately or not at all. The old man had sold names to survive the first purge. Then, in the end, faced with Vess's rise, he had chosen to stand in the throne room doorway and bar it with his own body for the ninety seconds Wren needed to reach Aldric's chains. He had paid his account in full. The seal-breaking was ceremonial. So was what came next.

The charter named a successor to the guildmaster's chair.

It named me.

"I can't accept this," I said.

"You can, though. That's the horrible part. Look at the requirements." She tapped the charter. "*One:* a keeper who knows every name in the ledger. *Two:* a keeper the gifted will trust. *Three:* a keeper who cannot be bought, because they have already been offered everything and refused it." She smiled, thin and real. "Congratulations. You're catastrophically overqualified."

"Wren—"

"Rafael." She said my name the way she said it only when it mattered, both syllables given their full weight, no irony anywhere in them. "You burned your pages rather than sell them. You carried dragon-fire rather than let the crown take anyone else. You stood in front of a court that wanted you dead and told the truth with lightning in your hands." She closed my fingers around the charter. "There is no one else. There never was."

* * *

So now I am the guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things, which sounds grander than it is. Mostly it means I sit in a bigger chair and the ink stains climb higher up my arm.

The first thing I did was rewrite the ledger.

Not amend. Rewrite. Every page, every name, in my own hand, with new rules bound into the cover itself — rules I paid a fair price for, negotiated honestly with a hedge-witch who owed me for a bottled sunset. The ledger cannot be copied now. Cannot be sold, stolen, or surrendered. It opens only for a keeper's hand, and it closes against any crown, any commander, any bargain. Lady Vess is dead; her lieutenants scattered like sparks off an anvil; but someday, somewhere, someone will want a list of the gifted again, and when they come asking, the answer will be the same one I gave the old guildmaster before the end:

*Every name kept. None sold.*

It took me eleven weeks. Wren sat across from me the whole time, doing her own books, occasionally reaching over to fix my penmanship. On the last page, past all the names, I wrote a single line of accounting that no auditor will ever question and every reader will understand:

*Balance carried forward: owed to the living, payable daily.*

* * *

Spring came to Ashvale the way it always does — badly, then all at once. The ash-choked streets cleared. The market square, the one where a child once burned, got a fountain, because the city voted on it and I cast the deciding ballot as a private citizen, thank you very much. Children play in the water now. Some of them spark when they laugh. No one calls the wardens anymore. There are no wardens.

Aldric rules a kingdom learning to live without magic's leash, and he does it the way he does everything since the crown broke: carefully, honestly, and with visible relief. He writes to me sometimes. Short letters, a mortal man's letters, asking after grain tariffs and border disputes and, once, whether the guild might broker peace talks with the hill-clans, since apparently "impossible" is now a diplomatic category. I keep his letters in a drawer. Not the official ones — the short notes at the bottom, the ones that say things like *Thank you for the eleven minutes. I intend to use all the rest.*

And Wren. Wren, who knew my gift all along and never sold it, never spent it, never once made me feel that love was a debt I owed payment on. We share rooms above the guildhall now, hers and mine, with a window that faces east. In the mornings the light comes in and catches the silver in my arms and turns it briefly gold, and she watches from the pillows with her ledger-brain running numbers I am not permitted to ask about, and I think: *contentment built on nothing hidden.* It turns out that was the only foundation it ever really needed.

Yesterday a girl came up the guildhall steps. Sixteen maybe, rain-soaked, terrified, hands sparking with a gift she'd been taught to hate. She stood in my doorway and asked, in the smallest voice, whether this was the place where people like her got erased.

I set down my pen.

"No," I said, and showed her my marked arms, my open ledger, my chair. "This is the place where we write you down — and keep you."

Her name goes in tonight. New page. New book, really — the old one is full, gloriously, impossibly full.

But as I dipped my pen just now, preparing to write her in, I noticed something I had not seen in all my weeks of copying: pressed between the final leaf and the binding, thin as a whisper, a single scale. Black at the edge, gold at the heart. Still warm.

Ysolde is dying in the northern reach — but dying, it seems, is not the same as finished.

And dragons, I am learning, always settle their accounts.
