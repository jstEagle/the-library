## 1. The Quiet Trade

The charm in Xanthe's palm was the size of a sparrow's egg, carved from river-worn bone into the shape of a sleeping cat, and it was whispering.

*There now,* it murmured, in a voice like dry leaves settling. *There now. The night is long but you are longer. There now.*

She turned it under the lamplight of her stall and permitted herself one small, private satisfaction. Three days of work. A knucklebone of lamb for the core — nothing grand, nothing that would make a Warden's resonance-glass so much as fog — and around it, patient as embroidery, the words she'd coaxed from an old woman who had died content two winters past and had been glad to be asked to stay awhile.

Not all of the dead stayed. That was the first thing people got wrong about bone-carving, if they thought about it at all, which they mostly didn't, because thinking about it got you burned. The dead were not a haunting. They were a leaving, and some of them left slowly, reluctantly, the way a guest lingers at the door with one hand still on the latch. Xanthe had learned to catch them on their way out. *One small kindness before you go? A word for the living? Something soft?*

Most said yes. The dying wanted, more than anything, to have mattered, and a charm that whispered comfort to a frightened child in the dark years later — that mattered. It was a good trade. She took their last warmth and gave it somewhere useful.

The trick was keeping it small. Keep it small, keep it quiet, keep it *boring*. In Caerlum, magic was not illegal the way theft was illegal; theft had a fine, a cell, a term. Magic was illegal the way plague was illegal. They burned you in the Ashmarket on market day, and they sold sausages nearby, because the crowd got peckish, because everything in Caerlum eventually became commerce.

So: no grand workings. No storm-calling, no prophecy, no raising of anything. Just little charms that held a dead person's kindness like a pressed flower holds summer. Small enough to hide. Quiet enough to survive.

Xanthe had survived three years this way, and she intended to survive thirty more, and then die peacefully in bed of old age and mild cheese, and she told herself this every morning while she set up her stall in Tanners' Row with its crooked awning and its display of completely innocent carvings: buttons, combs, buttons, hairpins, buttons. She was very good at buttons. Nobody had ever suspected the button-carver of anything, which was either a triumph or a commentary on buttons.

"Morning, Nystrom."

Brannick the cooper, filling half the street and most of it elbow. He was holding out his hand like a man asking for a tool.

"For the missus," he said. "The sleeping kind. She's been up three nights running with her hip, and my snoring isn't helping, though she claims it's my face."

"Your face is very restful," Xanthe said, selecting a charm from under the counter — a smooth oval of sheep bone, carved with a crescent. Inside it, a fisherman who had drowned sixty years ago (and who had been, by his own cheerful account, entirely at peace about it) hummed a low wordless tune that could pull a fevered mind down into sleep like a boat into still water. "Three coppers. And tell her to stop sleeping on the left side, it's the hip that complains."

"How do you—" Brannick squinted at her. Then he shrugged, the great shrug of a man who had decided long ago that Xanthe Nystrom knew things about bones the way other people knew things about weather, and found it unremarkable. "Three coppers. You're a marvel, but don't let it go to your head."

He clanked away. Xanthe exhaled and did not let it go to her head. That was the whole discipline of her life, really: not letting it go to her head, her hands, her voice. Carve plain. Speak plainer. Be the boring button woman until boredom became armor.

It worked. It had worked for three years. Which is why what happened next felt less like disaster than like a door quietly coming off its hinges in a house she'd thought was sound.

* * *

The trouble arrived wearing a grey traveling cloak and the expression of someone who had cried recently and professionally. A young woman, mid-twenties, gloves too fine for Tanners' Row, standing at the stall with her jaw set like a woman purchasing something shameful.

"You're the bone-carver," she said.

"I'm a bone-carver. There are four of us on this street."

"You're *the* one." Her voice dropped. "My mother died in the spring. I have her — I have a rib. Don't look at me like that, everyone keeps something. I want... I want to hear that she's not angry with me."

Ah. Xanthe kept her hands moving, sorting buttons, the picture of a craftswoman mildly inconvenienced. This was the dangerous kind of customer. Not the ones who bought comfort charms off the shelf without knowing why they worked. The ones who came with grief and a bone and a request that sounded, spoken aloud, exactly like what the Wardens burned people for.

"I carve buttons," Xanthe said pleasantly. "And combs. And—"

"My brother says you're the one." The young woman's chin trembled. "He heard it from the dyer's wife, who heard it from—please. Please. I'll pay whatever—"

"Madam." Xanthe leaned forward and lowered her voice to the register she used for talking customers down off ledges. "If I were the sort of carver people gossip about, do you imagine I'd still have fingers? Whatever you're grieving, take it to a temple. Light a candle. The candles are cheap on Thursdays."

Something flickered across the young woman's face — shame, mostly, and beneath it a stubbornness Xanthe recognized and dreaded. Customers with stubbornness came back. Customers with stubbornness and grief came back *worse*.

But she went. She pressed coins onto the counter anyway — payment, apparently, for having been refused — and walked off up the Row with her shoulders squared against the rain that hadn't started yet but was thinking about it.

Xanthe looked down at the coins. Then at the shelf of charms. And stopped.

The sleeping-cat charm — the one she'd spent three days on, the gentle one, the one that only ever said *there now* — was trembling.

Not whispering. Trembling. It rattled against the shelf like a tooth chattering, and when she picked it up, the voice inside it wasn't the old woman's dry-leaf murmur anymore. It was stretched thin and high, and it wasn't saying *there now.*

It was screaming.

Not words. That was the terrible part — Xanthe had listened to the dead for most of her life, and the dead always had words, even the confused ones, even the ones three hundred years gone whose speech had worn down to vowels. But this was a scream with no words in it at all, a sound like a soul caught in a door, and it poured out of the little bone cat and up her arm and into her teeth, and around her the market went on — Brannick arguing about hinges, a child whining over honey-bread — oblivious.

"*Stop,*" she breathed, cupping both hands around the charm, hunching over it like a woman sheltering a match. "*Stop, stop — who is this? What's wrong?*"

The scream bent. Shaped itself, barely, into something almost language, and what she caught of it — fragments, splinters —

*beneath*

— *the crown, the crown, it wears him, it wears him—*

— *small, oh, small, it is so small, find the—*

Then the old woman's voice surfaced again through the din, faint, exhausted, the way a swimmer surfaces:

*Girl. Girl, listen. Something is wrong under the city. Something has been wrong a long time. And there is a piece of it loose now, a bright piece, a child-piece — it's coming toward you, bone-speaker, it's already coming—*

The charm went silent.

Not quiet. Silent — hollow, the way a bell is hollow after it cracks. Xanthe turned it in her hands. The carving was unchanged: the sleeping cat, the soft ears, the closed eyes. But when she held it to her ear the way a customer might, hoping for *there now*, there was nothing. Whatever had passed through it had used it like a doorway and left it empty.

Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on the counter and made them stop, because Tanners' Row did not tolerate shaking hands any more than it tolerated magic, and because a Warden patrol had just turned the corner by the tannery — two of them, grey cloaks, resonance-glasses hanging at their belts like lanterns.

They weren't looking at her. They were walking their usual circuit, bored, wet-booted. One of them paused to kick a dog out of his path and laugh about it.

Xanthe smiled her button smile and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *something is coming toward me, and it is already coming, and I have no idea what it is, and the dead have never once screamed before today.*

She wrapped the cracked charm in cloth and put it in the bottom of her box, under the buttons, where things went to be forgotten.

The rain started at dusk, right on schedule. Caerlum rain, cold and sideways, turning the gutters to brown rivers. Xanthe packed up early — no sales after the charm broke anyway, her heart wasn't in the patter — and locked the stall, and stood a moment under the awning watching the street empty.

Tanners' Row at closing time was a familiar litany: shutters banging, the dyer hauling in his frames, Brannick's silhouette huge in his doorway, the smell of wet wool and tanning lime and, underneath, the river. Home. Ugly, cramped, watched-over home. Three years of it, safe, small, hers.

She was halfway to her door, key already in her hand, when she realized the street was too quiet.

Not empty — quiet. The particular quiet of a place holding its breath. The shutters had stopped banging. The dyer was frozen halfway through folding his frame, staring up the Row toward the Spire road, where the lamps burned their official white instead of the warm yellow everyone else used.

And down the middle of Tanners' Row, small and barefoot and soaked through, walking through puddles without seeming to feel them, came a child.

A girl, maybe seven, maybe eight, thin as a bundle of kindling, clutching something against her chest with both hands. She walked like sleepwalkers walk, straight and certain, and every step she took, Xanthe felt it — not in her ears, not in her mind, but in her *bones*, in the marrow, a low resonant hum like the note of a struck bell that wouldn't fade. Nothing she carved had ever done that. Nothing alive should be able to do that.

The child stopped in front of her stall. Looked up. Rain ran off her chin.

And she spoke — one sentence, clear and unhurried, in a language older than the Spire, older than the kingdom, older than the stones of Caerlum itself — and every bone in Xanthe's body answered it.
