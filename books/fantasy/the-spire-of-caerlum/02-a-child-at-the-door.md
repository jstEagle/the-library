## 2. A Child at the Door

The rain came down on Caerlum the way debts come due — all at once, and with malice aforethought.

Xanthe Nystrom heard it hammering her shutters and thought, not for the first time, that the city had a talent for weather. It saved its worst storms for nights when a body wanted nothing more than to sit by a dying fire, rub oil into her carving tools, and pretend she was an ordinary woman with ordinary hands.

She was not, of course. Ordinary women did not have to be careful about what they carved.

She sat at her workbench in the back room of her shop — *Nystrom & Daughters*, the sign said, though there were no daughters and never had been; she'd liked the sound of it, the suggestion of a family she'd invented out of whole cloth — and turned a piece of riverbone over in her fingers. A charm, half-finished. A widow on Candlemaker's Row had ordered it after her husband died, the way people did. They came to Xanthe because her charms felt *kind*. Warm in the pocket. Like being remembered.

They didn't know why. If they knew why, the Wardens would come, and the Wardens came with pitch and rope, and then there would be no more widows' charms at all.

"Small," she reminded herself, as she did every night, tapping the bone against her palm like a little ritual. "Keep it small. Keep it quiet."

Her gift worked like this: when she carved, truly carved, listening while she worked, the dead who had lived near the material spoke through it. Bone remembered best. Wood mumbled. Stone mostly sulked. She could put a grandmother's lullaby into a pendant for a homesick girl three streets over, and the girl would sleep, and never know she'd been sung to by someone forty years in the ground.

It was a small magic. That was the point. Small enough to hide inside a trade, small enough that even she sometimes pretended it wasn't real — that the whispers were imagination, that the comfort she sold was just good craftsmanship and a soft voice.

She was lying to herself with the bone halfway to the knife when the knocking started.

Not knocking, precisely. Pounding. Frantic, small fists on her street door, and under the rain she caught something worse: a sound from inside her own chest, a low hum in her sternum, like a tuning fork someone else had struck.

Xanthe went very still.

Her bones only answered to one thing. Carved things. Things that held the dead. And nothing on her shelves was singing tonight — she'd checked, the way she always checked, running her fingertips along each finished charm the way a mother counts children before bed.

The pounding came again. Then a voice — thin, high, exhausted — saying something in a language she had never heard and yet, horribly, almost understood. Not the words. The *shape* of them. The way you can hear your own name across a crowded market without knowing how.

She took up the poker from beside the cold hearth, which would stop absolutely nothing but made her feel better, and opened the door.

A child fell into her shop.

Not walked. Fell — caught itself on the doorframe with both hands, swayed there, dripping. Seven years old, maybe eight. Thin as a winter branch, hair plastered flat, wearing clothes that had been fine once and expensive once and were now ruined beyond any tailor's prayer. The child looked up at Xanthe with enormous dark eyes and said, urgently, desperately, the same strange syllables again.

And every bone in Xanthe's body *hummed*.

She staggered back a step. It was like standing too close to a bell. The hum ran up her spine and settled behind her teeth, and beneath it — faint, wordless, unmistakable — she felt something she hadn't felt uncarved in all her thirty-one years: *the dead*. Not one dead. Many. A chorus pressed up against the inside of the child like moths against glass.

"Oh no," Xanthe said aloud, to nobody. "No, no. I don't take strays."

The child, understanding none of this, understood all of it in the way that matters, and grabbed her apron with both small wet hands.

* * *

Getting the child dry was the work of an hour. Getting the child to *stop talking* was impossible, because stopping talking required the child to talk in the first place, and the child talked constantly — a low, urgent stream of that old, old language, rising and falling like water over stones. Xanthe wrapped her in a blanket older than both of them combined, sat her by the rekindled fire, put bread and honey in front of her, and watched the food disappear with the single-minded efficiency of someone who had not eaten recently or well.

"You're going to be the death of me," Xanthe told her. "I want you to know that. Three years. Three years I've kept my head down, kept my carvings dull, kept my ears shut. I've been so *boring*, do you understand? Boring is survival. And now there's a drowned kitten on my doorstep humming like a graveyard."

The child paused mid-bite, looked at her gravely, and said something long and melodic that ended on a note Xanthe's bones found deeply agreeable.

"Don't try to charm me. It won't—" It already had, a little. "Eat your bread."

Only when the child's shivering stopped did Xanthe dare look properly at the one thing she'd been avoiding: the child's fist, which had not unclenched since the doorstep. Even asleep, even eating, the small hand stayed curled around something.

"Let's see it, then," Xanthe said gently. "Whatever you're holding, it's the reason my ribs are ringing like a cathedral bell, so we may as well be introduced."

The child looked down at her own hand as if noticing it for the first time. Slowly, finger by reluctant finger, she opened it.

An amulet. Broken.

It had been a crown once — Xanthe saw that immediately, the way you see a face in a cracked portrait. A circlet of pale metal worked into seven points, and it had been beautiful, and someone had *snapped* it, deliberately, wrenching away two of the points and leaving ragged stumps. The break was old. The edges were worn smooth, polished by years of desperate handling.

Xanthe reached out — stopped herself — reached again, and touched it with one fingertip.

The hum became a chord.

It wasn't loud. That was the terrible part. It was *quiet* — a vast, patient quiet, like snowfall, like the pause before a king speaks — and in it Xanthe heard, for half a heartbeat, a voice. Not a whisper from a relic. Not a grandmother's lullaby. This was a soul, or the torn edge of one, singing a single sustained note of grief and patience and *waiting*, and underneath it, dozens of other voices, old voices, murmuring in the same pre-kingdom tongue the child spoke:

*Found. Found. Found at last.*

Xanthe snatched her hand back and knocked her tea off the bench. It was that kind of night.

"All right," she said, mopping at the floor with her sleeve, heart slamming. "All right. You are trouble. You are walking, talking, rain-soaked trouble with a broken crown in your fist, and I should put you back out that door and bolt it."

The child looked at her. Rain-damp, blanket-swaddled, bread crumb on her chin, and utterly unafraid — which was somehow worse than fear. As if she knew exactly what Xanthe was. As if she'd *come* because of it.

"How did you find me?" Xanthe asked. "Out of everyone in Caerlum. How?"

The child answered at length, earnestly, entirely in the old tongue, gesturing at Xanthe's hands, at her tools, at her own chest, at the amulet. Xanthe understood perhaps one word in twenty, and that word — repeated twice, with great emphasis — sounded like *nyss-karen*. Which was, she realized with a chill settling into her stomach, close enough to *Nystrom* that coincidence stopped being comfortable.

Someone had sent this child. Someone dead, or something older than dead, had pointed a mute, wandering fragment of — of whatever this was — at her door, in the rain, in the dark.

"I need to think," Xanthe announced. "You— sleep. There. By the fire. Don't touch anything, don't sing to anything, and above all don't *hum* at anything."

The child lay down where indicated, tucked the broken crown under her cheek like a doll, and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way only children and soldiers can sleep.

* * *

Xanthe did not sleep.

She sat at her workbench with a candle guttering and the oldest piece of bone in her collection laid out before her — a knuckle joint, brown with age, dug from the foundations when she first rented the shop. She'd never dared carve it. Too old. Too deep. Whatever slept in that bone had been sleeping longer than Caerlum had walls.

But her hands were shaking, and the child's breathing filled the room, and the broken crown sat on the bench between them, humming its patient snowfall hum.

"If I'm already hanged," she muttered, "I may as well be hanged for something interesting."

She picked up her finest knife, set the edge to the old bone, and began — not a charm, not a shape, just a line. One listening line, the way you'd open a door you weren't sure you wanted open.

The bone woke up.

Not a whisper. Not a murmur. A voice, dry and precise and startled, speaking perfect, crisp Trade-Tongue directly into the marrow of her skull:

"*Finally.* Do you have any idea how long I've waited in this shop? Now listen carefully, bone-carver — we haven't much time, and there is so very much you don't know about that child."
