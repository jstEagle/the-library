## 3. What the Bones Remember

The child had been asleep for two days, and in that time Xanthe had learned three things: the child's name was unpronounceable and sounded like water going over stones; the child would eat anything so long as it was warm; and the amulet — the little broken crown of gray metal — hummed when the child dreamed.

Not sang. Not whispered. *Hummed*, low and patient, like a hive thinking about honey.

Xanthe sat at her workbench with her back to the sleeping pallet and tried very hard not to think about it. Thinking about it made the bones in her own hands itch. She'd taken to wrapping them in linen at night, which was absurd — she was thirty-one years old, a respectable tradeswoman, a woman whose charms were famous up and down Copperlane for being *comforting* without being *peculiar* — and here she was, bandaged like a woman who'd lost an argument with a cheese grater, because her metacarpals wouldn't stop answering a child's dreams.

"You're being ridiculous," she told herself quietly.

The bones did not disagree, but they didn't stop itching either.

She turned instead to the relic on her bench, because work was the only prayer she'd ever trusted. It had come to her three winters ago from a rag-and-bone man with a limp and a dishonest smile: a knucklebone, human, yellowed with age, set in a cage of tarnished silver. He'd sworn it came off a saint. She'd sworn she believed him, and both of them had known better. But the bone had *weight*. Not the weight of the dead — she knew that weight, the faint tug like a fishhook behind her sternum — but something older, denser, like a stone that had been sitting at the bottom of a river since before the river had a name.

And old bones, she had learned, remembered more clearly than young ones. Young ghosts were all feeling — grief, spite, longing, the wet noise of unfinished business. Old bones were *archivists*. They'd had time to sort themselves out.

Tonight she needed an archivist. Because the child had spoken a word yesterday — one word, in that language like water over stones — while touching the amulet, and Xanthe's uncarved, unwrapped, entirely civilian hand had resonated hard enough to make her teeth ache. And because the Wardens had burned a charm-seller in Lowmarket on Tuesday, and Xanthe had heard, through three streets and a wall, the exact moment his charms went silent.

She fitted the bone-cage into her clamps. She took up her finest graver. And she did what she always did when she wanted a conversation: she carved a question into the thing that held the answer.

The first stroke was a spiral. Old bones liked spirals; spirals said *I have time.*

The second stroke was a name-shape, the little hooked glyph she used for *who?*

"Hello," she murmured, in the way she'd been doing since she was nine years old and had discovered that the lamb bone in the midden pit could tell her where its flock had grazed. "I know you're in there. I'm not going to sell you. I just want to talk."

The cold came up through the bench the way it always did — not a chill of air but a chill of *attention*, as if somewhere a lamp had been turned toward her. The knucklebone warmed against the silver, which was wrong, backwards, and exactly how she knew it was working.

*Who asks,* said a voice in her marrow, dry and precise and faintly amused, *with such good manners? Nobody has asked me anything with good manners in four hundred years.*

"I'm Xanthe Nystrom," she said under her breath. "Bone-carver. Copperlane. And I need to know about a crown."

*A crown.* The attention sharpened. *There are crowns and there are crowns, carver. There is the tin circlet the regent wears to funerals, and there is the one beneath—*

The voice stopped. Not faded. *Stopped*, like a door shut firmly from inside.

"Beneath what?" Xanthe whispered.

Silence. Then, slowly, reluctantly, the cold attention returned, and when it spoke again it had lost its amusement entirely.

*Beneath the Spire,* it said. *I was archivist to the Spire before your kingdom had this name, girl. I catalogued what was brought down into the deep vaults during the last interregnum. Do you know what a regent does when he cannot find a whole soul to destroy?*

Xanthe's hands had gone very still around the graver. "No."

*He keeps pieces,* said the archivist. *He keeps pieces, because a piece can be bound where a whole soul will fight. A piece is small enough to hide in metal. Small enough to lose. And a piece, if you are patient enough and wicked enough, can be fed to something older than crowns until the something grows strong enough to wear the regent himself like a coat.*

Behind her, on the pallet, the child stirred. The amulet hummed.

"The child," Xanthe said. Her mouth had gone dry. "There's a child in my house wearing a broken crown on a cord, and my hands ring like a bell when she sleeps."

The silence stretched so long she thought the connection had failed. Then:

*Then you are holding a fragment of the true king,* the archivist said, *and you should burn your shop to the ground and run, carver, because when the regent's men feel it ringing — and they will feel it ringing, they have hounds for exactly this — they do not ask questions. They ask for kindling.*

"They've been hunting 'resonance,'" Xanthe said. "That's the word. Warden proclamations. Unlicensed resonance."

*Yes. That is what they call it when a king's soul remembers itself.* The old voice was bitter now, bitter as frost. *I hid three fragments, in my time. Two were found. The third vanished — carried out by someone small, someone quiet, down through the rain-gates. We hoped. Four hundred years, we hoped.* A pause. *Is the child mute?*

"Yes."

*Good. Muteness is a mercy. What she carries does most of its talking through her anyway, whether she knows it or not.* The attention drew close, urgent now. *Listen to me, bone-carver. The Hollow King is nearly done dressing himself in the regent. When he finishes, no fragment will be safe anywhere under this sky. There is a rite. There is a place. The keepers below—*

The door of the shop rattled.

Xanthe's heart stopped, then restarted at double time. Through the fogged window she saw lantern-light sweeping the street — not the amber of the night-watch, but the white glare of Warden-lamps, the light they used specifically because it made shadows jump and guilt look obvious. Boots. Many boots. And a voice, amplified by a speaking-trumpet, flat as a ledger:

*"By order of the Regent's Justice: this street is closed for resonance inquiry. All residents to their doorways. Open upon inspection."*

*They're here,* the archivist said, unnecessarily. *Girl. Listen quickly. If they take you, take nothing, confess nothing, and whatever they put in front of you to carve — carve it badly. A bad carving tells them nothing. A good one—*

Boots on her step.

Xanthe did the three things her three years of staying small had taught her. She closed her fist around the knucklebone — *thank you, go quiet, thank you* — and felt the old attention fold away like a fan. She swept the shavings into the stove, where they curled and flared and became innocent ash. And she kicked the child's boot, gently, under the pallet, and got in exchange a pair of enormous dark eyes blinking awake and absolutely silent.

The knock came like a decision already made. "Wardens. Open."

"One moment — I'm decent—" She lit the lamp by the door to look domestic, untied her apron to look interrupted, and opened up onto white lantern-glare and three men in slate-gray coats, and behind them, in the street, a dozen more, going door to door, and a wagon with a brazier in it that was already burning things.

The one in front was young and tired-looking and held a tuning fork, of all things, a long steel fork with wire wrapped around its handle. He struck it softly against his palm. It rang — a thin, searching note — and Xanthe felt every charm on her display shelf answer it, faintly, honestly, the way they were made to.

Comforting. Harmless. *Small.* Three years of keeping her gift small, singing comfort and nothing else into pine and river-stone, and she prayed to every dead person she'd never formally met that smallness would be enough.

The young Warden walked the shelf, fork humming. His frown deepened. He struck the fork again, harder, and turned —

— and the amulet, hanging against the sleeping child's chest behind a curtain of sacking in the back room, rang back.

It wasn't loud. It probably wasn't even audible over the fork. But Xanthe saw the young man's head turn toward the curtain the way a compass needle turns north, slow and certain and terrible, and she saw the older Warden behind him see him do it.

"What's back there?" the older one asked.

"Storage," said Xanthe Nystrom, who had lied perhaps six times in her life and was discovering a talent for it. "Pickled eel. My winter stock."

The older Warden smiled without any warmth whatsoever. "We'll be the judges of the eel."

* * *

Later — much later, hours later, standing in the ashes-smelling dark of the rain-gate tunnel with the child's hand locked in hers and the sound of her own front door splintering still echoing off the wet stone — Xanthe would try to remember exactly how they'd gotten out, and fail. There had been the curtain thrown back. There had been the young Warden's face going strange, almost gentle, as the amulet's hum touched something in *him*. There had been the older Warden shouting orders. There had been the archivist's voice rising one last time out of the bone in her fist, furious and fast — *the cellar grate, carver, the rain-gate, MOVE* — and the grate giving way, and the black water of the storm-channel taking her boots, and the child not making a single sound the entire time, which was somehow the worst part.

What she remembered most clearly was simpler and worse: the brazier-wagon in the street, and in it, among the burning charms of her neighbors, the corner of her own display shelf, and her sign — *Nystrom & Daughters, Charms of Comfort*, painted by her mother's own hand twenty years ago — curling at the edges.

Small hadn't been enough. Staying small had only meant she'd stayed until they came anyway.

Ahead of her, the storm-channel ran down into the dark under Caerlum, toward the foundations, toward the catacombs, toward whatever the archivist had died four hundred years too soon to finish describing. *The keepers below,* he'd said. *Find the keepers.*

Xanthe tightened her grip on the child's small, damp hand, looked at the darkness that was, apparently, the rest of her life, and stepped in.
