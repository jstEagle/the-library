## 4. The Regent's Smile

Aldous Vane woke, as he had for the past eleven nights, with someone else's breath in his mouth.

It was not a violent sensation. That was the worst of it. He had expected — when it first began, in those early weeks after the coronation that was never quite a coronation — something like possession from the old plays: thrashing, blackouts, a stranger wearing his face. Instead there was only a settling. A weight redistributing itself through him the way water finds the low places in a cellar. Some mornings he could not say precisely which of his thoughts were his own.

He lay still beneath the linen and listened to the palace wake around him: boots on marble two floors down, the creak of the lift-chains in the east shaft, a kitchen boy whistling something jaunty and slightly off-key about a miller's daughter. Ordinary sounds. He clung to them the way a drowning man might cling to driftwood, telling himself, *this is mine, this morning is mine.*

*Ours,* said something at the base of his skull, gently, the way one corrects a child learning letters. *But do go on.*

Vane rose. He washed his face in the basin — the water was always cold; he had never once thought to complain about this until recently, when he realized he no longer minded discomforts at all — and dressed himself without assistance, which the chamber staff had learned to interpret as a preference rather than what it was. There were things he did not want witnesses for. The way his reflection sometimes lagged half a heartbeat behind his movements. The way his shadow, on certain evenings, pooled a shade too deep.

He was fifty-one years old. He had governed Caerlum for nineteen years as regent for a king who had died in everything but paperwork, and he had done it *well*, whatever the pamphleteers said. The granaries were full three years out of five. The bridges stood. He had kept the magic-proscriptions because magic had killed his brother, plain and simple, a hedge-witch's botched cure that had rotted Tomas from the inside over nine screaming months, and Vane had held his hand through all of it and sworn, at the end, that no power would ever again move unaccounted through his city. That was not cruelty. That was grief, given laws.

He told himself this every morning. He had become very good at telling himself things.

*You believe it,* the other observed, as Vane buttered his toast. It liked to talk during breakfast. It said the domestic hours were when a mind's defenses were lowest — all its attention spent on jam and appointments. *That is why you are useful. Most men need convincing. You come pre-convinced, with excellent justifications, neatly filed.*

"I am speaking to my toast," Vane said quietly. "I should like that noted by history."

*History will note you ended the long regency,* it said. *History is generous to men who finish things.*

The voice had a name, of sorts. Not one it offered freely — he had pried it out of it over months of negotiation the way one pulls a splinter, slowly and with bleeding — the Hollow King. It claimed to have been a ruler once, before the Spire, before the binding, though its memories arrived like furniture wrapped in cloth: shapes and weights but no faces. What it offered in exchange for lodging was straightforward. Order. Continuity. The certain knowledge, whispered directly into the machinery of his judgment, of which ministers lied, which merchants hoarded, which Warden patrols had grown lazy. Nineteen years of regency had taught Vane that ruling was mostly listening, and here was a listener that heard everything.

What it took in payment was harder to itemize. Certain memories went soft — his wedding day now had the texture of an etching, all line and no weather. Colors retreated at the edges some evenings. And there was the hunger, faint but persistent, like a craving for a food he couldn't name, which he suspected was not his own.

He had rationalized it, of course. He was, if nothing else, a man of considerable practice at rationalization. Everyone consumed something, he told himself. Wine. Ambition. Other people's labor. His arrangement was merely honest about its accounting.

Some mornings, in the gap between waking and remembering what he was, he believed it completely.

* *

The council met at the ninth bell, and it was there, standing at the head of the long table with the city maps weighted down by candlesticks, that the Hollow King went suddenly, utterly silent.

Vane had learned to read these silences the way sailors read weather. This one had a texture he hadn't felt before. Attention. The thing inside him had turned, all of its vast cold weight, toward something far away — and then, unmistakably, it *reached*, and missed, and made a sound that was not sound, a note of such frustrated longing that Vane's hand spasmed and knocked over the inkwell.

"Regent?" asked Chancellor Breck, alarmed.

"A moment." Vane pressed a handkerchief to the spreading blot. Beneath his sternum, the presence was moving like a man pressing his face against a window. *It is loose,* it said, and for the first time since their bargain began, it sounded afraid. *A fragment. Mine. It slipped the binding — how, HOW — it is in the city, it is small, it is hiding—*

"In the city," Vane repeated aloud before he could stop himself.

Fourteen faces turned toward him.

He recovered the way he always recovered: smoothly, with the slight smile the pamphleteers called the Regent's Smile and which he privately considered simply competence made visible. "I am informed," he said, "that a resonance event occurred last night. Small. Contained. But our instruments detected movement in the lower quarters, and you all know what resonance means." He let the pause do its work. Fourteen people shifted in fourteen chairs. Every one of them had attended the burnings; every one of them had gone home afterward and slept, telling themselves the same story Vane had built for them brick by brick over nineteen years: that fire was medicine, that caution was kindness, that a city without magic was a city without monsters.

"Seal the gates," he continued. "All of them. No one leaves Caerlum until we find what moved. Double the Warden patrols in the bone-carvers' quarter — resonance hides in worked material, you know this. House-to-house if necessary." He rolled the map closed with a crisp snap. "We are not cruel. We are thorough. There is a difference, and I expect every one of you to be able to explain that difference to your districts."

They bowed and scattered to their tasks, relieved to have orders, relieved to be told that thoroughness was a virtue. Vane watched them go and felt the Hollow King settle back into its corner of him, purring almost, like a great cat that has spotted prey.

*You see how easily it comes to you,* it murmured. *The sealing. The searching. In another life you would have hesitated — I felt you hesitate once, years ago, over a carver girl whose charm sang too sweetly. You let her go. Do you remember?*

He did remember. He remembered her hands, quick and careful, and the way she'd looked at him — not afraid, exactly. Watchful. He had filed her under "harmless" and moved on, and had told himself for years that the small mercy proved he was still a good man.

*I remember,* he said carefully. *And I will find her again, if she is what you're looking for.*

*She may be. Or the child. Or any of ten thousand hiding hearts.* The presence stretched within him, luxurious. *It does not matter which. What matters is that you will search, and search hard, and you will enjoy the searching, Aldous. Because I am going to show you something, when we find it. I am going to let you feel what it feels like to be whole. One taste. Just to keep you motivated.*

And the terrible thing — the thing he would examine later, in the cold hour before dawn, turning it over like a coin he didn't recall accepting — was that he wanted it. Not power. Power he had. He wanted the *wholeness*, the sensation of being one continuous self from morning to night, of owning every room in his own house. Nineteen years of regency and he had never once been complete, always holding the throne warm for a ghost, always the steward and never the king. Now there was a second tenant besides.

One taste, the Hollow King promised. One taste of unity.

Vane looked out the council window at his city — the slate roofs going gold in the late light, the Spire rising through the middle of it like a needle through fabric, stitching sky to stone. Somewhere down in those crowded streets was a fragment of something ancient, small enough to hide in a child's arms.

"Captain Renn," he said to the guard at the door, "find me Warden-Captain Ilsa Renn. Tell her the gates close at sundown."

Behind his eyes, patient and pleased, something ancient began, very softly, to hum.

* * *

That evening, alone in his chambers, Vane did a thing he had not done in eleven years. He unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk and took out the portrait — small, oval, paint flaking — of his brother Tomas, aged twenty-six forever, smiling with the terrible confidence of the healthy.

"You'd hate this," Vane told him. "All of it. The bargains. The burnings, maybe even those. You always said fear was a poor foundation for law." He turned the portrait over. On the back, in Tomas's looping hand: *For Aldous — mind the low places.* A joke between brothers. Their father had built cellars that flooded.

"Minding them," Vane said. "Every day."

From the deep of him, the Hollow King stirred, drowsy and indulgent, and said nothing at all — and its silence was somehow worse than any promise, because Vane understood, finally, in the marrow-deep way one understands a thing one has always known: it was not waiting for his obedience anymore.

It was waiting for his consent. And it could afford to wait, because every morning, the man who might have refused it woke up a little smaller, a little colder, a little more *convenient* —

— and far across the sleeping city, in a rain-lashed street where the lamps burned low, something answered the hum. Faintly. Defiantly. Like a struck bell refusing to fade.

The Hollow King sat up inside Aldous Vane's skull, sharp as a hound scenting blood.

*There,* it breathed. *There. Seal the city tonight, Aldous. Before dawn. We are going hunting.*
