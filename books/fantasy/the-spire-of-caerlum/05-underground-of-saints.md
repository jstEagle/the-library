## 5. Underground of Saints

The archivist had told her, in the dry whisper of a man who had spent four hundred years alphabetizing his own regrets: *Third well on Tanner's Row. The one with the cracked lip. Go down when the bells say nothing at all.*

Xanthe hadn't known the bells could say nothing at all. She'd assumed they were like everything else in Caerlum — always talking, always announcing something to somebody. But past midnight, between the hour of thieves and the hour of wet cats, there came a gap. A held breath. And into that breath she and the child had slipped, over the lip of the old well, down rungs slick with three centuries of moss.

The child went first. Of course the child went first. Xanthe had learned by now that arguing with her was like arguing with weather — technically possible, deeply unproductive, and you'd end up soaked either way.

"Slowly," Xanthe whispered, for the fortieth time.

The child looked back up at her and said eleven syllables that were definitely not *slowly*. They sounded, if anything, like an instruction involving speed. Possibly several speeds. Possibly a small lecture on the subject.

"I don't know what you're saying," Xanthe reminded her. "I have never known what you're saying. This is our whole arrangement."

The child made the sound again — the one Xanthe had privately started calling *the complaint*, because it was deployed exclusively when Xanthe failed to understand something instantly — and dropped down two more rungs without waiting.

Below them, the dark was not empty. That was the thing nobody warned you about Caerlum's underbelly. The city above was loud with the living and scrubbed clean of the dead; the Wardens saw to that, burning bones as diligently as they burned books. But below? Below, the bones had been left where they lay, and the dead had been talking to each other for hundreds of years, and Xanthe could hear them the way you hear neighbors through a thin wall. Murmurs. Arguments about grain prices from before the regency. Someone, somewhere down there, was still complaining about a wife named Odile, and had been for a very long time.

*Left,* breathed a voice near her elbow, feminine and brisk, the voice of someone who had been a ferry-woman once and had strong opinions about wells. *Left at the second channel, bone-carver, and mind the water, it bites.*

"Thank you," Xanthe whispered.

The dead didn't answer. They never did, not directly. Hearing them was like listening through a wall — you caught the shape of things, never the courtesy of a reply. Three years she'd lived like this, a woman taking dictation from ghosts who couldn't be bothered to confirm receipt.

At the bottom of the well shaft, the child stood ankle-deep in black water, holding up the broken crown amulet so it caught what little light filtered down. It hummed. Not audibly — Xanthe felt it in her teeth, in the marrow of her, the way she felt all true resonance. The fragment inside it was awake tonight. Restless. Like a bird that knew the cage door had been left unlatched somewhere above.

"We're going," Xanthe said. "I'm going as fast as my legs consent to."

The child took her hand. This was new. In six days, the child had tolerated proximity, accepted soup, and once — memorably — fallen asleep against Xanthe's shoulder during a Warden patrol, which had nearly stopped Xanthe's heart out of sheer terror. But hand-holding was unprecedented. Her grip was small and certain, the way children grip things they've decided not to lose.

They waded left, as instructed, into a channel that smelled of old stone and older prayers.

* * *

The passage opened without warning into a vaulted hall, and the hall was lit.

Not torch-lit. *Lit* — softly, warmly, from dozens of candles arranged around a long table where perhaps twenty people sat eating stew and arguing, if the noise was any indication, about turnips.

Twenty people stopped mid-argument and turned to look at the wet woman and the mute child who had walked out of their wall.

Xanthe's first instinct was the one three years in Caerlum had trained into her spine: *be small, be dull, be a woman selling trinkets, nothing to see here.* She opened her mouth to produce her market-day patter — *charms for luck, charms for love, no magic in them, ma'am, just good carving* — when the child stepped forward, lifted the broken crown, and spoke.

Six syllables. Clear and ringing, the language older than kingdoms, the one that made Xanthe's uncarved bones ache.

And a woman at the far end of the table — grey-braided, flour to the elbows, holding a ladle like a scepter — answered her.

Fluently. Warmly. With what sounded suspiciously like *you're late, we saved you some bread.*

Xanthe's knees went briefly unreliable.

"You speak it," she managed. "You can *understand* her."

The flour woman smiled. "We speak it badly. She'll tell you we mangle it terribly. But yes." She set down the ladle and crossed the room, wiping her hands on an apron that had clearly surrendered its original color decades ago. "I'm Maren. Keeper Maren, if you're being formal, which down here we mostly aren't, formality attracting Wardens like sugar attracts flies."

"She's been speaking it since she arrived," Xanthe said. "Nobody in the city — I asked scholars, I asked a priest, I asked a drunk who claimed to have traveled everywhere—"

"The drunk was probably right about the traveling and wrong about the listening." Maren crouched to the child's level. Spoke the six syllables back, gently, a question in them. The child answered, fast and urgent, hands moving, and Maren's face did something complicated — grief and recognition tangled together. When she rose, her eyes were wet and her voice was steady. "She says her name is Ede. She says the crown is broken and must be made whole. She says—" and here Maren paused, choosing, "—she says she has been looking for people who remember."

Around the table, the keepers had gone quiet. Twenty faces, all turned toward the child, and not one of them flinching. Not one of them edging toward the door. Not one wearing the particular expression Xanthe had catalogued over three years — the polite horror of people who suspected your charms were more than carving and wanted very much to be elsewhere.

"Do you know what she carries?" Xanthe asked quietly.

Maren looked at her. Really looked, the way the dead never could — direct, present, answering. "We know what the archivist told you to come here to learn. Yes. Sit down, bone-carver. Eat something. You look like a woman who hasn't had a hot meal since the regency began, and this conversation goes easier with stew."

It was, Xanthe would reflect later, the strangest sentence anyone had ever said to her, and also the kindest.

* * *

The stew was turnip-heavy and excellent. The bench was warm from other bodies. The candlelight made everyone look kinder than strangers had any right to look, and Xanthe sat among them feeling like a coin from a foreign country — same metal, wrong stamping, passed from hand to hand while everyone pretended not to notice.

A young man with ink-stained fingers — Tam, he said, though he pronounced it oddly, as if names were also a language he'd learned secondhand — leaned over and peered at the satchel of carvings at her hip.

"You're the charm-seller from Cooper's Lane," he said. "My sister bought a grief-piece off you last spring. For our mother. She said it whispered to her at night. Said it sounded like—" He stopped, embarrassed. "She said it sounded like Mum."

Xanthe kept her face pleasant and empty, the market face. "Good carving holds a feeling," she said. "That's the craft."

"Mm," said Tam, who clearly did not believe her, and whose eyes were shining with something that was not suspicion. "Mm-hm."

Down the table, an old man was teaching Ede a song in the old language, clapping a slow rhythm on the wood. Ede sang. Her voice, unused for speech, was perfect for music — clear and strange and aching, and half the room had stopped eating to listen, and one woman was crying openly into her stew without shame or apology.

Nobody was pretending anything here. That was the revelation Xanthe kept circling, unable to land. These people broke the law nightly and in company. They kept relics under the floorboards — she could feel them, a low chorus humming beneath her bench, generations of the preserved dead murmuring in their boxes like bees in winter. They had built a whole life in the dark, and they had done it *together*, and the thought landed in Xanthe's chest with an almost physical weight:

Three years. Three years of being small alone, when she could have been small *here*.

"Well," she said, to cover the sudden thickness in her throat, "your turnips are better than your security. We walked straight in off the street."

"The street is a well," Maren said placidly. "And the well is warded. You came in because we let you. The dead ferry-woman at the second channel — that's Aunt Hesk. She's been our doorkeeper for two hundred years. She only waves through people the bones vouch for." Maren tore a piece of bread and pushed it toward Xanthe. "You should know she argued for you. Said you listened properly. Said most of the living talk *at* the dead like they're furniture."

"I do try to be polite," Xanthe said faintly. "It seems rude not to."

Ede had stopped singing. She was sitting very still, watching Xanthe with that unnerving directness, and then she reached across the table, took Xanthe's hand — the carving hand, knuckle-scarred, nail-bitten — and pressed it flat against the broken crown.

The resonance went through Xanthe like a struck bell. And beneath it, clearer than the dead ever spoke, came something else. Not words. An impression, vast and patient and *waiting*: a king-shaped absence, a soul in pieces, a countdown running somewhere beneath the Spire like sand through a cracked glass.

Ede released her hand. Looked at her. Said, in the old language, two syllables that even Xanthe understood without translation, because some words live in the body before they live in the mouth.

*Hurry.*

Maren had gone pale. "What did you feel?"

"A clock," Xanthe said. "Running out."

Maren set down her cup. Around the table, the keepers exchanged looks — the practiced, wordless shorthand of people who had planned for this night for longer than Xanthe had been alive.

"Then finish your stew," Maren said, "because tomorrow we need to talk about price. What the unbinding costs. And bone-carver—" her kind face turned grave, "—I promise you will not like the list."

Under the floorboards, the boxed dead murmured agreement, and for the first time in three years, Xanthe wished she couldn't hear them.
