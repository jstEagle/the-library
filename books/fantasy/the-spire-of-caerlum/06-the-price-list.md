## 6. The Price List

Maren had been keeping secrets for sixty years, and she had learned that the hardest ones to keep were the ones you kept from yourself. So when she woke before dawn in the catacombs beneath Caerlum — as she did every morning, her back informing her that stone floors and old women were not natural companions — she made herself think the thought she'd been avoiding all night.

*The girl knows.*

She'd seen it last evening, clear as lamp-oil. When they had spoken of cost, Xanthe Nystrom had done what careful people do: she had gone very still, and very polite, and begun arranging her face like a shopkeeper arranging wares for a customer she intends to cheat.

Maren had run a sanctuary of outlaws for four decades. She knew a cheating shopkeeper when she saw one.

"Up," she said to the ceiling, to the keepers sleeping in the alcoves above. "Bread's on."

Nobody moved. Nobody ever moved. In forty years, the phrase *bread's on* had roused exactly one person, and he had been sleepwalking at the time.

---

The kitchen of the Underground of Saints was not, strictly speaking, a kitchen. It had been a charnel chapel once, back when the saints whose bones lined these halls were still permitted to be visited rather than hidden. Now it held a stove, three dented pots, and a long table scarred by generations of elbows, maps, arguments, and one memorable incident involving a goat.

Maren set the bread out and waited.

Xanthe came first — of course she did. The bone-carver moved through the catacombs like a cat through a room full of furniture it distrusted: silent, watchful, cataloguing exits. Behind her came the child, who answered to no name anyone could pronounce and whom the keepers had taken to calling Little Crown, mostly because Tam had started it and Tam's nicknames stuck like burrs. The child sat where Maren pointed, took bread when it was offered, and ate with the grave courtesy of someone performing a ritual older than bread.

Then the others: Tam, young and freckled, whose gift was making locks feel ashamed of themselves. Odile, gray-braided, who kept the boxed dead and spoke to them more kindly than she spoke to the living. Bram, enormous and soft-spoken, who could quiet pain with his hands and apologized to people while doing it. And Wren, sharp-eyed and fifteen, who heard lies the way other people heard off-key singing.

A good table. A small table. It had been smaller, once. That was part of why the price list mattered.

Maren poured tea all around and said, "Right. School's open."

Tam groaned. "I haven't finished yesterday's school."

"Yesterday's school was how to pick up a hot pot without swearing. You failed. This is remedial." She turned to Xanthe, and let the warmth drop away, because this part deserved cold clarity. "You asked what the unbinding costs. Here is the honest answer, and I'll thank you not to interrupt until the end, because I've given this speech twice in my life and both times the listener ran off halfway and got people killed."

Xanthe folded her hands. Said nothing. *Polite as a drawn blade,* Maren thought.

"The rite itself is old," Maren began. "Older than the regency, older than the Spire, maybe older than the kingdom, though our archivist friends disagree among themselves about everything except their fondness for arguing. What we know is this: the Hollow King is not a creature you can kill. It's a wearing. Like a coat. Like a crown. It exists by being worn, and it cannot be fought — only *unworn*. To unbind it, you must reach the crown chamber beneath the Spire, and there you must pour enough soul-hearing into the binding to dissolve it. The dead must be called to witness, and the dead answer only those who can hear them."

"I know this part," Xanthe said quietly.

"You don't know the next part." Maren leaned forward. "Soul-hearing isn't water in a jug, carver. It's not something you spend and refill. It's a *thread*, spun through you, and the unbinding pulls it out by the root. Every keeper who has studied the rite agrees on what happens after, because we have the accounts. Three recorded unbindings in all our history. Three guides. And here is the list — the price list, as I promised."

She held up one finger. "First: the hearing goes. Entirely. Permanently. Whatever else is taken, that much always is. The guide ends their days deaf to the dead — every whisper, every memory, every voice they've ever carried, gone as if it never existed. Some of them wrote that it was like going blind in a house they'd lived in all their lives."

Wren shifted uncomfortably. The girl couldn't imagine silence; none of the born-gifted could.

Second finger. "Second: the rite takes something *particular*. Not random — particular. It reaches into the guide and takes the thing most entwined with the hearing. Different for each. For Sera Voss, the last guide, it took her memories of her mother's voice. All of them. She remembered her mother existed. She remembered loving her. The voice itself — gone. She used to sit outside her mother's grave asking strangers to read the headstone aloud, just to hear words in roughly the right shape."

The kitchen was very quiet. Even the boxed dead behind the walls had gone still, listening.

Third finger. "Third — and this is the part nobody likes — the rite doesn't ask permission about *which* thing it takes. The guide doesn't choose. It finds the deepest thread and pulls. You can't hide your dearest thing from it. Believe me—" and here her voice went dry, "—people have tried. There was a man in the second unbinding who spent a decade beforehand trying to convince himself he loved nothing. He arrived at the rite armored in indifference. It took his ability to feel sunlight on his skin. He lived thirty more years and spent every warm day indoors, because the cold hurt less than remembering what he'd lost."

Maren set her hand flat on the table. "So. That's the price, bone-carver. Your hearing, certainly. Something dear to you besides, chosen by the dark, not by you. And no bargaining, no cleverness, no half-measures. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Xanthe said.

And there it was again — the stillness, the politeness, the shopkeeper's arrangement of the face. But Maren had been doing this for sixty years, and she caught what the others missed: the flicker in Xanthe's eyes, quick as a fish under ice. *Relief.* Just for a heartbeat. As if the list had told her something she already knew, and knowing it confirmed — what? A plan? An expectation?

*The girl knows what hers will cost,* Maren thought. *And she thinks she can carry it alone.*

Well. Maren had buried too many brave fools to let another one walk out of her kitchen unbaptized in the truth.

"There's one more item on the list," she said. "It's not a cost. It's a condition. The rite requires the guide to be *whole* when they begin — rested, fed, anchored to the living. A guide who arrives hollowed out, who has already spent herself down to the wick, fails. And a failed unbinding doesn't just fail, carver. It *frees*. Half-dissolved bindings are worse than whole ones. We would loose the Hollow King onto Caerlum wearing whatever was left of the regent, with no second chance, ever. So if you're planning to arrive at that chamber having secretly paid some private toll on the way — having bled yourself thin in advance so the final price feels smaller — then you are not a hero. You are a bomb. And I will have Bram sit on you."

Bram raised his teacup apologetically. "If it comes to that," he murmured. "Sorry."

Xanthe looked at him. Looked at Maren. And for the first time since she'd stumbled into their sanctuary, something in her composure cracked — not much, just enough for Maren to see the young woman underneath the carver. Tired. Frightened. Carrying a child's broken crown and a city's worth of guilt.

"I'm not trying to be a hero," Xanthe said.

"No," Maren agreed. "Heroes want to be seen. You're trying to be *spent*. There's a difference, and it matters. Heroes can be talked out of it. Spent people just get quieter." She pushed the bread basket across the table. "Eat. That's an order, not a kindness. From this hour forward, your job is to be whole. Sleep. Laugh, if you remember how. Let Tam teach you cards — he cheats, but badly, so it's instructive."

Tam opened his mouth to protest. Wren kicked him under the table. He closed it.

Little Crown reached out and touched the bread, then touched Xanthe's hand, then made a sound — not the old language this time, but something simpler, a child's noise of insistence. *Share.* Xanthe tore the loaf and gave half to the child, and watched the child eat, and something in her face went soft and wrecked all at once.

*There it is,* Maren thought grimly. *There's the thread the rite will find.* Whether it was the child herself or the hearing that bound them, Maren couldn't say — but she'd bet the sanctuary's whole store of smuggled salt that when the moment came, the price list would reach for whatever lived between this woman and this child.

She didn't say it aloud. Sixty years had taught her which truths to serve hot and which to leave to cool.

---

The morning passed in preparation. Odile brought out the maps of the Spire's underways, inked and re-inked by dead hands over dead decades. Bram checked supplies. Tam taught Xanthe cards, cheated badly, and was astonished to lose.

Maren spent the afternoon below, among the boxed dead, because there was a question only they could answer, and she wanted it answered before nightfall.

"Sera Voss," she said to the oldest box, the one holding the archivist Corvin, who had been dead two hundred years and garrulous for all of them. "The last guide. Tell me again what she lost. Precisely."

The bones whispered behind their lead-lined lid, and Maren bent close, and listened.

What she heard made her straighten slowly, her hand pressed to her mouth.

Not the mother's voice. That was what the histories said — but the histories were written by the living, and the living only record what they see. Corvin had been there. Corvin had heard the rite itself speak.

*Sera Voss offered her grief,* the old bones murmured. *But the rite does not take what is offered. It took the sound of her own name. No one could give it back to her. She died not knowing what to call herself, and smiling anyway.*

Maren stood in the dark a long time.

Then, above her, faint through sixty feet of stone, came the sound she had dreaded for forty years and prepared for every day of them: the great bell of the Wardenhouse, ringing not the hour, but the alarm.

Boots, somewhere above. Many boots. And Wren's voice, shrill down the stairwell:

"Maren! *Maren!* Torches on the street above — they've found the entrance —"
