## 7. Wardens at the Gate

Xanthe woke to the smell of bread and the sound of the dead humming.

The latter was normal. The keepers' sanctuary sat in a pocket of the catacombs where the old saints were buried, and the saints were chatty — not in words, exactly, but in a kind of low contentment, like cats purring in another room. It was the first place she'd ever slept where the silence wasn't hostile.

The former — the bread — was Maren's doing. The old keeper baked every morning as if ovens could hold back the end of the world, and perhaps, Xanthe thought, taking the warm loaf from her with both hands, they could. She'd seen stranger magic.

"You slept," Maren observed, in the tone of someone noting a minor miracle.

"I slept."

"The child still sleeps too." Maren nodded toward the alcove where the child lay curled around the broken crown amulet like it was a sibling. "And Pip has finally stopped asking if we're going to eat him, so all in all, a triumph of diplomacy."

Pip — a wiry keeper's boy of maybe ten — waved cheerfully from across the cavern, where he was teaching the child's hands to shape clay. The child didn't speak anything but that old language, the one that made Xanthe's bones ring like struck bells, but children apparently required no translation for mud. They'd been laughing about something for two days.

It should have felt safe. That was what frightened her, later, when she looked back: it had actually felt safe.

* * *

Warden-Captain Ilsa Renn had burned eleven houses in her career, and she remembered every one.

Not the people — the Wardens were careful to move people out first when they could, and the law only demanded ash and stone, not bodies. She remembered the houses. The way a burning house stood for a moment looking almost alive, windows glowing gold like a home again, before the roof came down.

She was thinking about that as she rode up through the Saintmarket district at dawn, because the informant's report had named this quarter, and because her orders were specific in a way that made her jaw ache.

*Resonance detected. Sweep the undercrofts. Detain carriers. Burn caches. Children found in possession are to be held for the Regent's assessment.*

Assessment. Vane's word. Everyone knew what the Regent assessed children *for*, even if nobody said it, even if saying it would cost them their tongues and probably their teeth.

Her lieutenant, Brandt, reined up beside her. "Informant says the entrance is behind the tannery. Old saint-crypt."

"Saints," Ilsa said flatly.

"Dead ones, Captain."

"That's what worries me." She'd grown up on stories about the catacombs — how the old kings buried their dead standing up so they could keep watching the city, how you didn't spit near a saint's crypt or your cows went dry. Superstition. She'd burned superstition out of herself years ago, along with everything else soft.

But her grandmother's bones were in one of those crypts. And her mother's. And somewhere in her chest, in the place where she kept the eleven houses, something shifted uneasily.

"Form up," she said. "Quiet approach. If there are children down there—"

"Orders say—"

"*If* there are children," Ilsa said, "we do our jobs. Which means we bring them out alive and let the assessment sort itself." She heard her own voice and didn't quite believe it. Neither, from his expression, did Brandt. But he saluted, which was the machine of law doing what the machine of law did: grinding forward, trusting that the gears knew best.

* * *

The warning came from a saint who had been dead four hundred years, and she delivered it by making every bone charm in the sanctuary rattle at once.

Xanthe came awake off her cot with her heart hammering. Around the cavern, keepers were already moving — dousing lamps, waking children, pulling aside the great loom that hid the north passage. Maren appeared at her elbow, unnervingly calm, pressing the child into her arms.

"Tanner's boy didn't come back last night," Maren said. "I thought he'd gone drinking. I was wrong, and I hate being wrong, and I'm going to have a very long conversation with him about it if we both survive."

"Wardens?"

"At the gate, or close enough. The saints say iron and fire." Maren's mouth twisted. "Four hundred years dead, and they can still smell a Warden coming. Useful lot, the saints."

The child clung to Xanthe, small fingers digging in, and the amulet against Xanthe's collarbone began to sing — that low, resonant note that predated kingdoms, that rang through her ribs like a second heartbeat. The child looked up at her with eyes far too old for the face and said something urgent in the old tongue.

"I don't understand you," Xanthe whispered. "But I think I agree."

Maren was marshaling the keepers — the old ones, the young ones, Pip clutching his clay-stained hands like they might be confiscated. "North passage to the deep stair," Maren was saying. "Single file. Nobody sings, nobody hums, nobody *resonates*, which I am aware is asking a great deal of some of you."

A few of the younger keepers managed tired smiles. Xanthe didn't. Because the amulet's song had changed — dropped half a note, gone dark — and in three years of listening to relics she had learned what that meant.

Something below was answering it.

"Keeper Maren," she said quietly. "What's down the north passage?"

Maren paused. In the dimness her face went carefully blank, which told Xanthe more than an answer would have.

"Older things than saints," Maren said. "Walk fast and don't touch the walls."

* * *

Ilsa Renn had been a Warden for nineteen years, and in nineteen years she had learned that the moment before a door came down was always the same: your own heartbeat loud, the world narrowed to wood and iron, and the strange, shameful hope that there'd be nothing behind it.

The crypt door was oak older than the city charter. Her sappers took it apart quietly, hinge-side first, and it swung open onto darkness and the smell of bread.

Bread. Fresh bread, in a hole full of outlawed magic-keepers, and her stomach growled at the worst possible moment, and she hated herself for it.

"Clear the entry," she murmured. Torches flared. Stone saints lined the walls, standing in their niches the way the stories said — upright, facing inward, watching. Their carved faces had been worn smooth by centuries, but Ilsa felt watched anyway, and she was not a woman who felt much of anything anymore.

Then the torchlight caught the floor, and she saw the chalk circles, the drying herbs, the small wooden toys scattered where children had left them in haste — a carved horse, a ball of rag and leather —

—and a charm. A little bone bird, no bigger than her thumb, abandoned on the flagstones.

It shouldn't have done anything. Charms needed living hands, living resonance; that was the whole basis of the law. But as she stepped past it, the bird *hummed*. One clear note, soft as a lullaby, and Ilsa's whole body went cold, because she knew that tune. Her grandmother used to sing it over the washbasin. Her mother hummed it at funerals. It was a Renn family song, from before the family had a name, and it was coming out of a piece of dead bone in a criminal's den.

"Captain?" Brandt, behind her.

"Nothing." Her voice came out wrong. She cleared her throat. "Sweep forward. Careful with the niches."

They moved deeper. And the saints — smooth-faced, four-hundred-years-dead saints — began, very softly, all together, to make the stone groan. Not falling. *Humming.* The same note, rising, filling the crypt like a held breath, and every hair on Ilsa's arms stood up, and somewhere in the passage ahead a child started crying.

"There!" Brandt lunged forward with his torch, and Ilsa saw them — figures fleeing up the north passage, keepers and children, a woman carrying a small wrapped bundle that glowed faintly at the collar like a coal —

"Stop!" she shouted, and hated the word in her mouth. "In the Regent's name, stop!"

They didn't. Of course they didn't. Brandt raised his hand for the fire-slingers, and Ilsa heard herself say, "Hold."

"Captain, the order is—"

"I know what the order is." The crying child. The humming saints. The little bone bird still singing her grandmother's song on the cold stones, patient as the dead, waiting to be picked up. Nineteen years of the machine of law, and it had never once asked her whether the gears were right. "Hold, Brandt. We take them at the stair. Alive."

He hesitated a half-second too long, and in that half-second, the ceiling decided for everyone.

* * *

Xanthe heard the crack above before anyone else — the dead shouting through her bones, *down, DOWN* — and then the passage behind them folded like wet bread.

Dust. Screaming. A thunder of stone, and when it settled, the way back was a wall of rubble, and through it, muffled but unmistakable, came the sounds of Wardens digging.

"They'll be through in minutes," Maren said grimly, hauling Pip up by the collar. "That's soldier digging. Soldiers dig fast and pray later."

Ahead, the passage sloped down into blackness. The amulet sang its dark half-note against Xanthe's chest, and now, in the quiet after the collapse, she could hear what it was singing *to*: something far below, vast and slow, turning over in its sleep like a body shifting under blankets.

The child tugged her sleeve. Pointed down. Said something urgent and old.

"Yes," Xanthe said, though she hadn't understood a word. "Down. I know."

Behind them, through ten feet of collapsed rock, a woman's voice — a Warden's voice, rough and human — called out: "There's a side gallery! East! They're not trapped, they're *moving*!"

And then, strangely, quieter, almost to herself, as if she thought no one could hear: "*Run, then.*"

Xanthe froze for half a step. She knew that voice. Not the voice itself — the *tone*, the sound of a person standing at the exact edge of the machine of law and looking down into its gears.

"Maren," she said. "One of them just told us to run."

"Then run faster," Maren said, "and feel guilty about it later. Guilt keeps. Moving doesn't."

They ran — down, into the black, past walls that Xanthe could feel *listening*, the amulet ringing louder with every step, and beneath them, closer now, that vast slow thing turned over and, unmistakably, began to wake.

Above, through stone and dust and distance, Xanthe felt one more thing: the faint, stubborn hum of a little bone bird, still sitting on the crypt floor, still singing a Renn family song to a Warden-Captain who couldn't decide whose side her bones were on.

The Spire waited below. And the Spire, Xanthe realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the dark, already knew they were coming.

*It's about time,* the dead whispered, all together, hungry and glad. *We've been waiting three years for someone who listens.*

She just wished they didn't sound so much like they wanted to eat her.
