## 8. The Ascent

The Spire did not have a door so much as a wound.

It stood at the heart of Caerlum like a needle through the world's thumb, white stone gone grey with rain and older grief, and where the keepers' maps said *entrance* there was only a seam in the rock that breathed. Cold air came out of it, slow as a sleeping man's exhale, and it smelled of dust and candle-tallow and something sweeter underneath — the smell of a room long shut, where someone had once been happy.

"That's not a good smell," Tam said. Not in words. Tam had never once spoken in words, not since the night he'd arrived on Xanthe's doorstep speaking the language before kingdoms. But Maren had taught them all enough of it by now — three weeks of scratched symbols on slate and patient fingers shaping sounds — that Xanthe caught the shape of it: *that smell is wrong.*

"No," Xanthe agreed, in Caerlum's plain tongue, because her mouth was too tired for anything older. "But it's an honest one."

Behind them, the catacombs had given them their last gift: a stair that climbed inside the Spire's own bones, hidden by saints who had apparently been better smugglers than holy men. Keeper Maren stood at the threshold with her lantern held high, and for a moment her face was doing something complicated — pride and dread fighting for the same ground.

"The rite is in the crown chamber," she said. "Top floor. The dead will know you're coming, bone-carver. They've been waiting three years for anyone who could hear them." She hesitated. "Listen carefully. And don't listen to everything."

"That's very mystical," said Bram, one of the keepers' runners, a boy of maybe sixteen with a crossbow he kept forgetting to hold properly. "Don't listen to everything. Wonderful. I'll write it on my tombstone."

"You won't have one if you keep talking," Maren said, but she touched his shoulder gently as she said it, and Xanthe felt the old ache of watching people be family to each other in ways she'd never learned.

Then the seam closed behind them, and they were climbing.

* * *

The first floor of bound dead was a granary.

That was the horror of it, Xanthe thought later. Not skulls on spikes, not rivers of blood — a granary. Rows of stone bins, empty now, and standing between them the shapes of people who had been bound mid-task. A woman still holding a sack that had rotted to nothing. A man bent over a ledger, his finger resting on a line no one would ever read again. Their bodies were dust wearing clothes; their souls were pressed flat against the binding like moths under glass.

They felt her coming before she reached the stairs. The whole floor shivered, and then the whispering started — not sound, exactly, but pressure, the way her own carved bones had always resonated. Except this wasn't carved. This was raw.

*Hear us. Hear us. Oh — oh, you can hear us, can't you—*

Xanthe stopped on the landing. Tam's small hand found hers, grip tight enough to hurt.

"I have to," she said, to no one, to everyone. Because she had known this was coming since Maren's warning, and she had known what it would cost, and she had come anyway, which meant the decision was already made and all that remained was the paying.

She knelt by the woman with the empty sack and laid her palm against the cold floor.

Listening was not like hearing. Hearing happened to you. Listening was a door you opened from your side, and every time she opened it, something of hers went through and did not come back.

The woman's name had been Odile. She had counted grain for thirty years and stolen a handful each week to feed her sister's children during the famine winter, and she had never told anyone, and she had died afraid of being found out rather than afraid of dying. The whole life poured into Xanthe in the space of a breath — and Xanthe gave back what she could, the acknowledgment, the *I see you, Odile, you were brave in a small way and it counted* — and when she rose, the binding on that floor cracked down its middle like river ice in spring.

The dust-shapes sighed and were gone.

And Xanthe stood there trying to remember the color of her mother's kitchen and finding — nothing. Not the memory itself; that was intact. Just the color. A kitchen rendered in grey, like a sketch of a place she'd loved.

"Costing you already," Bram said quietly. He'd seen her face.

"It's fine."

"It's clearly not fine—"

"Bram." Tam made a short sharp gesture: *stop.*

Bram stopped. Xanthe almost smiled. Three weeks ago the child couldn't tell any of them anything; now he ran the party like a small stern general. She took his hand again and they climbed.

* * *

There were nine floors between the granary and the crown chamber. She listened on six of them.

A chapel where forty faithful had been bound at prayer, their last hymn still hanging unfinished in the air — she gave them the ending they'd been reaching for, and lost the sound of rain on her rooftop, which had been her favorite sound in the world, though she supposed she wouldn't miss it until it rained.

A barracks where soldiers who had refused an order lay in rows — loyal men, the archivist's ghost had called them, loyal to a king instead of a regent — and listening to them cost her the memory-edge of her teacher's face, Old Sable who had taught her the first cuts of the craft, so that afterward she could recall his hands and his voice and his endless patience but his face was a smooth blank oval, and she sat down on a broken shield and cried for two minutes, hard and ugly, while Tam patted her arm with tremendous solemnity.

A library. A nursery. A cistern full of drowned wardens who had learned too late whose side the Hollow King was actually on.

Each time, the same transaction. Each time, she came away lighter in some specific, irreplaceable way. She was becoming, she realized somewhere around the fifth floor, a house being emptied room by room by a polite thief who always asked first.

On the seventh landing, it spoke to her.

* * *

It didn't use sound. It used the silence between floors, the long dark stretch of stair where even Tam's breathing seemed to hold itself.

*Xanthe Nystrom.*

Her whole body went rigid. Beside her, Tam looked up sharply — he couldn't hear it, she realized, or perhaps he could and simply knew better than to react — and Bram, bless him, noticed nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

*You have been generous,* the voice said. It was not a voice like Vane's, or like any living throat. It was the voice of a large empty space, a cathedral after the congregation has gone home. *Six doors opened. Six payments made. Do you know what I could give you, little carver?*

"I'm not negotiating with a parasite," she said aloud, because saying it out loud made it smaller.

*A parasite wears without paying. I pay. I always pay.* Something moved in the darkness above them — not a shape, more a leaning-in, the sensation of vast attention tilting toward her like a lamp. *You have spent yourself buying strangers their exits. I offer you the reverse. Every soul you freed today — returned to your hearing. Every ghost you ever silenced — singing again. Your gift, restored and doubled and trebled. You would never be alone in a quiet room again. You would never carve another charm just to feel something answer.*

And that — that was the knife of it, turned with terrible precision. Because it was true. That was the loneliest truth of her life: that she had spent three years surrounded by voices and starving anyway, because the dead were company the way a letter is company, warm on one side and cold on the other.

*I know what you are,* the Hollow King said, gentle as a hand on hair. *You are a woman who has spent her whole life translating for the dead because the living frightened you. Come wear my silence instead. I have room. I have such room.*

For one moment — one long, shameful, human moment — Xanthe wanted it. Wanted it the way the drowning want air. All those voices, all that company, forever, and all it would cost was a crown and a kingdom and a mute child's soul.

Tam tugged her hand.

He looked up at her, and in the dark his eyes caught what little light there was, and he made a sign she knew. Not the formal signs Maren had taught. An older one, from his language-before-kingdoms, one he'd used only twice: the hand cupped, then opened, like a bird leaving.

*What lives flies free. What's hoarded dies in the hand.*

"He's right," Xanthe said, and her voice shook and she let it shake. "You're offering me company, and you don't even understand what company is. Company is chosen. You're just a very large, very lonely thing, wearing a man who hates you."

The silence went very still.

*When you reach the crown chamber,* it said at last, and the gentleness was gone, and what remained was older than kingdoms and colder than the Spire's foundations, *you will pour yourself out for a king you have never met. And I will take what remains. We will both be filled, carver. See which of us fills faster.*

The presence withdrew upward, like a tide going out, and left behind it a silence that rang.

Tam was still holding her hand. He signed, slowly: *Afraid?*

"Terrified," she said honestly.

He considered this, then signed: *Good. Brave means scared and climbing anyway.* Then, after a pause, with the grave pragmatism of the very young: *Also I'm hungry.*

Xanthe laughed — a real laugh, cracked and wet at the edges, but real — and it echoed up the stairwell into the dark above, where something vast and hungry heard it and, for the first time in three hundred years, did not understand it at all.

Above them, faint through the stone, they could hear it now: the crown chamber. And beneath the hum of ancient binding, another sound — a man's voice, thin and ragged, arguing with no one.

Vane was still fighting.

"Come on," Xanthe said, and climbed.
