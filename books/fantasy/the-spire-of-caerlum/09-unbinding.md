## 9. Unbinding

The last hundred steps were the worst, because they were the easiest.

The Spire had spent nine floors trying to kill them — collapsing stairs, doors that breathed, corridors lined with the bound dead pressing their silence against Xanthe's ears like beggars at a feast she could no longer afford to host. But the final stretch of stair was clean. Swept, almost. The stone hummed underfoot with a frequency she felt in her teeth, and the walls were bare of ghosts.

Of course they were, she thought. He keeps his house tidy.

The child climbed ahead of her, small hand trailing the wall, the broken crown-amulet swinging at their throat. It had been singing since the seventh floor — not sound, exactly, but a resonance Xanthe felt in the marrow of her own carved bones, in every relic she'd ever touched, in the old archivist's knucklebone warm in her pocket. *Come home. Come home. Come home.* Three hundred years of a soul calling to its missing piece, patient as water wearing stone.

Xanthe's hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake. Instead they felt strangely light, the way they did after finishing a long carving, when all the work was behind her and only the listening remained.

Except this time, the listening was the work. And the payment was everything.

She thought about Maren's face when she'd said it — *I already know my price* — the keeper's eyes going soft and terrible. *You don't have to tell me,* Maren had signed. *But I'll know it when I see what's missing.*

What would be missing. She turned the thought over as she climbed, testing it like a flaw in ivory. Not her memories, exactly — those had been bleeding away floor by floor, colors first, then names, then the taste of her mother's bread, and those losses still ached but they hadn't stopped her. No. The rite required a soul-hearing guide. And a guide, once the unbinding finished, would have heard the last soul there was to hear.

Deaf to the dead. Forever. Every whisper, every comfort, every voice that had kept her company through three years of staying very small and very quiet — gone, all at once, like a door closing on a house full of singers.

*And what will you be left with?* the Hollow King had asked her, three floors down, in a voice like honey poured over a wound. *I can give them back. All of them. Your mother. The archivist. Every ghost you've ever soothed, made flesh and breathing, if you simply turn around and walk away.*

*I don't want them back,* she had said, which wasn't entirely true, and the lie had cost her something — a memory of her father's laugh, she thought, though she couldn't be sure, because she couldn't remember the laugh anymore either.

*Then what do you want?*

And she had said: *To stop being afraid of myself.*

The Hollow King had laughed at that. But it had also, she noticed, stopped offering.

* * *

The crown chamber opened before them without a door, because doors were for things that could be kept out.

It was vast and round and wrong — the proportions shifted when she wasn't looking directly at them, the ceiling receding like a held breath. At its center stood a throne of black glass, and in it sat Regent Aldous Vane, and he was not sitting like a man on a throne. He was sitting like a man holding a door shut from the inside.

He looked terrible. Magnificent and terrible, the way a burning house is beautiful right up until it isn't. His ceremonial robes hung off him as though he'd lost weight overnight; his hair had gone white at the temples in the week since the fragment escaped; and his hands gripped the throne's arms so hard the knuckles stood out like river stones.

"You," he rasped, seeing her. His eyes were his own. That was the horror of it — they were still, unmistakably, a tired man's eyes. "The bone-carver. From the Tanners' Row raid." A broken laugh. "Three years. Three years you stayed under my nose, and I never —" His jaw clenched; his whole body seized; and when he spoke again his voice came out layered, doubled, a chord of one note played by two throats. "**She should not be here.**"

"I know," Xanthe said. To Vane. "That's why I'm here."

The child stepped past her into the chamber, and the amulet sang louder, and every piece of carved bone on Xanthe's person answered it — her charms, her needles, the relics sewn into her coat lining — until she was less a woman than a chord waiting to be struck.

Vane's face contorted. "Listen to me," he gasped, and now he was fighting for every word, wrestling each one up from somewhere deep, like a drowning man surfacing between waves. "When it's done — when the king wakes — it will need a vessel. It always needs a vessel. Don't let it take the child. Don't let it take *me*. Burn the body. Do you understand? **Burn the body.**"

"I understand."

"No, you don't." His mouth twisted into something that wanted to be a smile and failed. "Nobody understands until it's wearing them. I was twenty-three years old. I told myself I was saving the kingdom. I told myself I'd hold it, just hold it, until the boy came of age. And it was so *grateful*, at first. So warm. Like being loved by something enormous." His voice cracked. "You don't put on a crown, bone-carver. You get worn."

Behind the throne — no, *through* the throne, *through* the room, everywhere and nowhere — the Hollow King stirred. Xanthe felt it the way she felt storms coming, in the ache of old bones. The shadows in the chamber deepened and organized themselves into something with shoulders. A shape assembled out of dark, crowned in nothing, wearing the absence where a king should be.

**Little listener,** it said, and its voice bypassed her ears entirely and spoke directly into the hollow places inside her, the ones shaped by three hundred years of other people's dead. **You brought my piece home. How thoughtful. Set it on the throne and go, and I will owe you a debt no living woman has ever held.**

"I'm not setting anything down," Xanthe said. Her voice didn't shake. Small mercies. "I'm taking something back."

The Hollow King considered her. She felt its attention like a searchlight.

**You know the price.**

"I know the price."

**And still you climb? For a child who cannot thank you. For a king you've never met. What a strange little creature you are.**

"Yeah," Xanthe said, and took the child's hand. "We're all strange. That's rather the point of us."

* * *

The rite, as Maren had taught it, was insultingly simple. That was the cruelty of magic: the great works never required complexity, only cost.

*Kneel at the boundary. Open your hearing wide — wider than you have ever dared. Let the fragment feel the whole it belongs to. Then pour. Everything you hear, everything you are, into the space between the pieces, and let the soul close over the gap like water.*

"And the guide?" Xanthe had asked.

Maren's hands had been very still. *The gap closes over the guide too.*

So Xanthe knelt, and set the child beside her, and did the thing she had spent three years refusing to do.

She opened.

Every ward she'd ever built in her mind, every wall, every careful smallness — she let it all down at once, and the dead came in like a flood. Not just the bound dead of the Spire, though they came too, nine floors of them, screaming their gratitude and their grief. Everyone. Every ghost she had ever soothed with a carved charm, every whisper she had ever pretended not to hear, every comfort she had sold in small doses to keep herself safe — all of it rushed home to her, and she gathered it, and she aimed it at the broken thing in the child's hands.

"*Come home,*" she said, in the language older than kingdoms, which her bones knew even though she didn't. "*Come home.*"

The amulet shattered.

Not into fragments — into *light*, thin and golden and starving, and it rose, and the Hollow King rose with it, and for one endless moment the chamber held three souls and a hole shaped like a fourth, and everything pulled.

The true king's soul — vast, ancient, worn smooth by three centuries of waiting — reached down through the light for its missing piece. The fragment leapt toward it like a spark to a bonfire. And between them, filling the gap, pouring itself out like wine from a cracked cup, went Xanthe's hearing. Her gift. Her curse. Her three years of whispered comforts and secret kindnesses and the long, lonely vigilance of a woman who made herself small to survive.

It hurt worse than anything. It hurt like being *unmade politely*. She felt every voice leave her one by one — the archivist last, pressing something like a kiss against the inside of her skull, saying *thank you, carver, thank you* — and then—

Silence.

Real silence. The first true silence of her entire life.

In the center of the chamber, the light closed. Whole. Complete. A king's soul, entire, settling into itself with a sound like a bell finally allowed to finish ringing.

And the Hollow King, unworn, unraveled.

There was no battle. That was the strange mercy of it. A thing that exists only by being worn has nothing left when the garment stands up and walks away. It thinned. It frayed. Its shadow-shoulders slumped not in defeat but in *confusion*, the way an old coat might look at its own empty sleeves, and then the dark came apart into ordinary darkness, and the darkness was just darkness, and the chamber was only a room.

On the throne, Aldous Vane made a small sound.

Xanthe crossed to him — her legs shaking now, now that it was over, the steadiness spending itself all at once — and found him changed. Smaller, somehow. The whiteness at his temples had spread, and his hands had unclenched at last, and his eyes — his tired, human, unforgivably human eyes — were clear for the first time in thirty years.

"You burned it?" he whispered.

"It burned itself," Xanthe said. "When nothing was wearing it."

A breath of laughter, wet and disbelieving. "Thirty years," he said. "I forgot what quiet sounded like." His gaze drifted past her to the child, standing in the settling light, and something in his face broke open — grief and relief in equal measure, the expression of a man seeing the shore after decades of drowning. "Is that—"

"Yes."

"The boy I—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, slower. "Tell him the regency was never meant to end like this. Tell him I meant to hold it. Tell him—" Another seizure of breath, and this time it didn't pass. His hand found hers and gripped it, hard, a dying man taking anchor. "Tell him I'm sorry. Not for the crown. For the *years*."

"I'll tell him."

"Don't soften it," Vane said, and his eyes were fierce, and they were his own, all the way to the end. "Let it be heavy. Heavy things teach."

And then the grip loosened, and Aldous Vane died the way he had lived for three decades — quietly, in service to someone else's throne — except that this time, at the very last, he was only a man, and the room let him go without taking anything else.

* * *

Afterward, there was light — real light, dawn-colored, pouring through windows that shouldn't have existed in a sealed chamber — and there was the child, standing over the shattered remains of the amulet, and there was Xanthe, kneeling in the wreckage of her own hearing, feeling absolutely nothing where the voices used to live.

She tested it, the way you test a tooth that's come loose. Reached out with the sense that had defined her since childhood.

Nothing. No whisper. No hum. No dead pressing at the edges of the world. Just stone, and air, and the enormous unhaunted quiet of a universe full of living things making noise at each other.

It should have felt like loss. Some part of her braced for the grief to arrive.

Instead, the child sat down next to her, leaned their small head against her arm, and signed — clumsily, in the keepers' hand-language Maren had been teaching them, getting half the shapes wrong:

*Hungry. Still.*

Xanthe started to laugh, and the laugh turned into something else halfway through, and she held onto the child and shook with it, grieving and relieved and hollowed-out and *here*, unbearably, ordinarily here, in a body that belonged to the living and a silence that belonged only to her.

Above them, far above, bells began to ring — Caerlum's bells, waking one by one across the city, though no hand had pulled a rope. The bound dead of nine floors were free. And somewhere below, a community of outlaws was surely looking up, wondering.

The child tugged her sleeve and pointed upward, then signed again, urgently:

*Who's going to explain all this?*

Xanthe wiped her face, looked at the dead regent, the empty throne, the impossible morning light — and realized, with a tired little hiccough of a laugh, that the answer was her.

"Oh no," she said. "They're going to make me *talk* to people."
