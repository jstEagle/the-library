## 10. After the Silence

The first thing Xanthe learned about being deaf to the dead was that the living were *loud*.

She had not expected this. She had imagined silence as an absence, a room with the furniture taken out. Instead, three weeks after she climbed down from the Spire on legs that barely held her, she woke every morning to a world that would not stop talking. Fishwives arguing over the price of eels. Children running races along the gutter outside her window. The cobbler two doors down singing — badly, tunelessly, with enormous commitment — while he worked his awl. None of it was carved into bone or whispered through relics. It was simply there, unbidden and unasked for, filling her ears the way the ghosts had once filled her skull.

She found she did not mind.

* * *

The shop had changed. The sign still read NYSTROM — CARVED GOODS, because repainting cost money and Maren said signs earned character the way faces did, but the window display told the truer story. No charms now. No whispering knucklebones, no comfort-tokens that murmured a dead mother's lullaby to grieving buyers. In their place: wooden horses with deliberately crooked legs. A puzzle box that did nothing but hold secrets, which was its whole magic and required none. A small fleet of ships with sails of pale birch, waiting for children who had never been allowed toys before, because in Caerlum even play had been policed by fear of what might be hidden inside it.

Xanthe sat at her workbench with a knife and a block of ash wood, carving a duck. Just a duck. It would say nothing when it was finished. It would sit in some child's hands and be exactly what it appeared to be, and that, she had discovered, was a kind of miracle all its own.

"You're making the beak too smug," said Tam, from the doorway.

Tam was nine, one of Maren's brood of strays, and appointed himself quality inspector of everything Xanthe made. He leaned against the doorframe with the particular insolence of a child who has recently learned the adults around him can be managed.

"Ducks are allowed to be smug," Xanthe said. "They've earned it."

"They look like they know something."

"Good. Then I've done my job." She blew wood shavings off the little body. "Take it to the window."

He took it, turned it over twice, frowned at it as though it might confess something under scrutiny, then set it in the display beside the ships. From the street came the sound of voices raised — not in anger, but in that peculiar civic fury that had gripped Caerlum since the Spire. The city was arguing. It had been arguing for three weeks without pause, and Xanthe suspected it would argue for years, and she thought this too was a kind of miracle.

* * *

The argument went like this: the old laws said magic was outlawed. But the regent's laws had been built on a lie, sealed over a stolen soul, enforced by Wardens who burned children. And so the question before every tavern table and council bench in Caerlum was whether the law itself had been the poison, or merely the hand that held it.

Xanthe stayed out of the debates. She had spent three years keeping herself small, and though she was no longer afraid, the habit of quiet ran deep. Besides, others argued better. Maren stood in the market square most mornings now, gray-haired and immovable as a dock piling, explaining to anyone who'd listen — and many who wouldn't — that you could no more outlaw the hearing of the dead than outlaw the tide. Half the square booed her. The other half bought her turnips. Maren declared this a respectable showing.

The king, meanwhile, was learning to rule.

They called him King Edran now, though he answered more readily to Edran than to any title. He was eleven years old and had been a fragment of himself for longer than he could remember, and now that he was whole he was discovering that wholeness was hard work. He attended council sessions where grown men spoke in circles. He memorized names — ward names, guild names, the names of streets that had burned. Twice a week he came to the shop, escorted by exactly one guard, which Ilsa Renn insisted upon, and he sat on Xanthe's stool and watched her carve and asked questions no councillor ever thought to ask.

Today was one of those days. The bell above the door rang mid-afternoon, and there he was, out of breath from taking the stairs two at a time despite the guard's protests, with sawdust already clinging to his sleeves before he'd touched anything.

"Is the duck done?"

"Nearly. Why? Do you want it?"

"No." He considered. "Yes. Maybe. Can I watch the feet?"

So he watched the feet, chin nearly resting on the workbench, while she shaped the tiny webbed curves with the tip of her knife. He was quieter these days than he'd been the first week, when wholeness had hit him like spring thaw and everything in him had rushed forward at once. Now he was settling into himself the way water settles, finding his own level. Sometimes, watching him, Xanthe felt a pang where her gift used to live — an ache like a missing tooth, the tongue returning again and again to the gap.

Once, she would have known what the dead thought of this boy-king. Once, every relic in her shop would have sung his lineage back to the first founders of Caerlum. Now she knew only what any person knew: that he chewed his lip when he was thinking, and that he was kind to the guard who followed him, and that he had begun, very carefully, to laugh.

It was enough. She kept telling herself it was enough, and slowly, day by day, it stopped being a telling and started being true.

"There's going to be a vote," Edran said suddenly. "About the keepers. Whether they get a charter. Aunt Maren says—" he caught himself, grinned. "Keeper Maren says if the council won't grant it, she'll stand in the square until they do, and she's very good at standing."

"She is," Xanthe agreed. "It's her deadliest art."

"I'm going to vote for it. I don't technically get a vote yet, but I'm going to stand next to her, and everyone will know what I think." He picked up a scrap of ash wood and began, clumsily, to carve it with a borrowed knife. "What do you think they'll decide?"

Xanthe set down her knife and looked out the window, past the crooked-legged horses and the birch-bark ships, to the street beyond — where a Warden walked his patrol without burning anything, where a woman was teaching her daughter to skip rope, where the cobbler had moved on from his ballad to something slower and worse. Where, three weeks ago, a crowd had pulled down the Spire's outer gates stone by stone, not in rage but in something gentler and far more frightening to the old order: the simple insistence on seeing what had been buried beneath them.

"I think," she said, "that they'll argue until winter, and then they'll decide right, because underneath all the shouting, people here are tired of being lied to. And tired of being afraid." She smiled at the boy. "And I think Keeper Maren will outlast the entire council. Stone wears down. She doesn't."

Edran laughed — really laughed, the sound bouncing off the rafters — and the guard in the doorway tried very hard not to smile and failed completely.

* * *

That evening, the keepers came to her.

Not all of them — there were dozens now, come up blinking out of the catacombs into a city that no longer hunted them, dispersed through attics and spare rooms across six districts — but a good handful. Maren arrived first with a sack of onions and opinions in equal quantity. Behind her came Tam, and old Sefa whose hands shook too much to carve but whose memory held every rite the underground had preserved through three generations of hiding, and young Pell who had been born below ground and was seeing open sky regularly for the first time in his life and still sometimes stopped dead in the street just to stare at clouds.

They filled her little shop the way they filled every space they occupied: completely, warmly, leaving no room for loneliness even if she'd wanted any.

"We brought dinner," Maren announced, dumping the onions on the counter next to the finished duck. "And news. And a question, which you're going to pretend you don't want to answer, and then you're going to answer it."

"That's ominous."

"The council's drafting the charter. First reading next week. They want testimony." Maren fixed her with the look that had outlasted three decades of persecution and several attempts on her turnip cart. "From someone who went up the Spire. From someone who paid."

The shop went quiet. Even the cobbler next door seemed to have paused for breath.

Xanthe looked down at her hands. They were steady. They had been steady since the crown chamber, since she poured the last of her hearing out into the unbinding and felt the dead go silent in her bones forever — a door closing, gently, the way a house closes its door behind a guest who has finally gone home.

"I don't hear them anymore," she said quietly. "You know that. Whatever I was, I spent it."

Maren crossed the room and took Xanthe's hands in her own cracked, strong ones. "Child. You think the council needs your ears?" Her voice softened in a way it almost never did. "We need your *witness*. There's a difference between hearing ghosts and remembering what they said. You're the only one alive who heard both kings. That doesn't leave you when the listening does."

Behind her, Sefa nodded. Tam, uncharacteristically silent, nodded harder.

Xanthe looked around the shop — at her family, loud and stubborn and alive, at the plain wooden toys that asked nothing of anyone, at the boy-king's abandoned wood-scrap sitting on her bench with its half-carved attempt at a bird. Three years she had survived by staying small. She had thought belonging was something you hid from, because everything you loved could be taken as evidence against you.

But the door was open, and the evening light came in gold, and nobody here flinched.

"All right," she said. "I'll testify."

Maren squeezed her hands once, hard, then immediately ruined the moment by demanding to know why the duck looked so smug.

* * *

Later, alone, Xanthe banked the fire and stood a long moment at the window. Rain was coming — she could smell it, ordinary rain on ordinary air, no whispers riding it, no warnings tangled in the wind. Below, a lamplighter made his rounds, and somewhere a child was refusing to come inside, and the cobbler had resumed his ballad with renewed enthusiasm and no improvement whatsoever.

She pressed her palm flat against the cool glass.

*Thank you,* she thought — to no one, to everyone, to whatever had listened all those years and finally let her rest. *I hope it's quiet where you are. I hope someone carves you comfort.*

Nothing answered. Nothing needed to.

She turned back to her workbench, took up the ash-wood duck, and gave its smug beak one last stroke of the knife — and that was when she noticed the scrap Edran had been carving, forgotten on the bench's corner. She picked it up and turned it toward the lamp.

It was a bird. Rough, lopsided, unmistakably meant to be a bird — and tucked into its base, so small she almost missed it, a single carved mark. Not a charm. Not a working. Just a shape, copied faithfully from memory: the archivist's mark, the sign of the dead who remembered what was buried.

A child who couldn't hear the dead either, marking the ones he owed.

Xanthe set it carefully on the shelf beside the finished duck, where the lamplight caught both their wings, and reached for a fresh block of wood.
