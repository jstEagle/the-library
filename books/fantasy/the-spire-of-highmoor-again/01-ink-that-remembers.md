## 1. Ink That Remembers

There are two kinds of people who end up copying ledgers in the under-croft of the Temple of Quiet Hours: those who have sinned greatly, and those who have never existed at all. I have spent twenty-three years being excellent at the second.

The lamp above my desk guttered, and I trimmed its wick without looking up. Around me, the archive breathed—that particular smell of old vellum, tallow, and the mineral tang of stone that has been cold for longer than anyone can remember. Three hundred feet of shelving. Forty thousand documents. And me, Isandro Okafor, debt-scribe, third-class, invisible by trade.

"Invisible" is not a complaint. It is a craft. I had learned it the way other children learned prayers: the soft shuffle that makes superiors forget you're behind them; the precise dullness of expression that makes eyes slide past you; the art of answering questions with exactly as many words as required and not one more. In Highmoor, where magic was forbidden and spirits were a century-dead rumor, invisibility was the only survival skill worth mastering.

That night I was working on the harvest ledgers from the year the river flooded—water damage, ink bleeding into brown ghosts, numbers dissolving mid-column. Tedious work. Holy work, the Abbot would say, though he'd say it with that look he got sometimes, the one like a man watching weather come in over mountains.

I finished the flood-year ledger and went to return it to its shelf, which is when I found the book that wasn't there.

I want to be careful about this part, because everything afterward depends on it. The shelf held codices 440 through 460, all restoration candidates, all catalogued. I had catalogued half of them myself. Between 451—the *Ledger of Tithes, Southern Slopes*—and 452 stood a book bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, with no number on its spine and no dust on its cover.

In an archive this old, dust is a kind of clock. Everything gathers it. Even things touched daily. This book had none, as if it had arrived that morning, or as if time simply hadn't bothered to notice it.

I should have reported it. That's what a good scribe does. An uncatalogued volume is a breach of order, and order is the temple's whole religion here in the valley—order, silence, and the long patient business of forgetting that anything else ever existed.

Instead, I sat down and opened it.

* * *

The early pages were prophecies, or what pass for them—riddling verses about the Spire of Highmoor, the great pale tower visible from every window in the valley, standing over us like a needle left in the world's flesh. I'd grown up under it. Everyone here had. We didn't look at it much. Looking at it felt rude somehow, like staring at a scar on someone's face.

*A crown shall fall and be worn again,* said one verse. *The door shall close so it may open,* said another. Standard prophetic furniture. The sort of thing temple scholars wrote to justify their salaries.

But the hand changed as the pages turned. Different scribes across decades, then centuries. Some entries were accounts rather than prophecy—dry records of things witnessed at the Spire's base, of lights, of voices, of "the thinning," whatever that meant. One entry from a hundred and six years ago described the night the covenant broke:

*The realms are parted. What was joined is severed. We who remain below will write what we saw, though no one above will believe us. The keystone is lost. Pray it was lost somewhere quiet.*

Somewhere quiet. I remember thinking, with the small dry humor that is my only inheritance from my mother, *well, they found it.* Highmoor is nothing if not quiet.

Then I reached the final entry, and stopped being able to think at all.

It was written in a hand I knew. Not a famous hand—not an abbot's, not a scholar's. A cramped, careful, slightly left-leaning script that pressed too hard on the downstrokes because the writer had been taught on slate first and never unlearned it.

My hand.

I don't mean it resembled mine. I mean it was mine, stroke for stroke, the way my own signature looks back at me from the deeds I copy. And the entry read:

*Isandro Okafor reads this tonight, and understands nothing yet, and that is as it must be. Tonight the bells will ring out of season, and the valley that cannot hold magic will learn that some things do not need magic to hunt you. Run before the third bell ends. The book will find you again when you are someone else.*

Beneath it, in smaller letters, as if added later—or earlier, or outside of time altogether:

*Forgive us for the hiding. It was the only mercy we could afford you.*

I did what any sensible person does upon finding their own handwriting in a century-old book predicting their evening. I closed the book, set it down very carefully, and told myself I was tired.

Then I picked it up again, because I am also the person who rereads damaged ledgers three times to make sure of a figure, and denial has never once survived my habit of checking the evidence twice.

The date matched. The name matched. The hand matched. Three matches, and my whole life balanced on them like a coin on its edge.

Here is the thing about discovering your life has been a story someone else wrote: it doesn't feel dramatic. It feels like a floor that has quietly been missing for years, and you only now look down. All my careful invisibility—the shuffling, the dullness, the practiced forgetting of myself—had I built it? Or had it been built *into* me, the way a mason builds a wall? Had I been hidden the way the book said, tucked into this valley the way you tuck a valuable into a lining, somewhere quiet?

*Forgive us for the hiding.*

The Abbot's face rose unbidden: his too-knowing warnings. *You are a careful boy,* he told me once—I use they/them in my own head now, but he never troubled himself with precision—*too careful. Careful men are made, not born, and someone made you.* I'd assumed it was a lesson about humility. Sitting in the dark with a dead prophet's book open on my knees, I understood it had been something closer to grief.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the lamp to need trimming twice. Long enough for my hands to stop shaking and start doing something useful, which in my case means copying.

Because that's the truth about me, underneath everything: I am a scribe. When the world breaks, I reach for ink. I copied the final entry onto a scrap of vellum, folded it into my shirt, and copied the passage about the keystone onto another. If my life had become a document, then by every law of my profession, I should have copies. Originals burn. Copies survive. It felt less like madness than like filing.

I was returning the grey book to its impossible gap between 451 and 452 when I heard footsteps on the archive stairs.

Not the Abbot's—he announced himself with a cough from the fourth step down, always, a courtesy I'd never questioned until that moment, when I realized how strange a courtesy it was. These steps were taken by someone who wanted silence, which meant they were bad at their job, because the under-croft stairs creaked in seven places and this person hit six of them.

I put the book back. I sat. I picked up my pen and bent over the flood ledger like a man praying.

Two figures came down into the lamplight. They wore grey traveling cloaks over greyer armor, and they moved like people accustomed to rooms moving aside for them. The taller one carried a rod of black metal that I told myself was a walking staff right up until I noticed it had no wear on it, no scratches, nothing—a tool kept perfect because it was used often and cleaned after.

"Scribe." The taller one's voice was pleasant the way frost is pleasant. "We're looking for a volume. Old. Grey binding. Perhaps you've seen it."

My mouth, trained in the discipline of few words, said: "No, honored one."

"Pity." He smiled. His companion drifted toward the shelves with the unhurried confidence of someone reading a room she already owned. "We've come a long way. From the capital. The Queen's inquisitors are always welcome guests, of course."

*Always welcome* is what invaders say. I bent lower over my ledger. My heart was performing some frantic arithmetic—*inquisitors, the Queen, Veyra, why here, why now, tonight, tonight*—and my face, bless its training, showed nothing but a scribe's polite boredom.

"The valley is under temple protection," I said. "No magic passes the boundary stones."

"No," he agreed warmly. "Which is why we walked in like honest pilgrims. Magic isn't the only thing that crosses borders, scribe. Men do. Knives do. Names do." He leaned closer, and I smelled iron and incense. "Especially names. Someone lost a name in this valley a long time ago. We've come to collect it."

I said nothing. There is a silence that answers questions and a silence that invites them, and I have spent a lifetime learning the difference. This was the second kind, and I deployed it like a blade.

He straightened eventually. "Well. If a grey book turns up, the temple will want to inform us. The Abbot has already agreed." Which meant either the Abbot had agreed or would agree, and I could no longer tell which possibility frightened me more.

They went back up the stairs, hitting all seven creaks on the way, as if they'd stopped bothering to pretend.

* * *

I gave myself until the lamp burned down a finger's width to decide, which is the most generous deadline I've ever met.

Run. The word sat in me strangely. Run where? I had debt papers older than I was, binding me to this valley until the temple's ledgers forgave me—which, given the interest structure, would be sometime after the sun burned out. I had no family. No friends worth the word, only colleagues who knew my penmanship. I had a cell, a desk, a shelf of books, and a routine so finely worn it fit me like my own skin.

And that, I finally admitted, was exactly the point. That was the cage. Comfortable, quiet, invisible—and built by other hands, for purposes I was apparently the center of. *Forgive us for the hiding.* No. But perhaps forgive me if I stop hiding.

The grey book was gone when I looked for it.

Between 451 and 452: empty shelf, undisturbed dust everywhere except the clean rectangle where a book should have sat. Either the inquisitors had taken it—and why ask me about it, then?—or it had done what it said books would do. *The book will find you again when you are someone else.*

Fine. Then I would go be someone else.

I took little: my good pen, my knife, the two copied scraps, the small hoard of coin I'd saved against a disaster I'd never been able to imagine until tonight. I took the travel writ from the Abbot's outer office—not stolen, exactly; unsigned, which in temple law is a distinction with a difference, and I chose to be the kind of scribe who notices such distinctions. It bore the temple seal. It named no bearer. It granted passage through the mountain pass to one unspecified traveler on temple business.

An unspecified traveler. For the first time in my life, the omission felt like a gift.

I was halfway up the stair from the under-croft, one hand on the rail, when the bells began.

Not the hours-bells—they ring in patterns, patient and predictable as breathing. This was the alarm, the full peal, all nine bells flung into dissonance, a sound I had heard exactly once before, at my grandmother's funeral, and even then only briefly, a mistake by a novice. Out of season. Out of all reason.

*Tonight the bells will ring out of season.*

The sound rolled down through the stone and up through my bones, and across the valley I could see, through the high slit windows, torches kindling along the boundary road—many torches, moving in formation, unhurried. They weren't chasing anything. You don't chase when you know precisely where your quarry must emerge. They were setting a table.

*Run before the third bell ends.*

The third bell was ringing now.

I ran.
