## 2. The Debt of Quiet Years

The first thing you learn in Highmoor is that magic does not live here. The second thing you learn is not to ask why.

I had learned both before I learned my own letters. There was a catechism every valley child recited, palms pressed flat against the schoolroom floor as if the stone itself might testify: *The Spire is broken. The spirits are gone. Magic is a debt unpaid, and we are the ones who owe it.* We said it every morning until it stopped being words and became weather.

So when I ran — ink still wet on my fingers from a dead man's prophecy, my name sitting in a hundred-year-old book like a knife left point-up on a chair — I did not run toward anything. I ran the way a fish runs when you pull it out of water: blindly, badly, and with a growing suspicion that the problem was never the water at all.

* * *

I was six years old when the temple bought me. That's the word they used, though they dressed it in kinder cloth: *assumed your family's obligations*, *took you under the mountain's protection*. My mother owed the temple forty marks for a winter she didn't survive to see the end of, and children, apparently, are fungible currency.

I remember the ledger clerk who signed for me. A thin man with ink to his elbows, he looked at me over his spectacles the way you look at a sum you're checking twice.

"Name?"

"Isandro Okafor."

He wrote it down. Then he crossed something else out on the page above mine — something already written there, which struck even my six-year-old self as odd, because why would there be anything written about me *before* I arrived? — and blotted the page so thoroughly that whatever it said became a gray shadow shaped like nothing.

"You'll work in the archive," he said. "Quiet work. You'll be good at quiet."

And here is the strange thing, the thing I only now understand: everyone agreed I would be. Not hoped. *Agreed.* The Abbot himself came down to look at me once, an old man smelling of cedar and cold air, and studied me for a long moment like a man reading fine print.

"This one will be overlooked," he told the clerk, and it wasn't a blessing. It sounded almost like an instruction.

* * *

By midday I had made it as far as the tannery district, which is what passes for clever hiding in Highmoor: the stink of lime pits and curing hides is so total that no one lingers, and anyone hunting you assumes you wouldn't either. I wedged myself between stacked hides in a drying shed, where the air was thick enough to chew, and tried very hard to become the person the temple had spent twenty years telling me I was.

A nobody. A quiet scribe. A man whose name appeared nowhere except in debt ledgers and the margins of other people's documents.

Except that it appeared in one other place now, didn't it? In a book older than the temple's oldest wing, in a hand that predated my grandfather's grandfather: *Isandro Okafor, scribe, on this night shall—*

On this night shall what? The rest had been burned away, or scraped, or — and this thought arrived with the gentleness of a trap closing — *hidden.*

Above the drying sheds, bells began again. Not the alarm pattern. Something slower. Deliberate. I knew the bell-codes the way I knew my own handwriting, which meant I knew these bells were calling all temple staff to sanctuary. All staff.

Except they'd know exactly one member of staff was not coming.

* * *

There were warnings, in those quiet years. Of course there were. Warnings are easy to ignore precisely because they come dressed as kindness.

The Abbot caught me once, fourteen years old, copying a marginal sketch of the Spire into the corner of a tax record — just doodling, just a boy's hand moving without permission. He didn't scold me. He sat down across the desk and folded his hands.

"Do you know why the valley forbids magic, Isandro?"

"Because the covenant broke," I recited. "Because the spirits went to war with—"

"Because the valley forbids *wondering*," he said, "and magic is only wondering that got answers." He tapped the little Spire I'd drawn. "Some things are kept small on purpose. Some lamps are dimmed so that moths won't find them."

"I'm not a moth, Father Abbot."

"No," he agreed softly, looking at me in that way that made me want to check my pockets. "You're rather more the lamp, I think. Though don't repeat that. Especially not to yourself."

He stood, patted my head like a grandfather, and left me with a doodle that suddenly felt like evidence.

I thought, for years afterward, that the old man was simply mad. It's remarkable how long you can sustain that theory when the alternative requires reorganizing your entire life.

* * *

From the gap between the hides I could see the lane through a knothole worn smooth by years of some other watcher's eye. And what I saw made my stomach drop in a way the bells hadn't.

The inquisitors weren't searching.

Understand what searching looks like: doors kicked, cellars probed, questions asked loudly and badly, lanterns swung into dark corners. I'd seen searches. Highmoor occasionally produced smugglers and worse, and the watch conducted its theater like anywhere else.

This was not that. Three figures in gray-and-silver moved down the tannery lane at the pace of men strolling to a wedding. They stopped at corners and simply *stood*, facing outward, like compass needles. One consulted nothing — no map, no list — and yet turned his head, precisely, toward the drying sheds, held there a moment, then moved on.

They were waiting. Positioned. As if someone had drawn the lanes of Highmoor on a board and placed them where any piece named Isandro Okafor must inevitably pass.

Which raised a question I did not want to sit with: how do you hunt a man you've never met?

You don't. You consult his file.

And the file on Isandro Okafor had been opened long ago. Opened, annotated, and — I could feel it now, the whole shape of it rising up like a bruise — closed again by the same hands that had blotted out a line in a ledger twenty years ago. My invisibility had never been luck. Nobody is overlooked for two decades by accident. Somebody had built the overlooking. Brick by patient brick. A cage with walls made of everyone else's disinterest, and I had lived inside it gratefully, calling it peace.

I put my forehead against the hides and breathed the reek of them and let the vertigo have me for a count of ten. Twenty years. Twenty years of being *good at quiet*, and it turned out quiet was a job I'd been assigned.

* * *

One more memory insists on being told tonight, and I've learned — tonight especially — not to argue with things that insist.

My last week in the archive, before the prophecy book. The Abbot was dying; everyone knew it; he'd begun giving away his possessions like a man settling accounts. He summoned me to carry a box of books to his cell, and while I stacked them he watched me with an expression I'd seen him use on damaged manuscripts: tender, and calculating the cost of restoration.

"They will come for you eventually," he said, conversationally, as if discussing next quarter's candle order. "When they do, go north. Don't take the south road. Whatever you hear about safety in the lowlands, the south road is a throat waiting to be cut."

"Who will come, Father?"

"The people who paid for your quiet." He smiled at my face doing whatever it was doing. "Oh, child. Did you think the temple bought a debtor's son out of charity? Forty marks. Do you know what the temple pays for forty marks' worth of debt? Nothing. Debt isn't bought, it's *transferred*. Someone transferred you to us. Someone wanted you kept somewhere specific, doing something specific, being specifically unremarkable." He coughed, waved off my protest before I could find one. "Ask yourself why a magicless valley needed a scribe who can't be found. Ask yourself why the valley is magicless at all."

"Why is it?" My voice came out very small.

He looked at me for a long time, and I swear on every ledger I ever copied, the old man's eyes were wet.

"Because you're sleeping in it," he said. "Now go. And Isandro — when you run, and you will run — try to be angry about it. Anger keeps a person *real* longer than fear does."

* * *

Night came down on the tannery district like a lid. Through the knothole I watched the three gray figures light lanterns — not search-lanterns but signal-lanterns, colors climbing a pole in ordered sequence — and far off toward the valley's mouth, other lights answered. A net, then. A net with a hole in it, deliberately, in exactly one place.

The north road. The way the dying Abbot had told me to run, weeks before I had anything to run from.

They wanted me to go north. Which meant either it was a trap — or it was the one direction they couldn't follow, and their patience was a kind of desperation. I thought about the prophecy's burned-away ending. I thought about a lamp dimmed so moths won't find it, and about whose lamp I might be, and who had spent twenty years standing between me and the moths, and whether that guardian was the temple or the thing the temple served.

I thought about the Abbot's last instruction, the one he'd whispered when I carried out the empty box: *Find Serah Vance. Tell her the knight owes the mountain a debt she doesn't remember paying.*

I didn't know who Serah Vance was. But I knew the north pass was the only door out of Highmoor, and I knew the inquisitors had left it open the way you leave open the one door a mouse believes it found itself.

Anger keeps a person real longer than fear.

I took off my temple gray, wrapped myself in a tanner's stained apron, and walked out through the reeking yard toward the north road and whatever had been waiting there all along — feeling, for the first time in twenty years, entirely visible.
