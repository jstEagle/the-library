## 3. What the Knight Buried

There are three rules to running cargo through the Graytooth Pass, and I learned all of them the expensive way.

One: never ask what's in the crate. Two: never take payment in promises. Three: never, under any circumstances, take a job that smells like the temple.

I broke rule three on a Tuesday, because the coin was silver and my boots were held together with prayer and pitch, and because the broker in Corvane said "a scribe" like it was nothing—a thin little person with ink-stained fingers who wanted moving through the pass before the winter snows closed it. A scribe. Scribes don't have enemies. Scribes don't have anything, mostly, except bad posture and opinions about marginalia.

The scribe was waiting for me at the Crooked Lantern waystation when I rode up at dusk, hooded against the wind, looking exactly as dangerous as a wet cat.

"You're Vance," they said. Not a question. Their voice was low and careful, the way people speak when they've spent their whole lives making sure no one looks at them twice.

"And you're the package." I swung down from the saddle. Up close, they were younger than I'd expected—mid-twenties, maybe, with the kind of face you forget while you're still looking at it. "Rule one of working with me: I don't care what you're running from. Rule two: don't lie to me about things I'll find out anyway. Rule three—"

"Let me guess," they said. "Never take payment in promises."

So they'd met the broker too. Fine. That was one point of common ground, which was one more than I usually managed.

We took the back room. The Crooked Lantern was the kind of place where the stew moved on its own and nobody asked why, which made it perfect for my purposes. I paid for two beds, then sat across from the scribe and held out my hand for the travel writ, because that was procedure—the broker had told me to check it, stamp it, burn my copy after we crossed the pass line.

The scribe hesitated. Just a heartbeat. Just long enough.

I should have known right then.

* * *

The writ was good work. Too good. The ink was temple-grade, the kind mixed with ash and silver that shimmers if you tilt it toward firelight, and the seal pressed into the wax—

The seal.

I set the writ down very carefully, the way you'd set down a snake you'd picked up thinking it was rope.

"Where did you get this?"

"It's mine." The scribe's hands were flat on the table, steady, but their eyes had gone somewhere else. Somewhere behind locked doors.

"This is the High Seal." I kept my voice down. The barkeep was pretending not to listen, which meant he absolutely was. "Do you know how many people outside the valley have seen the High Seal? I'll tell you. Me. Because I used to be one of the people who *carried* it."

They looked at me properly then, and I watched them do the arithmetic—the knight's oath-tattoo scarred over on my wrist, the way I sat like someone who'd once been taught to sit by men with titles. People always think disgraced means forgotten. Disgraced just means remembered badly.

"You were temple guard," they said.

"I was *Sworn*. Which is worse." I stood up, took my coin purse off my belt, and counted out what I'd been paid. Then I set it on the table between us and pushed it an inch toward them. "Find another smuggler. This job's done."

Because here is what I buried, and here is what came digging itself up out of the ground the moment I saw that seal: I had sworn my life to the temple of Highmoor at seventeen, and I had believed in it the way you believe in stone—totally, without examining it—and then one night I'd been ordered to stand outside a door while men inside did something to a screaming spirit, something that made the candles gutter and my teeth ache, and I had opened the door instead of standing there, and what I saw in that room is not a story I tell by firelight or any other light. They stripped my rank. They let me live because killing me would have raised questions, and questions are the one thing temples can't abide.

And now here was a temple-badged scribe, fleeing the valley the temple forbids anyone to leave, carrying papers signed with the authority of the man who'd let me walk away alive.

"I can't go back," the scribe said quietly.

"Then don't. Go back to your archive. Copy your ledgers. Live your small life." I shouldered my pack. "That's what I'd do."

"No, it isn't," they said, and the worst part was they were right.

I got as far as the stable yard. I swear on whatever oaths I've got left that I intended to keep walking.

Then the sky over the valley road turned orange.

* * *

Firelight does strange things to mountains. It makes them look like they're bleeding. I stood in the yard with my half-packed saddle in my hands and watched the waystation on the valley side—the tollhouse, the granary, old Maret's inn—catch light one building at a time, in the orderly way fires are set when they're set by professionals.

"Inquisitors," the scribe said beside me. When had they come out? They hadn't made a sound. "Veyra's people. They're sweeping the pass."

"How do you know?"

"Because they're not searching the valley." Their voice was flat, but underneath the flatness something was shaking. "They're waiting at every exit. Like they know someone has to leave eventually. Like they're fishing."

"With you as the bait on the hook."

They didn't answer, which was an answer.

Behind us, the barkeep of the Crooked Lantern appeared in his doorway, looked at the fire, looked at the scribe, and made the swift calculation of a man deciding whose trouble he could afford. He went inside and started boarding his windows. In Corvane, that's a formal declaration of neutrality.

"They'll be here within the hour," I said. "Inquisitors ride fast and talk slow. You want to survive the night, you come with me. Not because I like you. Because if they catch a temple scribe traveling under the High Seal with a *disgraced Sworn knight*, they're going to assume conspiracy, and they're going to assume it correctly, and I would rather die on a mountain than in a cellar answering questions."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever offered me," the scribe said.

"Don't get used to it. Get on the horse."

* * *

We rode until the fire was a smudge behind us and the cold was a fact instead of a threat, and then we stopped in the lee of a granite shelf where the wind couldn't reach and built a fire small enough to hide with a coat. The scribe—I caught their name finally, Isandro, offered like a coin they weren't sure was still legal tender—wrapped their arms around their knees and stared into the flames.

I rationed out dried meat and the last of the honest bread. Smuggler's hospitality.

"So," I said. "Half-truths. I'll start. I was Sworn Guard at Highmoor temple for six years. I opened a door I shouldn't have. They took my oath and gave me my life, and I've spent nine years being paid to move things through passes and pretend I don't remember what spirits smell like." I tore my bread. "Your turn."

Isandro was quiet long enough that I thought they wouldn't pay.

"I'm a scribe," they said at last. "Debt-bound since I was seven. The temple bought my family's debts and I've been paying them off in ink ever since. I've never left the valley. I've never—" A pause. "Three nights ago I found my own name in a book that shouldn't exist. And tonight's date written underneath it. Written before I was born."

The wind moved through the pines above us. My hand had gone still on my knee.

"A prophecy book."

"The archivists call it the Ledger. It's a thousand years old and the last entry is mine." They laughed, and there was nothing funny anywhere near it. "The Abbot has been warning me my whole life not to ask questions. I thought he was protecting me from disappointment. I think now he was protecting me from being *found*."

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Here's the thing about half-truths: they fit together like broken pottery, and if you hold the pieces up to the light you can sometimes see the shape of the whole vessel. A scribe hidden in a magicless valley. A prophecy naming them. Inquisitors who didn't search but waited. Veyra, whose entire war was supposedly about mortal sovereignty, burning her way through mountain passes over one ink-stained clerk.

Whatever Isandro was, they weren't cargo anymore.

"There's something you're not telling me," I said.

"There's a lot I'm not telling me." They looked at me across the fire, and for the first time their eyes didn't slide away—they held, sharp and scared and stubborn all at once. "You opened a door you shouldn't have. Nine years ago. What did you see in that room?"

And that was the moment I understood I was going to finish this job. Not for the silver. Silver I could get anywhere, from people who didn't carry temple seals and look at me like that.

"Ask me again when we're over the pass," I said. "If you still want to know."

"That's a half-answer."

"Best rate I offer on the first day." I lay back against my pack and put my arm behind my head. "Sleep. We climb at dawn, and there's a stretch above the treeline where we'll be visible from three directions. If we're lucky, the weather will hate us enough to hide us."

Isandro settled in across the flames. For a long time neither of us spoke, and the fire cracked and spat, and somewhere far below us the valley went on burning quietly, professionally, the way everything Veyra touched seemed to do.

"Vance," they said, just before sleep took them. "Why'd you come back? In the stable yard. You'd taken the coin. You were leaving."

I watched the embers and told myself the truth was tactical. Building trust. Standard practice.

"Because you looked like someone standing in front of a closed door," I said. "Deciding whether to open it."

They didn't answer. But I heard them breathing change, slow and even, and I thought: *well. There it is.* The beginning of the thing I'd promised myself I'd never feel for cargo again.

Dawn came gray and mean, and with it, hoofbeats on the valley road below—too many, moving too fast for tax collectors. I woke Isandro with a boot to the bedroll and pointed down through the trees.

Riders in black-and-gold. And at their head, on a pale horse, a woman in inquisitor's gray holding something cupped in her hands like a lantern.

Something that glowed.

"Spirit-fire," Isandro whispered, and went white as the frost. "She's tracking me with *spirit-fire*. But that's impossible. Nothing works here. Nothing magical works here."

"Then she brought it from outside," I said, already saddling, already calculating slopes and distances and odds I didn't like. "Which means either she knows exactly what you are—"

"—or she suspects," Isandro finished.

"Mount up," I said. "We're going up the pass the dead way. Nobody follows the dead way."

Nobody sane, anyway. But I was starting to suspect that neither of us qualified, and that whatever was waiting for us above the treeline had been waiting a very long time.
