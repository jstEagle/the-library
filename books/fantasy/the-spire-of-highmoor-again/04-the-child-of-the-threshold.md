## 4. The Child of the Threshold

The ruin had no name on any map I'd ever copied, which by now I understood to be less an accident and more a policy. Serah called it the Threshold, said it like a curse she'd inherited from her grandmother, and refused to camp within fifty paces of it until hunger overruled superstition somewhere around midnight.

"It's not a ruin," she said, chewing. "Ruins are things that ended. This is more like... a door somebody left half-shut."

"That's worse," I said.

"Everything about you is worse," she agreed comfortably, and threw me the last strip of dried mutton because apparently insults and charity were the same gesture in her economy.

I should explain what we were running from, or toward, or through, but honestly the categories had begun to blur. Three days out of Highmoor valley, past the pass where inquisitors had burned a waystation full of innocent people for the crime of possibly knowing us, and I still couldn't make myself believe the book was real. The prophecy. My name in ink older than my grandfather. *Isandro Okafor, on the night the bells ring wrong.* Tonight the bells had rung wrong, and I was here, and the ink was right, and every rational bone in my body — I had counted them during sleepless hours; there were two hundred six, same as anyone — insisted this was coincidence wearing prophecy's coat.

Serah didn't believe in destiny. She'd told me so twice, once while sharpening her knife and once while pointing it at me, which I took as a consistent position.

What I believed in was ledgers. And the ledger of the last three days read: waystation burned, temple seal now worth more as kindling than as authority, and a knight who'd been paid to move one scribe through mountains now moving that scribe considerably further than contracted, unpaid, and unasked. People don't do arithmetic like that without a reason. I hadn't asked hers yet. I suspected neither of us would enjoy the answer.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin, the kind of light that makes even beautiful country look like it owes someone money. The Threshold sat in a fold of hills where the grass grew wrong — too green near the broken arches, too pale beyond them, as if the land itself couldn't decide which side of something it stood on.

The arches were the first strange thing. There must have been thirty of them, free-standing, no walls attached, marching up the hillside in rows like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. Each one framed slightly different air. Through the nearest, the hillside behind looked the same but *quieter*, the way a room looks after everyone has left it. Through another, snow fell in a column that never reached the ground.

"Don't walk through any," Serah said, before I'd even finished looking.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were absolutely going to. You have a scribe's face. It's the face of a person about to touch something labeled *do not touch*."

I resented this because it was accurate. In the archive, forbidden shelves had been the only interesting ones. A lifetime of copying other people's records leaves a person hungry for anything unwritten.

We picked our way between the arches, and I noticed the birds — or rather, I noticed there were none, and then noticed that the silence wasn't empty. It had texture. Underneath the wind there was something like breathing, slow and vast, the sound a mountain might make if it slept.

And there were lights.

Small ones. Pale blue-white, drifting at knee height among the arches like seeds on an invisible current. I stopped. Serah kept walking three steps before realizing I wasn't beside her.

"What?" she said.

"You don't see those?"

She looked. Her jaw did something complicated. "No. And you shouldn't either. Valley-born can't see spirit-light. Nobody can, not really, not since—" She stopped herself, the way people stop themselves at cliff edges.

"Since the covenant broke," I finished. The words came out of me smooth and practiced, and I realized with a small cold horror that they came from the prophecy book, that I had memorized it against my will, the way you memorize a debt.

"Yeah." Serah's hand had found her knife hilt. Not drawing it. Just touching it, the way you touch a railing. "Which is why you seeing it is a problem."

"It's probably nothing. Reflections. Marsh gas—"

"In the mountains."

"Determined marsh gas."

Something laughed. Not Serah, and not me, though my mouth had gone dry enough to be a candidate. The laugh came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, high and bright and young, and one of the drifting lights flared, wobbled, and shot away between two arches like a child called home to dinner.

Serah swore with the fluency of a woman who had served in two armies. "Don't follow it."

I followed it.

In my defense: it was that or stand still while my entire understanding of myself finished collapsing, and movement has always been how I handle grief. I ducked under an arch — the third one, the one framing air that smelled faintly of rain that hadn't fallen anywhere in this world — and heard Serah cursing behind me and following anyway, because whatever else she was, transactional loyalty had apparently deepened into the kind that follows fools under haunted doors.

Beyond the arch, the hillside continued, but it continued *wrong*. The grass was silver-green. The sky above held two suns, one real and one a ghost of itself, dimmer, slightly offset, like a badly printed page laid over a good one. And sitting on a fallen lintel, legs swinging, watching me with enormous interest, was a child.

Not a human child. I want to be precise here, because precision was my trade before it became my curse. The shape was childlike — small, maybe seven years old, bare feet, a tunic woven of something like fog — but the edges of it moved. Light ran under its skin the way light runs under ice. Its hair drifted upward constantly, slowly, as if underwater. Its eyes were the exact color of the drifting lights, and they were old. That was the worst part. A child's face with eyes that had been watching for a hundred years.

"Hello," it said. Its voice was the laugh, and the laugh was bells rung wrong, rung *right*, I couldn't tell anymore. "You're late."

I looked back at the arch. Serah stood on the far side, knife drawn now, face bloodless. Her lips moved. No sound crossed. Between us, the arch framed a wall of faint shimmering air, like heat over a forge.

"We're separated by a door," I called, uselessly.

"She can't hear you," the child said, hopping down. Its feet made no sound on stone. "Doors only open one way for the quick. You're standing on both sides, though. Isn't that funny? You're the first thing in a century that stands on both sides."

"I don't understand."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "But you will. That's what late means." It tilted its head. "You're Isandro."

My whole body went cold. "How do you know my name?"

"You wrote it on me." It pressed a small fog-woven hand flat against its own chest. "A hundred years ago. When you made the covenant, you signed yourself into it. We all felt it when the signing tore." Its old eyes went wide and wet and suddenly, terribly young. "It hurt. Everyone went quiet and the Spire went dark and I couldn't find my mothers, and I've been waiting here ever since, because this was the last place the door worked, and you said—" its voice cracked like actual glass — "you said you'd come back."

"I think you have the wrong person. I'm a scribe. I copy ledgers. I'm twenty-three years old. I was born in a valley where magic doesn't work—"

"Yes," the child said, with terrible gentleness. "That was the hiding part."

I reached out — I don't know why, instinct, pity, the scribe's incurable need to verify sources — and touched its shoulder.

* * *

The world opened.

I stood atop a spire — *the* Spire — a needle of white stone piercing cloud, and it was singing. Not metaphorically. Stone sang, and light climbed it like ivy in fast bloom, and around its crown wheeled shapes that might have been birds and might have been thoughts, and every window glowed with faces, mortal and otherwise, mingled at the sill like neighbors at a festival. Below, a city spread in terraces, and the people in it cast shadows that sometimes didn't match them, and nobody minded, because shadows were just friends of a different sort. I could feel the covenant the way you feel your own heartbeat: a great cord of light running from every living thing up into the Spire and down again, binding, feeding, *remembering*. And at the center of the cord, at the keystone point where the whole arch of it bore, there was a name written in light, and the name was—

The vision tore away. I was on my knees in silver grass, and the child was crouched in front of me, worried, and my face was wet, and somewhere beyond the arch Serah was shouting words I finally couldn't hear because I was making too much noise myself.

I had seen it. I had seen the Spire of Highmoor, which every map in the archive marked with a careful scholarly note: *location uncertain; possibly mythical.* I had felt the covenant like my own pulse. And at its center —

"The name," I whispered. "At the middle of it. Whose name?"

The child looked at me with a century of patience and one century of grief.

"Yours," it said. "It was always yours. They just took it out of you and hid you where nothing could hear it." Its small hand found mine, weightless as breath. "My name is Wren. You gave me that too. Will you come home now? Will you fix the door?"

Behind me, the shimmer in the arch flickered — and through it, briefly, I saw Serah's face change as something on *her* side changed too, as if the Threshold had noticed us noticing it. Far off, down in the mortal valleys, thin and wrong and unmistakable, bells began to ring.

Not temple bells. Not alarm bells.

Bells I somehow knew were ringing for me.

Wren squeezed my hand. "They've started looking again," it said. "We should run."
