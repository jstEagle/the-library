## 5. The Usurper's Mercy

I had conquered four cities before breakfast, which by my standards was a slow morning.

The maps were spread across the long table in the war tent—Corvane, Ashfell, the twin river-towns of Merrow and Dunmark—and each bore the small red pin that meant *surrendered without siege*, and one black pin that meant I would have to be rude about it. My marshals hovered at the edges like well-dressed crows. Marshals always do. They think hovering communicates deference; mostly it communicates hunger.

"Ashfell's granaries are full," said Marshal Okonwe, tapping the black pin. "They can hold a year."

"They can hold a year," I agreed. "But their granaries are full because they've been hoarding against us since spring, and hoarded grain spoils morale faster than it spoils in the sack. Give them three weeks of rumors about what we did to Dunmark—" here Okonwe winced; Dunmark had been an embarrassment of enthusiasm on someone's part "—and they'll open the gates themselves and thank us for the privilege."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we starve them politely." I moved the pin myself, because a queen who lets other people move her pins is a queen who wakes up one morning with someone else's hand on her throat. It had happened before, in this very country. I should know. My hand was the someone else's.

The tent flap opened and my spymaster entered carrying the particular expression she reserved for news that was either wonderful or catastrophic. With Tessaly these were usually the same thing.

"Your Majesty. The valley."

I dismissed the marshals. They filed out with the studied slowness of people hoping to overhear something through canvas, and I let them hope. Fear travels fastest when it has to be assembled from fragments.

"Tell me," I said.

"The keystone has left Highmoor."

I set down the pin I was holding. Carefully. One learns, in this trade, that the difference between a ruler and a tyrant is often just how loudly things get set down.

"When?"

"Three days ago. The temple raised no alarm—the Abbot suppressed it, we believe, for nearly two days—but our listener in the tannery district confirms inquisitors entered the valley on the night of the alarm. The scribe Isandro Okafor is gone. The valley records show debt-purchase, temple indenture, twenty years' service. Nothing else. No visitors, no correspondence, no—" Tessaly consulted her notes, though I knew she knew them by heart, "—no *anything*. Twenty years and the file is thin as communion paper."

"That," I said, "is rather the point."

She looked at me. Tessaly is the only person alive permitted to look at me in that particular way, which I permit precisely because she is the only person whose looking costs me nothing. Everyone else's attention has a price attached.

"You built that emptiness yourself," she said. Not quite a question.

"I commissioned it. There's a difference. Emperors commission cathedrals; I commissioned a blank page." I walked to the map, to the small green square tucked into the mountains where magic went to die. Highmoor. A valley so saturated with null that a candle flame burned straight and spirits passed over it like birds over glass. For twenty years it had held the most important person in the world, and its greatest achievement was that no one—not the person themselves, not the temple, not the gods—had noticed.

"And the Abbot?"

"In custody. He asked to be brought to you personally. He was very insistent."

"He would be. Ambrose always did love an audience." I pulled my cloak from its peg. "Come. Let's go disappoint him."

* * *

They kept the Abbot in the stone chapel at the edge of camp, which I had ordered cleared of everything except a table, two chairs, and a window. Some prisoners you break with darkness. Ambrose you break with daylight—it forces him to see his own face reflected in the glass while he talks, and Ambrose has never once survived seeing his own face honestly.

He looked older than the last time I'd seen him, which had been eleven years ago, across a negotiation table, when he'd tried to buy back a heretical text I'd seized and I'd tried to buy his silence and we'd both pretended the transaction was about books. He sat straight. His hands were folded. His chains were loose enough to be a courtesy and tight enough to be a reminder, which is the only kind of chain worth using on a man of letters.

"Queen Veyra," he said. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." I took the chair opposite him. "You'll say *usurper* or you'll say *majesty* and either way we both know which one you mean, so let's skip the liturgy. You've been asking for me. Here I am. Admire the view."

He glanced at the window, then at his reflection, and flinched almost invisibly. Almost.

"You've taken the valley's silence and turned it into a hunting blind," he said. "You put the child there. All those years ago."

"I put a *child* nowhere. I put a *keystone* where it could do no harm, and the child was simply what grew around it, the way oysters grow around grit." I let that sit. "Though I confess I didn't expect the oyster to grow legs."

"No one ever does." His mouth twitched. Even now. Even chained. "Isandro was a good scribe. Meticulous. Kind to the novices. You know what the terrible irony of your plan was, Majesty? Your blank page became the gentlest person in the temple. All that engineered emptiness, and what filled it was *care*."

Something turned over behind my ribs. I have learned not to name such things in front of enemies.

"Sentiment," I said. "From the man who sold me a child's location for a promise of orthodoxy."

"I sold you a *theory*," Ambrose said sharply, and for the first time the old fire came through. Good. I wanted the fire; the fire was where the truth lived. "A theory your scholars confirmed and your inquisitors enforced. But I want you to understand something before you do whatever it is you dragged me across half a country to do. I have read the covenant texts more closely than anyone breathing. Closer than Dell Maro, if he still lives. And I am telling you: the prophecy does not say what your archivists think it says. 'The bearer shall reforge the covenant'—yes. But reforging is not restoring. Metal that has been reforged remembers the breaking. Whatever comes out of that Spire will not be the world that was. It will be something new wearing the old world's clothes, and it will not ask your permission."

"Neither did the old covenant," I said quietly. "That's rather my objection to it."

And here is the thing I have told perhaps four living souls, and now I suppose I am telling this page as well, because pages keep secrets better than people and this account may someday be all that remains of my reasoning.

When I was nine years old, in the city of Sarnholt, my mother made an offering at a spirit-shrine. This was normal; everyone did; the covenant was old then, and whole, and the spirits of the near-realm took our incense and gave us small mercies—a good harvest, a mild winter, a fever that broke. My mother asked the shrine for mercy for my father, who owed money to men who broke fingers as a form of correspondence.

The spirit heard her. That was the trouble. The spirit heard her, and decided that the most merciful outcome—for the family, for the debt, for the tidy arithmetic of fate—was for my father to die quickly, which he did, falling from a scaffold the next morning, insured against accident, his death paying his debts twice over. The spirit even sent a dream to explain it. My mother woke weeping and grateful. *It heard me,* she kept saying. *It heard me.* She thanked the shrine every week until she died.

I stopped going when I was ten. I started reading when I was eleven. And by fourteen I understood what the priests would not say aloud: that the covenant is not a treaty between equals. It is a leash we pretend is a handshake. Spirits do not love us. They *manage* us, the way a shepherd manages sheep—with genuine affection, perhaps, and absolute indifference to any single animal's understanding of its own life. Every prayer is a door we cannot close. Every offering is a signature on a contract we have never been allowed to read.

The free cities call me conqueror. Fine. Conqueror is a word mortals use for mortals, and at least it is a fight between things that can lose. But a covenant? A covenant is conquest made permanent, conquest sanctified, conquest that the conquered are required to *gratitude* for. I did not rise up against kings to kneel to weather-gods.

So yes. I broke the covenant. Or rather—I am breaking it, still, patiently, the way water breaks stone. The Spire dimmed the day my armies crossed the first border, and every free city that falls loosens the weave another turn. And the keystone—the living hinge, the thing the whole door swings on—if it stays hidden, stays *unmade*, stays a blank page in a silent valley, then the door never swings again. Not restored. Not reforged. Simply, finally, closed.

"You think I want power," I said to Ambrose. "Everyone thinks I want power. Power is incidental. Power is what you need when the alternative is kneeling. What I want—" I stood, and walked to the window, and looked out at my army, ten thousand tents smoking gently in the dawn, mortal fires, mortal smoke, answerable to no wind. "What I want is for a child born in Sarnholt to pray or not pray and know that either way, her father comes home from the scaffold. That is all. That is the entire architecture of my ambition. One generation of a world where mercy belongs to the people giving it."

Ambrose was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone soft and dreadful, the way it did in the archive when he'd found a corruption in a text he loved.

"And the cost?"

"There's always a cost."

"The spirits don't simply fade, Veyra. They *fall*. Have you seen a fallen spirit? I have. In the border ruins. They come apart slowly, over decades, screaming in frequencies only children can hear. There is a child-shaped one in the Threshold ruin past the pass—I felt it from the road, years ago, and I have dreamed of it since. Your quiet world will be built on a century of dying gods, and you will call it peace."

For a moment—just a moment—I saw it. The ruins, the falling, the long scream beneath the silence. And I found, examining myself with the honesty I permit only in chapels and on pages, that I could live with it. That was the terrible discovery of my life: I could live with it. Somewhere inside me was a room where that grief fit neatly on a shelf, labeled and dusted, and I hated the room and I kept it anyway.

"Then they should have read the contracts they wrote us," I said. "Mercy that doesn't ask permission isn't mercy. It's husbandry."

"And so you'll do the same. Husbandry. Of us."

"Yes," I said. "But I'll be honest about it. That's the improvement."

* * *

Outside the chapel, Tessaly fell into step beside me. She waited until we were past the guards, which is her way of telling me the news is bad.

"The keystone's path," she said. "Our listeners along the mountain road report a smuggler's waystation burned two nights ago—inquisitors, ours. They missed the quarry by hours. But there's a complication." She handed me a slip of paper, thin as communion paper, and I thought absurdly of Ambrose's file, and of oysters.

"The child," Tessaly said, "wasn't traveling alone. And the second traveler—the descriptions don't agree, but three separate witnesses used the same word."

I read the slip. Read it again. The word leapt off it the way certain words do, the ones that arrive ahead of their consequences.

*A knight.*

Not just any description. Red hair, sword of Corvane make, a scar across the left eyebrow. Serah Vance. Disgraced at the Battle of the Fords, struck from the rolls, presumed dead in the southern provinces. Presumed dead by everyone, that is, except the woman who had once offered her a commission and been refused—and who had noted, at the time, that Vance's refusal had less to do with loyalty than with a scruple, and that scruples, unlike loyalties, can be redirected.

"She's escorting the keystone," Tessaly said.

"No," I said, folding the slip into my sleeve, and I confess I smiled, and I confess further that it was not entirely a cold smile. "She's escorting a frightened scribe who happens to be the keystone. Which means she doesn't yet know what she's carrying. Which means, for the moment, the most dangerous woman on that mountain believes she's protecting someone."

"And when she finds out?"

"Then she'll have a choice." I looked east, toward the pass, toward the Spire standing pale and patient above the clouds like a needle waiting for thread. "Everyone does. That's what makes this different from the covenant, Tessaly. I intend to offer every soul in this war the choice the spirits never gave us. Most will choose badly. But some—"

I didn't finish it. Some sentences you leave open on purpose, the way you leave a gate open, to see what walks through.

"Prepare the vanguard," I said. "We march for Corvane. And send riders to the pass—I want the keystone's route mapped hour by hour. If the scribe reaches the Spire, everything Ambrose fears comes true, and everything I've broken gets rebuilt worse."

Tessaly hesitated. "And if the inquisitors take them first?"

"Then I'll question the keystone myself," I said, "and I will be *very* merciful."
