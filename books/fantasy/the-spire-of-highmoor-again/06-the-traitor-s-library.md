## 6. The Traitor's Library

Dell Maro's shop smelled of dust and old vinegar and something faintly burnt, like a candle that had been snuffed mid-prayer. The sign above the door read MARO & SONS, RESTORATION OF TEXTS, which was funny in the way that grief is funny, because Dell had no sons and restored nothing so much as he dismantled things—books, certainties, whatever else you brought him.

"Third shelf," he said without looking up from his desk. "Behind the false Psalter. Mind the trap. It bites."

I lifted down a fat volume bound in cracked leather, and as my fingers closed on it, a hair-thin wire sang somewhere under the shelf. I flinched. Somewhere in the walls, a small mechanism clicked twice and went quiet.

"It only bites people who take books they shouldn't," Dell said. "You're holding the right one, apparently. Congratulations. Sit."

Serah took the chair by the door, where she could see both windows and the street beyond. Wren sat on the floor at her feet, cross-legged, watching me with those eyes that were too old for a child's face and too young for anything else. I sat across from Dell at his scarred worktable, and I set the book between us like an offering.

Or a confession.

* * *

I should say what I knew before Corvane, because what I knew was almost nothing, and Dell Maro built cathedrals out of almost nothing.

The prophecy book from Highmoor's archive—I'd stolen it, or borrowed it forever, depending on your theology—had been read to pieces on the road. Serah and I had gone over it every night by firelight while Wren slept with their head against my pack like it was made of feathers instead of spirit-stuff. Most of it was the usual prophetic slurry: thrones falling, rivers running backward, a spire that wept. But the final entry was specific enough to keep me awake through three mountain passes.

*A scribe of no name shall find their name.*

*Tonight, when the bells speak wrongly, the keystone wakes.*

*What was hidden in the valley without magic is the door itself.*

Then, below, in ink so old it had gone brown:

*Let the self be unmade, and the covenant stand.*

"You've read this a hundred times," Serah had said the night before we reached the city gates. She wasn't asking. She was telling me to stop reading it.

"I've read it a hundred times," I agreed. "That doesn't mean I understand it."

"No one understands prophecies. That's how they get you."

But she kept watch a little longer those nights. And Wren, who rarely spoke, had taken to humming when they thought I couldn't hear—a small, tuneless sound that I eventually recognized, because I'd heard it once before, in the vision that came when I first touched them: the sound the Spire makes, if a Spire can be said to make sound, which apparently it can.

So: Corvane. The free city, last of its kind, all slate roofs and hanging gardens and bells that rang on schedule because nobody here had ever needed magic to tell them the time. And in the Tanners' Row district—not the tanneries themselves, Dell had been quick to clarify, that would be beneath him, though he charged like a man who owned tanneries—a shop with a false Psalter and a biting shelf.

Dell opened the book flat on his table. His hands were beautiful, the hands of a surgeon or a forger, and I learned later they had been both.

"Now," he said. "Before we begin, some ground rules. One: I ask questions, you answer honestly, because lies waste my time and my time is the most expensive thing in this room. Two: you don't touch anything else. Three—" he looked at Wren, then back at me, and something in his face shifted, some long-settled sediment stirred up—"you tell me why Veyra wants you badly enough to burn a waystation full of innocents over it. I taught that woman everything she knows about cruelty disguised as efficiency. I'd like to know what she's being efficient about."

"You taught Veyra?" Serah said, hand drifting toward her sword.

"I taught her palaeography, cartography, and the difference between ambition and purpose." He said it flatly, without pride or shame. "She learned the third lesson wrong. It happens. Sit down, knight. If I wanted you dead, I'd have let the shelf do it."

* * *

He worked through the afternoon, and I watched a traitor read.

That's what they called him, I learned—the Traitor Archivist. Twenty years ago he'd been First Archivist of the Royal Library in Vhalen, before Veyra took the throne there, before she purged the archives of everything that mentioned the covenant or the Spire or the spirit-realm. Dell had smuggled out six carts of forbidden texts under cover of a plague quarantine, and Veyra had hanged his apprentices for it, all four of them, publicly, because Veyra understood that the fastest way to hurt a scholar is not to kill him but to kill what he loves.

He never spoke of them. But sometimes, working, his hands would pause over a page, and I'd see him look at Wren—quickly, like touching a bruise—and then continue.

"Here." He tapped a passage in a text older than the prophecy book, written in a script that predated the valley, maybe predated the kingdoms. "The Concordance of Ashes. Read it aloud. You read Old Thalassic?"

"A little. Temple training."

"Aloud anyway. Some texts want to be heard."

So I read:

*When the covenant was forged, it required an anchor in each realm. A stone in the world of spirits. A word in the world of men. When the Spire broke, the anchor did not break—it fled. It put on flesh and hid where flesh cannot be found by spirit or spell: the valley without magic, the hollow in the world's memory.*

I stopped. My voice had gone strange in my own ears, thin, like someone else's.

"The keystone," I said. "It's a person."

"The keystone," Dell said gently, and gentleness from that man was more alarming than anger, "is you."

The room did not spin. I want to be clear about that; I'd expected spinning, the way you expect it in stories. What happened instead was worse. The room became very still and very detailed, the way a room does when you realize you will remember it forever—the vinegar smell, the dust turning in the light from the high window, Serah's breath catching, Wren's humming stopping mid-note.

"Explain," Serah said, in her parade-ground voice, because someone had to.

"Gladly." Dell turned pages with those beautiful hands. "A century ago, the Spire broke. Not fell—broke, the way a promise breaks. The realms tore along the seam of the covenant, and the spirits on this side began to fade. Withering, the temples call it. The valley of Highmoor was founded the same year, on the same day, by the same order that held the covenant. Coincidence?" He smiled without warmth. "There are no coincidences in founding charters. The temple didn't forbid magic in Highmoor. They built a place where magic *couldn't exist*, and they put their most dangerous possession in it—you—as a child, purchased under a debt contract no one has ever been able to produce, because it never existed. You weren't bought, scribe. You were hidden."

"My whole life—"

"Was a vault." He nodded. "Yes. They raised you to be invisible. No family name, no remarkable talents, no attachments they couldn't sever. The Abbot—what was his name? Never mind. The Abbot was your warden, not your father. Every kindness he showed you was maintenance."

I thought of the Abbot's warnings. *Stay small, Isandro. Small things are overlooked.* I had thought it was wisdom. It had been instructions.

"And tonight," I said, because my mouth was moving without consulting me, "the bells rang wrong. The entry says the keystone wakes when the bells speak wrongly."

"The temple's alarm. Someone triggered it deliberately—or the vault's own wards failed, because you'd already touched the prophecy book. Either way, the hiding is over. Veyra knows what you are now, or will within days. Which brings us"—he turned to the prophecy book itself, to the brown-inked line at the bottom—"to the part I imagine you've already guessed."

"*Let the self be unmade,*" I said.

"Yes."

"What does it mean? Precisely. In Old Thalassic. Don't soften it."

Dell looked at me for a long moment, and I saw him decide to respect me, which was its own strange comfort.

"'Self,'" he said, "in Old Thalassic is *anima*, but the root is older—it means the thread that names a thing. To unmake the self is to cut that thread. Not death. Death leaves the name behind; people remember the dead. This is—" he searched for the word and hated it when he found it— "*dissolution*. You would cease to have ever had a name. The Abbot would not remember buying a child. Serah would not remember the waystation, or the firelight, or you. Every mind that holds the thread of you lets go of it. And into that emptiness, the covenant pours itself. You become the anchor again—but nothing. No one. The stone in the spirit-world and the word in the world of men, except the word is erased."

Silence. Even Wren had gone still.

"There's no other way?" Serah's voice had lost its parade-ground edge. "No alternative reading? You're the great archivist. Find another reading."

"I am the great archivist," Dell said, "and I have spent twenty years looking for another reading, because the first time I decoded this passage I hoped to give it to Veyra as proof the covenant could be restored cheaply. There is no other reading. There is only the question of whether anyone would pay it." He looked at me. "Would you?"

And here is the thing I have thought about since, in whatever hours I've had for thinking: I didn't hesitate because I was noble. I hesitated because I was greedy—for the name. For Isandro Okafor, debt-scribe, tea-stealer, terrible singer, the small life I had hated and mourned in the same breath for twenty years. I had only just learned I had a self worth unmaking. It felt obscene to lose it so soon.

"I don't know," I said finally, which was the most honest sentence spoken in that room all day.

"Good," Dell said. "Honesty. Veyra would have answered instantly. She always knows her price; it's why she'll lose." He closed the Concordance. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow we decode the reforging rite itself—it requires the Spire's summit, a bloodline's consent, and a threshold-born witness. I suspect you already know two of the three."

Wren looked up at me then, and smiled, and for the first time I noticed that their smile was exactly the shape of the doorway in the border ruin where we'd found them.

Threshold-born. Of course.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the back room of Maro & Sons among the smuggled books with a candle stub, writing in my journal—which I mention now, because you should know that everything I wanted to save of myself, I saved there, in ink, like a coward hoarding coins before a flood. My name. The Abbot's face. The taste of Highmoor's bad tea. The way Serah hums when she thinks no one's listening, tuneless, like Wren.

Near dawn, I heard Serah arguing with Dell in low voices in the shop, and caught only fragments—*"too fast,"* *"scouts said weeks,"* and Dell saying, in the voice of a man reading a ledger he'd hoped was balanced: *"She wouldn't march unless someone told her exactly where to find us."*

I blew out my candle and listened to Corvane's bells begin the morning hour, on schedule, faithful, telling the time to a city that did not yet know it had run out of it.
