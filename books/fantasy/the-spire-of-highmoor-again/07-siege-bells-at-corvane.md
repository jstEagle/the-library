## 7. Siege Bells at Corvane

I've been robbed before. Held at knifepoint in a smuggler's den outside Vell, cheated by a fence with kind eyes, once even arrested by a magistrate I'd later learn was on my own payroll. I know the sound of betrayal—the particular silence it leaves behind, the way a room goes quiet around the place where trust used to be.

But I had never heard anything like Corvane's siege bells.

They rang wrong, is what I mean to say. A city that expects an army rings its bells in pattern—three strikes for distant riders, five for a host on the march, giving everyone time to bar doors and argue about grain prices. These bells just rang, all of them at once, no pattern, no warning. The sound of a city discovering it had already lost.

"They can't be here," Dell Maro said. He stood in the doorway of his library with a decoding tablet still in his hand, chalk dust on his sleeve like snow. "The scouts placed Veyra's vanguard nine days east. Nine days. Armies don't move nine days in three."

"Armies don't," I said. "Armies with someone inside the walls do."

He looked at me like I'd spat on his mother's grave. Then he looked at the bells, and stopped arguing, because the truth has a way of ending conversations faster than swords.

Isandro came down the archive stairs two steps at a time, Wren perched on their hip like the child weighed nothing, which by every law I understood of spirits they shouldn't have been able to carry at all. Isandro's face did the thing I'd learned to read across a hundred leagues of bad road: the jaw set, the eyes going somewhere far away and terrible.

"No," I said.

"You don't even know what I was going to—"

"I've known you since the tannery district, scribe. You get that face when you're about to do something self-sacrificing and stupid. It's your only face."

* * *

Here's what I'll say about the evacuation, because someone should say something fair about Corvane before the histories get written by whoever wins: the city didn't panic. That's not nothing.

The eastern gate creaked open onto the river road, and ten thousand people funneled through it in something close to order—carters first, then families, then the guildsmen who stayed too long locking up shops they'd never see again. Dell Maro directed traffic from a crate like he'd been born to it, shouting about which bridges were load-bearing and which were lies. Wren rode on Isandro's shoulders and made the streetlamps flicker as we passed, little pulses of green light, and the children in the crowd laughed instead of crying, which may be the bravest thing anyone did that day.

And above it all, past the walls, the army assembled out of the morning fog with a patience that turned my stomach. No horns. No war cries. Just rank after rank of Veyra's grey-and-crimson resolving out of the mist like the tide coming in, unhurried, certain. An army that knows the gates will open doesn't bother shouting.

"Someone gave them the watch rotations," I said to Isandro as we crossed the third bridge. "Someone gave them the grain stores, the gate mechanisms, the whole spine of the place. This isn't a siege. It's a delivery."

"I know." Isandro's voice was very quiet. "Dell thinks it might be the harbor master. Or—" and there it was again, that face, "—or it doesn't matter, Serah. Because I'm going to end it either way."

I should have stopped walking right there. I want to be honest about this part, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now: I heard them say *end it*, and I kept walking, because we were on a bridge full of refugees and there wasn't time. That's the excuse I've been carrying ever since. There wasn't time.

* * *

It was Dell who found the note, hours later, when the rearguard fires were burning and we'd gone to ground in the undercroft of a river shrine where the smugglers' guild used to stash untaxed brandy. A scrap of paper in Isandro's neat temple hand, tucked into the false bottom of their satchel where they thought no one would look.

*To the Queen's heralds, at the western postern, before dawn. I come freely. The bearer of the covenant offers herself in exchange for the free cities' quartering.*

*Herself.* Not even their name. They'd signed themselves away like a debt instrument, which I suppose was fitting, given everything the temple had done to make them feel like one.

"She'll take the bargain," Isandro said when I confronted them, holding the note up between two fingers like a dead fish. They didn't deny it. I'll grant them that much; they looked me in the eye while they lied to me. "Veyra wants the keystone, not corpses. One person against ten cities, Serah. You do the arithmetic."

"Oh, I've done the arithmetic." I crumpled the note. "You're a scribe who spent twenty years being invisible on purpose. You think she'll honor a bargain? She burned the waystation at Harrow Ford with forty people inside it because *one* of them might have seen our faces. You're not selling yourself, Isandro. You're buying a slower death and calling it mercy."

"And what would you have me do?" Their voice cracked, finally, all that temple-trained composure splitting down the middle. "March up the Spire with a knight and a child and good intentions? The prophecy says the reforging costs *the self, unmade*. Dell told us. If I go to her now, maybe—maybe—I buy the cities time to evacuate properly. Maybe Wren gets to grow up somewhere safe. Maybe—"

"Maybe nothing. You don't surrender to save people. You surrender because you're still the kid the Abbot bought, still paying off a debt that was never yours, and you've decided dying is just the last installment." I stepped closer. "I buried a version of myself once, Isandro. Under a court-martial and a bad winter and a bottle. I know exactly how heavy it gets, deciding you deserve less than other people. And I am telling you, as someone who dug her way back out—it's a lie. It's the worst lie there is, because you dress it up as virtue."

Wren was watching us from the corner, hugging their knees, lamp-green eyes enormous. Somewhere overhead, boots on the shrine floor. Too many. Inquisitors had reached the river district; we'd known since dusk. Our window was closing and we were spending it screaming at each other in a brandy cellar.

"We leave through the postern tunnels at first light," I said. "North, to the Spire road. We climb. All of us. Together. And if the cost is what Dell says it is, then you pay it at the top, where it means something—not in Veyra's tent like a criminal signing a confession."

Isandro opened their mouth—

—and that's when the ceiling came in.

* * *

I'll compress the next part, because some things don't bear dwelling on. The inquisitors dropped through the shrine above—weakened mortar, bought information, same story all day—and the undercroft became smoke and splinters and the ringing kind of quiet that follows an explosion. I remember Dell shoving the decoding tablets into a sack. I remember Wren screaming, a sound like glass remembering it was once sand. I remember reaching for Isandro.

And I remember Isandro already moving—not toward the tunnel, toward the *inquisitors*, arms raised, calling out in a clear carrying voice: "Here! I'm the one you want! Let the others go!"

Self-sacrificing and stupid. Right on schedule.

What happened next happened fast, and I've replayed it slow so many times the slowness feels more true than the event. I got my arm around their throat. I felt their pulse hammering against my forearm—fast, terrified, alive—and I thought, *not like this, you don't get to spend yourself like small coin, you don't get to decide alone anymore,* and then I did the thing I'll answer for however many years I have left.

I hit them. Hard, behind the ear, the way you fell a panicking horse. Isandro went down without a sound, and I caught them before the ground could, and for one moment I held all of them—all that borrowed invisibility, all that terrible decency—and I said, into their hair, where no one could hear:

"Loyalty without truth is just ownership. Fine. Then own me. Hate me tomorrow. Live first."

Then the wall exploded inward, and everything became running.

* * *

We almost made it.

Dell and I took the tunnel north with Isandro over my shoulder like a sack of contraband, which is the most honest description of my life's work I can offer. Behind us, in the collapsing undercroft, Wren turned to hold the doorway—turned and *blazed*, spirit-light pouring off a body too small for it, holding six armed inquisitors at bay the way a candle holds back a flood.

"WREN!" I screamed it until my throat tore. "Come on, love, come ON—"

The child looked back at me. Smiled, actually smiled, that gap-toothed spirit-child smile, and said in a voice like wind through a keyhole: "Keystone needs carrying, Serah-of-the-broken-oath. Wren is only a threshold. Thresholds hold doors."

Then the roof beam came down between us, fire swallowing the passage, and the last I saw of Wren was green light folding inward like a hand closing around something precious.

They have the child. Veyra has Wren. And Isandro woke at dawn on the Spire road, thirty miles north, with a knot on their skull, chains I forged myself around their wrists—my old campaign shackles, the ones I swore I'd never use on anything but cargo—and a look in their eyes I'll be seeing every night for the rest of my life.

"You hit me," they said. Not angry. Worse. Understanding.

"Yeah," I said, and unlocked nothing. "Climb anyway."
