## 8. The Long Ascent

I woke to the taste of iron and the discovery that my hands were cuffed in front of me like a criminal's—which was fair, because I had been behaving like one, and the cuffs were not the inquisitors' work.

I knew that immediately by the make of them. Temple iron is ornamental; these were honest, ugly things with a hinge like a jaw and a lock that had been closed by someone who loved locks the way some people love arguments. And there was only one person I'd met in the last month who could forge something that looked this much like a grudge.

"Serah," I said to the grey morning, "when I get free, I'm going to write a very strongly worded letter."

"You're awake." She sat on a rock ten feet away, whetting a knife she did not need to whet, the way she always sharpened things when she didn't want to look at me. "Good. We've got ground to cover."

"We?"

"Wren's gone ahead." A pause, and her knife stopped moving. That was how I knew. Serah Vance lied well about tolls and borders and the contents of other people's saddlebags, but she lied badly about anything that mattered.

"Gone ahead where," I said, though the cold already crawling up my arms told me.

She wouldn't answer at first. So I made her, the way you make someone confess to a crime they committed for your sake, which is the worst kind of confession to receive and the worst kind to demand.

* * *

Here is what happened while I slept off the blow she'd given me outside Corvane's burning gate: Veyra's scouts had taken Wren. Not killed—taken. The spirit-child had been carried off toward the mountain like a purse of coins, and a message had come down wrapped around an arrow, which is the usurper's idea of correspondence: *The keystone climbs or the child goes into the fire.*

And here is what Serah had done with that information: nothing. She'd read it by torchlight, folded it small, put it in her breast pocket, and decided—for me, without me—that I would still be climbing that mountain either way, so why complicate matters with truth?

"I was going to tell you," she said, on the second day of the ascent, when the trees gave out and the stone began.

"When."

"When it wouldn't stop you."

"And when would that be? The summit?" I rattled the cuffs for emphasis. They were heavier than they needed to be; I suspected that was deliberate, a little sermon in iron. "You knocked me unconscious, chained me, and marched me toward the woman who wants to unmake everything I am, and your plan was to mention the hostage situation eventually."

"It wasn't a plan. It was—" She stopped. The wind came up the slope and pushed her hair across her face, and she let it, which from Serah was a form of hiding. "You were going to walk into their camp. I saw you deciding it. You had the face of someone doing arithmetic with their own life."

"I was going to trade myself for Wren."

"I know what you were going to do. That's why I hit you."

We walked. Below us, far behind, the smoke of Corvane stood up straight in the windless morning like a pillar somebody had forgotten to knock over. Somewhere under that smoke was Dell Maro's library, and Dell Maro himself, alive or not, and every book he'd ever betrayed two governments to protect. I thought about him saying *the self, unmade*, in his dry archivist's voice, the way another man might say *the bill comes due*.

"Serah." I made myself say the next part slowly, because it was the truest thing I owned. "I forgive you."

Her head came around fast. Suspicion first—she wore suspicion the way other knights wore medals—and then something rawer underneath.

"Don't do that," she said. "Don't forgive me before I've earned it. I've watched men do penance that way. It doesn't take."

"I'm not doing penance. I'm telling you what I learned while I was unconscious, which is more than I usually manage awake." I held up the cuffed hands, turning them in the thin mountain light. "You took my choice away because you were afraid. I understand afraid. I spent twenty years being invisible because I was afraid. But here's the thing I've finally worked out, walking uphill in borrowed chains: loyalty without truth isn't loyalty. It's ownership. You kept me safe the way the temple kept me safe. In a box, labeled *do not open until the world ends.*"

She flinched. Good, I thought, and then immediately felt ashamed of thinking it, which is the whole trouble with honesty—it cuts both directions, and you have to stand still and take your own edge.

"The difference," she said at last, quietly, "is that I did it because I can't lose you too."

*Too.* There was a whole buried regiment in that word. I didn't dig. Not yet. Some graves you pass on the way up a mountain and save for the descent, if there is a descent.

"Well," I said instead. "Now we're even. Because I can't lose *you*, and I couldn't lose Wren, and apparently none of us can survive being loved correctly."

She almost smiled. On Serah's face an almost-smile is an event, like a comet—you mark it, and you don't expect another one soon.

"Unlock the cuffs," I said.

"No."

"Serah."

"They stay until the summit. Call it a compromise. You keep your hands, I keep my certainty." She turned back up the trail. "Besides, you'll thank me when the climbing gets bad."

I hated that she was right about the climbing. The Spire of Highmoor does not rise so much as accumulate—a thousand years of switchbacks stacked on each other's failures—and by the third day my chained wrists had become a pendulum I swung myself upward with, and I did, privately, in the dark of my own skull, thank her. Out loud, never. A scribe learns early which debts go in the ledger and which ones you carry.

* * *

On the fourth night we found the shrine.

It was half-swallowed by the mountainside, a doorway with no building left around it, older than the temple, older than the covenant, maybe. The stone above the lintel bore carvings worn to suggestion: figures holding hands, or holding chains—I couldn't tell which, and I suspect the carvers hadn't known either.

Wren would have loved it. That thought arrived like weather, sudden and sideways, and I had to sit down on the threshold and let it pass through me. Four days now since Corvane. Four days of not knowing whether the spirit-child was cold, whether Veyra fed them, whether spirits even ate, whether the bargain-chip arithmetic included keeping them alive once the chips were spent.

"They're alive," Serah said. She'd gotten good at reading me, worse luck. "If Veyra wanted them dead she'd have done it in the city and saved the postage."

"That's comforting in the way that a knife is comfortable."

"It's the only comfort on offer. Take it."

So I took it, and I put my chained hands against the old carved stone, and the stone—

The stone remembered.

It came through my palms like cold water rising: a vision not of the Spire this time but of hands, hundreds of pairs, mortal and spirit alike, laid over this same rock across centuries, each one leaving a thread of itself in the stone the way fingers leave oil in a psalter. The covenant had never been a document. It had been a habit. A thousand years of small touches, small promises, and I understood suddenly why Veyra feared it—not because it bound realms, but because it bound *people*, patiently, in ways no army could cut.

When I came back to myself, Serah had a hand on my shoulder and her sword half-drawn, scanning the dark for whatever she imagined had hurt me.

"It didn't hurt," I said. "That's the frightening part. Everything up here keeps trying to *include* me."

"Including what?"

I told her. All of it—the archive, the prophecy, Dell Maro's decoding, the keystone, the self unmade. I'd given her pieces before, the way you pay a guide in installments. This time I paid in full, sitting in the doorway of a dead shrine with my wrists in her ironwork, because if loyalty without truth is ownership then the reverse must also hold, and I wanted, desperately, to be her ally rather than her cargo.

She listened without interrupting, which is the knight's version of prayer. When I finished she was quiet long enough that I heard the mountain breathing around us.

"So at the top," she said finally, "you're going to give up your name. Your self. Everything. And none of us will remember you."

"That appears to be the price list."

"And you're going to pay it anyway."

"Yes."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for the argument, the counteroffer, some new scheme involving blunt instruments and my skull. Instead she said:

"All right. Then I'm going to remember you wrong on purpose. For as long as I can. Every day I'll invent something new about you and believe it, and maybe memory's like magic, like you keep saying—maybe it finds its way back through the cracks." Her jaw set. "Veyra can unmake you. She can't unmake what I decide happened."

It was the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me, and it was also completely insane, and I laughed until the sound echoed off the mountain and something far below—something with many boots—answered it.

* * *

We saw the fires at dawn on the sixth day.

Not Veyra's. Hers would be orderly, a grid of discipline in the valley mouth. These burned ragged and close together, strung along our backtrail like beads, and Serah went pale in a way I'd only seen once before, at the waystation.

"How far back?" I asked.

"Half a day. Less. Someone's driving them." She was repacking in fast, vicious motions. "Inquisitors don't march at night unless they're being driven."

"Then we don't sleep again."

"We don't sleep again," she agreed, and unlocked one cuff—just one, my right hand, freeing it for the rope and the rock. "Compromise number two."

We climbed. The air thinned until breathing felt like borrowing. The Spire showed itself properly at last through a col in the ridge: a needle of pale stone crowned with the broken ring of the old covenant-circle, catching the sun like a blade held up to swear an oath. One day's climb. Maybe less.

At noon, on a shelf of granite barely wide enough for two, I found the second message.

No arrow this time. It was simply *there*, written in frost on the stone at my feet although the morning sun had stripped all other frost from the mountain, in a hand I recognized with a lurch because I had copied its alphabet for eleven years in the temple archive:

*Come alone, keystone. Bring the knight if you must. Bring your guilt, bring your fear—they weigh nothing to me. But know that the child asks for you by name. Know that the child has begun to fade. Spirits cannot live long in iron cages, and mine is very beautiful.*

Below the words, drawn small and precise, was a picture of the summit ring, and standing in it, a figure with its arms open. Waiting.

"Isandro?" Serah was three steps up, hand extended, offering me the rope. "What is it?"

I looked at her hand. I looked at the frost, already melting in the sun, taking Wren's fading with it.

"Nothing," I lied, gently, for the last time. "Let's climb."
