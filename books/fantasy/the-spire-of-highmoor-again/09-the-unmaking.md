## 9. The Unmaking

The wind at the top of the world has no manners. It shoved me the moment I crested the Spire's last stair, as though it had been waiting a century for someone worth shoving, and I suppose it had—the stone up here was scarred with old lightning, and the air tasted of iron and something sweeter underneath, like rain on hot copper.

Below, Veyra's army moved through the mountain pass like ink poured into water, torches blooming in the dark in their thousands. Above, the sky did something I still don't have proper words for. The stars were there, but behind them—between them—was the faint suggestion of another country, a second night laid over the first, and both were bleeding through each other at the seams.

The covenant's wound. I could see it now the way you can see your own hand without looking: constantly, annoyingly, forever.

"You came the long way," said Veyra.

She stood at the Spire's crown where an altar should have been, alone, her honor guard left at the stairhead as if even she understood that what happened next required no audience—or perhaps that audiences would ruin it. Her armor was plain. Her eyes were not. There is a particular look worn by people who have decided the world is broken and appointed themselves its mender, and she wore it the way other queens wear crowns.

"Serah took the scenic route," I said. "Lots of chains involved."

"I know." Something almost gentle crossed her face. "She loves you. It makes people stupid. It made me stupid once, too."

"Wren."

"Is below. Alive, comfortable, and entirely contingent on your cooperation. You'll forgive the crudeness—I find threats distasteful, so let us call it arithmetic instead." She turned to face the wounded sky. "Do you know what you are yet? Truly?"

"Dell Maro had theories. The Abbot had warnings. I had a very quiet life copying ledgers, which I miss desperately."

"The keystone." She said it the way one says *plague* or *debt*. "When the covenant was forged, a thousand years ago, the two realms needed an anchor—a living point where spirit-law and mortal-law could agree to be the same thing. Your temple didn't hide you out of cruelty, scribe. They hid you because they knew what the old texts demanded of a keystone who woke up."

"And what's that?"

"*Choice.*" The word came out of her like a blade from a sheath. "That's all it ever was. A choice, freely made, by someone with power enough to matter. And here is mine to offer—" she stepped closer, and I felt the Spire itself lean toward her, listening, "—take it. All of it. Not the sacrifice the prophecy demands. The prophecy was written by mortals who feared power; I've read the older strata beneath it. The keystone doesn't have to unmake itself. It can *complete* itself. Take the whole covenant into your own flesh. Become the hinge of both realms. A god, Isandro Okafor. Spirits kneeling, armies dissolving at a word, death itself negotiating terms with you. Rule both worlds—and rule them *well*, which is more than any of the fools below have managed."

I looked at her hands. They weren't shaking. That frightened me more than the army did.

"You believe this," I said. "You actually believe you're offering me mercy."

"I'm offering the world stability. A broken covenant lets spirits meddle in mortal affairs—kings haunted, harvests cursed, wars whispered into ears by things that don't die when the war does. I broke nothing; I only refused to let it be reforged on the old terms. But you—" her voice softened, horribly sincere, "—you could hold both realms in one just mind. Mine would be a tyranny. Yours could be a stewardship. Say yes, and I will kneel to you myself, gladly, before my own army."

And here is the terrible confession of my life: for three full breaths, I wanted it.

Not the power. Power had never interested me; I'd spent twenty years perfecting invisibility. What I wanted was the *end of wanting*. No more debt, no more flight, no more friends turned jailers out of love, no more name shouted across battlefields by people who wanted to use or kill or save me. A god wants nothing because a god lacks nothing. Veyra wasn't offering me a throne. She was offering me rest.

I thought of Serah's face when she knocked me unconscious—love wearing theft's clothing. I thought of Wren's small cold hand in mine at the threshold ruin. I thought of Dell Maro saying, *the self, unmade,* and how I'd laughed, because I'd spent my whole life being told I was nobody anyway, and apparently the universe had been taking notes.

"No," I said.

Veyra's expression didn't change, but the mountain did—a tremor running up through the ancient stone, the sky-wound widening a finger's width, stars sliding against stars.

"Scribe—"

"You're right about one thing. A single just mind holding both realms *would* be better than a thousand squabbling kings." I walked past her to the altar-stone, where a shallow basin held rainwater older than kingdoms. "But I've read the ledger too, Veyra. Every covenant is a contract, and every contract has a consideration. Yours asks the world to trust one person to stay good forever. Nobody stays good forever. I couldn't even stay *found* for twenty years." I set my palms in the basin. "The prophecy's price isn't cruelty. It's honesty. The realms can't be joined by a ruler standing between them. They have to be joined by a door that stops pretending to be anything at all."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying you can keep your god. I'll take the other option."

I began the unbinding then—not with grand words, because the archive had taught me that the oldest magic is bookkeeping. I spoke the entries of my own life back into the stone. *Isandro Okafor, bought by the temple at six years, debt of forty marks. Scribe, third class. Copied eleven thousand pages. Liked plum wine, badly. Laughed at his own jokes.* Every fact a thread. Every thread I pulled loose and offered down into the Spire's heart, and the Spire took them with something like hunger and something like grief.

Veyra understood one heartbeat too late. "Stop—you don't know what you're—"

"I know exactly." My hands were glowing now, or maybe fading; from the inside it felt the same. "This is the part you never planned for, Queen. You thought the choice was between your order and the old prophecy. But the old prophecy was always going to win, because it asked for the only payment that costs the payer everything. You wanted a king above the covenant. The world needs a *doorway* instead. Doors don't rule. Doors don't remember being shut."

The first thing to go was small: the taste of plum wine. Then my mother's face—I never knew her, and suddenly I knew that I had never known her *on purpose*, the absence itself unraveling. Then Serah's voice saying *hold still, you idiot* in a hundred different tones of exasperation and care, thinning like smoke. I grabbed at it. I confess I grabbed. But the covenant does not accept partial payments, and love, it turns out, is the most expensive entry in anyone's ledger.

Wren's laugh went last, and I spent my final human strength refusing to let go of it, and lost, and was glad, somehow, even losing, that I had tried.

Far below, horns sounded. Veyra's army had reached the summit stair. She stood beside me, and I saw—this I got to keep until the very end—that she was weeping, furious at her own tears, a queen undone not by defeat but by watching someone decline her.

"Why?" she whispered. "Why refuse?"

"Because someone once wrote my name in a book," I said, and my voice was already becoming wind, already becoming the seam between two skies, "and it deserved a better ending than a throne."

The realms closed over me like water closing over a stone dropped from a bridge. Above, the double night knit itself whole. Below, ten thousand soldiers reached the summit and found no god, no queen's triumph, no savior—only an empty altar and a silence so complete that grown men sat down in the snow and wept without knowing why.

Somewhere in the settling dark, Veyra lowered her sword, and the war ended not with surrender but with forgetting what it had been for.

And far below, in the camp at the mountain's foot, a small spirit-child lifted their head as if hearing a bell that hadn't rung, and said—to no one, to everyone—a word that was not quite a word:

*"Oka—for."*

Then frowned, having no idea why.

* * *

Serah Vance stood at the edge of the summit snow, sword point-down, breathing hard from a climb she barely remembered deciding to make. Around her, soldiers wandered dazed among the stones, and Queen Veyra stared at the empty altar with the expression of a woman reading a letter she cannot recall receiving.

Serah looked at the altar too. Something ached in her chest—a bruise with no cause, a grief with no name, the specific weight of a word sitting on the tip of the tongue and refusing to fall off.

In her pack, pressed against her spine, was a worn journal with ink-stained covers that she had no memory of buying, filled with handwriting she almost recognized, telling a story she almost remembered living.

"Whoever you were," she said aloud, to the empty air, to the whole knitted sky, "you'd better come back for this. I don't lend books."

The wind answered. It sounded, very faintly, like laughter.
