## 10. A Name Like Morning

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a war, and I have learned to hate it the way other people hate the sound of their own name spoken by a stranger.

I used to think silence was just the absence of noise. Then I spent a season of my life listening for hoofbeats that meant inquisitors, and I learned that silence can be a held breath, a waiting thing, a wolf crouched at the treeline. Now, months after the Spire, silence has changed again. It's just quiet. Ordinary. The kind you get in a city square on a market morning, when the loudest sound is a fishmonger arguing about the price of eels.

I don't trust it yet. But I'm learning.

Corvane rebuilt faster than anyone predicted. Cities do that—like dogs, like soldiers, like anything that has learned not to expect permanence. The breach in the eastern wall where Veyra's siege engines had pounded for three days is now a garden, because some councilwoman with more sentiment than sense decided rubble made good soil. The spirits walk through it openly now. That still catches me sideways sometimes: a river-thing with a face like wet slate standing at a fruit stall, haggling politely, its voice sounding like water over stones. Nobody stares. Children chase wisps through the gutters the way they used to chase cats.

A century ago this would have been heresy. A year ago it would have been treason. Now it's Tuesday.

Veyra's army dissolved the way frost does—not defeated so much as warmed through. When the covenant reforged and the Spire lit from crown to root like a lamp being relit, half her soldiers reportedly wept. The other half reportedly went home. She herself... no one agrees. Some say she knelt at the summit. Some say she walked into the spirit-realm and did not come back. Some say she's living quietly under another name in a fishing village, which I confess I find the most unsettling possibility, because it would mean the world let her.

The free cities needed something to honor. That's what cities do after—they need a statue, a holiday, a name they can carve into stone so the grief has somewhere to stand. And there *was* someone to honor. Everyone agreed on that. The savior of Corvane, the keystone reborn, the one who climbed the Spire when armies couldn't, who refused a godhood that was genuinely offered—I know it was offered; I carried them up that mountain in chains I forged myself, and I heard what waited at the top.

But here is the trouble, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to admit it out loud:

No one could remember the savior's name.

Not vaguely. Not the way you forget a face but keep the shape of it. *At all.* The archivists found records—a travel writ, temple ledgers, Dell Maro's frantic marginalia—and every place a name should have been, there was a smoothness. Ink that hadn't faded so much as *healed*, like skin closing over a wound. Dell Maro wept over his own handwriting. He'd written pages about the scribe. He could read everything around the name—the debt, the valley, the prophecy—but the name itself slid off his eyes like rain off oiled leather.

They carved the monument anyway. "TO THE NAMELESS ONE," it says, in letters big enough to read from the ramparts. Underneath, smaller: "WHO GAVE WHAT CANNOT BE RETURNED."

I stood in front of it for an hour the day they unveiled it, arms crossed, telling myself I was being appropriately unsentimental. A woman next to me asked if I'd known them. I said yes. She asked what they were like. And I opened my mouth and discovered I had a hole where the answer should be.

I knew there had been someone. I knew I had loved them—that word sat in me like a splinter too deep to dig out, all feeling and no shape. I knew I had knocked them unconscious in a burning city rather than let them surrender themselves, and that they had forgiven me for it on a mountainside, which was somehow worse than anger would have been. Loyalty without truth is just ownership, they told me, and then they unmade themselves to save us all, and I have been turning that sentence over ever since like a stone looking for what's underneath.

But their face? Their voice? The way they said my name?

Gone. Smooth as the healed ink.

What remains is a journal. I've never told anyone this, but I woke up with it the morning after the Spire, tucked inside my bedroll like contraband. A scribe's journal—worn leather, swollen with damp, corners soft as old bread. Every page covered in a small, precise hand. Ink-stains on the cover shaped like nothing, like accidents, like a life.

I cannot explain owning it. I have tried. I went back through my memory of the ascent, the ruin, the waystation fire, and there is no moment when they handed it to me. There is no moment when I took it. And yet here it sits in my pack, traveling with me from city to city like a debt I agreed to without signing anything.

Some nights I read it. Most nights I don't, because reading it feels like eavesdropping on a ghost, and ghosts deserve better than that. But the entries are there, in a hand I almost recognize, describing things I remember and things I don't: the smell of the tannery district, the Abbot's warnings, the first time they touched Wren and saw the Spire. In the margins, once, in different ink, someone wrote: *"If you are reading this, I am sorry. And thank you."*

No signature. Of course no signature.

* * *

Wren lives with me now, which surprises exactly no one who met us both.

The spirit-child aged a little, these past months—or learned to look older, which may be the same thing for a creature of thresholds. They still have that unnerving quality of being slightly more *here* than everything else, as if the world were a painting and Wren had stepped out of the frame. They eat normal food now, mostly. They still refuse, on principle, to sleep indoors when the moon is full, and I have stopped fighting it. You pick your battles with children, whether they're mortal or not.

We share a small house near the eastern wall garden. Wren tends the flowers that grow in the siege-breach soil—spirits apparently find the ground there interesting, "sweet with endings," they said, which is either poetry or a warning and I've given up asking which.

People ask Wren questions constantly. About the Spire. About the covenant. About the Nameless One. Wren answers patiently, kindly, and always the same way: *"They gave their self so ours could stay whole. Remembering them is our work now."*

It's a lovely answer. It's also, I suspect, a deflection, because last month Wren said something else, and I haven't been able to put it down since.

We were walking home from the market. Dusk, the hour when the streetlamps catch and the spirits go luminous at their edges. Wren stopped in the middle of the square, in front of the fountain, and went very still—the way they do when they're listening to something on the far side of the air.

Then they spoke a single sound.

It wasn't a word. I want to be clear about that. It wasn't any language I know, or any language the scholars Dell Maro dragged out to examine it know either. It was two syllables, soft, rising at the end like a question, like a name being tried on for size. Wren said it once, looked around as if checking whether anyone caught it, and then said, brightly, in their ordinary voice:

"That was nothing. Don't worry."

I worried immediately, obviously. I'm a smuggler by trade; trusting people is against my religion.

Dell Maro brought out every text he owns—which is a horrifying number of texts—and found nothing. But an old spirit-woman at the river shrine, when Wren repeated the sound for her, went pale in whatever way spirits go pale, and said only: "That is how names sound before they exist. Or after."

Before they exist. Or after.

I've been chewing on that for weeks. Because here is the thing I finally understand, standing in front of a monument to someone whose name I carried in chains and lost in fire: memory, like magic, finds its way back. The covenant didn't erase the scribe out of cruelty. It wove them into the loom itself. You can't carve a keystone into a wall. It has to *be* the wall. And maybe a name woven into the foundation of two realms doesn't die—it just takes longer than a season to surface. Maybe it rises the way water rises through stone. Slowly. Inevitably. In places nobody thought to watch.

Maybe it comes back the way morning does. Not all at once. Just—light arriving at the edges first.

* * *

So: the stranger.

This happened four days ago, and I have been carrying it around like a hot coal, telling no one except Wren, who nodded as if they'd been expecting it, which was somehow the most frightening part.

I was in the border town of Ashford-on-the-Thresh, running a legitimate shipment for once—grain, boring grain, the kind of cargo that doesn't require lying to anyone. I stopped at a tavern called the Bent Nail because it was raining and I am weak. And while I was eating, a young man came in out of the weather. Ordinary in every way: mud-spattered traveler, tired eyes, a pack that had seen better roads. He ordered stew. He sat alone.

And when the innkeeper's daughter dropped a stack of bowls behind him—the crash, the shouting, the ordinary chaos of a tavern evening—he flinched, and looked up, and said a word.

Two syllables. Soft. Rising at the end like a question.

The same sound Wren spoke in the square. I felt it land in my chest like a hook set in old meat. I knew it instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall—can't place it, can't prove it, but *know* it.

The stranger froze, hand halfway to his spoon, staring at nothing. Then he said, to no one, in a voice full of genuine confusion:

"Sorry—I don't know why I said that. It sounded like—" He stopped. Laughed at himself, embarrassed. "It sounded like someone calling me. Like hearing my own name for the first time."

He ate his stew. He paid. He left into the rain, walking, I noticed, not toward the road south but toward the mountains. Toward Highmoor. Toward the Spire, though I doubt he knows that's where his feet are taking him.

I sat in that tavern until the lamps burned low, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. A spirit-child speaks a name-that-isn't-a-name in a Corvane square. Months later, a stranger says it aloud in a border tavern, and calls it his own. The healed ink in the archives. The journal I can't explain. The monument with the blank where a person should be.

Either the world is playing an elaborate joke on me—and I'll grant the world has the sense of humor for it—or something is coming back. Someone is coming back. Not whole, maybe. Not remembered. But surfacing, the way the dead surface in spring rivers, gently, inevitably, into light they don't recognize yet.

I finished my drink. I walked out into the rain. And then, because I am who I am—a smuggler, a liar, a woman who once forged chains to hold someone she loved and has regretted it every day since—I turned around, saddled my horse, and followed him toward the mountains.

Don't ask me what I plan to say if I catch him. I don't know. Maybe nothing. Maybe I'll just ride beside him for a while, the way you ride beside someone you're not ready to speak to, and see if the road remembers what I can't.

But I have the journal in my saddlebag. And I have Wren's word-that-isn't-a-word written on a scrap of paper in my breast pocket, in case I need to give it back to someone.

And I have this much faith left, which is more than I started with: that a name, like morning, always comes back.

You just have to be awake to see it arrive.
