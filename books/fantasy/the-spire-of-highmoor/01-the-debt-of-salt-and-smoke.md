## 1. The Debt of Salt and Smoke

The debt was eleven crowns, three silver bits, and a chicken.

Mara Vasquez had counted it a hundred times on the road out of Duskmere, because there was nothing else to count. Eleven crowns she'd borrowed from Master Odric to buy herbs when her own garden rotted in the wet spring. Three silver bits for the tincture that hadn't saved the cooper's daughter, which was the debt that actually weighed, though no ledger recorded it. And the chicken — well. The chicken was Odric being Odric.

"You'll note," he'd said, tapping the ledger with one liver-spotted finger, "that the hen was laying. That makes it a *producing* asset, girl. Debts compound on producing assets."

"It was a *chicken*, Master."

"A producing chicken." He'd smiled with all the warmth of a damp cellar. "You have until the first frost."

So she'd left before the first frost, which solved nothing except the immediate problem of being in the same valley as Master Odric, and created several larger problems involving mountains.

The Thornfells did not welcome travelers so much as tolerate them, briefly, before attempting to kill them. The smuggler roads — goat paths, honestly, widened here and there by people who preferred not to be seen — wound up through pine and scree and fog that came down like a lid. Mara had been walking for four days. Her boots had been good boots once. She tried not to think about them too directly, the way you try not to look at a wound.

What she carried: a satchel of herbs, a knife better suited to chopping parsley than anything else, seven coppers, and a jar of honey she refused to eat because eating it would mean admitting the journey might be long. What she knew: that Odric's reach ended at the valley's mouth, but that debts were like seeds — they traveled in ways you didn't expect. What she hoped: nothing specific. Hope required a plan, and her plan was currently *up*.

On the fourth evening the fog closed early and the path forked where no map she'd stolen — borrowed, she corrected herself; everything about her relationship with property was under renegotiation — said it should. She stood in the grey until her teeth chattered, then chose left, because left felt like the sort of choice a person made who still believed things worked out.

The path delivered her, an hour later, to a shrine.

It had been a shrine once, anyway. A low stone building hunched against the mountainside, roof mostly gone, walls furred with moss. Someone had cared for it long ago — there were old iron hooks by the door where pilgrims' offerings had hung, and the threshold stone was worn into a shallow smile by centuries of feet. Inside, a single alcove held the stump of a statue. Whatever god it had depicted was long since faceless; rain and time had done their patient work, leaving only a suggestion of shoulders and one hand, palm up, as if waiting to receive something or return it.

Mara stood in the doorway and felt something in her chest go strange and tight.

She hadn't thought about shrines in years. Hedge-witches weren't supposed to. Odric had opinions on the subject, delivered in his driest voice: *The gods are dead, girl, or sleeping, or never existed, depending on which scholar you ask, and in none of those cases do they pay debts.* He'd kept a little iron bell on his shelf, supposedly from a temple, and used it to call her from the garden, which she had privately always considered a blasphemy of the pettiest kind.

But her master was two valleys away with his ledger and his producing assets, and the fog was coming down like a held breath, and the shrine had walls enough to cut the wind.

"Nobody lives here?" she asked aloud, feeling foolish. "Good. I'm claiming it. Eviction notices available upon request."

The shrine said nothing. Shrines were good at that.

* * *

She made camp in the corner driest of corners, ate half the bread and stared at the honey jar without opening it, and got a small fire going with the last of her dry tinder. The flame threw shapes up the wall, and the faceless statue's raised hand caught the light and seemed almost — almost — to be offering her something.

"Don't," she told it. "I've seen how gods treat people. From a distance. In stories. It always ends with someone turned into a tree or a stag or a lesson."

Still nothing. But the fire popped, and for a moment the sound was almost like laughter, and Mara decided firmly that it wasn't.

She should have slept. Instead she found herself doing inventory again, but the other kind — the kind she avoided in daylight. Eleven crowns. Three bits. One chicken. One cooper's daughter whose name had been Sella and who had liked to whittle little boats and who had died holding Mara's hand while the tincture sat useless in her veins. One master who had looked at the body, looked at the ledger, and said, "We'll need to adjust the account for the unused dose."

Four days of mountain silence had scraped her thin. There is only so long a person can carry a thing alone before they set it down somewhere, anywhere, even in front of a faceless statue in a ruined shrine, even knowing no one is listening.

"I don't suppose," Mara said to the fire, very quietly, "that anyone's home."

The wind moved through the broken roof. She pressed on, faster now, words tumbling like stones down scree:

"Because I could use someone. Anyone. I'm not picky. I never prayed as a child because Mam said the gods took payment in kinds we couldn't afford, and I believed her, and then Odric said they were dead and I believed him too because it was easier. And now I'm sitting in one of your — your houses — talking to a rock with shoulders, and I don't even know what I'd ask for. Not money. Not even forgiveness for Sella, though — if you're taking requests —"

Her voice cracked. She hated that. She hated more that there was no one to see it.

"I just want to not be running," she finished, barely above a whisper. "That's all. That's the whole prayer. Somebody hear it, or don't, but I said it, and I meant it, and I'm going to sleep now."

The fire burned. The fog pressed at the broken roof. The statue kept its open palm.

Mara wrapped her cloak around herself and lay down, and it was in the borderland between waking and sleep that she heard it — or felt it first, a vibration through the floor stone, faint as a heartbeat in another room. Then the firelight bent. Not flickered — *bent*, leaning toward the alcove like wheat toward wind.

Every hair on her arms rose.

*You spoke.*

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere and most of all from inside her own skull, and it was not a whisper and it was not a wind. It was bronze — struck bronze, deep and resonant, the sound a great bell makes a moment after it stops ringing, still filling the air. It was a voice that had been waiting a long time to be used.

Mara sat up so fast she cracked her head on a beam. She didn't notice. She was staring at the statue, at the empty palm, at the firelight bending.

"No," she said. "No, no. I was talking to myself. People do that. It's a whole — it's very common—"

*You spoke,* the voice said again, and this time it sounded almost wondering, almost gentle, the way a giant might be gentle. *Three hundred years, and the first voice to fall on my ear is yours.*

"I take it back." Mara was on her feet now, backing toward the door, satchel clutched like a shield. "Whatever I said, I take it back. Un-hear it. I'm sure you can, you're — whatever you are —"

*I am what remains,* the voice said. *And you are the one who asked.*

"That was a mistake! A clerical error! I was tired!"

The presence moved — she felt it turn its attention on her fully, and it was like standing in the first ray of sun after a week of fog: unbearable and warm and utterly impossible to hide from. It saw the debt and the chicken. It saw Sella's hand in hers. It saw every lie she'd ever told, neatly sorted, and worse, it saw every truth, and there was no drawer in her she could shut.

*Mara Vasquez,* the voice said, and her own name in that bronze depth sounded like a verdict, like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was on her. *Hedge-born. Debt-bound. Keeper of a dead child's boats. You prayed, and I answered, and oaths do not unmake themselves.*

"What oath? I didn't swear anything! I said I *meant it*, that's not—"

*It is exactly that,* said Veyra, goddess of oaths, though Mara did not yet know her name, and the last thing Mara heard as she bolted out the door into the freezing fog was the bell-sound of something enormous and ancient settling around her soul like a mantle —

*Be seen, Mara Vasquez. You will not hide from me again.*
