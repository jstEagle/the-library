## 2. What the Mountain Answered

Mara ran until the trees stopped being trees and became a gray blur of needle and shadow, until her lungs burned like she'd swallowed the shrine's coals, until the only sound left in the world was her own heartbeat and the terrible, patient silence behind it.

She stopped at a stream because her body demanded it, not because her mind agreed. She knelt on the frost-stiffened bank, cupped water in shaking hands, and looked down.

Her reflection looked back.

That was the problem. Reflections were supposed to look *away*, in a manner of speaking — to be nothing but light and habit, a copy that did what it was told. But hers was watching her. Her reflection's eyes were too still. Its mouth curved, very slightly, at one corner, while Mara's own mouth was busy doing something considerably less dignified, which was trembling.

"You're running," said her reflection.

The voice came from inside her own skull and from the water at once, layered, resonant, like bronze struck over a deep bell. It did not echo off the mountains. The mountains echoed off *it*.

"I'm — " Mara's voice cracked. She tried again. "I'm washing my face."

"Liar." The reflection tilted its head, and the tilt was wrong, graceful in a way Mara's neck had never managed. "You swore you would tell me true things, little thief of prayers. You swore it by your breath, your name, and your need. Do you remember?"

Mara remembered. Last night, huddled in a ruined shrine with debt-collectors' torches somewhere below and nothing between her and the dark but cold stone, she had spoken aloud for the first time in six years — a ragged, furious prayer, half begging and half bargaining. And something had answered, and in her terror she had babbled promises, and apparently one of them had *stuck*.

"I didn't know what I was swearing to," she said.

"Few do. That is why oaths are sacred and also why so many people are in trouble." The reflection's smile widened a fraction. "I am Veyra. Goddess of oaths, keeper of promises, witness of vows made in darkness and daylight both. You called. I answered. We are bound."

"We are *not* — "

"You swore by your name," Veyra said, gentle as an avalanche beginning. "Mara Vasquez. Daughter of no one, apprentice of a hedge-witch who sold your services to men who break knees. You swore by your breath, which is still in your body. You swore by your need, which is considerable. I accepted. The binding is old law, older than your Spire, older than these mountains. It is, I am told, quite unbreakable."

Mara sat back hard on the frozen bank, water dripping from her fingers, and laughed — a thin, hysterical sound that startled a bird out of the pines.

"Of course," she said. "Of course this is my life. I finally pray, after years of keeping my mouth shut, and I get *indentured* to a god."

"Now you understand religion," said Veyra.

* * *

Mara had spent six years learning how not to be noticed. Her master Aldric had taught her the first lesson free: *the world takes what sees itself*. So she'd learned to walk close to walls, to keep her eyes down, to give small answers to big questions. She had hidden from debt-collectors, from tax-men, from Aldric's increasingly creative creditors, and once, memorably, from a bear.

She had never before tried to hide from her own reflection.

It was impossible, as it turned out. Pools, streams, the frost-glaze on a boulder, even the dark shine of her own knife blade — wherever there was light enough to catch her, there Veyra was, watching with an expression of vast, amused patience.

"You could stop looking at water," the goddess observed, as Mara detoured around a perfectly good spring.

"I could push *you* into a spring."

"That would require pushing yourself. There is a metaphor there, but I will let you find it in your own time."

"They sealed you away," Mara snapped, scrambling up a scree slope, gravel skittering under her boots. "Three hundred years ago. The Spire locked every god behind some kind of — of seal, and they burned anyone who ever heard one of you speak. If they find out what's riding around in my eyeballs, they'll burn *me*."

"Yes," Veyra said, thoughtful. "They will. They have done it eleven thousand four hundred times, by my last count before the sealing. I kept the tally myself, for a while. Oaths were involved."

Mara stopped climbing. Her stomach went cold in a way the mountain wind couldn't account for.

"Eleven thousand," she whispered.

"The Spire calls it the Cleansing of Voices. I call it what it was." For the first time, something moved through the reflection that wasn't amusement — something old and heavy, like a door swinging open onto a very deep cellar. "But that grief is not yours to carry yet, little oath-keeper. Right now your problem is smaller: you cannot outrun me, and you are about to run directly into five men who would very much like to burn you."

"What?"

"Behind you. Down the trail. Red-and-white tabards, silver pins shaped like a tower. They've been following your noise for an hour. You run," Veyra observed, "like a cart with one wheel."

Mara spun. Below, through the pines, she could see them: five figures in the Spire's colors moving up the smuggler's track with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where their quarry was going. One carried a censer, and even at this distance the smoke smelled wrong — metallic, hungry.

"Inquisitors," Veyra murmured. "God-hunters. Interesting. They do not hunt hedge-witches' apprentices. They hunt *voices*. Which means someone has already felt me waking inside you, or they would not bother."

"Can you help?" Mara hissed. "You're a *goddess*."

"I am a goddess bound in a frightened woman's reflection, three centuries starved, currently operating at approximately the strength of a strong opinion. Also, using my power makes you glow. Glowing is, historically, bad for god-speakers."

"So we hide."

"So you hide. Badly. Behind that split rock, quickly now — "

Mara threw herself behind a shelf of granite as the patrol crested the ridge. She pressed flat against cold stone, heart hammering, and listened to boots crunch closer. Closer. A voice, clipped and Spire-trained: *"Scent of ash here. Fresh prayer-residue. She's near."*

And then the worst thing happened, which was that her knife slid from its sheath and rang against the rock like a dinner bell.

"There," said the voice.

Mara ran.

She didn't decide to; her legs simply voted without her. She crashed downslope through pine and bramble, branches whipping her face, the patrol shouting behind her, and somewhere in the chaos Veyra's voice cut through, sharp and clear:

"*Left.* Not left-left, your other left — Mara Vasquez, sworn to honesty, if you survive this I am going to teach you basic spatial awareness as our first act together — "

Then the ground ended.

Mara had time to notice that the smuggler's map in her head had lied about the cliff edge, and rather less time to do anything about it. She pitched forward into open air, grabbed a root, and found herself dangling over a ravine with the patrol closing in above and a long, rocky drop below.

"Well," said Veyra, from the stream pool ten feet below her. "This is a fine mess you have sworn us into."

"Any ideas?" Mara gasped, arms burning.

"One. It involves glowing."

"Do it."

Light erupted up her arm like dawn poured into her veins — gold-white, searing, beautiful, and absolutely unmistakable. Every hair on her body stood on end. The root held, strengthened by something older than rope and iron, and the inquisitors above her recoiled with shouts of *"Divine manifestation! Voice-bearer!"* — and then, worse, one of them began to sing. A flat, grinding chant, and where it touched the air, Mara's golden light guttered like a candle in wind.

Pain lanced through her skull. Her grip slipped.

And then a man came out of the treeline like winter coming down a hill.

He was tall, gray-cloaked, moving with a soldier's economy, and he hit the chanting inquisitor from the side with his shoulder. The song broke. The man rolled, came up with a sword already drawn, and put himself between Mara and all five Spire men without a single wasted motion.

"Gentlemen," he said. His voice was quiet, rough-edged, tired. "That woman is under Thornfell Hollow's protection. Take it up with the smugglers' council, if you enjoy paperwork and missing fingers."

"Ser Kaelen." The lead inquisitor's lip curled. "The traitor of the Third Vigil. Come to die beside a voice-bearer? How fitting. The Spire remembers you."

"The Spire remembers everyone. That's its whole problem." He didn't move. "Walk away."

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then the inquisitor raised two fingers, and the patrol withdrew — slowly, backward, eyes never leaving Mara's fading glow. At the ridgeline, the leader paused. "We'll report the bearer, traitor. Reinforcements come faster than smugglers."

"They usually do," Kaelen agreed pleasantly, and waited until they were gone.

Mara hauled herself up over the cliff edge and collapsed on solid ground, shaking, her arm still faintly luminous at the veins. She looked up at her rescuer. Up close he was younger than his bearing suggested — thirty perhaps, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and the hollow-cheeked look of a man who hadn't slept properly in years. His sword hand trembled, very slightly. Not fear. Something else.

"You're glowing," he said.

"I'm told that's bad."

"It's catastrophic." He crouched, studying her with an intensity that made her want to crawl out of her own skin. "Golden-white, arterial pattern, rises from the blood rather than the breath. That's oath-fire. That's *Veyra's* fire." His jaw tightened. "Three hundred years since anyone burned like that. Three hundred years, and it happens to the first person I've met worth saving."

"How do you know what oath-fire looks like?"

Something closed behind his eyes, a door slamming on a room full of smoke and screaming.

"Because I stood in a cell and watched them burn a girl who glowed just like you," he said quietly, "and I was too much of a coward to stop it. So now you know what I am, voice-bearer. A knight of the Spire, cast out for asking questions. And you—" he straightened, offering her a hand up, his expression grim and utterly unsentimental— "are the most dangerous thing on this mountain. Which means we can help each other, or we can each die alone. I've done alone. I don't recommend it."

Mara stared at the offered hand. In the black ice of a nearby puddle, Veyra's reflection smiled that slow, knowing smile.

"He speaks truth," the goddess murmured. "Every word. I would know."

"Of course you would," Mara muttered, and took Kaelen's hand.

His eyebrows rose at the muttering. "Talking to yourself?"

"Negotiating," said Mara. "There's a goddess in my reflections and she's very chatty."

Kaelen went very still. Then, to her surprise, he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter.

"A goddess," he repeated. "In the *reflections*."

"She says it's old law. She says I swore an accidental vow and now we're stuck together."

"Oh, that's—" He rubbed his scarred eyebrow, visibly wrestling with several decades of doctrine at once. "That's the Binding Rite. First-form. It's in the oldest texts, the ones the Spire keeps locked away because they prove the gods can still *answer*." He looked at her, and beneath the exhaustion and the guilt there was a fierce, hungry light. "Do you understand what this means? If Veyra woke for *you*, the others may wake too. Everything the Spire built its power on — everything they've been murdering people to protect for three centuries — "

"Is a lie?" Mara finished.

"Is a lie," Kaelen said. "And I have spent seven years with no proof and no allies and a name that means traitor." He glanced back down the trail, toward Highmoor's distant white towers, then at Mara's still-faintly-glowing arm. "Reinforcements are coming. We need shelter, and I know exactly one place on this mountain where the Spire's writ runs thin. Smugglers. Criminals. The finest people I know."

"And what do they get out of hiding me?"

Kaelen's mouth twitched. "Blackmail, mostly. I have very compromising knowledge of their ledgers." He shouldered his pack and nodded upslope, into the deep blue shadows of Thornfell. "Come on, voice-bearer. Let me introduce you to the only family I have left."

Behind them, in a melting patch of frost, Veyra's reflection lingered a moment longer than physics allowed — watching the two of them climb, with the expression of someone who has just seen the first line of a very long story.

"Run well, little oath-keeper," she said softly, to no one who could hear. "The mountain is listening. And so, soon, will everything else."
