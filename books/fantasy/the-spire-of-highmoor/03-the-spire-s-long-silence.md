## 3. The Spire's Long Silence

Kaelen had been a knight of the Spire of Highmoor for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one thing that mattered: silence was load-bearing.

The Spire stood on silence the way a house stands on its foundation. Silence in the corridors. Silence at prayer. Silence most especially when an inquisitor walked past with white gloves and a ledger, and you counted your breaths until the sound of their boots faded, because a man who spoke to an inquisitor uninvited might be asked a question he could not answer safely.

He had carried his silence out of Highmoor in a satchel, along with his stripped-off rank-cord and the letter they had made him sign before the gates closed behind him — *I, Kaelen Vos, do confess to the crime of seditious doubt* — and he had kept it for six months in the low country, drinking in taverns where nobody knew his face and telling himself that doubt was a disease he could starve out.

Then he had seen fire come off a girl's hands in the shape of a word, and every wall he'd built came down at once.

She was asleep now, or pretending to be, curled in the corner of Ferro's storeroom with her back against a barrel of smuggled brandy, her cloak pulled up to her chin. The divine light had gone out of her hours ago. What remained was a thin young woman with chapped hands and the hunted look of something that had been running since before Kaelen ever met it.

"You're staring," Bram said.

Kaelen turned. Bram Thorne was leaning in the doorway with a bowl of stew in each hand, which in Thornfell's Hollow constituted a formal welcome.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous habit. That's how my sister got exiled from three villages." He handed over one bowl. "Ferro says you eat first and explain after. His house, his rules. Isolde says you explain first and eat never, if your explanation's bad enough. I'm the reasonable one, so I say do both at once."

Kaelen took the bowl. It was venison and barley and too much salt, and it was the best thing he had tasted since the Spire's kitchens, which said less about the stew than about the Spire's kitchens.

* * *

They gathered around the single lamp in Ferro's kitchen: Ferro himself, broad as a door and gray as winter, whittling a piece of pine into nothing in particular; Isolde, sharp-eyed and sharper-tongued, arms crossed; Bram, already on his second bowl; and Mara Vasquez, who sat as close to the wall as furniture allowed and watched them all like a woman counting exits.

"So," Isolde said. "You're going to tell us why the Spire's finest is dragging a burning girl through my mountains, or you're going to tell Ferro, who will be much less patient, and I'll get to watch."

"There's no gentle way to put it," Kaelen said. "So I'll put it plainly. She prayed, and something answered. A god answered. And if the Spire learns she exists, they will kill her."

Silence. Even Bram stopped chewing.

"They *what*?" Mara said quietly.

Kaelen looked at her. He had rehearsed this speech on the mountain trail, all the careful diplomatic phrasings, and looking at her face now he abandoned every one of them. She deserved plainness. Plainness was the only coin he had left.

"The Spire has executed god-speakers for three hundred years," he said. "Every recorded case. Men, women, children old enough to speak. The doctrine calls it 'quarantine of the divine contagion.' The inquisitors call it culling. There's a chamber beneath the Spire where the ledgers are kept, and I have read them. Four hundred and eleven names in the current volume alone."

"That's not possible," Isolde said. But her voice had gone flat, and Kaelen recognized the sound. It was the voice of someone checking a horror against something they already knew.

"My grandmother's village," she said. "Ashford's Cross. They said fever took it. Forty people. My mother's whole family." Her arms tightened across her chest. "There was no fever, was there."

"I don't know," Kaelen said, and hated how little it was worth. "But Ashford's Cross appears in a ledger entry dated nineteen years ago. One line. 'Contamination contained.'"

Isolde stood up, walked to the window, and stood there with her back to all of them for a long time. Bram reached out and moved her abandoned stew closer to the lamp, as though keeping it warm were the only useful thing anyone could do.

"Why?" Mara asked. "Why kill them? If gods are waking anyway—"

"Because the god-speakers are proof the seal is failing," Kaelen said. "And the seal is the Spire's whole claim to power. Three hundred years ago, the founders ended what the histories call the Divine War. Gods against gods, we're taught. Catastrophe. The founders sealed the survivors away to save mankind, and the Spire has guarded that seal ever since, and every tithe and tax and conscription in nine provinces rests on the idea that without the Spire, the old horrors come back."

"And you didn't believe it," Ferro said. First words he'd spoken. He said them to his whittling, not unkindly.

"No," Kaelen admitted. "I didn't. Eleven years I didn't quite believe it, and I told myself doubt was normal, doubt was even healthy, the Spire encourages examination—" He stopped. Laughed once, without any humor in it. "No. Let me tell it honestly, since honesty is apparently the price of stew. Two years ago, an inquisitorial team brought in a boy from the river valleys. Sixteen years old. He'd healed a drowning child, that was all. Pulled her out of the water dead and prayed over her until she wasn't. And I stood guard outside the chamber while they questioned him, and through the door I heard him saying, over and over, *it wasn't me, it was something kind, it only wanted to help* — and then I heard the silence start, and the silence did not stop."

Bram had gone very still. "They killed him."

"They killed him. And I filed the report confirming the execution was conducted according to doctrine. My signature is in that ledger too." He looked down at his hands. "A month later I asked my commander why a healing needed to be answered with a pyre. That was the whole of my crime. One question. They took my rank, gave me that letter to sign, and put me out the gates. Seditious doubt. I signed it, because the alternative was finding out what happened to knights who refused, and I was a coward."

"You were alive," Ferro said. "Different thing entirely. Cowardice is what brave men call survival when they're feeling generous."

"Ferro's philosophy," Bram stage-whispered, "is why we've survived twenty years of smuggling with all original limbs attached."

But Kaelen shook his head. "I told myself I'd been cast out for asking. The truth is worse. I was cast out for asking *once*, late, after signing my name under theirs. I didn't doubt loudly. I doubted politely, on schedule, in the correct amount. If the fire hadn't found me on that mountain — if I hadn't seen what I saw — I'd still be standing post somewhere, guarding a door behind which someone was screaming."

Mara had been listening with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't drunk. Now she set it down.

"You heard him," she said. "The boy. You heard what he said the god sounded like?"

"'Something kind,'" Kaelen said. "Those exact words."

She nodded slowly, and her eyes went distant in a way Kaelen was beginning to recognize — the look of a woman consulting a tenant she couldn't evict.

"That matches," she said simply, and did not elaborate, and no one pressed her, which told Kaelen more about these people than anything else they'd said all evening.

* * *

Later — much later, when the lamp had burned to a stub and Isolde had finally come back from the window and claimed the chair nearest the fire as hers by ancient right — Ferro laid out sleeping arrangements with the gravity of a general assigning battle positions. Kaelen got the pallet by the door. Mara got the alcove behind the salt racks, "because it's the only place in this building with two ways out, and I figure you'll both be happier knowing that."

He was right. They were both happier knowing that.

Kaelen lay awake listening to the hollow sounds of the mountain settling, and thought about doors. He had spent his life guarding doors. Behind one of them, a boy had died while Kaelen counted his own breaths and stayed silent.

Never again. He didn't know yet what shape the vow would take — whether it was protection, or penance, or just stubbornness wearing armor — but he made it in the dark with the seriousness he had once reserved for knightly oaths, and this time he swore it to no institution. Just to the sleeping girl behind the salt racks, and to a sixteen-year-old boy whose name he had never learned, and to whatever kind thing had wanted to help and been murdered for it.

* * *

Morning came gray through the smoke-holes. Kaelen woke to the smell of bread and to raised voices — well, to one raised voice and one voice that was enjoying itself enormously.

"—all I'm saying," Bram was declaring, "is that if we're harboring an apostate knight AND a living blasphemy, we should at least renegotiate rates. Hazard pay. Overtime."

"We're not *harboring* anyone," Isolde snapped. "We're *sheltering* them. Harboring implies we had a choice."

"You had a choice," Kaelen said, sitting up. "You could have turned us in. There's a Spire waystation two valleys east. The bounty on a confirmed god-speaker would buy this entire hollow twice over."

The twins exchanged a glance so fast and so fluent it was practically a conversation.

"Yeah, well," Bram said, suddenly finding the bread fascinating. "Bounties don't split four ways, do they."

"What he means," Isolde said, "is that our brother Renn took Spire coin once, years ago, for information he didn't know was information. What it bought, we don't talk about. So we don't sell to the Spire. Family policy." She thrust a plate at Kaelen hard enough to sting. "Also you cook terribly and we need the labor. Don't make it strange."

It was, Kaelen reflected, the least gracious welcome he had ever received, and it settled into his chest warmer than any commendation the Spire had ever given him.

Ferro appeared in the doorway, ducking under the lintel, and jerked his chin toward the alcove, where Mara had emerged blinking into the lamplight, hair escaping its braid, looking younger and more exhausted than the night before.

"Girl," Ferro rumbled. "That thing inside you. It talked to you in the night?"

Kaelen went cold. Mara's hand crept to her collarbone. "How did you—"

"You talk in your sleep," Ferro said. "Not your voice. Older. Like bronze being struck." He shrugged his enormous shoulders. "Hollow walls. Sound carries."

"It said what?" Kaelen asked quietly.

Mara looked around the kitchen — at the twins frozen mid-bicker, at Ferro impassive as the mountain itself, at Kaelen — and when she spoke, her voice was steady but her knuckles were white around her teacup.

"It said the other shrines are burning. All of them, up and down the range. Inquisitors, moving shrine to shrine ahead of the thaw." She swallowed. "It said we have maybe three weeks before they reach the deep shrines. The ones with keepers still awake. Still praying."

The silence that followed was the load-bearing kind. Kaelen felt it settle over all five of them, and understood, with a strange calm, that the family had already formed — that none of them had chosen it, and none of them would escape it, and the first thing it would ask of them was everything.

"Well," said Bram, reaching for the bread. "Anyone else think we should probably warn somebody?"
