## 4. Prayers in the Dark

The morning after Kaelen carried me half-frozen into Thornfell's Hollow, I woke up smelling of woodsmoke and someone else's soup, and the first thing I saw was a pair of twins arguing over whether I was worth feeding.

"She ate two bowls," Isolde said, holding the empty dish like evidence at a trial. "Two. That's an investment."

"That's an appetite," Bram countered. "Appetites are not investments. Investments pay out."

"Fine," Isolde said, "she'll pay us back by being interesting. Look at her. She's got a goddess living in her face. That's more interesting than anything you've ever done, and once you smuggled a live bear."

"The bear was *profitable*," Bram said with dignity.

I lay there under three blankets that smelled of mule and decided, groggily, that I liked them. It had been a long time since anyone argued about my upkeep as though I were worth keeping.

Ferro ended the debate the way Ferro ended everything, by walking through it. He set another bowl of barley soup beside me without a word, which from Ferro was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

"Thank you," I said.

"Eat," he said. "Gods don't keep body heat. You do."

It took me a moment to realize what he'd just admitted: he believed me. Or at least he believed something had happened to me in that ruined shrine, and rather than argue theology over breakfast, he'd simply decided to feed the girl the theology was happening to. There was a practicality to these people that I found, in those first days, almost unbearably kind.

Veyra, for her part, spent the morning being insufferable about it.

"You have allies now," she said, when I caught my reflection in the polished bottom of a copper kettle — which was, apparently, one of her preferred doors into my head. "You have soup. You have a knight who broke his oaths to save you and twins who would sell you for the right price but feel *badly* about it afterward. This is more than most vessels manage in a lifetime."

"Most vessels," I said under my breath, "don't exist. Because your kind were murdered."

"Murdered is a strong word."

"They were murdered."

"Sequestered," Veyra said, "with extreme prejudice."

I turned the kettle so her face swung away from mine, which did nothing whatsoever, because she lived behind my eyes too and we both knew it. "Why are you like this?"

"I am the goddess of oaths," she said. "Precision is my whole personality."

* * *

It was Isolde who brought the first strange news, three nights later, coming down from the high passes with frost in her braid and a look on her face I hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of a person deciding how much truth a room can hold.

"There's a shepherd up past Grey Saddle," she said, shrugging off her pack. "Old man, half-blind. His flock scattered in a rockslide last week — should've lost forty sheep and probably himself. Instead every animal walked home on its own, single file, and stood in his yard until he counted them." She paused. "He swears they followed a light. He swears the light had a voice, and the voice told him his name."

Bram snorted. "Sheep are stupid. They follow anything."

"Not in single file," Isolde said. "Not home. Not standing to be counted." She looked at me. "And that's not the only one. The ferryman at Coldwater — you know, the one everyone says is a miser — he pulled three drowning miners out of the river last month. Three. In one afternoon. Says his hands moved without him. Says something held them steady."

"Coincidence," said Bram, with the confidence of a man who had never once been right about anything mystical.

"Three drownings," said Isolde, "in the same bend of river, in the same week, all saved by a man who can't swim."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when everyone privately reaches the same conclusion and no one wants to say it first.

Kaelen said it. He was mending a strap by the fire, and his needle stopped mid-stitch. "That's the pattern," he said quietly. "Small gods answering small prayers. A light for a lost flock. Steady hands for a drowning man." He looked up at me, and there was something in his face — not accusation, more like grief wearing the shape of duty. "When the Spire finds out, they won't send soldiers. Soldiers arrest people. They'll send inquisitors. And inquisitors burn things."

"Burn what?" Bram asked. "The ferryman?"

"The shrines," Kaelen said. "The waystations. The old stones where people have prayed for three hundred years even though praying was forbidden, because people pray the way water runs downhill — you can't actually stop it, you can only make them do it secretly. Every hollow shrine in these mountains has a keeper. Someone who leaves bread out, trims the moss, remembers the names. The Spire calls them seditionists. They're mostly grandmothers." He set down the strap. "I've seen what happens to grandmothers the Spire decides are seditionists."

I felt Veyra stir inside me like a current under ice.

"Ask him," she said, "about the shrine at Hollowmere."

I didn't want to ask. Asking meant knowing, and knowing meant the next thing after that, which was always doing something. But the question came out anyway, because apparently some part of me had already signed on.

"What happened at Hollowmere?"

Kaelen's hands went still. "Two years ago," he said. "A keeper named Maren Odell. Kept a wayside shrine to — I never learned which god. The old names were struck from the records before my time. An inquisitor named Serane Voss led the party. They burned the shrine, then the keeper's cottage, then—" he stopped. Recalibrated. "Then Maren. She was seventy-one. She wouldn't tell them the god's name. So they decided she could keep it forever."

"And you questioned it," I said. "That's why you fell."

"I asked why we burned a grandmother," Kaelen said. "In the Spire, that's the same as heresy. Apparently mercy is contagious. They quarantine it." He laughed, short and bitter, and the twins went very carefully quiet around him, the way people do around a wound they can see but not touch.

Veyra spoke again, and this time her voice had none of its usual dry precision. It was low. Almost gentle.

"Mara. There are prayers going up all along these mountains. From shepherds, from miners, from mothers. Small desperate voices, the kind no one powerful has ever bothered to answer. And something in me — something older than me, or perhaps just the rest of me, sleeping in the deep stone — is beginning to hear them." She paused. "I am not the only one waking. I am only the first one loud enough for you to notice."

"And the Spire?" I asked.

"The Spire has noticed too. They are moving shrine to shrine with fire. They believe if they burn the places where prayers gather, the gods will starve before they finish waking." Her voice hardened back into bronze. "They may even be right."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because it wasn't noble. Novels would have you believe that heroism arrives like a trumpet call. Mine arrived as a headache and a bad night's sleep and a conversation I kept trying to lose.

I lay awake most of that night listening to the wind work the pass above the Hollow, and underneath the wind — faint, intermittent, like a signal from far away — I could hear them. Prayers. Not words, exactly. More like pressure. Like standing near a door with many people whispering on the other side. *Keep her safe. Let the leg heal. Let him come home. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Please.* Hundreds of them, layered, overlapping, human in the way that only desperation is fully human.

And threaded through them, here and there, a different sound. A sound like struck bronze. Like my name.

"You could ignore them," Veyra said, when I finally gave up on sleep and sat by the banked fire talking to my own reflection in the hearthstone. "You could run south. Take the twins' offer of passage to the coast. Change your name. Shave your head. I would dim myself; you'd have years, maybe a decade, before the Spire traced the divine fire to you. People have hidden from worse."

"People have also died of worse," I said. "Hiding is how I grew up. My master taught me that the safest place in the world is inside a lie small enough that nobody bothers checking it." I poked the coals. "And then I prayed once, honestly, in a ruin, and everything I'd built my whole survival on turned out to be made of paper."

"So you'll fight the Spire."

"No," I said, and meant it. "I'm not a soldier. I'm not a prophet. I'm a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice who knows eleven useful charms and how to make soup stretch. If I stand in front of an inquisitor, I die in front of an inquisitor, and the shrine burns anyway."

"Then what will you do?"

I thought about Maren Odell, whom I'd never met, who died at seventy-one keeping a secret she'd probably learned from her own grandmother. I thought about the ferryman whose hands had moved without him, and whether he was frightened, and whether anyone had told him what was happening to him or whether he was lying awake somewhere on the Coldwater road thinking he was cursed. I thought about the shepherd counting sheep by lamplight, whispering *thank you* to a voice he couldn't name.

Nobody was going to warn them. The Spire was coming with fire, and the people in the shrines were grandmothers and ferrymen and shepherds, and their only crime was hoping out loud where the wrong ears could hear.

"I'll carry warning," I said. "Shrine to shrine. Whatever ones are left. Tell the keepers the Spire is burning them, tell them to scatter, tell them—" I faltered. "Tell them the gods are waking, and they should decide for themselves what that means. Not the Spire. Not me. Them."

"That isn't nothing," Veyra said. "That is, in fact, the oldest job there is. Before gods had temples they had messengers. Runners between the holy places, carrying word of what was coming." She looked at me — I felt her look at me, from behind my own eyes, which is an experience I recommend to no one — and she said, softly, "It's called being a herald."

"I'm not a herald," I said. "I'm a fugitive who's about to become a slightly better-informed fugitive."

"You keep telling yourself that," she said, with the smugness of a goddess who had clearly already written the ending. "I'll wait."

* * *

Telling the others was harder than deciding. I'd known these people nine days. Nine days does not earn you the right to ask them to stand between you and the Spire's fire.

But when I laid it out — the prayers, the shrines, the plan, such as it was — Ferro grunted in a way that I eventually learned meant *yes*. Isolde started packing before I finished talking. Bram objected on principle, proposed three alternatives, was voted down four to one (he counted his own vote twice), and then spent the evening whittling me a walking stick with a little carved owl on the handle, "for wisdom, since clearly nobody else in this crew has any."

Kaelen was the one who understood what I was actually asking, because Kaelen had stood in front of inquisitors before and knew exactly what it cost.

"You know they'll burn the shrines anyway if they catch us warning them," he said. "You know some of the keepers won't leave. Old people love their stones. Some of them will die holding a broom against soldiers."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're going anyway."

"Somebody has to tell them," I said. "That's all. Somebody has to knock on the door and say *run*. If it isn't me, it's no one, because everyone else with this knowledge is either a goddess or a traitor to the Spire, and only one of us owns boots."

Kaelen looked at me for a long moment. Then he picked up his sword belt and buckled it on, which he hadn't done since the night he'd dragged me out of the snow.

"Hollowmere way," he said. "There's a keeper at the crossroads shrine below the pass. Widow named Etta. She feeds pilgrims in winter. Voss's circle will reach her within the month, if the reports I read before they expelled me were accurate." He paused, and his voice dropped. "Mara. If we run into inquisitors on the road, you run. Promise me. Swear it."

And there it was — the trap of my own making, sprung by the goddess of oaths herself. Because Veyra rose in me like warmth behind the breastbone, and I felt her attention sharpen on the shape of the words about to leave my mouth, and I knew — I *knew* — that whatever I swore aloud in front of her, she would hold me to.

"I swear," I said, "that I'll run *if running saves anyone*. Including me."

Silence. Then, from somewhere behind my ribs, a sound like distant bronze being struck, satisfied.

"Clever girl," Veyra murmured.

Kaelen almost smiled. "You're going to be trouble," he told me.

"I'm told it runs in the family," I said, and shouldered my pack, and we climbed out of the Hollow together into the grey morning, five of us and a goddess, heading up toward the shrines while somewhere ahead of us the smoke was already rising.
