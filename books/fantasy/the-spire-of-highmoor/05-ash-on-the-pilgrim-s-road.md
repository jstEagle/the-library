## 5. Ash on the Pilgrim's Road

I had buried my family twice. Once in the ground, when I was nine, and once in my memory, which was harder and took longer.

So when we came around the shoulder of Mount Cerrin and saw the smoke — not hearth-smoke, not campfire-smoke, but the fat black greasy column of a burning shrine — I felt that old familiar thing open in my chest like a door I'd nailed shut years ago.

"Isolde." Bram's hand on my arm. My brother, who knew exactly what that door looked like from the outside, because he'd watched me nail it.

"I see it," I said.

"They might still—"

"Bram." I shrugged off his hand gently, the way you do when gentleness is the only kind of strength you've got left. "I see it."

We'd been three days out of the Hollow, following the pilgrim road northeast through Thornfell's high passes, Ferro grumbling about his knees, Mara asking questions like a magpie collecting shiny things, Kaelen walking with that Spire-trained silence of his that made him look like he was perpetually about to apologize for existing. Five days' walk to the Shrine of the Weeping Mother, where a keeper named Odell was supposed to be warning the other shrines that the gods were waking and the Spire was burning everything that breathed sideways at the sky.

We were too late. I could smell it before I could see it. Burning cedar and burnt feathers and underneath it something sweeter and worse that I refused to name.

* * *

The shrine had been small. A stone circle roofed with slate, a spring, a garden. Now it was a black tooth against the grey rock, its slate shattered, its garden trampled by boots — too many boots, marching in formation, because the Spire's inquisitors never did anything as honest as rage. They did procedure.

The keeper's body lay by the spring. Old man, white beard, hands folded over his chest. Somebody had arranged him there after. That was the part that made my teeth ache: they burned him alive or near enough, and then they *tidied*. Laid him out neat as a Sunday dinner. Left a lead seal stamped into the dirt beside him — the Spire's sunburst, pressed deep, so every pilgrim who came after would know whose work this was and whose mercy it wasn't.

Ferro took off his hat. He was sixty and had smuggled salt, brandy, untaxed wool, and once — memorably — a bear, and he'd never once taken off his hat for anything. He took it off now.

Mara knelt by the body. She didn't touch it. Her lips moved, and I caught fragments — *Veyra, was he yours, did anyone answer him, was anyone listening* — and the air around her went strange and bright-edged, the way it did when the goddess leaned close to her skin.

"No," Mara said aloud, to nobody, to us. "He prayed alone. For forty years. And no one answered, because—" her voice cracked "—because they sealed them away and called it peace."

"Well." Ferro put his hat back on. "Then we're the answering service now, aren't we."

It should have been funny. It almost was. Mostly it just hurt.

* * *

That was when Bram shouted from up the slope.

"Isolde! Here — quickly, love, *quickly!*"

Bram only calls me *love* when something is genuinely terrible or genuinely wonderful, and given the day, I ran expecting terrible.

There was a hollow behind a fallen slab of the shrine wall, sheltered from the worst of it, and in the hollow was a child. A girl, maybe seven, maybe eight, soot-streaked, curled around herself like a comma. She'd hidden when the inquisitors came. She'd hidden so well they hadn't found her, and she'd been there ever since — and how long was *ever since*, the ash told me. Days. The burn on her arm told me more. She'd reached through flame for something — a doll, a hand, I didn't ask — and the wound had gone angry red and then quiet red and then that terrible waxy color that means the fire is inside now, in the blood, doing its slow work.

She was breathing in little sips. Her eyes found mine and they were clear, horribly clear, the way the dying are clear.

"Hallo, sprout," I said, because someone had to say something ordinary, and I have always been the one. "You gave those bastards the slip, eh? Hid right under their noses?"

Her mouth moved. No sound. Then, barely: "Grandda?"

And there it was. The keeper. Odell. Her grandda, laid out neat by the spring with his hands folded, while she hid in the dark listening to everything.

I am not going to describe what happened inside me then. Some rooms you don't give tours of.

"Mara!" I heard myself shout, and my voice sounded like someone else's, flat and far away. "MARA!"

* * *

They came running, all three of them, and then there was the awful crowded minute where everyone knows and no one can do anything. Ferro swearing softly under his breath. Kaelen going very still, checking the girl's pulse with two fingers, his face doing something complicated. Bram crouched with his hand hovering over the girl's hair, not touching, like touching might break whatever thread was holding her here.

"She needs a surgeon," Kaelen said. "A real one. The nearest one is two days down the mountain, and she doesn't have two days."

"So we make her have two days," I said.

"With what?"

"With—" and I turned to Mara, and I hated myself for it even as I did it, because I knew what I was asking. "With her."

Mara looked at me. And here's the thing about Mara Vasquez: she flinches. She flinches at loud noises, at kindness, at her own shadow. But she didn't flinch now. She looked at the girl, and then at the keeper by the spring, and something settled in her face like a stone finding the bottom of a stream.

"What do I owe you?" she asked me quietly.

"Nothing. You owe nothing. That's not why—"

"It's always why, with people." She almost smiled. "But all right. Not for payment, then. For spite."

She knelt and took the girl's burned hand in both of hers.

"Veyra," she said. "Oath-keeper. I don't know how this works. I don't know the words. But if you're listening — and I know you're listening, you've never once stopped listening, it's honestly a bit much — help her. Not me. *Her.* She hid under her grandda's murderers for days and she held on. That's an oath kept without any words at all. Honor it."

For a moment: nothing. Wind. Ash drifting down soft as terrible snow.

Then the bronze voice, and this time it didn't come from behind Mara's ribs. It came from everywhere, from the stone, from the spring, from the dead man's folded hands.

*"I heard her prayer three days past. I could not reach her. Through you, I can."*

Light came up out of the ground. Not firelight — oath-light, gold shot through with iron-grey, the color of a promise being kept whether or not keeping it is convenient. It poured through Mara like she was a window and the morning was finally coming through. The girl gasped. The burn began to change — the angry red fading, the waxy flesh flushing pink, the fever breaking off her like ice cracking off a branch in thaw.

Mara swayed. Kaelen caught her shoulder without seeming to move. The light guttered, steadied.

*"Small thing,"* Veyra said, and there was something in the great bronze voice that I would swear on anything you liked was tenderness. *"The first of many small things. This is how it starts. Not with thrones. With burns healed and debts paid and oaths kept one at a time until the world remembers how."*

Then it was gone, and Mara sagged against Kaelen, gray-faced, and the girl opened her eyes all the way and looked at the five of us crowding around her and said, with the imperiousness of the recently resurrected:

"You're not inquisitors."

"No, sprout," Ferro said, and his voice was rough as gravel in a bucket. "We're considerably worse. We're smugglers. Would you like some soup?"

* * *

We camped that night in the shrine's ruins because leaving felt like running and none of us wanted to run. Ferro made soup over a small fire — barley and salt pork and, gods forgive him, a truly criminal amount of wild garlic he'd been hoarding — and the girl ate two bowls and fell asleep against my side with her bandaged arm cradled like something precious.

Her name was Tilly. She told us between bowls. She also told us that Grandda used to say the Mother's spring water tasted of tears but the good kind, and that there were other keepers west along the ridge, and that the inquisitors had a list.

"A list," Kaelen repeated. His voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when the guilt was standing on his chest.

"Tilly," I said gently. "Did your grandda say what was on the list? Names? Places?"

"Shrines," she said. "All the shrines. He said they'd start with the little ones because nobody guards the little ones." She yawned enormously. "He said somebody ought to warn the big ones. The deep ones. Under the glacier."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke.

Kaelen and Mara exchanged a look over the flames — that look, the one they thought none of us noticed, the one that was already half a language. Ferro chewed his garlic thoughtfully. Bram raised both eyebrows at me across the fire, our entire childhood correspondence conducted in eyebrow.

Under the glacier. Where the gods were sealed. Where the Spire's oldest secrets slept in the ice.

Well. We'd known the direction eventually pointed uphill. We just hadn't known the mountain would be so eager about it.

* * *

Later, when Tilly was asleep and the others were turning in, I sat by the spring with a cup of tea I wasn't drinking and let the door in my chest stand open for a while. Nine years old again. Soldiers in the lane. My mother's loom still warm. The Spire's seal hammered into our doorframe afterward, neat as Sunday, so everyone would know whose work it was.

Bram sat down next to me without asking. He handed me the good blanket, which meant he loved me, because Bram would fight a bear for that blanket. We'd smuggled one together once; long story; the bear was involved.

"You're thinking we should stop escorting and start fighting," he said.

"I'm thinking I spent twenty years nailing a door shut," I said, "and today a seven-year-old kicked it open in an afternoon."

"And?"

"And I'm done hiding in the hollow, Bram." I looked at the spring, black under the stars, tasting of tears but the good kind. "Mara channeled a goddess tonight to save one child. One. And the Spire will call it heresy and write it down in their tidy books. So we get to the shrines first. All of them. We carry the warning, we carry Tilly, we carry whatever the hell else needs carrying. That's what smugglers are *for*, isn't it? Moving precious cargo where it isn't supposed to go?"

Bram grinned, slow and wide. "Mother would be proud."

"Mother would be *insufferable* about it."

Down the slope, Mara stirred in her bedroll and murmured something in a voice that wasn't entirely hers — two words, bronze-bright even half-asleep:

*"West ridge. Hurry."*

I stood up, tea forgotten, heart hammering like a fist on a door.

"Bram," I said. "Wake the others. We ride at first light — and Isolde's done hiring."
