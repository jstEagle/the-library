## 6. The Oath Beneath the Ice

Kaelen had spent eleven years learning to distrust anything that came easily, so when Veyra's voice — speaking through Mara, through the frost on the tent canvas, through his own aching knees at three in the morning — said *come north*, he did not argue. He simply woke Isolde with a boot to her bedroll and started packing.

"You're doing it again," she grumbled, hair like a crow's nest in a gale. "The thing where you obey the spooky voice without telling anyone why."

"The spooky voice is standing right there," Bram offered, thumbing toward Mara, who sat wrapped in two blankets by the dead fire, looking like a woman being dragged somewhere against her will by an invisible leash.

"It's not *standing* anywhere," Mara said wearily. "That's rather the problem."

Ferro, who had been pretending to sleep and fooling no one, rose with the groan of a man whose joints had opinions. "Glacier pass is closed this time of year."

"Not for us," Kaelen said.

"Ah." Ferro nodded sagely. "'Us.' The knight, the witch, and the four idiots who signed on for smuggling salt, not theology." He began rolling his bedding anyway. "Fine. But if I freeze to death on holy ground, one of you is carrying my body back down and explaining to my sister why."

"You'd haunt us first," Isolde said.

"I'd *absolutely* haunt you first."

* * *

The glacier was called Vaskrind by the old maps Kaelen had stolen from the Spire's archive during his disgrace — maps he'd taken on impulse, meaning only to spite the men who'd cast him out, and never understood why until now. It rose out of the high valley like a frozen wave, blue-green and ancient, humming faintly. Not with wind. With something underneath, like a bell struck once, three centuries ago, still ringing.

Mara felt it before any of them. She stopped at the ice field's edge, breath steaming, and pressed a hand flat against the air as though touching glass.

"She's louder here," Mara whispered. "Veyra. She's been *here*. All this time."

"Sealed," Kaelen said quietly. "I think... sealed here."

He led them along the crevasse line to a seam in the ice that should not have existed — too straight, too deliberate, edged with characters worn nearly smooth. Old Highmoor script. He recognized it from the archive, from documents stamped with the First Circle's seal. Documents about maintenance.

Maintenance. Of a prison.

"This is a door," Isolde said flatly.

"This is a *door*," Kaelen agreed, and set his palm against the script.

The ice did not melt so much as remember itself differently — the seam widened, and cold air rolled out smelling of stone and dust and something older, something like incense burned ten thousand years ago. A passage sloped downward into the glacier's heart, lit by veins of pale light running through the walls like frozen lightning.

"Well," said Bram. "I hate it."

"You haven't even seen inside yet," Isolde said.

"I preemptively hate it. It's efficient."

They went in single file, Ferro last with a lantern he didn't need but refused to abandon on principle. The passage opened into a vault that stole the breath from every one of them — a cathedral hollowed from living ice, its ceiling lost in blue shadow, its floor carved into concentric rings of script. And around those rings, embedded in the walls like insects in amber, were thrones.

Nine of them. Each one shaped for a body that had been bound into it — Kaelen could see the restraints, could see the scorch marks where something vast had been pinned down and held. Eight of the thrones were empty, their bindings dark and cold.

The ninth still glowed. Faintly. Stubbornly. Like a coal refusing the snow.

"That's her seat," Mara said. Her voice shook. "She's still in it. Part of her. They couldn't finish—" She turned to Kaelen, and her face was terrible and bright. "They never finished killing them. That's what your Spire has been doing all these years. *Finishing.*"

Kaelen couldn't answer. He had walked to the vault's far side, where a stone plinth stood apart from the ice — deliberately placed, deliberately mortal, because whatever had been done here needed recording by hands. A lead case rested on it, sealed with the sigil of the First Circle.

His hands were steady as he broke the seal. His hands were always steady. It was everything behind them that shook.

Inside were records. Ledgers, almost. That was the horror of it — the banality. Columns of names. Dates. Allocations of *yield*.

"They didn't fight a war," he heard himself say. His voice sounded very far away. "There was no ancient war. There was a... harvest."

Isolde took one of the pages and read aloud, her sharp voice going flatter with each line: "*'The binding of Veyra yielded sufficient oath-magic to anchor the wards of Highmoor for a generation. Recommend the remaining eight be drawn down slowly, to avoid—'"* She stopped. "*'To avoid collapse of the source.'*"

"*Source*," Ferro repeated softly.

"They called them sources," Kaelen said. "Not gods. Sources. My order's whole foundation — the war we're taught killed our protectors, the sacrifice the Spire made to save mankind — it's a lie written by the men who did the killing. They bound nine gods into this ice and drained them for power. Three hundred years. And then they spent three centuries executing anyone who might hear one of them wake up and say otherwise."

The silence rang. Even Bram had nothing.

It was Mara who moved. She walked to the ninth throne, the glowing one, and knelt before it — and when she spoke, it was not entirely her voice. It was bronze and frost and grief three centuries deep.

*"I remember their faces,"* Veyra said, through her vessel's lips, while tears ran down Mara's cheeks unbidden. *"My brothers. My sisters. I was the strongest, so they bound me deepest. I have watched through the seal for three hundred years while my worshippers were burned and my name forgotten, and I swore — I am goddess of oaths, little knight, I do not break my sworn word — I swore I would see it undone."*

"Mara," Kaelen said hoarsely. "Ask her if breaking the seal frees her. Or kills her."

He already knew the answer. He saw it in Mara's face before Veyra withdrew enough for Mara to speak for herself, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears.

"She doesn't know," Mara said. "She says it doesn't matter. She says—" Mara laughed, a cracked, exhausted sound. "She says oaths aren't kept because keeping them is safe."

* * *

They made camp in the vault's antechamber, because none of them could face the descent before dawn, and because the mountain felt like a place where secrets could be spoken. Ferro produced a bottle of plum brandy that had survived two smuggler raids and one avalanche, and they passed it around in silence that slowly softened.

Bram fell asleep mid-sentence, which Isolde declared the most sensible thing he'd done all week, and Ferro followed shortly after, snoring like a landslide. Isolde sat watch by the entrance, silhouetted against the glacier's glow, giving them — deliberately, Kaelen realized — her back.

Mara sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them spoke for a long time.

"I keep thinking about the ledgers," she finally said. "How tidy they were. How much they must have slept, afterward. The founders. After murdering gods, they just... went to dinner."

"People are good at that," Kaelen said. "I was. Eleven years I served them. I read the execution orders. I carried them out." He stared at the brandy in his cup. "I told myself the god-speakers were dangerous. That the Spire knew best. It took one girl — a fourteen-year-old who prayed for rain — to make me question it. One girl, and they put her down like a dog, and when I asked *why*, they took my rank and my name and gave me exile instead of a blade because I'd been useful once."

"You asked the question most people never ask."

"And it wasn't enough. Asking isn't enough, Mara. You have to—" He stopped.

"Have to what?" she asked softly.

He turned and looked at her, really looked — at the frost-scars on her knuckles from her master's debts, at the shadows under her eyes where a goddess lived, at the stubborn, terrified, magnificent woman who had run from everything her whole life and kept walking toward the fire anyway.

"I was raised on duty," he said. "Duty above love, above self, above sense. The Spire took that creed and made it a weapon. And I have spent this entire journey telling myself that what I feel for you is another duty. Protecting the vessel. Guarding the herald. I'm very good at dressing cowardice up as obligation — it's practically an order requirement." His breath came out ragged. "But there's no ledger for this. No order. No duty. So I'll say it plain, and you can tell me I'm a fool: I love you. Against every vow I ever took and every vow I broke. I love you, and I don't want to guard you. I want to stand *beside* you, which is considerably more dangerous for everyone involved."

Mara was quiet for so long he began composing his apology. Then she reached over and took his scarred hand in hers.

"When I prayed in that shrine," she said, "it was the first time in years I let anyone hear me. And Veyra answered, and I thought — *this* is what being seen costs. This is why I hide." Her thumb traced the old scars. "But you look at me like I'm not a vessel or a herald or a debt to be paid. You look at me like I'm just Mara, and somehow that's more terrifying than a goddess." She smiled, wet-eyed and crooked. "So yes. Fool. Both of us. I love you too, Kaelen of the disgraced and ridiculous name, and I refuse to feel guilty about it."

"Villalobos would be proud," he managed, and she laughed — really laughed — and leaned her head against his shoulder, and above them in the vault the ninth throne glowed a little brighter, as though a goddess of oaths approved of vows freely given.

From her watch by the entrance, Isolde said, without turning around, "Finally. I was starting to think I'd have to lock you two in a crevasse."

* * *

It was Mara who found it, near dawn — or perhaps it was Veyra, guiding her vessel's half-sleeping hand across the plinth. Beneath the lead records lay a final document, older than the rest, written in a hand that shook.

A confession. Signed by the First Inquisitor himself.

*"We did not defeat the gods. We ambushed them at prayer. May whatever hears this forgive us, for we have stolen the light of the world and called it order."*

And beneath the signature, a warning, added later in different ink:

*"The seal weakens where the faithless doubt. If it breaks, the Spire falls — and the High Inquisitor will burn anything, anyone, to prevent it. He knows what he is. He remembers being made."*

Kaelen read it twice. Then he looked north, toward Highmoor's distant spire, visible even from here — a black needle against the paling sky.

"He knows," Kaelen said quietly. "Serris knows what he is. Which means he's known all along that the seal is failing — and he's not trying to repair it."

"No," Mara breathed, understanding flooding her face. "He's preparing for the day it breaks. He needs a new vessel. A new throne." Her hand found Veyra's glow beneath her skin, and it flickered like a candle in wind. "And Veyra says he's already chosen one."

"Who?"

Mara lifted her eyes to his, and they were full of frost and fear.

"Me."
