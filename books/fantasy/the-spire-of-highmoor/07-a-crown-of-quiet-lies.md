## 7. A Crown of Quiet Lies

The trouble with carrying a goddess, Mara decided on the ninth morning after the glacier, was that the goddess was tidy.

Veyra did not sprawl in her mind like an unwanted guest with muddy boots. She arranged things. She shelved memories the way a careful housekeeper folded linens — and sometimes she took the ones with holes in them away to be mended, and they never quite came back the same.

Mara noticed it first with her mother's face.

She had always kept it somewhere close, the way you keep a chipped cup that's too precious to use: the broad brown cheekbones, the gap between her front teeth when she laughed at something Mara had said at four years old, some joke about a goat and a wedding cake that Mara had carried for twenty years without ever remembering what it meant. She reached for it now, sitting on a flat stone above Thornfell's Hollow while Ferro argued with Bram about the correct way to salt pork below, and found the goat but not the joke.

The laugh remained. The reason for it had been smoothed away like a river stone.

*You are doing this,* she said into the quiet behind her eyes. *Don't pretend you aren't.*

*I am maintaining you,* Veyra answered, and her voice was bronze struck soft, a bell wrapped in wool. *Grief is a stone in the vessel's waterline. I have carried three hundred years of silence, little oath-keeper. I know how much weight a soul can hold before it cracks.*

"I liked my stones."

There was a pause, the particular pause of someone deciding whether to lie. Veyra had learned lying from watching humans and had not yet gotten good at it.

*They were heavy.*

"They were *mine.*"

Below, Ferro won the argument about the pork by the ancient smuggler's method of simply salting it while Bram talked. Isolde laughed from the doorway of the wayhouse, sharp and bright as a knife being drawn, and Kaelen looked up at Mara on her rock with that expression he wore lately — the one that said he could see the shape of something wrong in her silhouette even if he couldn't name it.

He was getting too good at reading her. It was going to be a problem. It was already a problem, if she was honest, and honesty about Kaelen Ashvane was a door she had been keeping firmly shut since the glacier vault, where they had stood among the frozen proof of three centuries of murder and admitted — exhausted, raw, his hand shaking against hers — that whatever this was between them had teeth and roots and no intention of letting go.

She climbed down before he could climb up.

* * *

That night, the dream came.

Or not a dream. Veyra wore dreams the way she wore reflections — borrowed doors — and Mara knew within three heartbeats of walking into it that this one belonged to the goddess, because the sky was the color of old bronze and the road beneath her feet was paved with oaths. She could feel them underfoot, each one humming faintly: *I will return by harvest. I will keep your secret. I will love you until the mountains fall.* A whole civilization's promises, worn smooth by feet that had stopped coming.

At the end of the road stood a mirror, freestanding, tall as a gate.

"You could have just asked," Mara said aloud, arms crossed, because if there was one advantage to having a goddess in your skull it was that you got very fast at recognizing her stagecraft.

*This is asking.* The mirror rippled. *Look.*

Mara looked, because refusing felt worse, and because some treacherous part of her wanted to see.

A woman stood in the glass. Younger than Mara by a few years, with ink-stained fingers and a scholar's stoop, wearing the grey of the Spire's lower orders. Her face was ordinary and alive, creased around the eyes with laughing, and she was praying — Mara could hear it, a whisper in a dead language, and behind the whisper something vast turning over in its sleep like a bear in winter.

"This is—"

*A god-speaker. Three hundred years ago. Her name was Serevanne Holt, and she heard a sleeping god the way you hear me, and the Spire found out.* Veyra's voice went very quiet. *But they did not kill her. That was their first cruelty and their cleverest. Killing a speaker wastes the gift. Instead they gave her to the seal itself. They let it drink her.*

The woman in the mirror changed. It happened slowly, the way frost climbs a window — the laugh-lines filling and flattening, the color draining out of the eyes until they were two polished coins set in a face, the ink stains fading from her fingers as if even the memory of writing were being tidied away. What remained was beautiful the way a statue is beautiful. Perfect. Empty.

Mara's stomach turned over. "I know that face."

*You do.*

The hollowed woman aged in the space of a breath, decades pouring through her like sand, and the face rearranged itself into high cheekbones and a silver-streaked widow's peak and eyes like coins still, coin-eyed and calm, and Mara was looking at High Inquisitor Aldous Ren, the man who had burned a shrine and its keeper on the pilgrim's road and signed the order with a steady hand. The man whose sermons Kaelen had grown up memorizing.

"He was a girl named Serevanne."

*Serevanne Holt. And the seal drank everything that made her a person — every grief, every love, every memory with a rough edge — because pain is where the divine takes root. What was left was exactly what the Spire needed. An instrument that would never doubt, never grieve, never question an order again.* A pause. *And now my power grows in you, Mara Vasquez, and the Spire knows what we are doing to you, and they are not afraid. They are patient. Because they have done this before.*

The road of oaths hummed under Mara's bare feet. She crouched and pressed her palm against one — *I will return by harvest* — and felt it, faintly, still true. Still waiting. Someone, three hundred years dead, still meaning it.

"Why show me this?" she asked, standing. "Really. Not the polished version."

And here Veyra hesitated so long that Mara felt it in her own chest, a held breath that wasn't hers.

*Because I can offer you what the Spire offered her. Willingly given instead of taken. The same peace, freely chosen.* The mirror rippled again, and now it showed Mara herself — but a Mara with steady eyes and no flinch, no scar-memory of her master's debts, no ache where her mother's laughter lived. *I can take the weight. Your griefs. Your fears. The memories that hurt. I would leave everything you need. You would be a perfect vessel, and the Spire could not hollow you because I will have emptied you gently first. No one could ever make an instrument of you. You would be free.*

"Free," Mara repeated.

*Of pain.*

"And Kaelen?"

The mirror-Mara didn't answer, and that was the answer. In the glass, the steadier Mara smiled a statue's smile, and Mara understood with cold clarity that the woman in the mirror had no idea who Kaelen Ashvane was. Couldn't have picked him out of a crowd. Why would she? Love was nothing but pain with good posture. Grief was love with nowhere left to go. Empty the grief and you had emptied the love, quietly, cleanly, while the person thanked you for it.

"That's not freedom," Mara said. Her voice shook, which annoyed her. "That's just a nicer room in the same prison. You're offering me Serevanne's fate with a ribbon on it."

*I am offering you survival.* For the first time, something cracked in the bronze of Veyra's voice. *Three hundred years I slept in the dark knowing what they did to her, to all of us, and now they hunt you, child, and I cannot — I will not watch them do it again. If I must choose between your wholeness and your life, I choose—*

"Don't." Mara stepped forward until her reflection filled the mirror, the frightened real one, red-eyed and stubborn. "Don't finish that sentence. You want to protect me? Then protect *all* of me. The goat joke I lost. My mother's laugh. The night in the ruined shrine when I prayed like an idiot and you answered. All of it, even the parts that hurt. Especially those." She pressed her hand flat against the glass, against her own face. "You're the goddess of oaths. So swear me one. Swear you'll stop taking."

The silence went on so long that the humming road seemed to lean in and listen.

*If I swear it,* Veyra said at last, slowly, *and keep it, there is a cost. The power I hold in you is anchored in those memories — pain anchors divinity the way roots anchor trees. Refuse the pruning, and when the final hour comes, the seal or the Spire or my own waking will burn through you hotter than any vessel should bear. You may die, Mara. Truly. Or worse than die.*

"Then say it anyway."

*Mara—*

"Say it. An oath isn't worth anything if you only offer the easy ones. Isn't that your whole — isn't that the entire point of you?"

Another silence. When Veyra spoke again, the bronze in her voice had gone molten, and the whole dream-road blazed.

*Then hear me and be bound. By salt and smoke, by the shrine where you first called and I first woke: I, Veyra, keeper of vows, swear to keep every stone in your waterline. Your griefs, your loves, your small forgotten jokes. I will carry them with you and take none of them. This I swear upon my own name — and may I go nameless forever if I break it.*

The mirror shattered into light. The road of oaths dissolved beneath Mara's feet, and she fell upward into waking—

—and woke in the loft of the wayhouse with tears freezing on her cheeks and every memory intact, agonizingly, gloriously intact, her mother's missing goat-joke even drifting back to her like something returned from mending. Beside the bed, someone had left tea. Kaelen sat against the wall with his sword across his knees, awake, watchful, and when he saw her open her eyes he didn't ask what the goddess had shown her. He just said, quietly:

"You screamed once. Around midnight. Then you laughed."

"Did I?"

"At something. I couldn't tell what."

"The funniest joke anyone ever told me," Mara said, wiping her face. "It involves a goat. You had to be four years old."

Something eased in his shoulders — that guarded knight's posture loosening by one careful degree — and he handed her the tea, and their fingers touched a moment longer than the tea required, and neither of them made a joke about it, because some doors you leave shut and some you just stand next to, warming your hands on the frame.

*Thank you,* she told Veyra, into the quiet.

*Do not thank me yet,* the goddess answered, and her voice had gone strange and distant, like weather rolling in from far off. *Little oath-keeper — I have kept my word to you. Now listen. I need you to keep yours. There is a rider on the pilgrim's road, coming down from Highmoor, and she carries a white banner and the High Inquisitor's own seal. She is asking for the vessel by name. Mara — they know exactly where you sleep.*

Downstairs, a knock sounded at the wayhouse door. Polite. Patient.

Coin-eyed.

Mara put down the tea and reached for her boots.
