## 8. The March on Highmoor

Bram had always said the trick to a good lie was believing it yourself, at least for the length of time it took to sell it. He'd told himself a good one that morning: *this is just another job.* In and out, grab Ferro, grab Renn, be home in Thornfell's Hollow by supper with a story about how they'd all laughed about it later.

He didn't believe it. Not with the Spire's banners hanging over Highmoor's lower city like black teeth against the grey sky. Not with Isolde walking beside him so quiet he could hear his own heartbeat trying to fill the silence she left.

"Say something," he muttered. "You humming is less frightening than you thinking."

"I'm counting," Isolde said. "Guards on the gate. Fourteen."

"I count twelve."

"That's because you're bad at counting and worse at lying, which makes me wonder how you've survived this long."

"Ferro says it's because I'm charming."

"Ferro also once ate a pickled egg off a corpse because 'waste is a sin,' so perhaps don't cite him as an authority." She paused. "Twelve guards, Bram. And Ferro in irons behind them. And Renn."

Renn. Their brother — hers and Bram's both, by blood if not by temperament, because Renn had gone straight years ago and taken honest work as a caravan guard while the twins stayed crooked as river roots. Honest work hadn't saved him. He'd been caught carrying Mara's warning letters to the hollow shrines, along with old Ferro, who'd been caught being exactly where Ferro always was when trouble came looking.

The Spire hadn't killed them. That was the part that made Bram's stomach hurt. They'd *announced* the capture. Posted it in the square, ink still fresh: two god-speakers' collaborators held for trial, execution at dawn, all loyal subjects of the Spire welcome to witness the Spire's justice.

It was a trap so obvious it was almost insulting. Come for your people, little heretics, and we'll take the whole nest of you at once.

"We could not come," Bram said, because somebody had to say it out loud, and he'd rather it be him than let the thought grow teeth in Isolde's head.

Isolde looked at him. Just looked. She had their mother's eyes, which meant she could say more with one look than Bram could with a whole ballad, and what she said now was: *you know we're coming anyway.*

"Yeah," Bram sighed. "Stupid of me."

* * *

They gathered in the cellar of a dyer's house two streets from the square, the kind of place where the walls sweated color and everything smelled faintly of mordant. Kaelen spread a map of the lower city across a pickle barrel, weighted at the corners with whatever was handy: a knife, a candle stub, half a wheel of cheese that Ferro would have mourned.

Kaelen had changed since they'd pulled him out of the Spire's shadow. The disgraced knight still moved like a soldier — precise, economical, every gesture paid for in advance — but there was something looser in him now. Something that belonged to other people.

"The trial is theater," he said. "The real prize is Mara. They want her to come running so the Inquisitors can take her somewhere quiet and hollow her out." His jaw tightened. "So we don't give them Mara. We give them us."

"Flattering," Isolde said. "Go on."

"There are two ways into the holding cells beneath the magistrate's hall. The front, through the square, past fourteen guards—"

"Twelve," Isolde said.

"—or the old tithe tunnel from the days when the city paid its taxes in grain. It runs under the tanner's row and comes up inside the hall's cellar. I used it once, when I was still wearing their colors." He said it flatly, but Bram caught the flinch. Every step back into Spire ground cost Kaelen something.

"And the trap?" Mara asked quietly.

She'd been sitting on the stairs the whole time, half in shadow, and Bram realized she'd been listening to all of it with that look she got — like she was hearing a conversation happening somewhere underneath the words. Veyra did that to her face sometimes. Made it older.

"The trap," Kaelen said, "is that there are three hundred soldiers in this city and we have eleven people and a prayer."

"Prayers are doing more than people expect lately," Mara said.

And that was true everywhere, wasn't it? Bram had seen it coming down the pilgrim's road — shrines unsealed, small gods stretching after three hundred years of sleep. A well in Harrowmere that healed warts. A roadside stone that hummed when you told it the truth. The world was waking up, and the Spire was burning everything it could reach to put the morning back out.

"So that's the plan," Kaelen finished. "Tithe tunnel, take the cells, get Ferro and Renn, and be gone before anyone counts to a hundred. No heroics."

Everyone looked at Bram. Everyone always looked at Bram, because Bram was the one who did heroics and then complained about them afterward, which was different from planning them.

"No heroics," Bram agreed solemnly. "I promise nothing beyond mild derring-do."

"Bram."

"Fine. *No heroics.* May the gods witness my oath." He grinned at Mara. "See? I'm learning the local religion."

Mara smiled, but it didn't reach far. "Be careful what you swear in front of a goddess," she said softly. "I mean that."

Later — much later, with blood on his hands and the taste of smoke in his mouth — Bram would remember she'd warned him.

* * *

The tithe tunnel smelled exactly as bad as you'd expect a tunnel under tanner's row to smell, and Bram passed the walk complaining about it in a whisper, because complaining was how he kept the fear in its cage.

"You'd think," he whispered to Kaelen ahead of him, "that for three hundred years of stolen divine power, they could have invented a smell-proof tunnel."

"It was built for grain."

"Grain smells better than this. Grain smells like *bread*. There were aspirations, once, for this tunnel."

"Quiet," Isolde hissed, from behind, where she was watching their backs with a crossbow she'd named — gods help them all — *Duchess*.

Then Kaelen stopped, hand up, and Bram shut up, and the fear got out of its cage anyway and sat on his chest.

Because the tunnel wasn't empty. The Spire knew about the tithe tunnel. Of course they did — Kaelen had used it, and the Spire remembered everything its knights ever did, even the ones they threw away.

There were six of them in the dark, white-cloaked, blades already drawn, waiting patiently like spiders who'd been told exactly when dinner would arrive.

"Well," Bram said. "This is awkward."

The fight in the tunnel was short, ugly, and nothing like the songs. Songs never mention how loud it is, how close the walls are, how a man's breath sounds when you take it. Bram fought with a short blade and a longer temper, and Isolde's crossbow sang twice beside him, and Kaelen moved through the middle of it like something the Spire had built and then been foolish enough to discard.

When it was done, Bram leaned against the wall and shook, just a little, and lied to himself again: *just another job.*

Above them, faintly, through sixty feet of stone, the city bells began to ring.

Not the alarm pattern. Something else. Something older, arrhythmic, like the bells themselves had woken up confused.

Mara pressed her palm to the tunnel wall. Her eyes went silver-white for one terrible second. "The gods," she breathed. "They're stirring. All of them. The whole mountain range is waking up, and everyone in this city can feel it."

"Good or bad?" Isolde asked.

"Yes," said Mara.

* * *

The cellar of the magistrate's hall went easy. Two guards, one asleep, one bribable — the bribable one took Bram's coin and Bram's advice (*there's a fire in the tannery district, go be heroic elsewhere*) and ran, which was the smartest thing any Spire man did all night.

The cells were down a wet stair. And there, behind iron bars, gray-faced and grinning, was Ferro.

"Took you long enough," Ferro rasped. "I was starting to think I'd have to escape myself. Undignified."

"You're welcome," Bram said, working the lock with shaking hands. Behind Ferro, Renn stood silent, bruised around one eye, alive, alive, *alive*, and Bram felt something crack open in his chest that he'd have to deal with later, much later, preferably never.

They almost made it. That was the joke of it. They almost made it clean.

They were in the hall's cellar, twenty feet from the tunnel mouth, when the doors above burst open and the Inquisitor's men came down in numbers that turned the math from bad to arithmetic.

White cloaks. Steel. And at the top of the stairs, framed in torchlight, a figure in grey-and-silver whose voice carried down the stone like frost:

"Take the vessel alive. The rest are yours."

The fight in that cellar lasted four minutes and cost Bram things he'd spend the rest of his life counting. He fought back to back with Renn — his straight-laced, honest-work brother, who picked up a dead man's sword without hesitation, and Bram thought absurdly, *he fights like family, he must have learned it from us* — and Isolde's crossbow sang until it didn't, until a blade found her shoulder and she went down swearing with tremendous creativity, and Kaelen roared something wordless and became a storm.

And then the ceiling cracked.

Not collapsed — *cracked*, a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and light came down through the stone. Not torchlight. Silver-gold, moving, alive, pouring through the fractures like water finding old channels, and every man in that cellar — Spire and smuggler alike — stopped fighting and stared.

Through the cracks, distantly, Bram heard the square. Thousands of voices, not screaming — *singing*, or praying, or both, the populace pouring out of their houses into the streets as the waking gods rolled over the city like weather. Somewhere a shrine bell rang itself. Somewhere a fountain ran upward.

Mara stood in the middle of the cellar with her arms out and her eyes full of dawn, and Veyra's voice came out of her doubled, harmonized, terrible and kind:

"*Three hundred years. You built your throne on our graves, little Spire. Come and see what grows there now.*"

The Spire men broke. Some dropped swords; some fled up the stairs; some fell to their knees and started praying to exactly the thing they'd been ordered to kill. The grey-and-silver figure at the top of the stairs retreated, unhurried, promising with his silence that this wasn't over.

* * *

Afterward, in the smoke and the singing streets, Bram counted his family.

Ferro, limping but upright, already asking if anyone had food. Isolde, arm bound, refusing to sit down, glaring at the burning barricades like they owed her money. Renn, bloodied, standing next to Kaelen — his sister's knight, though nobody said it yet — and the two of them talking low like soldiers do. And Mara, pale as paper, leaning on a wall, Veyra's light guttering out of her like a candle in wind.

Eleven had gone into the city. Nine walked out. Old Wren and young Tam lay in the magistrate's cellar, and Bram couldn't make the number stop hurting no matter how many times he counted it.

"We can't go home," Isolde said quietly, beside him. "Look at it. We lit the fire, Bram. Half the lower city is praying in the streets and the other half is barricaded, and the Spire will burn every house in Thornfell's Hollow looking for us. There's no going back down the mountain."

Bram looked at his sister, at his brother, at the smuggler and the knight and the girl with a goddess in her veins, and he understood — finally, completely, with the clarity that only arrives after blood — that the point of no return wasn't a line you crossed.

It was people. It had always been people. You could lose anything else and find it again, but these ones —

"Don't die," he said, out loud, before his humor could catch it and dress it up. "Any of you. I mean it. I spent thirty years learning to need nobody, and then you lot happened, and it's ruined me, so just — don't die."

Silence. Then Ferro clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Wouldn't dream of it," Ferro said, lying magnificently.

Mara pushed off the wall. Her eyes were still faintly silver, and her voice, when it came, was hers alone — tired, certain, and aimed up the mountain toward the white spire crowning Highmoor's peak.

"They'll come for us now. All of them. The High Inquisitor won't send soldiers next time." She flexed her fingers like they ached. "Veyra wants the seal broken, and the seal lives in the heart of the Spire itself. So that's where we're going."

Bram looked up at the tower, black against the waking sky.

"Right," he said. "Anyone else think this plan has *heroics* written all over it?"
