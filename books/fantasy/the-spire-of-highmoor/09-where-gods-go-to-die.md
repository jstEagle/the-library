## 9. Where Gods Go to Die

The seal-chamber of the Spire of Highmoor was not what Mara had imagined.

She had imagined something grand — vaulted ceilings, ranks of chanting inquisitors, a throne of black iron. Instead, after three days of smuggler tunnels and one very undignified crawl through a drainage culvert that Bram still hadn't stopped complaining about, they had come up through a servant's stair into a room that looked like nothing so much as a cellar. Low ceiling. Damp stone. Rows of shelves holding jars of pickled things that Mara chose not to examine too closely.

And at the center, sunk into the floor like a wound, the Seal.

It was a circle of white fire, perhaps ten paces across, burning without heat or light — it gave off nothing, only *absence*, a hole in the world shaped like a ring. Through its center Mara could see... nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. A place where even the idea of looking refused to happen.

*Three hundred years,* Veyra said inside her skull, and her voice, usually struck bronze, was quiet now. Quiet the way a person is quiet when they've been walking toward their own grave. *That is where my brothers and sisters have been. Not dead. Worse than dead. Filed away.*

"Vessel." The High Inquisitor stood on the far side of the seal, and up close, without his procession of gray-robed acolytes, he seemed smaller than his reputation. Older, too. His face had the smoothness of a statue worn by rain — features sanded down until almost nothing remained, just two pale eyes and a mouth that had forgotten most expressions. "You came. I confess I hoped you would."

"You burned a child's shrine," Mara said. Her voice shook. She let it. "You burned an old woman who kept a candle lit for her dead son. I watched Isolde carry that woman's rosary for forty miles. So no, I didn't come because you hoped. I came because you're going to stop."

"I am going to stop," the Inquisitor agreed mildly, "one way or another. That is rather the point."

Behind her, Kaelen shifted. She could hear him breathing — the controlled, deliberate breaths of a man keeping himself from drawing steel in a room where drawing steel would get everyone killed. Ferro and the twins were somewhere above, holding the stairwell against acolytes, making enough noise that the whole Spire believed an army had come down the mountain instead of five people and a hedge-witch.

Only Kaelen had come this far with her. He stood three paces behind her right shoulder, exactly where he'd stood through every disaster since the patrol road, and Mara found she couldn't look at him directly. If she looked at him, she would think about the glacier vault, about his hands and the words he'd said half-buried in snow, and she needed every piece of herself sharp for what was coming.

"The seal is failing," the Inquisitor said. He walked along its edge, unhurried, his boots silent on the stone. "It has been failing for years. My predecessors papered over the cracks with blood — god-speakers, mostly. People like you. People like me." He paused. "I was a speaker once, Vessel. Did your goddess tell you that? Before they hollowed me. They took everything out and left obedience, and obedience has kept the world very tidy for a long time."

Mara's stomach turned. *He's telling the truth,* Veyra murmured. *I can taste it. Oaths leave a flavor.*

"So here is my offer," the Inquisitor said, and he turned to face her fully, and for one moment something moved behind those sanded-flat eyes — something old and terribly tired. "The seal needs a keystone. A living will to hold the circle closed. Every god we caught is behind that fire. If one of them takes the keystone's place willingly — if a vessel *ascends*, sits the throne from the inside — the seal becomes self-sustaining. Eternal. No more burnings. No more purges. No more gods waking in the mountains and setting the lowlands afire with miracles and mobs." He spread his hands. "Order, Vessel. Permanent, painless order. You would be the last god. Alone, yes. But alone *forever* is simply another word for safe."

The silence stretched.

And Mara, who had spent her whole life hiding — in hedgerows, in false names, in other people's debts, in the spaces between other people's families — felt the offer slide into her like a key into a lock she hadn't known she had.

*Alone forever.* No more running. No more fear. And power — real power, not Veyra's borrowed flame but her own, vast and calm and *hers*. She could protect them all. Kaelen. Ferro grumbling over his pipe. Isolde and Bram arguing about whose fault the culvert had been. She could stand between them and every Spire, every purge, every burned shrine, forever.

*Say yes,* whispered something that was not Veyra. It sounded like her own voice, aged and smoothed. *You've been alone your whole life. At least this way it would be a choice.*

"Mara." Kaelen's voice, low. Just her name. But the way he said it — the way he'd said it in the vault, raw and terrified and certain — pulled her back like a hand on her collar.

"No," she said aloud.

The Inquisitor tilted his head. "You haven't heard the—"

"I heard it. All of it. Forever and safe and alone." She took a breath that tasted of cold stone and old smoke. "I grew up alone. It wasn't safe. It was just quiet, and quiet isn't the same thing." Her voice steadied as she went, which surprised her. "You want to know what I learned running these mountains? Nobody survives alone. My master survived alone, and he died alone in debt to people who'll never find his body. You hollowed yourself out and called it peace, and you've been standing next to a hole in the world so long you think the hole is normal." She pointed at the seal. "That's not order. That's a mass grave with good bookkeeping."

Something flickered across the Inquisitor's ruined face. Grief, maybe. Or its ghost.

"They murdered you," Mara said, softer. "Your founders murdered the gods and stole their fire, and then they made people like you to guard the theft. And you've been guarding it so long you forgot there was anything to guard *against*." She lifted her chin. "So no. I won't be your last god. I'm not even Veyra's first choice for a vessel — I'm her *reluctant* one, and somehow that's worked better than any of your perfect obedient hollow saints ever did. Because I argue. Because I refuse. Because love isn't orderly, you absolute cellar-dwelling bureaucrat, and neither am I."

Behind her, she heard Kaelen make a sound that was trying very hard not to be a laugh.

The Inquisitor looked at her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Then there's nothing left to discuss."

He raised his hand.

*Now,* said Veyra, and the bronze rang through Mara's bones. *Now, vessel. Break it. Break it NOW—*

"But it will unmake you," Mara said — not to the Inquisitor. To the fire in her own veins. "You told me. Breaking the seal burns out the gift. It might burn out *you*."

*I have been in a jar for three hundred years,* Veyra said. *Do you imagine I am afraid of a little burning? Mara Vasquez. Hedge-witch. Debt-fleer. Reluctant herald, terrible liar, worse singer—*

"That last one is slander—"

*I name you my witness,* the goddess said, and her voice broke open like dawn. *I release you from every vow between us, given or accidental, spoken or stumbled into. Whatever comes next, you owe me nothing. Do you understand? Nothing. Now choose freely, stubborn girl. Choose as yourself.*

*Mara!* Kaelen was moving — the Inquisitor's acolytes flooding down the stair, white fire kindling in the Inquisitor's palm, and Kaelen put himself between her and all of it with his broken sword raised and his whole disgraced heart in his stance. "Whatever you're going to do," he called back to her, "do it fast!"

She reached for the seal.

Not with magic. With hands. Veyra's fire roared up her arms and she planted both palms against the white ring, and it did not burn — it *unmade*, unknitting her skin, her memories, the taste of Ferro's stew, the sound of Isolde laughing, the weight of Bram's arm around her shoulders in the culvert, Kaelen's voice saying her name in the dark—

*Let go,* Veyra whispered. *All of it. Even him. Even me. Let go and be free.*

"No," Mara said, through teeth gritted against eternity. "I'm keeping *those*."

And she wrenched the seal apart.

The world went white. Then it went *loud* — a sound like every bell in creation falling down a staircase, a sound she would later learn had cost her half her hearing, though in that moment she heard nothing at all. The white fire tore outward, and through the tearing poured everything that had been filed away: gods, hundreds of them, three centuries of imprisoned divinity unfurling into the world like banners in a gale. Mara felt them pass through her, around her, *acknowledging* her — a tide of vast, grateful, terrible attention — and then they were gone, up through the Spire's bones, out into the mountains, out into everywhere.

The gift went with them.

She felt it leave — Veyra's warmth, the bronze voice, the constant low companionship that had terrified her and saved her and become, without her permission, the truest friendship of her life. It drained out of her like heat from cooling iron, and with the last of it came one final whisper, small and human-sounding and unbearably fond:

*Well argued, stubborn girl.*

Then silence. Real silence. The first true silence in months, and it was the loneliest sound Mara had ever heard.

She was on the floor. She didn't remember falling. The seal-chamber's shelves had toppled; pickled things were everywhere; the white ring was gone, leaving only a scorched circle and air that tasted clean for the first time in three hundred years.

The High Inquisitor stood in the middle of it, staring at the empty space where his life's purpose had been. He looked, Mara thought distantly, like a man whose house had burned down and who had just realized he'd hated the house.

Kaelen reached her first. He dropped to his knees beside her, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Mara. Mara, look at me. Are you—" His voice cracked straight down the middle. "Talk to me."

"I can't hear you," she said, which wasn't entirely true — she could hear him, faintly, as if through a wall. "Also I appear to be lying in pickle juice."

A wet, ragged laugh escaped him, and then he was holding her, careful of everything, and above them the Spire of Highmoor shuddered on its foundations as three hundred years of stolen order came apart floor by floor — and somewhere overhead, Ferro was bellowing retreat, and Isolde was swearing with impressive creativity, and the gods of the world were walking free.

Mara closed her eyes against Kaelen's shoulder, deaf and magicless and more herself than she had ever been.

"Home?" she asked him, or tried to.

He bent close, lips near her ear so she could catch the shape of the word: "Home."

They climbed toward the noise, and the daylight, and the long way down.
