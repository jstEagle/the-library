## 10. The Long Way Down the Mountain

The world was quieter now, and Mara was learning to live inside the quiet.

Not silence — she still had that much left to her, a thin ribbon of hearing in her right ear, enough to catch Ferro's bellowing from across a campsite and Isolde's laughter when Isolde wanted to be heard. But the mountains had lost their music. No birdsong threading through the pines. No wind singing off the glacier. She had wept about it for two nights in the ruins of Highmoor's lower city, quietly, into Kaelen's shoulder, and then she had stopped, because there were other things to count.

She counted them now, descending the pilgrim's road through Thornfell with the morning fog burning off around them: one Kaelen, walking close on her left so she could see his lips; one Ferro, hauling a pack meant for two mules; two twins, arguing about whose fault the burned stew had been; zero gods in her head; and one horizon, wide and open and entirely unclaimed by anyone.

It was, on balance, the richest inventory of her life.

"You're smiling," Kaelen observed. "That's suspicious. Last time you smiled like that we ended up assaulting a fortress."

"I'm smiling because Bram just tripped over his own boot."

"I did *not* trip," Bram called back without turning around. "I performed a controlled descent. It's a smuggler technique. You wouldn't understand, you're a knight."

"A disgraced knight," Kaelen corrected, with the ease of a man who had said it enough times to make the word fit him like an old coat. "Disgraced. Say all of it."

"Controlled descent," Bram insisted, and then caught his toe on a root anyway, and Isolde laughed loud enough that even Mara caught most of it, bright and sharp as broken glass, and something in Mara's chest unknotted a little further.

They had been walking for six days. The road down out of Thornfell was old — older than the Spire, older than its three hundred years of silence — and the world along it was busy relearning itself. In the village of Harrow's Bend they had passed a well that ran sweet where it had run bitter for a generation. A woman at its edge had been leaving bread on the stones, not for any god in particular, she'd said, just — *in case anyone was listening again.* In case they wanted to know they'd been missed.

Mara had stood there a long time, half-deaf in the morning sun, watching steam rise off fresh bread in the cold air.

*In case anyone was listening.*

Someone always had been. That had been the whole tragedy of it — the Spire hadn't sealed gods away from the world. They'd sealed the world away from its own prayers, and called the silence peace.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a shrine.

It was a small thing, a waystone shrine on the shoulder of the mountain, the kind pilgrims had used before the purges: a stone roof, a stone bench, a niche where offerings could sit. Someone had already been here since the breaking. There were wildflowers jammed into the niche — early, stubborn, mountain flowers — and a child's carved wooden bird, and a strip of cloth with words stitched on it that Mara couldn't read until Kaelen held it near the fire and angled it toward her:

*For Veyra, who kept every promise even when we forgot to make any.*

Mara sat down hard on the stone bench.

Kaelen crouched in front of her. He didn't touch her yet; he'd learned that. First he waited, letting her find the shape of the feeling herself. It was one of a hundred small ways he had changed since Highmoor — the knight's instinct to command giving way, slowly, to something more patient.

"She's really gone," Mara said. Her voice came out flat and strange in her own ears; without the goddess in her head, she heard everything slightly wrong, slightly distant, like speech carried across water. "I keep waiting for the bronze-voice. For the — the weight. I prayed this morning. Just to test it. Nothing." She laughed, and it cracked halfway through. "Three hundred years she waited in the dark under the ice, and I knew her for two months, and I miss her like a limb."

"You gave up her gift," Kaelen said. "You didn't have to. The Inquisitor offered you—"

"I know what he offered me." The memory still came with a chill: the seal-chamber, the old man's hollowed eyes, the throne of quiet order he'd held out to her like a poisoned cup. *Become the last god. Orderly. Alone. Eternal.* "He spent eighty years being exactly what Veyra would have been if she'd won. If power had been allowed to finish eating him. I wasn't going to let it finish eating me." She looked at the little wooden bird in the niche, at the clumsy stitches. "And besides. She asked. Did I ever tell you that? At the end, with the seal cracking and both of us coming apart — she *asked* me. A goddess, begging a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice to break her. Because she wanted to be free more than she wanted to be whole." Mara swallowed. "The least I could do was want the same thing."

Kaelen reached up then and tucked a strand of hair behind her ear, careful of her bad side, careful of everything. "You know," he said, "when I found you on the smuggler's road with divine fire coming off your hands, my professional assessment was: doomed. Immediately and comprehensively doomed."

"And now?"

"Now my professional assessment is—" he glanced at the shrine, at the flowers, at the road going down and down into the green dark of the valley, "—that the Spire taught me to kneel to the wrong things for fifteen years, and I'm never doing it again. Except maybe to you. Occasionally. When you're right."

"I'm always right."

"There it is," he said gravely. "The vessel speaks."

She threw a pinecone at him. He caught it, which was insufferable, and grinned, which was worse.

* * *

Ferro found them there by the fire later, after the twins had turned in, and lowered himself onto the bench beside Mara with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions.

"Heard you talking about the goddess," he said, without preamble, because Ferro did not believe in preamble. "About missing her."

"Some."

"Aye." He turned something over in his big hands — a whetstone, worn smooth as a river pebble. "My mother used to leave salt out. Old custom, from before. Salt for the oath-keeper, so your word would hold weight." He snorted softly. "Spire priests came through our valley when I was nine. Took every grain. Made us watch them pour it in the river. Said superstition rotted a man's soul." His jaw worked. "Took me thirty years to figure out what actually rots a soul."

"Ferro—"

"They're saying the Spire's splitting," he went on, staring into the fire. "You'd know, knight-man won't say much but the twins have ears everywhere. Half the inquisitors walked out after the breaking. Some to make their own little kingdom of rules somewhere else. But some—" he tapped the whetstone against his knee, "—some are asking questions. Real ones. About the vault. About what was written in those records you found. There's talk of trials. Of reparations. Of shrines rebuilt with Spire gold."

"That's not ours to decide," Kaelen said from across the fire. "We broke the seal. What grows in the field afterward isn't the sower's crop anymore."

"No," Ferro agreed. "But somebody's got to plant it." He looked at Mara then, direct and unhurried, the way he looked at a mountain pass before committing the mules to it. "There's folk down-valley already asking for you. Not for the vessel." He shook his head quickly. "For the woman who told the truth about what the Spire did. Word travels. You know how it is."

"I'm half-deaf and magicless," Mara said.

"So am I, most mornings." Ferro stood, joints crackling, and clapped her once on the shoulder — gently, for him, which still nearly toppled her. "Point is, nobody's asking you to be anything. That's the whole point of what you broke, innit? Nobody gets to ask anybody to be anything ever again." He ambled off toward his bedroll, and added, over his shoulder, "Sleep. We've got the long way down tomorrow. And I mean *long*. Bram says there's a shortcut."

"Bram's shortcut cost us four days and a mule last spring," Kaelen called.

"Which is why," said Isolde's voice, muffled from her blankets, "we're taking the *other* shortcut."

* * *

On the ninth day they came down out of the trees.

Mara felt it before she saw it — or rather, she felt the absence of the mountain's pressure, the way Thornfell had loomed behind them for weeks and now simply... let go. The trail opened onto a long grassy shoulder above the valley floor, and below them the lowlands rolled away gold and green in the late light, rivers stitching through farmland, a market town smoking gently with woodsmoke and supper, roads going everywhere, going nowhere in particular, going wherever anyone pleased.

Nobody spoke for a while. Even Bram.

Isolde broke it first, as she broke everything. "Well," she said. "That's it. That's the world. Smaller than advertised."

"Louder than advertised," Bram said. "Look — there's a fair. Do you see banners? That's a fair. We are arriving in a town during a *fair*, Ferro, do you understand what this means, there will be pie, there will be *gambling*—"

"There will be work," Ferro said. "Cargo waiting in Lowmark, if the letter was honest. And if it wasn't, there'll be other cargo. World woke up. World needs moving around."

Kaelen had gone quiet beside Mara. She watched him take off the last of the Spire's black — he'd been shedding pieces of it the whole journey, trading the pauldrons for a padded jerkin, the cloak for wool — and now he unbuckled the sword belt with the Spire's sigil and weighed it in his hands.

Then he set it down on a flat stone at the edge of the trail, hilt facing the mountain, and walked away from it.

He didn't look back. Mara made sure she was looking at his face when he caught up, and he was wearing the expression of a man setting down something heavy after carrying it so long he'd forgotten his shoulders could be light.

"Not disgraced anymore?" she asked.

"Oh, still disgraced." He laced his fingers through hers. "Thoroughly. Permanently. Best thing I ever achieved." He nodded down at the valley, at the fair, at the noise and the pie and the ordinary human clamor of it. "So. Vasquez. No master. No goddess. no vows, no orders, no duty, no debt of salt or smoke." His thumb moved over her knuckles. "What exactly are you, these days?"

Mara considered it. Really considered it — the question she'd been fleeing since the night she'd run from her master's debts into the mountains, the question every god and knight and inquisitor had tried to answer for her: *vessel, herald, weapon, heir, sacrifice.*

Below them, Isolde shouted something about beating Bram to the bridge. Ferro rumbled after them like good-natured landslide. The wind came up the valley smelling of rain and baking bread, and Mara heard almost none of its music and all of its meaning.

"Free," she said. "Just free. And starving. And surrounded by idiots I'd burn down another Spire for."

Kaelen laughed — really laughed, the rare one, the one she'd only begun collecting since Highmoor — and pulled her toward the trail, toward the bridge, toward the noise.

Behind them, on the flat stone at the mountain's edge, someone would eventually find the Spire's black sword and carry news of it back up into the peaks. And in the shrine above, someone would add another flower to the niche, and another stitched scrap of cloth, because the gods were walking the world again on their own terms, and people, being people, would always want to say *thank you* and *we remember* and *don't be strangers.*

But that was the mountain's story now.

Mara went down into the evening with her family, laughing, half-deaf, wholly herself — and the road ahead went everywhere, and none of it belonged to anyone but them.

*The End*
