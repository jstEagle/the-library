## 1. The Date That Wasn't Tomorrow

The dead man's boots were worth more than his life had been.

Jude Nakamura turned one of them over in the lamplight of his workshop, running a thumb along the sole. Thornfell iron-nail stitching, done in the old style, each nail driven at a slight inward angle so the tread would grip wet stone. A mountain man's boots. A careful man's boots — and yet here he was, dead of a fall no one had witnessed on a pass no one could name, and here was Jude, holding his boots and cataloguing everything else for a widow who lived two valleys east and would never know how her husband had actually earned his winters' bread.

"That's the trouble with carelessness," Jude murmured to the empty room. "It only kills you once."

He set the boots by the door with the rest of the returned effects: a coil of good rope, half-burned; a tin whistle dented at the mouthpiece; a knife with a bone handle worn smooth as river stone; three pouches of coin that Jude counted twice and did not skim, because his reputation as an honest broker of dishonest goods was the only inheritance his father had left him that couldn't be confiscated. And then there was the last item, the one he'd saved for the end because it had made the hair on his forearms stand up when he first pulled it from the oilskin wrapping.

A book.

Not a ledger — Jude knew ledgers the way priests knew scripture, could read a smuggler's coded columns in his sleep. Not a journal, either. It was bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, too thin for its width, stitched with thread that caught the lamplight green when it should have been plain flax. No title on the spine. No name inside the front cover. Just paper the color of old cream, dense with ink, and a smell that rose off it like cold air off a cave mouth.

He'd found it sewn into the lining of the dead man's coat, which was the traditional hiding place of exactly one kind of thing: something you didn't want found by the people who killed you.

Jude poured himself a finger of plum brandy, sat down at his drafting table — the great oak slab scarred with twenty years of compass points, where the Thornfell mountains existed in more detail than anywhere else in the world, including the mountains themselves — and opened the book under his best lamp.

The handwriting was small and even, a clerk's hand or a scholar's, though the ink varied strangely from page to page. Some entries were black as tar. Some had faded to the brown of dried blood. Some, impossibly, still looked wet.

It wasn't organized like a book at all. There were no chapters. Entries simply began wherever they began, each headed by a date in the old reckoning, followed by a paragraph or two of prose that read — Jude squinted, turned the page, squinted again — like weather reports written by a madman.

*On this day the third bell of frost will ring twice, and the miller's daughter will not drown, though everyone at the ford will be certain she has.*

*On this day the road beneath the Hanging Stair will forget itself for one hour, and a mule train out of Vesseny will walk into the ravine, all nine animals and both drivers, and no search will find them because the mountain does not wish to be asked.*

*On this day a woman with red mittens will trade her shadow for passage and regret it before the tollgate is out of sight.*

Jude took a slow pull of brandy and laughed, once, without much humor in it.

Prophecy books. Every mountain range bred them the way stagnant water bred midges. Half-mad hermits, hedge-witches, disgraced archivists with more imagination than sense — he'd fished a dozen such volumes out of dead men's coats over the years, and every one of them had proven to be either fraud, delusion, or poetry. The trick was always the same: vague enough to fit anything after the fact. *A woman with red mittens.* Somewhere, some day, some woman would wear red mittens through a tollgate, and someone would dig up this book and gasp.

He flipped forward, idly now, the way you'd leaf through a stranger's letters. The dates marched deeper into the past century. The ink grew older, browner. He was about to close it and add it to the widow's pile — let her sell it to a collector, it might buy her a decent stove —

— and stopped.

His eye snagged on a date the way a boot heel snags on a root. Not because it meant anything at first. Because it *rhymed*. He knew dates the way he knew faces; he'd spent his whole life writing them in the corners of maps, dating surveys, dating deliveries, dating the little coded entries in his own ledger that recorded what moved through which pass on which night. Dates were the grid lines of his existence.

This date was tonight.

He told himself, immediately and firmly, that it was a coincidence of numerals. The old reckoning used different month-names than the lowland calendar; there were only so many combinations of season-day-year, and a book a hundred pages deep was bound to land near one eventually. Near. Not exact.

Except it was exact. Season, day, year. Tonight. Written down in faded brown ink by a hand dead longer than Jude had been alive, on a night that hadn't existed yet when the pen touched paper.

"Right," Jude said aloud, to the lamp, to the room. "And tomorrow I'll wake up with wings."

He should have closed the book. He wants me to say he almost closed the book, and honest as he tried to keep his accounts, he was never honest with himself about that moment afterward. He read the entry.

*On this night, in the hour when the last light fails and the lamps are lit against it, Jude Nakamura opens this book.*

The workshop went very quiet. Outside, wind worried the eaves; somewhere down the valley a dog complained about it. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were reading over his shoulder.

Jude Nakamura. His own name, spelled correctly, which was more than the tax assessor managed. In a book buried in a dead man's coat lining, in ink that predated him.

He laughed. It came out wrong — thin, high, nothing like a laugh — and he hated the sound of it and kept reading anyway, because a mapmaker's first instinct is always the same: *check the source.*

*He will not believe it,* the entry continued, in that same small even hand. *That is the nature of the man. He believes in coastlines and contour lines, in the honesty of distances, in a world that holds still long enough to be drawn. He will hold this book to the lamp looking for the trick — the hidden second hand, the forgery, the coincidence — and he will find none, and this will anger him more than fear him, and he will laugh.*

Jude looked up sharply at the shadows along the rafters, half-expecting a grin there, a listener, some smuggler rival's elaborate joke finally paying out. Nothing. Only the dark between beams, and the wind, and his own heartbeat going faster than he liked.

*He is right to look for the trick,* the entry went on. *There is a trick. But it is not in the book.*

Below that, the ink changed. The older brown gave way to something darker, newer-looking, as if the sentence had been added later — though the page showed no sign of erasure, no gap in the flow, just a seam in the color like a stream joining a river:

*In the same hour, the debt comes due.*

"What debt?" Jude said to the page. His voice sounded foolish. "Whose debt? Whose?"

The book, being a book, said nothing further on the matter. The entry ended. He turned the page and found the next date — tomorrow's date, he realized with a lurch he refused to examine — and beneath it a single line in that newer, darker ink:

*He tells himself it is a forgery. Forgeries can be bought. This cannot.*

Jude shut the book.

He shut it firmly, squared its edges, and set it face-down on the table, and then he stood up and walked around his workshop touching things — the rope, the whistle, the wall of pinned maps, the brass instruments hanging in their oiled cloth — the way a man pats his pockets after a scare, confirming the world still held its shape. Maps covered nearly every surface. The Thornfell in twelve sheets at three scales. The passes in winter. The passes in thaw. The smuggler roads that appeared on no official chart and never would, roads he'd mapped from memory and moonlight over two decades, roads that had fed his family through famine years and buried his parents in bad ones. If there was one truth Jude Nakamura had built his entire life upon, it was this: *the world can be known.* It might hide. It might lie about itself. But it held still for a patient man with a steady hand, and anything that could be measured could be trusted, and anything that could be trusted could not, by definition, turn around and write your name down a hundred years in advance.

The book sat on the table like a hole in that argument.

He made himself tea he didn't want. He inventoried the widow's goods a third time, finding errors in his own sums that he was fairly sure hadn't been there before, which was nonsense, numbers didn't move, that was the whole point of numbers. He ate half a loaf standing up. The light outside the single window went from grey to violet to the deep bruised blue of full dark, and the whole time, some part of him — the part that had found the book sewn into the coat lining and felt his arms prickle — kept counting toward something.

Toward the hour when the last light fails and the lamps are lit against it.

He lit his lamps early out of spite. All four of them, plus the big draftsmen's lantern over the table, flooding the workshop with warm yellow defiance. *There,* he told the book. *Lamps lit. Hour arrived. And I'm fine, and your author was a fraud, and tomorrow I'm riding this rubbish up to the widow and forgetting I ever—*

The ink moved.

He saw it in his peripheral vision first — a flicker, like a moth crossing the lantern — and turned his head slowly, the way you turn toward a sound in a dark house, already knowing, already refusing to know. On the face-down cover of the book, a thin line of green-gold light seeped out from between the pages, brightening as he watched.

Jude picked it up. Opened it. To tonight's page — he hadn't marked it, but it lay open to tonight's page anyway, and he decided not to think about that.

The entry had grown.

*In this hour,* the new words read, the ink still glistening, still settling into the paper as though freshly written, *the last light having failed and the lamps being lit, Jude Nakamura reads these words and disbelieves them.*

He looked at the lamp. At the window, black as spilled pitch. At the clock on his shelf — his father's clock, the one reliable thing in a childhood of unreliable things — whose hands stood at the precise turning of evening, the minute when daylight officially surrendered and every house in the valley struck its wicks.

The ink finished drying as he watched. The letters settled, became ordinary, became permanent. Became *past tense.*

"No," Jude said quietly. Conversationally, almost, as if correcting a colleague over a survey table. "No, see, that's not how time works. Time is a road. I've walked it my whole life. You don't get to come back down it and paint new scenery."

The book offered no rebuttal. Books never do; that was supposed to be their virtue.

He checked for the trick. He genuinely did — held every page to the lantern looking for palimpsest, scraped layers, invisible inks revealed by heat, the double-writing frauds he'd seen unmasked in border towns. He weighed the paper in his hand and gauged its age by feel, and the feel was wrong in a way he had no vocabulary for: old as the hills and new as this breath, both at once, like a river that is ancient and also made entirely of moments happening now. He examined the binding thread that glinted green and could not decide if it was flax or wire or grass from some field that grew nowhere.

No trick. Or none he could find. Which, per the book's own infuriating footnote, was not the same thing as no trick at all.

Jude sat down heavily in his drafting chair, in the yellow island of lamplight, with the mountains he had given his life to charting spread across the walls around him, and admitted the thing he would spend the next several days taking back and admitting again, over and over, like a wound that wouldn't stay closed:

Someone had mapped him.

Not his house, not his routes, not his habits — those any careful enemy could learn. *Him.* The interior country. The hour of his disbelief, the shape of his denial, the laugh that would come out wrong. Someone or something had drawn the interior Jude Nakamura with a surer hand than he'd ever drawn the Thornfell, and had done it a century ago, and had left the drawing in the coat of a dead man for him to find on the one night it would matter.

And beneath it all, in darker ink, in words that had not been there an hour ago and might not stay where they were: *the debt comes due.*

He got up, fetched the brandy bottle, and drank directly from it — a thing he hadn't done since his father's funeral — staring at the book across the table the way you stare at a pass you must cross knowing it's held by bandits.

"Fine," he said at last, hoarse. "Debt. Due. Whose, and to whom, and for what — because I swear on every map I ever drew, I owe no one. I keep clean books. Ask anyone."

The book's pages riffled once, gently, in air that was not moving, and settled open to a place deep in the middle of the volume. Jude leaned across the table against his better judgment, which had taken the rest of the evening off.

One word, in the newest ink, still shining wet:

*Grandmother.*

The lamp nearest the window guttered out, though the window was shut, and the wind outside went suddenly, completely still — the held-breath silence of the mountains at midnight, of the deep places between peaks, of the Court Beneath the Mountain waking up.

Jude Nakamura reached for his coat, and his keys, and the ledger of roads that existed on no other map in the world.

* * *

He was three miles up the night road toward his grandmother's old homestead before he let himself wonder why he was hurrying.

If the book was a fraud, there was no rush. If it was true — well. Then hurrying changed nothing either. Debts a century old did not mind another dawn.

But his hands knew what his mind was still denying: that the ink had shifted to match the hour, that his name had been waiting in the dark for him like a trapline, and that somewhere ahead, in a house that had stood empty for eleven years, an answer was already waiting — old, signed, and sealed.

Behind him, far below in the valley, his workshop lamp burned on over an open book whose pages were, at that very moment, quietly filling with the account of a man riding into the mountains at midnight, exactly as written.

The last line of the entry dried as the distance widened:

*He goes to her house. She is not there. What she left there has been waiting longer than he has been alive.*
