## 3. The Grandmother's Ledger

The person alive who might know what his grandmother had signed away turned out to be dead.

Jude learned this at half past nine the next morning, standing in the doorway of Marla Quist's herb shop on Coppersmith Row while she wrung her apron in her hands and told him, with the particular gentleness people reserve for delivering news they've already rehearsed, that Old Iyo Nakamura had passed in the spring. Quietly, in her sleep, up at the house above Thornfell's southern shoulder. The whole valley had come out for it, Marla said. Beautiful service. He'd sent no flowers because he'd been three hundred miles away running silk past a border checkpoint, and no one had thought to send word to a man whose trade depended on not being findable.

He thanked her. Bought a bundle of dried feverfew he didn't need. Walked out into the street and stood there in the ordinary morning noise of Lowmarket — cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, two children chasing a dog — and felt the strange double exposure of grief arriving months late, layered over fear that had arrived early. His grandmother was dead. The book had known tonight's date. Somewhere between those two facts, he understood, lay the shape of his problem.

There was only one thing left to do, then. The thing he should have done the moment he'd read his own name in a dead man's book.

He went home, packed light, and rode north into the mountains his maps had made famous — or infamous, depending on which customs office you asked — to the house where he'd grown up, and where his grandmother had lived alone for eleven years, keeping her own counsel the way she kept everything: carefully, and behind locks nobody knew existed.

* * *

The house sat on its shelf of granite like a thing grown rather than built, stone roofed with turf, chimneys breathing thin smoke into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Jude had inherited it outright — papers filed, taxes paid through a solicitor in Greyhollow who never asked questions — but he hadn't been back since the funeral he'd missed. Standing before the door with his hand on the latch, he felt what he always felt returning to any place after long absence: the sense of walking into a map drawn by someone else.

Inside smelled of cedar smoke and dried lavender and, beneath both, the faint mineral coldness of the mountain itself. Everything was where she had left it. Kettle on its hook. Her reading chair angled toward the window that looked down the long fall of the valley. A half-finished embroidery frame, threads still threaded, as if she'd simply stepped out to check the weather and the weather had kept her.

He made himself tea because his hands wanted occupation, drank it standing up, and then began to search.

Not wildly. Methodically. His grandmother had taught him methodical. *When you hide something, Jude-love, you don't pick the clever place. Clever places are the first places clever people look. You pick the boring place, the place so dull it becomes invisible.* She had hidden sweets from him this way when he was small — inside the flour tin, under the salt pork, once memorably inside a hollowed-out turnip — and he had grown into a man who found things for a living by remembering every lesson she'd ever taught him about concealment, including the ones she'd thought were lessons about sweets.

So he did not search the study. He searched the kitchen. He searched the pantry, tapping along shelves until, behind the third jar of preserved plums — the jar she'd never served, the one with dust on its shoulder like a glove worn on purpose — his knuckle came back hollow.

The wall panel swung inward on oiled hinges that had been maintained, recently and lovingly. Of course they had. She'd have checked them the way other old women checked their pulse.

Beyond the panel, a narrow room the width of a coffin and twice as deep, lined floor to ceiling with shelves. And on those shelves — Jude stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the lamp-light settle — the record of a life conducted like a smuggling run.

Ledgers. Dozens of them, bound in grey board, spines labeled in her small upright hand by year. He knew her ledgers; she'd shown him the household ones proudly, the columns of eggs and wool and candle-tallow, the family accounts balanced to the copper. These were not those. These were the others. The true ones.

He pulled the earliest volume and carried it out to the kitchen table where the light was better, and opened it, and read his family's history written in the language of debts.

* * *

It began, as famines do, with rain that didn't come.

The first pages were her hand at twenty-three years old, younger than he was now, the ink already urgent: grain prices climbing week over week. The blight coming down out of the high passes like weather. Lists — his great-grandparents, their children, the names of neighbors — each name followed by sums lent, sums lost, sums begged. He watched famine approach through the medium of arithmetic, which was somehow worse than watching it through prose. Numbers didn't flinch.

Then, forty pages in, the handwriting stopped being hers.

Or rather — he leaned closer, held the page to the lamp — it was her hand, but slower, pressed hard into the paper, each letter carved rather than written, as though the pen had been fighting her even as she guided it. And the ink was wrong. Not iron-gall brown, not walnut, none of the homely inks he knew from her household books. It was red. Deep red, gone almost black with age, and it had a texture ink shouldn't have, a faint raised quality, like a scar.

The entry read:

*Bargain struck this night with the Honored Magistrate of Debts, in the chamber beneath, in the presence of the Witness. Terms as follows: the Court shall preserve the lives of the Nakamura line through the famine winter — seed grain, meal, medicine, and the silence of the sickness — and in exchange the undersigned does pledge payment against the account of her bloodline, said payment to be rendered when the Court shall call, in the coin the Court prefers.*

Below that, a signature. *Iyo Nakamura,* in that carved, struggling hand.

And below that, pressed beside the signature where a seal would go, the print of a thumb — small, feminine, and unmistakably a print made in blood, because the whorls had ridges of a darker red at their edges where the excess had bled outward into the paper.

Jude set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

He told himself it could be anything. Dyes faded strangely. Old contracts had theater to them, ceremony, farmers signing with marks and drops of wax. He told himself this the way a man tells himself a bridge will hold while he is already crossing it.

Then he turned the page, and found the letter.

It had been folded in thirds and tucked into the binding's gutter, sealed with plain wax, and addressed on the outside in the normal brown ink of her everyday hand: *For Jude, when the red book finds him.* Not *if*. When. As if even the finding had been scheduled.

He broke the seal with a thumb that was not entirely steady, and unfolded paper gone soft and ivory with age, and read his grandmother's voice.

---

*Jude-love,*

*If you are reading this, then the Court has opened your account, which means you've seen the book, or something like it, and I am sorry — I have been sorry since before you drew breath, which is a peculiar thing to say and I mean it anyway.*

*I was twenty-three and the winter was eating us. Your grandfather's cough had gone to fever. Your mother was nine and hungry enough that she'd begun chewing pine bark, and I want you to understand something clearly, because everything else I have to say depends on it: I would do it again. That is not an apology. Hold on to that. Whatever else you take from this letter, hold on to that first.*

*They came up out of the mountain the way water comes — quietly, and filling every low place. The Magistrate did not look as I expected. He looked kind. I think now that kindness is the most terrible thing about them, that they wear it the way we wear coats, for warmth, and it fits them too well. He offered me terms and I signed them in my own blood because he explained, patiently, that blood was the only ink that binds a soul-account, and I was too frightened and too proud to ask what a soul-account was.*

*A sliver, Jude. That is what I sold. Not yours — you weren't born, you were barely a hope — but theirs, meaning yours, meaning the line, meaning you, because that is how their arithmetic works. One part in some number I couldn't count, cut from the portion of you that wasn't yet real, and held against our debt. They let me feel it happen. That was the price of understanding, and I paid it. It felt like having a tooth pulled from a mouth I didn't know I had. There has been a quiet place in me ever since. You may have wondered why your grandmother sometimes went somewhere in the middle of conversations and didn't come back for a minute. That's where I go. That's what's missing.*

*Here is what I can tell you that might save you, if anything can.*

*They cannot take. They can only be owed. Everything in that mountain runs on consent — twisted, trapped, tricked, but consent all the same. Read anything they put in front of you three times. Refuse nothing loudly and agree to nothing softly. And remember that a debt, even theirs, is a story about numbers, and stories can be miscounted by the teller. I saw the founding ledger once, when I delivered my signature. I was not supposed to see it and I only glimpsed a corner of a page, and what I saw made no sense to me then and has made a terrible kind of sense ever since. Theirs is the oldest account in the mountain. Ask yourself sometime, love, what a creature made entirely of collecting could possibly owe.*

*I kept the payments current. Every year on the eve of first frost I left a tithe at the boundary stone — bread and salt and a drop of my blood, token rent on the token piece — and for fifty-one years they did not call. I thought perhaps they never would. Perhaps I hoped myself into believing it. But interest compounds in the dark, and the dark under Thornfell has had a long time to work.*

*I am dead now, or will be when you read this, and I find I am not afraid of that. I am afraid only that you'll hate me, and that the hate will make you foolish, and that you'll go down there angry instead of careful. So: be angry afterward. Be furious at me for the rest of your life if you must. But on the road, be careful. Grief makes bad maps. I know you'll want proof of my love to carry alongside the proof of my betrayal, and I haven't the right to offer it, but here it is anyway: I named you. Did you know that? Your parents wanted to call you after your father's side. I argued for Nakamura, for my husband's name, my line, the name on the contract — because I thought, in the fog of those years, that if the debt must have a face, better it wear a name I chose than one assigned by chance. I gave you my enemy's ledger-name to keep you mine. I don't know if it helped. I don't know if anything helped. I know I sang to your mother while she grew, and she sang to you, and somewhere in all that singing is the part of you they never managed to put on paper.*

*That part is yours. Keep it.*

*Burn this if it eases you. Burn me too, in effigy, in memory, whatever the ritual requires. Only come back up the mountain after, whoever you are when you finish.*

*With everything I have left, which is more than they think,*

*Your grandmother,*
*Iyo*

---

Jude read it twice. Then a third time, standing at the kitchen table with the lamp burning low and the mountain pressing its shoulder against the north wall of the house, close enough to hear, or to imagine hearing, the slow grinding of stone against stone far beneath the floors.

Grief and betrayal did not arrive separately, as he'd somehow expected they would. They braided. That was the only word for it — strands twisting tighter with every line, love and fury wound into a single cord that pulled somewhere behind his sternum, and at the center of the knot, absurdly, a memory: himself at six years old, sitting on this very table's edge while she measured him against a doorframe, her thumb brushing his hair back from his forehead, saying, *there's my boy, growing like a ledger,* and laughing at her own joke, and him not understanding it, and understanding it now, sixty seconds too late and thirty years too late, forever too late.

She had weighed him. All those years. She had stood him against the doorframe and marked the pencil lines and privately measured the collateral.

He sat down. He found he had been holding the letter hard enough to crease it, and smoothed it flat against the wood with both palms, an apology of sorts.

Outside, the light had gone the bruised purple of early evening. Inside, the kettle ticked as it cooled. Ordinary sounds. He clung to them the way a climber clings to good rock.

Then the lamp flame bent — though no door was open, no window unshuttered — and every shadow in the kitchen leaned, just slightly, toward the north wall.

Toward the mountain.

Jude looked down at the open ledger, at the blood-print seal drying these fifty years into a small dark flower, and heard his own voice say aloud, to the empty room, in the flat tone of a man confirming coordinates: "Tonight."

Because he had counted. In the third year of the ledgers, in her hand again, steadier now, resigned: *Tithe paid, eve of first frost.* And the eve of first frost, in the high valleys, was reckoned not by the calendar but by the mountain itself — by the first night frost silvered the turf-roof houses along the shoulder. He stepped to the window, wiped the glass with his sleeve, and looked out at the roof of the house across the valley, where the last hour of daylight was laying down a thin grey rime like breath on a mirror.

First frost. Tonight.

The book in Greyhollow had given the date. The ledger gave the terms. And somewhere below him, patient as geology, the Court Beneath the Mountain had opened its books and found his name at the top of a column his grandmother had started writing before he was born.

Jude folded the letter along its old creases and put it inside his coat, over his heart, where smugglers keep the documents that matter. Then he took the ledger, and a blank map-sheet, and his satchel of pens, because whatever happened next, he intended to chart it — and went to the door to watch the frost come down.

Behind him, in the hidden room, the remaining volumes of fifty-one years of payments sat in the lamplight, and on the spine of the newest one, in his grandmother's last steady hand, he would later swear he could just make out four words he had not noticed before:

*Paid in part. Pending.*
