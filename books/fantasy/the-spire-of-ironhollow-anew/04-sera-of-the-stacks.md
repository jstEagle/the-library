## 4. Sera of the Stacks

The archive beneath Thornfell's surface station had no windows, which was the point. The Court Beneath the Mountain kept its records where sunlight could not reach them — not out of secrecy, exactly, but out of preference. Paper remembered better in the dark. Names held their shapes longer. And a name, once it held its shape, could be called back.

Sera Vess had catalogued eleven thousand of those names in her forty years below.

She knew them all by their weight.

This one was heavier than most.

She stood at the reading table in the third gallery, where the prophecy ledgers were kept under iron clasps and older oaths, and she did not touch the book. Not yet. Touching came last, after you had looked with your whole attention, because the Court's books noticed hands before they noticed eyes. A careless fingertip on the wrong page could register as intent, and intent was currency down here, and Sera had been poor for forty years without ever letting herself go into debt again.

The ledger lay open to the page that had arrived that morning from the surface collection point at Duskfall Station — a dead smuggler's effects, logged in by the customs-wardens, sorted by clerks who could not read what they carried, and finally routed to her because routing was her office and her burden and, for forty years, her cage.

A century-old book of prophecies. Common enough. The Court collected them the way magpies collect tin: not for use, but because they shone.

But this one had a signature.

Not an author's signature. Prophecy books rarely bothered with authors; the words came through people like water through a pipe, and pipes don't sign their work. This was a *witness* signature — the small, looping mark a Court scribe set beside any entry the Court itself had authored or amended. Sera had seen it perhaps six times in four decades. It belonged to the First Hand, the founding scribe, the one whose ink was said to be mixed with the mountain's stolen blood.

It should not have appeared in a book written ninety years after the First Hand died.

Unless the First Hand had never died. Unless the First Hand had simply stopped being counted among the living, which in Ironhollow was a different thing entirely.

And unless the entry it signed was this one:

*Jude Nakamura. Smuggler-cartographer. The debt called due upon the night of the waning moon, when the tollgate opens at duskfall.*

Tonight's date. Tonight's hour, if you read the marginal notation, which Sera had read three times and which had not changed between readings, though Court ink changed constantly when it wished to torment you. This ink was settled. Finished. A sentence already served, waiting only for the debtor to arrive at his own cell door and knock.

Sera pressed her palms flat against the cold table and made herself breathe the way the archivists were taught: in for seven, hold for four, out for eleven, so the heart slowed and the mind went smooth as still water. Because there was a rule. There was always a rule. The Court ran on rules the way the mountain ran on stone, and hers — the one carved into her indenture when she was nineteen years old and foolish and desperate enough to sign anything that promised her mother's medicine — was simple:

*An archivist who removes a record from the archive forfeits her account.*

Her account. Her soul, in the plain tongue. The sliver of herself that had been pledged when she signed, held in trust against her service, released only upon completion of term — and her term, she had calculated a thousand times, would outlive her mortal half by sixty years. She had broken the rule once. Once, twenty-two years ago, when a farmer's daughter from the surface valleys had come down as collateral for her father's debts, seventeen years old and crying so quietly it broke something in Sera that had never healed right. Sera had copied the girl's indenture page and slipped the copy into an outgoing parcel, and the girl's family had bought her free within the season, and the Magistrate of Debts had called Sera to his office and smiled at her with terrible warmth and said, *I know, of course. I let you do it. Consider it an experiment in loyalty.*

He had let her. He had watched her do it and said nothing, because a traitor he knew about was worth more than a servant he didn't.

She had never tried again. Twenty-two years. She had told herself it was prudence. Some nights, alone in her cell above the stacks, she admitted it was cowardice wearing prudence's coat.

And now a book lay open in front of her with a dead man's belongings around it and a living man's name in it, and the First Hand's signature gleaming wetly as if the ink had dried five minutes ago instead of ninety years, and Sera Vess understood — with the particular clarity that comes only when the trap has already closed — that she was going to break the rule a second time.

Because she had read the rest of the entry.

*The debt descends through blood. The grandmother bargained; the grandson pays. One shard, held in trust since before his birth, redeemable only at the seat of the Court.*

She had checked the reliquary registry afterward. Cross-referenced. Her hands had shaken, and she had let them, because no one watched an archivist shake — it was as unremarkable as breathing. Shard 4471-N: one-eighth portion, human origin, acquired by bargain, donor lineage Nakamura. Held in the vault beneath the chandelier hall for ninety-three years.

Ninety-three years. Jude Nakamura was — she consulted the smuggler's shipping manifest, also among the effects — thirty-one.

The arithmetic was obscene and it was ordinary. That was the worst of it. Half the glass in the reliquary held pieces taken from people too young to know they'd been spent. The nobles wore them at court functions, strung on silver wire, catching the light of iron chandeliers, and they called them *investments*, and they called the wearing *custody*, and the language was so old and so polished that no one down here flinched anymore.

Sera flinched. She had never quite learned not to. It was the half of her that had come from the sunlit side, the mortal half her fae father had left in her like a coal banked under ash — the part that remembered weather, and bread, and her mother's voice saying her name not as property but as a gift.

She looked at the page a long moment more. Then she took out her knife.

Not to cut the page. You never cut a Court book; the books bled information back to their masters. Instead she cut along the spine's false channel — every century-old binding in the collection had one, a relic of the scribes' smuggling days, when even the Court's own clerks had hidden pages from each other — and eased loose the single leaf that sat behind the prophecy, a leaf the cataloguers had never flagged because it appeared blank.

It wasn't blank. It was a receipt. The original pawn-ticket for shard 4471-N, in her grandmother's hand— no. In *his* grandmother's hand. In Miriam Nakamura's hand, signed in blood, witnessed by the First Hand.

The same hand that signed the prophecy. The scribe who wrote the bargains had written the foretelling. Which meant the prophecy was not a prediction at all.

It was an invoice, sent ninety years early, to make sure the debt felt inevitable.

Sera sat very still while that understanding moved through her like cold water through a pipe. *Inevitable.* That was the Court's oldest trick, the one under all the other tricks. You could fight a thief. You could argue with a creditor. But nobody fought fate; fate had already won by the time you heard about it. Make a man believe his loss was written before his birth, and he'd walk to his own surrender carrying the book himself, grateful for the directions.

Unless someone showed him the machinery.

Unless someone put the receipt in his hand and said: *this is not destiny, it is paperwork, and paperwork can be stolen, forged, or burned.*

She wrapped the leaf in oilcloth and slid it inside the lining of her coat, over her heart, where it beat against her own borrowed time. Then she took the prophecy book itself, weighed it in her hands — heavy, heavier than paper should be, heavy with everything it had eaten — and set it back in its cradle on the shelf, square and undisturbed, because taking the book would alert the wards and taking nothing would not. An archivist's theft should look like absence, not action. Let the ledger sit there smug and complete, missing only the one leaf no one knew existed.

Let them discover the gap in ten years. By then she intended to be somewhere with weather.

She walked out through the third gallery, past the cases of names, past the shelf where her own indenture slept in its iron box, and she did not say goodbye to any of it. Goodbye was a claim. Claims were traceable.

* * *

The surface station at Duskfall was the Court's front porch: a customs house dressed up as a waystation, where goods and names passed both directions and everything going down was weighed twice. Sera had leave to travel to it twice a year, to collect surface acquisitions, and her leave was genuine — that was the beauty of doing treason inside your own duties. No gatekeeper questions a woman walking out the door she is assigned to walk out of.

Above ground, the evening was doing what evenings did in the Thornfell foothills: pouring gold light down the valleys until the whole world looked freshly minted. Sera stood on the customs house steps and let it hit her face and had to close her eyes. Forty years. Forty years since full daylight, and it still undid her, still made her throat hurt, still felt less like light than like forgiveness.

The dead smuggler's remaining effects sat in a bonded crate awaiting auction: tools, a coil of good rope, a folding map case stamped with the mark of a man named Nakamura — the nephew, the manifest said, who'd inherit. The wardens had already forwarded the interesting item, the book, down to her archive. They thought they were being diligent.

They had delivered the weapon to the armory of the person least loyal to the Court in the entire mountain.

Sera signed for the crate with her office seal, loaded it onto a handcart, and wheeled it out past the tollgate — where the duskfall warden gave her the usual bored nod, because archivists carried nothing anyone wanted, being merely keepers of what everyone wanted — and turned east, toward the lowland road, toward the tavern towns where smugglers drank and grieved and divided the estates of their dead.

She had never met a smuggler she liked. They were liars with excellent memories, the whole tribe of them, and she had spent her life among beings who lied beautifully and remembered nothing. But she needed one now. She needed one who trusted maps above men, who laughed at prophecy, who would take one look at a pawn-ticket bearing his own family name and feel the ground tilt — and then, being a smuggler, start looking for the route around it.

She needed him to do what no one inside the Court could do: walk in from outside, unrecorded, unaccounted, a variable the ledger hadn't priced.

And she needed him not to know, yet, how much she wanted this for herself. That confession could come later, if it had to come at all. Desperation was a bad opening bid.

The leaf shifted against her chest as she walked, oilcloth whispering. Ninety years old, that scrap of blood-signed paper, and warm as if it had just left a living hand.

Somewhere down the eastern road, in a tavern smelling of tar and spilled ale, a man was learning tonight that his name was in a book. Sera quickened her pace. If she reached him before fear did, she might have an ally.

If fear reached him first, she'd have a fugitive — which, she supposed, walking west with stolen paper over her heart and the gold light dying behind her, made two of them either way.
