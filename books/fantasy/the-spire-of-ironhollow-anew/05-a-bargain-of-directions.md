## 5. A Bargain of Directions

*Sera*

The surface world was too bright. That was the first thing Sera had forgotten, and the first thing that came back to her as she climbed out of the Thornfell's western passes into late afternoon light — the way the sun lay on everything like an accusation, leaving no shadowed corner for a person to hide in. In the Court Beneath the Mountain, light was a courtesy, rationed and warm. Here it simply fell, indifferent, on dust and road-mud and the crooked sign of a tavern called the Drowned Lantern, and it illuminated her completely: a woman in a gray traveling cloak with a stolen page pressed flat against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

Three days she had walked. Three days of following the prophecy's coordinates — because of course the Court wrote its oracles in cartographic language, latitude and bearing, a fix that could be plotted. She had spent forty years among the stacks learning to read such things. She had never expected to read one that pointed *out*.

The page against her chest rustled though there was no wind. It wanted to go home. Stolen things in Ironhollow always knew their way back; that was why theft from the Court was punished so severely, and why no one had ever thought to guard against an archivist who had spent four decades memorizing which bindings muted a page's longing and which wax sealed it. She had wrapped the prophecy leaf in oilcloth and consecrated linen, and still it murmured against her ribs at night like a homesick child.

*Find him,* she told it silently. *You named him. You called him due. So show me where he drinks.*

The tavern door was propped open with a boot-scraper shaped like a leaping trout. Inside: pipe smoke, tallow smoke, the smell of spilled ale gone sour in the seams of old wood. A dozen men arguing over freight rates. And in the corner by the window, a lean man with ink-stained fingers bent over a leather folio, drawing with the particular economy of someone who drew maps the way other men breathed — without thinking about whether they should be doing it at all.

Jude Nakamura. The name in the book. The debt walking around in a body, drinking small beer and pretending to work while his eyes kept flicking, every few minutes, toward the satchel at his feet.

Sera had imagined this meeting a hundred times across three days of walking. In most versions she was eloquent. In some she was armed.

She crossed the room, set the oilcloth bundle on his table, and unwrapped it just enough to show him the first line of text — the line with his name in it, in ink that was still, faintly, wet.

Jude Nakamura went very still. Not the stillness of surprise. She recognized that difference; she had catalogued it. This was the stillness of a man who had already been surprised, thoroughly, and had spent the interval since building fortifications against a second assault.

"You're the second stranger this week to put that thing on my table," he said. His voice was flat and pleasant and entirely without welcome. "The first one bought me a drink and told me my account had been opened. I'd ask if you two share a tailor, but hooded figures don't strike me as the tailoring type."

"I don't want your coin," Sera said. "I want—"

"Everyone wants something. Sit down anyway." He kicked a chair out with one boot, not looking up from his map. "Because you walked in past three people who would rob you blind, carrying whatever that is wrapped like a relic, and you made a straight line for me. Which means either you're very good or very stupid, and stupid people don't survive the Thornfell passes alone. So sit, talk fast, and understand that I've already decided not to believe you."

She sat. She laid her hands flat on the table, empty, palms up — an archivist's habit, showing a text uncorrupted.

"My name is Sera. Forty years ago — I was younger; time runs differently down there — I ran from the Court Beneath the Mountain. They let me keep my life and take a position in their archive because I was useful, and because a runaway on the surface is a better lure than a prisoner underground. I am the lure, Mr. Nakamura. I have been the bait on this hook since before you were born."

"Charming," Jude said. "And the hook?"

"The hook is a sliver of your soul. Your grandmother pawned it before your father could walk. The debt has matured. The Court has opened your account, and tonight—" she tapped the page, "—tonight is written here."

"Tonight." He finally looked up, and his eyes were the brown of strong tea, dry and skeptical. "You know what I do for a living? I chart the unmapped. Every smuggler road through the Thornfell, every goat track, every cave that breathes. My maps are true because I check them against the ground twice, sometimes three times. Truth is a craft, lady. Prophecy is a rumor with good penmanship."

"Then prove it," Sera said quietly. "Prove your craft. You said your maps are true because you verify them. So verify."

He frowned. "Verify what?"

"*Yourself.*"

* * *

*Jude*

He should have thrown her out. He told himself that later, many times, in the dark stretches of what came after: *I should have thrown her out, and instead I did the test.*

But that was unfair to himself, because by then he had already done smaller tests alone, at night, with a candle. The book had known things. He'd spent two days cross-referencing it against his own records — the coded ledgers every smuggler-cartographer keeps, dates and deliveries and disasters — and the prophecies matched. Not vaguely. *Exactly.* The storm that killed the Corveras' mule train. The year the mine at Kettledeep flooded. His parents' deaths, entered in a century-old hand with a precision that made his stomach fold in on itself: *the bridge at Rillwater, autumn, both lost, the son survives to carry the debt.*

So when the woman said *verify yourself*, he didn't ask what she meant. That was the worst part. He already knew.

He turned his tankard aside and looked at the window.

The Drowned Lantern's glass was old and wavy, the kind that bends candlelight into ghosts. His reflection sat across from him in it, as reflections do. Except—

Except it arrived late.

It was a small thing. A blink out of rhythm — his eyes shut, and the man in the glass still watching for half a heartbeat before his lids came down. Jude shifted his hand on the table. The reflected hand followed a fraction behind, like a dancer who had learned the steps from a description rather than a teacher.

"Candlelight," he said. "Wavy glass. Refraction—"

"Look at your shadow," Sera said. "By the hearth. Don't turn your head. Just look."

He looked.

His shadow lay stretched along the floorboards toward the fire, and it was *drifting*. Not moving — shadows moved, that was their nature — but drifting the way a boat drifts when the anchor has quietly come loose. Half an inch left. Back. Left again. A slow tidal wander, patient as interest accruing. And as he watched, the shadow's edges blurred and reformed, and for one sickening instant it was not his shape at all but something smaller, hunched, wearing the outline of a shawl.

His grandmother's shawl. He knew its shape the way he knew the coastlines he'd drawn a hundred times.

Jude stood up too fast. His chair barked against the floor. The reflection in the window stood a full heartbeat after he did, and he saw it happen, saw himself arrive late to his own body, and something cold slid down his spine and pooled at the base of it.

"What's happening to me?" He meant it to sound like a challenge. It came out like a question a drowning man asks the shore.

"The shard is being drawn home," Sera said. "Slowly. The Court doesn't seize; they *collect*. Interest, Mr. Nakamura. Your reflection lags because part of what makes it you isn't yours anymore. Your shadow drifts because a soul-shard is what anchors a shadow to its owner. Yours is thinning. In a few weeks you'll stop casting one at all. In a month, the lag will spread. First your reflection. Then your memories start arriving late — you'll taste tea before you drink it. Then you'll say sentences a moment before you decide to. And then one morning you'll wake up punctual and perfect and *empty*, and you'll walk to Ironhollow on your own legs, smiling, to complete the collection."

The tavern noise went on around them — freight rates, weather, a fiddler tuning — and none of it touched the little frozen territory of their table.

"And you?" Jude said at last, sitting back down slowly, deliberately, gripping the arms of the chair. "What's your angle? Nobody crosses a mountain to deliver bad news for free."

"My angle is that I have run once before, and running wasn't enough, and I have spent forty years filing the evidence of what the Court does with collected souls." Her voice stayed level, but something under it trembled like a struck string. "They wear them. Did you know that? Collected souls become jewelry. Ornaments. Conversation pieces at parties. There is a noble in Ironhollow who wears a fisherman's last summer as a brooch. I have *catalogued* him." She stopped. Breathed. "I can't break them from inside. Believe me, I've tried — I tried forty years ago and it cost me everything I had left. But you're an outsider. Outside is the one direction the Court cannot see. Their whole power is built on accounts, and accounts require the debtor to come *in*."

"So I'm not a client. I'm a delivery."

"You're a key," Sera said. "Keys get used. I won't pretend otherwise. But keys also choose which lock."

Jude laughed — short, bitter, real. "You rehearsed that."

"Forty years."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she let him, and what he saw was exhaustion wearing competence like a borrowed coat. What she saw, though she couldn't have known it, was a man doing what he always did with terrain he didn't trust: mapping it. Weighing approaches. Marking hazards.

"Here's my problem with you," Jude said. "Every word you've said might be true, and it doesn't matter, because I have no way to check it. I deal in verifiable ground. You're offering me unverifiable sky. So we don't do trust. Trust is for people with time. We do terms."

"Terms," Sera repeated. Something in her face eased, almost imperceptibly — the relief of a negotiator offered solid ground.

"You know the Court. Its roads, its rules, its weaknesses. I know the Thornfell better than anything alive, including, I suspect, the mountain itself. You get my maps. I get your knowledge. Neither of us gives an inch more than the bargain requires. And—" he leaned forward, and now the smuggler looked out through his eyes, cold and precise, "—if I find out at any point that you're leading me into a cage, I will leave you somewhere in the deep passes where even the Court's collectors don't file paperwork. Are we agreed?"

"We're agreed," Sera said. "One amendment. When we reach the tollgate at Duskfall, they will ask *me* for payment, and it will not be coin. Whatever happens there — whatever I tell you to do, or not do — you follow my instruction exactly. Can you promise that?"

"I can promise to consider it with great seriousness."

"That's the best offer anyone's ever given me down there." Almost a smile. "Agreed."

He pulled his folio across the table and opened it, and began — right there amid the pipe smoke and the fiddler's tuning — to draw. Not the false trails and smugglers' misdirections he sold to clients. The true map. The deep roads. The ones that existed only in his head and in a cipher only he could read, drawn now in plain strokes for a stranger he'd met four minutes ago.

Sera watched his hand move and felt something she hadn't felt in forty years: the sensation of holding something true.

"One condition of mine," she said. "The book. The prophecy. Before we go any further — read me tonight's entry. All of it. I need to know what we're walking into."

Jude's pen paused. He reached into his coat and brought out the dead client's volume, bound in cracked green leather, and opened it to the marked page. He read aloud, voice flat as a surveyor's:

"*The debtor descends with the archivist. The roads will forget him. At the toll of duskfall, a name will be demanded and a lie will save what truth cannot. A shadow will make an offer in a familiar face. The vault opens to a thief the Court has already counted.*"

Silence. The fiddler, badly tuned, scraped into a jig.

"'The roads will forget him,'" Sera said softly. "Mr. Nakamura — how attached are you to your maps?"

Jude closed the book with a snap and drained his beer.

"Ask me again tomorrow," he said, "when I find out."

Outside the Drowned Lantern's warped windows, the sun was going down over the Thornfell, and far beneath its roots, in a court of iron and borrowed memory, something ancient turned a ledger's page and made a small, satisfied note.
