## 6. The Road That Isn't on Any Map

Jude had drawn the Kettle Road eleven times in his life, and he could have drawn it an eleventh time blindfolded, drunk, and bleeding.

It began at the split birch above Miller's Drop — a tree so old its trunk had grown around an iron nail some forgotten smuggler had hammered into it as a marker before Jude's grandfather was born. From there it ran south-southwest along the shelf of the escarpment, dropped through the Hollow Chimney where the rock narrowed to a shoulder's width, and emerged into the high pasture country where the sheep were too poor to steal and the shepherds too proud to talk. It was not the fastest way through the Thornfell. It was not the easiest. But it was *his*, mapped in his own hand with his own shorthand — the little crooked lambda that meant loose scree, the double-dot that meant water you could trust, the small careful circle that meant a place where two men could hide from three.

Sera stood at the split birch and looked at it the way she looked at everything: like she was reading it.

"That's a nail," she said.

"That's a marker. Nail's older than the town. You'll want to remember it."

"Why?"

"Because if you can't find the birch, you can't find the road." He shouldered his pack and checked the straps out of habit rather than need; he'd checked them four times already since dawn. "And if you can't find the road, we're just two people walking around in a mountain that wants to eat us. So. Remember the birch."

"I've memorized six thousand volumes of Court record," Sera said, falling into step beside him on the shelf path. Her boots were wrong for this terrain — soft-soled, made for archive floors — but she walked anyway, light and precise, like her feet were arguing quietly with every stone. "I think I can manage one tree."

The morning was the kind of grey that made distances honest. No gold light to flatter the peaks, no blue haze to soften them. Jude liked grey mornings. Grey mornings told you exactly what a mountain was thinking.

They made good time along the shelf. Two hours in, the escarpment curved as it always curved, and the Hollow Chimney opened ahead of them — the narrow throat of rock, dark even at midday, cold air breathing out of it like something asleep.

Except it wasn't where it should have been.

Jude stopped. Not dramatically. He just stopped walking, the way a man stops when a stair isn't where his foot expects it, except they weren't on stairs, and the Chimney was *there*, plainly there, exactly where the map said it would be.

It was forty feet too far east.

He didn't say anything. He took out his map instead — the working copy, grease-stained, soft at the folds — and held it up against the horizon. The line of the escarpment on paper matched the line of the escarpment in the world. The notch of Miller's Drop matched. The birch, when he turned and looked back along the shelf, was still visible behind them, pale and patient.

But the Chimney had moved.

"Problem?" Sera asked.

"No," he lied, and heard how easily it came, and hated it a little. "Taking a bearing."

He took one. The compass needle swung once, twice, and settled — steady, confident, pointing precisely the direction his map said the Chimney lay. Which meant either the compass was broken, or the world had been adjusted around the needle, or — and this was the thought he set down carefully and refused to pick back up — the needle wasn't pointing north anymore. It was pointing at whatever the mountain wanted him to believe was north.

He pocketed the compass. He'd navigate by eye. Eye and memory and dead reckoning, the old ways, the ways that couldn't be rewritten because they lived in a man's legs and not on paper.

* * *

By midday he knew.

He'd have known sooner if he'd let himself, but he'd spent the last three hours performing confidence the way other men performed religion — loudly, and mostly for their own benefit. The signs were small. A cairn that stood on the wrong side of the trail. A stream that ran downhill toward the valley instead of away from it. The light coming from slightly the wrong quarter of the sky, so that shadows fell at angles no hour of the day could account for.

Then they came over the shoulder of the Graywacke Steps — a staircase of tilted stone slabs he'd first climbed at fourteen, when his father was alive and still pretending smuggling was a trade like any other — and Jude stopped, and this time he did not pretend.

Below them, where the trail should have descended through the Larch Cut into the river valley, there was no Larch Cut.

There was a lake.

Not a large one. Perhaps half a mile across, black and utterly still, ringed with reeds that grew in waterlines marking three different heights at once, as though the lake couldn't decide how deep it had always been. There was no lake within a day's walk of the Graywacke Steps. Jude had camped on the shore of the absence of that lake more times than he could count. There was a shepherd's hut there, a ruin with a good roof, a place he'd marked with his own careful circle meaning *two men can hide from three*.

The hut was underwater. He could see it, faintly, down in the black — walls and roofline intact, sitting on the lakebed like a model in a shopkeeper's window, preserved, displayed.

"They're rewriting it," Sera said behind him. She had gone very quiet, in the way of someone reading a document that confirms the worst thing in it.

"The Court."

"The Magistrate keeps the surface roads in the Ledger of Approaches." Her voice was flat, recitation-flat, the tone she used when feeling would only get in the way. "Every road that leads to Ironhollow is entered there, and what is entered can be... amended. Your smuggler routes aren't secret from him, Jude. They're *recorded*. He's been editing your maps for years, probably. Slowly. So no one would notice."

"How long do edits take?"

"For a road? Days. Weeks if it's stubborn." She looked at him then, and her eyes had the particular shine they got when she was about to say something true and knew it would cost. "A road that's loved is harder to move than one that's merely known. I'm sorry. I don't know if that helps."

It didn't. Or it did, in the ugly way that truth sometimes does: it meant the mountain wasn't moving randomly. It was moving *deliberately*, and slowly enough to be polite about it, the way a creditor moves possessions out of a debtor's house one item at a time, hoping he won't count the rooms.

His legs felt strange. Not weak — stranger than that. As though the ground had been subtly resoled beneath his boots, a half-size wrongness in every step. Twenty years of mastery sat in him like ballast, and he could feel it shifting, sloshing, trying to find a level in a hold that kept changing shape.

"I need to sit down," he said, and sat down on a stone slab of the Graywacke Steps, and took out his notebook, and did the thing he trusted most in the world besides nothing at all.

Dead reckoning.

He wrote down everything he knew to be true. Distance covered since the birch: he counted paces from memory, reconstructed the morning leg by leg — 4,100 to the Chimney, give or take the detour around the slipped section, 6,800 more to the Steps. Bearing maintained: south-southwest by the sun until the sun started lying, then south-southwest by feel, which he wrote down and immediately crossed out because *feel* was the first thing a mountain could edit. Elevation gained: he estimated by his knees, which complained on schedule, and his breath, which thinned on schedule, and those schedules didn't lie even when the light did.

When he finished the arithmetic, he looked up at the lake and said, aloud, with a certainty he built plank by plank in front of Sera:

"There's no lake here. There's a hut here. The hut is real. The lake is an edit."

"You sound very sure."

"I am sure of almost nothing else right now, so let me be sure of this." He tore a strip from the edge of the page, wrote *HUT — NO LAKE* on it, and weighted it under a stone at the top of the Steps. "If we come back this way, we'll see which version survived. Paper against paper. My note against his ledger."

"And if his wins?"

"Then I'll learn something about the weight of his ink." He stood. His legs had stopped feeling strange, or he had stopped listening to them, which amounted to the same thing and cost less. "We go around. North shore. And Sera — from here on, when I tell you a direction, you don't check it against anything. Not the sun, not the compass, not the shape of the land. You check it against me. I'm the last unamended copy of this route that exists."

She studied him for a moment. "That's a terrible navigation system."

"It's the only one he hasn't figured out how to forge yet."

* * *

They went around the north shore, and the lake watched them go.

Jude felt it watching — that was the madness of it, the vertigo climbing his spine rung by rung. Water that still has no wind on it isn't calm; it's *attentive*. He kept his eyes on the far reeds and counted his paces and refused, with the whole stubborn weight of his craft, to look at the hut below the surface. Looking at it felt like agreeing to it.

The afternoon went wrong in smaller ways. A ridge he remembered as bare carried a stand of birches, young and innocent, as though they'd grown there for thirty years overnight. A switchback he knew intimately — he'd sprained his wrist on it at nineteen — had reversed itself, so that it now turned left where his body turned right, and his body argued with the ground for three full strides before surrendering. His own muscle memory was becoming a set of outdated documents. He was a cartographer whose atlas was being pulped faster than he could memorize it, and worse — much worse — he was beginning to catch himself *doubting*. Doubting whether the switchback had ever turned right. Doubting whether there had ever been a hut. The edit didn't stop at the landscape. Given time, given weariness, it worked inward, sanding the grooves of memory smooth, offering its own version in a friendlier hand.

At the last light he called a halt in the lee of a boulder field he chose not to recognize, because recognizing things had become a liability.

"We stop here," he said. "No fire."

"You're shaking."

"I'm counting." He had the notebook open on his knee, and by the last grey light he was writing — fast, cramped, pressing hard enough to score the pages beneath. Every feature they'd passed since noon. Every discrepancy between the world and the twenty years of maps folded against his heart. He was rebuilding his atlas inside himself, entry by entry, and the entries all ended the same way: *moved*, *added*, *removed*, *lying*.

"What are you doing?" Sera crouched across from him, her face half shadow.

"Keeping a ledger of his ledger." He didn't look up. "You said it yourself — what's recorded can be amended. Fine. Then I record too. He can rewrite the mountain, but every time he does, he has to make it match the old version closely enough that I won't notice. And I notice. That's the whole of my trade, Sera. Other people see mountains. I see *differences*. And right now this whole range is one long confession of guilt."

"He knows you notice."

"Yes." He blew on the ink, closed the book. "That's the part I'm counting on."

She frowned. "Explain that."

"A forger who knows his work is being examined gets careless in a specific direction — he overcorrects. He makes things too perfect, too consistent, too exactly what a memory would expect." Jude tapped the notebook. "Half these edits are flawless. But there's a stream back there running the wrong way, and birches with no leaf-litter under them, and a lake that forgot to give itself a bottom-feeder population or a smell. He's writing a mountain the way a man writes a lie he's rehearsed. And rehearsed lies have seams."

"You're navigating by his mistakes."

"I'm navigating by the fact that he thinks I won't." He allowed himself one breath of something that was almost laughter, thin and cold in the dark. "Twenty years of checking other smugglers' maps taught me that nobody lies cleanly. Not even a Magistrate."

They ate cold food in the dark. Sera slept first — or pretended to, curled against the boulder with the economy of someone who'd learned sleep as a skill rather than a surrender. Jude took first watch, as he'd insisted, and sat with his notebook and the enormous silent dark, and did the arithmetic again from the beginning, because repetition was a kind of anchor, and anchors were all he had left.

Somewhere past midnight, the vertigo came back, and this time it brought company.

It began as a sound — or the memory of a sound, which in this place amounted to the same crime. His grandmother's voice, humming. The tune she'd hummed while rendering tallow, while mending nets, while telling him stories about the family that had survived everything because the family stuck together. He hadn't heard that tune in fifteen years, and here it was, rising off the black stone like heat off a road.

He looked up.

On the ridgeline above the boulder field, where no trail existed on any map he had ever drawn or any map anyone had ever drawn, stood a figure. Small. Old. The posture of a woman leaning into a lifetime of work. Too far away to be sure, close enough to be certain.

It raised one hand — not beckoning. Pointing. Down and to the west, toward a fold in the mountain that Jude's dead reckoning said was impassable and his grandmother's ghost said was a door.

Then the moon came out from behind cloud, and the ridgeline was empty, and the humming stopped so completely that the silence rang.

Jude sat very still. Below him, faintly, from the direction of a lake half a night's walk back, came the sound of water settling — patient, attentive, deciding.

He woke Sera with a hand on her shoulder, and when her eyes opened he saw her read his face in the dark and go still.

"Change of route," he said quietly. "West. Through the fold. And before you ask — no, it's not on my map."

"Then why are we going?"

"Because something just showed me the way," Jude said, "and I've spent all day learning that the only thing left on this mountain I can't fact-check is a ghost wearing my grandmother's face. Either she's leading me home," he shouldered his pack, "or she's leading me deeper into the debt. Either way — she's the one thing out here that remembers me better than the Magistrate does."

He stepped toward the fold in the mountain, and behind them, soft as an amendment being made, the birches they had passed that afternoon were no longer there at all.
