## 8. What the Shadow Keeps

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Jude noticed his shadow was missing.

He was sitting on a fallen log at the edge of the campsite, three days into the descent toward the Thornfell's heart, turning the forged sigil over in his fingers and not thinking about it, which took effort. Sera slept wrapped in her cloak against the pack-saddle, breathing slow. Above them the pines made their endless whispering argument with the wind, and beyond the pines the mountain stood up into the dark like something waiting.

The firelight should have thrown his shadow long across the scree behind him. It threw nothing.

Jude went very still, the way he did when he realized a road he knew had been rerouted overnight. He looked at the ground where his shadow ought to be — a pale oval of stone lit orange at the edges, empty as an unpaid debt. Then he turned, slowly, to look behind him.

His shadow sat on a boulder ten feet away, separate from him, folded forward with its elbows on its knees in a posture he had never once held in his life. And it was wearing his grandmother's face.

Not *like* her face. Not a mask of it. Her face — the deep lines around eyes the color of wet slate, the silver hair pinned back with the copper comb she'd worn every day of Jude's childhood until the day they buried the comb with her. The face that had sung him to sleep in a house that smelled of cedar smoke and ink. It sat there on a boulder in the middle of the night, cast by no light he could find, and it smiled at him the way she used to smile when he'd come home with scraped knees and a lie ready about how it happened.

"Hello, little cartographer," it said, in her voice.

Every rational part of Jude Nakamura — the part that trusted measurements, that believed a line drawn true on paper would be true on the ground, that had built a whole life out of the conviction that the world could be known if you were careful enough — that part told him to wake Sera, or run, or both. But his hand, traitorously, had already gone still in his lap, because his body remembered what his mind refused to: that voice meant safety. That voice meant someone standing between him and the dark.

"You're dead," he said. His own voice sounded thin and young.

"Yes." She tilted her head, and the copper comb caught light from no source. "That was rather the point of me."

"I saw them bury you. I carried one corner of the box myself."

"And a fine job you did of it. You always were strong for your size." She unfolded from the boulder and stood, and Jude saw then that she wasn't quite wearing his shape anymore — she was taller than his shadow should be, narrower, dressed in the suggestion of a shawl rather than the suggestion of a man. "Grief is a strange country, Jude. No one maps it accurately. They all draw it smaller than it is."

"What are you?" He made himself ask it plainly, the way you'd ask a client what was actually in the crate. "Because my grandmother is ash under a rowan tree in Kestrel Hollow, and whatever you are, you're wearing her the way a thief wears a coat."

Something flickered across the borrowed face — hurt, quick and genuine, and that was the worst part, that it looked genuinely hurt. "I am the piece of you they hold," she said. "The sliver. Your grandmother sold a piece of you before you drew breath, and pieces don't sit quietly in glass boxes, little one. They reach. They learn. They've been listening through you since the day you found that book." She spread her hands — his hands, but older, knuckles swollen the way hers had been. "And when they reached, they found her. Everything she poured into you. Every story, every lullaby, every winter evening by the stove. It's all in here. Do you understand? They didn't make this face. *You* did. I'm wearing what you kept."

The wind moved through the pines and Jude felt cold slide down his spine like water finding a crack in masonry.

"They sent you," he said.

"The Court doesn't send. The Court *owes*." She laughed — her laugh, low and rough, the one that had always ended in a cough she'd wave away. "But yes. They know you're coming. They've known since before you did; that's what ledgers are for. And the roads below have been rearranged twice since you crossed the border-stone. You'll wander those corridors until your legs give out and your little archivist starves, and then they'll collect what's left of you at their leisure. Or—" she paused, delicately, the way she'd pause over a hand of cards, "—you can accept guidance from someone who knows the way."

"Someone who knows the way," Jude repeated. "For free."

"No one in my family has ever done anything for free, and neither have I. It's genetic. Well." The smile sharpened. "It's *hereditary*."

"There it is," Jude said softly. "There's the price."

"A price to be named later."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. That's the whole trick, isn't it?" He stood up off the log, slowly, keeping his eyes on her face because looking away felt like a physical pain and he wanted to remember that feeling, catalog it, note it down: *this is what it costs to look at her*. "A price to be named later means you name it when I have no leverage left. That's not a bargain, that's a hook with the bait already swallowed. I'm a smuggler, Gran. I invented that clause. I've written it into contracts myself, God help me."

"I know." Her voice gentled, and the gentleness was worse than anything else she'd said. "I taught you to read contracts before I taught you to read stories. Don't you think I know exactly what I'm asking? Don't you think I've had a hundred years in the dark to turn over everything I did?" For a moment the shadow wavered, and underneath the borrowed face Jude glimpsed something vast and thin and hungry, a shape like a torn flag in a windless place — and then it was her again, tired, small, old. "I am not asking you to trust me, Jude. I'm asking you to do arithmetic. Without me, you die lost in the dark. With me, you might live, and owe something terrible. Those are the only two columns on the page."

"Not the only ones." His throat was tight. "There's a third column. I go back up, I take Sera home, I live half a soul in the sun and never speak of this again."

"And leave the shard in their vault forever?" She shook her head. "A soul-piece doesn't rest, little cartographer. It *pulls*. You'd feel it every morning of your life, that small tug behind the sternum, like a compass needle pointing down. You'd dream in iron. And one day, twenty years from now, some grandchild of yours would find a book of prophecies with their name in it, and we'd do all this again, one generation further down." She stepped closer, and the fire didn't so much as flicker at her passing. "You were collateral before you were born. The only question left in your whole ledger is whether you pay it or break it."

Behind him, Sera stirred in her sleep and murmured something he couldn't catch.

Jude looked at the shadow wearing his grandmother's face and understood, finally, what the Court had done — the elegant, obscene thing. They hadn't tortured him or threatened him. They'd reached into his grief and put handles on it. Everything he loved about her, they'd shaped into a key, and now they stood at his shoulder offering to turn it.

And the cruelest part, the part he'd carry whether he lived or died: he *wanted* to say yes. Not for the roads. For the voice. For one more hour of hearing her call him *little cartographer*, even knowing it was bait, even knowing every warm word was iron underneath. Love, he thought, was the best lockpick ever forged, which was why the fae never needed to forge any other kind.

"If I say yes," he said carefully, "the price gets named when?"

"When I choose. Before Ironhollow, after it — my discretion entirely." She watched him with slate-grey eyes full of a patience older than mountains. "Standard terms."

"Standard terms," he echoed. "You realize you just described every bad deal in smuggling history."

"I realize," she said, "that I raised you to spot them anyway. Which is why I chose these terms. If you're going to be cheated, better by family. At least family tells you so to your face."

He laughed — he couldn't help it, a short, broken sound that Sera didn't wake to — and hated how much of it was relief. Because that was the truth under all of it: some part of him, deep in the marrow where the missing shard had left its hollow ache, believed her. Believed she was sorry. Believed that whatever the Court had built out of his love, somewhere inside it there was still a woman who had pawned her unborn grandson's soul to keep four children fed through a famine winter, and who had carried the weight of it for sixty years without once letting him see her flinch.

Maybe that part of him was right. Maybe it was the most sophisticated trap of all. He couldn't tell, and that inability — a mapmaker who couldn't read the territory of his own heart — frightened him more than the Court did.

"One condition," Jude said.

Her eyebrows rose — her expression, her exact skeptical arch. "Negotiating already. There's my boy."

"Sera comes to no harm from you. Not indirectly, not incidentally, not as a cost of doing business. Whatever your price turns out to be, it comes out of me and no one else. Swear it however you swear things."

The shadow was quiet for a long moment. Around them the fire settled, popping once, sending up a spiral of sparks that bent oddly, as though curving toward her.

"That's a costly promise," she said at last. "Costs are how they'll test you. They'll offer you exits that require leaving her behind. They'll make her the lever, because levers work better than chains."

"Then the promise will be expensive. Swear it anyway."

"I swear it," she said, and the words landed with weight, with actual weight, like stones set into a wall. Whatever she was, whatever the Court had made of her echo, the oath bound something real. "Sera of the Stacks is outside my ledger. Now." She extended a hand — his own hand, weathered and old. "Do we have a bargain, Jude Nakamura?"

He looked at her hand. He thought about his parents, whose deaths sat neatly recorded in a book of prophecies. He thought about the letter in his grandmother's hand: *forgive me the interest*. He thought about Sera sleeping ten feet away, trusting him with her life on the strength of a pact made over stolen pages, and how he'd lied to a customs-warden for her with barely a heartbeat's hesitation.

Everyone in his life who loved him had paid for it. It seemed fitting, somehow, that he'd learned the same craft.

"We have a bargain," Jude said, and took his grandmother's shadow by the hand.

It was cold. Not grave-cold — *ledger*-cold, the chill of a page that has been waiting a long time to be signed. Something passed between them, faint and unspooling, a thread of himself tightening toward the dark, and the hollow behind his sternum ached in a way he suspected he'd be feeling for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

When he let go, she was simply a shadow again — flat, black, wrong-angled — pooling at his feet like spilled ink, wearing the faint outline of a shawl.

"Rest," she said from the ground, from everywhere, gently. "We walk at first light. The mountain has moved the eastern galleries again, and you'll want your wits about you, little cartographer. It hates being known." A pause, and then, softer, in a register that might have been wholly hers and might have been the deepest cut of all: "You look like him, you know. My son. When you frown at the fire like that."

Jude didn't answer. He banked the coals and lay down with his back to her and did not sleep, and above him the stars came out over Thornfell, indifferent and precise, the oldest map there was — one nobody had ever managed to rearrange.

* * *

Dawn came grey and cold through the pines, and with it Sera woke, stretched, and froze mid-motion, staring at the ground beside Jude's bedroll.

"There's a shadow," she said carefully, "next to yours. It's not yours."

"It's mine," Jude said. He rolled up his bedroll with steady hands, because if his hands stopped being steady he was going to have to talk about last night, and he wasn't ready. "Long story. Short version: I hired a guide."

"To Ironhollow." Her voice dropped. "Jude. Guides to Ironhollow are paid. What's the fee?"

He cinched the bedroll tight and slung it over his shoulder and gave her the smile he used on clients when the cargo was dangerous and the route was certain.

"A price to be named later," he said.

Sera went pale. She opened her mouth — he could see the argument assembling behind her eyes, the whole architecture of *you idiot, do you know what those words mean down there* — and then she looked at the shadow pooled at his heels, and at his face, and closed her mouth again, because she was an archivist and she had clearly just found the entry in some internal catalogue, and whatever she'd read there made her decide to save it for the road.

They broke camp in silence. As they shouldered their packs and turned toward the dark seam in the mountainside where the smuggler roads dove underground, the shadow rose off the ground ahead of them, unhurried, wearing the outline of a shawl, and pointed one long finger toward a crack in the rock that Jude's own maps swore was solid stone.

"This way," said his grandmother's voice, kind as a hearth and cold as a vault. "And Jude — mind the third gallery. They've hung new doors in it, and doors down here aren't always doors."

Jude looked at the impossible passage, then at Sera, then at the shadow walking ahead of them into the dark like a woman leading her grandchildren home through snow.

He followed.
