## 9. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The last of the smuggler roads ended where no road should end: in a wall of stone that opened like a mouth.

Sera felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like old coins and cold candlewax, the particular hum of iron worked by hands that had never once trembled. The Court Beneath the Mountain did not announce itself. It *recognized* you. It slid beneath your skin and took inventory, and somewhere far below, she knew, a ledger was already turning its pages to find her name.

"Stay close," she murmured to Jude. "And whatever you see — whatever they show you — don't say it's beautiful out loud."

"Why not?"

"Because it will hear you. And things here believe what they're told."

He gave her a look — the flat, measuring look of a man recalculating a route — but he said nothing, which was its own kind of trust. Three days in the dark together had worn the transactional edges off their bargain without either of them admitting it. He still checked his maps twice each night. She still slept facing the exits. But he had given her the last of his water at the tollgate crossing without being asked, and she had lied to a warden with his name in her mouth, and there were debts between them now that had nothing to do with soul-shards or ledgers.

They stepped through the stone mouth together.

* * *

Ironhollow did not descend so much as unfold.

The passage widened by degrees, and then all at once, and Sera stood on a ledge of black granite overlooking a cavern so vast that her mind refused its dimensions the way a map refuses the curve of the world. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and from them — impossibly, obscenely — hung chandeliers. Iron ones, hundreds of them, tier upon tier descending into the dark, each link forged thin as hair and strong as sin, each holding candles that burned without flickering in air that did not move because the Court did not permit it to.

The light fell downward in golden sheets, and where it landed, the city glittered.

Spires of black iron and pale bone-stone rose from the cavern floor in clusters, connected by bridges arched like drawn bows. Water ran through channels cut in precise spirals — not for drinking, she knew, but for memory. The fae of the deep court distilled their recollections the way surface folk distilled spirits, and the canals carried it glowing faintly blue-green through the streets, past doorways carved with names that had been sold and resold so many times their original owners had been forgotten.

And the people — the Court, the *Court* — moved through it all in silks the color of bruises and candleflame, and every one of them wore their wealth where surface nobles wore rings.

Around throats, souls glinted. Small shards of light caught in glass and gold, some white as frost, some amber, one or two a deep bruised violet that made Sera's stomach turn, because she knew what color meant. Around wrists, anklets, threaded through braids and pinned to collars — fragments of other people's selves, traded and inherited and stolen, each one humming faintly with the voice of whoever had lost it. A duchess passed below them trailing laughter that belonged to a farmer dead two hundred years. A magistrate's clerk wore a child's wonder set in silver at his ear, and when he turned his head, Sera heard it sing a single note of joy, thin and endless, like a wire under tension.

Jude had gone very still beside her. "Those are—"

"Yes."

"They're *wearing*—"

"I know." She kept her voice level, archivist-flat, because if she let it shake he would see how much of her still lived down here. "Souls keep. Better than most currency. They don't tarnish, they appreciate with age, and the older the grief in them, the deeper the luster." She had catalogued eleven thousand such pieces in the archive above. She had dusted them. She had wound the clockwork cases that kept them singing. "Don't stare at any single one too long. They notice being noticed. Some of them call out."

"To who?"

"To whoever they used to be." She started down the ledge stairs, her hand trailing the cold rock. "Or whoever took them."

He followed, and she heard him breathing carefully, the way he breathed when he was memorizing terrain. Good. Let him map it. Maps could be stolen, redrawn, burned. That was the only language this place respected.

* * *

The archive district smelled the way it always had: vellum and iron filings and the sweet rot of memories left too long in open air. Sera led him through streets where every building leaned inward, listening, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the altitude of memory, nothing more.

She had run from these streets nine years ago. She had made it as far as the surface archive — a satellite post, a gilded exile, the Court's concession to those half-blooded creatures too useful to kill and too tainted to seat at table. Nine years of cataloguing prophecies and debt-records, of touching the Court's business only through paper and glass, of telling herself the distance was freedom.

Now she walked back into the body of the thing, and her feet knew the way too well.

That was the part no one warned you about, leaving a place like this. You could escape the walls. The walls did not escape you. Her stride still shortened automatically at the corner where the warden's post stood. Her shoulders still dropped into the deferential curve the half-bloods were taught before they could write their own names. She caught herself doing it and straightened, furious, and Jude glanced at her but asked nothing, and she loved him a little for that, and hated herself for the word *love*, and filed both feelings away in the drawer where she kept everything she couldn't afford to feel yet.

"The Magistrate will know we're here," she said. "Probably already knows. There's no sneaking into Ironhollow. There's only choosing the manner in which you're received."

"And you chose walking in the front gate."

"I chose the version where he's curious instead of certain. Curiosity buys time. Certainty spends it."

They crossed the Bridge of Recorded Voices — she had named it that herself, at fourteen, before she knew better — and below them the memory-canals glowed, and voices rose from the water. Fragments. A woman singing a lullaby in a language dead before the mountain had a name. A man swearing an oath. A child counting. The Court piped its acquisitions through the streets the way surface cities piped music, and every noble learned to walk over other people's lives without looking down.

Sera looked down. She always looked down. It was the one rebellion available to an archivist, and she had practiced it for sixty years until it looked like nothing at all.

At the far end of the bridge, the doors of the Hall of Accounts stood open.

Of course they did. That was the terrifying thing. Not bars, not guards, not wards flaring — just doors standing open onto a hall lit by a single chandelier the size of a harvest moon, iron links thick as her arm, and beneath it, waiting, a figure she would have known blindfolded by the sound of his breathing alone.

* * *

"Sera."

Her name in his voice went through her like a hook through silk.

Vessan, Magistrate of Debts, First Auditor of the Court Beneath the Mountain, came forward with his arms slightly open — not an embrace, never quite an embrace, but the *shape* of one, the offer of one, held out like bait held out to a fish that had already swallowed three hooks and called them gifts. He was tall in the way the old fae were tall, as though height were a courtesy they extended themselves, and his face was kind the way a well-made trap is kind — smooth, considered, every line placed for effect. His robes were grey shot with silver thread that moved when it shouldn't have, rearranging itself into columns of figures when he stood still long enough.

Behind him, the hall stretched away in ranks of desks where clerks scratched with pens that never needed ink, writing in ledgers bound in leather that Sera refused, even now, to think about too precisely.

"You've come home." Warmth poured off him like heat off a forge. He said it the way a father says it, the way a jailer says it, the way a spider might say it to the fly it had raised from the egg. "Nine years, and not one letter. I confess my feelings were wounded. I consoled myself with your work — the Kellam inheritance audit, exquisite. The Thornfell survey annotations. Exquisite. You catalogue like someone trying to make the world orderly enough to be safe in." His smile deepened by a degree. "I taught you that."

"You taught me that order is a cage with good lighting," Sera said. Her voice came out steady. Somewhere inside, the girl she had been at fourteen sat down heavily and began, quietly, to weep. "Master."

The title cost her. She paid it anyway, watching his face drink it.

"And you brought a guest." Vessan's gaze slid past her shoulder, and Sera felt Jude stiffen behind her. "Jude Nakamura. Cartographer. Smuggler. Grandson of Maren Nakamura, whose account—" he paused, delicately, savoring it, "—has matured. Tonight, in fact. You must forgive the excitement downstairs. We do so love a collection day. It gives the junior clerks something to aspire to."

"We came to negotiate," Sera said.

"Negotiate." He tasted the word. "My dear, you sound like the surface archives. Down here we have a simpler vocabulary. One owes, or one is owed. One pays, or one is paid. Everything else is embroidery." He drifted closer, and up close she could smell him — ink and cold iron and, beneath it, faintly, the blue-green scent of the memory-canals, as though he drank from them constantly, as though he had replaced his own past so thoroughly with other people's that he no longer had a scent of his own. "But you know this. You knew it when you stole page forty-one of the Prophecy of the Hollow Root. You knew it when you defected with it clutched to your chest like a talisman. And you knew it—" his voice softened, and the softness was the worst thing yet, "—when you broke our rule the first time, long before the boy was born. Before you ran. Before any of it."

The hall was silent except for the scratching of distant pens.

"You knew," Vessan continued gently, "that some accounts are never closed. Only transferred." He turned his attention to Jude with the pleasant efficiency of a man appraising livestock. "Walk with me, cartographer. Let us discuss what you're worth. And Sera—" the warmth returned, full-bore, terrible, "—do settle in. Your desk is exactly as you left it. I had it dusted every week. I never doubted you'd return. Runaways always circle back. It's in the nature of things with a piece of themselves missing."

He swept toward the inner offices, and Jude hesitated, and Sera gave him the smallest nod — *go, play your part, we planned for this* — and followed the sound of her own heartbeat down the hall she had once sworn never to walk again.

Behind her, the great doors closed without a sound.

Above, the iron chandelier burned on, patient as a ledger, and in its light Sera could almost believe she had never left at all — that the nine years of sun and paper-dust and careful, careful freedom had been the dream, and this warm, gilded, suffocating dark was simply where she had always been going.

Somewhere below them, a reliquary waited, singing with a dead woman's voice.

And somewhere deeper than that, in the root of the mountain, lay the truth that would unmake everything — though neither of them knew it yet, and the Magistrate, smiling, poured three cups of tea as though he had all the time in the world, and perhaps he did, and perhaps, Sera thought with a chill that reached all the way down to the shard of herself the Court still held, so did the ledger.
