## 10. An Inventory of Jude Nakamura

They did not chain him. That was the first insult.

Chains, Jude could have understood. Chains were the grammar of smugglers, the punctuation of every cell and hold and customs-house he'd ever slipped through. You could read chains. You could work with chains. But the Court Beneath the Mountain simply walked him down a corridor of black iron veined with something that glowed like banked coals, flanked by wardens whose faces were smooth as unstruck coins, and no one touched him at all.

Because you don't shackle the merchandise, he thought. You display it.

The corridor breathed. He was almost certain of it. The walls expanded and contracted with a rhythm too slow and too vast to be his imagination, and the air tasted of cold metal and, beneath it, something sweeter — old paper, maybe, or the inside of a violin. His grandmother's house had smelled like that, the winter she taught him to read contour lines by candlelight, her finger tracing the ridges of a map of a country he'd never seen.

He shut that memory away fast, the way you'd snuff a lamp before it lit something you couldn't put out.

*They taste lies,* Sera had told him, over and over, in the weeks of preparation, her voice low in the dark of the smuggler roads. *Not hear. Not smell. Taste. A lie has a flavor to them, Jude — like meat gone slightly off. Most of them can't tell you what's wrong with it, but they'll spit it out. So you don't lie. You tell the truth so narrow and so small that it fits through the eye of their needle.*

*And when the truth is too big to hide?*

*Then you make it boring,* she'd said. *Boredom is the one flavor they've forgotten.*

So Jude Nakamura, who had talked his way past three border lords and a customs-warden who could smell guilt the way sharks smelled blood, walked into the heart of Ironhollow with his face arranged into the particular blankness of a man deeply unimpressed, and made himself boring.

* * *

The hall of the Magistrate of Debts was not what he had expected, and he hated that he had expected anything at all. He had imagined a throne room, something grand and grasping. Instead it was — an office. An *accounting house*. The ceiling soared away into dark where iron chandeliers hung like frozen rain, each one holding hundreds of small lights that were not candles. He didn't let himself look at them long. He had a suspicion about what burned in them.

The floor was the worst part. It was a map.

Not inked — *inlaid*, in metals he had no names for, a map of the whole Thornfell range rendered so precisely that Jude, who had drawn those mountains from the inside of his own skull, felt the floor recognize him. Rivers of pale silver. Ridges of dark bronze. And at the center, where the hall opened into the Magistrate's presence, the mountain itself, Ironhollow, rendered in a metal that was not any metal at all but something that moved, very slowly, like a liquid deciding to be solid.

He was walking on his own life's work, drawn by someone who had never once been cold or hungry on any of those passes.

*Don't look down,* he told himself. *You're a cartographer. It's the one lie you can't afford — pretending you don't know this ground.*

They seated him. Not a chair — a stool, low, so that whoever stood before him would stand above. Of course. Everything in this place was a sentence with the verb buried.

And then the Magistrate of Debts came to appraise him.

* * *

He had braced for a monster. What came instead was a tall figure in grey robes that fell like poured ash, with a face that was pleasant the way a ledger is pleasant — balanced, orderly, every line accounted for. The Magistrate's eyes were the color of the space between entries. When the being smiled, the smile fit the face the way a forged signature fits a document: perfectly, and wrongly.

"Jude Nakamura," the Magistrate said, and the name came out sounding like a figure being read aloud. "Son of Maren and Tomas. Grandson of Elsbeth Nakamura, cartographer, of the Thornfell settlements." A pause, delicate as a pen lifting between entries. "You have your grandmother's hands. I remember her hands."

Something went through Jude's chest like a blade of ice, and he let it pass through and out the other side, and kept his face boring.

"You knew her," he said. Truth. Narrow. Small.

"I held her account for forty years." The Magistrate drifted closer, robes whispering like turning pages. "A remarkable woman. She came to us in the famine winter with nothing left to pledge but a thing she did not yet have — and we, being generous, extended her credit against the future." The smile deepened a fraction. "Against *you*, as it happened. She wept, you know. They always weep. It's in the terms. Grief is the co-signature."

Jude thought of the ledger hidden in her floor, the brown stain of old blood on the signature line, and made his voice come out flat as a road in winter. "I'm told I'm here to settle a debt."

"You're here to be *inventoried*," the Magistrate corrected gently. "Settlement comes later, if it comes at all. One does not settle an account one has not first read. Sit still, if you please. This will not hurt. That is not a promise — promises are currency here, and I don't spend them on inventory — merely a statement of practice."

The Magistrate raised one hand, and the hall *answered*.

* * *

Light gathered. It came up out of the floor-map first, the silver rivers brightening, the bronze ridges warming, and then the chandeliers above leaned — actually *leaned*, all of them, like a crowd pressing in — and Jude understood with a lurch that the lights inside them had turned to look at him. He kept his eyes forward. He was a man being measured for a coat. He was bored. He was so bored.

"Subject: Jude Nakamura," the Magistrate recited, circling him now, slow as a hand moving down a column of figures. "Age, thirty-one years. Trade, cartography and, ah—" the pause was a small knife, "—*logistics*. Unlicensed. Health, excellent. Scars, seven, of which four were honestly earned." A soft sound that might have been amusement. "Literacy, three scripts. Numeracy, exceptional. Sense of direction—" and here the Magistrate stopped, directly in front of him, and the pleasant face tilted, "—*extraordinary*. Not trained. Inherited. It lives in him like a second heartbeat. Do you know how rare that is? Most men navigate by the world. Your grandmother's line navigates by *itself*. The world simply agrees to be found by them."

"I was taught well," Jude said. Narrow. Small. Boring.

"You were *bred* well," the Magistrate said, without malice, the way one notes that a coin is of good silver, "which is why the collateral has held its value for thirty-one years. Most soul-shards degrade. They thin in the keeping, like wine badly cellared. Yours has not thinned. Yours has *ripened*." The grey hand rose, and the hall rose with it. "You may look now. I insist. An owner should see what he owns — or, in your case, what owns a piece of him."

The floor opened.

There was no other way to describe it. The inlaid mountain at the center of the hall — the moving metal, the liquid-solid heart of Ironhollow rendered in miniature — parted like a lid, and rose up out of it on a column of pale light: a reliquary of glass and iron, tall as a man, and inside it, turning slowly as if suspended in water—

Jude's breath left him.

It was small. That was the thing no one had warned him about. A sliver, a splinter, a shaving of light no bigger than a fingernail paring — and yet it *burned*, a white-gold ember turning in the dark of the glass, and it was so familiar that his whole body leaned toward it before his mind could stop him, the way a man leans toward a window in winter.

And it was singing.

Not with his voice. With *hers*.

The song had no words, or words too old and worn to hold their shapes anymore, a lullaby worn smooth as a river stone — and Jude knew it, knew it in his teeth, in the backs of his knees, in the place behind his sternum where he kept the memory of a kitchen and a candle and a woman's finger tracing mountains on paper. His grandmother had sung it off-key. The shard sang it *perfectly*, and that was the obscenity of it, that was the violation: the Court had taken the crooked, human, off-key thing and polished it, and now his grandmother's soul-splinter hung in glass singing her love for him in a voice with all the humanity sanded out of it, flawless as a coin.

*Don't cry,* he told himself, and the telling was the hardest work he had ever done, harder than any pass, any border, any night of dead reckoning through country that wanted him dead. *Don't cry. They taste it. Grief is a flavor. Make it boring.*

"Ah," the Magistrate said softly, watching him with those between-entries eyes. "There it is. Recognition. Do you know, in forty years of holding her account, I never once heard her sing that song *well*?"

Jude looked at the Magistrate. He let one degree of warmth into his face — calculated, metered, a smuggler's declared goods at the border — and said, in a voice that was almost steady:

"It was better off-key."

Something flickered across the pleasant face. Not anger. *Interest.* The Magistrate's head tilted, and for a moment the ledger-eyes were not reading him but *reading about him*, the way a merchant reads about a market he hasn't entered yet.

"Yes," the Magistrate murmured. "Yes, I believe she said something similar. About you, as it happens. In the last entry of her account, the year before she died." The grey robes drifted a half-step closer, and the voice dropped into something almost — *almost* — like kindness, which was the most frightening thing Jude had heard yet. "She wrote: *the boy sings it worse than I do, and I love him for it.* A sentimental entry. Unusual for her. Sentiment complicates accounts." A pause. "It also, occasionally, appreciates them."

The reliquary sank back into the floor. The mountain closed over it like water. The song cut off mid-phrase, and the absence of it was a physical wound, a hand pulled out of a hand.

* * *

The inventory continued. Jude sat on his low stool and let them weigh him.

Not with scales — with *questions*. The wardens brought instruments of black iron that hummed near his temples, his wrists, the hollow of his throat, and each hum drew something up for the Magistrate to read aloud. His fear, catalogued: moderate, well-managed, *interesting*. His anger, catalogued: considerable, *poorly aimed*, noted with what sounded like approval. His memories of the surface — the taverns, the roads, the clients, Sera — each one touched, weighed, and entered. When the humming iron passed over his memories of Sera, the Magistrate paused.

"The archivist," the Magistrate said. "Your guide. She is, of course, already home. She came in the east gate this morning, unremarked, as she believed." The pleasant face watched him. "You did know that. You must have. She is a very poor liar, our Sera. She always was. It's why I kept her in the archives — surrounded by better liars than herself. Books."

Jude's heart did not stop, because he would not permit it to. He made it a ledger instead. *Entry: they have Sera, or say they do. Entry: they want me to believe it. Entry: either way, the answer is the same.* He let his face show a flicker of concern — a man worried about a guide, a business associate — nothing more.

"I pay her half up front," he said. "Whatever she does is her own account."

"Mmm," said the Magistrate, and wrote nothing down, which was somehow worse than writing something down. "Half up front. How very like your grandmother's line. Always splitting the cost, always keeping the second half in reserve." The grey figure turned away, drifting back toward the inlaid mountain, and spoke over one ash-fall shoulder. "The appraisal is complete, Jude Nakamura. The shard is sound. The collateral has appreciated beyond all projection. Which presents the Court with an unusual question — one I confess I have not faced in a very long age."

The Magistrate turned fully, and the chandeliers above went still, every borrowed light hanging on the answer before it was asked.

"Whether to collect the debt," the Magistrate said, "or to *renew* it. A soul-shard like yours, ripening in our keeping, is worth more than it was lent for. But a soul-shard still *attached* to its owner — still fed, year by year, by love and grief and memory — that is worth more than any vault can hold." The between-entries eyes were warm now, horribly warm, the warmth of a hearth in a house you can never leave. "Your grandmother pledged a sliver and gave us a harvest. I find myself wondering what *you* might pledge, given the right terms. Given, say, the safety of a certain half-fae archivist who is currently in our cells, believing — poor, dear, honest creature — that she has already betrayed you."

The hall was silent. The floor-map glowed. Somewhere beneath Jude's feet, under the inlaid bronze and silver, under the moving metal heart of the mountain, the shard sang on in the dark, off-key now, he was almost sure of it, off-key and worn and human, waiting for someone to come and take it home.

Jude Nakamura bowed from his low stool — low, and slow, and perfectly respectful — and smiled the smile of a man with nothing in his pockets but lint.

"Then I'd like to hear the terms," he said.

And in the silence that followed, he felt — not heard, *felt*, the way you feel a door open in a house you thought you knew every creak of — something shift in the dark of the chandeliers above, some small borrowed light leaning closer, some attention he had not yet been introduced to, taking its first note in a ledger of its own.
