## 13. The Night Market of Borrowed Names

### Jude

The way down to the Night Market was a road that had never been built, and Sera walked it like a woman returning to a house she'd sworn never to enter again.

"Keep your eyes on my shoulders," she said, "and whatever you do, don't count the stairs."

"Why not?"

"Because they'll notice you counting. The Market lives on attention. Give it too much and it starts charging you for the privilege of looking." She glanced back at him, and in the faerie-light bleeding up from below, her face was half shadow and half something older and more careful. "You've been generous with your attention all your life, mapmaker. Down here, that's a vice."

They descended through a seam in Ironhollow's undercity — past cisterns where the water hung motionless as polished glass, past doorways that breathed — until the passage opened without warning into a cavern so vast that Jude's cartographer's heart stuttered and then began, helplessly, to measure it.

Stalls. Hundreds of them. Tiered down the walls of the cavern like swallow nests, each one lit by lanterns that burned colors light was never meant to burn: a green like the moment before a storm breaks, a violet that tasted of copper at the back of the throat. The air itself was merchandise. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune that made everyone who heard it briefly remember a summer they'd never had.

"The Night Market of Borrowed Names," Sera murmured. "Everything sold here is true, and everything is someone else's."

Jude pulled his hood lower and followed her into the crowd.

He had expected monsters. What he got instead was worse — ordinary-looking traders with ordinary-looking faces, haggling over goods he could feel pressing against his mind like fingers against a windowpane. A woman sold bottled lullabies, three for a song. A man with ink-stained fingers dealt in other people's handwriting, whole lifetimes of letters no one had ever answered. In one stall, a child — or something shaped like a child — arranged rows of small glass bells, each containing a name, chiming softly when passersby came close enough to almost recognize their own.

"Don't look at the bells," Sera said quietly.

"I wasn't going to buy one."

"That's how it starts."

She led him deep into the tiers, down and down, to where the stalls grew smaller and quieter and the prices grew stranger. Here the merchants didn't call out. They waited, the way spiders wait, and their goods sat under black cloth like sleeping things.

The stall they wanted was the last one, wedged into a crease of the cavern wall where the lantern-light gave out entirely. The merchant there was ancient in a way that had nothing to do with age — her face was unlined, but her eyes had the depth of wells, and when she moved, Jude heard the faint dry whisper of paper, as though she were made of folded documents.

"You've brought me a mortal," the merchant said to Sera. Her voice was pleasant and terrible, like a lullaby sung slightly off-key. "Mortals pay so beautifully. Everything they have is real."

"We're here for a soul-sigil," Sera said. "Forged well enough to pass the reliquary wards. You know the work I mean."

"I know the work." The merchant's smile widened by a fraction. "And you know my price has changed since the last time you shopped here, little runaway. Coin is nothing to me. Years are nothing. I deal in *true things*. Something remembered. Something loved." Her well-deep eyes slid to Jude. "Something warm."

Jude's hand went, before he could stop it, to his breast pocket, where his grandmother's ledger rode against his heart. He had brought it down the whole mountain. He had told himself it was for reference — the geometry of the vault, the pattern of the debt. But some part of him had known the truth: he carried it the way other men carry relics.

"What kind of memory?" he asked.

The merchant's attention settled on him fully then, and it was like being mapped. He knew the sensation intimately — he did it to coastlines, to rivers, to the hidden passes of the Thornfell. Being done to him was another matter entirely. He felt himself opened, indexed, appraised down to the grain.

"Oh, he has a good one," she breathed. "Oh, he's been *saving* it. There — behind the grief, behind all those gray memories of roads and ledgers. A kitchen. Rain on a tin roof. Flour on old hands. She's teaching him to fold dumplings and she's singing off-key on purpose because it makes him laugh, and he doesn't know yet — he can't know yet — that this is the last winter she'll—" 

"*Stop.*"

The word came out of him cracked and ragged. Around them, several nearby merchants turned with interest, scenting payment in the air. Sera's hand closed hard around his wrist.

"That one is not for sale," she said coldly.

"Everything is for sale." The merchant spread her hands, and beneath them the stall's black cloth stirred. "But I am not a thief, whatever the Court says. I only take what is *given*. So, mortal. Name your price in something else, if you can. Or name another memory — any memory — and we'll bargain like honest folk."

Jude stood very still.

He thought about the reliquary vault, and the forged sigil they needed to reach it, and the shard of his soul sitting in its glass case singing with his grandmother's voice. He thought about the ledger's geometry. He thought about the shadow wearing her face, and the price it had named, and the terrible arithmetic of debts compounding in the dark.

And underneath all of it, quiet as snowfall, he thought about the kitchen. The rain. The flour. Her voice sliding sweetly off every note while young Jude laughed so hard he couldn't shape the dough.

It was the one warm thing he owned. He had known that for years. Grief had sorted his childhood long ago, and left exactly one memory polished bright, and he had carried it through famine and smuggling and his parents' funerals like an ember cupped in both hands.

"There has to be another way," Sera said low, close to his ear. "Jude. Listen to me. There is always another way. It will cost time, risk, blood — take those instead. Never let them take the true things. That's the first rule of surviving the Court, and I learned it the worst way a person can learn anything."

"I know the rule."

"Then—"

"But rules are for keeping accounts," Jude said, "and I'm done letting anyone keep mine."

He looked at the merchant. His hands were steady. Some distant part of him noted that with cartographer's detachment, the way he'd note that a river ran fast here, that the crossing would be dangerous, that the far bank must be reached regardless.

"The memory," he said. "The kitchen. All of it — every detail, complete, not thinned. And in exchange: a soul-sigil good enough to fool the Magistrate's own wards, plus your silence about our faces, plus one answer to one question, asked now, answered true."

The merchant went still in a way that made the whole cavern seem to lean closer. Even the fiddler's stolen summer faltered.

"You bargain like a fae," she said, with something like respect. "Who taught you?"

"A dead smuggler's account book," Jude said. "*The* answer, merchant. Take it or I find another stall."

"There is no other stall," she said simply. "There is only ever me. Very well. Ask."

Jude leaned in. He kept his voice beneath the noise of the Market, beneath the chime of the name-bells.

"When I speak the mountain's name — and I will speak it — what happens to the debts? All of them. Every bargain the Court ever struck. Mine. Hers." He touched the ledger through his coat. "*Sera's.*"

For the first time, the merchant looked away from him. She looked down into her own folded-paper hands, and Jude saw — clearly, unmistakably — fear.

"Debts need a creditor," she said. "Break the creditor, and the debts do not vanish. They *return*. To the debtors. Every promise comes home to roost, with all the weight it carried when it was made." Her eyes lifted to his. "Your grandmother's bargain returns to her blood. Which means, little mapmaker, that when the Court falls, the sliver of soul you came to steal back may already be yours — and something else, something owed *by* your line, may come due in its place. Do you still want your sigil?"

The rain on the tin roof. The flour. The off-key song.

"Yes," Jude said. "All of it."

He held out his hand.

### Sera

She watched him pay, and it was the hardest thing she had ever done, which was saying something, because she had once knelt in front of the Magistrate of Debts and signed away her own surname.

The merchant did not take the memory the way mortals imagined memories being taken — no claw, no smoke, no dramatic severing. She simply took Jude's offered hand between both of hers, bowed her head as though in prayer, and *listened*. That was all. She listened the way the starving eat, the way the drowning breathe, and Sera saw the warmth go out of Jude's face the way heat goes out of a room when a door is left open in winter.

He didn't cry. His eyes stayed dry and wide and fixed on the middle distance, where presumably a kitchen was dissolving — rain, flour, the sweet deliberate ruin of an old woman's singing — and Sera found her own throat closing, because she recognized the expression. She had worn it leaving the archive for the last time, walking out past shelves that held every book she'd ever loved, knowing she could take none of them.

When the merchant released his hand, Jude swayed. Sera caught his arm.

"It's gone," he said. Not a question. An inventory. His voice was flat, professional, horrible. "I know I had it. I can see the shape of where it was. Like a river on an old map that's since gone underground." He laughed, one short broken note. "I even remember that I used to think of it every morning. First thing. Before I opened my eyes. I'd play it like a tune so the day couldn't take it from me." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "I don't remember why mornings needed it."

"Jude—"

"Sigil," he said to the merchant, rough. "Earn it."

What the merchant produced next made even the nearby stalls go quiet. It was a disc of dark glass the size of a coin, and it did not reflect the lantern-light; it reflected *Sera*, standing beside Jude, and then it reflected the version of her that had lived in the Court, and then a version Sera didn't recognize at all, and she stepped back from it sharply.

"A true forgery," the merchant said, wrapping it in gray silk. "It carries the shape of a soul without the substance — a mold, not meat. The wards will read it, sigh, and wave it through, because the wards are lazy and the Magistrate is proud. It will hold together for perhaps an hour inside the reliquary's presence. After that—" she shrugged, a sound of folding paper, "—it becomes what all forgeries become. But you will be finished by then. Won't you?"

"Yes," said Jude, in the voice of a man who intended to be finished whether he survived it or not.

"One more courtesy, free of charge, because I liked your bargaining and I will miss your memory terribly — it was a fine vintage." The merchant's well-eyes fixed on Sera. "Archivist. When you stand before the glass tonight, remember: the reliquary does not guard the souls. The souls guard the reliquary. They sing the wards awake. If you hear a song you know—" her gaze flicked meaningfully to Jude, "—do not answer it in kind. Answer it with something new. The old songs open doors. New ones break hinges."

Then the black cloth settled over her stall like a bird folding its wings, and where the merchant had been there was only a crease in the cavern wall, and the sense of having been, briefly, permitted.

They climbed back up through the tiers in silence. It was only at the mouth of the passage, where Ironhollow's iron light replaced the Market's impossible colors, that Sera finally spoke.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That's inadequate. I know it's inadequate. But I watched, and I need you to know that I *watched*, and I won't forget what it cost, even if you can't."

Jude stopped walking. For a moment she thought he might collapse, or rage, or do any of the things a man ought to do after selling the last warm thing he owned to a creature made of paperwork.

Instead he took out the ledger — his grandmother's ledger, the bargain written in her blood — and held it under Ironhollow's cold light.

"I lost the memory," he said slowly. "But not the *facts* of it. The facts are still here, in her own hand. Rain that winter. The kitchen with the tin roof. The way she sang." His jaw tightened. "I can't feel it anymore. But I can *read* it. And Sera — I've been reading this ledger wrong from the start. I thought the geometry matched the vault because the Court designed both." He tapped the page, once, twice. "It doesn't match the vault. The vault matches *this*. The Court didn't design its defenses around its own power. It designed them around its debts. Around *her* signature. Which means the founding theft and my grandmother's bargain are drawn from the same hand—"

"—the same *pattern*," Sera breathed, and the archivist in her woke up roaring. "Jude. The first theft is written into every contract the Court ever made. Your family's debt isn't separate from the founding lie. It's a *copy*. A descendant. If the original name breaks the Court—"

"Every copy breaks with it." He closed the ledger. His eyes were dry still, but something in them had changed — grief transmuted, right before her eyes, into the cold bright fuel of purpose. "Come on. We have an hour of forgery and a vault full of singing souls."

They started toward the upper galleries, toward the reliquary, toward the trap they both suspected was waiting.

And behind them, far below in the dark of the Night Market, a single glass bell chimed — soft, and clear, and unmistakably playing a tune that had just been sold, a kitchen melody with rain behind it, calling across the cavern to a man who no longer knew why it made him want to weep.
