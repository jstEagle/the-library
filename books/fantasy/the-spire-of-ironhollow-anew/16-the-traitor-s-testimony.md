## 16. The Traitor's Testimony

They dressed her for the occasion.

Not in finery — in her old archive grey, the long coat with the ink-stained cuffs she had worn for sixty years of cataloguing other people's sins. Someone had laundered it. Someone had pressed the creases flat with an iron hot enough to smell of scorched thread. The Court Beneath the Mountain did nothing without intention, and Sera understood this one perfectly: they wanted her to feel like herself again. They wanted her comfortable in the shape of what she had been, so that when she betrayed Jude Nakamura — and they were certain she would — the betrayal would fit her like a tailored glove.

She stood between two wardens of woven paper while a servant brushed the dust from her hair with fingers that were slightly too cold and slightly too many. In the polished obsidian of the cell wall, her reflection waited for her, and she did not let herself look at whether it lagged. That was Jude's affliction. Hers was older and quieter: a face that had learned, over decades beneath the mountain, to arrange itself into whatever truth the room required.

*You are going to lie to them,* she told the reflection. *And you are going to be good at it.*

The reflection said nothing. It never did. That was one mercy Ironhollow had never thought to steal from her.

* * *

The Hall of Accounts was smaller than she remembered, or perhaps she had grown larger somewhere on the surface, in the years of wind and rain and books that smelled of weather instead of wax. The iron chandeliers still turned slowly overhead, each link engraved with a debtor's name, and the light they threw was the color of old coins. Nobles lined the walls in their borrowed finery — souls strung around their throats like river pearls, glimmering faintly with light that belonged to someone else. She recognized a few. Lady Vessane, wearing what looked like a child's laughter. Old Corvel Ashgrave, whose pendant pulsed with a soldier's courage he had purchased secondhand and never once used.

And at the center, behind the great desk of black iron where all debts came to be weighed, stood the Magistrate of Debts.

He had not changed. That was the horror of him. When Sera was young and newly catalogued, she had believed he was simply ageless the way fae were ageless. Now she knew better. He was not ageless; he was *empty*. Whatever he had been before the founding had been poured out of him like wine from a cracked cup, and what remained wore his face the way a signet ring wears a seal. His warmth, when he turned it on you, was the warmth of a hearth in a house where no one has ever lived.

"Sera." Her name in his mouth sounded like an entry in a ledger. "Archivist. Runaway. Debtor." He smiled, and the smile reached every corner of his face except the eyes. "Thrice-named. You see how carefully we keep track."

"I taught half your clerks their penmanship," she said. "I'd have been disappointed if you didn't."

A ripple through the watching nobles. Someone laughed softly and then thought better of it.

"Still sharp." The Magistrate gestured, and a chair slid across the floor toward her — iron-legged, angled just so, positioned lower than his own seat but higher than the floor. A chair for someone being *received*, not condemned. Every detail a message. "Sit. We have much to discuss, you and I. Or rather—" the smile deepened, "—you have much to confess, and I have much already to know. But I find I enjoy hearing it in the culprit's own voice. There is a texture to confession that no ledger can reproduce."

Sera sat. The chains of woven paper on her wrists whispered as she folded her hands — they had not removed them, she noticed, though they would have parted at a breath. Another message: *we hold you, but gently, because we expect you to come to us freely.*

"The map," the Magistrate said.

* * *

Here is what the Court taught her, in sixty years beneath the mountain, in the archive stacks where forbidden names slept in lead-lined boxes: that truth was a currency, and like all currencies it could be counterfeited by anyone patient enough. Here is what the surface taught her, in three years of rain and honest work: that a lie told well enough becomes a kind of architecture — load-bearing — and everything built on it will stand until someone tests the walls.

Jude's map was beautiful. She had studied it for two days in her cell, memorizing it down to the grain of the paper, because that was the price of selling it convincingly: you could not offer a forgery with hesitation in your hands. The vault of the reliquary, drawn in his precise smuggler's cartography, with its approaches marked and its blind corridors inked in his confident hand. And threaded through it, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent decades reading documents for a living, the flaw he had built into it — the false bearing, the corridor that did not exist, the weakness that would draw the Court's defenders like flies to a wound.

They would test the walls tonight. Her task was to make sure they tested the wrong ones.

"You were found in possession of this." The Magistrate lifted the map from his desk, holding it between two fingers as though it might bite. "Drawn by the Nakamura man. Detailing the reliquary vault. I wonder—" and here his voice went soft and terrible and warm, the voice he had used on her when she was nineteen and had just been caught with a stolen page hidden in her sleeve, "—I wonder how it came to be in your cell. Wards do not fail in my Court, Sera. Doors do not open themselves. Paper does not walk. Which means either my security is compromised—"

"It is," Sera said. "That's why I'm here."

"—or," the Magistrate continued smoothly, as though she had not spoken, "someone *wanted* it found."

Silence. The chandeliers turned. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped onto iron with the patience of a thing counting centuries.

"Both," Sera said. "Let me tell you how it happened, Magistrate. All of it. Because you're right — there is a plan. And because I'd rather you hear the shape of it from me than piece it together from interrogations and lose the parts that matter."

His eyes narrowed by a degree. Around the hall, the nobles leaned in, souls glinting. Give them a show, she thought. Give them a traitor turning coat — it's the oldest story in any court, and courts love nothing so much as a story that flatters their cynicism.

*Become what they always said you were,* she told herself, and hated how easily the door opened. *Just for tonight. Just far enough.*

* * *

"I met him on the surface eight months ago," she began. "He came to me with a prophecy book and a dead man's effects, asking questions about the Court's record-keeping. I should have reported him. Instead I saw—" she paused, and let the pause do its work, "—an opportunity. You know what my sentence was, Magistrate. Catalogue the debts, serve the terms, and after three hundred years, consideration for restoration. Three hundred years. I've served sixty. I was not going to live long enough to see the end of my own ledger."

"So you made a private arrangement."

"I made a *trade*. My knowledge of your security for his maps — he is the only man alive who knows the Thornfell roads, and those roads run under your mountain like veins under skin. Yes, we planned to reach the reliquary. Yes, I gave him the layout. And yes—" she leaned forward, and dropped her voice, and watched hunger move through the assembled court like wind through wheat, "—when your wardens took us, I made certain the map stayed in my cell. Because I needed leverage. Because a prisoner with nothing to trade is a prisoner who gets *dissolved*, Magistrate, and I have seen what dissolution looks like in your ledgers."

It was true. Every word of it was true, or true enough, and that was the craft of it — Jude had understood, drawing the false map in the lamplight of the night market, that the best lie was a truth with one bone removed. She had come to sell his plan. So she sold it. All of it. The approach through the drowned stair. The forged sigil. The timing keyed to the Court's celebration. She laid it out before the Magistrate in the flat professional cadence of an archivist filing a report, and she watched him believe it, and the believing sat in his hollow face like light in an empty house.

Only the flaw she kept back. Only the corridor that wasn't there. Only the fact that every defense Jude wanted thinned — the western galleries, the root-stairs, the deep archive — she recommended reinforcing, knowing the recommendation would do the opposite, knowing that a court which trusts its informants moves its soldiers where the informant points *away* from danger, not toward it.

Trust, the Court had taught her, was a debt. Tonight she intended to collect on it.

"And the Nakamura man?" the Magistrate asked. "Where is he?"

"Gone." She let her shoulders sag — a small collapse, a woman spending her last coin. "The shadow took him. His own shadow, Magistrate, the fragment you've been cultivating — I don't know what bargain they struck, but it walked him out through the western galleries before dawn. Toward the root-stairs." She said it clearly. She said it *loudly*. Let the whole court hear where he was not.

The Magistrate regarded her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a dry, papery sound, like pages turning.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Sera?" he said. "Nothing. Nothing at all. But I recognize the quality of the work." He rose, and the chandeliers slowed above him as though even the iron deferred to his attention. "The testimony is accepted. Your cooperation will be entered in the ledger. Restoration of your standing will be... considered." The word hung there, fat with promise and poison both. "In return, you will assist in the recovery of the escaped asset. The shadow reports he carries something of ours."

"He carries his own soul-shard's memory," Sera said carefully. "What he learned touching it."

"Memory." The Magistrate smiled. "Yes. We are very interested in what the reliquary showed him. Memories are heavy things, Archivist. Heavy enough to bring down a court, if dropped from sufficient height." He waved a hand, dismissive, almost gentle. "Rest. Eat. Tomorrow you will guide our wardens along the routes you described. Consider yourself—" the smile widened by a fraction, "—reinstated."

The woven-paper chains fell away from her wrists.

She walked out of the Hall of Accounts upright, spine straight, face composed into gratitude she did not feel, past rows of nobles whose borrowed souls flickered as she passed — and only when the doors closed behind her did she allow herself to breathe, and only then did she understand the cost.

Her hands were shaking. Not with fear. With the ease of it. Sixty years since she had lied in these halls and the machinery had started without a single grinding gear, smooth as water, fluent as breath. The Court had always said she was a creature of deception — a half-breed, a mongrel archivist, a thing that belonged to neither world and therefore could belong to anyone. She had spent three years on the surface proving otherwise, proving she could be *one* thing, *hers*, whole.

Tonight she had become their proof instead. And the worst of it, the part that sat in her chest like swallowed iron, was that she would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, without hesitation — because Jude was down there in the dark, alone with a shadow that wore his grandmother's face, and the only way to save him was to keep being, convincingly and completely, exactly what the Court had always believed she was.

*Don't trust it,* she had told him through the paper chains. She hoped he hadn't. She hoped he trusted her precisely as little as she now trusted herself.

* * *

They gave her quarters in the east wing — her old quarters, she realized, the narrow stone room with the shelf where she had once kept a single pressed flower from the surface, thirty years ago. The flower was gone. The shelf held a carafe of wine and a plate of food too fine for a prisoner and exactly right for an asset.

Sera ate none of it. She sat by the arrow-slit window, high above the buried city, and listened.

Ironhollow was singing tonight. She could feel it through the stone — the low harmonic hum the Court made when it moved its weight, when wardens marched and wards were redrawn and the great gears of the mountain shifted their attention. And the song was moving west. Toward the galleries. Toward the root-stairs. Exactly as she had steered it, the Court pouring its strength into defending places where Jude had already been, leaving thin the paths he actually needed —

— and then the song stuttered.

Sera went still. Below her window, in the plaza of the buried city, the iron chandeliers swung against their own rhythm, and every soul-pendant in the Court flared at once, a thousand stolen lights crying out in a single discordant chord. Somewhere beneath her, deeper than the vault, deeper than the archive, something very old had just been touched.

The mountain's root. The name under the mountain.

Sera pressed her hand flat against the cold sill and felt the tremor run up through the stone, through her arm, into the place behind her ribs where sixty years of Court-taught fluency lived — and for the first time since she'd knelt in that hall and lied with a straight spine, she smiled.

"Hold on, cartographer," she whispered to the dark below. "I'm coming down the other way."

She was already pulling the shelf from the wall, where thirty years ago a flower had hidden, and where tonight — if her memory of the archive's blueprints was half as good as her memory of lies — an unmapped stair still waited, leading down.

Down past the vault. Down past the guards. Down to wherever a man went when he went looking for the truth a court was built on.

Down to the name.
