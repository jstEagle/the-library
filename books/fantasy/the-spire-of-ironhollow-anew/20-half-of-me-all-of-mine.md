## 20. Half of Me, All of Mine

The iron was still singing when Jude reached the reliquary.

It sang in a register no instrument could hold — a low, sweet keening that lived somewhere between a bell and a throat, and it came from everywhere at once now that the Court's iron had unbound itself from its moorings. Chandeliers that had hung for a century like frozen rain were swinging, slowly, shedding rust and light in equal measure. The nobles of Ironhollow had scattered into the dark corners of their own hall, souls unclasped from their collars, names slipping off them like wet silk. Somewhere behind him, Sera stood before the assembled remnant of the Court with her testimony still burning in the air, and somewhere below his feet, the mountain's true name lay open to the sky for the first time in a hundred years.

But here — here in the small vaulted room where his life had been kept on a shelf — there was only the reliquary, its glass lid standing open as if it had always meant to be open, and inside it, the shard.

His shard. The sliver of him that had been pawned before he drew breath.

It looked like nothing. That was the first cruelty of it. A splinter of pale glass no longer than his smallest finger, shot through with veins of gold that moved when he moved, that brightened when he thought of home. It should have been larger. It should have been *more*. A soul-piece ought to blaze; this thing glowed the way an ember glows in a dead hearth — stubbornly, quietly, waiting for someone to breathe on it.

Jude reached out and did not take it. Not yet.

He had learned that much beneath the mountain: you do not grab at what is owed to you. You let it come to terms with you first. So he stood with his hand hovering above the glass, close enough that the shard's light pooled against his skin like water, and he waited, and the singing changed. It bent toward him. It found the shape of a voice he knew better than any road he'd ever mapped.

*There you are,* said the voice of his grandmother, rising out of the shard the way heat rises off summer stone. *Look how tall you got.*

Jude's breath caught. He had heard her voice in dreams for eleven years, ever since the fever took her, but dreams were soft-edged things, forgiving things. This was not a dream. This was memory wearing sound like a coat, and it fit her exactly — the slight rasp at the end of each sentence, the way she always said *there you are* instead of hello, as though every meeting were a homecoming she'd been counting on.

"You're in there," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "You've been in there all along."

*Someone had to keep it warm,* she said. *Soul-shards go cold without tending. Cold shards turn bitter. Bitter shards turn into things.* She paused, and the gold in the glass dimmed and brightened like something breathing. *I tended yours. Every day. Even when I hated myself for putting it there.*

"I know," Jude said, and then, because the Court had taught him that lies have weight and truths have wings, he made himself say the harder thing: "I don't forgive you yet. But I want to."

The shard flickered. His grandmother's laugh came out of it, short and startled, the laugh she'd had when someone caught her doing something kind and she didn't want credit for it. *Honest to the last,* she said. *Your grandfather used to say you'd argue with your own shadow. He had no idea how right he was.*

* * *

The shadow arrived while he was still standing there.

He felt it before he saw it — a coolness at his left side, a subtraction of torchlight — and when he turned, it was peeling itself up off the wall the way smoke peels off a doused fire, gathering into the shape it had worn since the camp outside Duskfall: his shape, his height, his shoulders, but with his grandmother's face laid over it like a mask of silvered paper. Her eyes. Her deep-set, patient, unfooled eyes.

It had guided him here. Through the vault corridors, past the warden-posts, down the stair that wasn't on any map because the Court had never needed one — it had simply known the way, the way blood knows the way back to the heart. And it had done it without asking for anything, which in Ironhollow was more suspicious than any demand.

Now it asked.

*Take me with you,* the shadow said, in his grandmother's voice, and the words fell into the vault like stones into a well. *When you take the shard. Take both halves. Keep us together. I can be whole again, Jude. We can be whole. You'll barely notice the difference.*

And that was the lie, and it was a beautiful lie, and it was told in the voice of the only person whose lies he had never once checked against a map.

Because he *would* notice the difference. He understood it now, standing in the wrecked heart of a court built on stolen names: the shadow was not his grandmother. The shadow was the piece of himself the Court had been cultivating for thirty years — fed on his fear, watered with his grief, taught to speak in the voice that would move him most. It had grown fat on the shape of her love. It wore her face the way the Court's nobles wore souls: borrowed, beautiful, and not theirs.

If he took it back — if he let the fragment merge with the shard and the shard merge with him — he would carry Ironhollow out of Ironhollow. He would carry a thing that had learned to bargain, to hoard, to weigh love against debt and find love wanting. He would walk into the sunlight half-mortal and half-Court, and the Court's oldest trick was that its pieces, once swallowed, never stopped working from inside.

"No," Jude said. And then, gently, because gentleness was also a truth and he had learned to spend those too: "No. But thank you. For the stairs. For the gate. For grandmother's face, even though it isn't hers."

The shadow flinched. It had expected refusal — it had offered him bargains before, and he had refused before — but it had not expected *thanks*. Gratitude was a currency the Court had never minted. It had nothing to exchange it for.

*What will you do with me?* it asked, and for the first time since the camp at Duskfall, the voice underneath his grandmother's showed through — thin, young, uncertain. His own voice, aged backward. The voice of the boy who had been collateral before he was born. *The Court is falling. Debts dissolve. I'm nobody's asset anymore. I'm just—*

"Just a piece of me that grew up alone in the dark," Jude finished. "I know."

He took the shard from the reliquary.

It came to his hand willingly, warm as a held breath, and the singing stopped — not faded, *stopped*, the way a conversation stops when both people finally understand each other. In his palm the little splinter of glass blazed gold, and through its light he could see everything it had seen: a ledger signed in blood, a famine winter, a family saved, a boy born already spoken for. He could see his grandmother's hands, younger than he'd ever known them, pressing the shard into the Magistrate's palm while her other hand held a sleeping infant — his mother — who would grow up and die on a mountain road, never knowing what had been traded to keep her alive long enough to live.

Grief went through him like weather. He let it. He had spent three weeks under the mountain learning to suppress, to smile, to bow, to plan theft under the eyes of beings who tasted lies — and now, with the Court in ruins around him, suppression was over. He grieved openly, hugely, in the middle of the falling hall, and no one watched, and that was its own strange mercy.

Then he turned to the shadow, and he did the last thing the prophecy had asked of him without knowing it was asking.

"Come here," he said.

* * *

The shadow came. Of course it came. It had been coming to him his whole life; it had simply been walking ahead of him until now.

They met in the center of the vault, man and fragment, and where they touched, the air went quiet and thick as held breath. Jude pressed the shard against his own chest — against the sternum, over the place where his heartbeat lived — and spoke aloud, because some things must be witnessed to be true:

"I release you. Not sell you, not spend you, not trade you. Release. Whatever part of my grandmother's love is tangled in you, I keep. Whatever part of the Court's hunger is tangled in you, I leave here, in the ruin of the house that made it. Go home. Rest. Be done."

And he forgave her. Out loud. In the heart of Ironhollow, with the iron chandeliers swaying loose overhead and the founding lie still ringing off the walls, he said the words he had carried up the whole mountain like a stone in his boot:

"Grandmother — I forgive the bargain. You were starving and they were patient and you loved us the only way the world left open to you. You bought our lives with a piece of mine, and I am alive to stand here and say that I forgive it. The debt ends with me. Not paid. *Ended.* There's a difference, and I finally know what it is."

The shadow shivered. Then it came apart.

Not violently — nothing in this moment was allowed to be violent — but the way frost comes apart on a warm window: slowly, beautifully, surrendering its shapes one edge at a time. The silvered mask of his grandmother's face lifted away first, and for one impossible instant it hung in the air alone, and it smiled at him. Not the Court's smile. Hers. The one from the photograph he kept folded in the back of his field journal, the one taken the summer before the fever, the one where she was squinting against the sun because she refused to wear the hat he'd bought her.

Then the rest followed — the shoulders, the hands, the long dark line of him — dissolving upward into the vault's high dark, and the last of it went out like a candle going out on purpose, glad of the dark, glad of the rest.

And the shard sank into his chest.

* * *

He felt it happen the way you feel a river close over a thrown stone: a ripple, a settling, a new silence. Something clicked home behind his sternum that had been missing since before his first breath, and the strangest part was how ordinary it felt. No fire. No vision. Just warmth, spreading outward from the center of him like tea going through a cold body, and then — wholeness. Not the Court's wholeness, which was hoarding. A smaller, truer thing. A cup filled to its own brim and no further.

Half-mortal. Wholly his own.

He tested it immediately, the way he tested everything — the way a cartographer tests ground by walking it. He looked at his reflection in the fallen glass of the reliquary, and for the first time since the tollgate at Duskfall, the reflection moved *with* him. Not after him. With him. When he raised his hand, it raised its hand in the same instant, and Jude Nakamura, who had trusted maps above all things because maps never lied about distance, felt something in his chest unknot that had been tied since the night he read his own name in a dead man's book.

His shadow, cast on the wall by the last burning chandelier, was only a shadow. Flat. Obedient. His.

He walked out of the vault the way he'd walked into so many places in his life: quietly, checking his exits, noting the doors. But the exits didn't matter anymore. Nothing in Ironhollow could hold him now. The debts had dissolved; the iron had unbound; the Magistrate was dust and hollow memory somewhere behind him, crumbling into the same nothing the Court had always secretly been. The halls around him were emptying of their borrowed light, going gray, going honest, going dark — a house of stolen names forgetting its thefts one name at a time.

He found Sera at the top of the great stair.

She was standing in the doorway that opened onto the ascent passage, and she was crying, and she was laughing, and she was doing both at once in a way that only people who have survived something unspeakable can manage. Behind her, the last of the Court's nobility were drifting out of the hall like moths leaving a lamp that has finally gone out — unburdened, unnamed, free in the way that prisoners are free when the prison burns down and they realize they never remembered why the walls were there.

"There you are," she said to him, and the echo of his grandmother's phrase in her mouth hit him like a wave, and he understood that some words are inherited whether you mean to pass them on or not.

"Here I am," he said. "All of me. Well." He shrugged, and the shrug was lighter than any shrug of his life. "Most of me. The good accounting."

"The Court's ledgers are ash," Sera said. "Every debt, every name, every bargain — dissolved. Do you understand what that means? There are people on the surface who've been paying interest for generations who will wake up tomorrow and owe nothing, and never know why."

"They'll know," Jude said. "People always know. They just won't be able to prove it. Same as the roads." He smiled — really smiled, the crooked one, the one his mother had said could get him out of any trouble he could talk his way into. "That's how we'll know it worked. Things will stop adding up, all across Thornfell, and no one will ever find the error."

They climbed.

The passage up was long and it was steep and it was lit by nothing but the shard-light leaking faintly through his own skin, gold at the seams of him, and Sera walked beside him with her hand resting in the crook of his arm like a woman being escorted home from a war. Behind them, Ironhollow settled into itself with a sound like a long exhale — iron cooling, chandeliers lowering themselves gently to the floor rather than crashing, the mountain taking back its own weight. The Court Beneath the Mountain was becoming simply the mountain, which was all it should ever have been.

At the last turning, where the smuggler road broke toward daylight, Jude stopped and looked back once.

He had expected to feel triumph. What he felt instead was inheritance. That was the word for it, the true one, the one he'd carry up the last mile and out into the sun: not victory, not vengeance, not even freedom, quite. *Inheritance.* Because his grandmother's love had been real — it had been in the shard all along, tended daily through decades of cold, and the Court had never managed to counterfeit it no matter how carefully it dressed its shadow in her face. The debt had never been the inheritance. The love had been. The debt was just the wrapping paper, and wrapping paper is meant to be torn open and thrown away.

He kept the love. He left the debt in the dark, in the ruin, in the honest dirt of the mountain, where it belonged.

"We're almost there," Sera said. "I can smell pine. Actual pine. Do you know how long it's been since I smelled something that wasn't iron or ink?"

"Longer than me," Jude said, "and I've been smelling iron for three weeks." He put his hand flat against the tunnel wall — real stone, damp, threaded with roots that had never heard of courts or ledgers — and pushed through the last curtain of hanging moss.

Daylight hit him like a blessing.

It was late afternoon on the surface, gold going amber, the kind of light cartographers chase and never catch. The Thornfell spread out below the cave mouth in ridges and valleys he had drawn a hundred times from a hundred angles, and every line of them was exactly where he had left them. The mountain had rearranged itself for the Court; it had never dared touch the surface. His maps were still true. The world was still true. He stood in the light and felt the shard hum once, softly, in his chest — his grandmother's voice, wordless now, just warmth — and then go quiet, the way a song goes quiet when it has finished saying what it came to say.

Sera stepped out beside him and turned her face up to the sun, and flinched, and stayed anyway. Half-fae, standing in full daylight, choosing it. Her eyes watered and her smile didn't waver and Jude watched her and understood that whatever she had given up down there, she was spending the change on this: light, and altitude, and a road with no tolls.

"So," she said, when her eyes adjusted. "Where does a man go, the day after he unmakes a court?"

Jude looked out at the ranges — north, where the unmapped country began, the white spaces on every chart but his — and then down the near slope, toward the valley, toward the lowlands, toward a workshop with a cold stove and a wall full of pins.

"Home first," he said. "There's a book I need to burn."
