## 22. Epilogue: First Light on Thornfell

The fire had burned down to a good working heat by the time Jude Nakamura lifted the prophecy book from the shelf where it had sat untouched for a year — spine cracked, cover scarred, smelling faintly of iron and old rain.

He held it a moment in both hands, weighing it the way he weighed everything: like cargo, like something that could be traded or lost or stolen. It was none of those things now. It was just paper and thread and the ghost of a bargain that no one alive had any claim on anymore.

"Last chance," he said to it. "Any objections?"

The book said nothing. Books that answered questions had been the problem, once.

He crouched at the hearth of the workshop — his workshop, the word still settling into him like a stone into riverbed — and opened it to the end. He'd checked the last pages every month since Ironhollow fell, the way you check a wound for fever. First the entries had stopped appearing. Then the ink that remained had faded from black to brown to the color of nothing much at all, letters dissolving like salt in warm water. And three weeks ago, on the anniversary of the night Sera had knocked on his door with a stolen page and shaking hands, he'd turned to the final leaf and found it blank. Not empty-blank. *Clean* blank. The kind of blank a page has before anyone decides what belongs on it.

Tonight's date was not written anywhere in it. Tonight's hour belonged to no one but him.

Jude fed the book to the fire a few pages at a time, because burning a thing whole felt too much like fleeing, and he had never been good at fleeing. The vellum curled and flared. The binding caught last, the leather going soft and bright before it blackened, and for one breath he thought he saw — not words, exactly, but the *shape* of words, the memory of handwriting — lift on the smoke and come apart against the chimney stone.

His grandmother's voice did not sing out of the flames. He had released her months ago, down in the dark under Ironhollow, and whatever echo of her the Court had worn like a mask had gone with the Court itself. What remained of her lived where he'd chosen to keep it: in the ledger he still couldn't throw away, in the smell of cedar oil on an old compass, in the stubbornness with which he refused to leave a mountain unmeasured. Love without debt. He was still learning how heavy that was, and how light.

When the last page went to ash, he stirred the coals with the poker until there was nothing left to read.

* * *

Morning came up over Thornfell the way it always did this time of year — slow, gold, and full of birdsong with opinions. Frost silvered the window of the workshop, and through it the mountains stood in ranks like patient giants, their eastern faces catching first light while their shadows lay long and blue across the valley.

Jude pinned the new map to the wall.

It had taken eleven months to draw. Eleven months of walking roads that existed nowhere else, of pacing out passes he'd found half-buried since Ironhollow collapsed in on itself and took its rewritten geography down with it, of sitting on cold ridgelines with numb fingers and ink that froze in the pot until Sera leaned over his shoulder, breathed warmth onto the nib, and told him he was being ridiculous and romantic in roughly equal measure.

It was the Thornfell as no one had ever drawn it — as only two people in the world could have drawn it. The smuggler roads were all there, the ones he'd kept secret for fifteen years, but they were joined now by the ways beneath them: the deep roads, the Court roads, the stair that spiraled past the vault level down toward the root of the mountain, marked with a single careful notation in his own hand. *Sealed. Do not open. Some doors are better as stories.* And beside it, small enough that only someone looking would find it, a second notation: *She is free.*

That one wasn't cartography. He'd put it in anyway. A map, he had learned, could hold more than distance.

Sera arrived while he was still pressing the pins home — four brass tacks, one at each corner, driven in with the heel of his hand the way his grandmother had taught him to seat a frame. She came in with the cold still clinging to her shoulders and a basket of eggs she'd traded for at the valley farm, and she stopped just inside the door the way she always did when she wanted him to notice that she'd noticed something.

"You hung it crooked," she said.

"It's not crooked. The wall's crooked."

"The wall is fine. You're sentimental about your left hand." She set the basket down, crossed the room, and straightened the upper-left pin a quarter turn with two fingers — a correction so small it changed nothing and meant everything. Then she stepped back beside him and looked at the map properly, arms folded, morning light moving across it like water.

"There," she said quietly. "That's us."

Jude followed her finger. In the lower corner, beneath the legend and the scale bar, where other cartographers stamped their guild marks and their patrons' crests, he had inked two small figures: a man with a satchel of maps, and a woman with a book under her arm, standing at the mouth of a pass. No names. Names invited debts, and he was finished with debts. But anyone who knew them would know.

"Guild will want copies," he said.

"The guild can want."

"They'll pay."

"The guild," Sera said, "can pay." But she was smiling when she said it, the particular smile she saved for the moments when the world offered them something ordinary — money, trade, a Tuesday — and they got to take it like people who hadn't once stood in a court of iron chandeliers and bargained with things that wore souls as jewelry.

He turned from the wall and caught himself in the window glass — frost-edged, imperfect, honest. His reflection moved when he moved. All of it, all at once, the whole of him arriving in each gesture on time, no lag, no drift, no shadow a half-step behind wearing someone else's face. He had stopped checking months ago. He checked anyway, sometimes, the way a man who has been starved keeps bread in his pockets long after the famine ends. Old habits. Good ones, maybe. The habit of noticing what is yours.

His shadow lay on the floorboards where the firelight threw it, attached at the heels, behaving itself, keeping his grandmother's shape the way all shadows keep everyone's shape: by having none at all.

"Sera," he said. "Do you ever dream about it? The Court?"

She considered the question with the seriousness she gave all true questions. "I dream about the stacks," she said at last. "The archive. I'm shelving books and I know exactly where everything goes and I feel — safe, which is the trick of it, that's the whole trick, it makes the cage feel like competence." She shrugged. "And then I wake up here, and the bed is cold on your side because you get up at dawn like a farmer, and I remember that I chose the cold side of the bed. That's the difference. Down there, safety was given to me. Up here, everything is chosen. Even the discomfort." She glanced up at him. "Especially the discomfort. You're very uncomfortable. You've been hovering near that wall all morning like the map might escape."

"I'm deciding whether it's done."

"Is it?"

Jude looked at the Thornfell, redrawn. Every line of it drawn by his own hand, from his own feet, in his own time. There had been a year — most of his life, if he was honest — when he'd believed his story was already written, inked in some old book by some old bargain, and that the best he could do was move well within the margins someone else had ruled. He had been collateral before he was born. He had been an entry in a ledger before he was a boy. And then he had walked into the heart of the thing that owned him and paid its whole account with a single truth, and walked out owning the only thing that had ever really been at stake: the next page.

Some things are inherited, he thought. Her cheekbones. Her stubbornness. The cedar-oil compass on the shelf, and the grief, and the love underneath the grief, which turned out to be the part that lasted. Those had come to him whether he asked for them or not.

But the roads on that wall — the ones only he knew, the ones he'd chosen to share with exactly one person — those were not inheritance. Those were made. Drawn line by line by someone who had decided, freely, at great cost, what kind of man to be.

"It's done," he said.

He took down the old map then — the original Thornfell survey, the famous one, the one that had made his name among the guild and the smugglers alike, with its confident lines and its careful blanks and its author's mark in the corner: *surveyed and drawn by J. Nakamura*. He'd loved it fiercely once. Now it looked like a map of a country that had never quite existed — accurate to the inch and wrong at the root, because it had been drawn by a man trying to prove he was real instead of a man who simply was.

He rolled it, tied it with cord, and set it in the chest with the other relics: the ledger, the letter in his grandmother's hand, the forged soul-sigil gone dull and dead as tin. Things worth keeping. Things no longer running the house.

"What will you do today?" Sera asked, though he could tell from her tone she already suspected.

"Walk," Jude said. "There's a ridge above the Duskfall road I never mapped. Too steep, back when steep mattered to me. I thought—" He stopped, laughed a little at himself. "I thought I'd see what's there. If anything's there. Doesn't matter if there isn't."

"That's the whole point of you now," Sera said. "That's my favorite thing about you. It doesn't matter, and you go anyway."

They gathered the gear together, easy as breathing, two people who had learned each other's rhythms in the dark and found they worked even better in the light. At the door Jude paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back once at the workshop — the fire banked, the new map hanging true on its four pins, the window filling with morning.

Somewhere below, the valley was waking. Somewhere above, the Thornfell stood waiting, unmapped in its high places, holding its small secrets the way mountains do — not as debts, but as invitations.

Jude Nakamura stepped out into first light, and began to walk.
