## 1. The Taste of Borrowed Years

The night market in Ironhollow smelled of tallow and wet stone, of fried onions gone cold in their pans, of the particular sourness that rose off a crowd too poor to be afraid properly. Fear was expensive here. The people who came down into the Lantern Rows after dark were the kind who had already spent their fear on rent.

Silas Fairweather moved through them the way water moves through cracks — unhurried, unremarkable, going exactly where the gaps let him. He wore a coat the color of old ash with nine pockets, each one lined in lead-threaded silk, because you could never be too careful about what leaked. In his left breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, sat his tray: two dozen small glass vials on felt, each stoppered with wax, each holding nothing anyone could see.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole trade.

"Charms," he'd tell the curious ones, the ones who stopped at his folding table under the crooked lantern he'd hung from a butcher's awning. "Good luck. Steady hands. Sleep without dreams." He'd hold a vial up to the light and let them squint at the faint shimmer inside — a shimmer like heat off summer cobbles, like the ghost of something. "Bottled years," he'd say, when they leaned close enough. "Harmless as rain."

None of it was harmless. All of it was stolen. And every word out of his mouth was a small, practiced lie, worn smooth as a river stone from use.

He'd been working the Rows for eleven years now, ever since the Wardens burned the last of the hedge-witches out of the Tannery Quarter and magic went from crime to contagion. These days nobody spoke the word aloud. They said *the trouble*, or *the old sickness*, or nothing at all, just touched iron and looked away. The Wardens in their grey coats walked the market every third night, faces smooth as eggs, eyes the color of dishwater, and everyone pretended not to see them, and the Wardens pretended not to see anyone, and the whole city held its breath between patrols like a child hiding in a game that had stopped being fun years ago.

Silas had learned to breathe through it. That was his talent, really — not the theft, not the bottling. The breathing.

* * *

The sailor found him an hour past midnight, which was late even for the Rows.

Silas noticed him first as a wrongness in the crowd's current — a big man moving against the flow, shoulders hunched like a man walking into wind that wasn't there. Salt-cracked hands. A coat that had been good once, navy wool gone grey at the seams. Eyes that kept snagging on things: a paper lantern, a child's wooden boat on a stall, the dark mouth of the drain-channel that ran along the Row's edge carrying runoff toward the river.

Grief, Silas thought. Fresh grief, sea-borne. He'd learned to read it the way other men read weather.

The sailor stopped at the table. Up close he smelled of tar and long voyages and something underneath that Silas's fingers itched to name — a smell like open water, like distance, like a thing most Ironhollow folk had never once smelled in their lives.

"You're the charm-man." Not a question. His voice was gravel dragged over a hull.

"I'm the charm-man," Silas agreed. He smiled the smile he kept for this — warm but not too warm, the smile of a man with nothing to hide and slightly less than nothing interesting about him. "What ails you, friend? Can't sleep? Bad luck with cards?"

The sailor didn't answer right away. He was looking at the vials, at the shimmer in them, and his face did something complicated. Want and revulsion fighting for the same ground.

"They say you sell memories."

Silas let the smile drop by a degree. Careful. "They say a lot of things. I sell charms. Little comforts. If a comfort happens to feel like remembering, well—" he spread his hands, "—that's the charm working, isn't it?"

The sailor's jaw worked. Behind him the market flowed on, oblivious, and somewhere up the Row a Warden patrol turned the corner by the chandler's, three grey coats moving with that terrible unhurried patience they had, like men who knew the night belonged to them and saw no reason to spend it quickly.

Silas watched the sailor watch the Wardens, and understood.

"Come round the back of the tallow-seller's," Silas said quietly. "Better stock there. Things I don't show just anyone."

* * *

The alley behind the tallow-seller's stank of rendered fat and cat, and the lantern-light didn't reach it, and that was the point. Silas set his back against the wall where he could see both ends of the alley and took out three vials instead of the whole tray — never the whole tray, never all your goods in one place, a rule that had kept him alive through two purges and one very bad winter.

"Tell me what you're looking for," he said. "Not what you want to buy. What you're looking for."

The sailor stood with his fists opening and closing at his sides. When he finally spoke, the gravel had gone out of his voice.

"The sea."

Silas kept his face still. Inside, something turned over, slow and cold, like a body rolling in deep water.

"My daughter," the sailor said. "Maren. She was seven. We lived in Saltrow, before — before I shipped out proper. Every morning I'd carry her down to the breakwater and she'd stand on the end of it with her arms out like she could hold the horizon up by herself. Wind in her hair. Gulls screaming. She'd laugh at the gulls like they were telling her jokes." He swallowed. "She died in the fever year. You remember the fever year."

Everyone remembered the fever year. Half of Ironhollow remembered it; the other half had been carried out of it in lime-dusted carts.

"I've got no memory of the sea anymore," the sailor said. "That's the thing nobody tells you. Grief eats outward in. First it takes the sound of them, then the smell, then — I can't hear the sea in my head anymore, charm-man. Twenty-six years a sailor and my own head's gone quiet. It's just rooms now. Empty rooms." His eyes came up, wet and furious and ashamed of being either. "I heard you can put it back. Put a piece of it back. Just for a while. Just so I can sleep one night without the quiet."

And there it was. The moment Silas hated more than any other, the moment that came with every sale, the moment where he could be honest and ruin himself, or be kind and damn them both.

Because he *could* do it. That was the filthy miracle of his trade. Somewhere in his collection — in the cold cellar beneath his tenement, in racks of wax-stoppered glass, hundreds of small stolen hours — he had memories of the sea. Taken from sailors and fishwives and one customs officer who'd wept into his beer about a bay in the south. He had salt-wind and gull-cries and the green-black swell of open water beyond the harbor chain. He had, if he dug deep enough in the racks, something very close to what this man wanted: a morning on a breakwater, a child laughing at gulls.

Not *his* child. Never his child. But near enough that the sailor would feel it, would drown in it gratefully, would wake tomorrow poorer in a way no purse could measure and richer in a way no law could touch.

"That's a rare stock," Silas said. "Sea-memories. Most folk in Ironhollow have never seen the water except as cargo. Costs accordingly."

"How much?"

"Twelve crowns."

The sailor flinched. Twelve crowns was half a month's wage for a deckhand. Silas watched him do the arithmetic — watched him weigh one night of his daughter's sea against weeks of bread — and hated himself with a clean, professional efficiency, the way you hate a tool you're using.

"I've got eight," the sailor said.

Silas should have taken the eight. Any sensible predator takes the eight. But something in the man's face — the specific shape of a father missing a specific child — snagged on something in Silas that he kept locked in a room at the center of himself, a room with a girl's name on the door that he never opened while anyone was watching.

"Ten," Silas said, "and you don't ask me where it came from. Ever. Not tonight, not in five years, not if we meet again when we're both old men."

"Where did it come from?"

"Ten," Silas repeated, "and no questions."

The sailor paid. Ten crowns in mixed silver, counted out with those cracked hands, coin by coin, like a man paying for a coffin.

* * *

Here is how the trade worked, though Silas would have cut out his own tongue before explaining it to the sailor or anyone else in the Rows:

Magic wasn't gone from Ironhollow. Magic was *afraid*. It had gone underground, gone small, gone into the cracks between people, and in those cracks it did one thing better than any other: it carried feeling across a gap. Touch someone's brow with intent, with the old words or even just the old hunger, and you could draw a memory out of them like drawing thread from a spool. It didn't hurt, if you were gentle. It didn't even leave a hole, not at first. It left a smoothness. A place where the memory had been, worn over like a scar, and the person could walk around it for years before they noticed the shape of the absence.

Then you caught the thread in glass. Sealed it with wax and a word. And the memory sat in the vial, alive, faintly warm, shimmering like heat-haze — until someone pressed the vial to their skin, usually the inner wrist where the blood ran shallow, and the memory poured back out and became theirs for a little while. An hour. A night. Sometimes, if the seal was generous, a week.

It was theft. Silas had never once lied to himself about that, whatever lies he told his customers. Every vial on his tray was a stolen thing wearing a pretty dress. The only mercy he'd ever managed to build into the trade was selectivity: he took memories people wouldn't miss soon. A stranger's face glimpsed from a window. The taste of a meal eaten alone. The boredom of a long wait. Small change, picked from the bottoms of lives.

But grief made people *ask*. Grief made people come to him in alleys and beg him to take the big pieces — the sea, the dead, the summers before the war — and pay him for the privilege, and thank him after. And Silas had learned, over eleven years, to tell himself the story that made it bearable: *better the memory live in a bottle than die in a dying man. Better it pass to someone who'll cherish it. I'm not a thief, I'm a ferryman.*

The story worked about six nights out of seven.

The seventh night was tonight.

He'd taken the breakwater memory from a fishwife two winters ago — a woman named Ada Corrigan, who'd sold it to pay her husband's debts after the Wardens fined him into the ground. She hadn't cried when he drew it out. That was the worst part. She'd thanked him. She'd said, *I'd rather it be somewhere warm,* and pressed her palm flat against her own forehead as if checking whether she was still in there.

The memory was of a morning on the north breakwater. Gulls. Wind. A child on the seawall — not Maren, some other woman's child, some stranger's daughter with her arms thrown wide at the horizon — and the enormous patient breathing of the sea underneath everything.

Close enough. Close enough to break a man's heart, which was, Silas supposed, the entire product.

He pressed the vial into the sailor's hand and showed him where to crack the wax seal against his wrist.

"One night," Silas said. "Maybe two, if you're tired enough. When it fades, it fades clean. No sickness after. That I promise."

The sailor broke the seal.

Silas always watched this part. He told himself it was quality control. He watched the shimmer leave the glass like breath on a cold pane, watched it sink into the man's skin at the wrist and climb, watched the sailor's face go slack and then lit from within, watched his cracked lips part, watched his eyes lose the alley, the city, the whole grey weight of Ironhollow, and fill instead with something blue and endless and loud with gulls.

"Oh," the sailor breathed. "*Oh.*"

He stood there in the stinking alley with tears running freely down his face, smiling like a fool, like a bridegroom, like a man seven years younger and a hundred leagues away, and Silas Fairweather stood three feet from him with ten crowns of silver in his pocket and felt absolutely nothing that he could name, which was itself a kind of feeling, the worst one, the one he'd built his whole life to avoid.

"Thank you," the sailor said, when he came back to himself. He said it the way men say it to priests. "Thank you. Whatever it cost you — thank you."

It cost me nothing, Silas didn't say. It cost a fishwife everything, and she thanked me too, and that's the whole rotten engine of it.

"Mind the Wardens on your way home," is what he did say. "Don't be smiling like that in the street. Happy men get questions."

* * *

He packed the tray. He snuffed the lantern. He walked home the long way, as always, doubling back twice through the tannery run-off lanes where even Wardens didn't like to go, and let the city close behind him like water over a dropped stone.

His tenement was in Cindervine Row, four floors of leaning brick that had survived the war by being too poor to burn. He climbed the stairs in the dark without lighting anything — he knew every third step was soft, knew the turn at the landing, knew the door by the splinter at knee height — and let himself into the single room that was his entire kingdom.

The cellar hatch was under the bed. He lifted it and went down into the cold, into the racks.

Hundreds of vials. Thousands of hours. He walked the rows with a candle stub, the way he did every night, reading the little paper labels in his own cramped hand. *Rain on a tin roof. A mother's voice, singing, unnamed. The smell of bread, pre-war. Falling asleep in a hayloft, age unknown. Snow — real snow, mountain snow.* A library of other people's lives, catalogued and dusted and hoarded, and he was rich in it, rich beyond any fence or factor in the Rows, and he stood among it all in the candlelight and felt the truth press up against the inside of his ribs the way it did most nights:

*I am the richest poor man in Ironhollow, and I am alone in a cellar full of other people's happiness.*

He'd sold his sister's memory years ago. That was the room at the center of him, the one with her name on the door. He'd been young and starving and stupid, and a buyer had offered a sum that could keep him fed through a winter, and Lark's memory — Lark, laughing, Lark at nine years old with a scraped knee and a stolen apple, Lark alive — had seemed like the one thing he owned that was worth anything. He'd told himself he could buy it back someday. He'd told himself the buyer would keep it safe.

He'd never found the buyer again. He'd never stopped looking. And every night he came down into the cellar and checked the empty slot on the rack where her vial used to sit, slot number forty-one, and every night the emptiness looked back at him and said, *you did this. You are the thief. Everything else is costume.*

He blew out the candle and climbed back up and lay on his bed above his hoard, listening to the building settle, and told himself the ferryman story one more time, and it worked, mostly, the way a leaking roof works — you almost forget about it until it rains.

* * *

Three nights later, the sailor came back.

Silas was packing up the table near midnight, the market thinning, the Wardens due, when the big man appeared at the edge of the lantern-light — and stopped. Didn't approach. Just stood there, and Silas saw immediately that something was wrong, wrong in the bones of him, wrong the way a ship sits wrong in the water.

"You said it fades clean," the sailor said. His voice was wrecked. "You said no sickness after."

"It does. It doesn't. What happened?" Silas kept his hands moving, packing, casual, eyes flicking up the Row. Two streets away, grey coats. Not close. Not yet.

"There's a woman," the sailor said. "Been following me since yesterday. Grey coat, plain clothes under it. She knows. Charm-man, she *knows* — she asked me where I bought it, she asked me about the seal, she asked—" His voice dropped to a shaking whisper. "She asked me if I'd seen the map."

The word went through Silas like a fishhook.

*Map.*

"What map?" he said. And then, because the sailor's eyes weren't on him anymore — because the sailor was looking past him, up the Row, at the three grey shapes that had stopped moving and were now, with that terrible unhurried patience, starting to move again — Silas understood that the answer didn't matter.

What mattered was that the sailor had led them here. Not meaning to. Meaning to warn him, maybe. But led them all the same.

"Go," Silas said quietly, snapping the tray-case shut. "Out the far end, don't run, walk—"

The sailor grabbed his arm. His grip was a dock-rope tightening. "I never told them about you," he said. "I swear it on Maren. But she'll know you have it. Whatever it is — she'll know you have it."

And then he was gone into the crowd, and the grey coats were thirty paces off and closing, and Silas Fairweather stood in the ruins of his little stall with ten crowns and a case of stolen hours and the word *map* ringing in his skull like a struck bell, already calculating, already turning, already running — because in Ironhollow, competence meant knowing exactly when to stop being competent and start being smoke.

Behind him, unhurried as tide, the Wardens came on.
