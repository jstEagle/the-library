## 2. Blood on the Doorstep

Silas Fairweather had three rules for surviving Ironhollow, and he recited them every night on the climb to his room like a prayer he didn't believe in.

One: never be interesting. Two: never owe anyone anything they could collect. Three: never be home when trouble knocked.

He was breaking rule three at the exact moment trouble stopped knocking and started dying on his doorstep.

It was past midnight, and he'd come up the tenement stairs with his coat smelling of tallow smoke and someone else's ocean — the sailor's memory still ghosting at the edges of him, salt and gull-cries that weren't his and wouldn't stay gone until morning. He had a bottle of the stuff in his inner pocket, sealed in wax, worth two weeks of rent if the charm-sellers' row stayed honest, which it never did.

The stairwell lamp was dead again. He noticed that first. Then he noticed the shape crumpled against his door, dark as a spilled sack, and understood why the wick had drowned: blood soaks light as well as it soaks cloth.

His hand went to the knife in his boot before his mind caught up. Old habit. In Ironhollow you checked for the knife before you checked for the reason.

"Whoever you are," he said, "you've got the wrong landing."

The shape moved. That was the mistake — his mistake — because a corpse doesn't move, and a drunk doesn't move like *that*, slow and deliberate, dragging itself up the wall by its fingertips until it stood swaying in front of him, tall enough that Silas had to tilt his chin back to find its face.

A man. Grey-cloaked, travel-stained, forty or fifty or ageless. And bleeding from a wound in his side that made no sense at all.

Silas had seen wounds. He'd grown up in the Warrens, where knives were cheaper than bread and used more often. He'd seen guttings, slashings, a boy once who'd taken a crossbow bolt through the cheek and lived to complain about the scar. Wounds closed. Even bad ones, even dirty ones, they did what flesh does — they tried.

This one wasn't trying. The man's side was open in a long seam, and the blood came out of it steady and unhurried, like water from a cracked jug, and the edges of the wound were *grey*. Not pale. Grey, like ash, like the color of a thing that had been drained of the will to be red.

"That's not going to close," Silas heard himself say. Stupid. Obvious. His voice came out flat because his mind had already left the conversation and started running.

"No." The man smiled, and there was blood on his teeth. "It isn't. It's been four days. I'm told that's a long time, for this kind of hurt." He said it almost companionably, the way a man talks about weather. Then his knees went, and he grabbed Silas's coat with both hands, and the weight of him drove Silas back against the banister hard enough to crack a rail.

"Listen." The word came out wet. "I don't have time for you to be afraid. I know you're afraid. Be afraid faster."

"I don't know you."

"You're going to." The man fumbled inside his cloak one-handed, and Silas thought, absurdly, *knife*, and braced for it — but what came out was paper. A folded square of heavy stock, water-stained along one edge, tied shut with a thread of something that wasn't quite string and wasn't quite wire. The man pressed it into Silas's palm and then folded Silas's fingers over it himself, both of his hands around Silas's fist, squeezing until the paper's corners bit skin.

"She'll know you have it," he said.

"Who'll? Who's she?"

But the man's eyes had gone somewhere else. Somewhere behind Silas, or behind the world. He was looking at the stairwell the way a drowning man looks at the surface — and then, horribly, the way a drowning man looks when he realizes the surface is further than he thought.

"They took Brannock's name out of me already," he murmured. "They'll take yours too. They take everything, eventually. That's all they are. Taking, wearing a uniform." His grip tightened. "Don't let her read your face. She reads faces. When she comes — and she'll come — think about nothing. Think about *rain*."

"Brannock?" Silas said. "Fence, works off Candler's Row? What do you mean, took his name—"

Footsteps below. On the stairs. Heavy, even, unhurried — the tread of people who had never once needed to hurry, because everything in Ironhollow eventually came to them.

The man's head snapped toward the sound, and what crossed his face then stripped away every ounce of the calm he'd been wearing. It was fear. Not the sharp fear of a cutpurse caught lifting a purse — Silas knew that one intimately, wore it like an old coat. This was the deep, animal, bottom-of-the-well kind, the kind that has already done its arithmetic and found no answer.

"Stairwell window," the man breathed. "Third floor. Go. Now. Don't look back down the shaft, they see movement like owls see mice."

"And what do I do with—" Silas lifted the folded paper.

"Keep it. Hide it. Eat it before you give it up." The man laughed, a small broken sound. "Better: remember it. Paper burns. You don't seem like a man who forgets much."

"I forget things professionally," Silas said, and hated himself for saying it, because it was true and because the truth of it was exactly what had put him on these stairs at midnight with a stranger's blood soaking into his sleeve.

The footsteps reached the second-floor landing. Three sets. Maybe four. They'd stopped, which was worse than if they'd kept coming. Listening.

The man shoved him — actually shoved him, with the last of whatever was keeping him upright — toward the third-floor corridor. "Window. Go."

Silas went. He told himself later that he'd hesitated, that some better version of him had turned back, had dragged the dying man up to the roof, had done *something*. But the truth was his legs made the decision and his conscience just rode along, and by the time he reached the corridor's end his hands were already working the window latch the way they'd worked it a thousand nights, slipping out onto the wet slate of the adjoining roofline with the ease of a man whose whole life was built on exits.

Behind him, through the glass, he heard his door open.

* * *

Ironhollow's rooftops were a country unto themselves, and Silas was one of its quiet citizens. He moved low and fast across the slate, boots finding the old paths — the chimney gap behind the chandlery, the plank bridge over the alley behind Vess's memory-den, the gutter-run above the dye-works that stank of madder and lye. Rain had come an hour ago and gone, leaving everything slick and silvered under the gaslight haze, and the city below him glowed the way it always did at night: amber windows, black water, the citadel on its hill burning white at the crown like a lit candle nobody dared blow out.

He made six roofs before he let himself stop, wedged in the shadow of a bell-tower's base, lungs sawing, heart doing something arrhythmic and humiliating. From here he could see his own building. He told himself he wouldn't look. He looked.

Two figures stood in the street below his window. They wore the grey-and-black of the Wardens, and they stood the way Wardens stood — not searching, not questioning neighbors, not kicking doors. Just standing. Facing the building. As if the building might confess on its own if given time.

A third figure came out of his doorway carrying something. It took Silas's eyes a moment to resolve it, and when they did, his stomach dropped through six stories of air and kept falling.

They were carrying the stranger out horizontally. Between them. Like furniture.

And the stranger was moving. Not struggling — moving the way a man moves when he's being *worn wrong*, limbs flexing at angles that suggested the muscles were answering a question nobody had asked them. Silas watched the Wardens load him into a black carriage with no sigil, watched the door close, watched the carriage roll away without a sound — without, he realized after a moment, any horses he could see.

Then the two remaining Wardens looked up. Both of them. At once. At the roofline.

Not at his roofline specifically. Just — the roofline. Scanning. But Silas felt it anyway, that upward glance, like a hook passing through the dark an inch above his spine. He pressed himself flat against cold slate and thought about rain, because a dying man had told him to, and it was the only instruction he'd ever been given that made any sense.

Think about rain. Rain on the market awnings. Rain filling the gutters of Candler's Row. Rain the night his sister—

No. Not that. Never that; that memory was the one thing in his head he'd never bottled, never sold, never even shown the surface of. He shoved it down and thought about ordinary rain instead, anonymous rain, and held still until his knees ached.

When he finally risked another look, the street was empty. The Wardens were gone as completely as if the city had swallowed them, which, Silas was beginning to understand, was precisely what the city did.

* * *

He spent the rest of the night walking.

That was the old discipline: when the ground shifted, keep moving until you found new footing. He walked the Warrens' edge, past shuttered charm-stalls and the memory-dens with their blue lamps, past the watch-post where a bored constable glanced at him and dismissed him in the same heartbeat — uninteresting, unremarkable, exactly as designed. He walked down to the canal and stood on the rotted pier where he'd made his first sale at fifteen, a stolen afternoon of summer sold to a widow who'd wept holding the bottle, and he'd told himself even then that what he did was mercy, was recycling, was harmless.

Harmless. He turned the word over now and found cracks in it he'd never noticed, hairline fractures running all the way through.

Because here was the thing he couldn't stop circling, out there in the grey hour before dawn with the map a hot coal against his chest: the stranger had known him. Had climbed — been carried, crawled, *brought* — to Silas Fairweather's specific door. Out of all the doors in a city of two hundred thousand doors. Which meant somebody, somewhere, had decided Silas was the right address. And decisions like that got people visited by Wardens, and visits like that ended with men being carried out of buildings at angles their bones hadn't chosen.

Rule one, he thought bitterly. Never be interesting. Well. Someone had made him interesting without asking.

At first light he bought a bowl of barley soup from the corner woman on Tallow Street and ate it standing up, tasting nothing, and paid with a coin he took from the stranger's own purse — the man had pressed it on him along with the map, mumbling something about *expenses*, and Silas had told himself he'd refuse it, and hadn't. The soup woman asked no questions. Nobody in Ironhollow asked questions before noon.

By full light he'd found a hiding place for the map that satisfied no part of him: sewn into the lining of his spare coat, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked behind a loose brick in the chimney flue of a rented room he kept under a false name in Solder's Lane — a bolt-hole, one of three, because a man in his trade learned early that a survivor needs more than one life to hide in. He sat on the cot in that borrowed room and unwrapped the thing and finally, properly, looked at it.

Heavy paper. Water-stained. Folded into eighths, and when he opened it the creases resisted like old scars. Ink, mostly, fine and precise, the hand of someone trained — survey lines, depth markings, numbers in a script he half recognized from Brannock's ledgers. It was a map of waterways. Of the canals, yes, but also of things beneath the canals: galleries, cisterns, streets marked in a faded brown ink that seemed older than the rest, older than the city's official cartography, older maybe than the city's official anything.

And at the center, drawn in that same ancient brown, a symbol. A vertical line rising out of concentric rings, like a needle through the eye of a whirlpool.

Silas stared at it until his eyes watered. He knew Ironhollow's underworld better than most men knew their own kitchens, and he had never seen that mark anywhere — except once, years ago, scratched into the lintel of a condemned house down in the flooded quarter, and he'd assumed it was a smuggler's sign, a gang tag, nothing.

Nothing. Everything in his life had rested on most things being nothing.

He refolded the map along its scarred creases, rewrapped it, re-hid it, and sat back down on the cot with his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands, and let himself feel, for exactly sixty seconds by the cheap tin clock on the mantel, the full weight of what had happened to him.

A man had died — was dying, was perhaps dying *right now*, wherever they'd taken him — on Silas's stairs. Wardens had touched his building. His name, his trade, his careful grey little existence had been entered into somebody's ledger, and ledgers in Ironhollow only ever went one direction. He thought of the wound that wouldn't close, the grey edges of it, and felt a cold he hadn't felt since he was a child listening to the old ones whisper about the war. People said the Wardens took memories. Everyone knew that; it was a joke, almost, a bogeyman story parents told children who asked about magic. *Be good or the Wardens will drink your dreams.*

Nobody said the Wardens left wounds that refused to heal. Nobody said they carried men out of buildings like sacks. Nobody said *she'll know you have it* in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant and finding it fair.

Sixty seconds. Then he stood, splashed water on his face, and began doing what he did best: planning the small, shabby, essential logistics of staying alive. Money counted. Exits mapped. The charm of the sea in his pocket — he weighed selling it today, quickly, before his face got around; two weeks' rent bought time, and time was the only currency that mattered now.

He was pulling on his coat when he heard the knock.

Three raps. Polite. Patient. The knock of someone who expected the door to be answered, because in their experience it always was.

Silas went absolutely still, one arm in his sleeve. Through the thin wall he could hear the neighbor's radio, the drip of the shared tap, the ordinary machinery of the morning. And underneath it, from the landing, silence — the particular, attentive silence of a person waiting to hear whether anyone inside was breathing.

There was no peephole in the door. There was a gap beneath it, and the morning light that should have been falling through that gap was blocked. Blocked by feet. Two pairs of them, standing very close together, not shifting, not shuffling. Waiting.

*She'll know you have it.*

Silas took his arm out of his sleeve, slowly, without a sound. He gathered the coat, the money, the wax-sealed bottle of borrowed ocean. The window in this room opened onto a drainpipe he'd tested twice and trusted once. He was halfway across the floorboards, each step placed like a coin into a collection plate, when the voice came through the door — a woman's voice, level and pleasant and utterly without warmth, the voice of a clerk reading a form.

"Silas Fairweather. We know you're awake. We heard the clock."

He froze with his hand on the window latch.

"We only want to talk," the voice said, and beneath the words, faint as a smell, he heard something else — something under her skin the way the grey had been under the stranger's wound, a second voice layered beneath the first, vast and patient and hungry, saying nothing at all and somehow saying *everything, bring us everything.*

Silas threw the latch, kicked the window wide, and went out over the drop as the door behind him came apart in a sound like tearing cloth.
