## 3. What the Wardens Take

The memory-den was called the Drowned Lantern, and it was exactly the kind of place Silas Fairweather would have avoided on any other night.

It sat in the crook of Tanner's Run, where the street sank a half-story below the waterline and the buildings leaned together like conspirators sharing a secret. The door was unmarked. The windows were painted black from the inside, and the paint was fresh — someone had done it recently, which meant someone inside was nervous. Nervous people didn't open their doors for strangers. But the girl at the threshold had looked at Silas's face, then at the coin he held up between two fingers, and had decided that whatever was hunting him wasn't worth losing his money over.

That was the arithmetic of Ironhollow. Everything came down to what you could carry and what you could spend.

Inside, the den was warm and dim and thick with the smell of tallow and old sweat. A dozen low couches lined the walls, and on them lay the den's patrons, each with a charm pressed to their temple or cupped against their cheek like a compress. Some of them were weeping quietly. One man was laughing — a soft, helpless laughter, the kind that comes from somewhere deep and long-buried. A woman near the window kept saying *oh* under her breath, over and over, like she was watching something impossibly beautiful through glass only she could see.

They were drinking memories. Not their own. Nobody in Ironhollow drank their own; theirs were either too painful or too thin. They drank bottled years — a first snowfall, a wedding morning, the taste of a peach eaten in summer by someone who'd since died. Silas knew the trade intimately. He'd sold half of these charms himself, or ones like them, decanted from strangers who would never know what they'd lost.

He found a corner couch in the shadow of a support beam, paid the house girl double to leave him alone, and sat with his back to the wall and the map folded flat against his chest, beneath his shirt, beneath the sweat-damp linen, close as a second heartbeat.

He told himself he was safe here. He told himself the Wardens swept the markets and the piers and the grand houses of the Gilders' Quarter, not dens like this one, which paid their tithe and asked no questions. He told himself a lot of things that night. It was becoming a habit — telling himself stories, the way he bottled stories for other people. If you tell yourself enough of them, they almost hold.

The map itched against his skin. Not physically. Maps don't itch. But he could feel it there anyway, the way you feel a splinter you can't see: a small wrongness, a pressure, a sense that something had entered your body that did not belong to it.

*She'll know you have it.*

The stranger's words. He turned them over and over in his head while the den murmured around him. She'll know. Who was she? The Warden commander? Someone worse? He'd had hours now to think about it, and every hour the question got heavier instead of lighter. Because there was another question underneath it, one he kept trying not to look at directly:

Why give it to *me*?

Silas Fairweather was nobody. That was the whole architecture of his life — a careful, load-bearing nobody. No family. No debts worth collecting. No name that opened doors or closed them. He stole small things from small people and sold them smaller, and he'd built a life out of being too insignificant to matter. And now a dying courier had crossed half a city, bleeding out through a wound that wouldn't close, to press a folded scrap of vellum into *his* hands.

Nobody chose a nobody by accident.

He closed his eyes and tried to think about anything else. The den helped. Around him, the patrons sighed and wept and laughed at borrowed joys, and there was something almost holy about it, if you squinted — all these starved people feeding on secondhand light. Almost holy, and entirely obscene. Silas knew both truths at once because he was both the priest and the profiteer. He'd stopped apologizing for it years ago. Apology was a luxury for people whose hands were clean.

He was drifting — not quite sleep, not quite waking — when the music stopped.

There hadn't been any music. That was the thing. There had been the murmur of the den, the wet breathing of the drinkers, the creak of the couches. And then all of it stopped at once, the way a room stops when a predator walks in — every animal going still at the same instant, some old instinct firing down every spine simultaneously.

Silas opened his eyes.

The house girl stood frozen behind the bar, her hand still on a bottle, her face gone the color of candle wax. At the door, the girl who took coins had gone very quiet and very straight. And outside the painted windows, where the lamplight of Tanner's Run should have been flickering its usual greasy amber, there was a different kind of light. Colder. Steadier. Moving.

Boots. Many boots. Unhurried boots, which was somehow worse than running ones. Men who run are afraid of something. Men who walk slowly through the night are afraid of nothing, because everything is already afraid of them.

*Wardens.*

Silas was off the couch before his mind finished the word, moving low along the wall toward the back of the den, toward the memory of an exit he'd clocked when he came in — every thief clocks the exits, it's the first law of staying alive — but his legs felt slow, syrup-slow, and the fear had gotten into his lungs and made the air thin.

There was a back door behind the bar. There was also a cellar hatch, older, rusted, half-hidden under a rug that had been moved recently. The house girl caught his eye across the room and, with a tiny jerk of her chin, told him what he already knew: the back door was watched. Everyone's doors were watched. The Wardens hadn't found this place tonight by accident.

Someone had sold it. Someone always sold it. That was the other arithmetic of Ironhollow — the one that never balanced in your favor.

Silas dropped through the cellar hatch just as the front door came apart.

Not opened. *Came apart.* The hinges shrieked and the wood burst inward in a spray of splinters, and the cold light poured in after it, and with the light came the boots, six pairs of them, seven, eight, ringing on the floorboards above his head like hammers on a coffin lid.

He crouched in the dark among the barrels and the rat droppings and the sour reek of spilled charm-oil, and through a gap between the floorboards — a gap wide enough to see through, wide enough, God help him, to be unable to stop seeing through — Silas Fairweather watched what the Wardens do to people caught with magic.

* * *

There were eleven patrons left in the den. He counted them through the gap in the boards, counting because counting kept him sane, the way reciting prices used to keep him steady during a bad trade. Eleven. Plus the house girl. Plus the door girl, who hadn't run, who had stood her ground at the threshold with her chin up like she thought standing still might save her, and who Silas understood, with sickening clarity, had been the one who let him in.

The Warden commander came in last.

Silas had seen Wardens before — everyone in Ironhollow had seen Wardens, grey-cloaked and grey-eyed, patrolling in pairs, asking their flat questions, taking their flat tithes. You learned to make yourself furniture around them. You learned not to look at their eyes, which were the color of weather right before it turns.

But Silas had never seen a Warden commander. And he had certainly never seen one from four feet below, looking up through a floorboard gap, while the commander stood directly overhead and the lamp-light fell down between the boards in pale stripes across Silas's upturned face.

The commander's voice was wrong. That was the first thing. It was a perfectly ordinary voice — deep, measured, almost gentle — and it was wrong the way a perfect forgery is wrong. Every word arrived on time. Every pause landed exactly where a pause should land. It was a voice that had been studied rather than grown, and hearing it made the hair on Silas's arms stand up and refuse to lie back down.

"Good evening," the commander said to the den full of frozen patrons. "Please don't get up."

A laugh went around the Wardens. Not a cruel laugh. Worse — a comfortable one. The laugh of men doing routine work.

"We're looking for a courier," the commander said. "He was carrying something that doesn't belong to him. He passed through this district tonight. He won't have passed through it alone." A pause, placed precisely. "You've been drinking memories. All of you. Which means each of you has magic in your blood right now — thin, diluted, secondhand, but present. You know the law. You know what the law requires of us."

One of the patrons — the laughing man, the one who'd been laughing at some dead summer — started to cry. Not loudly. Just tears sliding down his face while he stayed perfectly still, the way you cry when you've understood that noise will only make it worse.

"Please," the house girl said. "We pay the tithe. We pay every month—"

"The tithe buys you time," the commander said kindly. "It doesn't buy you innocence. Nothing does. That's rather the point of the tithe."

Silas pressed his hand hard against his own mouth.

What happened next happened without violence, and that was the worst part. Silas had expected violence — clubs, screams, blood on the floorboards, the whole ugly theater of it. Instead the Wardens simply walked among the couches, unhurried, and each one took hold of a patron's head with both hands, one palm on the forehead, one at the back of the skull, and *pulled*.

There was no better word for it. Pull. Like drawing thread from a spool. Like pulling a nail.

The patron nearest the gap in the boards was the woman who'd been whispering *oh*. She didn't scream. She arched once, gently, like someone stretching in warm water, and then her mouth opened and something came out of her — Silas saw it, actually saw it, a shimmering ribbon of pale gold rising out of her parted lips like breath on a winter morning, except it moved with intention, it *flowed*, streaming upward into the Warden's palms, and the Warden's eyes — Silas could see them from below, tilted down through the boards — the Warden's eyes brightened as it came. Fed. Drinking.

And the woman's eyes changed.

They went from brown to grey the way a fire goes out. Not quickly. Slowly, evenly, from the center outward, color draining like water down a drain, until there was nothing left in them at all — no brown, no white even, just a flat uniform grey, the grey of ash, of slate, of sky before snow. Her body stayed upright on the couch. Her hands stayed loose in her lap. Her chest kept moving, in and out, in and out, because whatever process this was, it was careful, it left the machinery running.

It just took everything else.

When the Warden released her head, she slumped slightly, and a thin line of drool escaped the corner of her mouth, and she smiled. That was the detail that would follow Silas into his sleep for the rest of his life, he knew it already, knew it the way you know a scar has formed: *she smiled.* Vacant, beatific, empty as a scraped bowl. Whatever the pulling had taken, it had taken the part of her that could mind.

They went down the row. One by one. The laughing man stopped laughing halfway through and became quiet and grey and smiling. The door girl fought — she actually fought, got a hand free, raked her nails across a Warden's wrist, and the Warden didn't even change expression, just caught her hand and held it the way you'd hold a child's, and pulled, and she made one sound, one small sound like a question cut short, and then she was grey too, and smiling, and Silas understood that the sound she'd made was the last new thing she would ever say.

Eleven patrons. Two girls. Thirteen ribbons of pale gold light, drawn up out of thirteen heads, swallowed by eight sets of grey-cloaked shoulders.

And through it all, the commander stood in the middle of the room with his hands folded behind his back, watching, and his voice — when he finally used it again — was still gentle, still measured, still arriving exactly on time.

"Thank you for your cooperation," he said to the husks. "Your contributions will serve the city."

* * *

Silas didn't remember deciding to look up. His neck simply bent, and his eyes rose past the stripes of lamplight, past the floorboards, to the underside of the commander's jaw — because the commander had turned, slowly, scanning the room, and for one moment his face was angled down and forward, and the light caught him from below, the way stage light catches a mask.

And the mask slipped.

It was only a slip. Half a heartbeat. The skin of the commander's face moved wrong — moved *independently*, the way cloth moves when there's no body under it, or rather when there is a body under it but not the body the cloth was cut for. Along the commander's jawline, the flesh loosened, sagged away from the bone by a finger's width, and in the gap Silas saw not muscle, not sinew, but a darkness with texture. A churning. Something pale turning over inside something dark, slow and vast and patient, like the underbelly of a cloud, like silt rising in deep water.

And in the middle of that churning dark, something looked at him.

He couldn't say what it was. An eye, maybe, or the idea of an eye — a point of attention, cold and total, the way a fish's gaze is total, the way the deep sea is total. It found the gap in the floorboards. It found the stripes of shadow between them. It found, with a precision that made Silas's bladder clench and his heart try to climb out through his throat, the exact spot where Silas Fairweather was crouched in the dark among the barrels.

It looked at him for one second. Maybe less.

Then the commander's skin settled back into place, smooth and human and gently smiling, and the commander said, in that forged, punctual voice, "Check the cellar."

* * *

Silas ran.

Not toward the stairs — the stairs were death, the stairs were a funnel with eight grey cloaks waiting at the top of it. He ran deeper. Cellars in Tanner's Run were dug by smugglers three generations back, and smugglers dig twice: once for themselves and once for the day the first hole gets found. There was a second hatch behind the oil barrels, bolted from this side, and Silas's fingers found the bolt in the dark and threw it with a speed he would never be able to reproduce again, and the hatch opened onto a crawl-tunnel barely wide enough for his shoulders, brick-lined, dry-ish, smelling of river rot and old secrets.

Behind him, the cellar door above crashed open. Lamplight speared down into the space he'd just left. Boots hit the dirt floor.

"He's here," a voice said. Flat. Certain. "Fresh tracks in the charm-oil."

Silas crawled. Elbows and knees, the map crushed between his chest and the bricks, splinters and grit tearing at his sleeves, the tunnel so low his spine brushed the ceiling with every lurching stride. Behind him the boots spread out, methodical, kicking barrels, checking walls. Ahead of him the tunnel sloped down and curved, and the air changed — cooler, wetter, carrying the mineral smell of the old cisterns — and Silas followed the smell because the smell meant water and water meant ways out, and Ironhollow's drowned undercity had more ways out than the Wardens had ever mapped.

They'd never mapped it because mapping it would mean admitting it existed. And admitting it existed would mean admitting what flowed underneath the city. What had always flowed underneath the city.

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, terrible: *the tunnels remember.*

He shook it off. Fear makes you poetic. Fear had been making him poetic all night, and poetry was a luxury for people who weren't crawling through a brick throat with eight hunters at their backs.

The tunnel ended at a grate, rusted through at one corner, and Silas squeezed through the gap, tearing his shirt, skinning his ribs, and dropped into the cistern channel — waist-deep, thigh-deep, moving water, black and cold as a held grudge. He waded. Then he swam. Then he climbed, hands finding old iron rungs set into the slime-slick stone, up and up, until he hauled himself out onto a maintenance ledge beneath a grate of street-level moonlight, and lay there gasping, soaked to the bone, shaking so hard his teeth clicked.

Above him, muffled by stone and grate and distance, he could hear the den. Or what had been the den. The Wardens were finishing their work — he could hear the calm voices, the unhurried boots, and once, terribly, the commander's gentle forged tones giving thanks again for cooperation.

Thirteen people. Smiling. Grey. Empty.

Silas lay on the ledge and stared at the moonlight coming through the grate, and made himself do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the stairwell.

The courier had been hunted across half the city. The den had been found within hours of Silas entering it. Brannock's warning echoed in his skull — *you're already dead, boy, you just haven't been informed* — and now Silas understood it wasn't a figure of speech. It was a schedule. The Wardens weren't searching for the map. Searching implied uncertainty. They were *following* it — following him, specifically, the way a current follows a leaf. Every place he touched, they arrived. Every person near him, they drained.

Which meant the house girl was dead. The door girl. Thirteen strangers who'd never known his name, emptied out and smiling, because a dying man had chosen him in a stairwell.

And the thing beneath the commander's skin had looked at him. Had *found* him. Through a floorboard gap, in the dark, in a cellar, in seconds. It hadn't needed to search. It had simply turned its attention, the way you turn your face toward a sound, and there he was.

*She'll know you have it,* the courier had said.

Silas had assumed *she* meant the commander. Now, lying in the dark with river water drying salt-stiff in his clothes, he understood his error with a clarity that felt like a blade going in. The commander was a cloak. A glove. A skin worn by something else — something that had looked at him through a gap in the world, and knew his face now, and knew the weight of what lay against his chest.

*She* was bigger than the commander. *She* was whatever wore commanders.

And she hadn't sent soldiers after him. She'd sent *that* — that unhurried, gentle, thorough horror that didn't kill people but unmade them, that left the bodies walking and smiling and grey. Because killing Silas would end the hunt. But draining everyone around him — that was a message. That was the message written in thirteen husks in a cellar-lit den in Tanner's Run:

*This is what waits for anyone caught with magic. This is what waits for anyone near you. Keep running, little thief. See how many smiles you can leave behind you.*

Silas pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars.

He should throw the map in the water. The thought came, and it was reasonable, and it was the thought any sensible man would have. Drop it into the cistern. Let the current take it into the deep places where the Wardens' dredges didn't reach. Go back to being nobody. Pay his tithes. Bottle his small stolen joys. Live.

His hands wouldn't do it.

He took the map out — soaked at the edges now, the water-stained script blurred but stubbornly legible, lines and markers and a shape that recurred at the center of it like a signature — and he held it in the moonlight, and his hands, traitorous, exhausted, grieving hands, would not open. Would not let go.

Because of the woman on the couch. Because of the *oh*, oh, oh. Because of the door girl's one cut-short question. Because somewhere under the fear there was a colder, harder thing forming, the way frost forms on a window: the understanding that he had spent ten years stealing pieces of people and selling them back to them, and calling it survival, and never once looking at what fed on the other end of the trade. Tonight he'd looked. Tonight he'd seen the mouth.

The map wasn't his burden. It was his bill.

Somewhere above, a bell began to ring — the citadel bell, the one that rang for fires and executions and the announcement of bounties. Three strokes. Pause. Three strokes. The bounty pattern. His face would be on every post by dawn, next to a number large enough to buy a dozen den-fulls of silence.

Silas folded the map against his heart, found his feet on the wet ledge, and looked down the black length of the cistern channel, toward the drowned streets, toward the only direction left that wasn't behind him.

He knew one person in this city who owed him and couldn't afford to refuse him. One fence with a ledger and a debt and a talent for reading ruined scripts.

Brannock.

The bell rang a third time, and Silas stepped off the ledge into the dark water, and began to move.
