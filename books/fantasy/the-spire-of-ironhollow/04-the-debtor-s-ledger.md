## 4. The Debtor's Ledger

The water in the cistern channel was cold enough to make a man honest, and Silas Fairweather was not an honest man, so he swam fast.

He surfaced twice — once beneath a grate where he hung by his fingers and counted the boots of a Warden patrol passing overhead, once in the flooded cellar of a chandler's shop that had drowned forty years ago and never been drained, because Ironhollow had never met a problem it couldn't simply submerge. The city was built like that. Layer over layer. Whatever rotted below, they just poured new stone on top and called it progress.

By the time he hauled himself out through a coal chute into the alley behind Tanner's Row, the map against his chest had gone soft as skin and the sky above the rooftops had gone the color of old pewter. Dawn soon. He had perhaps two hours before the Wardens widened their search from the night market to the whole of the Low Quarter, and every charm-seller, cutpurse, and gutter-angel between here and the Wall would be asked one question:

*Have you seen him?*

Silas wrung out his coat, tucked the map deeper into its lining, and started walking.

Brannock kept shop in a street that didn't officially exist — a slot between a tannery and a shuttered prayer-house, so narrow that the buildings on either side leaned toward each other like conspirators sharing a secret. There was no sign above his door. There didn't need to be. Anyone who needed what Brannock sold knew how to find him, and anyone who found him without needing him was already dead or about to be.

Silas knocked the pattern: three slow, two quick, one slow.

A long pause. Then Brannock's voice, muffled through the door, dry as ledger-paper: "I don't buy before dawn."

"You'll buy this," Silas said. "Or you'll remember you owe me."

Another pause, longer. Then the sound of bolts — four of them, drawn back in an order Silas knew meant *come in quickly and be grateful I'm paranoid* — and the door swung inward on a wedge of lamplight and the smell of dust, lamp-oil, and old vellum.

Brannock stood in the gap: a big man gone soft at the edges, bald as a river stone, with forearms tattooed in faded ink from some sailor life he'd left behind and never mentioned. He wore an apron with too many pockets, and every pocket held something that wanted to not be found.

"Fairweather." He looked Silas up and down, taking in the soaked coat, the bruised knuckles, the wildness around the eyes. "You look like a man who's been swimming."

"I have."

"In your clothes."

"It was urgent."

"It always is." Brannock stepped aside to let him in. "Come on, then. Before someone sees you dripping on my step and starts asking questions I'd have to lie about badly."

The shop inside was a single long room, floor to ceiling with shelves, and the shelves were floor to ceiling with things that had no right to exist in a city where magic was illegal. Not charms — Brannock didn't deal in charms; that trade was Silas's own small, guilty corner of the market. No, Brannock dealt in *evidence*. Letters written in dead hands. Coins minted by kingdoms that no longer existed. Books whose pages whispered if you held them near your ear. He was, as far as Silas knew, the only man in Ironhollow who collected proof of the past instead of selling it, and he did it quietly, and he did it alone, and he had survived this long because nobody — including, most days, Silas — understood why.

Silas dripped on the floorboards. Brannock winced but said nothing.

"The map," Brannock said. "That's why you're here, dripping on my floor at an hour decent men are asleep. You've got something you need read."

"How did you—"

"Because you came to me instead of running. A man with nothing to decode runs. A man with a puzzle comes to the puzzle-man." Brannock pulled a stool from behind the counter and sat down heavily, folding his hands. "So show me."

Silas hesitated.

Every instinct he owned — and he owned many, they were the whole architecture of his survival — told him not to do it. Told him to turn around and walk back into the grey dawn and find some other way, some safer way, some way that didn't put the map in front of anyone else's eyes. That was the rule he lived by. *Touch nothing you can't carry. Tell no one what you can't afford to lose.*

But the map's script was water-eaten, half-drowned itself, the ink bloomed into ghosts of letters. And there was exactly one man in Ironhollow who could read ruined scripts, because ruined scripts were the only kind he collected.

And there was exactly one man in Ironhollow who owed Silas enough that refusing would cost him more than helping.

"Three years ago," Silas said, "you sold a Warden captain a forged provenance for a relic you swore to me was clean. It wasn't. When they traced it back, they traced it to my supplier, and my supplier died in the Grey Cells, and I lost six months of inventory and nearly my neck. You promised you'd make it right."

"I remember."

"You said, 'One day, Fairweather, I'll read for you anything you bring me, and I'll ask no questions.' Those were your words. I've saved them up. I've kept them warm." Silas reached into his coat and drew out the map. "No questions. That was the price."

Brannock looked at the folded thing in Silas's hands for a long moment. His face did something complicated — a flicker of greed, quickly mastered; then something colder underneath it, something that might have been fear.

"No questions," he agreed finally. "But I'll tell you this much for free: whatever that is, I already wish I hadn't seen it."

He unfolded the map on the counter, weighting the corners with four brass weights shaped like animals Silas didn't recognize — beasts from somewhere older than Ironhollow, maybe. The lamplight fell across the water-stained surface, across the blooming ink, across the strange geometry of lines that didn't quite match any district Silas knew.

Brannock bent over it. For a long time he said nothing at all. His lips moved sometimes, silently, reading. His thick fingers hovered above certain places without touching, the way a man hovers over a sleeping animal he doesn't want to wake.

Silas watched him work and felt the fear come up through the floorboards like damp.

It took a quarter hour. When Brannock finally straightened, he straightened slowly, the way an old man does, though he wasn't old. He took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, which was a thing Silas had seen him do maybe twice in ten years, both times when a deal had gone wrong.

"This isn't a map," Brannock said.

"It looks like a map."

"It's a map the way a knife is a spoon. Yes, technically. But it's made for something else entirely." He tapped the edge of the parchment. "This is a survey. Do you understand what I'm saying? Someone surveyed this route. Measured it. Marked depths, air-shafts, load-bearing walls. This is engineering. Old engineering. Pre-Warden engineering, if the script is what I think it is, and it is, because I've seen this hand before, once, thirty years ago, on a document I was paid very well to burn and didn't."

Silas's mouth had gone dry. "Read it anyway."

"I'm getting there." Brannock's finger traced a line down the map's spine — a channel, a route, descending in stages. "This is the undercity. The drowned streets. The ones the city sealed after the war, or says it sealed. First marker's here—" his finger stopped at a symbol like a fishhook crossed with a key, "—the Sunken Gate. Second marker's under the old aqueduct junction. Third marker..."

His finger stopped.

"Third marker," Silas said.

"There is no third marker," Brannock said quietly. "There's a destination. And the destination has a name, and the name is written in a cipher I last saw on Warden documents marked with a seal I was told I'd hallucinated." He looked up, and his eyes behind the smudged spectacles were afraid in a way Silas had never seen them afraid. "Do you know what the Spire is, Fairweather?"

"A story. A place mothers scare children with. *Be good or the Spire will drink your dreams.*"

"Yes. And also a real place, which is worse." Brannock laid his palm flat on the map, covering the third mark, as if hiding it would help. "Before the outlawry, before the Wardens, before all of it — there was a tower at the center of this city. Not built *by* the founders. Built *before*. Everything else grew up around it the way scar tissue grows around a splinter. And when magic was outlawed, the Spire wasn't torn down. It was buried. Sealed. Sank, some say, or was sunk. And everything above it — the citadel, the Warden halls, half the High Quarter — was raised on top of its bones."

Silas stared at him. "That's not possible. The citadel's foundations are—"

"—the Spire. Yes." Brannock gave him a thin, humorless smile. "You're standing in a city built like a cork in a bottle, Fairweather, and somebody has handed you a map to what's under the cork."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, far off, a bell rang the hour — five bells, grey light beginning at the edges of the shuttered windows.

"Why would a dying stranger give me this?" Silas asked, mostly to himself.

"Wrong question." Brannock began folding the map, briskly, decisively, pushing it back across the counter. "Right question is: why would she give it to *you specifically*, and the answer to that is going to be worse than anything you've imagined so far, and I want no part of finding out. Take it. Take it and go. We're square, you and me. More than square. Consider us strangers."

"You haven't finished reading it."

"I've finished all I intend to finish." Brannock stood, and his bulk seemed suddenly less soft, more like a wall coming down. "Fairweather. Listen to me, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to open my door and put you through it. Whatever she was — whoever killed her — the people hunting that map don't drain memory-charms out of street sellers. They take whole dens. Whole streets. They take people who *looked at* the wrong thing. You brought that thing into my shop, and every second it stays here, my name gets written in a ledger I can't pay off. So we are done. Debt paid. Go."

Silas picked up the map. His hands weren't steady.

"For what it's worth," Brannock said at the door, quieter now, "the hand that wrote this — the surveyor's hand — belonged to a woman. I'd bet the shop on it. And women who surveyed drowned cities before the outlawry weren't couriers. They were keepers." He opened the door onto the grey alley. "Go home, Fairweather. If you still have one."

Silas went.

* * *

He didn't go home. He went to ground instead — a rented coffin-room above a noodle house in the Ragmarket, four floors up, one window facing a wall, paid for in cash by a man with a face nobody remembered. He locked the door. He wedged a chair under the handle. He lay on the cot with the map inside his shirt, against his skin, and tried to sleep and failed, and tried not to think about Brannock's voice saying *the Spire*, and failed at that too.

*Built before. Buried under. The citadel sits on its bones.*

And the Hollow King — the thing in the dark the den-keeper had whispered about, the thing the Wardens served, the thing with the hunger he'd glimpsed beneath a commander's skin — what if it wasn't *in* the dark? What if it was *underneath*? What if the whole city, every street he'd ever stolen on, every stairwell and gutter and market stall, was a lid?

He must have slept eventually, because he woke to the smell of smoke.

For one stupid heartbeat he thought it was the noodle house downstairs, breakfast fires starting early. Then he heard the bells — not the hour bells, the other kind, the fast ugly ones — and he was at the window, looking down into the Ragmarket lane, and the lane was full of torchlight.

Not fire. Torchlight. Wardens carried torches even in daylight, because torches were a message: *we want you to see us coming.*

They moved through the market in files of six, unhurried, thorough. At each stall, each door, each stairwell, they stopped. Silas saw the grey-eyed commander from the night market walking the line, pointing, and wherever she pointed, two Wardens went in and came out escorting someone — a noodle vendor, a rag-picker, a boy who sold eels — and the escorted walked between them with their heads hanging and their eyes already going glassy at the edges, and Silas's stomach turned over because he knew what came next. He'd watched it happen in the memory-den. He'd run from it. He'd told himself it was a lesson about keeping low.

They weren't searching for him. Not yet. They were pulling the thread of his life — everyone he'd bought from, sold to, nodded to in passing — and the thread led everywhere he'd ever been.

Then the file of Wardens turned off the Ragmarket lane and into the narrow conspirators' slot between the tannery and the shuttered prayer-house, and Silas went cold all the way through, because he knew that street, and there was only one door on it worth knocking.

They didn't knock.

The door came off its hinges inward, and the lamplight of Brannock's shop spilled out into the grey morning, and the grey-eyed commander stood in the doorway a moment, silhouetted, before she went in.

Silas waited for Brannock to come out. Waited for the shouting, the struggle, something. Brannock was a big man. Brannock had four bolts on his door and tattoos from a hard life and a talent for surviving that matched Silas's own.

Nothing came out.

Ten minutes. Fifteen. The Wardens posted in the alley stood like fenceposts. Then the commander emerged alone, carrying something flat and wrapped in oilcloth — the size of a large book, or a bundle of documents — and behind her came two Wardens supporting a third figure between them, a big shape gone slack, feet dragging, head lolling, and even at this distance, even in the bad light, Silas knew the slope of those shoulders and the too-many-pocketed apron.

Brannock's eyes were open. That was the worst part. They were open and they were grey — not the grey of exhaustion, not the grey of fear. The grey of a room after everything in it has been taken out. And as they dragged him past the mouth of the alley, past the window where Silas stood pressed against the wall with his heart hammering like a fist on a door, Brannock's head rolled, and for one moment those empty grey eyes looked up — looked directly at the fourth-floor window, at the wall, at nothing — and his mouth moved.

No sound reached Silas. But he'd spent his life reading faces, reading buyers and liars and grieving sailors, and he read the shape of those lips easily, the way you read anything you've practiced ten thousand times.

Two words.

*She knows.*

Then the Wardens turned the corner, and Brannock was gone, and the alley stood empty in the growing daylight, ordinary again, as if shops were emptied every morning and big men carried away like furniture.

Silas slid down the wall until he was sitting on the floor of the coffin-room, the map crinkling against his chest, and he put his face in his hands.

*We're square,* Brannock had said. *Consider us strangers.*

He had known. Standing in his shop with his brass weights and his whispering books, he had known what it would cost to help, and he had helped anyway — had read the map, named the Spire, given Silas the truth — and then he had paid for it before the ink of the debt was dry. Because of Silas. Because Silas had come dripping to his door and called in a marker and put a target on the one man in Ironhollow who owed him kindness.

*Helping him is already fatal.* That's what it came down to. Anyone who touched the map, anyone who touched *him*, got taken. Which meant the crew he'd need — because he'd need one, no man crossed the drowned streets alone, no man decoded a pre-outlaw survey alone — would be a crew of the doomed. Every favor a death sentence. Every ally a name on the same ledger.

Unless he ran. Alone, tonight, down into the black water, following a dead woman's survey into the dark under the city, and hoped the map was kinder to him than it had been to everyone else.

Silas lifted his head. Through the window slit, the citadel loomed above the rooftops, black iron and pale stone, and somewhere beneath it — if Brannock had read true — slept the Spire, and the thing that had been drinking this city dry for a hundred years.

*She'll know you have it,* the dying courier had said.

Well. She knew. She'd always known. And now she'd taken the only man who could tell Silas what the rest of the map said.

Which meant there was only one direction left with anything in it, and it was down — down through the Sunken Gate, along the drowned survey lines, toward the third marker and the name beneath the city — and the only company he could find on the way would be thieves and outcasts and the kind of people who were already marked.

He stood up. He took the map out of his shirt and spread it on the cot, and looked at the fishhook-crossed-key symbol at its first marker, and thought: *someone has to know the way through the Gate. Someone who goes down there regular. Some gutter-rat with a boat and no sense of self-preservation.*

And then he thought, unbidden, of a pair of quick hands in the night market crowd, weeks ago, lifting his purse and leaving a note in its place that said *nice coat, thief* — and the laugh that had sounded, for one absurd moment, almost like—

He stopped himself before the thought finished. It couldn't be. It wouldn't be.

But he knew where she worked the crowds.
