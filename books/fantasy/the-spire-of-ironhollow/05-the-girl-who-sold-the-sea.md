## 5. The Girl Who Sold the Sea

The knock came at the hour when even the night market sleeps and only the lamps on the Gullet Bridge still burn, and Silas knew before he opened the door that it would be trouble, because nobody knocks politely at that hour unless they want something or they're dying, and he'd already had his fill of dying strangers this week.

He stood with his back against the doorframe, one hand resting on the little knife he kept wedged between the third and fourth floorboard of the landing, and said nothing. In his trade, silence was a tool like any other. Let them fill it.

"Fairweather." A girl's voice. Young, but sanded down at the edges, the way voices got when they'd been used for lying since they were small enough to fit through windows. "I know you're in there. Your lamp's lit behind the shutter, and you've got the habit of pacing when you think. I counted eleven passes."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You've been watching my window."

"I've been watching your window, your chimney, and the drainpipe you use when you don't want to use your stairs. You should oil that hinge, by the way. It squeaks in E-flat."

Silas opened the door.

She was small — small enough that he'd have taken her for twelve if not for the eyes. Nobody's eyes looked like that at twelve. They were grey-green, watchful, set in a face all sharp angles and soot, under a cap of dark hair chopped short with what looked like a fish knife. She wore a boy's coat two sizes too big with the sleeves rolled six times, and her boots had been resoled so many times there was more patch than leather.

And beneath all of it — beneath the grime and the swagger and the knife she was turning idly in her fingers like a lady with a fan — something else. Something he recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror.

"I know you," he said slowly.

"You don't."

"You do that thing with your chin when you lie. Tilt it left. Like you're looking down your nose at someone taller than you."

Her hand stilled on the knife. "Everyone does something."

"Not everyone does *that*." He stepped back to let her in, because it was cold on the landing and because he was already lost and they both knew it. "Come in before someone sees you standing there. The Wardens have been crawling the Gullet for three days."

"They've been crawling everywhere." She slipped past him into the room, and he caught a smell off her coat — river water and tallow smoke and, faintly, underneath it all, something sweeter. Something like a memory charm that had been carried too long against warm skin.

His stomach dropped, though he couldn't have said why yet.

She surveyed the room the way he imagined a magistrate surveys a debtor: the shelves of bottled memories catching lamplight, each one labeled in his careful hand. *First snowfall, 3 coppers. A mother's voice singing, 5. The sea, from a deck, wind in rigging — sold.* She read the labels upside down, fast, and he watched her do it and felt the particular nakedness of a locksmith watching someone pick his own lock.

"So this is where the famous Fairweather keeps his stock," she said. "Half the den-keepers in Ironhollow would pay a month's take just to know which floorboards you hide the good ones under."

"And you'd sell it to them for less."

"For half," she corrected. "I'm not greedy. Greedy thieves get caught." She dropped onto his one chair, uninvited, boots up on the table beside his map — his *map*, and his heart stuttered until he saw that the fold still hid the important parts, that she hadn't touched it, or at least hadn't opened it. "But I'm not here to rob you, Fairweather. Robbing you is plan B."

"What's plan A?"

"Employment."

* * *

He made tea because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. He didn't offer her any. She took the pot anyway and poured herself a cup, and drank it black, wincing, because his tea was terrible and always had been.

"Start again," he said. "From the beginning. How did you find me?"

"You sold me a memory four years ago. Down by the tannery stalls. You were wearing a different coat then — brown, with a tear in the elbow you'd sewn with red thread, which was stupid, because red thread stands out." She turned the cup in her fingers without drinking again. "You were selling charms out of a tray like a pie-seller. Sea ones. That was your specialty back then, wasn't it? The sea. You had maybe twenty bottles, all salt-smell and gull-cry, and people said they were better than anyone else's because—" she paused, and tilted her chin left, and lied, "—because you were talented."

"And now?"

"Now I'm telling you how I found you, aren't I? Keep up."

He kept up. He kept up all too well, because he was remembering now, the way memory comes back — sideways, in smells. Four years ago. The tannery stalls. A thin girl with hollow cheeks who'd counted out coins one at a time, who'd asked him three times whether it hurt, whether the person the memory came from missed it after, and he'd told her what he always told customers — that he never took from the unwilling, that these were given freely or lifted from those who had years to spare — lies wrapped around truths wrapped around lies — and she'd bought one bottle.

One bottle.

The sea, he thought. She bought the sea. And then the other thing surfaced, the thing underneath, the thing he'd spent four years keeping pressed down flat like a flower in a book, and his throat went tight around nothing.

It wasn't the sea she'd asked about first. First she'd asked about the one bottle on the tray that wasn't for sale. The small plain one, unlabeled, that he kept wrapped in wool at the bottom of the tray. *What's that one?* she'd asked. And he'd said *none of your business,* and she'd said, *it's a person's laugh, isn't it? I can tell. It looks warmer than the others.*

Her sister's laugh. His sister's laugh. Lira's laugh, stolen — no, *saved*, he'd always called it saved, saved off her deathbed by his own clumsy gift, the one magic he'd ever done that mattered — and bottled because he couldn't bear to let it go the way everything else of hers had gone.

"That was you," he said quietly.

"Don't flatter yourself. Half the city's bought something off you."

"The girl who asked about the unlabeled bottle. Who asked if it hurt."

Wren looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she saw in his face made her stop performing. She set down the cup. For a moment she was just a kid sitting in a thief's kitchen, feet not quite reaching the floor's crossbar, hands too big for her wrists.

"Yeah," she said. "That was me."

"Why?" The question came out rougher than he meant it. "Why did a child need a memory of the sea?"

"Same reason anyone needs one." She shrugged, and the shrug didn't fit right on her, like a coat buttoned wrong. "Because she didn't have any of her own worth keeping."

*She.* Not *I*. He heard it, the little grammatical slip, and filed it away the way he filed everything — carefully, guiltily, in the drawer of things he noticed and wished he hadn't.

"Your sister," he said.

The silence stretched long enough that the lamp guttered. When she spoke again her voice was flat as slate. "Are we going to talk about my family, or are we going to talk about yours? Because I came here to talk about the dead man in your stairwell."

* * *

"How do you know about him?"

"Because I watched him die." She said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "Two nights ago. Alley behind the Cordwainers' Row. Tall man, courier's badge torn off his coat, bleeding from a wound that wouldn't close — and I mean wouldn't, Fairweather, I've seen guttings and I've seen fever-rot and this was neither. It was like his body had forgotten how blood worked."

Silas sat very still. The folded shape of the map seemed to press against his chest even through two layers of clothing, where he'd taken to keeping it sewn inside his shirt like a second, heavier heart.

"There was another man," Wren went on. "Or something wearing a man. Grey coat, Warden-cut but wrong somehow, like a coat copied from a description instead of seen. And the courier — he fought, at the end. Bought himself ten seconds with a knife and died paying for them. And in those ten seconds he shoved something into his boot and kicked it toward the drain grate, and the Warden-thing walked past it twice without seeing, and I nearly wet myself deciding whether to grab it."

"You grabbed it."

"I grabbed it. And then I followed the trail of whoever came asking after it, which led me, over the course of two days, to a fence named Brannock who went pale as milk when I mentioned couriers, and then to a certain charm-seller whose shop on Cordwainers' Row had its door kicked in yesterday morning and blood on the step that somebody tried to scrub with river sand." Her eyes glittered. "You've been busy."

"It wasn't my blood."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "I know whose it was. I watched it come out of him."

The room felt smaller than it had. Silas rose, checked the shutter, checked the door bolt — habits of a week that already felt like a lifetime — and turned back to find her watching him with an expression he distrusted deeply because it was honest.

"You saw the killer," he said. "Describe him."

"Grey coat, grey gloves, and when the lantern light hit his face wrong, there was a moment — just a moment — where his skin moved like water moves when something swims under it." She held his gaze steadily, refusing to flinch from her own words, daring him to call her a liar or a madwoman. "I've been sleeping with the lamp lit ever since. Not that it helps. You can't burn a thing like that."

He thought of the memory-den. Of the commander's face rippling. Of forty people emptied into husks while he hid under a table counting his own heartbeats. *You can't burn a thing like that.* No. But apparently you could carry its map sewn against your ribs and drink terrible tea while a child described it to you across your own kitchen table.

"If the Wardens know you saw—"

"They don't know what I saw. They know somebody saw; I made sure a cutpurse in the Warrens got caught carrying my description instead of me. That buys me maybe another day." She leaned forward. "Which brings us to business. Here's plan A, Fairweather, and it's simple. You're going somewhere. Everything about you screams *man going somewhere* — you sold your stock cheap last week, you've got river-passage money hidden in your flour jar — yes, the flour jar, I checked while you were making tea — and you've got that map-shaped lump under your shirt that you keep touching like a prayer-bead."

He stopped touching it. She grinned.

"Wherever you're going, you'll need someone who knows the undercity, the drowned streets, the ways the Wardens' maps don't show. That's me. I grew up down there. In exchange, you take me with you, and you tell me what the courier was carrying that was worth killing for — because I've got a piece of it too, Fairweather, and pieces want to be together, and things that want to be together make noise that things like *that* can hear."

"And if I say no?"

"Then tomorrow a Warden informant named Quill gets a note describing your face, your trade, and your flour jar." She spread her hands, apologetic. "Blackmail. Plan A through Z, all the same plan, just different prices."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. At the sharp chin, the rolled sleeves, the knife turning and turning. At the ghost of the girl at the tannery stall counting coins one by one, asking if it hurt.

He thought about Lira, and the bottle wrapped in wool in the floorboard cache, and how every road out of this city ran through the same country: grief. Maybe that was why the courier had chosen him. Maybe grief was the only currency the dead trusted.

"One condition," he heard himself say.

"Name it."

"You never sell it. Whatever piece you're carrying. However bad it gets. Some things you carry, you don't spend."

Something flickered across her face — quick as a fish under water, gone before he could name it — and she tilted her chin left and said, "Deal," and for once he didn't catch the lie, or maybe there wasn't one, or maybe there was and he chose not to look.

She stood, drained the cold tea like medicine, and pulled a scrap of paper from her coat: a rubbing, charcoal on cloth, of a symbol stamped into the courier's boot-heel. A spiral, broken by seven lines. He knew it. It matched the watermark on his map.

"Down Among the Drowned Streets," she said, reading the old words around the spiral. "That's where this goes, isn't it? Undercity. The flooded quarters." Her grin came back, bright and crooked and terrified. "Good. I hate dry land. Never trusted anywhere the water can't reach."

They shook on it — her grip surprisingly strong, her palm calloused in the specific pattern of a rope-climber and lockpick — and it was done, and Silas Fairweather, who had survived nine years in Ironhollow by needing no one, had acquired a crew of one.

It was only later, bankling the lamp while she curled up in the chair like a cat that had decided the territory was hers, that he understood what had been nagging him since she walked in. The sweet smell under the river-grime on her coat. Old tallow and wax and something floral, long faded.

The smell of a memory charm carried against the skin for four years.

She still had it, he realized. The sea he'd sold her. She'd never opened it. She carried it like he carried Lira's laugh — sealed, unspent, a thing too precious to use and too precious to lose.

And he lay in the dark listening to her breathe and wondered what, exactly, he'd just agreed to carry — and whose grave he was walking toward, and whether the drowned streets remembered the names of the people who'd sold their seas to get out of them.

Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled once and stopped, as if interrupted.

Wren's eyes opened in the dark. "They're early," she said.
