## 6. Down Among the Drowned Streets

The undercity didn't have a name anymore. It had a smell.

Wren went down first, of course. That was the arrangement — she led, Silas followed, and neither of them pretended he had a choice. The hatch was behind a shuttered chandler's shop on Candle Row, and it opened onto a ladder that went down farther than any ladder had a right to go. She could feel him hesitating above her, could hear the wet rasp of his breathing in the dark.

"You can still turn back," she said, not meaning it.

"I can't."

"Right." She dropped another rung. "Then stop breathing like a man being buried and climb."

The water came up to their shins at the bottom, black and flat as poured glass. Wren lit no lamp. Lamps were for people who trusted the dark, and she'd grown up in it; she knew what it wanted. Instead she navigated by touch and memory — left hand trailing along the wall until she found the groove the smugglers had cut a hundred years ago, right hand counting archways. Three arches past the second bell-post, turn toward the sound of falling water.

Silas splashed behind her like a man wading through his own funeral.

"There are things down here," he said quietly. Not a question.

"There are things everywhere." She kept her voice low anyway. Sound carried strangely under Ironhollow — it pooled in the vaulted ceilings and came back to you from directions you hadn't spoken in. "Leeches the size of your forearm. Eel-dogs. And the drowned, sometimes, if the tide's been bad."

"The drowned."

"People who came down and didn't come back up." She glanced over her shoulder at the pale smear of his face. "Don't worry. They're not ghosts. Ghosts would be kinder. They're just bodies that forgot they finished dying. You'll know them by the way they stand very still in the water, listening."

"Listening for what?"

"For you to make noise." She turned forward again. "So don't."

* * *

They walked for hours, or what felt like hours — time ran soft down here, stretched thin between one dripping archway and the next. The water rose to their knees, then fell away again as the streets climbed toward some drowned hill that had once been a neighborhood with a name. Wren knew these streets the way other girls knew prayers: without understanding them, but perfectly.

She'd been eleven when her mother took her down the first time. Run, hide, be quiet — those were the three lessons, taught in that order, and she'd learned the fourth one alone later: *never trust anyone who offers to teach you a fifth.*

Now there was a man behind her who paid debts and bottled memories and walked like someone who'd never once had to run from anything worse than a debt collector. He was afraid — good, fear kept you sharp — but he was ashamed of being afraid, which was going to get him killed.

"The map," she said, holding out her hand without stopping.

She heard paper unfold behind her. "It says *where the river remembers its name.*"

"That's the Old Canal." Something cold slid against her ankle and she kicked it away before her heart could finish stuttering. "Before they capped the springs and flooded everything, this whole level was called Vell's Crossing. There was a river. A real one, with fish in it, before the Wardens decided water was too hard to police."

"And where does a river remember its name?"

"Where somebody carved it into stone deep enough that the flood couldn't lick it off." She stopped at last at a wall of fitted granite, older than anything above, furred white with mineral bloom. In the torchlight of her memory she could see the letters — but memory wasn't enough. She needed light now, and light meant risk.

"Lamp," she said. "Small one. Low."

Silas unhooded a stub of candle no bigger than his thumb. The flame leaned toward the wall like it was reading too.

There it was, cut deep beneath the mineral crust: **VELL'S CROSSING**. And below it, in a smaller, newer hand, an arrow pointing down into the black mouth of a culvert, and two words:

*She waits.*

Wren stared at them longer than she meant to. "Your dead courier did this."

"She wasn't dead yet, then."

"No. I suppose she was busy." But something about the handwriting bothered her — the little hook on the *S*, the impatient slash of the *w*. She'd seen it before. Somewhere. Recently. She filed the feeling away the way she filed everything, in the locked room at the back of her head where she kept things she wasn't ready to open.

"In," she said. "Candle stays lit. If we lose the light, we lose each other, and then the eel-dogs eat whichever of us screams loudest."

"Comforting."

"I'm not paid to comfort you."

"You're not paid at all. You blackmailed your way onto this expedition, remember?"

"And I'd do it again." She ducked into the culvert. "Walk."

* * *

They heard the patrol before they saw it — the particular hollow knock of Warden boots on drowned stone, carrying down through the ceilings like knuckles on a coffin lid.

Wren killed the candle so fast Silas made a small wounded sound. Then her hand found his collar and hauled him sideways into an alcove, into standing water that came up to their waists, cold enough to squeeze the breath out of him. She pressed her palm flat against his chest — *be a wall, be nothing, be stone* — and felt his heart hammering against her fingers like something trying to escape a cage.

Three lights bobbed into view around the far bend. Warden lanterns burned a color that wasn't quite fire — a grey-white gleam that made the water look like milk and the shadows look like holes. Wren had seen that light all her life from a safe distance. Up close it did something wrong to the eyes. It made you feel *catalogued*.

The Wardens waded past thirty feet away. Their armor didn't splash. That was the detail that had always frightened her most, the thing she'd never told anyone: Warden armor never splashed. Water moved out of their way like it had been asked politely.

One of them paused.

Wren stopped breathing entirely. Beside her, Silas had gone rigid, and she could feel the map's folded corner digging into her ribs where she'd taken it from him — because of course she had; he carried it like a man carries a love letter, and love letters got people killed.

The Warden tilted its helmeted head. Under the edge of the visor, where a face should have caught the lantern light, there was only a smooth grey shine, like the inside of a shell.

Then a voice — not from the Warden, from somewhere deeper in the tunnel, a woman's voice flattened by distance — called a number, and the Warden turned and waded on, obedient as weather.

They waited until the lights were long gone. They waited longer than that.

"They don't have faces," Silas whispered finally.

"They have faces." Wren pushed off the wall, wringing out her sleeves. "That's the worst part. I saw one once, when I was small. The face is still there. It's just not *theirs* anymore. Something wears them the way you wear gloves." She started walking. "Come on. We're close, and I want to be gone from this level before the tide turns."

"What happens when the tide turns?"

"The drowned wake up."

He didn't ask anything else after that.

* * *

The cache was behind the altar of a church that hadn't held a congregation in eighty years.

Vell's Crossing had drowned slowly — you could see it in the walls, the old waterlines laid down like tree rings, year after year of rising. The church had drowned last and proudest, its steeple still breaking the surface of some flooded nave, and its crypt sat above the waterline in air that tasted of stone and old incense and something else, something faintly sweet, like fruit going off.

Wren found the loose block by feel, exactly where the arrow had promised. Behind it: an oilcloth packet, dry as bone, tied with waxed cord.

She handed it to Silas because her hands had started shaking and she didn't want him to see.

He unwrapped it in the candlelight. Letters. Dozens of them, bound in bundles, written in that same hooked hand — and others, older hands, official hands. Sealed orders. Ledgers. A document bearing the seal of the First Concord itself, the founding council whose statues stood in every square above, whose names were carved in gold over the Warden citadel gates.

Silas read. His lips moved. Then they stopped moving.

"Wren."

"Just read it out loud. I want to hear it said."

He cleared his throat. When he spoke, his voice came out strange, stripped of its merchant's polish. "*'By order of the First Concord, the account of the Spire's surrender shall be amended as follows: the mages did not refuse disarmament. The mages surrendered. The record will show they refused.'*" He looked up. "This is a forgery order. This is the founding myth of the city, and it's — it's a *draft*. With corrections in the margins. Someone edited the war."

"Read more."

"*'The Spire was not stormed. The Spire opened its doors.'*" He turned pages faster now. "*'The prisoners taken from the Spire number four hundred and eleven. Prisoners released upon completion of binding: zero.'*" His voice cracked on the last word. "*'The binding requires a living vessel. The vessel has been selected. Let it be recorded that the vessel consented.'*"

"Consented," Wren repeated. The word tasted like copper. "Four hundred mages walk out under a flag of surrender and the history scrolls say they fought to the last. Four hundred people go into a hole in the ground and the history scrolls say they died gloriously in battle. And everyone above — every schoolchild, every festival, every statue—" Her voice rose and she strangled it down to a whisper, because shouting in the undercity was suicide, but her hands wouldn't stop shaking now. "Everyone above pours wine for the heroes of the Concord every Founding Day. We thank them for saving us from magic. We *celebrate*—"

She stopped. Pressed her fist against her mouth.

Her mother had taken her to Founding Day every year. Had bought her honey-bread from the festival stalls and lifted her onto her shoulders so she could see the pageant wagons, the actors in grey robes playing wicked mages struck down by shining Wardens. Her mother had wept at the burning of the effigy Spire. Every year. Grateful tears.

*She believed it,* Wren thought. *She died believing it, thanking the men who built the lie, and she taught me to believe it too, and I clapped.* 

The wonder she'd felt coming down here — the strange bright thrill of following a dead woman's map into the bones of the world — curdled in her chest and went hard and hot. This wasn't treasure. This was a confession, and the confessor had died bleeding in a stairwell to deliver it, and the city above was singing a different song over her grave.

"There's a name," Silas said quietly. He was holding one letter apart from the rest, handling it like it might bite. "Signed at the bottom of nearly all of them. *Marrow.* M-A-R-R-O-W. And this one — the oldest one — it's addressed to her. *'To Archivist Marrow, with the author's compliments and apologies.'*"

"Marrow." Wren turned the name over. It snagged on something. "The stranger. The woman in your stairwell. Did she have a name?"

"I never asked. She was too busy dying." He swallowed. "But Odile — Brannock's contact, the one the map points to next — Odile Marrow. She said her mother was a courier."

"A courier with an archivist's handwriting." Wren looked at the letters, at the neat hooked script, at the impatient slashed *w*, and the locked room at the back of her head swung open all at once. "I've seen this hand before. On a notice board, years ago. Warden archive copies — public decrees, posted with the transcription credit at the bottom." She met Silas's eyes. "Silas. The woman who wrote the lie was the same woman who spent her life collecting proof of it. Your dying courier *was* the Archivist's daughter. Or—" the thought landed cold, "—or the Archivist herself, older than anyone guessed, still running."

Silas repacked the letters with hands that weren't steady either. "Odile," he said. "We need to find Odile."

"Yes." Wren took the candle from him. "And we need to find her fast, because if her mother bled out in your stairwell, then whatever comes looking for that blood already knows where it fell — and it knows the letters existed, which means it knows where she would have hidden them, which means—"

Above them, faint but unmistakable, filtered down through eighty feet of drowned stone: bells. Warden bells. Not the alarm pattern — the other one. The slow, spaced tolling that every child in Ironhollow knew without being taught.

A tolling for the dead.

Except Wren had lived her whole life in the spaces between the city's lies, and she knew what it meant when the Wardens rang those bells out of season, out of pattern, three times instead of one.

It meant they were announcing a hunt.

The candle flame leaned toward the tunnel mouth, as if even fire wanted to run.
