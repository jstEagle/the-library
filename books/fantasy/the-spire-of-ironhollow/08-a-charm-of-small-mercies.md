## 8. A Charm of Small Mercies

The passage cost forty crowns, and Silas had eleven.

He stood in the doorway of Madge Corrin's counting room and did the arithmetic again, as if shame were a sum that might come out different the third time. Forty for the ferryman's cousin who ran barges through the drowned quarter after dark. Eleven in his boot, six in his coat lining, and a strip of copper he kept for luck that wasn't luck at all but a charm someone had sold back to him years ago, its memory gone thin as smoke.

Twenty-three crowns short. Seventeen, if the ferryman's cousin haggled, which she would.

"Sit down before you fall down," said Madge Corrin.

Her counting room sat above her shop on Tallow Row, where she dealt in candles by day and in quieter goods by night. The room smelled of tallow and old paper. A scale hung from the beam above her desk, brass pans worn bright on one side and black with use on the other. Everything about Madge was balanced like that — half respectable, half not, and careful never to let either half outweigh the other.

"I need a buyer," Silas said. "A good one."

"Good ones are expensive."

"I know what I'm selling." He set his satchel on her desk, and his hands wanted to shake and didn't, because he had spent twenty years teaching them not to. "One memory. Deep stock. Clean pull."

Madge looked at him over her spectacles the way an apothecary looks at a patient who has just named his own poison. "Deep stock," she repeated. "Silas Fairweather, you've never sold deep stock in your life. You skim. You skim beautifully, I'll grant you. A sailor's afternoon of sun, a widow's one good Christmas, a child's first snow — little charms, little mercies, no harm done to anyone that they'd notice. But deep stock is a different animal. You take deep stock out of a man, you leave a hole shaped like it. He'll spend the rest of his life falling into it."

"I know how it works."

"Do you?" She leaned back. "Because the way I hear it, you've been telling yourself a story for twenty years. That you're not really a thief. That you're a kind of... apothecary. Dispensing comfort. And maybe that story kept you honest when honesty was impossible. But stories have prices too, Silas, same as memories, and tonight you've come in here to pay yours."

Outside, rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere below, a customer bought candles. The ordinary sounds of Ironhollow going about its frightened evening, and none of them knowing that three streets away a Warden patrol had turned onto Cooper's Lane, though of course Silas couldn't know that yet either.

"The map needs crossing," he said. "The Sunken Gate doesn't open for love or threats. It opens for coin. Forty crowns."

"And your crew?"

"Waiting. Odile knows the letters. Wren knows the waterways. Brannock—" He stopped.

Madge waited. She was good at waiting; it was most of her trade.

"Brannock is dead," Silas said, "or something near enough to it. Taken the night after he read the map for me. Which means the Wardens know the map exists, and probably know my face by now, which means every day I stand still is a day they close their hand around me. So yes. I need passage, and passage costs forty crowns, and I have seventeen."

"What's the memory?"

And there it was. The question he'd been carrying up her stairs like a stone in both hands.

"It's mine," he said. "It's not stolen. It's not borrowed. It's the only thing in my collection I ever paid full price for, because it's the only thing I grew myself. A summer. My sister's summer, the last good one, seen from inside it — my own eyes, my own ears, not hers. The river behind the Fairweather house. She was nine. I was twelve. There was a willow, and a rope swing somebody's older brother had tied off, and she laughed—"

He stopped again. His hands were shaking now, and he let them.

"You'd sell that?" Madge asked quietly.

"I'd *carry* it another fifty years if I could afford to. But carrying it won't get us under the Sunken Gate, and getting under the Sunken Gate might save every person in this city who's ever had a memory taken from them. I can do the sums, Madge. I've always been able to do the sums. That's the whole trouble with me."

Madge studied him for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the window, and checked the street with the unhurried care of a woman who had survived thirty years of Ironhollow by never once being surprised in public.

"There's a buyer," she said. "Came to me two days ago asking after deep stock, willing to pay well over market. Name of Master Quill. Soft-spoken. Gloved. Pays in fresh-minted crown, no haggling." She turned back. "I don't like him."

"Then why send me to him?"

"Because I don't like *you* either, right now, and misery loves company." She took a ledger from a drawer, wrote a name and an address, tore the page out, and held it without giving it over. "Midnight. The back room of the Sign of the Drowned Bell. You'll come out richer than you've ever been. Whether you come out otherwise whole — that's between you and whatever god watches over fools."

Silas took the page. "You could just refuse me."

"I could. And you'd find another buyer by morning, a worse one, and I'd have lost my cut and my conscience in the same hour. At least this way I keep the cut." She almost smiled. "Go home, Silas. Sleep on it. That's my professional advice, free of charge, worth exactly what you're paying."

But he didn't go home, because his home was a burned question mark now, and sleeping on it would mean dreaming about a rope swing and a willow tree, and he had done the sums already. He had always been able to do the sums.

* * *

The Sign of the Drowned Bell stood where Candlemakers' Row gave up pretending to be respectable and became warehouses and memory-houses and worse. Its sign was an actual bell, green with river rot, hanging silent over the door — a bell that had drowned, the story went, pulled up from the undercity with weed still growing in its throat.

Inside: low light, lower voices, and the particular smell of places where people traded in things that used to belong to souls. Smoke and spilled beer and, beneath it all, the faint sweet-rot smell of bottled memories past their prime.

Master Quill had taken the back room and turned it into something out of a magistrate's office: a table scrubbed white, two chairs, a single lamp turned down to a coal of light. He himself was grey — grey coat, grey gloves, hair the color of ash — and his face was the kind of face you forgot while looking at it. Smooth, patient, unremarkable, like a wall in a house you'd visited once as a child.

"Master Fairweather." His voice matched everything else: soft, level, forgettable. "Madge Corrin speaks highly of your work. Please, sit."

Silas didn't sit. "You know my name."

"I make it my business to know the names of craftsmen." Quill folded his gloved hands. "May I see the piece?"

Every instinct Silas owned told him to walk out. He catalogued them, the way he catalogued everything: the wrongness of the lamp, the newness of the money-bag sitting untouched on the white table, the way Quill's gloves hadn't creased once since Silas entered the room. Then he thought of the Sunken Gate, and Wren waiting with the barge, and Odile with her dead mother's letters, and he sat down anyway, because he had always been able to do the sums, and the sums said *one memory, forty crowns, everyone lives.*

He drew out the vial.

It was a small thing, blue glass, stoppered with wax he'd sealed himself. Inside, the memory moved like weather — green-gold flickers, the sound of water, and underneath it all, so quiet you had to hold it close to hear: a girl laughing.

Quill reached for it and stopped, hand hovering. "Might I listen first? Professional courtesy. I wouldn't buy a horse unsaddled."

"No one listens to deep stock without paying for the listening."

"A fair point." Quill produced a second purse from his coat and set it beside the first. "For the courtesy, then."

Silas hesitated. Every rule he lived by said *never open a vial in front of a stranger* — a memory uncorked was a memory vulnerable, spilling into whoever stood nearest, imprinting on any mind hungry enough to drink it. But the man had paid. And Silas needed this sale clean, witnessed, done.

He thumbed the wax seal. The stopper came free with a small sigh, and the summer rose out of the vial like heat off a road.

For three heartbeats the room was the riverbank. The lamp became sun through leaves. The smell of smoke became willow bark and river mud. Somewhere in the walls of the Drowned Bell, a girl of nine was laughing, high and helpless, the rope swing arcing gold against the sky—

—and then Quill breathed in, slow and deliberate, the way a man breathes before a long dive, and the laughter stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Cut off mid-note, the way a throat closes around a scream.

The summer snapped back into the vial like a fish yanked out of air, and Silas corked it with fingers that no longer felt like his own, because in those three heartbeats he had seen — felt — the shape of the thing drinking across the table from him. Not a mind. A *mouth*. A vast, patient hunger wearing a smooth forgettable face, and beneath the grey coat not flesh but something like sediment, like the silt at the bottom of a drowned place where everything settles and nothing rots because nothing is allowed to die—

"You've listened," Quill said. His voice was still soft. Still level. But now it was also *full*, the way a jar is full. "A lovely piece. The girl. Your sister, I believe."

Cold walked up Silas's spine. "I never said that."

"You didn't need to." Quill unfolded his hands and laid them flat on the white table, and Silas saw now that the gloves weren't creased because there were no knuckles under them worth bending. "Do you know what I am, Master Fairweather? I am what the Wardens send for when the Wardens want something *thoroughly*. Patrols take bodies. Commanders take confessions. I take inventory." He tapped the vial with one grey finger. "That map you carry. The woman who died delivering it. The daughter reading her letters. The thief girl. The fence, poor Brannock — such a waste; he knew so much and we got so little time with him. Inventory, Master Fairweather. All of it, coming due."

Silas ran.

He was moving before the thought finished forming, chair going over backwards, shoulder hitting the door — locked, of course it was locked — and he put his elbow through the shutter instead, glass and old wood bursting outward into the alley, and dropped three feet into rain and muck and ran. Behind him the back room stayed horribly quiet. No shouting. No pursuit. That was the worst part. Pursuit meant panic, and this thing didn't panic. It simply noted, and filed, and followed at its leisure.

He went the way the old thieves went: over the candle-shop roof, along the drying lines, down through the gap behind the tannery where the stink covered everything including scent itself. Rain hammered the tiles. His lungs burned. At the mouth of Gull Street he risked one look back.

Nothing. Just rain, and lamplight, and the Drowned Bell's drowned bell swinging gently overhead without a breath of wind to move it.

Then he heard it. Not footsteps. Not voice. Just, rising out of every drain and gutter and cellar grate along the street at once, faint as memory itself:

*a girl laughing.*

They had it. The thing across the table had drunk the listening-deep of it — three heartbeats of his sister's last good summer — and now it carried the laugh the way a fisherman carries a lure. Playing it through the city's throats. Calling him home the way you call anything that runs.

Silas pressed his back to a wall, in the dark between two lamps, and made himself do the only thing left that he was any good at.

He did the sums.

They had his face. They had his trade. They had the taste of the map's bearer and three heartbeats of the memory he'd been starving himself to protect. Running was dead; they'd run him down laughing. Hiding was dead; they'd already emptied Brannock. Which left only the thing he'd sworn off since the stairwell, the thing that had gotten Brannock taken and Wren's face on a warrant and a stranger bleeding out among his own broken bottles:

He stopped running and started spending.

By dawn he'd hit four fences and two memory-houses, trading favors like a gambler down to his last shirt, and by the time the grey light came up over the citadel he had what he needed: word sent ahead to Wren and Odile at the barge, a change of clothes, a scar of lampblack across one cheekbone to bend his face just enough, and one last purchase — passage money counted out, forty crowns even, plus the ferryman's cousin's fee for leaving tonight instead of tomorrow.

Paid for, every coin of it, out of Madge Corrin's finder's fee, which meant Madge had known. Had perhaps always known. Had sold him to the thing with grey gloves for a cut, and then sold him his own life back at markup, and he found he couldn't even hate her for it. In Ironhollow everybody did sums.

At the barge, in the dripping dark under the Sunken Gate's outer arch, Wren crouched at the mooring line and watched him come down the wet steps with eyes that missed nothing.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been shopping." He handed her the crowns. "We sail tonight. Before the tide turns, before anyone asks questions, before—" His voice caught, just once, on something he refused to name. "Before it gets any more expensive."

Wren counted fast, then frowned. "This is more than we need. Where'd it come from?"

Silas looked back up the stairs, toward the city where somewhere a grey-gloved mouth was holding a stolen laugh like a coin between its teeth, saving it, savoring it, waiting.

"Let's just say," he said, "the price came due."

And far above them, in the counting room on Tallow Row, Madge Corrin blew out her lamp and sat alone in the dark with her ledger open to a page she had already torn out once — and written in again, in a hand that shook slightly, a single line:

*Fairweather, S. — deep stock, one piece — sold.*

*Buyer: pending.*
