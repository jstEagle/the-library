## 9. The Ferryman's Toll

Wren had been down to the Sunken Gate twice before she met Silas Fairweather, and both times she'd turned back at the last stair.

Not because of the water. Water didn't frighten her. She'd grown up in the flooded quarters where the canals ate the ground floors and the poor built stairs to their own front doors. Water was just the city breathing.

It was the quiet that turned her back. The Gate sat at the bottom of the drowned district like a held breath, and around it there was no sound at all — no rats, no drips, no wind off the black canal. Just stillness, thick as tallow, pressing on her ears.

Now she stood at the top of those same stairs with three people behind her who had no idea what they were walking into, and she found she wasn't afraid anymore. Fear was a coin you spent once. She'd spent hers years ago, in a room that smelled of fever, holding her mother's hand while the coughing took everything except the song.

"You're sure this is the way?" Odile asked behind her. The ex-Warden's voice was flat as always, but Wren had learned to hear the questions under it. *Are you leading us into a grave? Are you worth following?*

"It's the way." She tapped the map's edge where the stranger's ink had bled but not faded. "The Gate's marked plain as anything. What isn't marked is what it costs."

"And what does it cost?" Silas asked.

Wren didn't answer right away. She looked at him instead. He was doing his market-face — the easy, competent half-smile of a man selling charms he'd stolen off other people's heads — but his eyes kept flicking to the water below, and his thumb kept worrying the strap of his satchel. He hadn't slept since Brannock was taken. None of them had, really, but Silas wore sleeplessness like an ill-fitting coat, all pulled seams and hidden stains.

"You'll see," she said, and started down.

The stairs were cut from old foundation stone, each step slick with a skin of green-black growth. Lamplight from the drowned district died ten steps down, so Wren lit the stub of candle she carried in her coat and let its small flame make a small world. Behind her: Odile's measured tread. Behind her: Brannock's cousin Tam, who'd joined them two days ago out of some mixture of grief and guilt and who hadn't said six words since. And behind him, quieter than any of them, Silas Fairweather, moving like a man trying to take up less space than a body allows.

The water rose around their ankles by the fortieth step, their knees by the sixtieth. Wren counted without meaning to. It was a habit from childhood, counting things — cracks in ceilings, coins in a purse, days since her mother stopped singing. Counting made the world a list, and lists could be survived.

At the bottom, the stair opened onto a platform of black stone, and beyond the platform the water stretched away into darkness, wide as a lake, still as glass. And rising out of that stillness, maybe fifty yards out, stood the Sunken Gate itself — a colossal archway of green bronze crusted with centuries, its top lost in the cavern dark, its base swallowed to the lintel by water that did not ripple even when Wren stepped into it.

Between the platform and the arch floated a boat.

There was no one in it. There was no oar, no sail, no rope. It simply rested on the dead-still water, lantern hanging from its prow, burning without flicker.

"There," Wren said.

"That's the marker?" Tam whispered. "That's just a boat."

"That's the ferryman," Wren said.

* * *

She'd heard the stories in the dens and dives of the undercity, the ones told low, after the third drink, when people wanted to scare themselves. The Gate had been old before Ironhollow was a city. Old before the war, if you believed the drunkards. Old enough that whatever kept it had never bothered learning human manners.

You hailed the boat. You paid the toll. You crossed.

Nobody agreed on what the toll was, exactly. Some said blood. One man swore it was your name, spoken backward into the water. But the oldest story — the one the dredger-women told their daughters — was memories. The Gate was a mouth and memories were its food, because memories were the only coin that couldn't be counterfeited, only taken.

Wren had never tested the story. Now she cupped her hands around her mouth and called across the black water, her voice cracking against the stillness like ice on a pond:

"Passage! We ask passage!"

Nothing. The candle flame bent toward the water as though leaning to listen.

Then the boat moved. No wake, no sound — it simply arrived, crossing fifty yards of lake in the time it took Wren to blink twice, and its prow kissed the platform soft as a cat. Up close, the lantern's light was wrong: not yellow but a pale, drained grey, the color of eyes after the Wardens finished with you. In that light the boat's interior showed benches of wet wood and nothing else. No ferryman. Only, carved into the tiller post, letters worn nearly smooth:

*PAY WHAT YOU CARRY THAT CANNOT BE REPLACED.*

"Well, that's helpful," Tam muttered.

"It's a riddle," Odile said. "Or a trap."

"It's a price tag," Wren said. She set her candle on the platform stones and stepped over the gunwale into the boat, which didn't rock at all beneath her weight, which was somehow worse than if it had. "Memories. That's the toll. I told you what it costs."

She felt rather than heard them go still behind her.

"Memories," Silas repeated. His voice had gone careful, the way it went careful whenever money entered a conversation. Wren had watched him haggle in the night market and knew every register of that voice. This was the lowest one. The one he used when something mattered. "You knew this. You brought us here knowing this."

"I told you I'd been here twice and turned back, didn't I?" Wren shrugged. "I turned back because I wasn't ready to pay. Not because I didn't know the price."

"And now?"

"Now we don't have a choice." She nodded back up the stair, toward the city, where somewhere above them the Wardens were pulling Brannock apart memory by memory, hunting the map they carried in a wax-cloth roll inside Odile's coat. "Every hour we wait, they close another door behind us. This Gate is the way through to the deep markers. There is no other way through. So we pay."

"With what?" Tam said. "I'm not giving up—" He stopped. Wren saw him do the arithmetic everyone did, the silent inventory of the self: *what would I miss most?* She saw the answer arrive in his face and saw him flinch from it.

"You give what you choose," Wren said. "But it has to be real, and it has to hurt. That's how you know it counts."

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I've thought about it for six years." She turned to face the grey lantern, and when she spoke again she kept her voice level, because level voices were free, and everything else cost. "My mother sang me a lullaby. Every night until she got sick, and then every night after, sick, until her voice went. I remember every note. I've carried it through every bad year since. If I have to put something on the scale, it goes on the scale."

"Wren." Silas stepped toward the boat, and for once the market-face was gone entirely. What was underneath was older and more tired. "Listen to me. You don't have to—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she meant. She softened it, barely. "Don't do the noble thing, Silas. Don't offer to pay my toll for me. You can't. That's the whole point. Nobody can carry this for anybody else." She held out her hand to help him aboard, and after a moment, jaw tight, he took it. "Get in. All of you. Let's get this done."

* * *

They crossed together, because the boat would hold four and the stories said the Gate judged the party whole. Odile sat straight-backed near the prow with the map-roll against her chest. Tam hunched in the middle, hands pressed flat to his knees like a boy in a schoolroom. Silas sat across from Wren, and in the grey light his eyes were very bright, and very busy, and Wren recognized the look because she'd worn it herself for years: the look of someone frantically hiding their valuables.

The boat slid out over water that gave back no reflection. Not theirs, not the lantern's, not the archway's. Looking down was like looking down a well with the bottom painted black.

Twenty yards out, the ferryman appeared.

He didn't climb aboard. He didn't walk on water. He was simply there, seated at the tiller, between one blink and the next, as though he had always been sitting there and their eyes had finally consented to notice. He wore robes the color of canal-bottom mud, and where his face should have been there was a smoothness, featureless as the back of a spoon, that somehow still managed to regard each of them in turn.

When he spoke, the voice came from everywhere — the hull, the water, the hollow of Wren's own skull.

*FOUR SOULS. FOUR TOLLS.*

"We know the terms," Wren said. Her voice didn't shake. She'd practiced not shaking in mirrors until the practice became true. "Payment first, then passage."

*THE SMALL ONE UNDERSTANDS,* the ferryman said, and the featureless face inclined toward her, and Wren felt something lean on her mind, gentle and enormous, the way deep water leans on a diver's chest. *WHAT DO YOU BRING?*

Wren closed her eyes.

She went to it the way you'd visit a grave. The kitchen in the flooded quarter, the good chair by the stove, her mother's hands — cracked knuckles, flour in the creases — and the voice, thin by then but steady, threading through the steam:

*Sleep, my bird, the lamps are low,*
*the tide has taken rower's oar,*
*and what the tide brings home tonight*
*no lock can keep, no bolt restore—*

Her mother's voice. Six years dead and still note-perfect, still hers, the last living thing Wren had left of her besides a scar on her palm and a temper she tried to manage.

*Take it,* Wren said, to the dark behind her eyes. *All of it. Take it clean.*

The leaning pressure became a lifting. She felt the song rise out of her like heat leaving a cooling stone, felt the melody loosen from its moorings note by note — and here was the thing nobody warned you about: it didn't feel like theft. It felt like relief. Like setting down a load so heavy she'd forgotten her spine was curved beneath it. For one long breath she was lighter than she'd been since she was nine years old.

And then the silence where the song had been, and the grief arriving to fill it, and she understood the trade exactly: the ferryman took the pain and the treasure in the same motion, because they were the same thing. That was the toll. That had always been the toll.

She opened her eyes. Her face was wet. She wiped it with her sleeve, briskly, businesslike.

"It's done," she said. "Paid in full."

The featureless face regarded her a moment longer. Then, softly — and this frightened her more than anything yet, this almost-gentleness — *YOU PAID LIKE ONE WHO HAS PRACTICED LOSING.*

"Yeah," Wren said. "Well. Practice makes perfect."

Tam went next. He gave up something he wouldn't name aloud — only pressed his fists to his mouth for a long moment afterward, and shook, and said, in a raw whisper, "It was the day my brother was born. Before any of it went wrong." The ferryman took it, and Tam stared at his own empty hands like they belonged to a stranger.

Then the smooth face turned to Silas Fairweather, and Wren watched the hoarder meet the collector.

*AND YOU, KEEPER OF OTHERS' YEARS. WHAT DO YOU BRING?*

"I bring a question," Silas said.

The stillness that followed had texture. Even the grey flame seemed to hold itself rigid.

"The toll is a memory," Silas said, and now the market-voice came out fully, warm and reasonable, the voice of a man who had talked a hundred desperate people out of a hundred precious things and called it commerce. "But I've spent my life handling memories. Bought, sold, stolen, bottled. I know their weight better than anyone alive. So before you take from me — let me offer you a trade instead. A bargain. A better deal."

*SPEAK.*

"You don't want our memories. Not really. Look at what you took tonight — a lullaby, a birthday. Trinkets. Sweet, small, *spent*. You sit at this gate century after century collecting pocket-change griefs." Silas leaned forward, and Wren saw the trick coming and couldn't stop it and, honestly, didn't entirely want to. It was magnificent and awful, watching him work, watching him try to sell fire to the thing that owned the sea. "But I can tell you where there's a hoard. A real one. A den full of bottled memories on Tallow Row — hundreds of them, decades deep, every flavor of joy and sorrow ever squeezed out of this city. Freely given, freely taken, no stealing required. That's worth more than four trinkets. That's a treasury."

"Silas," Odile said quietly. "Those are people's memories. Stolen ones."

"They're already stolen," Silas said, not taking his eyes off the ferryman. "They're already in bottles. I'm just redirecting traffic."

*YOU OFFER ME THE MEMORIES OF STRANGERS,* the ferryman said. There was no anger in it. That was the worst part. There was nothing in it at all, and the nothing went on and on. *IN PLACE OF YOUR OWN.*

"I'm offering you volume," Silas said. "Quality, even. I'm offering you a deal instead of a mugging."

The featureless face turned fully toward him, and the boat stopped moving, and the black water beneath them went blacker, and Wren's scarred palm prickled with cold.

*KEEPER,* the ferryman said. *I HAVE CARRIED KINGS ACROSS THIS WATER. I HAVE CARRIED THE MEN WHO BUILT YOUR CITY AND THE WOMEN WHO BURNED IT AND THE MAGES WHO HID THEIR FIRE IN THE DARK. EVERY ONE OF THEM TRIED TO PAY WITH SOMETHING THAT WAS NOT THEIRS.* A pause, vast as the cavern. *IT IS ALWAYS THE POOR WHO HAGGLE, BECAUSE IT IS ALWAYS THE POOR WHO KNOW EXACTLY WHAT THEY OWN.*

Silas's jaw tightened. "Then name your price properly. Because I'm not handing you the only things I've got left just because you've got a monopoly on the river."

*EVERYONE SAYS SO,* the ferryman said, and Wren could have sworn — could have staked her life, what was left of it, on hearing it — that underneath the hollow voice there was something like weariness. Something ancient and tired and almost sad. *AND ALMOST EVERYONE, IN THE END, WEeps.*

Silas didn't weep. Wren would say that much for him later, telling the story in some dive or other. He sat in the grey lantern-light with his hands open on his knees and his whole face shut like a shop with its shutters drawn, and he said, "No deal. Find another way to charge me, or find another customer."

The boat began to move again. It moved past him, in fact, gliding on toward the arch while he sat rigid, un-tolled, and Wren understood with a cold drop in her stomach that the ferryman had simply — decided. Accepted his terms. Or filed him under *unfinished business.*

*THREE ARE PAID,* the voice said, from everywhere. *THE GATE OPENS FOR THREE. THE FOURTH MAY SWIM, OR BARGAIN AGAIN AT THE FAR SIDE. THE GATE IS PATIENT. IT HAS WAITED LONGER THAN YOUR CITY. IT CAN WAIT FOR HIM.*

The bronze arch loomed, and beneath its lintel the black water shivered for the first time — a single ring spreading outward, like something far below turning over in its sleep.

"Wait." Wren grabbed Silas's sleeve. "Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm not planning anything," Silas said. His voice was steady. His hands weren't. "I'm going to do what I do best. Talk my way through a locked door."

"You're going to drown us all."

"Probably not," he said, and managed the ghost of the market-smile, and Wren hated him a little for it, and hated herself more for noticing that under the smile he was terrified, genuinely terrified, and going forward anyway — which was, she supposed, the only kind of courage either of them had ever actually owned.

The arch swallowed the boat. Cold air poured down from the vaulted dark above, carrying a smell Wren had never smelled in the undercity: rain, and green growing things, and something older, mineral and bright, like lightning about to happen.

On the far side, the ferryman's voice followed them into the dark, softer now, almost courteous:

*WE ARE NOT FINISHED, KEEPER OF OTHERS' YEARS. THE GATE REMEMBERS DEBTS. AND SOMETHING ELSE DOES TOO — SOMETHING THAT SENT A DYING WOMAN TO FIND YOU. IT KNOWS YOUR NAME.*

Ahead, out of the black water, a single pale light kindled — small, steady, deliberate — like a lamp lit in a window to guide someone home.

Or to lure them in.

"Down," Odile breathed, unrolling the map with shaking hands. "The next marker's down. Whatever that light is — the map says we follow the water down."

Wren looked at the light. She thought about songs that no longer existed anywhere in the world except in the shape of the hole they'd left behind.

"Then we follow it," she said.
