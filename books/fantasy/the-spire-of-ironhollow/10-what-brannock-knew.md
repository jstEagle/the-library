## 10. What Brannock Knew

The fence's shop smelled of tallow and old paper, and the door stood open.

Odile Vasseur had been a Warden archivist for eleven years before they stripped her rank, and in eleven years she had learned one rule above all others: an open door in Ironhollow was never an invitation. It was either a trap or a corpse. Sometimes both.

She drew her knife — not the ceremonial warden-blade she'd surrendered at her dismissal, but the ugly little paring knife she used for opening letters — and stepped inside.

"Brannock," she said quietly. "It's Odile."

Nothing answered her except the drip of water somewhere behind the shelves. The lamps were still lit. That was the detail that stopped her heart for a full second: the lamps were lit, the ledger lay open on the counter with a pen resting in its gutter, ink dried black where it had bled from a hand that never came back to lift it. Brannock Vey was a miser about lamp-oil. He counted drops of it the way other men counted coins. If he had left this room alive, every flame would have been pinched out behind him.

He hadn't left the room alive.

Not exactly.

She found him in the back, among the crates of contraband he catalogued with obsessive care — bottled memories mostly, charms and trinkets, the harmless-looking trade that kept half of Ironhollow fed and all of it afraid. He sat slumped against a crate marked *FRAGILE — SALT GOODS*, and his eyes were the color of dishwater.

Grey. Not clouded, not blind. Grey the way iron is grey, the way ash is grey, the way everything the Wardens touched eventually became. Husked. She had seen it once before, years ago, through the viewing glass in the Citadel's processing wing: a smuggler brought in after his mind had been drained down to the studs. They called them husks in the official reports because *victims* implied wrongdoing on someone's part, and the Wardens' reports never implied wrongdoing on anyone's part but their prisoners'.

But Brannock wasn't dead. His chest moved. Slowly, shallowly, like a bellows with a split seam, but it moved.

"Brannock." She crouched in front of him, keeping her voice level the way you keep a lantern steady near spilled oil. "Can you hear me?"

His lips parted. What came out was not a word. It was a sound like wind through a keyhole, thin and tuneless, and then, after a long moment, another sound, and another, and slowly Odile realized he was trying to speak — that somewhere behind those grey eyes there was still a man, pressed flat as a flower in a book, trying to get out.

"I know," she said. "I know. Don't force it."

She looked at his hands. That was the tell, if you knew where to look — the hands of the drained were always clenched, because whatever ate the memory left the body last, and the last thing a person did was hold on. Brannock's fingers were white-knuckled around something small. She worked it loose gently, the way you'd take a stone from a child's fist.

A memory-charm. One of his own stock, a cheap thing in a chipped glass vial, the kind sold at night markets to people who wanted to feel something pretty for an hour. But the liquid inside was wrong. It should have shimmered, silver-gold, like lamplight through honey. This was dark. Swirling. Shot through with threads of grey that moved even when she held it perfectly still.

*Residue.* Her mother's word, though her mother had never known how much of her daughter's education would come back to this. When the Hollow Court's extractors drained a mind, they didn't take everything clean. They left lees. Sediment. The dregs of what had been drunk, pooling in whatever vessel was nearest. And Brannock, dying by inches in his own shop, had apparently done the only clever thing left to him: he'd grabbed a container.

He'd saved himself the only way a memory-fence could. He'd bottled what was left.

Odile sat back on her heels and stared at the vial, and her hands, which were steady hands, trained hands, hands that had handled first-edition grimoires and evidence ledgers and her mother's death-letter without trembling, began to shake.

Because she knew what was in it. She knew what Brannock had been doing for the past three days, ever since a bleeding stranger and a map had passed through Silas Fairweather's stairwell and set the whole rotten machine of the city grinding toward them. He'd been reading. He'd found things. And the Wardens had come for what he knew, and they had taken it, and what remained — the residue, the lees, the dregs — was sitting in her palm in a chipped glass vial.

Everything Brannock knew. Or the ghost of it.

"You clever, miserable old man," she whispered.

* * *

Reading residue required equipment she no longer had access to: a resonant basin, distilled moonwater, a quiet room and three hours. Odile had none of these. She had her mother's letters, a rented room above a fishmonger's that smelled exactly as you'd expect, and the growing certainty that she did not have three hours.

So she improvised, which was a polite way of saying she broke rules she'd spent eleven years enforcing.

The basin was a washbowl. The moonwater was rainwater she boiled twice and prayed over, not because prayer worked — nothing worked, that was rather the point of the age — but because ritual shaped intent, and intent was half of any reading. The rest was technique, and technique she had. She'd spent her archivist years studying the extraction protocols from the inside: how the Hollow Court's devices worked, what they took, what they missed. Everyone assumed the archives were full of books. The archives were full of people, or had been, once. The Wardens' founding generation had written down everything they did to the mages they captured, in meticulous, self-satisfied detail, certain they were building a permanent record of a permanent victory.

Her mother had died carrying a map to prove otherwise. Odile had inherited the doubt along with the grief, and she had carried both into the Citadel's deepest stacks, and she had read until she understood what the Wardens feared enough to outlaw.

Now she knelt over a washbowl in a room that stank of herring, poured the charm's contents across the water's surface, and watched.

The grey threads spread. Then, as the water stilled, they began to arrange themselves — not randomly, never randomly; memory had structure the way ice has structure, and even residue remembered its shape. Images surfaced like bodies coming up slow from deep water.

*A ledger. Numbers in Brannock's crabbed hand. A shipment of salt-goods that weighed too much.*

*A woman's voice, laughing: "You charge double for anything that makes them cry, Brannock, I've seen your books—"*

*A knock at the shuttered door. Late. Wrong hour.*

Then the images changed, and Odile stopped breathing.

*Grey cloaks filling the shop like smoke pouring under a door. The Warden commander's face — she knew that face, Commander Hale, twenty years in the field, decorated, dull-eyed — except his eyes weren't dull now. They were wet. Shining. Hungry. And beneath the skin of his cheeks something moved, something that was not muscle, something that shifted like eels under ice.*

*"Where is the map, fence?"*

*"I don't—"* Brannock's voice, cracking. *"I swear on my mother's grave, I don't have it. Fairweather has it. The charm-seller. I told him nothing, I refused him, I sent him away—"*

*"You read it."*

Silence in the vision. The particular silence of a man deciding whether a lie will save him.

*"You READ it," the thing wearing Hale said again, and its voice doubled on the last word, two voices in one throat, one bored and bureaucratic, one vast and cold and old, so old that hearing it made Odile's teeth ache. "Show me what you understood."*

And then Brannock had shown them, because they made him show them, because the draining was not violence the way a beating is violence — it was violence the way drowning is violence, patient and total. The residue showed it sideways, in fragments: the sensation of being opened, of pages turning in him that he could not close, of everything he was being read aloud by something that never stopped reading—

—and in the middle of it, because Brannock had been Brannock to the end, a gambit: he had *thought* the answers at them, loudly, desperately, and underneath the true answers he had buried the one thing that mattered, wrapped it in misdirection the way he wrapped contraband in salt-shipments—

*The Spire is real. The Spire lies beneath the citadel. The Wardens guard the prison they built around their own master.*

They had taken it anyway, of course. Taken everything. But taking a thought and understanding it were different acts, and Odile saw — she saw clearly, in the grey swirl of the residue — the moment the thing inside Hale received the knowledge and *did not comprehend what it meant*. It heard the words. It filed them. It did not understand that Brannock had just told it, in the only language left to him, that its own house was built on its own grave.

Or perhaps it understood perfectly, and simply didn't care, because it already knew. Because it had always known. Because you do not forget the prison you built yourself.

The vision shuddered. The last fragments rose: Brannock's hand closing on the vial. The grey tide going out of him and leaving the husk behind. The Wardens leaving him alive — deliberately, she realized, deliberately, a message in flesh, *this is what happens* — and the door standing open behind them because even the smallest cruelty served as advertisement.

The water went still. The bowl held nothing but rainwater and a faint oily shimmer, and the room smelled of herring and tallow and the end of a man's life.

Odile knelt there for a long time.

* * *

She had grown up on the official history. Every child in Ironhollow did. *In the Year of Ashes, the mage-lords turned their arts upon one another and upon the innocent, and the war of magic burned the old world to its bones. From the ashes rose the Wardens, who swore the Oath of Silence, who bound magic forever so that no child would ever again die in a fire conjured by a quarrel between gods. Remember the war. Fear the art. Trust the grey cloak.*

Her mother had taught her to read with a primer printed by the Citadel itself, and then, when Odile was older, had taught her to read *against* it — had shown her how official histories are written by survivors, and survivors are rarely neutral, and victors least of all. *Ask who benefits, little owl,* her mother used to say, tapping the page. *Every time. Ask who benefits.*

For eleven years in the archives, Odile had asked. She had found gaps — records sealed, decades missing, entire purges of documents dated suspiciously soon after the war's end. She had found the letters, the ones hidden in the drowned streets with the map, proving the outlawry was built on a lie. She had thought she understood the shape of it: a false flag, a manufactured war, an excuse to seize power.

She had not understood the truth, because the truth was worse.

The Hollow King was not a Warden. The Wardens were not men who had seized power after the war.

The Hollow King was the war. Or rather, he was the reason it ended the way it ended — not with victory, not with peace, but with a hoarding. He had been the first mage, the greatest of them, the one whose name had been struck from every record with such thoroughness that even the archives held only a title. And when the war threatened to consume everything — when magic raged unchecked and the world teetered — he had not ended it. He had *imprisoned* it. All of it. Gathered every working, every gift, every spark of the art into one place and locked the door, and then built a city on top of the lock, and staffed the city with grey-cloaked wardens sworn to hunt down any ember that escaped, and called it salvation.

He had outlawed magic to keep it all for himself.

Odile sat in the dark of her rented room, the residue-read still cooling in her mind like a brand, and felt something settle in her that was not quite anger and not quite grief. Anger was hot. Grief was heavy. This was neither. This was the cold, clear feeling of looking at a building whose blueprints you'd memorized and finally seeing the load-bearing wall.

*Power hoarded is power rotted.* That was why the Wardens' masters grew strange, why commanders like Hale wore things under their skin that had no business under human skin. You could not sit on top of a prison of raw magic for two hundred years without it seeping into you. The Hollow King had swallowed the world's soul whole, and it had been digesting him ever since, and everything grey in Ironhollow — the fear, the informants, the drained-eyed husks in the processing wing — was the taste of it leaking out.

Brannock had known the edges of it. Brannock, who owed everyone and trusted no one, had read the map's script and understood enough to be worth killing, and had spent his last living hour hiding the truth in the only vault the Wardens couldn't search: a cheap charm in a chipped bottle, entrusted by instinct to the one person in Ironhollow who might know what to do with it.

Except he hadn't entrusted it to her. He'd been holding it when they came. Coincidence, then. Or — and Odile's mother's voice murmured this in her ear, *ask who benefits, ask who chooses* — or Brannock had known exactly who would come looking for him, and had chosen to die holding the truth where she would find it.

The old fence had owed her money for six years. Perhaps he'd decided to pay his debts in the only currency that mattered anymore.

Odile rose. She washed the basin. She burned the vial's empty shell in the stove and watched the glass slump, because residue could be read twice by anyone with the training, and she intended to be the only reader.

Then she took out paper, and her mother's code, and began to write.

*To the charm-seller Silas Fairweather, if he lives, and to the thief called Wren, if she stands beside him:*

*I have read what Brannock could not say aloud. The enemy you flee and the destination you seek are the same. The Spire lies beneath the Citadel. The King who outlawed magic is the King who keeps it. There is no running far enough, because the whole city is the cage, and we are inside it with him.*

*Come to the fishmonger's on Tanner's Row before the next bell. Bring the map. Bring everything the dead woman wrote. We finish this in his cellar, or we don't finish at all.*

*— O.V.*

She sealed it, dressed in her oldest coat, and went down the stairs into the grey morning, past the fishmonger already gutting the day's catch, out into streets where grey cloaks moved in pairs and everyone she passed looked at the ground.

Two hundred years, she thought. Two hundred years the world had been told that magic was the monster. And the monster had been the one holding the key the entire time, growing fat in the dark, wearing the law as a skin.

Well. Skins could be broken.

She had seen it done — in the residue, in the fragments of a dying fence's stolen knowing, one image had risen at the very end, unbidden, unwanted, glowing at the center of the grey like coal in ash: a spire of light beneath a drowned foundation, and a door swinging open, and something vast exhaling after two centuries of held breath.

Brannock had glimpsed it in the map's script. He had died holding the picture of it.

Odile turned toward Tanner's Row and walked faster.

The bell for the third watch would ring within the hour. She intended to be at the fishmonger's when it did — and she intended, when Silas Fairweather arrived with his map and his guilt and his crew of strays, to look him in the eye and tell him the one fact that would change everything:

They had never been fleeing toward the Spire.

They had been marching straight into the Hollow King's open hand.
