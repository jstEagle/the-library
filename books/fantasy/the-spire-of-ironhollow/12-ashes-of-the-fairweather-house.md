## 12. Ashes of the Fairweather House

They came at dusk, which was wrong.

Wardens worked at dawn. That was the pattern Silas had learned in nine years of keeping his head below the rooftops — dawn raids, when the night shift was drunk or asleep and the day shift hadn't yet found its cruelty. Dawn was when they kicked doors. Dawn was when the grey-eyed ones walked the streets with their ledgers and their long needles and their patience.

This was dusk, and the bells of the citadel were ringing three-two-one, three-two-one, the cadence that meant *containment*, and Silas knew before Odile said a word that it was already too late to save anything.

He was halfway down Tannery Row with a coil of rope over one shoulder and two flasks of lantern oil in his satchel when Wren caught up to him, running flat-out, her braid half-unraveled and her face the color of old tallow.

"Silas." She grabbed his sleeve. "Silas, they're at your street."

The words didn't land right away. They arrived as nonsense, as noise, the way a foreign language arrives. His street. Gullwing Stair was nothing. Gullwing Stair was six tenements leaning on each other like drunkards, a shared privy, a widow who fed cats she couldn't afford, and forty-three people who had bought charms from him over the years because grief is everywhere and hope is cheap when it comes bottled.

"They're not arresting," Wren said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and that was what made him understand. "They're burning."

* * *

He ran. He would be ashamed later — he would take the memory out someday, examine it, find it wanting — but in the moment he did not think about the map, or Odile, or the plan, or the Spire waiting under the citadel like a heart under a floorboard. He dropped the rope in a doorway and ran through Tannery Row with the stink of lye and rendered fat in his nose and Wren's footsteps behind him, and he ran because forty-three bottles of borrowed hope had his fingerprints all over them and every one of those bottles had a buyer, and buyers have names, and names go in ledgers.

The smoke told him the truth from four streets away. Not cooking smoke, not chimney smoke — that was grey and lazy. This was black and eager, rolling up between the rooflines in fat ropes, and underneath it he could smell something sweeter and worse that he refused to name until naming was unavoidable.

Gullwing Stair was an orange wall.

The whole face of the row was alight, fire climbing the timber like ivy in fast motion, and the heat hit him at thirty paces like a hand pressed flat against his chest. People stood in the mouth of the street in their nightclothes even though it wasn't night, holding children, holding nothing, watching. And moving through them, unhurried as men walking off a meal, came the Wardens.

Not many of them. Eight. Nine. Enough. Their white-and-grey livery was immaculate. Two carried torches still lit, though there was nothing left to light. One held a ledger.

A ledger.

Silas stopped so suddenly Wren walked into his back. He watched the Warden with the book move down the line of displaced tenants, and watched the man stop at each one, and ask a question Silas couldn't hear but knew by heart because he'd asked versions of it himself for nine years, gently, in the dark, with coin in his palm: *Tell me what you remember.*

Except the Wardens weren't buying. They were checking names. The tenant would speak, the Warden would glance at the page, and either he'd nod and move on — or he'd look up, and signal, and two others would take the person by the arms and walk them toward the wagon at the end of the street. A small grey wagon with no windows.

"Mrs. Hessel," Silas said. The name came out of him on its own.

Wren looked at him. "What?"

"The widow. Third floor. She bought—" His throat closed around it. She'd bought a memory of her wedding day, because her husband's face had gone soft in her mind like wax left in sun, and she'd wanted to remember being loved even if it wasn't hers anymore. It hadn't been hers. It had been his theft, his bottlework, his sin sold at two coppers a charm. "She bought from me."

"Everyone here bought from you," Wren said. Not cruelly. Just true, the way she said most things. "That's why they're here. That's the list."

And he understood then, standing in the heat-shimmer with ash beginning to fall on his shoulders like dirty snow, that this was not a raid. This was arithmetic. Somewhere in the citadel's archives, someone had traced his trade — maybe from the informant at the sale, maybe from Brannock, husked and hollowed in some cell, his memories read like letters — and pulled every thread. Every charm. Every customer. Everyone who had ever held a piece of stolen magic in their hands and felt, just for a moment, that the world was bigger than Ironhollow allowed.

They weren't hunting the map anymore. They were burning the mapmaker's whole country.

"We can't—" Wren started.

"Get down." He hauled her behind a rain barrel as one of the Wardens turned, scanning the crowd's edges. Up close now, close enough to see the commander's face, and Silas felt his stomach drop through the cobbles, because he recognized the set of the jaw, the too-smooth skin, the eyes that were grey the way slate is grey — uniform, depthless, wrong. The thing beneath the skin was looking out again. It swept the street once, slowly, like a tongue across teeth.

It paused on the rain barrel.

Silas put his hand over Wren's mouth. She didn't breathe. Neither did he. Ten seconds. Twenty. The thing's gaze hung on the barrel the way a fly hangs on meat, and then a tenant screamed — a woman whose son hadn't come out — and the attention slid away, and the moment passed, and Silas discovered he had been gripping Wren hard enough to bruise.

"We have to go," she whispered.

"I know."

"Silas." She turned his face toward her with both hands, and her eyes were fierce and wet. "We *have to go*. There's nothing here to save. That's the whole point of what they did."

He looked past her at the fire, at the tenement where he'd slept for eleven years, where Marlowe had slept, where the loose floorboard under the bed still hid the first charm he'd ever made — a memory of his sister laughing, kept not to sell but to survive on, one swallow of joy rationed across the worst years. The flames took the third floor while he watched. Beams went down inside with a sound like breaking bones.

Marlowe. He thought her name deliberately, defiantly, the way a man touches a talisman. *I still have you. You're in my head. They can't burn you out of me.*

Yet.

* * *

Odile found them at the safehouse near the tannery sluice — a low brick room that smelled of wet wool, where Brannock used to store contraband lace back when contraband lace was worth hiding. She came in fast, hood back, and stopped dead when she saw their faces.

"I heard the bells," she said. "I came as soon as—"

"You knew." Silas didn't recognize his own voice. It was very quiet, and very flat, the voice of a man standing at the bottom of a well. "You're ex-Warden. You know how they work. Three-two-one means containment sweep. Did you know they'd pull the ledgers? Did you know they'd go after—"

"I didn't know tonight." She said it steadily, but her hands weren't steady. "If I'd known tonight I'd have told you, Silas. I'm not—" She stopped. Started again. "My mother died carrying that map. I've spent six years making sure her death bought something. I'm not going to waste it protecting my own conscience."

"It's not your conscience," Wren said from the corner, where she sat sharpening a knife that needed no sharpening. "It's his customers. It's a street full of people whose only crime was being sad in the wrong city."

"And ours will be next," Odile said, "if we don't move *now*. They traced him through the trade. Which means they'll trace the crew. Which means every name connected to Silas Fairweather is already ink on someone's list." She looked at him, and beneath the archivist's composure he saw something rawer — fear, yes, but also a terrible kind of recognition. "You know I'm right."

He did know. That was the worst part. He sat down on a crate of damp lace and put his head in his hands and let the truth arrive all the way: *this is what I built.* Nine years of telling himself the trade was harmless — charms, little comforts, bottled feelings nobody missed because he only took the duplicates, the repeats, the second copy of a happy day. Nine years of calling it mercy. And the ledger had crossed the city and touched every life he'd brushed against, and now the Wardens were collecting those lives like debts, and the fire on Gullwing Stair was his signature at the bottom of the page.

"My sister," he said, to no one, to everyone. "When I started, I told myself I was doing it for her memory. Keeping it alive. Keeping *something* alive in this grey pit." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Turns out everything I touch burns. I just never had the courage to watch it happen before."

Wren got up. Crossed the room. Stood in front of him with her arms crossed and her chin up, sixteen years old and harder than the citadel walls.

"Listen to me," she said. "You stole my sea. You stole Mrs. Hessel's wedding. You probably stole something from everyone on that street, and yeah, maybe it was wrong, and maybe someday you'll pay for it proper. But those Wardens aren't punishing *you* tonight. They're feeding. There's a difference, and you'd better learn it fast, because guilt that big makes a man stupid, and stupid gets us all husked."

Silas stared at her. Somewhere in the sluice above, water hammered stone, patient as centuries.

"When did you get wise?" he asked.

"Bought it off a charm seller," she said. "Cheap."

Despite everything — despite the ash still in his hair — something in his chest unclenched a quarter turn. He stood up. He looked at Odile. "The undercroft route. Through the drowned foundations. How fast can you get us in?"

"Tomorrow night, if the tide tables hold. Tonight, impossible."

"Then tomorrow night." He said it and hated it, saying it — every hour a delay was another name checked, another wagon loaded, another door kicked in at some dawn that hadn't learned the new schedule yet. "But we don't sleep here. We move to the ferryman's side of the gate tonight, and tomorrow we go down."

They gathered what they had, which was almost nothing: rope recovered from Tannery Row, oil, the map, Odile's forged credentials, Wren's knives. Silas took one last look around the lace room and found himself cataloguing it the way he catalogued everything now — *what could be stolen from this room, and what would it cost, and who would grieve it* — and made himself stop. Made himself leave the habit behind like an old coat.

They went out through the sluice tunnel single file, Odile first with the lantern, Wren last as rearguard because she insisted and because she was, infuriatingly, the best of them at hearing trouble coming.

Trouble came anyway.

* * *

It happened at the junction where the sluice tunnel met the old coal road — a wide place, echoing, three ways out and none of them good. Odile's lantern caught it first: movement at the far arch, white-and-grey, four figures, then six, fanning out with the unhurried competence of people who had done this a thousand times.

"Back," Odile breathed. "Back the way we—"

"Torchlight behind," Wren said. "Two more. We're bracketed."

There was a grate to the left, rusted, leading down toward the drowned levels — too narrow for pursuit, too flooded for certainty, a gamble. Odile pointed at it. "That's our door."

"It's also a funnel," Silas said. "They follow us straight down, we're fish in a bottle."

"Then somebody makes them not follow." Odile was already working the grate bolts. "I have credentials. I can stand in the arch and show papers, buy you sixty seconds of confusion."

"And then they arrest you," Silas said.

"Then they arrest me." Her jaw was set. "Sixty seconds. Take it."

"No." Wren stepped forward, and her voice had changed — gone flat and calm, the calm of someone who has already done the math and doesn't intend to argue about it. "Your face is known to the commander. Mine isn't. I'm a gutter kid; kids run from Wardens all the time. I run loud, draw them down the coal road, double back through the tanner's drain. I've done it a dozen times." She shoved her bundle into Silas's arms. "Get her through the grate."

"Wren—"

"Count of sixty," she said, and grinned at him, that sharp crooked grin he'd first seen across a blackmail negotiation, and something in his chest tore cleanly down the middle because he knew — he *knew*, the way you know weather coming — that she was lying about doubling back. Or that the lie would fail. Or that the grey-eyed thing didn't chase, it *waited*, and waiting beats any runner in the world.

"Don't," he said.

"Silas Fairweather," she said, backing toward the coal road, drawing her knives, "you stole my sea when I was nine years old and it was the kindest crime anyone ever committed on me. So shut up and owe me this one." And she turned and ran into the dark, shouting — actually shouting, reckless, glorious — "*Oi! Grey-eyes! Your mothers were lies!*"

Torches swung. Boots thundered. Six of the eight broke toward the coal road after her.

"MOVE." Odile wrenched the grate open and Silas went through into the cold black water to his knees, hauling himself along the drowned pipe, and behind him he heard the sounds he would hear for the rest of his life however long or short it proved to be: boots, shouts, a scuffle that was brief and professional, and then — cutting through everything, echoing down the pipe after him — Wren's voice, furious and unafraid, getting further away:

"*You want magic? Come and find some!*"

Then a sound like a struck bell, deep and wrong, the sound he'd heard in the memory-den, the sound of the thing beneath the skin doing whatever it did.

Then silence, except the water.

* * *

They surfaced an hour later in the ferryman's territory, soaked, shaking, silent. Odile wrung out her coat with mechanical precision. Silas sat on the wet stone with his arms around his knees and Wren's bundle in his lap — her spare knives, a stub of candle, a river-glass marble she'd kept since she was small enough to play with marbles — and he could not make himself open it, because opening it made her real and absent at once, and leaving it closed meant she might still come walking around the corner with that grin, demanding payment for services rendered.

"She doubled back," he said finally. "She knows those drains better than the rats. She doubled back."

"Yes," Odile said, in the tone of a woman filing a report she didn't believe.

"If they hurt her—"

"They won't hurt her." Odile sat down beside him. Her voice dropped. "That's the horror of it, Silas. They'll drain her. Carefully. Completely. And she'll wake tomorrow grey-eyed and obedient and *alive*, and she'll thank them, and part of you will wish they'd simply killed her, and that wish will be the truest thing you've ever felt." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious. "That is what waits at the end of every road in this city. That is what your Hollow King built. So grieve tonight. Rage tonight. But tomorrow we go down into the Spire, and we end the thing that did this — or we die trying, which at least keeps our eyes our own color."

Silas closed his fists around the bundle until the knife-hilts bit his palms. Above them, faint through the stone, the citadel bells rang three-two-one, three-two-one, and stopped.

Somewhere under all that stone, in a cell with no windows, a girl who had given up her mother's lullaby without hesitation was giving up everything else.

"Tomorrow," he said.

But the word tasted like ash, and ash was all he had left of home.
