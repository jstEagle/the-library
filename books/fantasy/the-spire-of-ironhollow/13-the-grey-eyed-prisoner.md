## 13. The Grey-Eyed Prisoner

The Warden citadel had raised Odile, and like any parent it knew exactly how to hurt her.

She stood in the queue at the Iron Gate in her old uniform — brushed, buttoned, the silver quill-and-chain of the Archivists' Corps pinned at her collar — and let the gate-sentries read her face. That was the trick of it. Wardens didn't check credentials so much as they checked posture. A woman who walked like she belonged was a woman who belonged. Everything else was paperwork.

"Archivist Vane," said the sentry, squinting at the badge she'd lifted from a dead colleague's locker three years ago. "You're not on today's rotation."

"I'm on nobody's rotation." She kept her voice flat with boredom, the tone that made junior officers look away first. "Inquisitor's request. Records transfer. Sealed."

He looked at the seal — wax she'd pressed herself with a forgery of the Inquisitor's mark, good enough to pass at arm's length, which was the only length sentries ever got — and stepped aside.

The gate swallowed her, and Odile walked into the belly of the machine her mother had died trying to photograph.

Her mother. *Sera Vane.* The name still caught in her chest like a fishhook. Thirty years an archivist, twenty of them quietly copying documents that contradicted the official history into cipher and hiding them in waterproof oilskin under the floor of their kitchen, until the night the Wardens came for her and Odile, fourteen years old, hid in the coal cellar and listened to her mother lie beautifully about nothing and get taken anyway. They'd returned her effects in a box. The box did not contain her hands.

Odile had spent her adult life inside this building learning one lesson: the citadel did not destroy its enemies. It *drained* them, slowly, politely, and filed what remained.

Now she climbed the archive stairs two at a time, nodding to faces she half-remembered, hating how easily her body remembered the rhythm of the place. Third floor: interrogation wing. Fourth: holding cells. Fifth and above: where they kept the ones who weren't prisoners anymore but hadn't been released either.

The girl would be on five.

*Please be alive,* she thought, and corrected herself. *Be more than alive.*

* * *

The fifth-floor corridor smelled of lye and something sweeter underneath, something like overripe fruit left in a closed room. Odile had smelled it once before, as a young archivist, when she'd been ordered to catalog the contents of a cell after a "processing." She'd cataloged a chair, a table, a bucket, and a man sitting perfectly still with grey eyes, breathing, blinking, gone. Her supervisor had signed the inventory without looking up.

Two wardens stood post at the junction ahead, halberds grounded, faces hidden behind the featureless iron masks that gave the order its name. Odile slowed. There was no records-transfer excuse that got you onto the fifth floor. The fifth floor didn't exist on any roster she could cite.

She turned instead toward the stairwell door marked MAINTENANCE — DUMBWAITER ACCESS, because every fortress built by frightened men eventually grew servant passages, and the Wardens' fear had been built in a hurry during the war. The dumbwaiter shaft ran the height of the holding floors, wide enough for a supply crate, narrow enough that a slim archivist with a prybar and a grudge could climb it.

She climbed. The rungs were greasy with old tallow. Somewhere below, the citadel's great engine-room thrummed through the stone like a second heartbeat, and Odile tried not to think about what fed it.

At the fifth-floor hatch she stopped, pressed her ear to the wood, and listened.

Voices. Two, maybe three. And underneath them, fainter, a sound that made her grip the prybar until her knuckles went white:

Humming. Off-key. Defiant. A street tune, some gutter ballad about a ferryman and his toll, sung through cracked lips by someone determined to keep a piece of herself the Wardens hadn't inventoried yet.

Alive. More than alive. Still *herself.*

Odile eased the hatch open an inch.

* * *

The cell block was a long gallery of iron-barred rooms, lit by gas lamps turned down to a bruised amber. Three wardens stood around an open cell halfway down, and Odile's stomach dropped when she saw the apparatus they'd wheeled in beside them.

A siphon-chair. Copper tubing, glass reservoirs, leather restraints. She'd seen the schematics in the deep archive, classified under SANITATION EQUIPMENT, because even here, even among themselves, they couldn't bear to write the true word.

And in the chair — no. Not in the chair. Tied *beside* the chair, dumped against the wall like cargo, because the chair was occupied by a middle-aged man whose eyes had already gone grey. Brannock. The fence. She recognized him from the description Silas had given, from the ledger of debts Silas carried like a rosary. His reservoirs were full. Two of them. Amber-gold liquid catching the lamplight, and Odile felt sick looking at it, because she knew what it was. Years. Whole years, poured out of a man and stoppered.

They hadn't finished him. That was the worst part. They'd taken what they needed and left him breathing, because the Hollow King's order was explicit — the courier's contacts were to be drained *gradually*, so the residue stayed fresh. Readable. Like keeping a book open to your page.

The third warden crouched in front of the other cell, and Odile saw the girl.

Wren. Half-drained already — Odile could see it in the way her skin hung slightly loose on her frame, in the grey creeping in at the edges of her irises like frost on a window — but sitting up straight against the bars, chin high, humming her filthy little song while a man in an iron mask loomed over her.

"—told you," Wren was saying, voice hoarse but steady. "I don't have it anymore. I gave what I had to the ferryman. Ask him. Oh wait." A crooked grin. "You can't."

The warden backhanded her through the bars. The humming stopped. Then, muffled, wet, stubbornly rhythmic, it started again.

Odile had planned for stealth. She had a route mapped, a timetable, contingencies. She had told Silas and the others to stay away because this part required someone the citadel trusted.

But watching that girl hum through a split lip while a monster in a mask waited for permission to unmake her, all of Odile's careful plans burned down to a single hot point.

She pulled the fire-alarm lever.

Every citadel had them — a legacy of the war years, wired to summon the garrison to fire stations. The bells began to crash. Boots thundered on stairs. The three wardens at the cell block exchanged a look, and the senior one barked, "Stay with the prisoner," to the youngest and ran.

One guard. Odile counted her breaths. The boy couldn't have been older than nineteen. He stood rigid, terrified of the bells, terrified of his orders, and Odile stepped out of the maintenance alcove in her crisp uniform with her forged seal held up like a talisman.

"You. Archivist Corps, attached to Inquisitorial processing. I'm here for the prisoner's residual extraction — the reservoirs need sealing before transport or the residue spoils." She said it fast, bored, annoyed, the way real authority talked. "Where is your supervisor?"

"He— the alarm—"

"The alarm is not your problem. Your problem is that these samples degrade every minute we stand here listening to bells." She jerked her chin at Brannock's reservoirs. "Uncuff me access to the cell log. Now."

He fumbled for the ring of keys at his belt, and that was when Odile drove the prybar's short end into the base of his skull, precisely hard enough. He folded. She caught him before he hit the ground, lowered him gently — she wasn't her mother's daughter for nothing; she catalogued everything, even her own guilt — and took the keys.

"Bells," she muttered to herself. "Of course it works. It worked in 61 and it'll work in 91. Fear never updates its methods."

She unlocked the cell.

* * *

Wren came off the wall like a spring trap, and for a moment Odile had a forearm across her throat and the girl's knee driving toward it, before Wren registered the absence of a mask, the absence of grey eyes, the presence of a stranger holding out keys instead of chains.

"Wardens wear masks," Odile said quietly. "I don't."

Wren didn't relax. Of course she didn't. But she stopped trying to kill Odile, which in the circumstances was nearly tenderness. "You're the cartographer's daughter."

"Word travels."

"Ferryman talks." Wren swayed, caught herself on the bars. Up close the drain was worse — her eyes were half-frosted, her hands trembling with a fine constant shiver, and there was a hollow behind her cheekbones that hadn't been there when Silas described her. "You're here for me? Why? I'm nobody's map."

"You're the courier's witness." Odile cut the wrist restraints off the nearest empty chair and started checking the boy-guard's pulse out of habit. Alive. Good. "And you're the only person alive who's seen what my mother died protecting. So yes. You matter. Don't argue, you're bad at it."

Something flickered across Wren's face — surprise, quickly buried, the expression of a girl who had been told she mattered approximately never. "Brannock," she rasped. "They've been at him for two days. Can he walk?"

Odile crossed to the fence and took his hand. His fingers were cold and pliant as wax. His eyes tracked nothing. But when she leaned close, she heard it — a whisper-thin thread of breath forming shapes. Not words. *Residue.* The drained leave echoes in their throats, her mother's notes said, like water still moving in a pipe after the tap is shut.

She put her ear to his lips.

"...king..." Brannock breathed. "...the king... was the first... he outlawed it... so no one else..."

The bells were dying down. Somewhere above, boots were regrouping, and officers would soon be asking why a fire alarm had sounded on a floor that officially contained only sanitation equipment.

"We can't carry him," Odile said, and hated every word. "If I take him, neither of us leaves. I'm sorry."

Wren looked at the husk of the old fence for a long moment. "He owed Silas money," she said finally. "Silas will want that debt settled. Tell him—" Her voice cracked, just once. "Tell him Brannock paid."

* * *

The dumbwaiter again, going down this time, Wren climbing above her, both of them moving through the dark shaft while the citadel's alarms wound down into confused silence. Twice they froze as lantern-light swept the hatch below them. Once a warden stood directly beneath Odile's feet, close enough that she could hear him chewing, and she stared at the underside of the floorboards and thought about her mother's hands, which were in a box somewhere in this building, and about how many boxes this place must contain.

They reached the cellar level, then the drainage tunnel that smelled of river rot and freedom. Only when the citadel's walls were a dark shape behind them and the drowned streets opened ahead did Wren stop, brace against a wall, and shake.

Not crying. Odile had seen enough crying to know the difference. This was the shaking of a body remembering it had been afraid and hadn't been allowed to show it.

"They asked about the fragment," Wren said, when she could talk. "For two days. What fragment, whose memory, where did the courier touch you, did she speak to you." She laughed, a small broken sound. "I told them nothing because there was nothing to tell. Except."

Odile waited.

"Except there *was* something." Wren turned, and in the phosphor-light of the tunnel lamps her half-drained eyes looked almost silver. "When I was little — seven, maybe eight — before the fever took my mother, before everything — there was a day I can't explain. I woke up knowing things. A room I'd never seen. A rite I'd never learned. Words in a language I didn't speak, and a door, a great door under the city, and a way to open it." She hugged her arms around herself. "My mother found me reciting it and went white as bone. She said, *where did you hear that,* and I said I didn't hear it, I *knew* it, and she held me so hard it hurt and said, *then it chose you, and God help us both.*"

"The courier," Odile said slowly. "My mother's contact. The woman who gave Silas the map."

"She touched everyone she passed. I figured that out later. Ferryman says memories jump, sometimes. Skin to skin, stranger to stranger, like sparks." Wren's jaw tightened. "She gave me a piece of her. A piece of the Spire's own unbinding. I've been carrying it since I was eight years old without knowing what it was. And now the Wardens have scraped half of *me* looking for it, and I can still feel it sitting there, under everything, like a coal that won't go out."

Odile reached out — hesitated — and then did the thing her mother would have done, the thing she'd sworn she'd never be soft enough to do. She pulled the girl into her arms.

Wren went rigid. Then, with the suddenness of a dam failing, she gripped back, hard, her forehead against Odile's shoulder, shaking all over again, and this time it might have been crying after all.

"I've got you," Odile said. It surprised her how easily the words came. "You hear me? Whatever they took, whatever's left — I've got you. Nobody drains you twice."

Above them, faint through the stone, the citadel's bells began again — not the fire signal this time. Something new. A pattern Odile had only heard described, in documents stamped with seals she'd spent her life forging.

General mobilization. They'd found the empty cell.

She pulled back, kept her hands on Wren's shoulders. "Can you walk to the smuggler's cellar?"

"I can dance there if it helps."

"Good." Odile allowed herself one breath of relief, one only. "Because we have a rite to rebuild, and you're carrying half of it in your head — and I think Silas Fairweather is carrying the other half in his pocket, whether he knows it or not."

Wren wiped her face with her sleeve and grinned, grey-eyed and ferocious. "Then let's go collect our thief."
