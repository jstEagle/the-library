## 14. Bargain with the Hollow King

They took his boots first.

Silas didn't understand that until later — until he understood everything about the Hollow King and the way the man collected things. Boots had soles worn to the shape of a foot. They were memory of a kind. Everything was.

The Warden escort marched him down through levels of the citadel he hadn't known existed, past checkpoints where guards looked through him as if he were already husked, already grey. His wrists were bound in iron cuffs etched with script that itched against his skin like ants walking. Null-iron. He'd sold charms made from its shavings once, back when he was stupid and new to the trade — charms that held nothing at all, that only *felt* like they held something. People had paid well for them. Grief made buyers generous.

He thought about that now, descending, because thinking about anything else meant thinking about Wren's face when the net took her, or Odile shouting his name as the corridor collapsed behind them, or Brannock's body going still in the doorway with the seconds he'd bought ticking away unused.

So: boots. Null-iron. The itch of it.

The stairwell went down further than the citadel had foundations. That should have frightened him more than it did. But somewhere around the hundredth step his fear had burned down to embers, and what was left was a strange, exhausted clarity — the same clarity he'd felt the night the courier died on his stairs with her blood pooling between the boards and her hand pressing the map against his chest like she was sealing a letter.

*She'll know you have it.*

She'd known. And now he did too.

* * *

The room at the bottom was not a throne room.

That was the first thing that surprised him. He'd expected stone and shadow, banners and bones — some theater of dread built by a man who called himself a king. Instead the chamber was small, round, warm-lit, and lined floor to ceiling with shelves. Thousands upon thousands of small glass bottles, each one glowing faintly with its own color: amber, violet, sea-green, the deep bruised blue of a memory someone had loved enough to keep.

A library. A hoard. A dragon's cave made of charm-glass.

Silas knew exactly what he was looking at, because he'd been making poor imitations of it for eleven years. Every bottle here was a stolen life. Every bottle here was someone's best day, pried loose and shelved.

"You're a collector," Silas said, before the guards even finished positioning themselves at the door. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "I had a shop like this. Smaller. Worse lighting."

From the shadows between two shelves, something laughed.

It was not a pleasant sound. It was the laugh of a man who had forgotten how laughing worked and was reconstructing it from notes. Then the Hollow King stepped into the light, and Silas forgot to be clever.

He looked ordinary. That was the horror of it. A broad, tired man in a plain dark coat, silver at the temples, with the kind of face you'd trust with your purse or your daughter. He might have been a magistrate. A schoolmaster. Someone's fond uncle. Only his eyes gave him away — not grey like the husks', but *full*, every color at once, swirling slow like oil on water, like all the bottled light on all the shelves had been poured into them and never quite settled.

"Silas Fairweather," the King said. "Memory-thief. Charm-seller. Son of a laundress and a man who left. Brother of—"

"Don't." It came out of Silas like a reflex, fast and hard, before his mind could catch up to why. "Don't say her name."

The King smiled gently. "You see? You don't have the map on you. You don't have the rite. You don't have anything, Mr. Fairweather, except one thing I want. And you guard it without even being asked."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," the King agreed. "You don't. That's rather the point." He gestured, and a chair scraped itself across the floor toward Silas — no hand near it, just the wood moving with a smooth, obedient grace that made Silas's skin crawl, because that was magic, real magic, casual and effortless, the thing an entire city had been taught to burn people for. "Sit. Please. This doesn't need to be an interrogation. I've read enough interrogations to find them tedious. Let it be a negotiation between professionals. You have a trade. So do I."

Silas sat. The cuffs stayed on. The chair was, insultingly, comfortable.

"You're the one who outlawed magic," Silas said. "Odile found the letters. The war, the Purges, the Wardens — all of it built on you."

"I built a dam," the King said mildly. "You're standing downstream of it, so you think it's a wall. Understand my position, Mr. Fairweather. I was there when the last war ended. I saw what unbound magic does when two nations hold it — cities folded like paper, rivers taught to run uphill, children born knowing how to kill before they know their own names. I ended that. Yes, I kept the key. Someone had to. Call it theft if you like." His eyes glittered, amused. "You of all people can hardly afford the word."

"And everyone who ever touched a spell got drained for it. Grey-eyed. Hollowed out."

"The reservoir has limits." The King said it the way a merchant says *prices fluctuate*. "I take only what must be taken. Order requires a budget, Mr. Fairweather, as any tradesman knows. Ironhollow is safe. Safe, lawful, magicless, and alive. Would you trade that for the old world? Because I've seen the old world. I stood in it. There is no version of its return that doesn't begin with fire."

It was a good speech. Silas could hear how good it was — the practiced cadence, the reasonable weight of it, the way it made horror sound like bookkeeping. He'd used lesser versions of it himself, selling charms to people who couldn't afford them. *You need this. Everyone pays. It's the way of things.*

"That's not why I'm here, though," Silas said. "You didn't drag me down three hundred steps to debate civic policy. You want the map. Kill me and dig it out of whatever hole I hid it in."

"Oh, I'll find it eventually. Maps are patient things; so am I. No — I brought you here for a different reason entirely." The King crossed to a shelf, ran one finger along the bottles without touching them, a connoisseur's idle drift. "Do you know what I admire about your work, Mr. Fairweather? Your charms are honest. Small, yes — a taste of the sea, an hour of sunlight, a stranger's wedding day. But you never sell a memory you love. In eleven years, you have kept your own hands empty of your own goods. Everything precious to you, you've kept. Everything else, you've traded." He turned. "That's not greed. That's discipline. I recognize it. We are, you might say, colleagues."

"We're nothing alike."

"We're *exactly* alike. You simply lack scale." The King lifted a single bottle from the shelf — deep green, glowing soft as river-light — and held it up between two fingers. "Which brings me to my offer."

* * *

Later — much later, in the years after, when people asked Silas what the Hollow King had offered him and he gave the honest answer, *my sister*, they always assumed it was a lie, some trick, some conjurer's illusion dressed up as temptation. They assumed no one could actually deliver such a thing.

But Silas had been in the trade. He knew the color of a true memory when he saw one.

"This," the King said, "was confiscated nineteen years ago. From a tenement in the Lantern Quarter during a routine purge. A child's memory — a girl, seven years old. She was watching her older brother practice juggling stolen apples in the kitchen while their mother sang off-key over the wash. Do you know it?"

The room tilted.

Not metaphorically — Silas's balance went, the chair swaying under him, the shelves smearing into ribbons of colored light. Because he knew it. Of course he knew it. He'd lived it, and then he'd lost it, and then for nineteen years he had carried the wound where it used to be, a missing tooth his tongue returned to nightly.

Lissa. Laughing, apple in hand, juice on her chin, saying *you drop everything, Si, you drop everything except me.*

"It can't be," he heard himself say. "Her memories burned with the house. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said what the Wardens told them to say. We archive, Mr. Fairweather. We destroy nothing we can use." The King set the bottle on the table between them, and the green light spilled across the wood like water. "Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, so attend. Give me the map — freely, knowingly, with your whole will behind it. In exchange, this returns to you. Not a copy. Not an echo. The memory itself, restored to its place, seamless. You will remember your sister's face as clearly as you remember your own hands. And more than that—" he leaned forward, voice dropping into something almost kind, "—you will keep it. Forever. My protection extends to what is mine, and what I return becomes mine to protect. No Warden will ever drain it. No accident will ever erode it. She will stay with you until you die of old age in a warm bed, and she will be the last thing you think of."

Silas couldn't speak.

He had prepared for torture on the walk down. For pain, for threats, for Wren's name spoken in that terrible gentle voice. He had not prepared for this: the thing itself, sitting three feet away, glowing, *hers*, after nineteen years of reaching into his own head and finding smooth blankness where the most important person in the world had been.

Because that was the part nobody understood about losing a memory instead of a person. A dead sister left a grave he could visit, letters he could reread, a mother who remembered her aloud. But a *taken* memory left nothing to visit. Just a shape of absence. Just the knowledge that he had loved someone enormously and could no longer say why, could no longer picture her, could sometimes wake at night with her name dissolving on his lips like a dream escaping at dawn.

He'd told himself for years he barely missed it. He'd built a whole competent, careful, profitable life on top of the absence and called it living.

And here it was. Here she was. One bottle. One signature away.

"What's the catch," he managed. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"There is no catch. That's what you must understand." The King spread his hands, magnanimous. "I am not a trickster. Tricksters waste time. I am a collector, and collectors pay fair prices. The map for the memory. Your freedom besides — you and the girl and the archivist, walked out of this citadel untouched. All I require is that you stop. Stop carrying, stop running, stop feeding hope to a city that is better off asleep. Give me the map, and go home, and spend the rest of your life remembering."

Silas reached for the bottle.

His hand stopped an inch away — not because anyone stopped it. Nothing held him. The guards hadn't moved. The King watched him with those impossible eyes, patient as tide.

His hand stopped because some part of him, the part that had survived eleven years in Ironhollow by knowing exactly which deals rotted from the inside, was screaming. And underneath the screaming, quieter, another voice — Odile's, dry as paper: *My mother died carrying that map. Ask yourself why she chose you.*

Why *had* she chosen him? A dying courier with a hundred doors to pick, and she'd picked the stairwell of a charm-thief. Coincidence was a currency Silas had never been able to afford.

But Lissa.

Gods, but Lissa.

He could feel the shape of the deal fitting itself around him like a coat cut to his measure. Take it. Take the map out of his hiding place — the loose brick in the chimney of Odile's safehouse, he'd tell them, he'd draw it, he'd walk them straight to it — and then take the bottle and walk out into the morning and sit in the sun and remember his sister laughing, forever, safely, while the world above stayed asleep and the grey-eyed husks stacked up in the Wardens' ledgers and nobody ever again had to choose between memory and survival.

Everyone pays, he'd told his customers. It's the way of things.

His fingers trembled an inch from the glass.

Inside, faintly, he could almost see her — a smear of gold and motion, a child's blur of laughter rendered in light. Nineteen years. He had gone nineteen years unable to answer the simplest question of his life: *what did she look like?*

"Take your time," the King said softly. "I mean that sincerely. Memories keep. It's what makes them superior currency."

Silas closed his eyes.

And in the dark behind his lids, unbidden, came a different memory — newer, smaller, cheaper. Wren on a rooftop, wind-torn, grinning around a stolen apple with juice on her chin, saying *you drop everything, Fairweather, you drop everything except yourself.* He'd laughed. He'd almost told her. He'd looked at her and seen, for one vertiginous second, a ghost wearing a stranger's grin, and he'd shoved it down where he shoved everything.

An apple. Juice on the chin. Twice now, in one lifetime, fate had handed him that image and he'd let both versions slip through his fingers.

His hand withdrew.

"No," Silas said.

The word came out cracked and small and it was the hardest thing he had ever said, harder than the rite would be, harder than anything waiting above. He opened his eyes and made himself look at the bottle — at her — and say it again.

"No. Keep her. Whatever she is in there, she isn't yours to give, and I'm not buying her back with everyone else's tomorrow."

Something moved behind the King's swirling eyes. Disappointment, maybe. Or appraisal. Or hunger recalibrating.

"A pity," the King said, and set the bottle back on the shelf, among the thousands, in its exact former place. "For you. Though I confess—" and now the gentleness drained out of his voice entirely, leaving something old and cold and vast underneath, the thing the Warden commander's skin had been stretched over, "—I suspected you'd refuse. The courier suspected it too. She spent a great deal choosing you, Mr. Fairweather. I've always wondered what she saw."

"She?" Silas's heart stumbled. "You know who she was."

"I know everything that passes through my city." The King rose, and the light of a thousand bottles bent toward him as though he were their sun. "And I know you didn't come down here alone in spirit, whatever your friends believe. Someone marked your trail. Someone has been carrying a piece of her since before you ever met her." He smiled, and it was almost warm, which was worst of all. "Go and ask her, Mr. Fairweather. Ask the girl what she's been dreaming. Ask her why she really found you that night on the rooftops."

The guards moved. The door opened. The null-iron cuffs itched.

"And Mr. Fairweather," the King added, as they dragged him toward the stair, "when she tells you — and she will — do try to bring me the map afterward. The offer stands. Memories keep."

The door shut. The stairs rose ahead of him, three hundred steps back into the burning world.

Silas climbed, and climbed, and the whole way up, one question rang in the hollow where his sister used to be:

*What had Wren been carrying?*
