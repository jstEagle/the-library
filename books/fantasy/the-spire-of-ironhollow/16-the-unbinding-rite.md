## 16. The Unbinding Rite

The smuggler's cellar smelled of brine and old candle smoke, a low brick room beneath a fishmonger's shop that hadn't sold fish in twenty years. Water seeped through the walls in slow dark threads. Somewhere overhead, Ironhollow went about its evening — carts rattling, bells ringing the hour — as if the city weren't holding its breath.

Silas laid the letters out on a plank table in the order Odile had taught him: oldest at the left, newest at the right, each one flattened beneath whatever weight they'd scavenged. A bottle of ink gone thick. A hammer. His own palm, pressed flat over the corner of the first page to keep it from curling.

The paper was thin as mothwing. The handwriting belonged to a woman who had known she was running out of time to write it.

*If you are reading this,* the first letter began, *then the map found someone, which means I am dead, which means we are all of us out of time.*

Odile sat across from him with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back with a strip of red cloth — Wren's cloth, though Wren had never admitted giving it. She read like she fought: fast, total, no wasted motion. Her finger traced a line of script and stopped.

"Here." Her voice came out strange, scraped thin. "This is where it starts."

Wren leaned over her shoulder from the crate she'd claimed as a chair, one boot up on the edge, chewing on nothing because there was nothing to chew. Half her hair had been shorn off by the Wardens' shears when they took her, and the ragged half hung around her face like something torn rather than grown. It made her look younger. It made Silas's chest hurt every time he looked at her, so he tried not to look.

"Read it out," Wren said. "All of it. I'm sick of you two reading like it's a will."

"It *is* a will," Odile said.

"Then read it like one."

Odile looked at Silas. He nodded. She cleared her throat, and read.

---

*"My mother taught me the unbinding before I was old enough to know why it mattered. She said the Spire is not a building. It is a lock, and the lock is alive, and everything the Hollow King ever stole is stacked behind it like water behind a dam.*

*"A lock built by mages can only be opened by mages. So he killed them all — or thought he did. But a rite is not a mage. A rite survives. He burned the books and drowned the archives and salted the earth of the old quarter, but he could not burn what lives inside a person, freely given.*

*"That is the whole of it. That is the terrible simplicity of it. The Spire opens for a memory of absolute love, given without reservation, without hope of return, without even the wish to keep it. Not stolen. Not sold. Not traded or hoarded or hidden away in a charm to be worn against the skin on cold nights.*

*"Given. All of it. Forever.*

*"He built his whole kingdom on taking. He cannot imagine a door that only opens outward."*

---

Silence settled into the cellar like silt.

Wren spoke first, because Wren always spoke first. "Absolute love. Freely given." She said it flat, testing the words for traps. "That's it? That's the whole ritual? No blood, no moonlight, no chanting backwards?"

"The rest is scaffolding," Odile said. "Positions, timing, the fragment you carry as the keyhole's shape. But yes. That's the heart."

"Then it's easy." Wren spread her hands. "I've got a fragment of my mother's lullaby left. I gave the ferryman the song itself, but I kept —" She stopped. Recalculated. Silas watched her do the arithmetic and arrive somewhere she didn't want to be. "No. Wait. The ferryman took the lullaby. What I've got left is just —"

"Just what?" Odile asked gently, and Silas hated how gentle, because gentleness was the sound a blow makes just before it lands.

"A feeling," Wren said. Her jaw set. "Warmth. Like being small and safe. There's no face attached anymore. Is that enough?"

Odile didn't answer right away, and the not-answering was an answer.

*Absolute love.* Silas turned the phrase over the way he'd turn a charm in his fingers, checking for flaws. He knew memories. He knew their weights and textures, the way grief felt different from longing, the way joy bottled too long went sour. He knew what a memory of absolute love weighed.

It weighed everything.

"Then it has to be mine," he said.

Both women looked at him. The candle flame leaned in the draft and righted itself.

"Your sister," Odile said. Not a question. Brannock must have told her, before Brannock — Silas pushed the thought of Brannock away, husked and hollow-eyed, a man-shaped absence they'd left behind in the flooded streets. Later. Grieve later. Or never; he'd had practice at never.

"My sister," Silas said.

"You don't have to —"

"Yes, I do." He said it quietly, and the quiet made it worse somehow, made it land like a verdict instead of a boast. "Think it through. Wren's fragment is a keyhole shape, not a key. Yours—" he nodded at Odile "—you loved your mother, but your mother *is* the stranger who wrote these letters, and the letters say given, not inherited. And me." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "Me, I've spent fifteen years bottling other people's love and selling it back to them at two coppers a charm. Do you know what I have of my own? One thing. One whole thing. Everything else I ever owned I pawned off piece by piece to eat."

"And you'd give up the last of it," Wren said, "for a city that burned your house down."

"For a city that doesn't remember why it's afraid." He met her eyes. "For everyone the Wardens drained into grey-eyed nothing while the rest of us learned not to look up. For you." He said it simply. "They took you because of me, Wren. Because of my trade, my map, my mess. If I get to pay for that with a memory instead of a life, that's not a sacrifice. That's a bargain."

"That's horseshit," Wren said, but her voice wobbled on the last syllable, and she looked away fast, blinking hard at the candle, at the letters, at anything but him.

* * *

They worked through the night, because the rite demanded precision and precision demanded hours none of them could spare, and so they spent them anyway, spending time like a currency they were already overdrawn on.

The letters were more detailed than the first reading suggested. The stranger — Odile's mother, Maren, though Odile still couldn't say the name aloud, Silas noticed; she called her *the author*, *she*, *my mother* only once, and then her voice broke and she went back to *the author* — Maren had written the rite down in pieces, scattered across eleven years of letters, encoded in recipes and shipping manifests and one long false prayer to a god nobody in Ironhollow worshipped anymore. Odile decoded. Wren held the fragment in place, eyes closed, murmuring the shapes of it — *warmth, warmth, like a kitchen, like a hand on my hair* — while Odile matched the resonance against the script. Silas did what Silas did best.

He memorized.

Every line. Every pause. The way the rite required three voices at the threshold and silence at the center. The way the giver had to speak the memory aloud — not describe it, *speak it*, live it one last time in words, and then release it mid-sentence, unfinished, forever unfinished. That detail snagged in him like a fishhook.

"Why unfinished?" he asked.

"Because if you finish it," Odile said, not looking up from the page, "you keep it. Completion is possession. The rite requires you to let go *while you still want it*."

"So I'll spend the last minute of my sister's existence in my head talking about her, and then stop. Mid-word."

"Mid-word."

"And after that I won't know why I stopped. Won't know there was anything to stop."

"No." Odile finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "After that, you'll be a man standing in front of a door he opened, with no idea whose face used to matter to him."

Silas nodded slowly. "And the map chose me for this."

"The map chose the man who held a piece of the rite inside him and didn't know it." Odile gathered the pages, squared them. "You sold a memory years ago, Silas. You've told me you don't remember whose. It was hers. It was this — the unbinding, the shape of the lock, the whole architecture. She carried the rite in her head because paper burns and people can be searched, but a memory sold to a charm-thief looks like trinkets. She hid the revolution inside your inventory." A ghost of a smile. "She gambled that someday the thief would turn out to be something more than a thief."

"And if I wasn't?"

"Then the world stayed dark and she died for nothing. Gamblers die either way. At least she bet on someone."

* * *

Near dawn, Wren fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, her chin dropping to her chest, the red cloth slipping from her hair onto the table. Odile draped a coat over her without waking her and stood looking down at her for a long moment.

"She reminds me of her," Odile said quietly. "Of my mother. Stubborn in exactly the wrong places."

"People say that about stubbornness like it's a flaw."

"In my family it's a terminal condition." Odile sat back down. For a while she just watched the candle. Then: "You should say goodbye to it. Before we go. Tonight, or in the corridors under the citadel — wherever there's a last quiet minute. Don't save it for the center of the rite. Walk in already grieving. It's kinder."

"Kinder to whom?"

"To whoever's standing next to you when you forget." She met his eyes. "Because someone will be. And they'll see your face change, and you'll look at them like a stranger, and they'll have to be the ones who remember for you. That's the cost nobody writes down. The rite takes one memory from you. It gives everyone who loves you the burden of carrying the shape of what you lost."

Silas looked at Wren, asleep in the borrowed coat, one hand curled near the red cloth.

"I'll say goodbye," he said.

"Do you want —" Odile hesitated. "I could record it. Afterward. Write down what you tell me tonight, so there's a version of her that survives outside your head. You wouldn't feel it as yours. But the city would have it. The archive would have it. Someone, someday, would know her name."

Silas thought about that. Thought about his sister's name in a stranger's careful hand, filed among the truth of the war and the lie of the founding and the confession of the Hollow King — a girl reduced to a footnote in somebody else's liberation.

"Yeah," he said finally. "Yeah. Write her down. Get the laugh right."

"I'll try."

"You won't get it right. Nobody gets it right. But get it close."

* * *

An hour past dawn, boots sounded on the stairs above — three steps, deliberate, then a coded knock: two quick, one slow. Their signal. Their lookout, the fishmonger's ancient grandson who watched the street for coppers, coming down with news.

But the knock was wrong. Two quick, one slow, then — a fourth rap, urgent, arrhythmic. The panic-code. The one that meant *they've found you*.

Wren was awake before the echo faded, knife already in her hand. Odile swept the letters into their oilskin wrap. Silas snuffed the candle with his palm, and in the sudden dark he felt it settle over him — calm, cold, complete. The acceptance he'd been circling all night arrived all at once, the way dawn arrives: not gradually, but all together, and undeniable.

Whatever happened next, he knew what he was carrying down into the dark. He knew what it would cost. He had decided.

The cellar door burst open above them, and light spilled down the stairs — torchlight, orange and flickering, and silhouetted against it, a figure in Warden grey.

"They're not waiting for us to come to the citadel," the figure gasped — the grandson, sixteen years old, white-faced, breathless. "They're coming here. They know about the cellar. Silas — they know about *everything* —"

Odile was already moving, stuffing the rite into her coat. Wren kicked open the cellar's rear hatch, flooding the room with the smell of the canal.

"Undercroft or nothing," Wren said. "We go now, we go through the water, and we finish this tonight."

Silas touched his breast pocket once — the pocket where nothing physical lay, where the most precious thing he owned lived behind his ribs — and followed her into the dark.
