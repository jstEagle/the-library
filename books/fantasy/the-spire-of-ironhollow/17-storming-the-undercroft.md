## 17. Storming the Undercroft

The drowned foundations of the Warden citadel smelled like a grave that had been left out in the rain.

Wren crouched at the lip of the flooded stairwell, a rope in her fists and the taste of iron on her teeth, and tried not to think about how many people had come down here before them. The water below was black as spilled ink, and it moved when there was no wind to move it.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," she said.

Silas knelt beside her, checking the knots for the third time, because checking things was what he did instead of being afraid. "Because the map says so."

"The map also says 'beware the weeping door,' which is not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be reassuring. It's supposed to be true." He glanced at her, and in the grey light of Odile's lantern his face looked older than it had a month ago. Everyone's did, now. "You can still stay up top."

Wren snorted. "And miss all this?" She gestured at the dark, the water, the distant hum of the citadel above them — the great stone heart of Ironhollow beating directly over their heads. "I've been half-drained by Wardens, Silas. They took pieces of me I'll never get back unless we open that Spire. I'm not staying up top. I'm going first."

She went first. That was the arrangement they'd all agreed on without ever saying it aloud: Wren led through tight places, Odile knew the citadel's bones, Silas carried the map, and Brannock — Brannock came last, because nobody trusted what was left inside him, least of all himself.

* * *

They went down into the water up to their ribs, then their chins, holding their packs overhead while Odile's lantern painted trembling gold across stone that had been under the city since before the city had a name. Wren swam with her eyes open. She'd grown up in flooded cellars; she knew how to read the dark down here. Old stairs. Collapsed arches. A carved lintel with letters worn to ghosts.

Then the corridor opened, dry air rising from somewhere deep, and the map's first marker appeared on the wall — the same spiral-and-key sigil that had been burned into the courier's map, scratched into the drowned stone by hands long dead.

"We're under the citadel," Odile said quietly. "Under the Hall of Records. Under everything." Her voice had a strange flatness Wren had learned to recognize: the sound of a woman cataloguing her own fear so she could file it away later. "My mother walked these halls. I trained in them. And all that time—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. "All that time it was underneath us."

"All that time," Wren said, "he was sitting right on top of the thing he stole the world to hide."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, a bell began to ring.

Everyone froze. One bell, then two, then a third, each higher and faster than the last — not the fire bells of Ironhollow, not the tide bells of the harbor. Wren knew every alarm this city had. This one she'd only heard once, from the outside, the night the Wardens burned Fairweather House.

"That's the citadel waking up," Odile said. "That's the full call."

"They know," Silas breathed. "They already know we're here."

"No." Odile turned, lantern high, and her face had gone hard as an archive seal. "If they knew exactly where we were, they wouldn't ring the general alarm. They'd just come. This means something tripped a ward — maybe hours ago, maybe days. It means they're searching." She started walking, fast, toward the far end of the corridor. "Which means we stop being careful and start being quick."

Wren grinned despite everything. "Finally. I hate careful."

* * *

The map's final leg was a descent, always a descent, marked in the courier's cramped hand with distances and warnings. *The weeping door.* *The gallery of names.* *Do not touch the water where it stands still.*

They found the weeping door forty minutes later — a slab of black stone seeping groundwater in slow beads, like the wall itself was crying. Beyond it lay a vaulted gallery lined with niches, and every niche held a name.

Thousands of names. Carved in neat rows, floor to ceiling, receding into the dark further than the lantern light would reach.

Odile stopped in front of the first row and made a sound Wren had never heard her make — small, bitten-off, like a hinge breaking.

"These are the purged," she whispered. "Every mage recorded in the citadel archives. Every name struck from the rolls after the outlawing. They didn't erase them." Her fingers hovered over the carvings without touching. "They buried them. Down here. Where no one would ever—"

"Wren." Silas's voice, low and urgent. "Water. Look at the water."

At the far end of the gallery, past the last of the names, a pool stood perfectly still. Not moving. Not reflecting the lantern light properly either — it reflected the gallery, yes, but a version of it with figures standing in the niches, tall and wrong, and Wren's skin crawled so hard she nearly dropped her knife.

"Do not touch the water where it stands still," she quoted. "Yeah. Let's not."

They skirted it along the right-hand wall, close enough that Wren could see the still water shiver — just barely, just once — as she passed. Like something turning over in its sleep. She didn't look back. Looking back was how you died in stories, and Wren intended to live long enough to be an old thief who lied about all this in taverns.

Then the corridor beyond the gallery narrowed, dropped, and opened into the undercroft proper — and Wren felt it before she saw anything.

Heat. No. Warmth. A warmth coming up through the stone like sunlight through a blanket, impossible this far underground, and with it a feeling she had no honest word for. The closest she could get was *remembered*. The whole space remembered something, and the memory was leaking through the rock.

"There," Silas said hoarsely.

Ahead, past a collapsed barricade of ancient masonry, the passage forked. To the left, a stair spiraled down into that sourceless glow. To the right, a long service corridor ran toward a distant red glow — torchlight, lamplight, something moving.

Boots. Many boots. Coming closer.

"They're between us and nothing," Odile said quickly, counting by ear the way only an archivist-soldier could. "Two patrols, converging on the junction. If we take the stair now they'll cut our—"

"They'll cut our retreat," Wren finished. "So don't retreat."

Everyone looked at her.

"I'm serious." She pulled both knives. "We go down. Somebody keeps them off the stair long enough for us to get clear of the junction. Then that somebody follows. That's the whole plan. It's not complicated."

"It's a terrible plan," Silas said.

"It's my plan. I never claimed it was good."

* * *

It was Odile who stepped forward first.

Not dramatically. That was the thing Wren would remember afterward — there was nothing dramatic in it at all. Odile simply set the lantern down on the floor, adjusted it so its light fell across the junction, drew her Warden-issue short sword — the one she'd never surrendered, the one that should have gotten her killed a dozen times over — and rolled her shoulders like a woman settling in at her desk.

"The corridor narrows thirty paces in," she said. "One at a time, no more than two abreast, and they won't risk a husking blast down here — the resonance would crack the foundation and drop the Hall of Records on their master's head." A thin smile, entirely without humor. "He won't allow it. Which means it's steel work. Steel work I can do."

"You can't hold two patrols alone," Silas said.

"I held a corridor alone during the Salt Riots for six hours." She was already walking toward the junction, unhurried, straightening her collar like she was reporting for a shift. "These aren't salt rioters. But they're following orders from men who are afraid of me, and afraid men fight badly." At the edge of the light she paused, and looked back at them — at Wren, at Silas, at Brannock hunched in the shadows behind them with his grey-rimmed eyes and his shaking hands.

"Find my mother's work," Odile said. "Finish it. Write my name in your report if you survive — Odile Vasseur, archivist, second class, disgraced. Make sure someone records that I was here."

Then she walked around the corner and was gone, and a moment later the corridor lit up with the clash of steel, and Wren grabbed Silas's arm and hauled him toward the stair.

* * *

They ran down into the glow.

Behind and above them the fighting rose and fell — shouts, the ring of blades, once a scream that was cut short, once a burst of that horrible wet-crackling sound Wren had heard in the den the night the Wardens drained everyone she'd ever drunk cheap wine with. Each time the wet-crackling came, Wren's stomach clenched, because that sound meant the thing under the commander's skin, the eating thing, and Odile was alone up there with it.

"Don't listen," Silas gasped, catching her elbow as she faltered on the stairs. "Listen to me. She told us what to do. We do it. That's how we honor—"

"Don't say it."

"—that's how we honor what she's buying us. Come *on*, Wren."

The stair let out into a chamber that stole the breath from Wren's lungs.

It was round, vast, older than any stonework in the city above — and growing out of its center was the Spire. Not the mythic tower of the stories. Its root. A column of pale stone wide as a chapel, veined with light that pulsed slowly, like a heartbeat, like breath, like something sleeping very deeply and very close to waking. The warmth Wren had felt through the floors poured off it in waves, and standing in it felt like standing in summer, like standing in a kitchen where someone you loved was baking bread, like every safe place she'd ever invented in her head all at once.

Wren's fragment stirred.

It lived at the base of her skull, the piece of the dying stranger's memory the Wardens had never managed to pry loose — a scrap of song, a scrap of light, a woman's voice saying words she couldn't quite hear. Now it woke and pressed against the inside of her mind like a hand against a window.

*Here,* it said. *Here, here, here.*

"She's real," Wren whispered. Her eyes were burning. "Silas. She's actually real. The Spire. It's been down here the whole—"

A sound from the chamber entrance behind them.

Brannock.

He stood at the top of the stair, swaying, and even from twenty feet away Wren could see what was wrong. His eyes were almost fully grey now. The residue of whatever he'd once been — the fence, the liar, the man who owed Silas money and paid it back in bad jokes — was draining out of him visibly, like ink through paper. The Wardens hadn't finished him when they took him. Something else was finishing the job now, some hunger riding along in his hollowed-out skull, drawn home to the power it had been bred to guard against.

"*Run,*" Brannock rasped. His voice was two voices. "They broke me open. It's still in me. It wants— it wants to be near it, wants to drink the root, if it reaches the Spire—"

"Brannock." Silas started toward him.

Brannock raised a hand, and the gesture was so much like his old self — that lazy, dismissive wave he used to end arguments about debts — that Wren's throat closed.

"No. Listen. Last job." The grey eyes flickered, and for one heartbeat the old cunning surfaced in them, sharp and warm. "Fairweather. You were right. About the ledger. About everything. I always meant to square us up." He smiled, and it was terrible and human at once. "Consider us square."

Then he turned around and went back up the stairs, and a few seconds later Wren heard him shouting — shouting in a voice that wasn't entirely his anymore, drawing them, calling the patrol down the other corridor, *this way, this way, the thieves are here* — leading them away from the Spire's chamber, deeper into the undercroft, anywhere but here.

The sounds that followed were not steel work. There was no steel in them at all.

Wren stood with her fists clenched and her knives useless and listened to a half-husked fence die loudly enough to buy three strangers a few more minutes, and she understood, with a clarity that hurt worse than the draining had, that this was what the crew was now. This was what they'd become to each other. People who went up the stairs so the others could go down.

"Come on," she said roughly, wiping her face with the back of her wrist. "Nobody dies twice today. We finish this."

* * *

The Spire's root chamber had a door.

Of course it did. Everything in Ironhollow had a lock, and this one was the grandest Wren had ever seen: a double leaf of pale stone, seamless, carved over every inch with the spiral-and-key, and no handle, no hinges, no seam of light. Just the pulse behind it, faster now, the way a heart beats faster when it hears footsteps.

Silas put his palm flat against it and flinched like he'd touched a stove.

"What? What is it?"

"It knows the map." His voice shook. "Wren — it's reading the map through my hand. It's reading *me*." He pulled back, staring at his own fingers. "There's a rite. The letters — Odile's mother's letters — the unbinding needs—"

The alarms changed tone. The general ringing cut off, and in its place came a single deep tolling, slow and deliberate, from somewhere far above. Once. Twice. Three times.

Odile had described it once, late at night, in the flat voice she used for terrible facts: *the master's summons. When it rings, every Warden in the citadel stops being a person and becomes an instrument.*

"He knows," Wren said. "He finally knows exactly where we are."

"Yes." Silas pressed his forehead against the cool stone of the door, and Wren watched him gather himself the way she'd watched him do it a hundred times over these weeks — the little thief, the memory-bottler, the man built entirely out of careful exits — deciding, this time, not to leave.

"And there's a cost," he said quietly. "To open it. There's always a cost. I think I've known since the ferryman." He turned to look at her, and his eyes were wet and steady. "When we open this door, Wren, I need you to remember something for me. Whatever happens. Whoever I am after."

The tolling above grew louder. Boots in the corridors, dozens of them, converging. Behind them, faintly, the wet-crackling sound of the hungry thing, coming home.

"Tell me," Wren said.

But Silas was already reaching for the door, and the Spire's light was rising through the floor like dawn through water, and whatever he meant to say was swallowed by the sound of the undercroft doors bursting open behind them.
