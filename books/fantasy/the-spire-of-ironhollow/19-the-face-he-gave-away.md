## 19. The Face He Gave Away

The Spire's heart was a room the size of a chapel and shaped like a held breath.

Silas stood at its center on a floor of black glass that showed his reflection not as he was but as he had been — a boy of nine years, standing in a doorway of a house that no longer existed, waiting for someone to come home. Around him rose walls of woven light, threads of gold and green and blue running through the dark like roots grown wrong-side-up from some impossible tree. The air tasted the way memory tasted when you pulled it out of a bottle too fast: sweet, then sharp, then gone.

Odile stood at the first point of the triangle they'd chalked on the glass, the letters from the drowned cache spread open in her hands, her mother's handwriting glowing faintly where Wren's blood had been mixed into the ink to wake it. Wren stood at the second point, her half-drained eyes burning brighter than they had any right to. Silas was the third.

"The rite doesn't want power," Odile said. Her voice was steady, archivist-steady, but Silas could see the tremor in her fingers where they held the pages. "It wants weight. Something given freely that costs everything to give. That's what my mother wrote. *A lock built from theft can only be opened by a gift.*"

"And if we're wrong?" Wren asked.

"Then the Hollow King kills us slowly instead of quickly," Silas said. "Same as every other plan we've had."

He meant it as a joke. It didn't land. Nothing landed anymore; there was a hollowness in him that had opened somewhere between the ferryman's gate and the citadel's drowned foundations, and it was growing, patient as water finding a crack.

Because he already knew what the rite wanted.

He'd known since the smuggler's cellar, since Odile read the requirement aloud — *a freely given memory of absolute love* — and all three of them had looked anywhere except at each other. There was only one memory in Silas Fairweather's keeping that qualified. There had only ever been one.

Lira.

Her name surfaced in him like a body coming up from deep water, and he let it.

"You don't have to," Wren said quietly.

"Yes, I do." He rolled his shoulders back. His hands were shaking, so he put them in his coat pockets, the way he did when he was stealing, the way he did when he was lying, the way he did when there was nothing left between himself and the truth except the shape of his own cowardice. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Freely given. Nobody holding a knife to me. Just—" he laughed, a small broken sound, "—just a man deciding."

Odile began to speak the opening words. The letters' script lifted off the page in slow golden threads, winding around the three of them like smoke with intent. The floor of black glass began to warm beneath Silas's boots.

And the memory came forward to meet him.

*

She was eight years old and she was laughing.

Not the polite laugh she used for their mother, not the careful laugh she'd learned after their father left, but the real one — the one that started in her belly and broke upward into something wild, her whole face involved, her whole self. She was laughing because Silas had fallen off the kitchen chair trying to reach the honey jar, and he was sitting on the floor covered in flour, and she was laughing so hard she couldn't breathe, and he was laughing because she was laughing, and the kitchen smelled of bread and rain through an open window and—

*Lira.*

That was her name. Lira Fairweather. Eight years old. Gap in her teeth where the new ones were coming in. Hair the color of wet chestnuts, always escaping its braid. A scar on her chin from falling out of the plum tree two summers past, which she wore like a medal.

He had carried this memory for twenty-two years. He had never bottled it, never sold it, never once risked it in a trade, even in the worst winters, even the year he ate candle wax to fill his stomach. Other memories he'd spent like coin. This one he kept the way a drowning man keeps air.

And now he understood, with terrible clarity, that keeping it had been a kind of theft too. Not from Lira — she was dead, dead eleven years now, fever, the quarantine houses, the mass graves outside the city wall that no one was allowed to mourn at. He hadn't stolen it from her. He'd stolen it from the world. From whatever came next. He'd locked a piece of love in a lead-lined box inside his skull and sat on it while magic died in chains beneath his feet, and he'd told himself it was grief, and grief it was, but it was also greed, and the Hollow King would have recognized it instantly. The King hoarded power. Silas hoarded her.

The same sin. Different scale.

"Silas." Odile's voice, far away. "You have to speak it. All of it. Out loud. The rite needs it spoken — a memory given voice is a memory given away. That's the mechanism. She wrote it three times in the margins. She underlined it."

"I heard you."

He closed his eyes.

The golden threads tightened around him, gentle as hands.

*

"Lira," he said.

His voice cracked on it. Twenty-two years of saying nothing about her, of deflecting, of changing the subject, of letting the city's silence around the plague dead become his own silence, and now the name came out of him like blood from a reopened wound.

"Lira Fairweather. My sister. She was eight years old the last time I made her laugh like that."

The threads drank the words. He felt them leave — actually felt it, a coolness moving through him, a tide going out.

"She laughed from her belly. When she really laughed, her whole face changed. Everything changed. Rooms got warmer. I'm not — I know how that sounds. But rooms got warmer."

He was crying. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather. He didn't stop speaking.

"She had a gap in her teeth. New ones coming in. She stuck her tongue through it when she was thinking. She had a scar on her chin from the plum tree on Corver Street — she climbed it even though Mother said not to, and when she fell she didn't cry until she saw the fruit squashed in her hand, and then she cried about the plums, not the cut, and I have loved her for that ever since, for grieving the plums—"

His knees went soft. The black glass was hot now, glowing faintly underfoot, and the walls of woven light had begun to turn, slowly, a vast wheel of color gathering speed.

"She fell asleep against my shoulder during the long sermons and I pretended to be annoyed and I wasn't. I was never once annoyed. I would have sat in that pew until my bones turned to dust if it meant she kept sleeping there, safe, breathing, her weight against my arm."

Cooler. Colder. The memory was leaving him in layers, and each layer took something with it — not just the image but the warmth underneath the image, the reason the image mattered. He could feel the shape of her still, but the shape was becoming hollow, a mold, a thing defined only by its emptiness.

"No," he said, and kept talking anyway. "I mean yes. Take it. Take all of it."

Wren was crying too. He could hear her, a small rough sound, and he thought: *she knows. She bought a memory from me six years ago and she has known ever since what I was carrying.* He couldn't look at her. If he looked at her he might beg her to make him stop.

"Her hair was the color of wet chestnuts. She braided it crooked. She hummed when she worked — badly, tunelessly, constantly — and our mother said she sounded like a kettle with ambitions, and Lira laughed, that laugh, the belly-laugh, and the kitchen got warmer—"

The last of it was going. He grabbed for it, instinct, twenty-two years of reflex, his fingers closing around the final piece —

her face

— and he held it for one heartbeat, two, looking at it the way a man looks at a lit window from a cold street.

Then he opened his hand.

"Lira," he said, and the word came out clean, without grief attached, because the grief was leaving with it, everything was leaving with it, "this is your face. This is your face. I'm giving it back. I'm sorry I kept it so long."

*

The Spire opened.

There was no thunder. That was the strangest part. After the alarms and the drowning corridors and Brannock dying in the dark to buy them seconds, after all of it, the actual moment was silent — a sound like a great inhale taken everywhere at once, and then the walls of woven light unfurled like a flower deciding, after a thousand years, to bloom.

Magic poured out.

Silas felt it pass through him — through everyone, through the whole buried spine of Ironhollow — a wave of heat and color and *recognition*, as if the city itself had been holding its breath for a century and finally exhaled. The threads of the rite dissolved into it. The letters in Odile's hands flared white and crumbled into ash that drifted upward, against gravity, toward the light.

He stayed on his knees on the cooling glass.

He tried to remember why he'd come here.

That was the thing no one had warned him about — not Odile with her archives, not Wren with her hard-won fragments, none of them. They'd talked about the cost like it was the memory itself, the loss of one bright room in the house of his mind. But a memory isn't a room. It's a load-bearing wall.

He reached for Lira and found a fact: *I had a sister. She died.* Nothing else. No face. No laugh. No scar, no plums, no warm kitchen, no name — even the name was gone, just a blank space in the sentence where a name should be, and the blank space hurt worse than anything, hurt like a tooth pulled wrong, hurt like—

Like the sea, he thought, and didn't know why he thought it.

"Silas." Wren was crouching beside him. Her hand hovered near his shoulder without landing. "Hey. Hey. Look at me."

He looked. Her half-drained grey eye and her living brown one, both full of tears.

"What did I lose?" he asked. His voice sounded old to him. "Tell me what I lost. You knew her. Tell me—"

"I only met her once," Wren whispered. "When I bought the charm. But I'll tell you everything I remember. Every day, if you want. I swear it."

Above them, the Spire's light was still rising, flooding up through a thousand years of sealed stone, and far above — Silas could feel it even kneeling, empty-handed, hollowed-out — something ancient was stirring in the dark above, in the throne room of the thing that had imprisoned all of this, something that had just felt its hoard begin to slip its chains.

The Hollow King knew.

And he was coming down.

* * *

They climbed.

The way back up through the citadel's foundations was chaos — the drowned corridors draining as the freed magic moved through them, water running backward, stone groaning, alarms that had been silent for centuries waking one by one into shrieking chorus. Wren led, because Wren always led when the way was bad. Odile followed with her knife drawn and her mother's ashes still dusting her sleeves. And Silas climbed because Wren pulled him, because Odile pushed him, because his body remembered how to survive even when the rest of him had forgotten why.

"Why is he coming down here?" Odile shouted over the noise. "The Spire's already open. The magic's free. What does he think he can still take?"

"He doesn't want to take it back," Wren called over her shoulder. "Think about it — he can't. The rite burned the lock. He wants to take the people who opened it. He wants—"

"Witnesses," Silas said.

They stopped. Even the corridor seemed to pause, water shivering around their ankles.

"That's what he's always done," Silas heard himself say, and part of him marveled that the knowledge was still there, floating loose now, unanchored to any reason. "You don't erase a war. You imprison it. You keep the prisoners alive so the prison stays justified. He needs us alive. We're the proof that someone defied him. Without us, the story ends and he has to ask what the story was *for*."

Odile stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"I don't know," Silas said honestly. "But I know it."

The stairwell ahead ended at a door of black iron, and beyond the door, faint through the metal, came a sound that stopped Wren's hand on the latch.

Singing.

A single voice, low and unhurried, singing a song about a king who loved his kingdom so much he locked it in a box so nothing could ever take it from him — not the seasons, not the years, not the people themselves. A lullaby. A nursery rhyme every child in Ironhollow knew without knowing where they'd learned it.

The Hollow King was singing his own myth to himself as he walked down to meet them, and the door's iron was frosting over, and beneath the frost, light was bleeding through in the shape of a keyhole.

Wren looked back at Silas. Odile's grip tightened on her knife.

The frost on the door began to melt — from the other side.
