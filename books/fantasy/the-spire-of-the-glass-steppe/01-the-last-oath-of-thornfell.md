## 1. The Last Oath of Thornfell

The guild hall had never been quiet before. Paloma Hartwell knew that the way you know a tooth has gone bad — by the absence of its ache.

For nineteen years, Thornfell Hall had roared. Boots on the flagstones, maps unrolled and argued over, the great hearth spitting sparks while a dozen voices tried to sing the Wayfarers' Crossing Song in four different keys at once. Now there was only the wind worrying the shutters, and the wet rasp of Master Odell breathing two rooms away, and Paloma's own footsteps, which she took care to make loud, because silence was a thing that grew teeth if you fed it.

She carried the evening meal on a tray: barley soup gone lukewarm because she'd stood too long at the window watching the storm come down off the Gray Teeth, and half a loaf of the black rye bread that no one baked properly anymore. The hall stretched out around her like the ribcage of something enormous and dead. Forty cots along the east wall, each one empty, their blankets folded with guild precision — *because we might be needed*, the folding said, though nobody had slept in them since the winter the toll-keepers came for the ledgers. The map racks stood bare. The oath-board above the hearth held forty-one names burned into cedar, and thirty-nine of them were crossed through with the traditional single line: *gone ahead on the road*.

Nobody wrote *dead*. Wayfarers didn't. You just drew the line and let the road have them.

Two names remained uncrossed. Odell's. And hers.

She paused at the threshold of his room, the way she always did, giving him time to pretend he wasn't in pain. He never took it. That was Odell — he'd sooner swallow glass than grant anyone the mercy of pretending.

"Soup's cold," he said before she'd even set the tray down. His voice was a dry leaf dragged over stone. "You're getting slow, apprentice."

"You're getting dead, master." She said it lightly, the way they'd agreed to say it, back when they'd made the joke together and it was still funny. "Eat anyway."

He ate. Three spoonfuls, then he waved her off with a hand that had once drawn the famous Vane Pass route freehand, from memory, in a snowstorm, and now couldn't hold a spoon without tremors. The coughing fit that followed bent him double. Paloma held his shoulders and counted the seconds until it passed, the way she'd been taught, and hated how practiced she'd become at counting.

When it was over, he gripped her wrist. His fingers felt like kindling.

"Sit," he said. "I want to say a thing, and I'll only have the strength to say it once."

"Master—"

"*Sit*, girl."

She sat on the edge of his cot. The storm pressed against the shutters like a beggar asking to be let in.

"The Guild of Wayfarers," Odell began, formal, in the cadence of the old oaths, "was founded on a single truth: that no soul should walk an unknown road alone. Not roads of stone. Roads of grief. Roads of debt. Roads that don't appear on any map because the person walking them hasn't drawn them yet." He coughed, wetly. "Forty-one of us swore to that. Thirty-nine are ahead on the road now. Which means the whole weight of the thing sits on two sets of shoulders, and one of those sets is failing."

"Then I'll carry it," Paloma said. "You taught me how."

"That's not what I'm saying." His grip tightened. "Listen to me, because I've watched you these last months and I know what you're doing. You're keeping the hall swept. You're oiling the hinges. You're mending cots nobody sleeps in. You think if you keep everything perfect, the guild stays alive. But a guild isn't walls, Paloma. A guild is an *oath between living people*. And when the last oath goes quiet—" he tapped his own chest, twice, where his heart labored—"the guild dies. Not before. Not after. With me."

"I won't let it."

"You can't stop it. And here's the part that will make you angry." He pulled her closer, and his breath smelled of fever and dried yarrow. "I don't want you to. I release you, do you understand? When I go, the oath releases. Walk out of this hall a free woman. Marry a baker. Grow turnips. Forget every pass and ford I ever carved into your skull."

Paloma laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You spent nineteen years filling my head with passes and fords. You think it pours back out?"

"I think," Odell said, and now his eyes were bright and terrible, "that you've mistaken loneliness for loyalty. They wear the same coat, girl, but only one of them keeps you warm."

She didn't answer, because the shutters exploded inward.

Not all of them — just the small one over the washbasin, torn off its leather hinges as the storm drove something through it: a shape, a man, blood and rain and desperation, crashing across the floorboards in a spray of glass and water. Paloma was on her feet with a knife in her hand before her mind caught up — nineteen years of guild training arriving faster than thought — and then she saw what the shape actually was.

A stranger. Younger than her, maybe thirty, dressed in fine clothes ruined past recognition. Silk shirt soaked black with rain and something else. One eye swollen shut. Blood sheeted down his temple from a gash in his hairline, and his lips were torn as if someone had beaten him around the mouth. Behind him, through the broken shutter, the storm howled down from the Gray Teeth like a pack of things released.

"Bar the door," the man gasped. "They're coming. Bar the door."

"There's nothing to take here," Paloma said, knife steady. "We're a guild hall. We're *empty*."

"Not taking." He crawled toward her, leaving red handprints on the clean boards she'd scrubbed that morning, and something in her chest twisted at the sight of it. "Giving. Please. You're a wayfarer — I saw the sign, the boot-and-road, on your post. Please. Thirteen turns." He fumbled inside his ruined coat and brought out a fold of paper, pressed flat and dark with damp, and thrust it up at her with both hands like a man offering his heart on a plate. "Take it south. To the Glass Steppe. Before the thirteenth turn of the moon. Swear it. *Swear it.*"

Behind her, from the cot, Master Odell made a sound. It took Paloma a moment to recognize it, because she had never heard it before in nineteen years.

Her master was laughing.

"Look at that," Odell wheezed. "Storm sends me a client on my deathbed. The road has a sense of humor after all."

"Master, he needs a physician, not—"

"He needs a wayfarer." Odell pushed himself up against the wall, gray-faced, furious with his own weakness. "And you're the last one. Take the map, Paloma."

The man swayed. Paloma caught him under the arms — he weighed nothing, or felt like it, the way falling things feel light — and lowered him to the floor beside the hearth, where the coals still glowed. Up close she could see the rest of him: the bruising along his ribs, the way his breathing hitched wrong on the left side, the strange waxy pallor beneath the blood. He looked like a man who had already been dying for some time and had simply run out of the strength to hide it.

His good eye found hers. It was a brown eye, warm even now, even terrified, and it held hers with a ferocity that had nothing to do with fear.

"Thirteen turns," he whispered. "My name is—" A cough tore through him, bending him nearly double, and when it passed, whatever name he'd meant to give was gone, spat out somewhere in the blood on the floor. He pressed the folded map into her hands instead, folding her fingers closed over it with both of his. His hands were freezing. "Doesn't matter. The map matters. Thirteen turns. Don't let them finish it."

"Don't let *who* finish *what*?" Paloma demanded.

But his eye had rolled back, and he slid sideways, and caught himself only because she was already holding him. Outside, the storm screamed. Somewhere far off, down the smuggler's road, she thought she heard hooves — many hooves, riding hard through weather that would kill anything with less reason than hatred.

Odell had stopped laughing. He was looking at the map in her hands with an expression Paloma had seen exactly once before, on the face of a dying guide they'd carried down out of the Vane Pass twenty years ago, who had looked into the valley below and refused, to his final breath, to describe what he'd seen.

"Open it," Odell said quietly.

Paloma unfolded the paper. The paper was warm. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the ink — deep red-black, glistening faintly, as if it hadn't finished drying, as if it were still deciding what to be. Lines crawled across it: mountain ranges, a river delta, and then, in the lower third, a vast blank space shaped like a held breath, waiting to be filled.

Waiting to be filled by thirteen turns of moonlight, she understood suddenly, and did not know how she understood.

"Master," she said. Her voice sounded very young. "What have I just sworn to?"

Odell sank back against the wall, exhausted, and looked at his last apprentice — the last wayfarer, standing in a hall full of folded blankets and crossed-out names, holding a living map while a dying stranger bled on her clean floor and hoofbeats came up out of the dark.

"You haven't sworn yet," he said. "That's the mercy of it. Decide fast, girl. Because the moment you do—"

The hooves reached the gate.

* * *

Paloma blew out the lamp.

In the sudden dark, lit only by hearth-glow, she knelt beside the stranger and put the map into his ruined coat herself, close against his heart, where the warmth was thinnest. Then she rose, crossed the hall, and slid the great bar into place on the door — the first oath the guild had heard spoken aloud in years, and it wasn't words at all, just wood settling into iron brackets, just a woman choosing, alone, to stand between a door and whatever was coming through it.

From the cot behind her, very softly, Master Odell began to hum the Crossing Song.

It was the loneliest sound Paloma had ever heard, and she found she was humming it too.
