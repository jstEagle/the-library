## 2. A Map Drawn in Dying Ink

The stranger lived through the night, which Paloma had not expected, and did not thank her for it either, judging by the sounds he made.

She'd dragged him into the guildhall's old infirmary—a room that hadn't held a patient in nine years—and done what her masters had taught her before the teaching stopped. Clean wounds. Bind what bleeds. Warmth, water, watchfulness. The rest was up to whatever stubborn thing kept his heart going.

By gray morning he was sleeping instead of dying, which she counted as progress. She sat across from him on a stool with a cracked seat and studied him the way she'd study a road: looking for the parts that didn't fit.

He didn't fit anywhere. His coat was good wool gone to ruin, cut in a southern style she'd only seen on merchants who could afford to be robbed twice. His hands were soft except for one callus, high on the right palm—the kind you got from holding a ledger pen, not a sword. And under his jaw, half-hidden by three days of beard, a scar shaped like a crescent moon.

Bloodied, storm-wrecked, half-dead—and carrying a map worth killing for. Toll-keepers didn't chase men through mountain passes for nothing.

On the table beside him, the folded map sat where he'd pressed it into her hands. It hadn't moved all night. Paloma had checked eleven times anyway.

* * *

It moved at dusk.

She was stirring barley into a pot when she heard it: a sound like a moth against paper, soft and frantic. She turned. The map lay open now—she was certain she'd left it folded—and its surface *shivered*.

Paloma crossed the room slowly, the way you approach a snake or a debtor. The map was redrawing itself. Lines she could swear had shown a river were fading to pale ghosts while new ink bled up through the vellum like bruising: a different river, a different bend, a small dark mark where no mark had been. The roads shifted as she watched, patient as growing things.

The ink wasn't drying. That was the wrongness of it, she realized. Ink dries. This glistened, faintly wet at every line's edge, and when she tilted the map toward the firelight the lines seemed to pull *toward* her eyes, hungry for attention.

"Alive," she said aloud. "You're made of something alive."

"Made of me."

The voice came out cracked as old pottery. The stranger had woken. He was watching her over the edge of the blanket, gray-faced, eyes too bright with fever.

"It's drawn in my blood," he said. "Or what's left of it. Every night it rewrites itself because I'm—" He stopped, swallowed. "Because I'm running out. The map knows how much is left better than I do. When there's none of me remaining, the last line draws itself. That's the thirteenth turn of the moon."

Paloma set down the ladle. "Thirteen turns. You said that. Before you fell on my floor and ruined my night."

"I'm sorry about your floor." A ghost of humor, quickly drowned. "My name is Ivo Serrano. And I need to hire you, Wayfarer. Whatever's left of the guild. I'll pay everything I have."

"Everything you have is bleeding onto my vellum."

"Then I'll pay in truth," he said, "which costs more."

She should have refused. Every lesson her dead masters ever gave her said the same thing: a contract written in dying ink binds the living. But Paloma Hartwell had been alone in this hall for nine years with four ghosts and a ledger of unfulfilled oaths, and when Ivo Serrano looked at her with those fever-bright eyes, she felt something crack open that she'd spent a long time sealing shut.

So instead of refusing, she pulled up the stool and said, "Start talking. Truth first. Lies after, if you must—but I'll know them. It's part of the trade."

* * *

He talked until the fire burned low, in fragments, like a man handing her pieces of a broken bowl and apologizing for the missing ones.

His brother's name was Teodor. Two years ago, Teodor had fallen ill with a wasting sickness no physician in Serrano's home city could name—lungs filling slow as a well filling with sand. And Ivo, a clerk with soft hands and a pen-callus, had gone looking for miracles the way desperate men do: downward.

"There are courts beneath the mountains," Ivo said. "Everyone knows the stories. Nobody believes them until they need to." He laughed, and it hurt him; Paloma saw him decide not to laugh again. "I found the fault below the Karst Spire. I walked down. There's a court there, lit by lanterns, and the lanterns are—" His hands shook. "They're souls. Bought souls. Hanging there, burning."

"The Mountain Below buys souls," Paloma said flatly. "And you bought your brother's life."

"I traded mine for his." He said it simply, the way you'd report a sum in a ledger. "The Pale Steward asked what I'd give of equal weight. I thought—I was so sure—a life was a life. One for one. Fair trade." His eyes went to the fire. "Teodor woke up the next morning breathing easy. And I walked home lighter than I've ever been, if you understand me. Lighter every day since."

Paloma understood. She'd seen soul-hollowed men once, in her traveling years—smiling, agreeable, forgetting the names of their own children mid-sentence. She'd assumed they were mad.

"Why change your mind?" she asked. "If it's done, it's done. You saved him."

Ivo was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Because I found out what the Steward does with the surplus," he said finally. "A soul isn't just fuel for a bargain. It remembers. It keeps singing, down there in the glass, even hanging from its hook. And the Steward—" His jaw worked. "The Steward lets the court *listen*. My brother recovered, whole and grateful. And every night since, some part of me has been burning in a wall beneath a mountain while the thing wearing my face up here forgets my mother's birthday."

He looked at her then, and it wasn't the look of a man begging. It was the look of a man presenting terms.

"Thirteen turns of the moon," he said. "When the moon completes its cycle, the contract seals. My soul stops being mine-in-abeyance and becomes theirs forever. But until then, the door isn't locked. Only guarded. If someone carries me—or what's left of me—to the court before the seal, the trade can be unmade. The Steward honors its ledgers. It has to. It's the only law it has."

"And you want a Wayfarer to carry you there."

"I want the last Wayfarer to guide me there. Through passes the toll-keepers don't watch. Past roads that serve the mountain." He gestured weakly at the map, which had redrawn itself again while he spoke—she could see new lines now, a route south, threading between marked dangers like water finding cracks in stone. "This is my blood, Paloma Hartwell. It knows where I have to go. It's been trying to get there since the night I sold myself."

She stared at the shifting lines. Somewhere south, past the smugglers' roads she knew better than anyone alive, the Glass Steppe waited—mirror-flat, soul-plain, hiding the court of the Mountain Below beneath it.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "Toll-keepers will come for that map. They came for you already—they nearly finished the job. And if they catch a Wayfarer guiding stolen goods toward the court, they won't kill us. They'll take us below and let us hang there a while first."

"Yes."

"You're dying anyway. The trip might finish you faster."

"Yes."

"And if we fail—if the moon turns thirteen times before we arrive—"

"Then bury the map somewhere the Steward can't hear it singing, and go home," he said. "That's the whole contract. Guide me south. Everything else is weather."

Paloma stood. Walked to the window. Outside, the storm had passed and the sky was rinsed clean, and there hung the moon—fat, silver, one turn of thirteen already behind them.

Nine years alone in this hall. Nine years keeping oaths for mentors who were ash and gravestones, guarding a guild that was a name and an empty chair. Her masters' last lesson rang in her skull like a bell: *the guild dies when its last oath does.* She had thought that meant she should keep the oaths safe, keep them polished, keep them in a box until she joined the ghosts.

But maybe an oath wasn't something you kept. Maybe it was something you *spent*.

She turned back to the bed. Ivo Serrano watched her with the patience of a man who had already priced the odds.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When this is done—when your soul is back where it belongs—you carry the guild's name with you. You tell people a Wayfarer got you there. Not the empty hall. Not the graves. *Me.* The guild doesn't die quiet."

Something moved across his ruined face that might have been a smile. "Agreed."

"Then shake on it, Serrano. Blood contracts make me nervous."

His hand was fever-hot and light as a bird's bone. They shook, and Paloma felt the strange double-weight of it: the grip of a dying man, and beneath it, faint as a second pulse, something answering from far away and far below. Something that had felt the touch, too.

She packed through the night. Rope, rations, her master's brass compass, the good knife. At dawn she opened the door to find the map lying by the threshold—folded, neat, waiting—with a single new line drawn down its southern edge in fresh, glistening ink:

*A road not on any other map. Trust it. — I.*

Below the words, the ink was still moving.
