## 3. The Smuggler's Road South

The pass behind Thornfell had no name, because named things got taxed.

Paloma led them up through the scree before dawn, when the toll-keepers' lanterns still burned low in the valley below — green flames, wrong-colored, the kind the mountain court favored. She moved the way her master had taught her: three steps, listen, three steps, look back. Behind her, Ivo Serrano rode a borrowed mule with his hands tied loosely to the saddle horn so he wouldn't slide off, and he apologized every time he swayed.

"You don't have to keep saying sorry," she said.

"I know." A pause. "Sorry."

The mule was named Turnip and belonged to a farmer who would be compensated or not, depending on how the guild's accounts stood at the end of things. Paloma tried not to think about the guild's accounts. The guild was a hall with one lamp lit and one apprentice walking out of it into the snow, carrying a contract that weighed more than any cargo she'd ever hauled. Everything else was arithmetic she wasn't ready to do.

They climbed until the valley lights vanished, then climbed some more, through a notch in the ridge that looked like solid rock from below but opened like a throat if you pressed your shoulder against the right stone. Only Wayfarers knew it. Only Wayfarers, now, meant her.

"Where does it come out?" Ivo asked. His voice had gone thin since Thornfell, like paper held up to a window.

"The Weepstone Valley. Two days ahead of anyone following us." She checked his hands. His fingers were cold in a way that cold fingers shouldn't be — not numb, exactly. Absent. "Can you feel these?"

"They're there," he said, after too long a pause. "I think they're there."

* * *

That night, fever took him properly.

She built a fire small enough to hide under a rock overhang and sat with his head in her lap while the fever talked. He talked too, between shivers — fragments, half-addressed to people who weren't there.

"Milo," he said once, clear as a bell. "Milo, don't sign it. Don't—" And then something in the dream turned, and his face went slack and grateful, and he whispered, "You did it. You bought the favor. God help us both, you did it."

Paloma smoothed his hair back from his forehead without deciding to. It was just what you did for clients. Her master had done it for her, the winter she had the marsh-fever, murmuring *you're safe, you're found, the road holds you.* The words came up out of her now, unbidden, in the old cadence, and she hated herself a little for how much she wanted someone to say them to *her.*

In the morning Ivo remembered none of it, except that he woke crying and pretended he wasn't.

"I heard my brother," he said anyway, later, staring into the dead fire. "In the dream. He was singing. Milo always sang when he'd done something terrible. Said it kept the guilt from settling, like shaking flour through a sieve." He flexed his absent-feeling fingers. "He sold me, Mistress Hartwell. Not my body. My song. The part of me that would have forgiven him someday. He sold it to the court beneath the mountain, and they gave him rain for his dying fields, and I've been walking north ever since trying to figure out how to want it back."

"Why north? They're south. The mountain's south."

"Because I needed to find someone who could get me back down alive," Ivo said. "And the last Wayfarer hall was north."

Paloma said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't an apology, and the guild didn't apologize anymore. The guild was a lamp and a girl.

* * *

The Weepstone Valley earned its name honestly — water bleeding out of the cliffs in a hundred thin threads, freezing overnight into curtains of glass. Beautiful country. Dead country. Nothing grew there, and nothing followed them in, which was the point.

On the second day they picked up a tail anyway.

Not a person. A sound. Faint, rhythmic, coming up the valley behind them: the click of a counting-frame. Toll-keepers didn't walk; they reckoned. Somewhere back down the trail, something was adding them up.

Paloma cut off the trail onto a goat path so narrow Ivo had to be half-carried along it, and the clicking faded by evening. But when she checked the map that night — the map, folded eight times, warm as a living thing — a new line had drawn itself across the Weepstone Valley in ink that glistened wet. It marked where they'd slept. It marked where the clicking had stopped.

"It knows where we are," she said.

"It's mine," Ivo said quietly. "Or part of me is. The court wrote the original. Every night I get a little closer to them, and the map gets a little more honest about whose side its on."

"Then we move faster."

"Faster kills me." He said it without drama, the way you'd report weather. "I've worked that out. The closer we get to the mountain, the less of me comes along. My body's starting to forget things. Yesterday I forgot how to swallow for almost a minute. Today I couldn't remember my mother's face — I remember that I *had* one, but it's just... a shape where a face should be." He looked up at her, and his eyes were still his own, still stubborn, still afraid. "I'm spending myself getting there. So whatever trick you're planning at the end of this road, plan it fast."

Paloma fed the fire another stick and thought about her oath. *Guide whole, deliver whole, abandon never.* Her masters had died owing that promise to empty air. She would not.

"Then we don't go faster," she said. "We go *elsewhere*. There's a route south of here nobody uses because it doesn't exist. I'm going to make us exist somewhere else entirely."

"That sounds like smuggler nonsense."

"It's the best kind."

* * *

For six days they traveled through places that weren't on any map but hers — a dry riverbed that ran beneath a ridge instead of over it, a forest where the trees grew in rows because someone had planted them two hundred years ago and everyone had forgotten why. Ivo worsened by degrees. He stopped feeling hunger. Then he stopped feeling the cold, which frightened her worse than the hunger, because the cold was everywhere and if it couldn't find him anymore—

One evening she caught him sitting very still with his hand flat against his own chest.

"What are you doing?"

"Checking," he said. "Making sure it's still going." He smiled at her, apologetic even now. "It's slower than it was. Like a clock winding down. I can hear it better at night. Did you know you can hear your own heart if you're quiet enough? Mine sounds like footsteps leaving a room."

"Don't talk like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you've already read the last page."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "My brother used to say I was a man who loved things too much to keep them. Maybe that's all this is. Me loving being alive too much to let go of it gracefully." He pulled his coat tighter. "But I'd rather be ridiculous than finished, Mistress Hartwell. If that's all right with you."

"It's not," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, and turned away to check the mule's hooves for no reason at all.

* * *

They crossed out of the nameless country on the ninth day, and the world changed.

The land flattened. Grass came up silver-gray, stiff as wire, stretching to a horizon so straight it looked ruled. And far south, floating above the plain like something drowned in sky, stood the mountain — pale, glassy-sided, catching the moonlight even in daylight.

The Glass Steppe.

Ivo slid off the mule without help, which was new, and stood looking at it. The wind moved his hair. His face had gone gaunt and translucent, and Paloma could see the pulse in his throat beating slow, slow, slow.

"There it is," he said. "The thing that owns me."

"Not yet."

"Almost." He turned to her, and he was smiling, and it was the worst thing she'd seen since the hall went dark. "Thank you for the nine days, Paloma. Whatever happens—"

"Stop." She grabbed his sleeve. Under her fingers his arm felt like packed snow. "Nobody thanks anybody yet. We're not done. And we're picking up two more travelers whether you like it or not, because I can't carry you down into that mountain alone, and there's a village at the steppe's edge where people know things. Come on. One more road."

Behind them, faint across the silver grass, carried the unmistakable sound of a counting-frame clicking — much closer than it had been in the Weepstone Valley.

And ahead, at the edge of hearing, threaded through the wind itself, someone was playing a fiddle.
