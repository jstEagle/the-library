## 4. The Musician Who Fell

The trouble with falling, Sera Voss had learned, was that you kept falling long after you hit the ground.

She had landed three years ago in the last honest tavern at the edge of the steppe — a crooked timber place called the Drowned Bell, where the beer was sour and the patrons were worse and nobody asked why a woman with court-trained hands poured drinks like she hated them. Three years of pouring. Three years of keeping her fingers stupid on purpose, so they wouldn't remember the shapes of the scales the Mountain Below had taught her.

Then, on the ninth night of the waning moon, the door banged open and the past walked in wearing a smuggler's coat.

Sera saw them before they saw her: a woman with wind-scoured cheeks and a guild badge worn smooth as river glass, carrying half a stranger's weight on her shoulder; and the stranger himself, gray as ash, walking only because the woman refused to let him do otherwise. Behind them trotted a mule with the smug expression of an animal that has never once been grateful.

The woman scanned the room the way Sera remembered soldiers scanning a battlefield — corners first, exits second, faces third. Her gaze snagged on Sera's hands behind the bar, and something flickered there. Recognition, maybe. Or just the wariness of someone who'd learned that hands could lie.

"Two rooms," the woman said. "Hot water. Whatever food you have that won't kill him."

"We have soup," Sera said. "It might kill him anyway."

Something almost like a smile crossed the woman's face. "He's dying. He'll risk it."

* * *

They took the corner table, the one with the best sightlines, because of course they did. Sera brought the soup herself, partly because the serving girl was terrified of the gray man — he made the lamps gutter when he passed, though Sera doubted anyone else had noticed — and mostly because she wanted to look closer at the thing folded into the woman's breast pocket.

She smelled it before she saw it. Under the tallow smoke and sour beer, under the fever-stink rolling off the dying man, there was another scent entirely: cold stone, deep water, and something sweet and wrong, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. She knew that smell. She had spent seven years breathing it in the galleries beneath the mountain, where the lanterns hung and sang.

The map. It could be nothing else. A Wayfarer's map drawn in dying ink, bound for the Glass Steppe, and the man carrying its price slumped over his soup like his bones had been quietly removed.

"You're going to the Steppe," Sera said. Not a question.

The woman looked up. Her eyes were the color of a frozen lake — beautiful, and liable to crack under you if you trusted them. "We're going to the court beneath the mountain."

"No one goes to the court."

"I know."

"They don't sell back what they buy." Sera heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she'd used in the galleries when petitioners came weeping with their bargains. "I served there seven years. I played for their feasts while their Steward counted souls like coin. You think you'll charm them? Outbid them? The Pale Steward doesn't bargain, Wayfarer. It *collects*. And everything it collects stays collected until the mountain itself wears down to sand."

The gray man lifted his head. His eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "You're wrong about one thing," he said, each word costing him. "I didn't come to buy. My brother already sold me. I came to steal myself back."

And oh, that landed. That landed in the old wound, the one shaped exactly like a lament she'd played once, for one soul among hundreds hanging in its lantern, singing so quietly no one else even heard it. Seven years of playing pretty lies for monsters, and the one time she'd played truth —

"You were the musician," the man said, watching her face. "The one who fell. They still talk about you down there. They play your lament backwards, now, as a warning."

Sera set down the bowl. Her hands, those traitor hands, had begun to shake.

"My name is Ivo Serrano," the man said. "This is Paloma Hartwell, last of the Wayfarers' Guild. In four turns of the moon, my soul gets sealed into the mountain forever. We're going to need music that the dead can dance to. And I'm told—" he coughed, wet and terrible, "—you owe the court a debt of your own."

* * *

She told them no, of course. That night, and the next morning, and twice more over bad beer, she told them no with escalating eloquence. She laid out every reason with the precision of a court recital: the toll-keepers on the southern passes who served the mountain and would smell the map like wolves smelling blood; the fault-line descent that killed one traveler in three; the thirteenth moon rising over a plain flat enough that you couldn't hide from anything, least of all yourself. She told them the court would strip Ivo's soul to its threadbare essentials before he ever reached it. She told them Paloma's guild oath wouldn't save her, because oaths were just rope, and rope held whoever tied it — or hanged them.

"And what will you do instead?" Paloma asked, on the fourth telling, in the gray hour when the tavern was empty and the fire had burned down to its red bones. "Pour soup until the world ends?"

"That's more than most people manage."

"You played a lament for a stolen soul." Paloma said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "You lost everything for one act of decency, and now you pour beer in the last town before hell and call it peace. Is that what falling sounds like, musician? Because it sounds to me like holding your breath."

Sera should have thrown her out. Instead she sat down across from her, and found her own voice coming out smaller than she intended. "You want to know why I really fell? Not the pretty version? The soul I played for — I never even asked its name. I heard it singing through the lantern-glass, and it sounded like my mother, and I played for *that*. For my grief. Not for the soul. And then I ran, and let everyone else pay for my sentiment." She turned her cup in circles on the scarred wood. "The court doesn't sell back what it buys. But sometimes, late at night, I wonder if it lets things get stolen. If it leaves doors unlocked, out of boredom. Or cruelty. Or some game I can't see the shape of yet."

Paloma was quiet a long moment. Then: "Come with us and find out."

"Why? You've managed fine alone. Last of the guild. Lone wolf and all that."

"Because I've been traveling alone for eleven years," Paloma said, "and I only realized this week that I've stopped hating it." She said it like it cost her something. Like it was a confession she hadn't meant to make. "Because Ivo has maybe four turns left and he spends them trying to make Pip laugh. Because I'm going to walk into the Mountain Below with three people and a mule and no plan worth the name, and I'd rather do it with someone whose hands shake for the right reasons."

* * *

She packed that same night. It took ten minutes; exile teaches you to travel light.

At dawn she stood in the tavern yard with her instrument case on her back — a court viol, dark-wooded, its strings silver-wound, tuned to a scale no living teacher had taught her — and watched the others assemble. The gray man, upright by sheer spite. The Wayfarer, checking straps with quick competent hands. And the child, Pip, moon-pale and solemn, watching Sera with the unnerving directness of someone who hears more than words.

Pip tilted her head as Sera approached, listening to something beneath the morning air. Then the child smiled, small and startled, and said:

"Your song got longer. It used to end sad. Now it doesn't know how it ends yet."

Sera's throat went tight. "Well," she managed. "Let's go find out how it finishes."

Paloma swung up onto the mule's lead and pointed south, toward the passes, toward the flat white gleam on the horizon where the Glass Steppe waited like a held breath. "Toll-keepers on the southern road," she said. "We go around, through the karst. It'll add days we don't have."

"Days we don't have," Ivo agreed, cheerfully grim, "is practically our motto."

They started walking. And Sera Voss, exiled musician, fell again — but this time into step beside other people, and the ground, for once, held.

Behind them, far off along the southern road, dust rose where dust shouldn't be. Riders, moving fast. Moving their way.

Pip heard them first.
