## 5. The Child Who Hears Singing

The village had no name on any map Paloma had ever carried, which was the first good sign. Unnamed places stayed off toll-keepers' ledgers. It sat where the farmland gave up and the steppe began — a scatter of turf-roofed houses around a well, a shrine with no god in it, and a long horizon of silver grass that moved like water without ever going anywhere.

They had been three days on the smuggler's road, and Ivo had spent two of them asleep in the cart.

That was the arithmetic Paloma did each morning now, and each morning the numbers came out worse. He woke less. When he woke, he ate less. His hands had begun to look like something borrowed — the color of old candle wax, the veins showing through like roots under ice. Sera had stopped saying *he's holding on* and started saying nothing at all, which was worse. She just tuned her lute more often, the way other people prayed.

"Village ahead," Paloma said, reining the mule off the track. "We need salt, lamp oil, and a night under a roof. In that order."

"We need to not be seen," Sera said.

"We won't be. Nobody looks twice at a dying man in a cart. They look away." She said it flatly, and hated how true it was. She had watched a whole guild hall full of people look away from a dying man once. She had been the only one who didn't.

The village took them the way small places take strangers: with long looks and short answers. An old woman sold them salt and oil and bread that was mostly crust, and took Paloma's coin without counting it, which meant she'd already counted it by eye. A man mending a net asked where they'd come from, and Paloma gave him the answer she gave everyone — *north, family business* — and he nodded at the cart with the particular gentleness people reserve for funerals that haven't happened yet.

It was at the well, while Sera filled the water skins, that Paloma first heard the singing.

It wasn't Sera. It wasn't anyone. It came from behind the shrine, thin and high and strange — not a melody so much as a thread of one, the way a voice sounds through a wall. Paloma's hand went to her knife out of pure habit, and then she made herself let go of it and walk around the shrine instead.

A child sat on the shrine's low back wall. Small, maybe eight, maybe ten — steppe children were hard to age, all wind and weather in one package. Ragged coat too big, boots too small, hair the color of dry grass. And the child was singing, eyes closed, head tilted, singing that thread of melody with total absorption, the way Sera played when she forgot anyone was listening.

"That's a pretty song," Paloma said.

The child's eyes snapped open. They were the pale gray of the sky before snow, and they fixed on Paloma with an expression no eight-year-old should own — the look of someone doing arithmetic.

"It's not mine," the child said. "It's his."

"Whose?"

"The man in the cart." The child hopped down off the wall, light as a bird off a fence. "He's got a song left, but it's almost finished. Last verse. Maybe less." The child said it the way other children said *it's going to rain*.

Paloma's mouth went dry. "What do you mean, *song*?"

The child looked at her like she was the strange one. "Everyone's got one. You can't hear yours, but I can hear yours." A pause, and then the child hummed — three low notes, patient, stubborn, with a strange hollow space in the middle of them, like a house with one room sealed shut.

Paloma went cold from the boots up. Because she knew that hollow space. It was the oath. The guild oath she carried in her chest like a second heartbeat, the last living bond to Thornfell and everyone who had ever slept under its roof. The child had hummed her soul, and her soul had a locked room in it.

"Who are you?" she asked quietly.

"Pip." The child shrugged. "Just Pip. Ma died when I was small. Nobody here hears what I hear, so they say I'm moon-touched, which is a rude way to say it, I think." Pip kicked at a stone. "Your friend in the cart — the wax-colored one — his song's the strangest I ever heard. It's got a hole in the middle where the loud part should be. Like somebody cut out the chorus and sold it."

Paloma's knees nearly went. *Sold it.* She crouched to the child's level. "Pip. Listen to me carefully. That man's name is Ivo. The hole in his song — can you tell where it went?"

Pip's face did something complicated — fear and longing wrestling in a small space. "South," Pip said finally. "Down. It went down, and it's still singing there, but it's singing for someone else now. That's the worst part. It doesn't miss him. Songs don't miss." Then, softer: "He does, though. I can hear it under the song. That part's almost louder."

* * *

She told Sera by the cart, in the low fast voice they'd developed for bad news. Sera listened, water skin forgotten in her hands, and when Pip's account was done she sat down slowly on the cart's rail.

"A soul-hearer," she said. "The court used to keep them. I saw one once, through a door I was never supposed to see through. They're how the Steward knows which souls are ripe." She looked at the child across the square, small and ragged and unbothered, sharing crusts with the mule. "If the toll-keepers ever learn what that child is—"

"Then we make sure they don't." Paloma heard her own voice and was surprised by it. Not the guild voice, the flat professional one. Something else. Something that had decided things without asking her.

Ivo woke at dusk. He woke less often now, and each waking was shorter, like a man surfacing from deep water for one breath before the weight pulled him back. But this time his eyes found Paloma immediately, clear and urgent.

"The map," he said. "Show me the map."

She unfolded it in the cart's shadow. The dying ink had crawled again overnight — the route had shifted south by a finger's width, and where the Glass Steppe had been drawn as a blank silver space, there was now a small mark. A circle. A door.

"It's found the fault," Ivo whispered. "The way down. We're close, Paloma. Close enough that—" He stopped. His gaze had drifted past her, to the shrine wall, where Pip sat watching them with open, unembarrassed curiosity.

"The child hears me," Ivo said. Not a question.

"Hears your song, anyway. Says it's almost finished."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. "Then the child knows something I've been afraid to count." He looked back at Paloma, and there was no self-pity in his face, only that terrible practicality he'd carried since the first night. "One more turn of the moon. Maybe less. Whatever we're going to do down there, we do it fast."

* * *

The toll-keepers came two hours past midnight.

Paloma heard them before she saw them — not the men, but the mules. Six sets of hooves moving wrong for this hour, too fast, too purposeful, coming up the south track. She was out of her bedroll and moving before the thought finished forming, and she found, with a cold little shock, that she was not the only one. Sera already had the lute cased and the cart's brake off. Ivo, gray as ash, had dragged himself upright and was holding the map against his chest like a shield.

"Toll-keepers," Paloma breathed. "Six. Maybe a scout ahead. They're not patrolling — they're *hunting*. Someone talked."

"Nobody talked," Sera said. "The map talks. It's been singing its position to the mountain since the day he drew it. I should have thought of that. I *did* think of that, and I told myself we had time." She closed her eyes. "I was wrong. I was wrong about that court once before, too. It's becoming a habit."

There was no time for self-blame, hers or anyone's. Paloma did the arithmetic of the village in her head — one road out, south, straight toward the riders. The fields east, all furrow and mud, too slow with a cart. And west, behind the shrine, the grass, the endless silver grass, going on to the horizon like a road that had never been built by anyone.

"We take the cart into the steppe," she said. "Grass hides wheel tracks by morning. Pip—"

She stopped. Because Pip was already there, standing in the moonlight between the houses, a small bundle over one shoulder, eyes wide and absolutely unafraid.

"They're coming for the map," Pip said. "I heard the riders' songs an hour ago. Toll-keepers all sound the same. Like coins in a tin cup." The child looked at Paloma, and the pale eyes were steady. "Take me with you. Nobody here hears me. Nobody here *wants* to hear me. And you're going where the singing is, and I want to hear the singing where the singing is."

"Pip—"

"You'll need me." No bravado in it. Just fact, laid out plain as bread. "Down there. Under the mountain. You'll need ears like mine, and you know it. I heard you talking by the well. You're going to steal his song back." Pip's chin came up. "I want to watch."

There was no time to argue and, Paloma realized, no real desire to. "Behind me," she said. "Hold on. Whatever happens, don't let go."

They went out through the grass with the cart muffled in sacking and Sera humming low under her breath — not a song, just sound, a soft gray noise to blur their own. Behind them the village woke in pieces: a dog, a door, a lantern. Then hooves on the south track, and a voice calling for the strangers from the north, and the old woman's voice answering, slow and unhelpful, giving them the only gift she had, which was time.

Paloma ran at the cart's shoulder with the reins in her fist and Pip on her back, thin arms locked around her neck, light as a bird, warm as a coal. The grass closed over them like water over a stone. Ahead, the moon sat low and swollen over the silver plain, one night shy of full.

And somewhere in the middle of the running, in the middle of the fear and the grass and the child's breath against her ear, Paloma Hartwell noticed something so ordinary she almost missed it.

She was not alone.

Not tonight, not for days — she cast back and couldn't find the moment it had changed. Somewhere between a fevered man's confession and a fallen musician's lament, somewhere in the small daily work of counting doses and sharing bread, she had stopped being the last of the Wayfarers traveling a dead road, and become something else. Something with a cart and a mule and a child on her back and a woman's humming behind her and a dying man's map against his chest. She had not noticed when it happened. That was the strange part. Family, it turned out, didn't announce itself. It just accumulated, quietly, the way miles did.

"Paloma," Pip whispered against her ear, as the village lights sank behind them. "His song changed. Just now. While we were running."

"How?"

"It got a new note." Pip's voice was full of wonder. "It sounds like yours."

Paloma didn't answer. She ran, and the grass parted, and behind them, far back on the south track, a rider's lantern swung — and stopped — and began, slowly, to follow.
