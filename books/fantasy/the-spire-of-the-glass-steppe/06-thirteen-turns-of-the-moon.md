## 6. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

The Glass Steppe did not begin. That was the first thing I understood about it.

One morning there was grass, thin and silver-headed, and the next morning there wasn't — just a plain of something like ice and something like water and nothing at all like either, stretching north to a mountain that seemed no closer than it had three days ago. Paloma called it glass. Pip called it the mirror-country. Sera didn't call it anything; she walked with her fiddle case held against her chest like a shield, as if she expected the steppe to remember her hands.

I called it honest. Because when I looked down into it, I did not see my face.

I saw the night I signed.

* * *

We had made camp on the steppe's edge before crossing — real ground, the last of it — and I had dreamed of my brother's kitchen. In the dream the stove was warm and Tomás was humming and I was telling him about the debt, the ruinous, clawing debt, and how the man in the gray coat had said there was another way, an old way, a court beneath the mountain that bought and sold in rarer coin than money. *You won't miss it,* I had told Tomás. *It isn't flesh. It isn't even memory. It's just — the part that sings.*

Tomás had laughed. That was the worst of it. Not the knife of his agreement, not the way he'd gone alone to the gray-coated man while I slept off the wine, but the laugh — easy, warm, the laugh of a brother who thought he was being handed a gift. *Sold your soul for me? You sentimental fool.* He'd poured two cups. He'd drunk to my generosity.

He'd never asked what it would cost me to keep living without it.

I woke on the bare steppe with frost on my eyelashes and no fire burning, because Paloma would not light one out here — *the court watches its own front yard*, she said — and I lay still a long time, listening to my heart.

It had been getting quieter for weeks. Like footsteps moving away down a long corridor. But lying on the glass, with the waning moon overhead and its twin beneath me — because the steppe held the sky the way a held breath holds words — I could hear it plainly for the first time. Fainter each night. Thinner each dawn.

Four turns left. Maybe five, if the cold was kind, and the cold out here had no interest in kindness.

"Ivo." Paloma's voice, low, from the edge of camp. "You're staring into it again."

"It stares back."

"That's the trouble." She crouched beside me, wrapped in her guild-gray cloak, her eyes on the reflection where mine should have been. In the glass she appeared whole and sharp-edged, but I knew better now — Pip had told me what the steppe showed everyone. Not faces. Regrets. What I saw when I looked at Paloma's reflection was a hall of empty chairs and a master coughing blood into a cloth, over and over, a woman standing in a doorway watching the last light of her life go out one oath at a time.

"You see yours too," I said. "I hear you stop breathing when you look down."

"Wayfarers don't look down."

"You're looking down right now."

She was quiet a moment. "Mine's short," she admitted. "A list of names. Everyone I guided somewhere and never came back to check on." She stood, briskly, the confession folded away again. "Eat. We cross today. The Steward's toll-keepers ride the south track, and once we're on the glass they can't follow — but they'll be waiting at the far edge, so we move fast and we sleep little."

"And if my heart stops before we reach the fault?"

Paloma looked at me then, really looked, and whatever she meant to say — some wayfarer's comfort, some professional reassurance — she discarded it. "Then we carry the map faster," she said. "Your soul's in this cargo, Serrano. I don't abandon cargo."

It was the closest thing to tenderness anyone had offered me since Tomás, and it hurt worse than the truth would have.

* * *

Crossing the Glass Steppe was like walking across the surface of a confession.

There were no landmarks. No stones, no grass, no trail. Paloma navigated by the stars and their reflections both, reading the doubled sky like a page written twice for emphasis, and we followed her in silence because sound carried strangely out here and nobody wanted to learn why. Beneath our boots the plain gave back not our faces but our worst hours, bright and patient as stained windows.

Pip saw them all, of course. The child heard them, too — heard us, our souls or what remained of ours, as music. On the second day of the crossing Pip fell into step beside me and said, without preamble:

"Yours is almost finished."

I kept walking. "Finished how?"

"Like a song that's nearly done." Pip scuffed a boot on the glass, which made no sound at all. "Most people's songs have a lot of noise in the middle. Yours has a hole where the middle should be. Where the singing part goes." A pause. "That's what he took, isn't it? When your brother sold it. He didn't take all of you. Just the part that sings."

"Yes."

"So you've been walking around with a song that only has verses." Pip looked up at me with those moon-pale eyes, matter-of-fact as a physician delivering a prognosis. "No chorus. No wonder you're quiet all the time."

Sera, ahead of us, laughed — a short, cracked sound. "Out of the mouths of orphans."

"You'd know," Pip said sweetly. "Yours has a broken chord in it. Third verse. It never resolves."

Sera stopped laughing.

That night we slept in a hollow of the glass where the reflections ran shallow, and I lay awake doing arithmetic with my heartbeat. Counting the gaps between beats the way you count the turns of a waning moon. Three turns left, maybe four. Somewhere behind us a toll-keeper's lantern had stopped following at the steppe's edge and simply waited, patient as the mountain itself. Ahead of us the fault line waited too — a black seam at the base of the mountain, the door down into the court where my soul hung in its lantern among ten thousand others, purchased fair and square by a brother who had raised a cup to my generosity.

And I found, lying there in the dark with my heart ticking down like a clock someone had forgotten to wind, that I was not afraid.

I had been afraid for two years. Afraid in the gray man's parlor, afraid on every road since, afraid each morning I woke and felt the hollowness where the singing used to be. But here on the glass, with my regret laid out beneath me in the plain's cold light — Tomás's laugh, the two cups, the wine — fear had burned itself down to something else. Something with edges. Something useful.

Because here was the thing I had finally stopped pretending not to know: I had done it wrong.

Not the selling — though that too. But after. After I learned what the court took, what the hollow felt like, what my own voice sounded like coming out of a throat with no music left in it — after all that, I had crawled toward Thornfell and hired a dying guide with coins I couldn't feel, and I had planned, all along, to die politely on the way. To let the thirteen turns run out. To let Tomás's bargain stand completed, my soul sealed in the mountain forever, and the shame buried with me where no one would ever make me say it aloud.

*You let him do it,* the glass said, in no voice at all. *You drank the wine. You went to sleep. You woke up empty and you thanked him.*

"Yes," I said aloud, to the dark, to the plain, to myself.

Paloma stirred across the hollow. "Talking to the steppe is how it gets its hooks in you," she murmured.

"I'm apologizing."

"To who?"

"To the part of me that's hanging in a lantern down there, waiting for me to give up on it." My heart beat, faintly, twice. "I spent two years thinking I was already dead. Just waiting for the paperwork." I sat up. The moon hung above the mountain, one turn past full and falling, and its reflection lay beneath us like a coin on a table. "But it's not dead, is it. Whatever they took. It's *waiting*. And I've been out here dying on schedule like a good little purchase while it waits for me to show up and fight for it."

Paloma was silent a moment. Then: "That's the most alive you've sounded since Thornfell."

"Don't get used to it. I have maybe four turns left."

"Then spend them loudly."

* * *

On the third day of the crossing, the steppe showed me my brother.

Not truly — I knew that. Only the glass doing what the glass did, giving my regret a shape so I would have to walk around it or through it. Tomás stood a hundred paces out on the plain, exactly as he'd been that night, sleeves rolled, pouring wine, laughing at my sentimentality. As I walked, he walked, keeping his distance, keeping his cup full, and every hour he grew more solid until by dusk I could almost smell the wine.

"He's not real," Sera said quietly, walking at my shoulder. She had seen her own figure out there days ago, she admitted — herself on the court's marble stair, setting down her fiddle case, walking away from a soul-shaped lantern while it screamed. "They're weights, not ghosts. The steppe measures what you'd trade anything to undo, and it puts it in front of you, and it watches what you do."

"What am I supposed to do?"

"That's between you and it."

At moonrise I stopped walking. I turned and faced my brother across fifty paces of glass, and I said what I should have said two years ago in a warm kitchen with two full cups on the table.

"You shouldn't have done it. I forgive you anyway, because carrying both is what killed me, and I'm done dying on schedule." My voice came out rough and strange, half a stranger's voice, but it carried across the plain. "I'm going down there, Tomás. I'm taking it back. And when I come out — if I come out — I'm going to live loudly enough for both of us, and you're going to hear about it every single day, and that will be your punishment."

The figure smiled. The figure always smiled; that was the cruelty of it. Then it raised its cup to me — *to your generosity*, it mouthed — and the moonlight bent, and it was only glass again.

But something in my chest answered. Faint. Far. One clear note rising up through the seam of the world, like a voice calling up a well.

Pip spun around, eyes wide. "Did you hear—"

"I heard."

Down at the mountain's foot, where the black fault cut the glass like a wound, a light had appeared. Not a lantern. A door, opening downward, spilling pale gold onto the plain — and standing in it, tall and thin and courteous as a winter tree, a figure in gray, waiting with the patience of stone.

The Pale Steward had come up to meet us.

Behind it, deep in the dark, ten thousand small lights hung in ranks like a city of lamps — and one of them, nearest the door, was flickering. Fast. Eager. Like a song reaching its final bar, waiting for the chorus.

Paloma unslung her pack and touched the folded map at her bottom, and the dying ink glowed faintly through the canvas, as if it knew it was nearly home.

"Well," she said. "Cargo's expecting us. Let's not keep it waiting."
