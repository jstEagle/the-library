## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The fault opened in the steppe like a healed scar, a seam of darker glass where the plain had knitted itself together wrong. Paloma found it by Pip's face, not her own eyes. The child stood at its edge with their head tilted, listening to something under the world, and their expression was the same one they wore when Sera tuned her strings — rapt, slightly afraid, hungry for more.

"There," Pip said. "It's loudest here. Like someone left a door open on an orchestra."

Paloma crouched and pressed her palm to the seam. Cold came up through it, cold like well water, like the inside of a locked room. Below, faint as breath, she could feel a vibration that might have been music or might have been machinery or might have been ten thousand small things trying to be heard at once.

"That's them," Ivo said behind her.

She turned. He had walked most of the steppe leaning on Sera's arm, and he looked worse than when they'd started — grayer, thinner, as if the land itself had been quietly siphoning him. On the Glass Steppe his reflection barely kept pace with him anymore, lagging half a step behind, sometimes forgetting to blink.

"The souls," he said, when Paloma raised an eyebrow. "I can't hear them like Pip does. But I know. It's like standing outside a house where everyone you love is having dinner without you."

"Then let's get you to dinner," Paloma said, and dropped through the seam before her courage could reconsider.

* * *

The way down was a smuggler's descent, which meant it was ugly, narrow, and would have killed anyone who hadn't spent fifteen years learning how stone lied. She went first, calling holds back up the chimney of rock in the clipped guild cant her masters had drilled into her: *loose flake, trust the crack, three points always.* Sera passed Ivo down hand over hand. Pip simply flowed down after them like water finding its level, humming the whole way, and the hum, Paloma noticed, changed pitch whenever the route changed direction — the child navigating by song.

An hour down, or three — time went strange below the steppe — the chimney opened, and Paloma stopped breathing.

They stood on a ledge above a cavern the size of a cathedral built by something that had never seen one. The walls were glass, all of them, every surface, smooth and dark and deep enough that looking into them felt like falling. And hanging in the air over the cavern floor, rising in slow spiral tiers toward a ceiling lost in darkness, were lanterns.

Hundreds. Thousands. Each one a soft gold-white glow the color of candlelight through honey, each one pulsing gently, out of rhythm with its neighbors but somehow in rhythm with all of them together, so the whole vast court breathed light like a sleeping chest.

"They're singing," Pip whispered. They had both hands over their mouth, eyes enormous. "All of them. All different songs. It's — " Their voice cracked. "It's the most beautiful thing I ever heard."

"It's a prison," Sera said flatly. Her knuckles were white on her instrument case. "Don't let the acoustics fool you. I played here for six years and never once saw a lantern leave."

Paloma said nothing. She was watching Ivo.

He had gone very still, staring up into the tiers of light, and his lips were moving as though counting — or praying, or reading something written there only he could see. His lagging reflection had stopped following him entirely. It stood a full pace behind, gazing upward, and Paloma realized with a lurch that it was gazing at one particular lantern, low in the seventh tier, burning dimmer than its neighbors. Flickering. Almost out.

"That's me," Ivo said quietly. "Isn't it. That little guttering one."

"Yes," said a voice like frost forming.

* * *

He descended from the dark at the top of the cavern, and Paloma understood immediately why the court had no throne and needed none. He didn't walk so much as arrive, unfolding downward through the air in long pale increments, until he stood on the glass floor before them — tall, thin, translucent as the walls themselves, his face smooth and courteous and utterly empty of anything you could bargain with. Or so it wanted you to think. Paloma had dealt with toll-keepers and tithe-lords and one customs official in Vell who'd smiled exactly like that while confiscating everything she owned. Emptiness was a costume. Underneath, everything wanted something.

"The Pale Steward," Sera murmured, and bowed the shallow, bitter bow of someone greeting an old employer.

"You are expected," the Steward said. Its voice came from everywhere at once, from the glass, from the lanterns, from beneath their feet. Its gaze settled on Ivo with what might have been tenderness, in the way a ledger is tender toward a sum. "Thirteen turns, Serrano. You are early by two nights. How industrious."

"I've come to buy something back," Ivo said. His voice shook. He made it keep going anyway, and Paloma felt something in her chest turn over at the sound. "My soul. I want to make a withdrawal."

A sound moved through the court then — not laughter, nothing so warm. The lanterns flickered in unison, a ripple of light passing up through the tiers like wind through wheat.

"Withdrawals," the Steward said, "are not among our services."

"Then I'm opening negotiations." Ivo glanced sideways, and Paloma stepped forward on cue, because this part they had rehearsed on the road for nine days, and because she was, whatever else was true, the last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild, and guild craft did not stop working just because the guild was dead.

"What my client means," she said, "is that he'd like to discuss terms. We understand the original contract was — let's call it informally drafted. His brother signed, yes, but no consideration passed *to* the seller, and the goods—" she nodded up at the guttering lantern— "were obtained under duress. A dying man's consent isn't consent. Any magistrate on the surface would tear that contract apart. We're offering you the chance to renegotiate before someone above makes you."

It was beautiful nonsense, mostly. There was no magistrate above with jurisdiction here. But she watched it land — watched the Steward's smooth face do something almost like thought — and knew she'd guessed right about the thing underneath the emptiness: courts, even courts of glass and grief, cared about their rules being *seen* to hold.

"You argue like a guildswoman," the Steward said at last.

"I am a guildswoman."

"The Wayfarers' Guild is extinct."

"The last of its oaths is standing right in front of you," Paloma said, and hated how much it cost to say it plainly, how the words came out sounding proud when they were really just lonely.

The Steward regarded her for a long moment. Then it turned its attention back to Ivo, and its voice softened into something genuinely terrible, because it was genuinely reasonable.

"A contract may indeed be unwound. Ours are not cruel; they are merely exact. The soul was purchased at a price, and a price must be paid for its return — not coin, which we have no use for. A binding of equal weight. Something that anchors a life as surely as that lantern anchors his." It gestured upward, delicate, at Ivo's dim flame. "Something sworn, and permanent, and freely given. Bring me such a thing by the thirteenth turn of the moon, and the lantern goes home with you. Fail, and the sale concludes, and what remains of Mr. Serrano will spend the rest of his short mortal span as a hollow man walking. Those are our terms. They do not move."

Paloma's mouth was already open, already shaping the next line of the negotiation — *name your counter, we'll consider structure* — when she understood.

Freely given. Permanent. Anchoring a life as surely as a soul.

Her oath. The last living bond of the Wayfarers' Guild, spoken over her dead masters' bodies, the single thread still tying her to Thornfell, to her mentors, to everything she had ever been. The only thing she owned that weighed anything at all.

Beside her, Ivo had gone white. He was looking at her, and she could see him doing the same arithmetic she was, arriving at the same sum, and shaking his head before the numbers finished.

"No," he said. "No. Whatever it's thinking — Paloma. *Look at me.* Whatever price—"

"Terms received," Paloma said loudly, over him, to the Steward, in her best guild voice, steady as a bridge cable even though her hands had gone cold as the fault they'd climbed down through. "We'll return before the moon turns."

The Steward inclined its smooth head, and the lanterns rippled once, all together, like a held breath.

* * *

They climbed in silence. Pip stopped humming halfway up the chimney, which frightened Paloma more than anything the Steward had said. At the top, under the indifferent stars of the steppe, Ivo caught her wrist — his grip shockingly weak, and getting weaker.

"I know what weighs as much as a soul," he said hoarsely. "You told me about it. Night four, on the smuggler's road, when you couldn't sleep. Your oath. The last one. You told me it was the only thing keeping the guild alive."

"And I also told you," Paloma said, "never to trust a guide who talks too much when she can't sleep."

"Paloma—"

"Two nights," she said. She pulled her wrist free, gently, and turned her face toward the east, where the last sliver of the old moon was bleeding away into the horizon, and somewhere beyond it, patient and pale, the thirteenth turn was coming. "Get some sleep, Ivo. Tomorrow I start figuring out how a smuggler steals something instead of buying it."

Above them, faint through the healed scar of the fault, ten thousand souls sang on — and now that she'd heard Pip describe them, Paloma couldn't stop hearing it herself: a whole cathedral of small lights, each one a song, each one waiting to find out who would come back for it.
