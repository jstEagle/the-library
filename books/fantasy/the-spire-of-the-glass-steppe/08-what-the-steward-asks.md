## 8. What the Steward Asks

The Pale Steward did not speak the way living things spoke. Its voice arrived already inside your head, cool as water finding a crack, and Sera Voss felt her hands go cold on her instrument's neck before it finished its first sentence.

"You bring me a lantern I have already purchased," it said. "And you ask me to un-sell what was sold."

The court stretched around them like the inside of an instrument nobody had ever dared play. Walls of black glass rose in faceted columns, and between them hung the lanterns — souls, hundreds of them, each one a soft gold light that pulsed in slow rhythms, some quick as hummingbird hearts, some so faint they were nearly gone. Sera had stood in this court once before, eleven years ago, with a bow in her hand and favor in her ears. She had thought it beautiful then.

She knew better now. It was not a court. It was a larder.

Ivo lay propped against Paloma's pack, his skin gray as old candle wax, his breath so shallow the glass seemed to drink each exhale before it could finish. Pip stood pressed against Sera's side, small fingers twisted into her coat hem, whispering under their breath: *so many songs, so many almost-done songs.*

Paloma stepped forward. The Wayfarer looked exactly like what she was — road-worn, sharp-eyed, a woman built out of stubbornness and empty rooms — and she planted herself before the Steward's tall pale shape the way she'd plant herself before any toll-keeper: like the price was negotiable because she said it was.

"We're not asking for charity," Paloma said. "We're asking for terms. You sold him once. Everything sells twice, if you know the buyer."

Something moved behind the Steward's face — or where a face should have been. It was tall and narrow, draped in white that wasn't cloth, and when it turned its attention on Paloma, Sera saw the lanterns nearest them dim slightly, as if flinching.

"Everything has been bought by something," the Steward agreed. "That is the first law beneath the mountain. Very well, Wayfarer. Name your coin."

"Ivo Serrano's soul. Returned. Whole."

"And in exchange?"

Paloma opened her mouth.

"No." Ivo's voice cracked across the chamber like dry wood splitting. He dragged himself up onto one elbow, and the effort cost him visibly — Sera could see the life going out of him in real time, a candle guttering toward its wick. "No terms. No trades. We take nothing from this place. Paloma, don't—"

"Hush," Paloma said, without looking at him. "You've done your haggling. It got you here."

"The exchange must be of equal weight," the Steward said. "This is also law. A soul weighs what it has loved." Its pale head tilted, considering, and Sera felt the awful sensation of being *read* — every memory turned over like stones in a streambed. "The Serrano soul is heavy. It loves a brother who betrayed it. It loves a road. It loves—" the attention slid, deliberate and slow, to Paloma "—it has begun to love its guide."

Sera watched Paloma's jaw tighten. Watched her not deny it.

"What do you want?" Paloma asked.

"What anchors you," said the Steward. "Your guild oath. The last living bond of the Wayfarers' Guild. Give it to me, and the mountain will hold two weights instead of one. Refuse, and the man walks out of here breathing but hollow — a body with no song in it, walking until it forgets to walk."

* * *

Silence, except for the lanterns. Except for Pip, whose whisper had stopped entirely, which frightened Sera more than anything the Steward had said.

She had known this was coming. From the moment they'd descended through the fault, from the moment she'd seen the way the oath lived in Paloma — visible only to someone who had spent years learning to see what anchored people, because Sera's own anchor had been torn out at the roots when the court exiled her — she had known the Steward would find the seam and press it.

An oath was a kind of music. Sera understood oaths the way she understood compositions: as structures that held a person together, that gave the random noise of a life a key, a tempo, a reason to resolve. Paloma Hartwell was not a woman. She was a chord, and the guild oath was the root note underneath everything, and if you pulled that note out—

"—and if she gives it up," Sera heard herself saying, "what's left?"

The Steward regarded her. "What is left of anyone, musician. Themselves. Unaccompanied. Most find it unendurable." A pause, thin as paper. "You did not."

The words went through her like a blade finding an old scar. Eleven years, and the court still remembered exactly where she was soft.

Ivo hauled himself upright. It took him three tries. When he finally stood — swaying, held up by nothing but fury and whatever dregs remained in his blood — he looked smaller than the dying stranger who'd stumbled into Thornfell hall, and somehow more solid too.

"Then I refuse," he said. "Both of us refuse. I'll walk out hollow. Better hollow than—"

"You don't get to decide that alone," Paloma snapped.

"Neither do you!"

They stared at each other across three feet of black glass, and Sera saw it plainly, the thing she'd been watching grow since the smuggler's road: two people who had spent their whole lives alone learning, far too late and far too fast, what it cost to belong to somebody.

"The moon turns in four hours," the Steward said mildly. "After that, the sale completes regardless. Decide quickly, love each other slowly. Those are my only courtesies."

It withdrew — not leaving, never quite leaving, just receding into the glass like a figure sinking into frosted water — and the lanterns brightened again around them, uneasy, watchful.

* * *

Pip tugged Sera's sleeve. "Play," the child whispered. "Please. They're all listening. All the lanterns. They haven't heard anything kind in so long and the almost-finished ones are getting quieter and if it gets quiet enough down here they stop trying to finish."

Sera looked down at the small moon-touched face, and then at the walls of hanging lights, and understood what Pip was really asking. Not a performance. A kindness. Maybe the first this place had seen since it was built.

She lifted her fiddle. Her hands shook — they always shook now, near the court — and she set the bow anyway, and began to play the lament. The one she'd written eleven years ago for a stolen soul whose name she'd never learned, the one that had gotten her exiled, the one the court had called treason and she had called the only honest thing she'd ever made.

The notes went out into the black glass and came back changed. The court amplified everything; that was its nature, and its cruelty. Every phrase she played returned to her doubled, tripled, braided with itself until the whole chamber rang like the inside of a bell, and the lanterns began to sway. Slowly. Then all together. Gold lights keeping time to a lament written for one of their own.

And through it, low, off-key, fearless, Pip sang along — singing the souls themselves, giving voice to the almost-finished songs, and Sera realized with her whole body what the child was doing: masking them. Muddying the water. Giving her cover without ever having been asked, the way family does things without being asked.

*Across the chamber, Ivo gripped Paloma's wrist.* Sera couldn't hear what they said over the music. She didn't need to. She watched Ivo shake his head, over and over — *don't, don't, don't* — and watched Paloma look at him the way she'd once, on the steppe, looked at the horizon after admitting how long it had been since anyone waited up for her by a fire.

Then Paloma closed her eyes. And when she opened them, she had the look Sera had seen on her only twice before: the look of a woman who had already decided and was simply waiting for the world to catch up.

Sera played harder. Bought her another minute. Another bar. The thirteenth moon would be rising soon above the steppe, and somewhere overhead the light was already turning, and all she could do was stand in the belly of the mountain and pour everything she had left into four strings, buying seconds the only way she'd ever known how.

Paloma drew a breath. Stepped toward the place where the Steward waited in the glass.

"Steward," she said, and her voice cut clean through the lament. "I'll trade."
