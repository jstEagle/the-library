## 9. The Stealing of a Soul

The court slept to a lament.

That was the joke of it, Paloma thought, pressed flat against a wall of glass that hummed like a struck bell. Sera Voss had been exiled for playing this exact song — a slow, grieving thing for a stolen soul — and now she stood in the center of the court beneath the mountain, bowing it out note by note, and the whole glittering dark was folding itself down to listen. Lantern-souls dimmed to embers. The Pale Steward's attendants swayed where they stood, their long faces slack with something almost like peace.

Almost like mercy. But the court didn't know what mercy was. It only knew music the way a lock knew a key.

*Move*, Paloma told herself. *While the song holds.*

She moved.

The floor of the court was glass over glass, and every step rang. She'd spent three hours last night learning which panes sang loud and which stayed quiet, walking the perimeter on soft-soled boots while Ivo slept against Pip's shoulder, his breath so shallow she'd twice put her fingers under his nose to check. Now she picked her way along the quiet panes, past soul-lanterns hanging from hooks of black iron, each one burning its own color. Blue for the honest dead. Gold for the beloved. A sickly green one near the wall that made her skin crawl until she realized it was still *lit* — someone in there was still alive somewhere, still paying.

Ivo's lantern hung at the far end, apart from the others, in a niche carved like an open mouth. Of course it was. The Steward kept its newest purchases where it could watch them.

It burned white. Not the white of clean snow — the white of a page before anything is written on it. Ivo's whole life, unwritten again.

Paloma reached the niche and stopped, because her hands were shaking, and a smuggler's first rule was *never touch the goods with shaking hands*. She pressed her palms flat against her thighs and breathed the way Master Corren had taught her: in for four, hold for seven, out for eight, until the tremor went down to a flicker.

"You're not stealing," she whispered to herself. "You're delivering. You're just delivering to the right person."

The lantern's hook was iron, and iron in this place remembered who it belonged to. She'd seen what happened when Sera touched a wall without asking permission — the glass had gripped her sleeve like a hand. So Paloma didn't grab. She coaxed. She worked her fingers under the hook's curve the way you'd slide a coin out of a sleeping man's pocket, slow as growing grass, and murmured the old guild words under her breath — not an oath, nothing binding, just the little courtesies wayfarers used to say to doors and locks and rivers. *Nothing taken that isn't given. Nothing broken that can be mended.*

The hook did not want to let go.

But the hook had never been sung to, either. And across the court, Sera shifted into the second movement of the lament, the part where the melody climbed and climbed looking for a note it never found, and the whole court sighed, and the iron — tired, ancient, listening too — loosened by a hair.

Paloma lifted the lantern free.

It weighed nothing and everything. Warmth came through the glass into her palms, and inside the flame leaned toward her hand like a cat toward a familiar lap, and she felt it *know* her, the way Ivo's fevered hands had known hers on the road, gripping her sleeve in the bad nights.

"Hello," she breathed. "I'm getting you out of here."

Behind her, small and clear, Pip began to sing.

Not the lament. Pip's own song — the one the child said every soul had, the one they'd heard humming since the steppe-edge village, tuneless-seeming but true. It wound around the lantern's light and wrapped it, muffled it, folded it into the music the way a smuggler folds contraband into a shipment of grain. To any waking eye, the light moving through the court would look like just another lantern swaying in the lament's draft.

Pip's voice didn't waver once. Twelve years old, moon-touched, orphaned twice over, and steadier than any guild master Paloma had ever served under.

*This is what family is*, she thought, absurdly, crouched beneath a stolen soul in a court of the dead. *This, exactly this.*

She turned back toward the fault — the crack in the world they'd descended through, now a gray seam high on the far wall — and got four steps before the music changed.

Sera's bow stuttered.

Just one note. One wrong note, tiny as a splinter, and Paloma knew instantly it wasn't Sera's doing, because Sera Voss did not play wrong notes even drunk, even heartbroken, even exiled. Something else had put that splinter in the song.

At the center of the court, the Pale Steward opened its eyes.

They were eyes like frost on a window — you could see shapes behind them, moving, but never clearly. Its head turned with the slow inevitability of a millstone, past Sera, past the swaying lanterns, and settled on the niche at the end of the row.

On the empty hook.

The lament died in Sera's strings.

"*Run*," Sera said, and her voice cracked across the court like a whip, and then she did the bravest, maddest thing Paloma had ever witnessed: she planted her feet, drew the bow one more time, and played *louder* — a wild, jagged reel, nothing like a lullaby, a song with teeth in it — throwing herself between the Steward and the rest of them.

Paloma ran.

The glass floor no longer cared about being quiet; it shrieked under her boots, every pane ringing alarm. Behind her the Steward rose to its full height, and it was taller than it had pretended to be, tall as the fault itself, and when it spoke its voice came from everywhere, from the walls, from the lanterns, from the iron hook still warm in her memory:

*"THE DEBT IS MINE."*

"The debt was paid!" Paloma shouted back, sprinting, the lantern held against her chest like a heartbeat. "You took his brother's bargain and called it fair — well, hear mine: *this soul was sold by a man who didn't own it!*"

The lantern flared in her arms. Even Ivo's soul, it seemed, agreed.

Pip caught up with her at the base of the fault, small hand grabbing her belt, and above them Sera's reel climbed into something desperate and glorious, buying seconds, spending them all. The Steward moved — glided, rather, its robes making no sound at all, which was somehow worse than sound — and the nearest lanterns guttered as it passed, souls flinching in their glass.

"Up!" Paloma shoved Pip toward the seam. "Climb! Don't wait for me!"

"I'm not leaving you." Pip's chin set in a way that was going to get them both killed someday. "Ivo said — he said we don't leave people."

*Ivo said.* Barely conscious for two days, and still giving orders. Paloma barked a laugh that was half sob and followed the child up the fault.

The climb was brutal. Handholds of shattered rock, glass dust cutting her palms, the lantern strapped now against her back with her bedroll straps because she needed both hands. Below, the reel cut off mid-phrase — a horrible silence — and then started again, ragged, slower. Sera was tiring. Sera was *retreating while playing*, backing toward the fault note by note, and the Steward was letting her, herding her, herding all of them toward the thinnest part of the seam where the thirteenth moon's light was already leaking down like water finding a crack.

Because that was the trap. Paloma saw it three body-lengths up and her stomach dropped. The moonrise would reach the fault before they cleared it. And everything the Steward owned, it owned *by moonlight* — Sera had told them that on the road, spitting the words like a curse. Under the thirteenth moon, the debt would reassert itself. The lantern would simply — return. Distance meant nothing here. Ownership meant everything.

Unless the ownership broke first.

Her oath. The thought arrived whole, cold, complete, the way the worst truths do. *A binding of equal weight.* The Steward hadn't been speaking in riddles. Her guild oath — the last living thread to Thornfell, to Corren, to every dead mentor whose graves she'd sworn she'd honor by carrying the guild forward — that was the only thing on her or Ivo heavy enough to balance a soul.

"Not yet," she gasped, climbing. "Not yet, not yet—"

Light bloomed below.

The thirteenth moon crested the steppe's edge and poured itself down the fault, silver and absolute, and Paloma felt the lantern shudder against her spine like a living thing hearing its name called. The strap pulled taut. The glass grew hot. Somewhere behind her the Steward's voice filled the shaft, patient as stone:

*"IT RETURNS TO ME."*

"No," said a voice above her.

Ivo.

He was lying flat at the lip of the fault, gray as ash, held up by nothing but will and Pip's earlier pushing, and he reached down with both hands — the hands of a dying man, the hands of a brother who had sold his own soul and regretted it with his final strength — and gripped hers.

"It doesn't belong to him," Ivo said, and his voice was wrecked and certain. "And neither does she. *Pull.*"

Paloma pulled. Ivo hauled. Pip braced against a rock and sang louder than Paloma had ever heard anyone sing, a raw wordless thing that drowned out the Steward's voice entirely, and the lantern screamed heat against her back and then—

Then Sera was there, scrambling over the lip, dropping to her knees beside Ivo, adding her hands, adding her weight, four people pulling against a mountain's claim, and the moonlight reached for the lantern and found—

Found nothing to hold. Because the light wasn't in the lantern anymore. In the last wrenching moment it had passed, silent as snowfall, out of the glass and into the space between all their gripping hands, unowned, *shared*, and the Steward's cry behind them was not rage.

It was confusion. The court beneath the mountain had never once, in all its centuries, encountered a debt split four ways.

The fault spat them out onto the steppe like a wave finishing with a boat.

* * *

They lay in a tangle on the mirror-flat plain, gasping, the moon enormous above them, and for a long moment nobody moved. Then Ivo rolled over, pressed his palm flat against the ground, and laughed — a real laugh, rusty and disbelieving.

"I can feel the cold," he said. "Do you understand? I haven't felt cold in *years*."

Paloma sat up. The lantern lay beside her, empty, its glass clouded over like a closed eye. Her hands were bleeding. Her back was screaming. And high up on the mountain's flank, at the mouth of the fault, something pale and vast had emerged and stood watching them, patient as winter.

It raised one long arm. Not in threat. In summons.

*"THE OATH,"* the steppe itself seemed to whisper, the glass plain carrying the words up through her knees, her spine, her teeth. *"YOU KNOW WHAT REMAINS UNPAID, WAYFARER."*

Paloma looked at her family — Sera cradling her cracked instrument, Pip leaning into her side, Ivo breathing deep and alive and staring at the stars like a man reading them for the first time — and stood up.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "I know."

She walked out onto the glass to meet it.
