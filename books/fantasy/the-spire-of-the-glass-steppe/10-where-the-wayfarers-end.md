## 10. Where the Wayfarers End

The glass steppe held the moon the way a cupped hand holds water.

Paloma walked out onto it alone, and the plain carried her reflection ahead of her — not her face, but her oath. She saw it in the mirror-flat surface as she had seen every regret on this crossing: a small gold thread knotted at the center of her chest, glowing faintly, frayed at one end where Thornfell had burned.

The messenger waited for her at what seemed like the middle of nowhere, though on the Glass Steppe there was no middle and no edge, only distance that agreed to end wherever you needed it to. It looked like a woman made of frost and lamplight, wearing the shape of someone Paloma had once known — because the mountain court always wore shapes you would follow without question. Tonight it wore the face of Master Ellery Vane, dead three winters, kind-eyed and patient as ever.

"That's cruel," Paloma said.

"It is efficient," said the messenger with Ellery's voice. "You would have followed a stranger only halfway."

"Then be efficient somewhere else. I'll follow it anyway." She stopped ten paces out and planted her boots the way she'd been taught — weight even, hands loose, nothing to grab. "You want the oath. Take it."

The messenger tilted its head. Behind them, far across the dark plain, she could hear Pip singing — high and clear and steady, the song that had masked a stolen soul's light all the way up the fault, still going now, still guarding Ivo's chest where his heart beat loud and stubborn and *his*.

"The Oath of the Wayfarers' Guild," the messenger recited, and each word landed on the ice like a struck bell. "*To guide the lost past every danger. To spend myself before I spend my charge. To hold the road when the road will not hold itself.* Sworn upon the last hearth of Thornfell. The final living bond of a dead order." Its borrowed eyes were gentle. "Do you understand what you surrender?"

"My guild dies tonight," Paloma said. "It died when the hall burned. This is just the funeral." Her voice caught, and she let it. "I'm the last one. If I keep the oath, it lives in me and nowhere else, and I can't pay your price and keep it both ways. So." She spread her arms. "Take it. All of it. The whole guild goes into the ground so he can walk out of yours."

"You understand there will be nothing left," the messenger said. "No order. No title. No binding. You will be an unsworn woman on an empty plain."

"Yeah." Paloma thought of an empty hall, one lamp, a dying master telling her the guild dies when its last oath does. She'd spent two years afraid of being the end of something. She hadn't noticed until this moment that endings were just doors with bad lighting. "I've been practicing."

The messenger raised Ellery's hand.

What happened next did not hurt. That was somehow worse. The gold thread in her chest simply *unknotted* — gently, the way a mother loosens a child's grip — and lifted out through her ribs and into the frost-bright air, trailing light, and Paloma felt every mile she had ever guided leave her at once. Every pass. Every hidden ford. Every traveler delivered, every promise kept, every night she had held the road when it wouldn't hold itself.

The Wayfarers' Guild ended on the Glass Steppe under a waning moon, and its last act was the one it had been sworn for all along: getting someone home.

The messenger closed its borrowed fingers around the thread of light. For a moment it looked almost sorry. Then Ellery's face came apart into frost, and the frost into moonlight, and the moonlight went down into the glass, and Paloma stood alone on the plain with nothing binding her to anything at all.

It felt like falling. It felt like standing very still.

She heard footsteps behind her — uneven, human, alive — and then Ivo Serrano's arms came around her from behind, careful, as if she might break, which was ridiculous, because she was fairly sure she was the only thing on this plain that hadn't.

"You didn't have to," he said into her hair.

"I know." She turned in his arms. His breath fogged in the cold, real and warm and *his*, and beneath his jaw his pulse jumped quick and strong, and Pip had been right about the song: it wasn't finished anymore. It had verses now. It had a terrible amount of chorus left. "That's what makes it worth doing."

Sera limped up with her cracked fiddle slung across her back, and Pip arrived at a dead run and hit Paloma's legs like a small cannonball. For a while nobody said anything. The moon went on setting. Somewhere below their feet, behind a door of glass and patience, a Pale Steward sat with a payment it could never spend, because an oath was not a soul and could not hang in any lantern, and it had bought the one thing in creation that was already free.

* * *

They buried the map at dawn.

Paloma dug the grave herself, in the one place on the steppe where the glass gave way to honest dirt — a seam of grey soil near the fault's mouth, soft enough to open with bare hands. The map had gone still. She'd checked it every hour since they'd climbed out of the court, half expecting the living ink to start crawling again, rewriting itself toward some new errand. But it lay quiet in her pack like a sleeping animal, its lines settled at last, its destination reached.

"It's finished," Pip said, crouched at the graveside. The child's head cocked, listening. "It's not even a map anymore. It's just... paper that remembers being useful."

"That's most paper," Sera said.

Paloma laid it in the earth. Thirteen turns of the moon, drawn and redrawn in ink made from something alive, guiding four people who had no business finding each other across half a broken country. She thought about saying words over it. An old guild rite, maybe — *the road remembers, the road lets go*. But the guild was gone, and the rites belonged to it, and maybe that was fine. Maybe some things should be buried with their oaths.

So instead she just said, "Thanks for the worst job I ever took," and shoveled the dirt back on top.

Ivo put a flat stone on the mound. Sera tucked a sprig of something — some steppe weed that had no business flowering in winter but did anyway — beside it. Pip patted the stone twice, the way you'd pat a dog, and announced that it was a good map and had done well.

Then they stood there in the cold morning light, four people with nowhere in particular to be, and Paloma realized she was waiting for grief to arrive and it wasn't coming. What came instead was stranger: space. Room in her chest where the gold thread used to sit, empty and aching and *hers*, unclaimed by any order, any master, any vow.

"So," Ivo said. "What does a person do with no oath?"

"Honestly?" Paloma blew out a breath that steamed. "No idea. Never been one before."

"I hear it's common," Sera said dryly. "Most of the world manages it daily."

Pip tugged her sleeve. "We could make a new one."

"We're not making an oath, Pip—"

"Not an *oath*. A *place*." The child pointed east, along the seam of dirt, where the steppe began its long climb toward the passes Paloma knew better than anyone alive. "Right here. Where the road forks. Everyone who comes over the mountains has to come past this spot, and there's nothing here, and travelers die of nothing all the time." Pip shrugged, matter-of-fact, the way children deliver verdicts. "We could be the something."

Nobody answered right away. The wind moved across the glass and made it sing, low and clear, the same note Pip had been holding for three days straight.

And Paloma looked at the seam of soil, and the fork of roads, and the three ridiculous people she had collected against every rule of her profession — a smuggler's instinct said *travel light, owe nothing, stay moving* — and found that the instinct had gone quiet. Unemployed. Along with everything else.

"Well," she said. "Someone has to know the passes."

* * *

Spring came to the steppe the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once.

By then the waystation had walls, mostly. A roof, partially. A great iron stove salvaged from a toll-keeper's station that nobody had come back to claim, and a sign that Ivo carved himself and hung above the door on the first warm day:

**THE LAST HEARTH**

*Travelers welcome. Guides available. Music nightly.*

He'd argued about the name — said it was too heavy, too much memory — but Paloma had insisted, because the guild's last hearth had burned at Thornfell, and hearths were meant to burn. That was the whole point of them.

The first caravan came through in the third week of thaw: six wagons, nervous drivers, a merchant who'd heard rumors of a guide on the steppe who knew the hidden passes and charged fair rates. Paloma led them over the mountains and back in nine days and returned to find Sera giving a concert to an audience of two mules and Pip, who was conducting with a stick. Ivo was cooking. He cooked the way he did everything now — like a man who had been handed his life back secondhand and intended to wear it out completely.

At night, when the stove ticked and the wind sang on the glass outside, Paloma sometimes caught herself reaching for the knot in her chest that wasn't there anymore. Old habit. She'd find nothing, feel the hollow ache of it — and then Pip would lean against her shoulder, or Ivo would slide a bowl into her hands without asking, or Sera would strike up something fast and foolish, and the ache would fill with something else. Not duty. Not oath. Just them, showing up, again and again, needing no binding to do it.

Family, it turned out, was not inherited. It was built, board by board, meal by meal, night by cold night, by people who kept choosing each other after every reason to choose anything else had been stripped away.

One evening near midsummer, a knock came at the door. A girl, sixteen maybe, travel-worn and hollow-eyed, standing in the light of the Last Hearth with a folded paper clutched in her fist — an old map, ink-stained, its lines half-faded.

"They told me on the road," the girl said, "there was a wayfarer here. Someone who guides the lost."

Paloma looked at her — at the storm-soaked cloak, the desperation, the map held out like a plea — and felt the ghost of a gold thread tighten sweetly around a heart that owed nothing to anyone.

She stepped aside and opened the door wide.

"That's me," she said. "Come in. Tell me where you're going."
