## 1. Charms for the Quiet Dead

The dead came to Felix Dunmore the way rain came to Greyhollow: quietly, constantly, and mostly unwelcome.

He had learned to work around them the way a miller works around water — letting the current turn his wheels without ever once stepping into it. By lamplight, in the narrow back room of his shop on Candlemaker's Row, he carved. Knife to bone, bone to charm: knuckle-bones of oxen for travelers who wanted sure footing, river-otter ribs for young wives hoping against hope of an easy birth, the long pale finger-bones of crows strung on waxed thread for children who swore they heard things under their beds and, being children, were usually right.

Felix never told them what else the charms did.

He never told them that when he pressed his thumb into the hollow of a carved piece, something pressed back. That the dead gathered to bone like moths to a lit window, and if you knew how to listen — and Felix had never been given any choice about knowing — they whispered. Not words, exactly. More like the memory of words, worn smooth as sea glass. *Cold. Lost. My name was—* And then nothing, over and over, until he carved them quiet.

That was the craft no one paid him for. Each charm he finished got a second pass with the knife, small marks hidden in the scrollwork, and each mark was a door held open just wide enough for one voice to slip through and be gone. The old woman across the street called his shop's little brass bell "the sweetest sound in Greyhollow," because customers always left lighter than they arrived. She didn't know why. He made sure she didn't.

Because magic was outlawed. Had been outlawed since before Felix was born, since the Wardens' Edict came down from the citadel like frost across glass: *no craft of the unseen, no speech with spirits, no traffic between the mortal world and the Court.* They said the Ninefold Court had abandoned mortals first. They said the border wards were there to keep the fae out. Nobody said what the wards kept in.

Felix kept his head down and his knife busy and answered the dead only in whispers, only at night, only ever one voice at a time. It was not much of a life. But it was his, and it was quiet, and he had arranged both facts with great care.

* * *

On the evening the world ended — though he would not know that yet, would spend another hour believing it was only an ordinary evening gone slightly wrong — Felix was working on a commission for Mistress Odile Fenwick, seventy-one years of her, widow of a cooper, keeper of a garden that bloomed out of season because she talked to it.

She had come to him three days ago with a shawl drawn tight and a look he recognized: the look of someone carrying a sound they couldn't put down.

"My husband," she'd said. "Forty years gone, Mr. Dunmore, and lately I dream of him standing at the foot of the bed, and he's not *sad*, mind, he's — insistent. Like a man trying to remember where he left his keys." Her hands had twisted in the wool. "I'm not mad."

"No," Felix had said. "You're not mad."

He hadn't asked what the dream said. He didn't need to. When she left, he took down a piece of swan bone he'd been saving — dense, old, patient stuff — and began to carve.

Now, by the light of two tallow candles, he turned the finished charm in his fingers. A small oval, no bigger than a thumbnail, worked with nine interlocking arcs. His knife found the hidden channels. His thumb settled into the hollow he'd made at its heart.

And the dead came, as they always did.

Not Odile's husband — not yet. First the usual congregation drifted close, the way beggars drift toward a bakery's warmth. *Cold,* sighed a voice that had been cold for two hundred years. *Lost,* agreed another, younger, wetter, drowned somewhere in the Greywater. *My name was—* began a third, and Felix breathed the small breath, the practiced one, and began the quieting.

It was delicate work, listening without holding. You let the voices wash the way you'd let cold water wash a burn — acknowledging, easing, never gripping. He murmured the soft syllables his grandmother had taught him before the wardens took her, syllables with no meaning in any living tongue, and one by one the whispers thinned and smoothed and slipped away through the little doors in the carving.

*Thank you,* said the last one, always the last one, some child or childlike thing that had followed Felix longer than he could remember. *Thank you, thank—*

Then everything stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. The way a heart stops — not a winding-down but a severance, silence arriving all at once like a slammed door.

Felix's thumb was still in the charm's hollow, and through it, faint as a candle seen through a wall, one voice remained. Old. Female. Warm as bread and utterly terrified.

*Mend what was broken,* it said. *Boy. Carver. Mend what was broken, mend it now, before the—*

The voice cut off mid-word.

Every hair on Felix's arms stood up. The candles didn't gutter; they *bent*, both flames leaning east toward the wall, toward the street, toward the hills beyond Greyhollow where the old ward-stones slept under moss and sheep-cropped grass, as if blown by a wind that existed nowhere in the room.

"Mistress Fenwick," Felix said aloud, absurdly, to the empty shop. "I'll have this ready tomorrow."

His own voice sounded wrong. Thin. As if the room had grown larger around it.

He set the charm down. He picked it up again. His hands, which had not shaken since he was nine years old and a warden nearly caught him listening at a grave-fence, were shaking now.

Outside, the evening bell rang six o'clock, and its note went strange halfway through — flattened, dragged, like a bell rung underwater. A dog began to howl near the tanneries. Then every dog in Greyhollow began to howl, a rising chord of it, and beneath the howling Felix heard something he had spent his whole life hearing only in fragments:

All of them. Every voice. At once.

The dead of Greyhollow — the drowned and the buried and the forgotten, the ones he'd quieted and the thousands he'd never reached — were not whispering anymore. They were *saying*. One word, one name, one warning, layered in ten thousand throats:

*Broken. Broken. Broken.*

Felix stumbled back from the workbench. The swan-bone charm lay on the scarred wood, and it had begun — he watched it happen, helpless — to glow. Not bright. A pale, pearly light, like moonlight remembered rather than seen, seeping up out of the carved arcs one by one. Nine arcs. He counted them with his eyes because counting was a thing a sane man did. All nine were filling with light that belonged to no candle and no star.

"Grandmother," he whispered, which was a prayer, though he'd have denied it under torture.

He ran to the front of the shop and shoved open the shutters.

Greyhollow sprawled below Candlemaker's Row in its usual evening clutter — chimney smoke, market stragglers, lamps kindling window by window. Ordinary. Almost ordinary. Except that everyone in the street had stopped moving. A fishwife stood with a herring half-gutted in her hands. Two boys froze mid-game, their knucklebones hanging above a chalked square. Every face was tilted upward.

Felix looked up.

The sky was going out.

Not clouding — *going out*, the stars winking away in no order he could read, constellation by constellation, as if someone high above were pinching flames between invisible fingers. The Two Sisters vanished. The Shepherd. The Long Keel that every sailor in Greyhollow steered by. Darkness poured down between the dying lights, a darkness with texture to it, weight, like velvet laid over the face of the world.

And along the eastern hills, where the old ward-stones slept, a line of blue fire erupted — the border wards, the great unbroken circle no living soul had seen blaze in four hundred years — flaring once, magnificently, like a struck match.

Then shattering.

Felix felt it go. There was no other word for it. Something vast tore along a seam running from the hills down through the earth, through the roots of the city, through the soles of his feet and up his spine, and every bone in his body — every *carved* thing within a mile, he'd learn later — screamed. The fishwife dropped her herring. The boys' knucklebones hit the ground and cracked, all of them, simultaneously, into powder.

In Felix's hand, the swan-bone charm burned cold. The nine arcs blazed full now, and through them, clear as a voice through a opened window, the old woman's presence spoke again — no longer afraid, somehow, but urgent, tender, the way you'd speak to someone standing on ice.

*Now you see it, carver. Now you see what we've been holding shut all these years with our small quiet voices. The covenant is broken. The door is open. And the dark behind the stars has noticed Greyhollow.*

"What do I do?" Felix said. Aloud. To a bone charm, in the middle of a street full of frozen strangers, with the sky dying overhead. Any warden patrol within earshot would hang him for less. He found he could not bring himself to care.

*Mend what was broken,* the voice said — and now he heard the shape beneath the words, the truth beneath the kindness. *But you cannot mend it alone, boy. No one alive can. Find the ones who remember. Find the spire.*

"The spire's a ruin," Felix said. "A story. It fell before my grandmother's grandmother—"

The charm went dark. Not silent — dark, its light withdrawing inward, banking itself like a coal covered for morning. He could still feel it, faintly, pulsing against his palm. Waiting.

Around him, the street exhaled. The fishwife moved. The boys snatched up their ruined game pieces and stared at the dust on their palms. Somewhere a shutter banged, and then the whole city seemed to wake at once — doors, voices, the first thin cries of alarm threading up from the harbor district where the sky had been darkest.

Above it all, where the stars had been, the darkness remained. Complete. Patient.

Felix Dunmore stood in his doorway with a glowing bone charm in his fist, listening to the dead of Greyhollow say his name — not *boy, carver*, anymore, but *Felix, Felix, Felix*, ten thousand voices converging like water finding a drain — and understood, with the terrible clarity of a man watching the floor give way beneath him, that his quiet life was over.

The first warden's horn sounded from the citadel hill, long and low, calling the patrols home.

Or calling them to hunt.

He didn't wait to find out which.
