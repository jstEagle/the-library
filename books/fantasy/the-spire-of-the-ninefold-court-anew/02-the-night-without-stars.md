## 2. The Night Without Stars

The stars did not go out one by one. They went out all at once, the way a candle dies when a door is flung open — and Greyhollow screamed.

Felix felt it first in his hands.

He had been closing the shop, wiping bone dust from the workbench with the particular tenderness he reserved for the end of a day that had gone well. Three charms sold. A fourth commissioned: a knucklebone pendant for a fishwife whose husband drowned two winters back and who only wanted, she said, to know if he'd made his peace. Felix hadn't told her what the charm whispered when he held it — *cold, cold, but not angry* — because some mercies were better delivered as silence. He'd carved her a river-stone instead, smooth and blank, and charged her for luck rather than grief.

Then the old woman's charm had spoken again. *Mend what was broken.* And before he could set it down, before he could even finish the thought *broken how*, every dead voice in Greyhollow had gone silent at once.

That was the true terror of it. Not noise — Felix had lived with noise for thirty years, the murmur of the interred rising through cobblestones like water through chalk. The silence was the wound. It was as if someone had reached into his skull and closed a door he hadn't known was open, and in the dark behind that door something enormous turned over.

He ran to the window.

The sky was wrong. Not clouded — *emptied*. Greyhollow's stars were thin things, city-dimmed, but they had always been there, the way lice are always there, unnoticed until absent. Now there was nothing above the rooftops. No star, no moon, no pale wash of light on the weathervanes. Just black, absolute and near, pressing down on the chimney pots like a lid on a pot about to boil.

And beneath the black, at the edge of the Lamplighters' Quarter where the old border stones stood in their crooked ring, the air had begun to shimmer. Not glow. Shimmer — the way heat rises off summer cobbles, except this was winter, and the shimmer bent the wrong way, inward, as though the night itself were being poured down a throat.

Felix's hands were shaking. He looked at them — carver's hands, scarred, steady enough to split a wishbone without splintering it — and watched them shake, and understood with terrible clarity that whatever he had heard in that old woman's charm had not been an echo.

It had been a summons.

The first scream came from Copperlane. Then another, closer. Then many, overlapping, the sound of a city discovering all at once that its skin had been peeled back.

He should have run. Later, in the long accounting he would make of this night, Felix would count the ten seconds he stood at his own window, watching the shimmer spread along the border stones like frost climbing glass, and mark them as the most honest ten seconds of his life. Because he didn't run. He picked up the old woman's charm from the bench, wrapped it in wool, tucked it against his heart where the whispering lived, and walked out into the starless street to see what had been broken — because someone had to hear it coming, and he was, apparently, the only one left listening.

* *

Greyhollow by lamplight was a city pretending hard at normalcy, and failing.

Doors slammed. Shutters banged down over windows. Somewhere a bell rang the alarm pattern — three, pause, three — except the ringer had lost his nerve halfway through and the rhythm dissolved into frantic clanging that meant nothing and everything. People ran past Felix carrying children, chickens, portrait frames, nothing at all, just running for the animal comfort of motion. A man in a nightshirt stood in the middle of Tanner's Row shouting scripture at the empty sky. Nobody listened. There was no sky to shout at.

The wards had fallen. Everyone knew it the way you know a tooth has cracked — not by seeing it, but by feeling the wrongness when you bite. For four hundred years the border stones had kept the Ninefold Court on its side of the night, and now the stones stood in their ring like pulled teeth, sockets bleeding light into air that shimmered and folded and occasionally showed, for one sickening heartbeat, glimpses of somewhere else. A forest of white trees under a violet sun. A bridge made of woven hair. A doorway standing alone in a meadow, open, patient.

And things coming through.

Felix saw the first one clearly near the corner of Gilders' Walk: a tall shape that might have been a heron wearing a man's coat, walking with terrible deliberation, its head swiveling in increments like a clock hand. It ignored the screaming woman it passed. It ignored Felix entirely. It had come here looking for something specific, and it moved through the panicked streets with the unhurried certainty of a creditor entering a debtor's house.

More followed. Small ones, skittering along gutter-tops, faces like folded letters. One vast thing that never quite resolved — Felix's eyes slid off it each time, leaving only an impression of antlers and a smell like a struck bell. They poured through the gaps in the ward-ring, and they were not rampaging. That was somehow worse. They were *arriving*. As though the fall of the wards were not a catastrophe but an invitation finally answered.

The dead, when they came back to him, were screaming.

It hit him in front of the chandler's shop — the silence tearing open and every voice in Greyhollow's soil flooding through at once, hundreds of years of the buried all talking over each other in raw panic. *The gate is open, the gate is open, they're coming through, we can feel them walking on our graves, boy, boy, listen, LISTEN—*

Felix staggered against the wall and pressed his palms over his ears, which helped nothing, because the voices had never come through his ears.

*I hear you,* he said inside the roar. *I hear you. One at a time.*

They didn't quiet. But they sorted themselves, the way they always did around him, oldest and loudest pushing forward, and the message assembled itself out of the chaos like bones knitting into a skeleton:

*Something old is awake under the spire. Something that remembers your name.*

His name. His. Felix Dunmore, bone-carver, seller of charms, keeper of small mercies — his name in the mouth of something old enough to remember a covenant he'd never heard of.

"Move."

A gauntleted hand closed on his shoulder and spun him around, and Felix found himself looking up at a warden-captain.

* *

Isolde Vane had spent nineteen years learning to read a crowd the way other people read weather.

Her patrol had swept down from the Wardenhouse within minutes of the first report — wards down, sky dark, things in the streets — and what she saw in the faces along Gilders' Walk frightened her more than the sky did. Panic she expected; panic was fuel, and wardens knew how to burn it. What frightened her was the *shape* of the panic. These people weren't fleeing something they'd seen. They were fleeing something they'd *felt*, a wrongness in the bones of the city, and Isolde had felt it too — a pressure behind her eyes since midnight, a sensation like standing too close to a struck drum.

The surge had a source. Her instruments said so. The resonance-plates in the Wardenhouse had lit up at the moment the stars vanished, every needle slamming east-southeast, triangulating on a single point in the Lamplighters' Quarter with a clarity Isolde had seen only twice in her career. Once had been a smuggled fae-lamp. Once had been a murder.

Both times, the source had died or burned.

She tracked it through the panicking streets, following the plates' afterimage in her mind, and arrived at a narrow shop with a bone-carver's sign creaking on iron hooks: DUNMORE. CHARMS, MEMENTOS, KEEPSAKES. The door hung open. Inside, a man stood braced against the counter as though the room were pitching in a storm, and when he turned, his face was the face of someone surfacing from deep water.

"Felix Dunmore." She put the name between them like a drawn blade. "Captain Isolde Vane, Greyhollow Wardens. You're under arrest for unlicensed magic."

"I haven't—" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse, and then said, quietly, absurdly: "Did you feel it? The silence?"

"The silence," Isolde said, "is the least of my concerns."

"It shouldn't be." His hands had come up — not in surrender, she realized, but cupped slightly, the way a man cups water. "Captain, the wards fell because something called through them, and I need you to listen to me very carefully, because the things coming through those gaps aren't raiders. They're *responding*. To a summons. And the summons used words I've never heard, in a voice I have heard, and it knows my—"

"Hands where I can see them."

He obeyed. And that was when it hit her.

Later, Isolde would struggle to describe it even to herself. It began as the pressure behind her eyes sharpening into pain, and then the pain opened, and the dead of Greyhollow poured through.

Every warden carried some sensitivity — it was why they wore the resonance-plates, why they took the tincture that dulled the nerve the Court's creatures walked on. Hers had always been modest. Manageable. Now it was a floodgate blown off its hinges. The dead of four centuries rose through the cobblestones of Gilders' Walk and crashed against her senses like a wave against sea-walls already crumbling, and she heard them — *all of them* — the drowned of the harbor, the plague-pits under Copperlane, the paupers' field, generations of Greyhollow shouting at once:

*The warden. The warden hears. Tell the warden—*

Isolde dropped to one knee. Her sword-arm buckled. The street tilted, the starless dark rushing up, and dimly, through the roaring, she registered that the bone-carver had caught her — this man she'd come to arrest, gripping her shoulders, saying urgently into her ear:

"They're mobbing you. You can hear them, can't you? Gods, you poor woman, you've been hearing them your whole life and nobody ever taught you how to close the door."

"*What*—" Her voice came out shredded.

"You're like me. Only nobody gave you the mercy of knowing." He said it without triumph, without accusation — just the flat grief of a man recognizing a wound he knew intimately on someone else. "I can teach you to shut it out. Later. Right now you don't want to shut it out, Captain, because they're trying to tell us both something, and I'm the only one in this city who can make it into words."

The wave crested. Broke. Receded — and in the trough of it, Isolde Vane, nineteen years a warden, hunter of illegal magics, breaker of hedge-witches and charm-sellers, knelt in the street holding the wrist of the very criminal she'd traced, and listened.

*Under the spire,* the dead said, in a thousand voices braided into one. *Something old is awake. It has waited out the long silences. It remembers the covenant. And it has chosen the carver.*

She looked up at Felix Dunmore. His face in the lamplight was hollow-eyed and afraid and absolutely certain, and Isolde understood, with the cold clarity of a woman watching her whole life reorganize itself around a new truth, that arresting him would be the most dangerous thing she could do — and that letting him walk free might be worse.

"The spire," she said hoarsely. "You mean the Spire of the Ninefold Court. That's a ruin. It's been dark for four hundred years."

"Yes," Felix said. "And tonight something in it said my name."

From the direction of the border stones came a sound neither of them had heard before — a horn, deep and sweet and wrong, blown from the far side of the fallen wards. The shimmering air along the ward-ring pulsed once, like a throat swallowing.

And out of the dark between two ruined customs-houses stepped a figure in a patched coat, half-lit by lantern-glow, with the unmistakable amber-slitted eyes of half-fae blood and a smuggler's grin that said she'd been listening to every word.

"Well," said the stranger. "This is going to cost somebody."
