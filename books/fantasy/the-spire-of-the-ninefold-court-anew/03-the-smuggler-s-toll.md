## 3. The Smuggler's Toll

Rook Ashvane smelled trouble before she saw it — had always been able to, a half-blood's curse or blessing, depending on the night. Trouble had a scent like struck flint and old honey, and it was pouring off the two figures huddled in the doorway of the Drowned Lantern as if they'd bathed in it.

The tavern was hers by habit, not deed. She kept the corner table with the wobbly leg, the one with sightlines to both doors and the cellar hatch, and she nursed the same cup of sour wine for an hour while the lamplight did its slow work of making everyone look guilty. Tonight everyone looked guiltier than usual. The city under the starless sky had the texture of held breath — Greyhollow's usual grime and gossip gone taut as drumskin, every door barred, every whisper about the Court.

And into that held breath walked a warden captain and a bone-carver who smelled of grave-dust and lilies.

Rook didn't move. She watched them cross the room — the woman first, tall, iron-grey braid, moving like someone who'd forgotten how to be anything but a weapon; the man behind her, young, hollow-eyed, his coat pockets clinking faintly with carved bone. He flinched at things no one else could hear. Rook knew what that flinch meant. Her mother had worn it too.

They stopped at her table. The warden set down a coin purse heavy enough to make the wood groan.

"Rook Ashvane," the warden said. "Captain Isolde Vane. We need the tunnels."

"Everyone needs the tunnels." Rook turned her cup a quarter-turn on the ring of spilled wine. "Nobody ever just *wants* them."

"The border wards fell three nights ago." The warden's voice was flat as slate. "Something came through. Something worse is coming. I need passage to the Ninefold Court, and you're the only smuggler alive who knows the ways under."

"And him?" Rook tipped her chin at the carver.

"Felix Dunmore." He said it himself, quiet, like an apology. Up close he smelled worse — not unpleasantly, but *thick*, layered, the way a churchyard smells after rain. A dozen dead pressed close around him; Rook's skin prickled with their nearness, though she couldn't hear them, only feel them the way you feel weather changing. "I have to reach the spire."

"You and half the beggars in this city want something from the spire."

"They're not beggars," Felix said. "They're— " He stopped himself. Swallowed. "There are voices. In my charms. One of them asked me to mend what was broken."

Rook went very still.

She had heard those words before. Not from the dead — from her mother, twenty-two years ago, the last morning of her exile, kneeling in the frost of their doorway with her hands pressed together as if in prayer or surrender. *Mend what was broken, little rook. That's all any of us were for.* Then the wardens had come, and her mother had walked out the gate without looking back, because looking back was forbidden to the exiled, and whatever her mother had done — whatever name she'd given up — she'd obeyed even that.

Rook drained her wine. It tasted like vinegar and decisions.

"Toll's doubled," she said. "Starless nights, doubled tolls. Smuggler's arithmetic."

"How much?" said Vane.

"Not coin." Coin she could get anywhere, and coin could be traced, and traced coin got smugglers hanged. She looked at Felix instead, at the clinking pockets, at the way the air around him seemed to lean in and listen. "Him. Whatever's in his head when we reach the spire — I hear it too. All of it. Everything the spire says, everything the dead say through him, word for word, no holding back."

Felix blinked. "Why would you—"

"That's the price. Take it or find another guide, and good luck, because there isn't one."

Vane's hand drifted toward her sword, the old reflex of a woman used to buying obedience with steel. Rook smiled at her with all her teeth. "Go on, Captain. Cut me down in a public house three days before the world ends. See how far your reputation gets you through a tunnel full of things that eat reputations."

"Rook." Felix's voice was soft, but it landed strangely, the way his words always seemed to land — as if someone else were helping him say them. "Why do you want to know what the spire remembers?"

Because her mother's name was carved in it. Because names in the spire weren't stone, they were *sentences* — sentences served, sentences commuted, sentences never finished. Because she had spent twenty-two years telling herself she didn't care whether her mother's sentence had ended, and lying to yourself for two decades leaves a scar shaped exactly like a question.

"None of your business, corpse-whisperer," she said. "That's the other part of the toll. You don't ask me why."

He held her gaze a long moment. His eyes were the color of pond ice, and behind them something shifted — several somethings, murmuring. Then he nodded once.

"All right," he said. "Deal."

*Rookie,* the dead must have thought. *Trusts so easily.* But it wasn't easy, Rook saw. It was exhaustion wearing trust's clothing. He'd been alone with his ghosts so long that a stranger wanting inside his head felt, to him, almost like company.

Rook stood, slung her satchel over her shoulder, and hated how much that small nod had cost her to accept.

The tunnels began beneath the tannery district, where the smell of lime pits covered the smell of everything else, including magic. Rook led them down through a hatch she'd bribed a tanner to leave unwatched, into passages older than Greyhollow itself — pre-warden work, some said, cut when the two realms still shared a wall instead of a wound.

The dark down here was honest dark, thick as wool. Rook lit no torches. Her fae blood saw fine in it — the world rendered in silver edges and heat-shimmers — and she liked her guides dependent, disoriented, forced to trust the sound of her voice.

"Rules," she said, her words bouncing off stone. "One: touch nothing that glows. Two: if I say *down*, you're down before you finish hearing it. Three—" she paused at a junction where three corridors breathed three different temperatures of air, choosing by instinct and old memory, "—nobody sings. The tunnels remember songs, and they sing them back wrong."

"What do you mean, wrong?" Vane's hand never left her sword-hilt. Even blind down here, the woman was a drawn blade.

"You'll know it when you hear it. It'll be your own voice, but it'll be singing something you've never learned." Rook stepped over a seam in the floor where the mortar was stained rust-red. Old blood, very old. "Walk where I walk. Exactly."

Behind her, Felix made a small sound. Not fear. Recognition.

"There are graves in these walls," he whispered. "So many. They've been waiting so long, they've forgotten they were people. They just... wait."

"Don't tell them that," Rook snapped. "Don't tell them anything. If they know you can hear them, they'll follow you, and I am not explaining a trail of grateful skeletons to whatever lives past the third gate."

"Something lives past the third gate?"

"I said don't ask questions either. New rule. Rule four."

But Felix had gone quiet in that listening way of his, head tilted, and Rook felt the temperature of the air change — felt something vast turn its attention toward them from far ahead, the way a sleeping thing turns toward warmth. She quickened her pace.

They walked hours. Vane counted steps aloud until Rook told her to stop, because numbers were also songs, in a way, and the walls were greedy. Felix stumbled twice and both times the dark seemed to catch him gently, which Rook chose not to think about. Once, far behind them, something began humming a lullaby in a voice like Vane's, and Vane's knuckles went white on her sword, and none of them sang back.

At the third gate — a slab of black basalt carved with nine interlocking rings, worn nearly smooth — Rook finally stopped and let them rest. She shared out water and hard bread without ceremony. It wasn't kindness. It was maintenance of assets.

Except that Vane took her share and, before eating, set it briefly on the ground beside Felix's, the way soldiers feed the dead in field camps. An old habit, half-superstition. And Felix looked at her — really looked — and said nothing, and something passed between the two of them that Rook pretended not to see: two people discovering they'd been carrying the same weight from opposite ends.

It made her chest hurt in a way she filed under *tunnels, bad air.*

"My mother walked this road," she found herself saying, before she could stop it. She cursed inwardly. Twenty-two years of silence and the starless dark pried it out of her in one night. "When the wardens exiled the covenant-folk. There was a whole line of them. Carvers, hearth-speakers, light-callers. My mother was—" She stopped. Picked at the bread. "Her name's in the spire. Exile names go in the spire. Everyone knows that. Nobody knows what happens to them there."

"Maybe the spire will tell us," Felix said quietly. "It seems to tell everything else."

"It tells *him* everything else. I paid for the privilege of eavesdropping, remember? That's all this is." She stood, brushing crumbs from her knees, retreating into transactional armor. "Rest's over. Gate's warded — old warden-work, ironically enough. Captain, your seal should open it. Consider it a discount."

Vane pressed her iron seal to the basalt. For a moment nothing. Then the nine rings shuddered and began to turn, grinding apart like a jaw remembering hunger, and cold air poured through — air that smelled of ash and honey and enormous, patient age. The air of the Court.

Rook stared into that darkness beyond, and her heart did something complicated and unwelcome.

Because rising from the opened gate, faint as memory, she could smell it: old honey and struck flint. Trouble. The same scent her mother had left in their doorway all those years ago, soaked so deep into the wood that no amount of sanding ever took it out.

And beneath the honey and flint, threaded thin as wire through the dark, she heard — actually *heard*, with ears that had never worked this way before — a voice. Faint. Patient. Coming from the direction of the spire.

It was singing. A lullaby. Correctly.

A lullaby only two people in all the worlds had ever known.

Rook's blood went cold, then hot, then cold again. Felix turned to her, pond-ice eyes wide, and she knew from his face that he heard it too, that the dead around him had gone suddenly reverent and silent, listening.

"Rook," he whispered. "Who taught you that song?"

She gripped her satchel strap until her knuckles burned.

"Nobody," she said, and stepped through the gate.
