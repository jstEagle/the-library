## 4. What the Spire Remembers

The tunnel smelled of wet stone and old iron, and it breathed.

Felix felt the breathing before he understood it — a slow swell and release in the air, like the lungs of something vast asleep beneath them. Rook walked ahead with a hand-lamp whose flame burned the wrong color, green-gold and steady, shedding no heat. Isolde came last, her warden's coat swapped for plain wool, though she still moved like a soldier, weight balanced, eyes counting exits that did not exist.

"Stay between my footfalls," Rook said without turning. "The tunnels under Greyhollow were dug by people who bargained with the Court for every stone. Some of those bargains are still being collected. Step wrong and you might pay them."

Felix kept his eyes on the smuggler's heels and tried not to listen.

That was the trouble, of course. He had spent twenty-six years learning how *not* to listen — how to fold the dead's whispers into a hum at the edge of hearing, like rain on a roof, like a neighbor's argument through thin walls. But since the night the wards shattered, the hum had become a chorus. The dead of Greyhollow pressed against his skull like moths against glass, all of them saying variations of the same thing: *go, go, go.* He'd assumed they meant run. Walking down into the earth toward a realm he'd only heard about in outlawed lullabies, he was beginning to suspect they meant *follow.*

"How much farther?" Isolde asked.

Rook's laugh was low and unamused. "Warden-captain asks the smuggler how much longer the illegal route takes. That's a new one."

"I'm not a captain anymore."

"You burned your seal in front of me. I watched it happen." A pause; the lamp swayed. "Doesn't mean the habit leaves you."

They descended in silence after that. The stairs were cut from black granite veined with something pale — bone, Felix realized eventually, or something very like it, threaded through the rock the way marrow threads a femur. His fingers itched to touch it. Carvers knew bone the way priests knew scripture, and this stone was *full* of residue, full of voices worn down to static. He kept his hands fisted in his pockets.

Then the tunnel ended, and there was no more not-listening to do.

* * *

They came out onto a hillside under a sky that had never been anyone's.

There were stars overhead — but they were not stars as Felix knew them. They hung too low, too large, each one a slow-turning lantern of colored fire, and some of them drifted when he wasn't looking directly at them, rearranging themselves like figures in a dream who resent observation. Below the hill spread a country of silver grass and black water, of trees whose leaves chimed faintly in a wind he could not feel. And rising out of it all, at the center of everything, the way a heart sits at the center of a body:

The spire.

It was dying. Felix saw that from a mile away, the way he could see death in anything — a charm, a bird, an old woman's hands. The spire stood taller than any tower in Greyhollow, slender and spiraled like a unicorn's horn carved from white stone, but its light was gone. Where it should have blazed, it held only a gray luminescence, the glow of coals an hour after the fire, guttering toward nothing. Cracks ran up its length like veins in marble. Around its base, far off, he could see structures — domes, bridges, gardens gone to ash-gray — the Ninefold Court itself, huddled close to the failing thing like children around a cooling hearth.

"That's it," Isolde said quietly. "Gods. In the warden histories it's always described as—" She stopped.

"Bright," Rook said. "Yeah. My mother used to say you could see it from the mortal side, before the wards went up. Said it looked like a second moon that had decided to stand still and be home instead."

Felix said nothing. He couldn't. Every dead voice in his head had gone silent at once — and in that silence, something else had begun. Not a whisper. A pull. Faint, directional, patient. Like a hook set behind his sternum, line running taut across a mile of silver grass, anchored somewhere in that gray stone.

"Felix?" Rook was watching him sideways. "You've got the look. The one you had in the shop when the old woman's charm started talking."

"It's calling," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened by awe. "Not the dead. The spire. It knows I'm here."

Isolde's hand went to her hip, found no sword where her seal used to hang, and dropped. "We don't know what it wants. Warden doctrine says—"

"Warden doctrine broke the covenant," Rook said. "Maybe try something else."

They crossed the silver grass while the false stars wheeled overhead, and the closer Felix came to the spire, the louder the pull became, until it was less a sensation than a fact, the way hunger is a fact, the way gravity is a fact. The Court's survivors emerged along the way — he caught glimpses of them, tall and lovely and wrong, faces like portraits painted by someone who had only heard descriptions of human beings. They did not approach. They watched from doorways and ruined arches with expressions he couldn't read, and one of them wept silently as he passed, tears running down a face too beautiful for grief, and Felix thought: *even here, even now, someone is mourning.*

At the spire's base, the white stone rose smooth and warm-seeming, and the cracks near the ground were wide enough to put a fist inside. Gray light seeped from them, pooling on the flagstones like spilled milk.

"Don't—" Isolde started.

Felix touched the spire.

* * *

The world went away.

He stood — or existed, or was remembered — in a hall made of dawn. Nine thrones ringed a circle of living flame, and upon them sat nine voices, and the voices were the truest part of them, because in this place bodies were afterthoughts. Four were fae, crowned in antler and starlight and river-mist and thorn. Five were mortal: a smith with burn-scarred forearms, a midwife with blood beneath her nails that never washed away, a shepherd boy who could not have been older than fifteen, an old man with a miller's stoop, and a woman whose face Felix somehow already knew, though he'd never seen it — a face like patience given flesh.

Nine voices, speaking one word together. He could not hear the word. He understood it anyway.

*Covenant.*

And then the memory turned, the way memories turn when you hold them too long, and showed him what the word had bought.

A border, gentle as a hem. Two realms folded close but not bleeding into one another, trading light for grain, music for memory, fae craft for mortal stubbornness. The spire burning bright above it all, fed by the nine voices freely given — not sacrificed, *given*, renewed each generation by choice, because a covenant sealed by force is just a cage with better manners. Mortals came to the Court openly. Fae walked Greyhollow's markets. The shepherd boy's descendants tended the flame. It worked. For three hundred years, it worked.

Then the wardens came.

Felix felt the breaking rather than saw it. He felt a decree signed in ink and fear — *magic is outlawed, the borders are closed, the Court is a lie told to children* — and he felt five mortal voices torn from their thrones one by one, not killed but *severed*, their names struck from the rolls, their gifts declared crimes. The smith's line. The midwife's line. The shepherd's. Each severance cracked the spire like a blow to a bell. And the four fae voices held on, held on, holding a bridge with fewer and fewer hands, until the Court itself grew proud and bitter and stopped reaching back, and the last mortal heir died in a Greyhollow alley with her gift unpassed on, and the flame went gray.

But the memory was not finished.

It narrowed. Drew in. The dawn-hall dimmed to embers, eight thrones empty and cold, and on the ninth throne — the throne of patience — something flickered. Faintly. Stubbornly. A voice that had refused, all these years, to fully let go, keeping one ember of itself banked in the dying stone the way a carver banks a coal overnight so the forge can wake again by morning.

And the ember noticed him.

Felix felt its attention turn upon him like a lamp swung in the dark, and he felt it *search* him — not his face, which it could not see, but something deeper, the shape of his gift, the texture of the dead voices he carried, the particular grain of his listening. It searched for a long moment. Then, in a whisper so quiet he felt it more than heard it, worn thin as paper, impossibly tired and unmistakably glad:

"*Felix Dunmore.*"

His own name. Spoken by a voice that had been waiting — how long? decades, centuries? — inside a dying stone.

"*Carver's son. Last listener. You took your time.*"

He tried to speak and had no mouth. He tried to ask *who are you* and had no tongue, but the question formed anyway, and the ember answered with something that was almost laughter, almost sorrow.

"*I am the ninth voice. The one they forgot to kill properly. And child — I need to show you the price written in your bones, before the regent shows you her version of it. Come closer. There is so little of me left, and so much you must—*"

Hands seized his shoulders. The dawn-hall ripped away like a curtain torn down, and Felix came back to himself on his knees in gray light, gasping, Rook's grip bruising his arms and Isolde shouting something about his pulse, his eyes, *your eyes were glowing, Felix, they were glowing gray—*

"The ninth voice," he rasped. "It's alive. One of them is still alive. It's been alive in the stone all this time, alone, and it—" He swallowed. His whole body was shaking. "It knows my name. It's been waiting for me. It says it has to show me something before the regent does."

Rook and Isolde exchanged a look over his head — the look of two women recalculating everything — and beyond them, up the long avenue of ash-gray gardens, torches were kindling one by one.

The Court had seen him touch the spire. The Court was coming to collect him.

And beneath his fear, beneath the shaking, the ember's whisper still pulsed faint and patient behind his sternum, like a second heartbeat that was not his own:

*Come closer, Felix Dunmore. We haven't much light left — and less time than that.*
