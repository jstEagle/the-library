## 5. The Court of Ash and Courtesy

The last stretch of Rook Ashvane's tunnel smelled like every home she'd ever been thrown out of: wet stone, cold iron, and the faint green rot of magic left too long unattended. She walked point with a hooded lamp, as she had for two days, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the altitude of the pass, the thin air of the deep ways beneath Greyhollow. It wasn't. It was the door.

She felt it before they reached it — a pressure like a hand laid flat against her sternum, gentle and absolute. The threshold-ward her mother had helped weave when Rook was small enough to ride in a supply sling, back when the tunnels were trade routes and not escape hatches. It still recognized her blood. Of course it did. Blood was the one thing exile couldn't burn out of you.

"Stop," she said, and Felix stopped so fast he nearly walked into her shoulder blades. Isolde Vane, three paces back with her hand nowhere near her sword because Rook had made her swear it wouldn't be, halted like a drawn bow.

"What is it?" Felix asked. His voice did that thing it always did — pitched low and careful, the way people talk in sickrooms. As if the dead were listening.

They were, probably. But not here. Not yet.

"It's me," Rook said — not to them. To the dark seam in the rock ahead, where the lamplight fell wrong, where shadow pooled like water finding its level. "Rook Ashvane. Daughter of Maerlin Ashvane, of the Ninth Voice." Her mouth wanted to stumble on the name. Twenty years of not saying it had built up scar tissue around it. "Open."

The seam opened.

Not dramatically. That was the worst part. The Court's magic didn't crash or blaze anymore; it *sighed*, the way an old house settles, the way a dying fire collapses into itself. Light came through the gap — real light, honey-colored, the first true sunlight any of them had seen since the stars went out over Greyhollow — but it came thin, guttering at the edges, like gold leaf laid over ash.

Beyond the door lay the Ninefold Court.

* * *

Rook had been seven the last time she stood on the terraces of the Court, and memory had kept it whole: nine spires ringing the great one like courtiers around a throne, gardens that bloomed in colors mortal eyes had no names for, bridges of spun glass singing in the wind. She had expected ruin. She had braced for ruin.

What she got was courtesy.

The Court was beautiful the way a corpse arranged for viewing is beautiful. Every rose still bloomed. Every fountain still sang. And everything — the marble underfoot, the leaves overhead, the light itself — had gone the color of old paper, drained to sepia and bone-white, gorgeous and bloodless. The gardens weren't dead. They were *embalmed*. Preserved by whatever dregs of power the spire still leaked, held in a perfection that had nothing living underneath it.

And the survivors came out to meet them as if nothing whatsoever were wrong.

They descended from the terraces in procession, forty or fifty of them, dressed in silks the color of the drained world — cream, ivory, ash-grey — and their faces were smooth and kind and utterly empty of surprise. A tall one in silver led them, and he bowed to Felix first, which told Rook everything about how news traveled here.

"The bone-carver," the silver one said. "The covenant's last thread. We have awaited you longer than you know."

"He's not a thread," Rook said. "He's a person, and he's under my protection until terms are settled."

The silver one turned his smile on her, and it was a lovely smile, and it contained absolutely nothing. "The smuggler. Yes. The Court remembers your family's service."

*Service.* Rook's teeth met hard enough that she heard it in her jaw. Her mother hadn't served the Court; her mother had *fled* it, carrying a half-fae infant through wards that flayed the skin off anyone who tried, and if the Court called that service then the Court could choke on the word.

Felix touched her elbow — barely, a featherweight, but he'd learned in two days exactly where she kept her temper and how close he could stand to it. She let him.

"I need to see the regent," she said instead. "Before anything else. Before hospitality, before ceremony, before any of the beautiful poison you're about to offer us. Terms first. That's how smugglers do business, and I'm the only one of us holding a contract."

Isolde made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

* * *

They gave them a guesthouse anyway — terms first or no. That was the Court all over: it would feed you whether you wanted feeding or not, would wrap you in comfort whether you consented or not. The guesthouse stood at the edge of the First Terrace, all pale wood and gauze curtains, and the meal laid out for them could have bought a Greyhollow street outright. Wine that tasted like summer remembered rather than lived. Bread warm as a held hand. Fruit Rook hadn't seen since childhood and had spent twenty years pretending not to miss.

Felix ate almost nothing. He sat with his cup untouched, head tilted slightly — his listening tilt — and after a while he said quietly, "There are voices here. Under the floor. Under everything. Hundreds. They're not... restless, exactly. They're waiting."

"Don't listen too hard," Rook said. "This place will use whatever you carry."

"So will yours," Isolde said, to Rook, without looking up from the blade she was cleaning. The disgraced warden had said maybe forty words since the tunnel mouth, but she missed nothing, and Rook was beginning to hate how much she'd come to rely on being watched by those grey eyes.

"My stake's my own," Rook said. "That was the toll."

"And we've honored it," Felix said mildly, which was his way of ending an argument — he simply declined to continue having it. He looked up at her across the table, and his eyes were tired and steady and far too kind for a man who'd spent his life being hunted for the thing that made him useful. "But if it helps — whatever name you're carrying into that hall tomorrow — you won't carry it alone. That's what this is now. In case nobody mentioned it."

Rook looked at him for a long moment. Then at Isolde, who pretended very hard to be interested in her whetstone.

"Go to sleep," Rook said, roughly, and went out onto the terrace alone.

Above her, where nine spires should have burned with nine colors of light, only darkness answered. The great spire stood at the heart of it all like a black needle through the world's fabric, and Rook found — to her fury — that looking at it hurt worse than looking away.

Her mother's name was carved somewhere in that stone. Maerlin Ashvane, of the Ninth Voice. One of the nine who had sealed the covenant between mortal and fae, whose voices had been woven into its foundations, who were supposed to sustain it with song until the end of the world.

Until mortals outlawed magic and the Court went silent, and the covenant starved, and one of its own voices decided the whole rotten arrangement deserved to die and took her daughter and ran.

*Chose exile,* Rook corrected herself. *Chose me.* That was what she'd built her whole life on — that the flight had been for her sake, that the flaying-wards and the hunger years and the smuggling runs through bleeding borders had all been the shape of a mother's love. Some days she believed it. Most days she believed it the way you believe in a bridge you've never tested.

Tomorrow she'd find out what the bridge was made of.

* * *

The regent received them at dawn in a hall of white glass, and Rook understood immediately why the survivors still called her *the regent* and never, ever her name.

She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful. Tall past mortal measure, robed in grey silk that moved like smoke underwater, crowned with a circlet of nine bare branches — the nine voices' sigil, stripped of everything green. When she spoke, her voice filled the hall without rising, soft as snowfall, and every word arrived already agreed with.

"You bring me the carver." Her gaze rested on Felix with an appetite so refined it took Rook a moment to recognize it as appetite at all. "The last soul who can hear the dead of both realms. Do you understand what you are, child?"

"A bone-carver," Felix said. His voice shook only a little. "I make charms. For grief. Mostly."

"Grief is the oldest currency," the regent said, "and you mint it. Yes. You will do."

"We haven't agreed to do *anything*," Isolde cut in, stepping forward, and Rook saw the warden's discipline fighting her revulsion and winning by inches. "We came to learn what reforging requires. Nothing else."

"What it requires," the regent said, "is a voice of the covenant. Nine sealed it; nine must speak to unseal and reseal. Eight of the nine are dust, their echoes long devoured by the silence." She paused, and something moved behind her face — not warmth, never warmth, but the ghost of triumph wearing warmth's clothing. "One remains. Preserved. Held beyond the reach of time's decay, in the stasis-vaults beneath this hall, against precisely this hour."

The floor did not tilt. Rook made sure of that. She kept her feet planted and her face carved smooth and her heartbeat dragged down by main force, while inside her chest twenty years collapsed into a single white-hot point.

"She means my mother," Rook said.

The regent regarded her with polite interest. "You are Maerlin's get. Yes. There is a resemblance — in the defiance, chiefly." A ripple of courteous laughter through the assembled courtiers, dry as moth wings. "The Ninth Voice sleeps below us, smuggler. Untouched by these twenty wasted years. Ready to rise and sing the covenant whole. You should be proud. Your blood is the hinge on which both worlds turn."

*Untouched.* Rook thought of her mother frozen mid-breath for two decades while her daughter grew up learning to hate her, learning to forgive her, learning both badly. Thought of the woman in Rook's earliest memories — laughing, fierce, smelling of rain and iron — reduced to an heirloom in a vault. An instrument. Held in reserve like coin.

"You'll want her consent," Felix said quietly.

Every eye in the hall turned to him. The regent smiled.

"The Court does not trouble sleeping voices with questions," she said. "It wakes them when they are needed. That has always been our way."

"Our way," Rook repeated. Her voice came out flat and strange, and she realized distantly that it sounded exactly like her mother's. "You mean you'll drag her out of that vault and pour her into a spell whether she chooses it or not. The same way you let the covenant starve. The same way you let the wards fall and the sky go black and my city fill with things that eat people." She stepped forward, into the regent's shadow, and let every year of resentment she owned stand up in her spine. "Here are my terms, regent. Nobody touches that vault until my mother wakes *choosing* it. Or I walk back through those tunnels, and I take the only man alive who can hear the dead with me, and the Court can keep its embalmed garden and its courtesy and starve politely forever."

Silence, total and glittering.

The regent studied her for a long, long moment — and then inclined her head, a fraction, gracious as a knife sliding home.

"Terms accepted," she said. "For now. The vault lies beneath us, smuggler. You may visit your mother whenever sentiment moves you. Perhaps you can ask her yourself what she would choose." The grey eyes gleamed. "Though I confess I have wondered, these twenty years, why a woman who loved the Court once — and loved her daughter more — chose to abandon both. Perhaps the waking will answer questions we did not know to ask."

Rook walked out of the white glass hall with her chin level and her hands shaking, and behind her she heard Felix say, low and urgent, her name — *Rook* — twice.

Because he had tilted his head. Because he was listening.

"Felix," she said. "What. What do you hear."

His face had gone the grey of the Court's drained roses.

"There's a voice down there," he said. "In the vault. And Rook — it isn't asleep. It's been awake the whole time."
