## 6. Warden's Confession

The fire in the smugglers' camp had gone down to coals, and Isolde Vane could not stop looking at her own hands.

Twenty years she had worn the warden seal — a disc of grey iron stamped with the nine-pointed star of Greyhollow's covenant, cold at first and then warm, always warm, as if it remembered something. She had sworn her oath on it at nineteen, kneeling on wet flagstones while High Warden Corvane laid his hand on her head and told her that order was a kindness, that law was a lamp held against the dark. She had believed him the way you believe the ground beneath your feet: without thinking, because the alternative was falling.

Now the sky over both realms was torn open, the stars were gone, and the ground itself had begun to lie to her.

She sat apart from the others. Rook Ashvane had taken the first watch at the tunnel mouth, all restless wings and narrowed eyes, pretending not to listen. Felix Dunmore slept by the fire with his back against his pack, bone charms strung across his wrists like a man wearing his sins openly. Even asleep he looked like he was listening.

Isolde turned the seal over in her palm. In the firelight the nine points of the star threw small shadows, nine thin blades pointing inward.

"You're going to do it," said Felix, without opening his eyes. "I can hear them arguing about it."

She went still. "Hear who."

"The dead." He opened his eyes then, and they were the color of river ice, old and tired and far too young all at once. "They've been following you since the shop. Since before the shop, maybe. They don't talk the way people talk. More like weather. But there's one that's been circling you all day, getting closer every time you touch that thing." He nodded at the seal. "It keeps saying the same word. *Corvane.*"

* * *

She should have lied to him. It would have been easy; lying to civilians about the nature of warden work was practically a chapter in the manual. But the dead boy sitting cross-legged behind Felix — the one only Isolde could half-see when the light failed, a smudge of shadow with eyes like drowned lamps — had been watching her for hours, and there was no lying to the dead. They already knew how you ended.

"Corvane was my mentor," she said. "High Warden Aldric Corvane. He built the patrols. He wrote the statutes that outlawed mortal magic. When I took my oath, I thought I was taking up a shield."

"And instead?"

Instead. She looked into the coals and let herself remember, which was the cruelest thing she had done in years.

"I was twenty-three. Newly made full warden. Corvane summoned me to his study above the Hall of Seals — the room where they keep the ledgers, the oaths, the old instruments. There was a door at the back I'd never seen open. That night it stood ajar, and through the gap I saw light. Not lamplight. It moved like water moves under ice, slow and gold and *alive*, and it came from below, up through the foundations, and Corvane was standing in front of that door with a length of copper pipe in his hands, and the pipe was glowing."

Felix had gone very quiet. Behind him, the dead boy leaned closer, hungry for the story the way the starving lean toward bread.

"He caught me watching. Most men would have shouted. Corvane smiled and beckoned me in, and he showed me — because I think, even then, he wanted a witness. He wanted someone to see what he'd done and call it necessary. The pipe ran down into the rock, into the roots of something enormous, and the light flowed up it and into the Hall's reservoirs, and from the reservoirs into the wards around Greyhollow." Her throat tightened. "He called it *tithing*. He told me the Court's spire was a parasite. That the old covenant wasn't a treaty between equals but a feeding arrangement — that the Ninefold Court grew fat on mortal grief, that every prayer the border folk whispered and every charm they burned and every death they mourned aloud was a meal for things with too many eyes. He said the spire drank our sorrow and gave back nothing but pretty lights in the sky."

"But that's not what the spire is," Felix said softly. "I touched it. I heard it. It remembers nine voices sealing it — mortal voices, fae voices, freely given, holding the border closed out of love, not hunger."

"Yes." The word came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh. "I know that now. Then, I believed him, because believing him was easier. Believing him meant the wardens were shepherds. So I asked the only question an honest young warden asks: *where does the tithe go?* And Corvane said, 'To keeping the city safe,' and I saluted, and I walked out, and I did not ask again for seventeen years."

The coals shifted. Somewhere in the dark beyond the fire, water dripped in the tunnels with the patience of a clock.

"Seventeen years," Felix repeated. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — wondering.

"The tithing never stopped. It grew. Every year another pipe, another siphon, another branch of the root tapped and bled. The spire dimmed a little more each winter and we told the border villages it was natural decay, ancient things fading, nothing anyone could mend. We outlawed mortal magic because magic *heard* the spire screaming, and we couldn't have that. Carvers like you, hedge-witches, bone-listeners — you weren't criminals, Dunmore. You were witnesses. You were the ones who might have stood in a market square and said, *the sky is being stolen.*" She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you know what the worst part is? The wards didn't fail tonight because the Court attacked. They failed because we drained the well dry. My order broke the covenant with a copper pipe and seventeen years of polite arithmetic, and everything that's come through the border since sundown is *our fault.*"

The dead boy made a sound — not a word, more a pressure, a cold hand closing around her wrist. Felix flinched, then nodded slowly, translating.

"He says you didn't know." Felix's voice was gentle in a way that hurt worse than anger. "He says the ones who knew are the ones who never sleep. He says—" Felix tilted his head, listening to something Isolde couldn't catch. "He says Corvane stopped sleeping eleven years ago."

Isolde's hands went cold. "When did you—"

"I didn't tell him anything. They talk to each other, the dead. They compare notes." Felix wrapped his arms around his knees. "Tell me about the night he died. Because there's a death hanging off you like a wet coat, Captain, and I think it's his."

* * *

She had never spoken it aloud. Not to the tribunal, not to her confessor, not to the mirror.

"There was a raid," she said at last. "Three years ago. A carver in the Lowmarket — an old woman, Maren Ostwick — was found teaching a girl to bind a hearing-charm. Standard violation. I led the arrest team myself. Corvane insisted on coming, which he almost never did anymore; he'd grown strange in his last years, pale as paper, always cold, always standing near the reservoirs like a man warming himself at a hearth. We broke down her door at dawn and she was waiting for us. Not frightened. Waiting. She looked straight at Corvane and said, *'You've been drinking from a grave, Aldric.'*"

Felix made a small sound. "Maren Ostwick. The charm — the one that begged me to mend what was broken — it was hers. Her granddaughter brought it to me two days before the wards fell."

"Then you've heard more of her than I ever managed to silence." Isolde pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "She resisted. Or that's what the report says. What actually happened: Corvane raised his hand and something came out of him — not warden craft, we're not permitted craft, that was always the hypocrisy at the heart of us — something older, something he'd learned from the pipes, from the light. It hit her and she fell, and she didn't get up, and Corvane stood over her body and said, very calmly, *'Witnesses.'* Like it was a procedure. Like I was a procedure."

"And you covered for him."

"For three days. Then he summoned me to the study again, the same room, and he was thinner than I'd ever seen him, translucent almost, lit from inside by that stolen gold, and he said, 'Isolde. The tithe is failing. The spire is dying faster than we can drink. When it goes, the border goes, and everything the Court has swallowed will come back through. I need you to help me tap the root directly.' And I understood — finally, stupidly, completely — that he hadn't been protecting Greyhollow for seventeen years. He'd been *feeding on it*. The light was in him. It had changed him. And he was going to crack the last seal on the world to keep his furnace lit."

Her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"So I refused him. And he reached for me the way he'd reached for Maren, and I put my blade through his chest, and the light poured out of the wound like smoke from a snuffed candle, and it screamed. It *screamed*, Dunmore, in a voice made of everyone's grief it had ever drunk, and then it was gone, and I filed the report that said he'd died defending the city from a rogue carver, and I got his office, and I have not slept a whole night since."

Silence. The fire ticked. Rook, at the tunnel mouth, had turned around somewhere in the telling and was watching with an expression Isolde couldn't read — something between contempt and kinship.

"That's why the regent knows your name," Felix said quietly. "That's why the Court let you walk in. You're not just a warden to them. You're the woman who killed their thief."

"I'm the woman who spent three years guarding the pipes he left behind." She lifted the seal. Up close, in the dying firelight, she could finally see what she had refused to see for two decades: the nine-pointed star was not a star at all. It was a sigil of containment. Nine points, nine taps, nine wounds in the spire's root, rendered in iron so no one carrying it would ever look closely enough to recognize a cage. "Every oath I ever swore was sworn on this. Every arrest. Every carver I dragged into the cells. Every charm I confiscated and fed into the furnaces." Her hand did not shake. She had trained it too well for shaking. "Loyalty isn't a virtue if the thing you're loyal to is a wound."

She stood. From her belt pouch she took the flint and steel she used for lighting signal-lamps, and Rook said, sharply, "Captain—" and Felix said nothing at all, and the dead boy drifted forward to watch with his drowned-lamp eyes wide.

Isolde Vane set her warden seal on the flat stone beside the fire, knelt, and struck spark against it until the iron glowed. It did not burn easily. Twenty years of oaths hold heat the way stone holds the day's sun. She held the flint to it anyway, and fed it kindling-thoughts: Maren Ostwick's face at dawn. The girl in the Lowmarket learning a hearing-charm. The gold light screaming as it died. The stars going out one by one over a city she had sworn to protect from exactly this.

The seal cracked down its nine lines all at once, like ice letting go of a lake in spring, and the pieces reddened, curled, and went to ash that smelled — she would swear this to her grave — like starlight.

She stayed kneeling a moment longer. The dead boy came and stood beside her, close enough that the cold of him soaked through her cloak, and for the first time in three years the cold felt less like judgment than company.

"It doesn't absolve you," Felix said quietly. "Burning it."

"I know."

"But it changes what you're carrying it *for*."

"Yes." She rose, ash drifting from her fingers, and looked toward the tunnel mouth, toward the black road that climbed toward the Court of Ash and Courtesy, toward a spire whose light her order had stolen and a regent who hoarded what remained. "I can't give the spire back seventeen years of light. But I know where every remaining siphon is buried — every pipe, every reservoir, every hidden tap between here and the root-vaults. If we're going to reforge this covenant, we do it with the veins open and bleeding, and the regent will use them against us unless someone who helped dig them walks ahead with a hammer."

Rook pushed off from the tunnel wall, wings rustling, and her grin had teeth in it. "Well. Now *that's* the most useful confession I've heard all week, Captain. The regent's expecting a broken warden and a terrified carver." Her eyes gleamed. "Shall we go disappoint her?"

But Felix had gone rigid by the fire, head cocked, listening to something in the dark that made the color drain from his face.

"Captain," he said. "The dead are saying the root-vaults aren't empty anymore. Someone's awake down there. And she's asking for Rook."

"Rook?" Isolde frowned. "Asking for *Rook*?"

Felix swallowed. "By the name on the spire."
