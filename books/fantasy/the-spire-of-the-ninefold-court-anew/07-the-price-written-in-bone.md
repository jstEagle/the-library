## 7. The Price Written in Bone

The spire's voice came to him not as sound but as cold.

Felix had spent his whole life hearing the dead as whispers at the edge of things — a moth-wing flutter in a carved charm, a sigh caught in the grain of old bone. But this was different. This was the voice of the spire itself, and it did not speak so much as *remember* him, the way winter remembers a river it once froze. He sat where Rook had left him, cross-legged on the root-vault floor beneath the darkened tower, and the voice poured into him like water finding cracks in stone.

*Felix Dunmore,* it said, though his name felt older when the spire said it — as if it had been waiting for that particular arrangement of syllables since before Greyhollow had a name at all. *Bone-carver. Listener. Last thread.*

"I'm not the last of anything," Felix said aloud, because talking kept the fear from setting like mortar. "I sell charms for coppers. I hide from wardens. That's all."

*You are what remains,* the spire said. *And you have come to learn the price.*

Around him, the root-vault breathed. The walls were grown, not built — pale lattice-work of bone and living wood braided together, veined with channels that had once run silver with light. Now they held only a faint gray luminescence, the glow of embers an hour past dying. Somewhere above, Isolde stood watch at the vault's mouth, her burned-out seal still warm in her fist. Somewhere beyond that, Rook argued terms with a regent who smiled too beautifully. And somewhere in between — everywhere, really — were the dead he'd carried for twenty years, patient as coins in a pocket.

*Show me,* Felix said. It was not courage. It was simply that dread, given long enough, curdles into something almost like readiness.

The spire showed him.

* * *

He saw the covenant as the spire remembered it: nine voices standing in a circle where the tower's heart now stood, mortal and fae and something between, their hands joined over a seed of light. He saw them speak the terms into being, each word a thread, each thread a law:

*A life freely given.* Not taken. Not bargained. Not stolen from another by trick or force. Given — wholly, knowingly, gladly if it could be managed. The light of the spire was nothing more than that gift, burning slowly across centuries, warming two realms against the dark between them.

And when the mortals outlawed magic and turned their faces away, and the Court stopped answering out of pride and grief, the gift had no hands left to renew it. The flame had eaten down to its last century. Its last decade. Its last season.

*It has chosen you,* the spire said, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. *Not for your blood, nor your craft. Because you listen. A covenant must be spoken by one who can hear all nine voices — and you carry more dead than any soul now living.*

Felix's hands had gone numb in his lap. He looked at them — carver's hands, nicked and scarred, stained faintly gray at the fingertips from years of bone dust — and thought: *these are the hands it wants.*

"How long," he asked, "do I have?"

*Until the sky tears,* said the spire. *Days. Perhaps fewer.*

* * *

They began teaching him that night.

Not the spire — the dead. His dead, rising up through the channels of the vault like warmth returning to a house, gathering around him in the dark where Isolde couldn't see and Rook didn't yet know to look.

First was Maren Hollis, whose finger-bone charm Felix had carved eleven years ago in a winter so cold the harbor froze gray. She'd been a net-mender, drowned off the Teeth in a storm, and she had stayed in her charm ever since because her daughter still wore it and Felix could never bring himself to tell him to let go. Her voice settled over him now like a shawl.

*The first lesson,* she said, *is that giving is not losing.*

She showed him. In the memory-space the dead could build between them, she took him back to the storm, to the moment the wave took her — except in this remembering, she was not afraid. She had seen her daughter's face on the dock that morning and thought, *I have been given thirty-one good years, and I will spend this last one buying her a longer life,* because the boat she'd tied fast instead of abandoning was the boat that carried six others home. The gift had been hers before the sea ever touched her. The sea only collected it.

Felix came out of the lesson shaking, and colder than he'd been.

That was the thing about the lessons — the cost of them. Each one the dead taught, they taught by *emptying*, pouring some measure of themselves into him so he would understand not with his mind but with his marrow. And each measure they gave, he lost. Not their voices — those remained, thin and faithful. Their *warmth*. The particular heat of a remembered person, the thing that made hearing Maren feel like sitting near a stove rather than reading a letter from a stranger.

After her lesson, when he reached for her inside the charm, she was there, but dimmer. Farther away. Like a lamp moved to another room.

"Stop," he whispered to the dark vault. "I won't do this. I'm not going to learn by spending you."

*Then you will fail,* said the spire, gently. *And both realms will bleed together until there is neither.*

* * *

Old Sefa came next — the woman from the shop, the one whose charm had begged him to *mend what was broken* on the night the stars went out. Her bone was ash-gray now, cracked clean through from the surge that had shattered the wards; he'd carried the pieces wrapped in wool against his chest ever since, refusing, in some stubborn animal part of himself, to accept that she was gone entirely.

She was not gone entirely.

*You always did argue with your elders,* she said, and even diminished, even stretched thin as smoke, her voice had the same wry crack it had in life, when she'd haggled him down three coppers on a luck-charm and then paid full price anyway because she said honest work deserved honest coin.

"Sefa. Please. There has to be another way—"

*There is always another way,* she agreed. *And every other way ends with Greyhollow under the Court's tide, and the Court under its regent's thumb, and children born in a world with no stars to wish on. Sit down, boy. I've been dead four months. Let me be useful at it.*

Her lesson was the hardest. She taught him the shape of the final word — the word that would seal the covenant, the word that had to be spoken *while* the life was given, so that the giving and the speaking were one act, indivisible. To teach it she had to give him the memory of her own death, whole: the fever, the small room, the terrible clarity of the last hour when a person takes account of everything they are leaving and finds, if they are lucky, that the ledger balances.

*See?* she said, when he was weeping and could not stop. *Nothing taken. Everything given. You've heard hundreds of deaths, Felix Dunmore. You think you don't already know how this is done?*

"I know how it's done," he said bitterly, wiping his face. "I've listened to it my whole life. That doesn't mean I want to—"

*Nobody wants to,* she said. *Wanting isn't the currency. Willing is.*

When she withdrew, her warmth was nearly gone. He held the cracked halves of her charm and felt them cool in his palms, cooling like a hand going slack, and he understood that when the reforging came, whatever spoke through her would be her shape and her words and not-quite-her — a candle after the flame, holding the light a moment longer out of love.

He wrapped the pieces carefully anyway. Some debts you carry even knowing you can't repay them.

* * *

On the third day — if days meant anything under a starless sky — Isolde found him asleep against the vault wall with tears frozen on his lashes and every charm in his pack laid out in a careful ring, the way he arranged them for listening. She crouched, studied the bones, and he watched understanding move across her face like frost forming.

"You're practicing," she said quietly. "For it. For the giving."

"The dead are teaching me." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Did you know that's how covenants are sealed? Someone has to stand in the middle and say yes. Nine voices to make it, one voice to remake it. Mine, apparently. The spire waited two hundred years for someone who could hear well enough to say the words right."

Isolde was silent a long moment. Then she sat down beside him, armor clinking, and pulled her cloak half over his shoulders with the brisk, graceless kindness of someone who had forgotten how.

"When I burned my seal," she said, "I thought I was ending my order's sin. Siphoning the light, breaking the covenant — I thought that was the worst of it." Her jaw tightened. "But we didn't just steal the spire's warmth, did we. We stole the *time* it should have had. Every year my mentor fed the wardens' wards with that light was a year the flame burned lower. So the debt is ours too. Mine."

"You don't owe a life, Isolde."

"Don't I?" She looked at the ring of charms — at the little gray fragments of Sefa's bone — and something in her soldier's face cracked open, just slightly. "What does it cost you? The lessons."

"Warmth," Felix said. "Theirs. Every time they teach me, they give me a piece of themselves and it doesn't come back. They're emptying themselves out so I'll be ready." He stared at his hands. "Twenty years I told myself I was keeping them safe. Carrying them. And now it turns out I was just... storing them. Saving them up. Like the spire saved its light." His voice broke on it. "Like fuel."

Isolde put her hand over his — gauntlet and all, clumsy and heavy and real.

"Then here's what a keeper would say," she murmured, "instead of a hunter. Fuel burns brightest at the end. Whatever they have left, they're choosing to spend it on you. Don't you dare waste it by refusing."

* * *

That night, the last lesson came unbidden. Felix woke in the vault's gray glow to find all his dead gathered close — Maren, Sefa, the ferryman Cobb, the twin boys from Tanner's Row, every voice he'd ever tucked into bone — arranged in a circle around him, exactly like the nine who had first sealed the covenant, and he understood with a lurch that they were rehearsing.

*One more thing you must know,* Sefa said, and her voice was barely a breath now, a shape of a shape. *The word must be spoken truly. Freely. If even one part of you holds back — one corner of your heart still bargaining — it fails, and you die anyway, and worse, you die *useless*. So ask yourself now, while there is time: can you mean it?*

Felix looked around the circle at the people he had carried, the family he hadn't known he had until the dead claimed him, and beyond them, up through the vault, toward Isolde keeping watch and Rook still locked in her poisoned negotiations and the city of Greyhollow huddled starless beneath its broken wards.

He thought of his ledger, the way Sefa had shown him. Thirty-one years of hiding. Of loneliness worn like a second coat. And lately — only lately — warmth. Real warmth. Found family. Belonging he had chosen and that had chosen him back.

*I can mean it,* he said. *Ask me again at the end, and I'll mean it twice.*

The circle of the dead stirred — something that might have been grief, might have been pride — and then, far above, through leagues of bone and stone, Felix heard a sound that stopped his heart: the great doors of the Court's hall slamming open, and Rook Ashvane's voice, ragged with fury, cutting through the courtesy like a knife through silk—

*"You can't have her! Do you hear me — you can't HAVE her!"*
