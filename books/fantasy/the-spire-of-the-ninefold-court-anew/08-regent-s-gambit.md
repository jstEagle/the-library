## 8. Regent's Gambit

The Court had always been beautiful the way a snare is beautiful — silk over wire, sweetness over rot. Rook Ashvane had grown up on its edges, in the smugglers' warrens where beauty was something you sold rather than trusted, and she had promised herself she would never forget that. But three days of ash-and-courtesy hospitality had worn her edges smooth. Three days of honeyed wine and servants with too many joints in their fingers. Three days of watching Felix fade like a lamp running out of oil as the dead taught him lessons no living man should have to learn.

So when the regent summoned them to the Hall of Ninefold Echoes at moonrise, Rook was already braced for the knife.

She simply hadn't expected it to come for her mother.

"The covenant requires a voice," the regent said. She stood beneath the great shattered arch where nine statues had once held hands across mortal and fae bloodlines; now only four remained whole, their faces weathered to suggestion. Her gown was the color of deep water, and it moved without wind. "One freely given. The spire has *suggested* the bone-carver, but suggestions are merely the spire's opinion. I am its regent. My word is law until the light returns."

Felix went very still beside her. Isolde's hand drifted toward a sword that the courtesy-wards had long since taken from her.

"You're saying," Rook said slowly, "you'll put someone else in the fire."

"I'm saying the Court will not die for one mortal's scruples." The regent smiled, and it was the smile of a woman counting coins she hadn't earned. "Your mother's name is carved in the spire's third root. She has been kept in stasis these forty years, preserved against just such a need. She is a covenant voice by blood and by inscription. She is *ready*."

Rook heard herself laugh — a short, ugly sound that scattered across the marble. "Ready. She's been a knife in your drawer for forty years."

"Every ruler keeps knives." The regent's gaze slid to Felix, softening into something almost kind, which was worse. "You needn't grieve for him, carver. You may go home to Greyhollow whole. Isn't that what you wanted? To be spared?"

And there it was — the trap laid so gently Felix might have walked into it out of sheer exhaustion. Rook saw the temptation flicker across his face, the terrible human arithmetic: *his life or mine.* She grabbed his wrist before he could answer.

"He doesn't get to be spared," she said. "That's not how *freely given* works, and you know it. Your own spire will spit her back out."

"The spire," said the regent, "will take what it is offered, or the sky finishes tearing and both our realms bleed out together. Choose your grief, smuggler. It arrives either way."

* * *

They were escorted back to their guest-wing by guards who pretended not to watch them, which meant they watched them constantly. The moment the door closed, Isolde set her palm flat against the wood and listened the way wardens listen — not with ears but with the trained stillness of a woman who had spent twenty years hearing lies through walls.

"Four guards outside. Two more on the stair. They're not guarding us." She turned. "They're guarding the *door*. We're being kept fresh."

"Kept out of the way," Rook said. "Until they take my mother down to the roots."

Felix sat on the edge of the bed, gray-faced, his hands curled around an old woman's bone charm that he hadn't let go of since Greyhollow. When he spoke, his voice came out scraped thin. "The dead here are screaming. They've been screaming since we arrived, but tonight it's—" He pressed the charm to his ear as if it were a shell. "Tonight they're saying *soon*. Something happens soon."

"The reforging," Isolde said. "She's moving up the ceremony. Before we can interfere."

"She already has," Felix said quietly. "I can feel the spire pulling. Like a lung drawing breath." He looked up at Rook, and his eyes were full of a pity she didn't want and couldn't refuse. "I'm sorry. It's tonight."

Rook had spent her whole life preparing for the news that her mother was dead. Exile had made death a kind of mercy she could imagine clearly, cleanly, with an ending attached. But this — her mother alive, preserved, *stored*, about to be fed into an ancient furnace because a regent with cold eyes had decided one grief was cheaper than another —

"We go now," Rook said. "Through the root-vaults."

Isolde raised an eyebrow. "The vaults under the spire. Where the Court's oldest wards live. Where even the regent's guard won't follow, if half the stories are true."

"That's the idea."

"And the other half of the stories?"

"They say the vaults eat people." Rook was already prying up the floorboard she'd marked on their first night, beneath which lay the maintenance-shaft the smugglers' guild had used before the borders closed — back when her mother had been the one teaching her which stones sang and which ones lied. "Lucky for us, I'm very hard to digest."

* * *

The root-vaults were older than the Court above them, older perhaps than either realm. They descended in spirals of black stone veined with what looked like frozen lightning — the spire's roots, thick as rivers, running down into dark that breathed. Warm air rose around them, smelling of loam and ozone and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in a sealed room.

Rook led with a shard of glowstone cupped in her palm. Behind her came Felix, one hand trailing along the root-flesh, whispering continuously to voices only he could hear. And behind him walked Isolde Vane, disgraced warden, seal-less, carrying a stolen warden-lantern whose beam cut the dark like a moral judgment.

"The dead are quieter here," Felix murmured. "Not gone. Just... listening. Everything's listening."

"Then everyone can hear me say this once." Rook stopped walking. The glowstone trembled in her grip. "My mother's name is Maren Ashvane. She was a gate-singer — one of the voices that sealed the covenant originally, before the first reforging. When mortals outlawed magic, the Court gave its covenant-voices a choice: stay and fade, or exile and live. She chose exile. For me. Because I was half-mortal and the Court was starting to starve, and starving courts do ugly things to mixed blood." Her throat worked. "Then the wardens came for her anyway, and instead of letting them take her somewhere worse, she surrendered herself to the Court's stasis. A bargain. Her body for my freedom. And I spent forty years telling myself she'd abandoned me, because abandoned was easier than *sacrificed*. Easier to hate her than to owe her."

Nobody spoke. Above them, muffled by miles of stone, the Court began to ring its ceremony-bells early.

"Move," Rook said hoarsely. "Before I remember how to feel things."

* * *

They found the stasis-vault at the bottom of everything: a chamber shaped like the inside of a seed, walls of pale amber light, and in its center a figure floating in a column of slow silver.

Maren Ashvane did not look forty years preserved. She looked asleep — mid-thirties, sharp-cheeked, hair the same impossible rust-red as Rook's, wearing the traveling coat Rook remembered from a market day when she was six. Her chest barely moved. Around the column, the spire's roots converged like worshippers, drinking the faint warmth off her skin.

"There should be a release-word," Isolde said, scanning the amber walls. "Stasis isn't a cage. It's a contract. There's always a term written somewhere—"

"Mother." Rook stepped up to the silver column. Her reflection warped across its surface, split into a dozen versions of herself, none of them brave. "It's me. It's Rook. I know you probably can't hear — they said stasis is like drowning slowly, no dreams, nothing — but if any part of you is in there, I need you to know I found your name. In the spire. Third root. I traced every letter with my thumb like you taught me with the market-stones, and I—"

The column flickered.

Silver light stuttered, and Maren Ashvane's eyes opened.

Rook's heart stopped, restarted wrong. "*Mother—*"

"*Rook.*" Her mother's voice came out rusted, thin as leaf-skeleton, but unmistakably hers. Her hand pressed against the inside of the column, and Rook pressed hers against the outside, forty years and four inches of silver between them. "*You shouldn't be here. Listen — listen — the regent means to give me to the spire. Don't let her. Not because I'm afraid to burn.*" A terrible pause. "*Because I've been burning for forty years already, little ember, and I am so tired.*"

"Then we break you out. Felix knows another way, maybe, or—"

"*No.*" Gentle. Absolute. The way her mother had said *no* to second helpings and dangerous roads and hope, all Rook's childhood. "*Look at me. Truly look. Stasis doesn't preserve, it postpones. What's left of me is threadbare. If they pull me out, I unravel within the year, in pain, far from anything I love. But the covenant needs a voice freely given, and mine was given long ago — I gave it the day I walked into this sleep so you could run free. All the regent would do is spend a gift that was never hers.*"

"But you'd be *gone*."

"*I have been gone since you were twelve, my heart. This is only my echo, kept warm.*" Her mother's forehead tipped forward against the silver, and for a moment her face was exactly the face from the market day, laughing at a juggler, flour on her cheek. "*Let me finish what I chose. That is the last thing I will ever ask of you. Let me go as myself — not dragged, not spent by that cold woman, but released, freely, by the daughter I did it all for. Family isn't keeping each other. It's knowing when to open the hand.*"

Rook's legs nearly went. She felt Felix's hand land warm between her shoulder blades — steadying, asking nothing.

"If I do this," she whispered, "it has to be right. Not the regent's ceremony. Ours. You hear me? I'm not handing you to her machine."

"*There's my girl,*" her mother said, and smiled, and the smile cracked Rook open like an egg.

Behind them, at the chamber entrance, boots rang on black stone. Isolde turned, lantern rising, and Rook saw the regent step into the amber light flanked by a dozen of her jointed guards — and behind the regent, the Court itself had come: hundreds of the fading fae, crowding the descending spiral, drawn by the bells, splitting visibly into factions as some knelt to their regent and others, faces gaunt and desperate, looked instead at the red-haired smuggler standing between her mother and the fire.

"The vault is sealed for the rite," the regent announced. "Bring the vessel."

Overhead, far above, through miles of stone, the torn sky screamed — and every root in the chamber shuddered at once, as if the spire itself had begun, at last, to choose sides.
