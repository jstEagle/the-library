## 10. Nine Voices, One Home

The gate opened at dawn, and dawn was the right hour for it — the hour when the dead have mostly gone to bed and the living haven't yet decided what they're afraid of.

Felix Dunmore stood in the market square of Greyhollow and watched it happen. It was not a door. He had expected a door, some grand arch of white stone or a seam of fire in the air, but the Court had never thought in doors. The gate was a *listening* — a place where the air grew attentive, where the light came down a little softer than it did anywhere else, and if you walked through it slowly enough you felt yourself being heard all the way through, every bone catalogued, every grief weighed and found acceptable.

The stars were back above the spire's far echo. That was the thing Felix kept checking, like a tongue returning to a missing tooth: the sky over Greyhollow held its nine constellations again, and beneath them, faint as breath on glass, the Ninefold Court shimmered in its own sky, two heavens leaning toward each other like old friends across a table.

"Stop staring," Isolde Vane said beside him. She was out of uniform for the first time since he'd known her — a plain grey coat, her hair bound simply, no seal at her throat because she had burned it and there was nothing to hang there anymore. "You'll make the gate self-conscious."

"It's a hole between worlds."

"It's an *agreement* between worlds," she corrected. "There's a difference, and I've spent eleven years not knowing it." She said it lightly, but her hands weren't light. They hung at her sides with the particular stillness of someone learning new habits. For years those hands had signed warrants, broken wards, taken names. Now, on the bench beside her, they rested near a ledger open to a blank page.

KEEPERS OF THE GATE, the page's header read, in her careful warden's hand. Below it, one name so far: her own.

"You could come," she said, not looking at him. "The Court will offer you anything. You know that. You reforged their whole covenant; you're practically furniture now. Sacred furniture."

"The shop has customers," Felix said. "Dead ones. Living ones too, lately, which is stranger." He touched his pocket, where a half-carved charm clicked against his knuckles. "And someone has to teach your keepers which whispers are prayers and which are warnings. You can't hear them, Izzy. You'd arrest a grandmother for humming."

"I would not."

"You arrested me for carving."

"I arrested you for *exploding*, and history has vindicated me, because look what happened next." But she was smiling when she said it, small and crooked, and she let her hand rest briefly over his — warden-warm, human, alive — before withdrawing to her ledger. "First keeper's duty," she murmured. "I'm supposed to log everyone who crosses today. Both directions. Do you know what that means?"

"That you'll finally learn everyone's business?"

"That the realms are going to be *neighbors*," she said. "That there will be fae children buying candied plums on Copperlane by winter, and mortal masons teaching Court-stonework, and arguments, and treaties, and someone will have to stand at this gate every morning and say *yes, you may*. Eleven years I was trained to say no to everything with a drop of magic in it." She looked up at the listening air of the gate, at the softness in the light. "I'd like to spend the rest of my life saying yes. Someone should check whether I'm any good at it."

The first crossing came then — a Court-folk woman, tall and grey-eyed, carrying a basket of moths that were also lanterns or possibly the reverse. She paused at the threshold, afraid, and Isolde stood, and Felix watched her do the hardest thing he had ever seen anyone do, harder than burning a seal or facing a regent made of hoarded light:

She bowed, and said, "Welcome to Greyhollow. Mind the third cobblestone; it bites."

* * *

Rook Ashvane crossed the gate forty times that first day, and would cross it four hundred times a year for the rest of her life, and never once got tired of the feeling of it — that slow, thorough attention, like walking through a room where someone very old and very fond of you is reading your face.

Her boat wasn't a boat, technically. It was a skiff of woven reed-light that rode the gap between the realms the way a ferry rides a river, and she'd won the concession fair and square by being the only smuggler alive who knew both skies well enough to steer it. The wardens had stamped her papers herself — Isolde had done it personally, smirking the entire time — and Rook had framed the first forgery-proof license in her cabin as a joke, then caught herself dusting it weekly.

"Passengers for the twilight run!" she called, poling off from the Greyhollow side into the soft dark between the skies. "No contraband, no curses, no complaints — the toll is a story, payable at the midpoint, and yes, Marisol, *you still owe me from Tuesday*."

She had twelve passengers. A fae stonemason going to see the cathedrals he'd only heard about through the wards' old cracks. Three siblings from Greyhollow visiting the orchards where their grandmother had been born before the exile laws. An old Court herald who wept quietly the whole way, looking back at a realm he hadn't dared hope to see again.

At the midpoint — that breathless place where neither sky owned you — Rook did what she always did. She rested her pole, took out the small bone tile she wore on a cord around her neck, and turned it in her fingers.

Her mother's name was carved there. Not the stasis-slab's version, cold and claimed and owned by a regent's ambition. This was the name as it had been given, freely: **Serevanne**, daughter of the ash-orchard, voice of the covenant, who had chosen exile over a cage and paid for it with centuries of silence.

She was at peace now. That was the strange part, the part Rook was still learning to hold without dropping. In the root-vaults, in the moment before the rescue became something else — before Felix had put his hand on the slab and the spire's last living voice had whispered *let her choose* — Serevanne had looked up at her daughter with eyes gone clear and quiet, and she had said, *You came. You shouldn't have. I'm so glad. Now let me go, little bird, and go be somebody's family instead of my ghost.*

So Rook had. It had been the worst bargain of her life and the best one, and she had cried exactly once, privately, in the dark between skies where nobody could bill her for it.

Now she held the tile up to the twin lights and said, "Evening, Mum. Twelve aboard. The mason's nervous; tell him the mortal bread won't hurt him. And—" she smiled, crooked as her mother's had been, "—Marisol still owes me from Tuesday. I know you'd have liked her."

The tile warmed, just slightly, the way a stone holds afternoon sun after the sun has gone. Not a voice. Just a warmth. Just enough.

She poled on toward the Court, where the spire stood lit at last — nine colors braided up its length, visible now from both worlds, a candle in the window of a house that had been dark for eleven years and was, tonight, expecting company.

* * *

Felix reopened the shop on a Thursday, because Thursdays had always been quiet and he wanted quiet for it.

He swept first. Then he unwrapped the tools — his father's knives, his grandfather's burins, the whole inherited liturgy of bone and edge — and laid them out on the workbench, and stood there a long moment in the smell of beeswax and dust and the particular mineral sweetness of worked bone that had been the background music of his entire life.

The dead noticed immediately. They always did.

They came in gently — that was different now, that was everything. Before the reforging, the dead had pressed against him like water against a dam, hungry, directionless, sometimes cruel without meaning to be. Now they arrived the way visitors arrive: knocked, waited, settled into the corners of the shop like cats finding their spots. The old woman whose charm had started everything — Maren, he knew her name now, had known it since the night she gave herself to the spire — took up residence by the window, where she could watch the street and comment on everyone's hats.

*You left the shutters crooked,* she observed.

"I did not."

*Straighten them anyway. First impressions.*

He straightened them. He was, he had learned, allowed to be bossed around by the dead when the dead loved him; it was practically the founding principle of his whole strange gift.

The first customer of the reopened shop was a child — a fae child, actually, first of her line to set foot in Greyhollow legally in eleven generations, clutching her mother's hand and staring at the shelves of charms with the expression of someone entering a cathedral. Felix crouched to her height.

"What are they?" she asked.

"Bones," Felix said honestly. "Old ones, given kindly. Each one remembers someone. When I carve them right, the remembering gets — comfortable. Like a coat you can wear when the world's too loud."

"My grandmother died in the Court," the child said, with the devastating directness of the young. "Before the light came back. Mama says she can't hear her anymore."

Felix looked at the mother, who looked stricken, and he understood — grief didn't stop at borders, and the new covenant's grace didn't reach backward for everyone. Some of the dead had gone into the spire's light entirely, and their families were left with ordinary silence, the kind everyone else had always carried.

"Come here," he said gently, and led them to the workbench.

He chose a piece of riverbone, pale and dense, and he carved while they watched — and here was the miracle he still hadn't gotten used to, that he could carve *with* the dead now instead of merely for them, that Maren leaned over his shoulder and guided his burin and the child's grandmother, wherever she was in the wide warm dark of the relit spire, seemed somehow to lean on Maren's other shoulder, the way voices carry through a wall when the wall is built of love instead of fear.

When it was done, the charm held a whisper. Not a haunting. A *hello*.

The child pressed it to her ear and her whole face changed — went bright and wet and laughing all at once. "She says my hat's crooked!"

"They always say that," Felix said, glancing at the window. "*Always.*"

That evening, after the shutter was latched and the lamps were low, Felix sat alone at the workbench with the best piece of bone he had ever owned — antler, gifted freely by the Court's herd-keepers, white as the first star. He had been saving it. He had known for weeks what it was for.

He carved nine niches around its curve, small and precise. Into each, he set a single grain-bone, each carrying a voice:

Maren, by the window. The drowned boy from the canal, who liked to hear about weather he'd never feel again. The soldier who had taught him that courage was mostly showing up tired. Isolde's mentor — no, not him; that niche he filled with Isolde herself, alive, laughing, *family, not ghosts*, that was the whole point — and Rook, mid-laugh, mid-insult. Rook's mother, serene as held sunlight. The child from this afternoon. The spire's own last living voice, which had chosen, in the end, to rest. And the ninth niche he left for whoever came next — because there was always someone next, and the door of the shop, like the door between the worlds, was going to stay open.

He strung it on a cord and hung it above the workbench, where the lamplight moved through it, and the nine small voices hummed together like a chord.

Outside, the first star of evening rose directly over the gate, and Isolde was logging crossings by its light, and Rook's skiff was somewhere between two skies singing badly to twelve delighted passengers, and the world had edges again, good ones, the kind you choose.

*Tomorrow,* Maren said comfortably, settling in for the night. *Tomorrow there's a woman coming about a charm for her husband. Terrible hat. You'll want the pale knife.*

Felix smiled in the empty shop, surrounded by family, and reached for the pale knife.
