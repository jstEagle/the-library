## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The counting-room of the Ninefold Court smelled of cold iron and old paper, and to Mara Winterbourne it smelled like safety.

She had thought this once, years ago, and had never quite managed to unthink it, though she suspected—dimly, the way one suspects a draft under a door—that a person ought not to find comfort in the smell of a place that kept accounts on human lives. But comfort was a rare commodity in the city of Corveth, and Mara took hers where she found it: in the ruled lines of a ledger, in the soft scratch of her nib, in the lamplit certainty that every debt in the world could be written down, weighed, and balanced if only one were precise enough.

Precision was her whole religion. She had no memory of acquiring it, which was itself a kind of precision—the oath-bound did not remember their oaths—but she practiced it faithfully all the same.

It was the third hour past midnight, and the Court's great hall above her was dark. Down here in the counting-room only three lamps burned, and only one of them was hers.

Mara sat at the last desk in the last row, the desk nobody wanted because the ceiling sloped low over it and the drain from the apothecary annex above dripped in wet weather. She liked the desk. It faced the wall. Nobody passing by could read her work upside down, and nobody ever needed to speak to her except across the counter at appraisal hours, and even then they spoke to the counter, or to the brass plate bolted to it—*SPEAKSPEAKER OF THE THIRD DESK*—rather than to her.

That suited her fine. Invisibility was a skill like any other, and Mara had perfected it the way other people perfected swordsmanship or music: daily, deliberately, until it stopped being an act.

She turned the page.

*Account of Borrowed Years — Client: Merrin Osta, cooper, age fifty-one.*

The account was a sorry one. Merrin Osta had borrowed eleven years from the Court at the age of thirty-four—years taken out of his own middle age and advanced to his youth, so that he could court a woman who would not wait for him. Standard terms. The woman had died anyway, two years into the loan, and Merrin had spent nineteen years trying to buy the years back so he could die on schedule, as a man ought, instead of living on borrowed time like a house lived in by someone else's ghost.

The Court did not forgive such debts. The Court did not do anything so imprecise as forgiveness. The Court *balanced*.

Mara dipped her pen and wrote in her small, exact hand:

*Balance owing: nine years, four months, six days. Interest accrued per Schedule C. Recommended resolution: collection in kind, per §14 of the Terms of Borrowing.*

Collection in kind meant the collectors would come for what remained of Merrin Osta's allotted span, and he would die nine years early, in a room somewhere, of nothing anyone could name—a man simply emptied, like a cask run dry. Mara had written such resolutions before. She had written forty-one of them. She knew because she kept a private tally in the back margin of her own daybook, small ticks, unnumbered, that she never looked at directly.

She looked at them now. Forty-one ticks.

Her hand did not shake. Her hands never shook. That was part of what the oath had made her—or what it had taken away; she had never been able to decide which. A swordspeaker's hands were steady the way a scale is steady: not because they feel nothing, but because feeling is beside the point. Weigh, record, balance. The ledger does not weep.

Somewhere below the steadiness, in a place she did not visit often, something in her noted that a woman who tallied the ruin of strangers in ticks of a pen ought perhaps to feel more than this—and then the thought slid off her, smooth as water off oiled leather, and she went back to work.

* * *

At the fourth hour, the memory arrived.

Memories came up from the vaults on nights when the appraisers worked late, carried in lead-lined boxes by a mute under-vault-keeper named Sorrel who wore gloves even in summer. This one came in a bottle the size of a plum, blown in green glass, and it glowed faintly the way bottled memories did—not with light exactly, but with the suggestion of light, like a window seen from a dark street.

Sorrel set it on her counter, tapped the docket twice, and left without looking at her. No one looked at Mara much. She had long ago stopped wondering whether this was her doing or theirs.

The docket said: *Bottled memory. Provenance uncertain. Appraise for resale or dissolution.*

Uncertain provenance was the worst kind of work, because it meant touching the thing. Mara drew on her second pair of gloves—the thin ones, kidskin, for handling memories bare-handed was how appraisers ended up keeping other people's griefs like cats that followed them home—and lifted the bottle into the cradle of her lamp.

Inside, the memory moved.

They always moved. A bottled memory was not a picture; it was a weather. This one showed her—when she tilted it toward the flame—a summer afternoon, or the shape of one: a courtyard, laundry strung between walls, a child's voice laughing somewhere off-frame. Sunlight lay across flagstones in warm stripes. Someone was singing, badly, two streets over, and the smell of bread came through the stone.

Ordinary. Almost insultingly ordinary. Mara had appraised memories of coronations and massacres, of first flights and last words, of a wedding night that had sold for more than the bride's family's entire street. And here was this: laundry, sunlight, bread, laughter. Worth almost nothing to anyone but whoever had lived it.

Which raised the question the docket couldn't answer: why had someone paid good silver to have it bottled? People sold memories to the Court for one of two reasons—to be rid of them, or to be rid of the weight of carrying them alone. Grief sold well. Terror sold well. Joy was rarely surrendered.

Unless the joy was unbearable. Unless the sunlit afternoon belonged to someone dead, and the owner could no longer stand the way it kept happening to them, every day, whole.

Mara held the little green bottle up and watched the laundry sway in a wind that had stopped existing decades ago. For a moment—one breath, no longer—she felt something press against the inside of her chest, some ache with no address on it, as if the memory recognized something in her and leaned toward it.

Then she set it down, and the feeling went where all her feelings went, down into the deep water where she kept them.

*Appraisal:* she wrote. *Common grade. Sentimental value high; market value negligible. Recommend dissolution and credit of proceeds to the Unclaimed Kindnesses fund.*

Dissolution meant pouring it into the Court's great basin, where forgotten memories mingled into a warm gray soup that was sold by the ladle to people who wanted to feel *something*, anything, they didn't have to remember afterward. It was, Mara thought, the closest thing the Ninefold Court did to mercy. Even that, they charged for.

She initialed the entry. M.W., in her tight, upright script, each letter identical to its brothers. Somewhere in the ledgers of the Ninefold Court there were ten thousand initials in ten thousand hands, and hers were the most legible and the least alive, and she was proud of this, or had decided to be proud of it, which amounted to the same thing after enough years.

* * *

The fifth hour brought the balancing, and the balancing required silence.

This was the heart of the night's work: the Great Reconciliation, done nightly, in which the Court's impossible inventory was checked against itself. Bottled memories against the vault registry. Borrowed years against the actuarial tables. Doors—Mara handled only the paperwork on doors, thank all the quiet gods—against the treaty schedules that governed which erased countries might still be reached, and by whom, and for what fee.

She worked down the columns with her lips slightly parted, the way she always did, though she'd have denied it under torture. Column against column. Figure against figure. The pleasure of it was bottomless and shameless: the world was chaos, the city outside her wall was hunger and rumor and knives, and here, on these pages, everything agreed with everything else. Debts equaled assets. Every impossible thing the Court owned was exactly accounted for. Nothing was missing.

Nothing was ever missing. That was the point of the Court, the thing Corveth's merchants whispered about in wine-shops: you could sell your mother's face to the Ninefold Court and buy it back next year, and it would be there, waiting in its bottle, perfect, because the Court lost *nothing*. The Court's books were the one honest thing in a dishonest world.

Mara believed that absolutely. She would go on believing it for another few hours yet.

She was halfway through the door-schedules when she noticed the discrepancy.

It was nothing. A rounding error, a clerk's slip, the kind of mote a lesser accountant would sweep past without seeing. Line 409 of the Treaty of Severance schedules: *Vessany — status: dissolved by treaty, all access sealed, inventory: nil.*

Inventory: nil. And yet, in the column for sealed-door maintenance fees, someone—decades ago, the ink had gone brown—had recorded an annual payment. One crown per year. Paid to whom? To keep up what? A dissolved country had no doors. A dissolved country had no *anything*; that was what dissolved meant. The treaty had unwritten Vessany from the maps and from the memory of everyone who signed, and the Court's own ledgers confirmed it: nil, nil, nil.

Except for the crown. One crown a year, for sixty-one years, maintaining a nothing.

Mara sat very still in the drip-and-lamplight of the third desk.

There were two things she could do, and she knew both of them the way she knew her own handwriting. The first was to flag the discrepancy for the Auditors, as procedure demanded. Procedure was the wall she had built her whole life inside of; procedure had fed her, clothed her, made her invisible and therefore safe. Flag it, file it, forget it. Some auditor three ranks above her would frown at line 409 for a moment, initial it, and the machine would roll on.

The second thing was to copy the line into her private daybook, the one she kept in the false spine of her copy of the *Terms and Schedules*, and say nothing to anyone.

She told herself she chose the second option out of precision. An accountant did not report an anomaly she did not yet understand; that would be sloppy, premature, an imprecision of a subtler kind. She needed to know whether it was one crown a year or sixty-one crowns total. She needed to see the original schedule. She needed—

She needed, some small voice observed from the deep water, to know why her heart was beating faster over a single line of brown ink. Why the word *Vessany*—a word she was certain she had never heard before tonight—sat in her mouth like the taste of a fruit she'd eaten once, long ago, in a country that did not exist.

Mara copied the line. She closed the book. She blew out her lamp, and sat a while in the dark with her steady hands flat on the desk, listening to the building settle around her like an old animal.

One crown a year. Sixty-one years. Somebody, somewhere, was paying rent on a ghost.

* * *

The bells rang the sixth hour as she climbed the stairs to lock the outer doors—her last duty, always hers, because the third desk was nearest the door and invisibility has its costs. The antechamber was vast and dim, all black marble and the nine banners of the Ninefold Court hanging motionless overhead, and Mara walked through it the way she walked through everywhere: quickly, quietly, a gray figure in a gray corridor, remarkable only for being unremarkable.

She had the first bolt half-thrown when she heard the knock.

Not a knock, quite. A dragging, thumping insistence, low on the door, as if someone had fallen against it and was trying to climb. Mara froze with her hand on the iron. Appraisal hours had ended at midnight. The Court received no one after the sixth bell; that was rule the eleventh, carved into the lintel along with the other twenty-six rules, and Mara had spent her life inside those carvings.

The thumping came again, weaker.

Every precise instinct she owned said: walk away. Bolt the door. Report the disturbance at morning bell, per §9, and let the wardens earn their keep. It was what the ledger would counsel. It was what the oath—if she could have remembered it—would surely command.

But her hands, those steady, scale-balanced hands that never shook and never hesitated, were already lifting the bolt before she had decided anything at all.

Later—much later, in a country that did not exist—she would understand that this was the first true choice she had made in eleven years. Tonight she only knew that the door swung open onto the fog of Coin-Row, and a man fell through it, bleeding from a wound in his side that soaked her ledger-daybook where it lay on the mat, and that he was clutching something folded tight in his fist like a miser's coin.

His eyes found her face. They widened—not with pain. With recognition.

"You," the stranger whispered, pressing the folded thing into her hands with the last of his strength. "Vessany remembers you."

Then the bells finished striking, and he was still, and the fog rolled in around them both like ink poured into water.
