## 2. A Stranger at Closing Time

The last hour before closing was Mara's favorite hour of the Court's long day, and she had never told anyone so, because it seemed like the kind of confession that could be used against you.

At that hour the counting-room emptied. The appraisers went up to their suppers in the Spire, the junior clerks went down into the city to spend wages they'd earned balancing accounts of other people's lives, and the great brass lamps along the counters were turned down to a honeyed murmur. What remained was the smell of the place—ink and cold iron and, faintly, the green-apple sweetness of bottled years—and the sound of Mara's pen moving across the ledger in small, exact strokes.

She was finishing the evening's reconciliation when the bells above the doors began their closing song.

Nine notes, one for each auditor, descending like water down a stair. Mara did not look up. She had learned, in six years at the Court, that the closing song was not a suggestion but a law: whatever transaction remained unfinished when the ninth note faded belonged to tomorrow, and tomorrow always came. There was comfort in that. The world outside the Court was weather and rumor and hunger; inside it, everything balanced.

The account she was closing belonged to a cooper from Harrow Lane who had borrowed eleven years against his own future to pay for his daughter's cough-cure, and who had come in that afternoon to make his first repayment: one year, decanted and stoppered in a squat grey vial that sat now on the counter before her, glowing faintly, the way borrowed time always glowed—a low amber light, like lamplight through cider. Eleven years purchased, ten still owed. Inside the vial, if she tilted it—and she did tilt it, because precision was her religion—one could see the year itself: snow falling on a street she didn't recognize, a woman laughing with her head thrown back, a dog bounding through drifts. A good year. An honest one. The cooper had sold nothing false.

Mara entered the figures. *Received: one (1) year, unspent, quality fair-to-good. Balance remaining: ten.* Her hand did not shake. It never shook. That was what First Auditor Corvane had said of her at her last review, in his voice like dry paper: *Winterbourne's ledgers are the cleanest in the building. Whatever else she is, she is exact.*

Whatever else she is. She had wondered, sometimes, what he meant by the *else*. But wondering was a luxury the ledger did not allow, and she had stopped allowing herself luxuries years ago—or rather, she realized with a small cold start she could not explain, she had never remembered allowing them at all.

She stoppered the ink. She set the vial in its cradle in the vault-tray for the night clerks to carry below. She squared the ledger's corners with the edge of the counter, once, twice, the way she always did, until the book sat aligned to the counter's grain like a ship at anchor.

That was when the knocking began.

Not at the front doors—the front doors were already locked behind their three bolts and their whispering wards, which murmured like sleeping men when anyone touched them. The knocking came from the delivery entrance, the narrow door beside the coal chute that existed for couriers bringing consignments after dark. It was a hard, urgent, human knock, fists and the flat of palms, and beneath it she could hear breathing, ragged and wet.

Mara stood very still.

The rules were clear on this point. Rule the eleventh, inscribed on the placard beside every clerk's station in Corvane's own crabbed hand: *No transaction begins or ends outside the hours. No exceptions. Exceptions are how empires fall.* In six years she had watched couriers turned away in rain, in snow, once carrying a coffin-shaped crate that wept. The rules held. The rules were the walls that kept the impossible things on their shelves.

But the breathing was getting worse. She could hear it changing, a wet whistle drawing itself into each inhale and letting it out too fast, the way a bellows sounds when the leather is torn. And underneath her obedience something older than the rules shifted its weight—an instinct she did not recognize and could not name, the body's blunt arithmetic: *that is a person dying on the other side of that door.*

"Who's there," she called. Her voice sounded strange in the empty room, smaller than she'd meant it.

No answer. Only the knock again, weaker now, and then a sound like a body sliding down wood.

Later—much later, when she had time to take her own actions apart and itemize them the way she itemized everything—Mara would not be able to say why she crossed the room. She would tell herself it was prudence: better to see the trouble than have it find you. She would tell herself it was curiosity, which was at least honest. But in the moment there was no reasoning at all, only her feet on the floorboards and her hand on the courier-door's bar, lifting it, because some part of her already knew the way to this door the way she knew the way to her own bed.

The lock, she noticed distantly, was already undone. Someone had oiled it recently. She had never oiled it.

The man fell into her arms.

He was perhaps fifty, dressed as a drover—oilskin coat, mud to the knee—but no drover smelled of ozone and burnt sugar, and no drover's coat hid a wound like the one spreading under his ribs, black in the lamplight, soaking through cloth and her sleeves both before she understood what was happening. His skin was the grey of paper left out in rain. His lips were moving.

"The door," he rasped. "Lock it. They'll follow the blood."

She locked it. She did not know why she obeyed him; she only knew that her hands moved before her fear finished forming, throwing the bar, and something in her chest unclenched a fraction—as if the room itself exhaled, as if the wards along the walls leaned in closer, wrapping the counting-room in a silence suddenly thick enough to hold.

"Sit," she said. "There's a stool—I can fetch the house surgeon—"

"No." His hand shot out and caught her wrist with a strength that astonished her, and then his eyes found hers and she stopped trying to speak at all. They were pale eyes, almost colorless, and they were looking at her the way a man looks at a house he grew up in, seen again after forty years. "You're the swordspeaker," he whispered. "Gods keep me. You're really her."

"I think you've mistaken me for someone else." The words came automatically, the way corrections came, the way *the balance is off by three coppers* came. "I'm a ledger-keeper. I don't—"

"You don't remember." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; he sagged against the counter, dragging the folded thing from inside his coat as he went down. "None of you ever do. That's the whole art of it, isn't it? That's the whole *crime*." He pressed the thing into her hands—paper, stiff with age and damp, folded into eighths, warm from his body. "Then take it. Take it and read it and hate me for giving it to you."

"What is it?"

"A map." He smiled, and his teeth were red. "Of Vessany."

The word landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash and heard none. She knew the word. Of course she knew the word—every child in the Nine Cities knew it as a rhyme, a skipping-song nonsense syllable: *Vessany, Vessany, over the sea and under the sand.* A country that wasn't. A name the maps had forgotten on purpose. The Court traded in impossible things, but even the Court had laws, and first among them was the law of the Ledger of Erasures: some things were bought out of the world entire, and those things were not for sale again.

"I can't accept this," she heard herself saying, clerk's voice, clerk's cadence. "Unregistered conveyance. It'll have to be appraised, and I'd have to log your name, and—"

"Mara."

Her name. Not Winterbourne, not *clerk*, not *you there*—her given name, spoken in this stranger's ruined voice as though he had said it ten thousand times, as though he had taught it to her himself. The counting-room seemed to tilt. Somewhere far above, in the Spire, a clock she couldn't hear was striking, or she imagined it was.

"How do you know my name?"

His answer was a bubble of blood at the corner of his mouth. He slid down the counter to the floor, and she went down with him, kneeling in his cooling blood without caring about the stain—it would come out of the boards with lye, she noted absurdly, mostly lye and scrubbing—and took his shoulders, and his head lolled against her collarbone, and he gathered breath the way a drowning man gathers water.

"Vessany remembers you," he whispered.

Three words. Four syllables of nothing, a skipping-rhyme fragment, a dead country's ghost-name. And yet they went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor—not pain, exactly. Worse. *Recognition.* Something in her rose toward the word like a plant toward a window, straining, starving, and found only glass. Behind her eyes, for half a heartbeat, there was smoke. Smoke and blossom, an orchard in flower burning all at once, and a woman's voice calling a name across the fire—

The half-heartbeat passed. The vision sealed itself shut like water closing over a stone. Mara knelt in the blood of a dying man in a locked counting-room and could not remember what she had just almost remembered, and the absence of it frightened her more than the blood, more than the man, more than anything the Court had ever shown her in a bottle.

"Stay awake," she said. "Tell me who did this. Tell me what you—" 

"They came for the map." His fingers twitched against her arm, spelling urgency. "Auditors' men. Grey cloaks. They let me run, girl—they *let* me. A wounded man runs slow and leaves a trail." His colorless eyes fixed on hers with terrible clarity. "They're not following me. They're following *this*." His tap on the folded paper was barely a touch. "And now they'll be following you."

"No one follows me," Mara said. "I balance accounts. I go home. I sleep. No one has followed me in six years."

"That," the stranger said, "is because until tonight there was nothing to follow." And then, with the last of his breath, as if the words themselves were the payment that bought his exit: "Don't trust the ones who arrive late. Late arrival is a signature. Ask yourself—who profits from a corpse in the counting-room?" His eyes were going dull even as he spoke, dimming like lamps turned down. "Ask yourself who benefits... when the last oath-bound sword... spends her nights... adding columns..."

He died between one word and the next, the way a candle dies, mid-flicker, leaving the shape of the light behind.

For a long time Mara did not move.

The counting-room was very quiet. The brass lamps hummed. On the counter the cooper's borrowed year glowed in its vial, snow falling forever on a street where a woman laughed, and Mara knelt in a stranger's blood with a dead country folded into eighths against her chest, and understood, with the flat certainty of a figure refusing to sum, that her life had ended and she hadn't been informed.

She should call for help. That was procedure. There was a bell-rope for emergencies, deaths in the counting-house, attempted thefts—she had pulled it twice in six years, once for a rat that turned out to be a minor imp, once for an appraiser's heart. Her hand went to the rope and stopped.

*Don't trust the ones who arrive late.*

She looked at the rope. Plain hemp, tassled, connected to a bell in the warden's gallery two floors up. The wardens answered within a minute, always; she had timed it once out of boredom. Within a minute, always.

So why, asked the cold new voice at the base of her skull, had no warden come running at the sound of the knocking? Sound carried in the counting-room; everyone knew it; it was why conversations at the counters were conducted in the Court's soft trade-tongue. The knocking had been fists on wood, loud, urgent, sustained. The door had opened. A man had bled out on the floor of the most closely watched room in the Nine Cities.

And the wardens, whose boots she could hear on the stairs right now—finally, finally, unhurried, four sets of them, arriving with the leisurely tread of men coming to collect something they already knew was there—the wardens were late.

Too late. Conveniently late. Late the way a forged signature is smooth.

Mara rose. Her hands, of their own accord, did three things in swift sequence, and she watched them do it as if from a distance, as if reading entries in someone else's ledger. First they wiped the blood from her sleeves as best she could with the stranger's oilskin coat, which she then arranged over him decently, covering the wound. Second they took the folded map from where it had fallen—no, it had not fallen; she was holding it; she had been holding it against her sternum this whole time as if her body had known what her mind refused to—and slipped it flat inside her bodice, against her skin, where it lay like a second heartbeat. Third, they picked up her pen, uncapped the ink, and drew a single line through the evening's final entry, the cooper's account, marking it *unreconciled—carry forward.*

A lie, in the ledger. Her first. Small, procedural, invisible. Her hands did not shake.

The door to the corridor opened without a knock, which told her everything before she saw their faces.

Four wardens in grey, lamp-black cloaks beaded with rain that had not been falling when the sun went down. Their captain was a broad, comfortable-looking man named Doss whom Mara had passed in the galleries a hundred times, who had once complimented her handwriting. He looked at the body on the floor. He looked at her. His face performed surprise the way a bad coin performs gold—correctly, at a glance, and not at all in the hand.

"Clerk." His voice was gentle, regretful, exactly calibrated. "What happened here?"

"He knocked at the courier door," Mara said. Her voice came out level, precise, the voice of a woman reconciling an account. She marveled at it from somewhere far away. "He was wounded. He gave me something and died. I pulled nothing, Captain, because by my count the wardens were already—" she paused, delicately, a scalpel's pause, watching his face, "—late."

Something flickered behind Doss's comfortable features. Not guilt. Recognition—the look of a man hearing a phrase in a language he'd been assured she didn't speak.

"Shock," he said kindly. "It's a terrible thing, finding a body. You'll want to give your account upstairs. First Auditor Corvane will want to hear it personally." He stepped aside and gestured to the corridor with a courtesy that was also a cage. "Come along, Clerk Winterbourne. Leave the map on the counter, please. We'll log it with the rest."

The word *map* dropped into the room like ice into water.

"I gave the man's effects no examination," Mara said. "Procedure requires inventory by a second officer."

Doss smiled. It reached his eyes the way winter reaches a window: visibly, without warming anything. "Of course," he said. "We'll search for it together, then."

Behind his shoulder, three wardens had already begun, casually, expertly, turning the room over—lifting the ledger, patting the stranger's coat, running fingers along the counter's underside—and Mara stood very straight in the middle of her clean, ordered, rule-bound world, feeling the folded paper pulse against her skin like a stolen coal, and understood that the rules had never once been meant to protect her.

They had only ever been meant to keep her from noticing the walls.

She walked with the wardens up the stairs toward Corvane, spine straight, steps measured, the perfect clerk to the last—and inside her, beneath six years of quiet and order and balanced sums, something with a sword's weight and a burned orchard's heat turned over in its sleep, and began, slowly, to wake.
