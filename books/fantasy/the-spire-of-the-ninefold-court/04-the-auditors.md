## 4. The Auditors

The summons came the way all summons from the Ninefold Court came: folded into an ordinary envelope, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and left somewhere she could not fail to find it. Mara found hers inside her boot.

She had not put it there. That was the point. The Court wanted her to know that its reach extended into the spaces between her skin and her own possessions, into the small private geography of a woman dressing alone before dawn. She stood in her narrow room above the chandler's shop, one stocking on, and read the single line of script—her own handwriting, or close enough to wound her:

*Mara Winterbourne, keeper of the Ledger of Small Debts. Present yourself for annual review at the ninth hour. Wear nothing you cannot account for.*

The map was folded into the lining of her coat, against her ribs. She had slept with it there, though she told herself it was only caution. Now she took it out, weighed it in her hands—the strange lightness of it, as if the paper had been hollowed—and made the only decision available to her. There was no hiding place in her rooms the Court's auditors would not think of first. So she hid it where she had hidden everything else worth keeping: behind her teeth.

She pressed the map flat between two pages of her personal ledger—the honest one, the one she kept of debts freely owed, a habit from a life she could not remember living—and spoke the binding word every swordspeaker learned before they learned to swing a blade. She did not know what the word meant anymore. Her mouth knew it the way her hands knew sword-forms her mind did not: *Vessany remembers you*, the stranger had said, dying, and the word rose in her throat unbidden, tasting of smoke and orchards.

The map thinned to a whisper, a pressure against the page, gone. Not destroyed. Filed. Somewhere in her own body, in whatever vault the oath had built when it took her memory as payment, the map of Vessany waited to be withdrawn.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them until they stopped.

*Wear nothing you cannot account for.* She dressed plainly, counted her coins aloud like a child at lessons, and went down into the city while the lamps were still burning themselves out against the grey.

* * *

The Spire of the Ninefold Court did not stand in the city so much as the city arranged itself around it, streets bending toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone. Nine floors, nine auditors, nine ledgers—one for each kind of impossible thing the guild traded in. Mara had worked three floors below the First Auditor for eleven years and had climbed past the fourth landing exactly twice.

The counting-room smelled of tallow and old paper and, faintly, of the sea—a smell with no possible source, since the nearest coast was four hundred leagues east. New keepers asked about it. Old keepers stopped asking. It was the smell of things being kept, Mara thought. Of inventory. Of everything the Court had ever taken and locked away so carefully that even the air remembered it.

At the door to the review chamber, a junior warden patted her down with hands that were thorough and impersonal as scales. He found her ledger of free debts, frowned at it—it was not contraband, but it was unusual; keepers were not encouraged to keep accounts the Court did not set—and let her pass.

"First Auditor Corvane will see you now," said a voice from nowhere, which meant from everywhere. The doors opened onto a room that was much larger than the floor it sat on, because rooms in the Spire were appraisable assets too, and this one had been assessed as *vast*.

Corvane sat behind a desk of black wood at the far end of all that space, so that anyone entering had to cross the whole distance under his gaze, like a debtor walking the length of a ledger line. He was not what new keepers expected. No cruelty in his face, no appetite. He looked like a man who had been very tired for a very long time and had decided, decades ago, to stop mentioning it. His hair was the white of paper left too long in sun. His eyes were brown and patient and missed nothing, which was worse than sharpness.

"Mara Winterbourne." He pronounced her name carefully, the way one handles an item of uncertain provenance. "Sit."

She sat. The chair across from him was the only other chair in the vastness.

"Annual review," Corvane said. "You know the form. Eleven years, no irregularities, no debts outstanding, no unauthorized acquisitions." He opened a folder—paper, actual paper, which meant he intended to show her something. "Let us begin with the routine and end wherever we end. Your last appraisal. A bottled memory, vintage indeterminate, seller a fishmonger off Cordwainer Row. You valued it at forty marks. Walk me through your reasoning."

Mara relaxed by a degree. This she knew. "The bottle was genuine second-pressing, not a forgery—the seal had the true wax bloom, not the poured kind. The memory inside was a wedding, ordinary, provincial. But the bride's face was wrong for the groom's decade. Clothing said sixty years past; the memory itself felt thirty. Someone had aged it deliberately, or it had been stored near something older and had absorbed the years. Either way, imperfect goods. Forty marks, sold as curiosity, flagged for the Ledger of Borrowed Years in case the aging proved transferable."

"And the borrowed-years account you balanced Tuesday. The Vane sisters. One sold the other eleven months."

"Eleven months and six days," Mara said. "I rounded down in the ledger and recorded the surplus separately. Surplus years accrue interest. It's in the standing rules."

"It is in the standing rules," Corvane agreed. He turned a page without looking at it. "Your precision is noted, as it is noted annually. You are, I believe, the most accurate keeper on the fifth floor. Possibly the third." He looked up then, fully, and Mara understood that everything before had been the runway and this was the takeoff. "Tell me about last night."

"The counting-room closed at the tenth bell. I balanced my day-book. I went home."

"A stranger came to your door."

"No stranger came to my door."

"He came through it," Corvane said mildly. "Bled on your floor. Died in your arms, or nearly enough. And gave you something before he went."

The lamp between them hissed. Mara heard herself say, in a voice so level it frightened her more than any tremor would have, "No one died in my arms, First Auditor. If a man bled in the counting-room, the wardens who arrived would have found the blood. They found none. I swept the floor myself before locking up. There was nothing to report, and reporting nothing is not reporting."

Silence. Corvane studied her the way she had once studied a bottled memory held to the light—looking for the bloom of true wax, looking for the flaw, looking for the age beneath the apparent age.

And Mara discovered, in that silence, something she had no ledger column for: lying felt like remembering. Not like forgetting, which was what she had always assumed dishonesty would be—a turning away, a blankness. It felt like the moment she had pressed the map into her own ledger and spoken the word her mouth knew. Like reaching into a vault in herself and drawing out something that fit her hand perfectly, something with her own fingerprints already on it. *No one died in my arms.* Where had that steadiness come from? Who had she been, before the oath took the name of it, that she could lie to the First Auditor of the Ninefold Court and feel—homesick?

"That will be all, then," Corvane said, and closed the folder, and Mara knew with absolute certainty that she had not passed anything, merely been granted a reprieve whose terms were unwritten.

She rose, bowed the precise degree owed to a First Auditor, and walked the long walk back across the vastness. At the doors his voice followed her, unhurried as settling dust.

"Keeper Winterbourne. Should anything come to your attention that cannot be accounted for—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk— "the Court pays well for accounts."

* * *

First Auditor Corvane waited until her footsteps faded, and then waited longer, which was his habit. In eleven thousand reviews he had learned that the room after the room told him more than the room itself. Keepers thought they had survived him. They walked out straightening their coats, congratulating their nerve, and their shoulders dropped a half-inch too soon, and he saw it, because seeing was the whole of his work.

He opened the drawer where he kept the things that were not yet ledgers.

The warden's report lay on top: a man, unnamed, dead of wounds taken elsewhere, found in the gutter on Chandler's Lane forty minutes after Keeper Winterbourne's building went dark. No blood trail leading to her door—which was itself a finding; blood does not politely erase itself, and erased blood has a smell, and the wardens had been sent too late by someone who had known exactly how late to send them. Corvane had read the routing order twice. The delay bore the countersignature of Third Auditor Hesse, and Hesse did nothing without instruction, and the instruction had come from above Hesse, which meant from the Ninth, which meant the review Mara Winterbourne had just undergone was theater staged for an audience of one auditor who was also, possibly, its subject.

He did not mind being a subject. Subjects were still agents. What he minded—he pressed his thumb against the ache behind his eyes, the ache that had lived there for thirty years like a tenant who paid no rent—was that he could not remember why the Ninth Auditor feared a dying smuggler's gift to a fifth-floor bookkeeper. He knew that the Ninth feared it. Fear leaves tracks in institutions the same way blood leaves tracks on floors, and Corvane had spent his career reading tracks.

He drew out the second item in the drawer: a treaty, thirty-one years old, signed in nine places. He had helped draft it. He remembered the drafting with perfect clarity—the salt language, the clauses that did not erase Vessany so much as make its erasure *accounted for*, filed, balanced, a debt entered on the world's books and marked paid. He remembered the taste of the ink. He remembered the orchard country described in annex seven, and the names of the villages, and the number of the doors.

What he could not remember—not ever, not once in thirty years, though he had searched that particular dark a thousand times—was why, when he recited annex seven to himself at night, as he did, as penance or as prayer, he did not know—

His hand had stopped moving. Ink pooled under his nib, blooming on the page like wax.

He did not know why he regretted it. That was the fact he kept in the deepest part of the drawer, beneath the treaty, beneath even his own commission. Every clause he had drafted, he could defend. Every balance the Unmaking Ledgers struck, he could justify before all nine chairs. And underneath the justification, structural as a foundation stone, load-bearing as guilt: regret. Regret with no object, no origin, no name attached. He had long ago stopped telling himself it was conscience. Conscience attaches to reasons. This was older than his reasons. It was, he suspected sometimes, in the small hours when the Spire creaked around him like a ship, the only piece of himself the ledgers had failed to itemize—and therefore the only proof that there had ever been anything in him worth taking.

A knock. Hesse, gray-faced, holding a routing slip. "First Auditor. The Ninth requests—"

"I know what the Ninth requests." Corvane blotted the ink stain without looking at it. "Send the collectors to the memory-markets. Quietly. And have someone watch the Winterbourne woman's building—but watch, Hesse. Nothing touches her until I say."

Hesse hesitated. "The Ninth's order says—"

"The Ninth's orders route through me. That is what *First* means." He said it without heat, and Hesse went, and Corvane sat alone in the vast room with the treaty and the stain and the ache behind his eyes.

She had lied to him. He had watched her do it—the stillness of her hands, the level voice, the small perfect economy of *there was nothing to report*. Eleven years of flawless accuracy, and tonight she had produced a flawless falsehood, and the two skills, he knew from long professional experience, were the same skill wearing different clothes. Precision was precision. A keeper who could balance a borrowed-years account to the day could balance a lie to the syllable.

Which meant she had the thing. Or knew where it was. Or *was* where it was, in some sense he would not understand until he understood the girl herself—and there his file betrayed him, thin as it was. An orphan of nowhere in particular, apprenticed young, oath-bound at nineteen like all swordspeakers, payment taken per standard terms. Standard terms. He pulled her intake record and read the payment line again, as he had read it every year on the anniversary of her binding, though he had never asked himself why he kept the habit.

*Payment received: one (1) memory, content withheld per standard terms, depositor: the Court.*

Withheld. Not unspecified—*withheld*. Someone had chosen not to know what they took from her. In thirty years of auditing the Court's cruelties he had never seen that notation. Debts were itemized or they were not debts. A memory taken blind was not payment; it was theft wearing payment's coat.

Corvane sat for a long time in the lamp-hiss, an old tired man in a room too large for any honest purpose, holding the intake record of a bookkeeper who had lied to him beautifully, and feeling—without reason, without name, with that same foundation-stone certainty—that the debt the Court had taken from her and the debt he could not remember owing were entries on the same page.

He wrote a single line in his private book, the one no auditor was permitted to keep: *The Winterbourne account is open. Watch it. Watch yourself watching it.*

Then he put out the lamp, and in the dark the Spire settled around him, nine floors of impossible inventory, and somewhere below, in a room full of bottles, a memory thirty-one years old sat waiting to be withdrawn by whoever finally spoke the right word.

He almost knew the word. It sat on his tongue like ash.

It began with *V*.
