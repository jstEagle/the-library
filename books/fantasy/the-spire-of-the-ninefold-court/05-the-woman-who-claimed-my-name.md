## 5. The Woman Who Claimed My Name

The memory-markets of Low Sallow woke before dawn, the way carrion birds wake—because something had already died, and there was work to be done.

Ilsa Winterbourne had been awake for hours. She stood beneath the crooked awning of a tea-stall that smelled of scorched tin and cardamom gone bitter in its jar, watching the stalls unfold like moths from their cases: trays of bottled memories racked by vintage, by intensity, by the delicacy of their provenance; dealers with scales calibrated to weigh regret against wonder; children too young to know better hawking stolen first kisses to men old enough to have forgotten what kissing was for.

She had crossed three cities to be here. She had spent four years of her own life—actual years, drawn out of her marrow and sold at a loss—to learn where Mara Winterbourne kept her ledgers. And now she had found her, or would soon, and Ilsa's hands were shaking so badly she had to wrap them around her cup just to make them stop.

Not fear. She wanted to be clear with herself about that much, at least. Not fear. Something closer to the vertigo you feel standing at the edge of a well you once fell into, knowing the water is still down there, knowing it remembers you.

* * *

The Court's collectors worked the morning markets the way customs men work a harbor: unhurried, inevitable, entitled. Ilsa had been one of them once—still was, technically, though her writ had lapsed somewhere between Vellamere and here, and she did not intend to mention it. She knew the collector's walk, the flat unhurried gaze that said *I am not shopping, I am inventorying*. She wore it now as camouflage, drifting past the memory-sellers' row while her eyes did other work entirely.

It was the third stall that gave her what she wanted. A dealer named Ossory—a soft man with ink-stained cuffs and the guilty posture of someone who has sold things he shouldn't have—was wrapping a bottle in waxed paper with unusual care, and asking unusual questions of his runner. *"Did she say when she'd come back? Did she say anything about—"*

He stopped when he saw Ilsa looking. Collectors made people stop talking. It was one of the profession's few pleasures.

"Morning," Ilsa said, pleasant as rot. "You've got a customer who buys precision. Bottled memories, high clarity, recent vintages. Am I right?"

Ossory's face performed several small calculations and settled on stonewalling. "I serve all kinds."

"You serve one kind twice." She set a coin on his tray—not a large one, but an honest one, which in Low Sallow was rarer and more unsettling than generosity. "A woman comes to you. Younger than me. Carries herself like a clerk but stands like a soldier. Buys clean memories, no embellishment, nothing sentimental. She checks the seal-work twice and counts her change."

Something flickered behind Ossory's eyes—the look of a man recognizing a description he'd hoped never to hear spoken aloud. "That could be anyone."

"It could," Ilsa agreed. "But it isn't. When does she come?"

"I don't—"

"Ossory." She leaned close enough that he could see the scar through her eyebrow, the one she'd earned the night Vessany ended, though he couldn't know that. "Four years ago I watched a country get folded up like a letter and put away. Everyone in it went into the fold. I'm the only letter that ever climbed back out. So when I tell you that the woman you're afraid of is smaller than the thing I'm not afraid of, you can believe me or you can spend the rest of your life wondering if I'll come back to ask again."

He told her. Of course he told her. They always told her; that had always been the trouble with Ilsa Winterbourne, according to everyone who had ever tried to keep a secret from her. She asked the way a knife asks.

Dusk, then. The woman came at dusk, after the market emptied, when the light made honesty expensive and discretion cheap.

* * *

Ilsa followed her for two hours before she spoke.

It was not sentimentality that stayed her hand, whatever she later told herself. It was craft. A collector learns patience the way a swordspeaker learns forms—until it lives in the body below thought—and besides, she wanted to see how Mara moved through the world. What she saw unsettled her more than any ambush could have.

Mara Winterbourne walked through Low Sallow the way water walks through a pipe: perfectly, invisibly, without touching the sides. Street vendors didn't mark her. Pickpockets' eyes slid off her. Once, a warden of the Court passed within arm's reach, and Ilsa saw the warden's gaze cross Mara's face and register *nothing*, the way you register a wall. It was a trained absence, a discipline of unremarkability so complete it looked like a gift. Ilsa knew that discipline. She had seen it before, on another girl, years ago, in an orchard that was burning.

*You taught yourself that,* she thought, watching her sister buy a twist of bread from a vendor who forgot her the moment she turned away. *Or someone taught it to you. Or someone cut it into you along with everything else.*

At the mouth of Tanner's Row, where the gutters ran amber with dye-water and the lamps came on one by one like reluctant witnesses, Ilsa stepped out of the shadow of a doorway and said her name.

"Mara."

Mara stopped. She didn't startle—that was the first uncanny thing—but turned with a smooth economy, one hand already resting near the plain sword at her hip, weight settling onto her back foot without her seeming to decide it. Her eyes were gray and level and utterly unfamiliar, and Ilsa felt something in her chest crack along a seam she had spent four years pretending wasn't there.

Because she knew those eyes. Not this woman's expression—the ledger-keeper's composure, the stranger's courtesy—but the eyes themselves. Their color. Their particular way of narrowing, left lid a fraction lower than right, when assessing a threat.

*Mama's eyes,* Ilsa thought, and hated herself for thinking it, because Mama's face was the first thing the treaty had taken from her and she no longer trusted her own memory of it.

"You have the advantage of me," Mara said. Her voice was cool, precise, pitched to carry exactly far enough. A voice built for counting-houses.

"Do I?" Ilsa smiled, and knew even as she did it that the smile was wrong, too sharp, too hungry, grief wearing a merchant's mask. "My name is Ilsa. Ilsa Winterbourne."

She had rehearsed this moment across three cities and four lost years. She had imagined Mara's disbelief, Mara's anger, Mara's careful questions. She had prepared proofs: the birthmark, the lullaby, the name of their childhood dog. She had planned to ration them, one at a time, the way you feed a fire you don't want to scare with too much heat.

What she actually said was none of it. What she actually said was:

"You're my little sister, and they stole eleven years off your life to pay for your oath, and I want them back."

* * *

To her credit—for Ilsa was keeping score, some petty ledger of her own—Mara did not laugh, or run, or call for the wardens whose shadows filled every street in this district. She stood very still, and Ilsa watched her do the thing she had apparently been built to do: assess, weigh, file.

"Winterbourne," Mara repeated. "That's a common enough name in the river provinces."

"It's not common anywhere. That's rather the point of us." Ilsa kept her hands visible and empty—an old habit from dealing with frightened sellers, though nothing about this woman read as frightened. "Say it aloud. Winterbourne. Tell me it doesn't sit strangely in your mouth."

"Names often do, the first time." But Mara's brow had creased, that fractional asymmetry, and Ilsa clung to the sight of it like a drowning woman clinging to driftwood. "Who told you about me? Was it Corvane?"

The name landed between them like a dropped glass.

"So you do know him," Ilsa breathed. "First Auditor Corvane. You know him well enough to reach for his name when a stranger claims kinship."

"I know him the way any keeper knows an auditor. He signs my reviews." Mara's hand hadn't left the vicinity of her sword, and now Ilsa understood why, and the understanding hurt worse than the accusation would have. Mara wasn't guarding against her. She was guarding *for* the Court—out of reflex, out of oath, out of eleven purchased years of loyalty compacted into her spine like sediment. "You should be careful, throwing that name around. And you should be careful claiming things about me. The Court takes a dim view of fraud practiced on its officers."

"Fraud." Ilsa laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "Gods. Four years, and my sister calls me a fraud to my face."

"My sister died in Vessany."

The words came out of Mara flatly, factually, the way she might report an exchange rate—and then she stopped. Went pale. Brought one hand halfway to her mouth as if to catch something escaping.

Ilsa stared at her. "Say that again."

"I don't—" Mara's voice had lost its counting-house polish; underneath it was something rawer, younger, alarmed. "I don't know why I said that. I don't know how I know that."

And there it was. There it was, the seam showing through the seamless surface, the proof Ilsa had crossed mountains to find and now wished she could unfind, because seeing it hurt more than doubting ever had. Mara Winterbourne, last oath-bound swordspeaker, keeper of ledgers, stood in Tanner's Row holding a piece of knowledge she could not account for, and her face was doing what faces do when a locked room is discovered inside a house you thought you knew floor to ceiling.

"The treaty took Vessany," Ilsa said gently, and hated the gentleness, because gentleness meant pity and pity meant admitting how bad this was. "Folded it out of the world. Every map, every record, every mind outside its borders. Nobody remembers it because nobody's allowed to. Except the people who were *in* it, and they're either dead or erased or—" she gestured at Mara, at the whole composed impossibility of her, "—or bought and repurposed. Your oath, Mara. Swordspeakers swear on paid memory. Everyone knows the guild takes years. Nobody asks *which* ones. They took your whole country off your shelf and sold it to fund your loyalty. You've been keeping their books with hands that used to hold a different sword."

"You can't prove any of that."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "Proof is the Court's currency. They minted all of it and melted down the molds. All I've got is—" she reached into her coat, slowly, letting Mara watch every inch of the motion, and drew out a small object wrapped in oilcloth, "—this."

She unwrapped it. A dried apple, shriveled to the size of a plum, threaded on a cord. It had traveled folded inside a book, hidden inside a wall, buried under a salt flat, and it smelled—even now, even after everything—faintly of smoke and cinnamon.

"Our mother made these every autumn," Ilsa said. "From the orchard on the south slope. You used to steal them off the drying racks and blame the starlings. You were six, maybe seven. You'd stand in the kitchen with your cheeks full like a hamster and swear on the Ninefold—" she caught herself, grimaced, "—swear on anything that the birds did it, and Mama would laugh until she had to sit down, and you'd laugh too, and neither of you knew—I didn't know—that I'd spend the rest of my life unable to remember her face except in that kitchen, in that light."

The lamp above them hissed. Somewhere a door slammed; the city went on with its business of forgetting.

Mara was looking at the apple the way a person looks at a word in a language they dreamed they'd forgotten. Her lips parted. Nothing came out. Then, barely audible, in a voice that had nothing of the counting-room in it at all:

"*Starlings.*"

"Yes," Ilsa whispered.

"I hate starlings." Mara frowned, correcting herself with visible effort, each word pried loose like a nail from old wood. "No. I—there's no reason to hate starlings. I have no memory of—why would I hate starlings? I've never—"

"Orchard," Ilsa said. "South slope. Drying racks."

Mara pressed the heels of both hands against her eyes. For a long moment she simply stood there, breathing, a woman performing maintenance on her own foundations. When she lowered her hands, the clerk was back in place, the composure reassembled—but Ilsa had seen the scaffolding now. You can't unsee it.

"This proves nothing," Mara said quietly. "A dried apple and a story. Stories are the cheapest commodity in this city. You must know that better than anyone—you used to deal in them." She paused. Frowned again, deeper this time. "Why did I say 'used to'? I don't know your history. Why does half my mouth know things my mind doesn't?"

"Because they took the head and left the body," Ilsa said. "Ask your hands sometime what they remember. Ask them about the third form, the low sweeping guard nobody's taught in thirty years. Ask them what our father called dinner when it burned."

"Stop." It came out sharp, a blade's worth of sound, and Mara's hand did go to her sword hilt then, fully, though whether to draw or merely to anchor herself, even she perhaps couldn't have said. "Stop. I have a life. I have work that matters, accounts that balance, an oath I chose—" her voice caught on *chose*, minutely, a hairline fracture in the word, "—an oath I *chose*, and obligations, and tomorrow morning I am due at the Court, and you are standing in front of me selling me a country I have never heard of, a family I have never had, and a theft I have no evidence for. Do you understand what you're asking? You're asking me to believe that everything I am is collateral."

"I'm asking you to believe that everything you are was *stolen*," Ilsa said, "and that I'm the only witness left who knows the difference."

They stood facing each other in the lamplight, two women with the same eyes, one of them certain and one of them terrified of becoming certain. Down the row, a memory-dealer was closing his shutters, and through the gaps came the faint sweet smell of bottled childhood going stale in the dark.

"I won't come with you," Mara said at last. "I won't abandon my post on the word of a stranger with a sad story and good timing. If the Court erased a country, there will be records. Ledgers. Entries. Debts leave paper trails, even impossible debts—*especially* impossible debts. I'll look. Quietly. Through proper channels." She said it as though proper channels were a religion and she a devout woman comforting herself with liturgy. "If you're lying, I'll find that too, and I'll hand you to the auditors myself. Are we understood?"

Understood, and heartbreaking, and so exactly like her that Ilsa had to turn her face away toward the gutter-lamps until the tightness in her throat eased. *Look through their records. Use their channels. Trust their paper.* Eleven years of ownership, compounding interest.

"We're understood," she said.

Mara nodded, once, precise as a stamp on a document, and walked away down Tanner's Row, straight-backed, invisible, dissolving into the crowd like sugar into tea—gone so completely that within ten breaths Ilsa could not have pointed to her in a hundred strangers.

Ilsa stood alone in the lamplight a long while, turning the dried apple over in her fingers, and let herself feel, for exactly the length of one breath, the full horror of what she had done: she had taken a woman whose life fit her like a well-worn glove, and told her the glove was stolen goods.

Then she closed her fist around the apple and went to find a room, because tomorrow the real work began—and because she needed to be awake, and armed, when the Court noticed that its most reliable ledger-keeper had started asking questions.

She did not yet know that Mara would not wait for morning. That even now, three streets away, Mara Winterbourne had stopped beneath a lamp, drawn out the map a dying man had pressed on her, and was staring at a single word written in the margin in handwriting that was almost—almost—her own:

*Vessany.*

And that the word, as she watched, was slowly rearranging itself into another:

*Sister.*
