## 6. Nine Doors, None of Them Ours

The map's first road began at a tannery.

Mara had walked the Saltmarket district a hundred times in her years of errands for the Court—delivering appraisals, collecting debts, carrying sealed boxes whose contents she was paid not to wonder about. She knew the district the way she knew the ledgers: by its entries. The tannery on Corvis Lane, assessed at forty crowns and falling. The dye-works with its three unpaid interest notes. The row of lodging houses where the poor of Vell's Crossing stacked themselves like coins in a purse, each one thinner than the last.

But she had never seen the door.

That was the first thing that unsettled her—not the door itself, but her own certainty that it had not been there before. Mara Winterbourne did not miss doors. She missed nothing; that was her function, her craft, the quiet pride beneath the numbness. She could count the number of steps between the tannery gate and the well without looking. She knew which shutter on the corner house hung by one nail and had hung that way for six years. And yet here was a door set into the flank wall of a collapsed dyer's shop, a door she would have sworn on any ledger in the Ninefold Court had not existed yesterday.

It was a small door, waist-high in the rubble, made of orchard wood gone silver-grey with weather. No handle. No hinges she could see. Just a ring of iron, green with age, and carved into the grain above it, so shallow she had to tilt her head against the morning light to read it:

*Vessany keeps what Vessany loves.*

Her breath went strange in her chest. She recognized the words. Not from any document—the Court's archives held no mention of Vessany beyond a single line in a treaty appendix, *territorial adjustments rendered moot*, four words that unmade a country as casually as striking an entry from a ledger. She recognized them from the map. From the flash behind her eyes when she'd touched it: the burning orchard, the woman's voice saying *run home, little bird, run*—and then, underneath all of it, these words, spoken in a child's cadence, a skipping rhyme or a blessing, *Vessany keeps what Vessany loves.*

Mara stood in the Saltmarket slum with her ledger-case strapped tight across her back and her hands very still at her sides, and she made herself do what she always did: she counted.

One door. No handle. One ring of iron. Seven words of carving. Zero entries in any Court record accounting for its existence.

The numbers refused to balance, and for the first time in her career, the refusal did not frighten her. It *invited* her. That frightened her more.

She looked down the lane both ways. A woman was beating a rug two houses down, methodically, without looking up. A boy chased a hoop past the tannery. Ordinary life, indifferent to her. Whatever came next, she would do it alone, because there was no one she could tell. That thought, which had once been the bedrock of her contentment—that her life required no witnesses—now felt less like safety than like a cell with the door left open.

She knelt in the rubble. The stone was cold through her skirts. She took the iron ring in her right hand, and her hand remembered something her mind did not: how to grip, how to pull low and smooth rather than high and jerking, the way one opens a gate one has opened ten thousand times.

The door swung inward without a sound.

---

Warm air came out. That was the wrongness of it, the first wrongness—air that smelled of smoke and ripening fruit and cut grass, air warm as late summer, pouring out of a slum wall in the cold grey of early spring. Mara knelt there breathing it, and some animal part of her brain said *orchard*, said *home*, said *you know this smell*, and the rest of her said *you have never smelled anything like this in your life.*

Beyond the threshold, instead of the dark interior of a ruined shop, there was light. Gold light, slanting, the light of an afternoon rather than a morning. Rows of trees marched away from her into haze—apple trees, heavy with fruit, their leaves moving in a wind she could not feel from where she knelt. And the trees were burning.

Not consumed. Burning the way a painting is painted: flames stood up from the branches in fixed ribbons, orange and white at their hearts, and the apples within the fire were whole, gilded, unbruised. Smoke rose in sculpted columns and simply stopped, mid-curl, thirty feet up, as if the sky itself had refused it passage. Where flame met grass, the grass neither blackened nor bowed. Everything was paused. Everything was *mid-*.

Mara had spent her life among impossible things. She had appraised a bottled memory of a drowning—a vial of grey-green light that wept condensation—and balanced accounts of borrowed years, and once inventoried a door to a country that no longer existed, though she had never been permitted to learn which country. She had thought herself finished with astonishment. She had thought the Court had priced wonder out of her, the way you price salt out of bread.

She knelt in the rubble of the Saltmarket and felt wonder come back into her like blood into a sleeping limb, painful and sweet, and she hated herself a little for how much she wanted to step through.

She stepped through.

The ground inside was soft, orchard loam, and warm. The heat of the frozen fire touched her face like an open oven held at a distance—present, insistent, not yet painful. She walked between the rows with her hand out before her, testing the air the way she'd test the edge of a blade, and the flames let her pass close enough to see the fruit inside them, apples with skins like hammered gold, each one perfect, each one thirty years past ripe and none the worse for it.

There were ladders in the orchard. Wooden picking-ladders, leaned against trunks, and baskets beneath the trees, half-full. And there were people—or the shapes of people. Far down the third row, a figure stood on a ladder with one arm raised toward a high branch, and the figure did not move, and the flame had taken the ladder's top step and stopped there, and Mara understood, with a cold that started in her stomach and climbed, that the fire had caught these people mid-motion, mid-harvest, mid-life, and that whatever preserved them had preserved their dying too. They were not statues. They were not dead. They were *paused*, the way an account is paused when the auditor cannot decide whether to write it off or call it due.

Thirty years, she thought. Thirty years this orchard has been burning, and no one has come to put it out, because no one remembers it is burning. Because a treaty struck it from the maps and a guild struck it from the ledgers, and a fire with no witnesses is not a fire at all—it is only weather in an empty room.

Her eyes were stinging. She told herself it was the smoke.

She went deeper. She told herself she was gathering evidence, cataloguing, doing what she was trained to do—but the ledger-case stayed shut on her back and her hands stayed empty at her sides, and she knew she was not working. She was looking for something. She did not know what. A name carved in bark. A face she might recognize. Some entry in this burned and beautiful place that would balance against the hollow in herself, the memory-shaped absence the oath had left, the debt she could not itemize because she did not know what had been taken.

Halfway down the fifth row she found a tree with names cut into it.

Old cuts, healed over into ridges, the way bark heals. Three sets of initials, at three heights—one high, one middle, one low, the heights of three children of different ages climbing the same trunk year after year. She brushed the ash-frost from the bark with her sleeve. The lowest carving was small and deep and fierce, cut by a determined hand: *M.W.* Her own initials. Cut by a child, into a tree in a country that had never existed, thirty years before she was—before she was anything. Before the oath. Before the Court. Before the numbness.

Mara put her fingers into the grooves of the letters, and they fit.

She was still standing there, fingers in the bark of a tree that was burning and not burning, when the fire moved.

---

It should not have been possible. Nothing in the orchard had moved since she entered; the whole country was a held breath. But the flame on the nearest tree leaned—there was no other word for it, the ribbon of fire bent toward her like wheat bending toward wind—and where it bent, it *unfroze*. The pause broke around her the way ice breaks around a dropped stone, in running cracks, and suddenly the fire was fire again, hungry and loud, and the heat hit her full in the face and the smoke was real smoke, choking, and somewhere down the row the figure on the ladder made a sound.

Mara ran. She was not a runner; she was a counter, a keeper, a woman who moved through the world precisely and quietly. But her legs knew things. They took the row at speed, dodged a root her eyes had barely registered, and when a burning branch came down across her path her body dropped and rolled under it with a fluency that belonged to someone else, someone trained, someone who had done this a thousand times in a country that no longer existed. Her mind watched her own body from a distance and wrote, in the calm clerk's voice she used for everything: *Subject displays combat reflexes inconsistent with recorded history. Recommend investigation.*

The smoke was winning. The orchard that had been frozen gold was now roaring orange, thirty years of paused combustion coming due all at once, an entire country's deferred death demanding payment with interest, and Mara stumbled against a trunk and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was going to die in a place that was not on any map, and that no ledger anywhere would record it.

Then someone took her by the collar and threw her.

Not gently. A hand fisted in the back of her coat, a pivot, a heave—she went through the doorway sideways, hit the rubble of the dyer's shop, and rolled, and the last thing she saw through the shrinking rectangle of silver-grey wood was the orchard roaring and a woman silhouetted against it, walking backward out of the fire with a long knife in one hand and her coat over her mouth.

Then the door swung shut, and the Saltmarket was cold and grey and ordinary, and Mara lay in the rubble coughing ash onto stones that had never been warm.

---

The woman crouched beside her, and it was Ilsa.

Of course it was Ilsa. Mara had met her twice since the stranger died in the counting-room—once in the memory-markets off Chandler's Row, where Ilsa had appeared at her elbow among the bottle-stalls and said *I know what they took from you*, and once outside the Court itself, waiting under the eaves in the rain, saying nothing, just watching, until the wardens came and Ilsa melted away. *Sister*, she kept saying. *I'm your sister.* Mara had filed the claim where she filed all unverifiable claims: pending, unbalanced, dismissed.

Now Ilsa knelt over her in the ash-grey light, soot-streaked, breathing hard, and there was no market crowd to disappear into and no rain to hide behind, and Mara saw her properly for the first time. Older than Mara by some years. Dark-haired where Mara was fair, broad-handed, with a scar through one eyebrow and eyes the exact grey-brown of orchard loam. Eyes she had seen somewhere before. Eyes she had perhaps seen every day of a childhood she did not remember.

"You followed me," Mara rasped.

"I've been following you for three weeks." Ilsa pulled a flask from her coat and held it to Mara's lips. The liquor was harsh and clean and brought tears to her eyes. "You walked into a burning country alone. With a ledger on your back."

"It's evidence."

"It's a *ledger*, Mara. You carry it like a rosary." Ilsa sat back on her heels. Up close, in the flat slum light, the family resemblance was worse than uncanny—it was structural, the same jaw, the same set of the mouth, arranged differently, like the same house built twice by different hands. "You want to know what happened in there? The preservation broke when you crossed the threshold. It's been holding for thirty years because nobody fed it. Then you walk in wearing an oath the Court bought with stolen Vessani years, and the whole country wakes up and says *oh, there's one of ours*, and starts trying to finish dying."

"Then why didn't it finish me?"

"Because I got you out." Ilsa said it without triumph, which somehow made it worse. "And because the fire wasn't trying to kill you. It was trying to *reach* you. There's a difference. You'll understand the difference eventually, and you'll wish you didn't."

Mara pushed herself upright against the broken wall. Her hands were shaking. She looked at them—at the soot in the creases, at the steadiness returning finger by finger—and made herself say the thing she had crossed the city to avoid saying.

"The tree had my initials."

"I know."

"You knew about the tree."

"I grew up climbing it." Ilsa's voice did not waver, but something behind it did, some load-bearing grief shifting its weight. "Lowest carving. Small and deep and fierce. I used to tease you about it—I said you cut your initials like you were trying to hurt the tree into remembering you." She looked away, down the lane, at the rug-beating woman and the boy with his hoop, ordinary life going on around a door that led to a burning country. "You don't remember any of it. I know. I've had time to make peace with being forgotten. What I can't make peace with is *them*. The Court didn't just take your memories, Mara. They took a whole country and called it a territorial adjustment, and they keep it in ledgers like the one on your back, and every day you carry that thing, you're carrying the instrument that erased us."

Mara's hand went to the strap across her back. Reflex. Possession. She hated the reflex even as she felt it.

"If I give it up," she said slowly, "they'll know. Corvane will know. He already suspects. And without the Court's protection—" She heard herself, heard the clerk's arithmetic, the arithmetic of a woman calculating whether her own sister was worth the cost of her salary, and she stopped. Started again, plainer. "I don't remember you. I want to be honest about that, because you deserve honesty more than you deserve comfort. I look at you and I see a stranger who knows too much about me. Every instinct I have says you're either a liar or a trap."

"I know."

"But my hands rolled under a burning branch like they'd done it a hundred times. My fingers fit the cuts in that tree. And you pulled me out of a fire that should have killed us both, and you didn't even hesitate." Mara stood, joints aching, ash sifting from her coat. "So here are my terms. Not trust. Terms. You walk with me, you answer what I ask, and you don't touch the map. In exchange, I'll stop filing you under *pending*. Provisionally."

Ilsa laughed—a short, rough sound with no real humor in it, but real relief. "Provisionally," she agreed. "You always negotiated like this. Even at six. You once traded me two days of chores for half my honey-bread and convinced me it was my idea."

The words landed somewhere below Mara's ribs, in the hollow, and echoed off walls she couldn't see. Honey. Something in chapter sixteen of her future was already keeping a list of things the speaking would take, and *the taste of honey* was on it, though she didn't know that yet—all she knew was that Ilsa's throwaway sentence had hurt in a way that felt less like grief than like *recognition*, like a key turning in a lock she'd been told was decorative.

"The door," Mara said, to cover it. "When it closed—did you see where it went? The road on the map has eight more doors after this one. If the preservation breaks every time I cross—"

"Then next time we go in together, and we go in fast." Ilsa hauled her upright, gripped her arm, steadied her—an older sister's grip, proprietary and sure, and Mara let herself be steadied for exactly three seconds before stepping back. "But not today. Today you're covered in ash and the Saltmarket is asking questions, and there's a warden patrol that comes down Corvis Lane at the half bell. We move."

They moved. Out of the lane, into the warren, two women walking fast through the poor quarter with soot on their coats and a secret between them shaped like a small silver-grey door.

Behind them, in the wall of the ruined dyer's shop, faintly, through the seam of a door that should have been shut, came the smell of apples and smoke—and, softer than either, a sound like a woman's voice, very far away, singing a skipping rhyme to three children whose initials were carved in a burning tree:

*Vessany keeps what Vessany loves.*

*Vessany keeps what Vessany keeps.*

And Mara, walking, did not look back, because she was afraid that if she looked back, she would hear the rest of the rhyme—and some part of her, the part that fit its fingers into old carvings, already knew how it ended.
