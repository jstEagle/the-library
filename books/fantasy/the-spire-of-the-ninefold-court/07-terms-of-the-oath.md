## 7. Terms of the Oath

The smugglers' fire was a small, mean thing, tucked into the hollow of a collapsed aqueduct where the smoke could rise through a crack in the old stonework and disperse before anyone in the city above could name it for what it was. Ilsa had chosen the spot years ago—or someone had chosen it for her, in another life, and her feet still remembered the way. She arranged the firewood the way you were taught to arrange it in Vessany: three sticks leaning against each other like dancers waiting for the music to start.

Mara sat across from her, wrapped in a borrowed coat two sizes too large, her hands held out toward the flames. Her hands. Ilsa kept looking at them when she thought Mara wouldn't notice—the long fingers, the burn scar across the left knuckle that their mother had kissed better with honey and a nonsense word, the way they curled slightly at rest, as if around a grip that wasn't there.

She didn't know yet what her hands knew. Ilsa was about to tell her, and she was afraid of it in a way she hadn't been afraid of anything since the day she'd sold her first memory to buy passage out of a country that no longer existed.

"You're staring," Mara said, without looking up.

"I'm deciding how to begin."

"Beginners' advice: begin."

Ilsa laughed—a short, startled sound, because it was *Wren* who used to say that, Wren who had never once let a silence sit comfortably, Wren whose initials were carved into a burning tree on the other side of a door they'd both walked away from an hour ago. The laugh died in her throat. She fed a stick to the fire.

"My name isn't Ilsa Winterbourne," she said. "Not entirely. It's Ilsabet. Ilsabet Winterbourne of Low Marsh Row, city of Corvel, in the country of Vessany." She watched Mara's face as she said it, watching for flinches. "Say the last part back to me."

Mara's mouth opened. Closed. She frowned, genuinely puzzled at herself. "Ves—" The word seemed to catch somewhere behind her teeth, like a fishhook in cloth. "Why can't I—"

"Because it's been taken out of you," Ilsa said. "Carefully. Professionally. By people who are very good at taking things out." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "You've heard of treaties, yes? Wars end, borders move, countries change hands. That's not what happened to us. There was no war. No invasion, no plague, no famine. There was a treaty signed by nine men in a room in Corvel, and one clause in that treaty, and after the ink dried, Vessany did not fall. It was *unwritten*. Every map redrew itself overnight. Every ledger entry changed. Every neighbor remembered a marsh where our city stood. And every person inside the border—" she tapped her own temple, then pointed across the fire, "—was edited. Their names, their histories, the language they dreamed in. All of it filed away in ledgers so complete that the world forgot there had ever been a debt to collect."

"And the Court keeps those ledgers."

"The Court *is* the treaty's executor. They call it custodianship. Nine auditors, nine ledgers, and the Unmaking Ledger bound shut with clauses written in memory instead of ink." Ilsa's voice went flat; she made it go flat on purpose, because if she let any feeling into it she would start shouting and shouting would bring collectors down on them faster than firelight ever could. "I spent eleven years finding this out. Eleven years buying scraps in memory-markets from people who didn't know what they were selling. A woman who remembered a song but not its words. A man with a map tattooed on the inside of his eyelids. A child's shoe that hummed when you held it near salt water. Piece by piece, I rebuilt a country out of other people's leftovers, and every scrap told me the same thing: three girls grew up on Low Marsh Row, and all three of them swore oaths, and only one of them got to keep her name."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, on the aqueduct's broken spine, a nightbird called twice and went quiet.

"There were three of us," Ilsa said softly. "You. Me. And Wren."

"I don't remember her." Mara's voice was steady, which Ilsa recognized as effort, not calm. "I don't remember any of it. I keep reaching for—I can see a kitchen. Copper pots. Someone singing. But it's like looking through fogged glass. I know there's a shape there. I can't make out the face."

"That's how they leave you. Not empty—*furnished*. They fill the space where your life was with a life that fits the same shape. A childhood in some border town. A reason to be alone. An aptitude for numbers." Ilsa smiled, and hated how the smile felt on her face. "They gave you the Court itself, didn't they? Gave you rules and ledgers and a place where precision counts as virtue, so you'd never once wonder why nothing in your past felt warm."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone very controlled, the voice of a woman balancing accounts. "If you're telling me the truth—and I want you to be wrong so badly that I can't trust my own judgment—then answer me this. Why me? Why would the Court take a child of Vessany and make her a swordspeaker? If they wanted us erased, why keep any of us close?"

"Because oaths have to be paid for," Ilsa said. "And ours were worth paying."

She got up, crossed to her pack, and drew out the thing she had carried for eleven years wrapped in oilcloth: a sword. Not a beautiful one—the blade was nicked, the grip leather replaced twice—but she unwrapped it with the care you give relics, and laid it across her knees as she sat back down.

"This is a swordspeaker's blade. Do you understand what a swordspeaker is? Not a duelist. Not a soldier. In Vessany we—" she caught herself, corrected, "*they* believed words had weight, true ones especially. A swordspeaker learned forms the way monks learn psalms, and each form was bound to a word, and speaking the word while holding the form could do things steel alone couldn't. Cut things that weren't physical. Open things that weren't doors. Wake things that were supposed to stay asleep." She turned the blade so the firelight ran along it. "But power like that doesn't come free anywhere in the world, and least of all in Vessany, where everything true had a price itemized. So the oath was structured like a loan. The Court—this was before the Unmaking, when the Court still traded honestly, or thought it did—advanced you your gift, and the payment was memory. Chosen memories, surrendered willingly, one per year of service. You chose what to give up. That was the mercy of it. That was also the trap."

Mara had gone very still. "What kind of memories?"

"Anything. Everyone paid differently. Our mother paid the smell of rain on her wedding day. Old Sereth, who taught all three of us our first stance, paid his father's laugh—he said it was the cheapest thing he owned and the dearest, and he paid it anyway, laughing about the irony until the day he couldn't remember why he found anything funny." Ilsa looked up. "And you paid—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to draw blood. Mara's hands had closed into fists in her lap, and Ilsa saw them trembling—not with fear. With something older than fear. "I don't know what I paid. Don't tell me. Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because if you tell me what I lost, I'll grieve it," Mara said, "and right now the not-knowing is the only thing between me and—" She stopped. Started again, more quietly. "I've spent six years being good at not wanting things. It's the first skill the Court teaches, even if they don't write it down. Let me keep it a little longer."

Ilsa understood. Gods help her, she understood completely, and it made her angry, because understanding was a luxury the situation couldn't afford. She wrapped the sword again and set it aside, and instead reached across the fire and took Mara's hand—Mara let her, stiffly, the way you permit a stranger—and turned it palm-up in the firelight.

"Then don't listen to me," Ilsa said. "Listen to your hands."

She pressed Mara's fingers closed around nothing, miming a grip, and guided them—first position, the Salute to the Well, the opening form every Vessani child learned before they learned letters. She moved slowly, barely guiding at all, just suggesting.

Mara's hands took over.

It was small, at first—a settling of the wrist, a rotation that came from somewhere below thought. Then the whole arm joined in, the elbow dropping to the precise angle, the shoulder rolling, the weight shifting onto the back foot and forward again, and Mara's breath changed to match it, in through the nose, out through the teeth, the rhythm of a form performed ten thousand times. Her hands moved through the air gripping a sword that wasn't there, and they *knew* it, knew the balance and the reach and the sweet spot where the edge sang, knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, and her face—her face was the worst part. Confusion breaking apart into recognition breaking apart into something that had no name except grief arriving early, grief for a self standing right there in the firelight, visible and unreachable, waving through glass.

"Stop," Mara whispered. Her hands didn't stop. "Ilsa. Make it stop."

"It stops when you decide it stops. That's yours. Nobody can take the forms from you—they tried, believe me, the treaty's drafters would have loved to edit muscle the way they edited maps. This is what they couldn't get. This is what stayed." Ilsa kept her voice low and even, though her eyes were burning. "Your mind belongs to their ledgers, Mara. Your hands belong to Vessany. As long as you can do that, they haven't won. Whatever they took, whatever you paid, the payment was never the whole of you. There was always a remainder. You are the remainder."

The form finished. First position flowing into second, second into third, and then—Mara's hands hesitated, hovering—and then they moved into a form Ilsa had never been taught, a closing sequence reserved for oath-bound speakers, intricate and terrible, and halfway through it Mara made a sound like something tearing loose, and wrenched her hands down to her sides, and sat there shaking, staring at her own fingers as if they belonged to a stranger who had just confessed to murder.

"What was that last one?" she asked. Her voice was wrecked.

"I don't know. I never earned the right to learn it." Ilsa released her hand. "Only sworn speakers know the sealed forms. Only sworn speakers could open—" She stopped herself. Too much, too fast. But Mara was already looking at her with the expression Ilsa had dreaded and prayed for in equal measure: the look of a woman doing arithmetic she can't refuse to finish.

"The door in the dyer's shop wall," Mara said slowly. "The orchard. It opened for me before I touched anything. Before I did anything. I walked up to it and it—" She flexed her fingers. "It *recognized* me."

"Doors in Vessany were sworn, too. Everything true was sworn." Ilsa fed the fire another stick, buying time, steadying herself. "Which means either the Court's edits missed more than they caught, or—" she met Mara's eyes across the flames, "—or whoever holds your oath never collected the final installment. There's a clause in every swordspeaker's contract. The seal-breaker clause. The last payment, the one that closes the account forever: the speaker surrenders the memory of the oath itself. Who gave it, why, what it was for. Pay that, and you're free—hollow, but free. Refuse to pay, and the oath stays live. Dormant. Waiting."

"And mine is dormant."

"Yours is dormant, and unpaid, and the Court has spent thirty years pretending you don't exist because if the Ninth Auditor ever reads your file closely, he'll find an open account with a living debtor, and open accounts are the one thing the Unmaking Ledger cannot survive." Ilsa leaned closer, and the fire threw her shadow huge against the aqueduct stones. "That's why the stranger found you. That's why the map exists. That's why Corvane's collectors will tear this city apart stone by stone until they find us. You're not carrying a map, Mara. You're carrying an unpaid debt, and debts are the only currency the Court fears."

Mara looked into the fire for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had found that ledger-keeper's steadiness again, but underneath it, for the first time, Ilsa heard something else—not warmth exactly, but the place where warmth would grow back, like green shoots through salted ground.

"Then teach me," Mara said. "Everything you bought with eleven years. Start with the forms. Start with the words."

"You understand what it costs to speak them."

"I understand costs," Mara said, "better than anyone alive."

Ilsa almost laughed again, and this time she let herself, because it was Wren's joke and Wren deserved to be quoted, wherever she was, whatever she'd become. She pulled her pack close and began unpacking it: oilcloth bundles, a wax tablet, a bundle of papers soft with age, the whole ragged archive of a resurrected country.

They worked until the fire burned low and the sky above the aqueduct crack went grey. Ilsa taught her the first three forms and the words that woke them—small words, safe words, words that only sharpened the eye and steadied the breath—and Mara learned them with a speed that frightened them both, her body remembering ahead of her, always ahead of her, like a dog running toward a home that had been demolished years ago.

Near dawn, Mara fell asleep sitting upright, mid-sentence, her hands still loosely curled around a sword that wasn't there.

Ilsa covered her with the borrowed coat and sat watch, and let herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. Three sisters. One oath dormant. One sister already lost—she didn't let herself think past that yet. And somewhere behind them, patient as interest accruing, the Court would already know the fire had been lit. Collectors moved fast when an account stirred.

By full light, she'd need to move Mara again, deeper into the salt country, along roads only the erased remembered.

She was still planning the route when she saw, far off across the rooftops, a single figure standing motionless on the aqueduct's spine, silhouetted against the greying sky—not approaching, not hiding. Watching. And even at that distance, even in that light, Ilsa knew the posture: the particular stillness of a man who has read every ledger in the world and is wondering, just this once, whether he wrote the entry himself.

Corvane had found them.

He didn't send the collectors.

He simply raised one hand—an odd, formal gesture, almost courteous—and turned, and walked away along the broken stones, leaving Ilsa with her heart hammering and a message she couldn't yet read:

*I know. And I am coming myself.*
