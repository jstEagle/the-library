## 8. Corvane's Kind Offer

The summons came at dawn, which meant it had been written the night before, which meant someone had known before she woke what her day would be.

Mara found the letter tucked into the lock of her door—a liberty no courier would dare take with a swordspeaker's rooms, and therefore a message in itself: *we come and go where we please.* The paper was Court stock, heavy cream vellum watermarked with the nine interlocking circles that no one outside the guildhall had ever been permitted to reproduce. The ink was black, the hand was Corvane's, and the entire communication consisted of four words:

*Come early. Wear gray.*

She stood in the doorway a long moment, the letter held between two fingers as though it might bite. Behind her, her rooms were exactly as she'd left them: bed made with hospital corners, ledger-shelves dusted, the map—wrapped now in oilcloth and hidden inside the false bottom of her writing desk—silent as a sleeping animal. She had checked on it three times in the night. It had not moved. Maps did not move. She had told herself this each time, and each time the telling had sounded less like reassurance and more like prayer.

*Wear gray.* Gray was the color of auditors' clerks, of record-keepers, of people who existed to be overlooked. Gray was also, though Corvane could not know how it stung, the color she had worn every day for eleven years without ever being asked.

She wore gray.

* * *

The Ninefold Court at dawn was a different building than the Court at closing time. By lamplight it was all warmth and commerce—the murmur of appraisers, the clink of memory-bottles racked like wine, the soft wet sound of years being counted out into a client's palm. But before the doors opened, the guildhall belonged to its bones: marble veined with something dark that old clerks swore was ink, staircases that wound past windows showing weather from countries that had never existed, corridors whose lengths changed when unobserved, so that a clerk sent to fetch a file might return an hour older or an hour younger and neither she nor anyone else would think to check.

Mara walked the familiar route to the First Auditor's offices and let the building test her. A corridor she had walked a thousand times delivered her to a door she did not recognize. She stood before it, breathed, and said aloud, "I am Mara Winterbourne, oath-bound, keeper of small debts, and I am expected."

The door considered this. Then it became the correct door, and opened.

Corvane's office occupied the tower's ninth floor—the auditor's floor, one room per circle—and it was the only room in the Court Mara had entered fewer than five times. It smelled of cedar and cold iron. There were no ledgers visible anywhere, which in a guildhall was its own kind of obscenity; it was rather like meeting a priest in a house with no gods. What there was: a desk of black oak, a window overlooking the city's rooftops going gold with morning, and Corvane himself, standing at that window with his hands clasped behind his back like a man watching troops he had personally sent to die.

He was not what strangers expected. The First Auditor of the Ninefold Court should have been vast, or cruel, or beautiful in some unsettling way. Instead he was a lean, ordinary man of perhaps sixty, gray at the temples, with the mild face of a schoolmaster and hands that were very clean. Only his eyes gave him away—eyes the color of ledger-lines, that moved across everything they saw as if tallying it.

"Keeper Winterbourne." He didn't turn. "You're punctual. I'd have wagered against it, after the week you've had."

"I don't know what you mean, First Auditor."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I don't imagine you do."

Now he turned, and gestured her to the chair before his desk. She sat. He remained standing, circling slowly to the far side, and Mara understood the choreography: he wanted her looking up at him through this first part. He was a careful man. Every angle of this meeting would be deliberate.

"You've served the Court eleven years," he said. "Eleven years, and in that time your accounts have balanced to the last copper. Not once—not *once*, Keeper—have your appraisals required second review. Do you know how rare that is?"

"It's my work, sir."

"It's more than work. It's a discipline of the soul." He settled behind the desk at last, folding his hands atop the black oak. "Anyone can count. What you do is harder. You look at a bottled memory—a man's wedding day, say, or the last afternoon of a childhood—and you price it without flinching. You weigh grief in drams and sell it by the ounce, and you sleep afterward. That is not callousness, Keeper. Callousness is cheap. What you have is rarer." His eyes settled on her with the weight of a stamp coming down. "*Precision*. Precision under pressure. The Court prizes nothing higher."

Mara kept her breathing even. Somewhere below them, faintly, the front gates were opening; the day's first clients shuffling in with their sorrows bundled under their arms. "Thank you, First Auditor."

"Don't thank me yet." He smiled—it was a kind smile, genuinely kind, and somehow that made it worse. "Let me tell you about the Unmaking Ledgers."

The words went through her like cold water through cloth. She did not move. She had trained herself, over eleven years and one terrible week, not to move.

"Every guild has its founding instruments," Corvane continued, conversational, almost gentle. "Our charter. Our oath-forms. And beneath those, older and heavier, the Ledgers themselves—nine volumes, kept in the vault below the counting-floor, recording not debts owed *to* the Court but debts the Court owes. Debts incurred in the acquiring of certain... assets." He watched her. "You've heard rumors. Of course you have. Clerks always hear rumors. Bottled countries. Years harvested from the dying. A door that opens onto a place that isn't anywhere anymore."

"The Unmaking Ledgers are a story told to frighten apprentices," Mara said. "Sir."

"Is it?" He tilted his head. "Then why does your pulse just quickened, Keeper? Why are your hands flat on your knees like a woman holding herself down?"

She looked down and saw that they were. She made them relax.

Corvane rose, crossed to a cabinet she had taken for decorative, and unlocked it with a key from his watch-chain. From it he lifted a single sheet of paper and laid it on the desk before her, turning it to face her with two fingers, the way one turns a card.

It was a sketch. Charcoal, hurriedly done, by someone standing in a doorway looking out. It showed the counting-room at closing time—the long counter, the lamp, the racks of bottles—and a figure hunched over the counter's edge. A bleeding figure. A stranger, folded nearly double, one arm outstretched toward the clerk behind the counter, pressing something into her hands.

The date in the corner was eight days old.

"An artist in our employ," Corvane said, "keeps a studio across the lane. He works late. He sees more than he knows." The First Auditor sat back down. "A dying man entered our counting-room after hours and placed an object in your keeping. You reported no such transaction. Your ledger for that night records only routine closings. Which means either you failed to notice a man bleed to death three feet from your counter—" he let the silence stretch, delicate as spun glass— "or you noticed, and you lied in the Court's own books."

Mara looked at the charcoal stranger. Up close she could see the artist had caught the detail she herself had tried for a week to stop seeing: the man's fingers, curled around the folded map even as he died, as if it were the last warm thing he would hold.

"He was dead when I reached him," she said. "I called the wardens."

"You called the wardens *after* the doors locked. Fourteen minutes after, by the gate-log." Corvane's voice stayed mild. "Fourteen minutes, Keeper, is a great deal of time for a precise woman."

*Vessany remembers you.* The whisper rose in her memory with its original warmth still attached, breath against her ear, blood-warm. She put it down again. She had gotten better at that. Practice.

"I searched the body," she said carefully. "For identification. As procedure requires."

"And found?"

"Nothing."

The word landed between them, and she knew—even as she said it—that he knew, and that he knew she knew, and that none of this mattered, because that was not what this meeting was for. Lying to Corvane was like lying to a scale. The scale did not care whether you believed your own weight.

"Here is what troubles me," Corvane said, rising again, moving to the window, hands clasped behind him. "Not the theft—I'll come to that. What troubles me is the *timing*. Eleven years of perfect service, and then, within a single season: a dying smuggler with a map of a country we erased, a collector named Winterbourne haunting the memory-markets asking questions about your oath, and now—" he glanced back at her, and his eyes were not schoolmaster's eyes at all— "an inquiry from a certain party in the Salt Quarter who claims to represent persons unknown, offering to buy the same map. Everyone wants what you're hiding, Keeper. Even, I suspect, you."

The room was very quiet. Below, the Court's morning sounds continued, oblivious: the chime of the appraisal bell, a client laughing too loudly at nothing, the ordinary music of impossible trade.

"What do you want from me?" Mara asked.

"*Want* is such a grasping word." Corvane returned to the desk and, instead of sitting, rested his knuckles on the black oak and leaned toward her. "I want to offer you a promotion."

She blinked. Of all the sentences in the world, she had not prepared for that one.

"There is a position," he said, "vacant these thirty years. Keeper of the Ninth Ledger. Sole custodian of the Unmaking volumes. It carries a seat at audit, a stipend you couldn't spend in a lifetime, and access—" his voice dropped half a tone, going intimate, going *kind*— "access to the deep archive. Where the Court stores, among other things, the oaths of its bound servants. Including receipts."

The word *receipts* rang in her skull like a struck bottle.

"Every oath paid in memory generates a record, Keeper. What was taken. How much. In whose coin. Yours is down there. Eleven years you've walked around the hole in yourself, wondering what shape it was. I am offering to hand you the shape." He straightened. "All I ask in exchange is the map. Surrender it to me, quietly, tonight, and tomorrow morning you begin the most important work this guild has done in a generation. No more counting other people's grief. You'd keep the ledgers that decide what the world is allowed to remember."

Mara sat very still.

And here was the trap of it, the beautiful, jeweled trap: she *wanted* it. She wanted it with a hunger that frightened her more than the wardens did. Eleven years she had lived beside the absence in herself the way one lives beside a bricked-up door—pressing an ear to it sometimes, hearing nothing, telling herself the nothing was fine, the nothing was peace. And now this man offered her the key and a salary besides. Her mother's face. Her reason. Whatever the sword had been *for*. All of it filed somewhere in the dark below her feet, catalogued and shelved, retrievable for the price of one folded piece of moving ink.

That was the mercy in it. That was what made it monstrous. He wasn't wrong that it would heal her. He was only wrong—or lying—about what she'd become in order to be healed.

Because she had read enough ledgers to know how this one balanced. The keeper of the Unmaking Ledgers would be the woman who had surrendered the last proof that Vessany had existed. The Court would not simply file the map. It would unmake the map, the way it had unmaken the country, the way it had unmaken her—neatly, permanently, with a receipt. And then Mara Winterbourne would spend her remaining years maintaining the silence she had sold herself into, with a seat at audit and nothing left inside her that wanted to scream.

"They told me you were fair," she said at last.

"I am scrupulously fair," Corvane said. "Fairness is a system, Keeper, not a sentiment. I apply it evenly."

"To the country you erased?"

Something moved behind his eyes then—fast, gone, like a fish under ice. For one unguarded instant the First Auditor looked less like a man making a threat than a man standing in a draft he couldn't locate. "The treaty was signed by nine governments and ratified by this Court under lawful authority," he said, and his voice had gone slightly, carefully flat. "Whatever you think you feel about Vessany, you weren't there. Neither was I."

*Neither was I.* He said it like a man reciting a lesson. And Mara, who had spent a week learning the exact texture of her own holes, heard the shape of another one.

"You regret it," she said quietly. "Don't you. And you don't remember why."

The silence that followed lasted four full seconds. She counted them, because counting was what she did, because rules were the floor under her feet and right now the floor was the only thing holding.

"That will be all, Keeper," Corvane said. "Consider the offer. You have until the market bell tomorrow evening. After that..." He resumed his seat, unfolded a fresh page, and began to write as though she had already gone. "After that, the matter passes to the wardens, and I will have no authority to intervene. Mercy has office hours, Mara. I'd hate for you to arrive after close."

She rose. She bowed—the full bow, fist to heart, the obeisance of a bound servant, because he had not formally released her from protocol and protocol was armor. At the door, her hand on the handle, she stopped.

"For what it's worth, First Auditor," she said, without turning, "if I had the map, I wouldn't give it to you either. Not for the ledgers. Not for the receipts. Some things aren't priced yet."

She heard the pen stop scratching.

"Good evening, Keeper Winterbourne," Corvane said, to a room she had already left.

* * *

The stairs took her down nine floors and delivered her, as stairs sometimes did, to a landing she didn't recognize—a narrow gallery lined with tall cabinets, each drawer labeled in a hand older than the building. She stood catching her breath, letting the building finish its little test, and realized she was shaking.

Not from fear. That was the thing she would carry out of this day like a stone in her shoe. Not from fear, and not entirely from temptation, though the temptation was real and enormous and would sit with her all night at the edge of the oilcloth bundle in her desk.

She was shaking because of the fish under the ice. Because for half a heartbeat she had seen Corvane's regret, raw and causeless, and recognized its texture perfectly—because it matched her own. Two people walking around bricked-up doors. One of them had built his own wall. Perhaps, she thought, descending at last toward the counting-floor and the smell of morning trade and other people's traded mornings, perhaps that was what the Court actually was. Not a guild. A masonry. A brotherhood of bricked doors, each keeper sworn to forget what was behind his own, and paid handsomely to maintain everyone else's.

The market bell would ring tomorrow evening. Between now and then she had one night, one map, and one choice wearing the mask of many.

In her rooms, she pulled the oilcloth bundle from the false bottom of the desk and unwrapped it. The map lay quiet under her lamp—but quieter than before, she thought. Dimmer. As if it had heard the offer too, and was waiting to see what she was worth.

And along its western margin, where yesterday there had been blank vellum, new ink was surfacing. Fine, brownish lines, seeping up through the paper like blood through a bandage.

Not roads this time.

Writing.

Her handwriting.
