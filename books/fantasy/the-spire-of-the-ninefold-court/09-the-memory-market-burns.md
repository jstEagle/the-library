## 9. The Memory Market Burns

The market woke before dawn, as markets that trade in sleep always do.

Mara had been there twice in her life—once on Court business, once by accident—and both times the place had unsettled her in a way she could not itemize. It was called the Tallow Rows, a warren of stalls and shuttered kiosks beneath an aqueduct whose arches had been bricked up so long ago that the mortar had gone the color of old teeth. By day it sold nothing but candle stubs and cheap tallow and the ordinary wares of the poor. But between the last star and first light, the shutters came down, lamps were hooded with amber glass, and the memory-market opened its other face.

She came now because Ilsa had come here. That was the whole of her reasoning, and it shamed her. Three days since Ilsa had vanished from the smugglers' camp above the salt road, leaving nothing but a cold fire and a note in a hand Mara almost recognized: *They know where I bought what I bought. If I don't come back, don't follow.* Which was, of course, the surest way to make Mara follow.

The Court did not raid markets often. Raids were expensive; ledgers preferred slow arithmetic to sudden violence. But when they raided, they raided thoroughly, and Mara had spent enough years balancing accounts of confiscation to know the shape of what was coming: every bottle seized, every buyer's name taken, every seller bound for audit. The Tallow Rows held perhaps four hundred memories tonight, most of them small things—a first kiss, a dead father's laugh, the smell of a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere else in the world.

*Small debts,* she thought, and hated herself for thinking it in the Court's own vocabulary.

She moved through the crowd with her hood up, keeping to the edges. The market had its own etiquette: no names spoken aloud, coin passed in cupped hands, sellers who dealt in grief wearing grey scarves so buyers would know to lower their voices. A woman at the third stall was tasting a bottled summer from a spoon, weeping without noise, her husband's hand on her shoulder. A boy of maybe twelve sat behind a curtain selling hours he had stolen from his own sleep, because sleep was the one thing children had in surplus.

And near the aqueduct's central pier, in a kiosk hung with brass instruments, was the stall Ilsa had described in one of her few moments of candor: *Old Sef, the mapmonger. He sold me the coordinates. He'll remember you if he remembers anyone.*

Sef turned out to be a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a man who has read too many documents not written for him to read. When Mara set a silver mark on his counter—the Court's own currency, which she hoped he wouldn't notice—he looked at it, then at her, and said quietly:

"You're the second Winterbourne this month."

Her heart did something complicated. "What did she buy?"

"Nothing." He wiped the mark off the counter with his sleeve, fast, as if it burned. "I gave it to her. Free. Because I knew her mother." His eyes flicked past Mara's shoulder, toward the mouth of the Rows, and went flat and still in a way she had learned to read in appraisals: the stillness of a man watching something valuable about to be destroyed. "You should go, keeper."

"Why?"

"Because," said Old Sef, "they're already here."

Corvane had not wanted to attend the raid personally. That was the first lie he told himself about it, and he was an honest man in most respects—a flaw the Court had long ago trained out of everyone but him, precisely because honesty in an auditor was more useful than obedience. Obedience could be bought. Honesty, properly managed, could be *directed.*

He stood at the mouth of the Tallow Rows in a plain grey coat, watching his collectors move through the amber lamplight in their pairs, and told himself the second lie: that he was here because protocol required an auditor's presence at any seizure exceeding three hundred items.

The truth was simpler and worse. The truth was that the map had surfaced, that a dying courier named Vellin had pressed it into the hands of Keeper Winterbourne before expiring on the counting-room floor, and that Corvane had known Vellin—had signed Vellin's travel permits himself, eleven years ago, for reasons he could no longer reconstruct. And the further truth, the one that sat in him like a swallowed stone, was that when he had read the raid manifest and seen the words *Tallow Rows* written in his own clerk's hand, something under his ribs had said: *not again.*

Not again what? He did not know. There was a shape in his memory where an answer should have been, smooth-edged, worn hollow, the way a riverbed holds the form of water long after the water has gone. Thirty years ago he had helped draft a treaty. He knew this the way he knew his own signature. He could recite its clauses. Clause Nine, the binding clause, required a surrender of memory from each of its drafters—*so that the signatories might never be tempted to unmake what they had made*—and whatever those surrendered memories had contained, they were beyond even his reach now, sealed in the Unmaking Ledgers in a vault he was authorized to open and had never once been able to bring himself to enter.

"First Auditor." His senior collector, Brannoc, materialized at his elbow. She was a broad woman with a collector's flat calm, and she did not waste breath. "Four hundred and six items catalogued. Two resistances. One of them is your missing keeper."

"Which one?"

"The one who isn't resisting yet." Brannoc paused. "She's talking to the mapmonger."

Corvane felt the stone under his ribs turn over. "Take the inventory. Leave the mapmonger to me." And then, hearing himself, hearing the small deviation from procedure, he corrected it: "No. Take everything. Take him too. Procedure, Brannoc. Always procedure."

But she was already looking past him, up the Row, and her hand had gone to her whistle.

---

Later, Mara would try to assemble the moment the raid began, and find that it refused assembly, the way the worst moments do—coming to her only in pieces: the whistle's single clean note; the amber lamps going out in a wave, stall by stall, as the traders killed their lights; the sound of bottles breaking somewhere ahead, and the strange, terrible silence of spilled memory evaporating in the dark, a whole childhood becoming nothing but smell.

Then torches. Grey coats. Collectors moving in pairs with lead-lined satchels, sweeping the stalls, and the poor of the Tallow Rows pressed against the brickwork of the aqueduct, silent, because the poor of every city learn early that silence is the only shield that doesn't cost anything.

A woman beside Mara was holding a bottle against her chest with both hands, the way one holds an infant. A collector reached for it. The woman did not run, did not scream; she simply tightened her grip, and the collector—professional, unhurried—took her wrist and bent it back until the fingers opened. Mara heard the small wet sound of a joint going. She heard the bottle uncorked and emptied onto the cobbles, standard procedure, destroy what can't be carried. The woman sank down over her empty hands and made no sound at all, and that was the thing that broke something loose in Mara—not the cruelty of it, which was ordinary, but the *procedure* of it, the ledger-logic, the sense that all of this was merely an entry being balanced.

*Mara Winterbourne keeps the ledgers,* said a voice in her head that sounded exactly like her own. *This is yours. You signed off on raids like this for nine years. You just never stood on this side of the counter.*

She should have run. Every instinct the Court had built into her said run: you are one keeper, they are twelve, you are carrying contraband in your coat, your face will be on tomorrow's warrants. She took one step back, into shadow—

—and the shadow already had someone in it.

The warden had come around the aqueduct's pier on the quiet side, cutting off the escape route, which was correct procedure, and he was young, which was also correct procedure, because wardens were recruited young and kept stupid about what they enforced. He saw her hood, her face, and recognition moved across him like weather.

"Keeper Winterbourne. First Auditor wants you alive."

"I'm sure he does," Mara said, and meant it as delay, as negotiation, as anything but what happened next.

Because he grabbed her arm, and her body answered before her mind did.

It was not a decision. That was the horror of it, and later the only mercy: there was no moment where Mara chose violence, no instant of weighing or resolve. Her weight shifted to her back foot. Her free hand came up along his forearm, turning his grip, and her body dropped through an angle she had no name for but her muscles clearly did, some form older than the Court, older than her oath, written into her before she ever signed anything. The warden was stronger than she was and it did not matter at all. She felt the world go slow and precise, the way a well-balanced account goes precise—every element visible, every debt clear—and then her hand found the knife at his belt, his own knife, and she drove it once under the edge of his jaw, into the space where the Court taught its wardens no armor goes, because no enemy was ever supposed to get that close.

He looked at her with surprise. Not pain—surprise, the surprise of a man who has followed procedure all his life and cannot understand how it has failed him. Then his knees went, and she lowered him to the cobbles because her body would not let her drop him, and she knelt there with his blood coming warm over her fingers and understood, with total clarity, what she had done.

She had cut down a warden of the Ninefold Court in the execution of lawful seizure, and every lamp in the dark rows seemed suddenly very bright, and her heart was singing.

That was the part she would never tell anyone. In the first seconds after the killing, while the warden's blood cooled under her knees, Mara Winterbourne felt *exhilaration*—a rising, ringing joy, the joy of a lock finally turning, of a form completed, of a self snapping home into a socket it had been carved for. Her body knew this. Her body had been waiting nine years to be used, and it was grateful, and it sang.

Then the song curdled.

Because the warden was a boy. Twenty at most. He had a mole below his left ear and chewed nails and a whistle lanyard tied in a sloppy knot, and somewhere a mother had taught him to tie knots, and Mara had put a knife into the one unprotected inch of him with the effortless rightness of a woman signing her own name. This was what she had been before the oath. This was what her oath had been *purchased to remove*: not her memory of sword-forms—they had left those, oh, they had left those beautifully intact—but her knowledge of what she was willing to do with them. They had amputated her consent and called it peace.

"Mara." A hand closed on her shoulder, and she came up swinging, and Ilsa caught her wrist the same way the warden hadn't—turning with the blow, spending its force, close enough that Mara could see her face in the torchlight: smoke-streaked, bleeding at the hairline, alive. "Mara. Look at me. We have to go *now.*"

"Sef—" Mara's voice came out wrong. "The mapmonger. They're taking Sef."

"I know. I tried." Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Brannoc's people had him before I got within thirty feet. Mara—listen to me. He gave me up. Before they took him. I watched his lips move and then Brannoc looked straight at my hiding place. Whatever he sold, he sold it cheap."

"He didn't sell anything. He gave you the coordinates for free because he knew our mother."

Ilsa went very still. "Our mother."

And there was no time for it, none at all, because a torch flared at the far end of the Rows and Brannoc's voice cut the dark like a blade itself: *"There! Both of them—take them!"*

They ran.

They ran the way water runs, down service stairs and under the aqueduct's dead arches, through a drain grate Ilsa must have scouted days ago, out into the grey pre-dawn streets where honest people were lighting their first fires and knew nothing, would never know, what had been emptied out two streets away. Behind them the Tallow Rows burned—not all of it, only the mapmonger's kiosk, only the records, only Sef's forty years of careful cartography going up in deliberate flame, because the Court does not seize what it can instead erase.

Mara ran until her lungs tore, and then walked, and then stopped in some yard between warehouses where Ilsa finally let her stop, and the exhilaration was entirely gone now and what remained was worse: a perfect, itemized clarity. She knelt in the dirt and was sick, and Ilsa stood over her and did not touch her, which was its own kind of kindness.

"One warden," Mara said at last, wiping her mouth. "I killed one boy and I want to say it was them that made me do it. I want to say the oath, the reflexes, the Court—I want it to be theirs." She looked up at her sister, or the woman claiming to be her sister, and her voice came out level and cold and true. "It wasn't. My hand knew exactly what it was doing. It's been waiting to do it for nine years. They didn't take my violence, Ilsa. They took my *permission* to feel it. And the moment I stopped asking permission—" she laughed, and it was an ugly sound, "—it was the best I've felt since I signed."

Ilsa crouched down to her level. Up close her resemblance to Mara was unbearable—the same stubborn line of the jaw, the same winter-grey eyes, and Mara could no longer pretend she couldn't see it.

"Then you understand what we are," Ilsa said quietly. "We're not victims of the Court, Mara. We're its products. Mother knew. It's why she hid us. It's why she taught us the forms in the orchard before—" She stopped. Something crossed her face that Mara recognized, having just worn it herself: a person colliding with a memory that isn't there anymore. "Before," Ilsa finished. "Before whatever happened. Before the part they took."

"They'll have Sef in the Spire by noon," Mara said. "And Corvane will have him reading maps until he's given up every coordinate, every client, every name—including ours." She rose. Her legs were steady. That frightened her more than the shaking would have. "And when he's done with Sef, he'll burn him too. Another entry balanced."

"So what do we do?"

Mara looked east, where the sky was going the color of old parchment, and where, somewhere past the city's edge, past the salt flats, past the border that appeared on no living map, the erased country waited with its frozen fires and its collapsed villages and its house with their childhood in it.

"We stop running," she said. "We go get the rest of the map. From Corvane's evidence vault, if we must—from Sef himself, if he's alive. Because I've done the accounting, Ilsa, and here's the balance: I killed for this. I paid blood into it. And I keep the ledgers, so believe me when I tell you—" she held out her hand, the one still faintly stained, though she'd scrubbed it twice, "—a debt like that doesn't close. It *compounds.*"

Above the rooftops, far away, the spire of the Ninefold Court caught the first light, nine-sided and golden and utterly indifferent, and Mara Winterbourne looked at it the way a debtor looks at a creditor's window.

Somewhere inside it, she was certain, Corvane was already writing her name into a new column.

Let him. She was keeping her own books now.
