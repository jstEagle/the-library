## 10. What I Took From Myself

The map had been lying to her for six days, and on the seventh it told the truth.

Mara discovered this the way she discovered most things at the Court: not through revelation but through bookkeeping. She had spread the map across her narrow table under three candles and a borrowed magnifying glass of ground wyrm-glass, and she had done what she always did with an account that refused to balance. She stopped looking at the numbers and started looking at the spaces between them.

The roads were wrong. She had known this since the first night—the ink crawled when she wasn't watching, the highways of vanished Vessany rearranging themselves like sleeping animals—but wrongness was not the same as randomness. She had spent eleven years in the counting-room of the Ninefold Court, and if the Court taught its keepers anything, it taught them this: *nothing moves without a reason, and every reason leaves a residue.*

So she took a clean sheet of vellum and began to chart the movement itself. Each morning, before the light came up, she marked where each road lay. Each evening, after the Court's bells rang the closing hour, she marked them again. By the fourth day the pattern emerged—not in the roads but in their *drift*. They weren't wandering. They were converging. Slowly, patiently, like water finding a low place, every road on the map was leaning toward a single point in the western margin.

And there, in the margin itself, beneath a century of what she had taken for decorative flourish—those curling vines, those looping filigrees that every cartographer's hand adds to fill empty space—she found writing.

It only appeared when she held the page at the angle of a candle flame. The flourish resolved into letters, the letters into figures, the figures into a string of coordinates and a date.

Her breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door opens onto winter.

She knew the handwriting.

Not because she had seen it before. Because it was hers. The slight forward lean of the numerals. The way the sevens were crossed twice, a habit she'd never been able to break even though old Auditor Vess had told her, years ago, that double-crossed sevens invited forgery. The little hook at the end of the final stroke, as though the hand that wrote it had been about to add something more and thought better of it.

*Before the oath.* That was the part that made her hands shake. The date was thirty-one years old. She was twenty-nine.

She sat with that arithmetic for a long time, the candle burning down, the city outside her window going quiet street by street. Somewhere below, a drunk was singing a song about a fisherman's wife. It was the most ordinary sound in the world, and it kept her from screaming.

*You wrote these coordinates before you existed,* said the reasonable voice in her head, the one that sounded like Corvane, dry and precise. *Therefore they are not yours. Therefore someone has forged your hand, which is possible, forgery being one of the nine arts the Court keeps.*

But the Court also taught its keepers how to detect forgery, and Mara had been very good at that lesson. Forgers got the surface right and the rhythm wrong. A forged hand performed; a true hand merely *was*. These numbers did not perform. These numbers were tired. They had the tiredness of someone writing quickly by bad light who already knew where they were going and only needed to make sure someone else could follow.

Someone had written them in her hand, thirty-one years ago, in a country that no longer existed.

Or—

Or she had written them herself, in a life she could not remember, and the oath had taken the memory of taking them along with everything else.

The second possibility should have frightened her more. Instead, she found, it steadied her. Because the first possibility meant the world was full of enemies clever enough to counterfeit her soul, and against that there was nothing to be done. But the second possibility meant only that she had lost something—and losses, however vast, could be itemized. Could be tracked. Could, in principle, be recovered.

She was a keeper of ledgers. She knew exactly one way to fight a debt.

* * *

She began the private ledger that night.

It was not a grand thing. She bought a cheap bound notebook from the stationer on Candle Lane, the kind apprentices used for rough sums, with a mottled grey cover and pages thin enough to read the shadow of the next page through. Unremarkable. Destroyable. If the Court's collectors ever turned her rooms over—as they had done once already, politely, while she stood in the corridor drinking tea with a warden who watched her the way cats watch mouse-holes—they would find nothing worth the finding.

But inside, in a cipher of her own devising—simple, brutal, unbreakable except by patience, because she built it herself over two sleepless nights and trusted no elegance but her own—she began to write down everything.

*Item:* One map of Vessany, acquired from a dying man at closing, third of his name unknown. Ink animate. Roads converge westward. Margin bears coordinates in my own hand, dated 31 years prior.

*Item:* Flash-memory, triggered by contact with the page. An orchard burning. A woman's voice saying — she had written the words out phonetically, in the cipher, because writing them plainly made her chest hurt — *"Run home first, then run anywhere."*

*Item:* First Auditor Corvane asked four questions he should not have known to ask. Conclusion: the stranger's death was reported before the wardens arrived. Conclusion: someone inside the counting-room reports to someone above the counting-room.

*Item:* The woman Ilsa Winterbourne claims sisterhood. Evidence offered: a scar matching mine (verified), knowledge of our mother's lullaby (unverifiable), hatred of the Court (consistent). Evidence withheld: whatever she sold to Corvane. Probability of treachery: high. Probability of honesty despite treachery: higher. Decision: proceed together, watch always.

*Item:* My hands know seven sword-forms I have never learned. When I hold a blade my body goes quiet in a way my mind does not. Hypothesis: the oath did not erase what I was. It only erased my permission to know it.

She filled eleven pages the first week. Then she added a twelfth page, and titled it, after long consideration, *Costs.*

Because the Court had taught her one other thing, the thing underneath all the other things, the lesson so fundamental that no one ever spoke it aloud in the guildhall: *everything is paid for.* Every impossible thing the Court traded—bottled memories, borrowed years, doors to erased countries—had a price, and the price was always paid by someone, and the Court's genius was simply that it arranged matters so the payer was rarely the party who benefited. That was the whole architecture. That was the Spire from foundation to weathervane.

If her oath had been purchased—if, as Ilsa insisted, her forgotten years had been the coin that bought her place behind the counting-room's safe green door—then somewhere there was a record of the transaction. And somewhere there was a record of what else the transaction had cost. Oaths of memory, Ilsa said, were never paid in a single lump. They were paid in installments, drawn down whenever the oath-holder brushed too close to what had been surrendered.

Which meant that every flash, every fragment, every hour of missing time since the stranger died—that was not chaos. That was *billing*.

She began to track it the way she would track any account. Dates. Triggers. What was lost, described as precisely as grief allowed. On the ninth day she noticed that the taste of her morning tea had gone flat—not the tea itself, which was the same cheap blackleaf she'd bought for years, but her *memory* of what it had tasted like before. She wrote it down. On the eleventh day she reached for the name of her childhood street in Greyharbor and found the street itself intact but its name gone, a house standing on an unlabeled road. She wrote that down too. Her handwriting, she noticed, grew smaller as the entries accumulated, as though the page were getting heavier.

On the fifteenth day she caught herself unable to remember why she had wanted to be a swordspeaker—which was absurd, since until a month ago she had not known she *had* been one—and she sat at her table with the pen hovering over the paper for the better part of an hour before she could bring herself to write:

*Item: Reason, original. Gone. Do not remember having it. Remember only that its absence aches, the way a socket aches around a pulled tooth.*

Then, beneath it, because she was honest even in cipher, even alone, even with herself:

*Query: If the cost continues at current rate, and the door lies nine days' travel west, what will remain of me to walk through it?*

She had no answer. She left the query standing in the ledger like an unpaid balance, which was, after all, what it was.

* * *

Corvane sent for her on the sixteenth day.

Not a summons—a note, delivered by a junior warden who would not meet her eyes. *Keeper Winterbourne. The Unmaking Ledgers require a second pair of hands for the autumn reconciliation. Your discretion in prior matters recommends you. Come at the ninth bell.—C.*

Mara read it four times. *Your discretion in prior matters.* He was telling her he knew she had lied to him, and that he valued the lie—or that he wanted her to believe he valued it, which amounted to the same invitation either way. He was opening a door and asking her to pretend it wasn't a trap.

She almost didn't go. She had the map sewn into the lining of her coat now, and the private ledger in a loose stone behind her hearth, and every instinct of eleven careful years told her to stay small, stay invisible, stay precisely where she had always been safe.

But safety, she was beginning to understand, had been the product all along. The Court sold safety the way any guild sold its goods, and the currency had been herself, spent in installments, year upon year, and she had called the spending *peace*.

She went.

The upper floors of the Spire were warmer than the counting-rooms, hung with tapestries that depicted the Ninefold Court's history in the style of hagiography: the Founding Charter signed in gold, the First Auditors receiving tribute from grateful kings, the Great Reconciliation in which some earlier generation of keepers had "restored order" to a province whose name the tapestry artist had, with a fine instinct for the guild's real business, simply omitted. Mara looked at that blank space in the woven border longer than she meant to. Someone had stitched over it. Even the tapestries kept ledgers here.

Corvane received her in a round room with nine windows and no tenth wall. He was not what people expected. People expected the First Auditor to be vast, ceremonial, armored in office. He was instead a spare, grey man with ink-stained fingers and the patient posture of someone who had spent decades sitting very still while other people panicked. Only his eyes moved much, and they moved the way an abacus moves: click, click, click, arriving at totals.

"Keeper." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Thank you for coming."

"You said the Unmaking Ledgers need reconciling."

"I said that." He folded his hands. "They do. But I'll confess the reconciliation can wait a season, and I wished rather to speak with you about your future."

Here it was, then. Mara kept her face in the neutral arrangement she used for difficult clients—the expression that said *I am listening and I am costing you nothing*. "My future."

"You are the best keeper this floor has produced in forty years," Corvane said. It did not sound like flattery; it sounded like an appraisal, which from him was more unsettling. "You balance accounts that defeat senior auditors. You notice residues. You noticed, I think, more than you reported, regarding a certain visitor on a certain closing night."

"The man died in the doorway," Mara said. "I reported everything I saw."

"Yes." His eyes clicked. "Everything you saw."

The silence stretched. In the Court, silence was a tool with two edges, and both men at the table knew how to hold it. Mara let him have three heartbeats of it and then—because she had learned something in the past month about the strategic value of imperfection—she let her gaze flicker, just slightly, toward the window. A guilty tell. A small one. The kind of flaw that makes a lie load-bearing.

Corvane saw it. She watched him see it, and watched him decide what it meant, and understood that she had just fed him exactly the wrong conclusion in exactly the right amount. He believed she was hiding something small. A detail. A trinket from the dead man's pockets, perhaps. Keepers skimmed; it was practically a benefit of employment. He could work with a skimmer.

What he could not yet imagine—and she held this thought like a coal in her cupped hands—was that she was hiding a country.

"I'm going to offer you something," he said, "that has not been offered to a keeper of your tenure in the entire history of the guild. But not today. Today I want only to ask you a question, and I want you to consider whether you wish to answer it honestly, because the offer depends on the answer."

"Ask."

"Do you want to remember?"

The question went through her like a blade finding the seam in armor. Not *did you take anything*, not *do you have the map*—he had skipped all the small games and gone straight down to the root of her. He was not asking about theft. He was asking about hunger.

She thought of the margin of the map, the coordinates in her own tired hand. She thought of the unlabeled street in Greyharbor. She thought of the ache around the absence where her reason used to live.

"No," said Mara Winterbourne, and lied with her whole steady heart. "I want accurate books. Memory is just inventory. Inventory that lies to you constantly. Give me a ledger over a recollection any day of the week."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—disappointment? relief? She genuinely could not tell, and the not-telling frightened her more than either answer would have. "A precise answer," he said softly. "We shall see whether it remains precise." He rose, and the audience was clearly over, and at the door he paused with his hand on the frame and did not look back at her.

"When the offer comes, Keeper, it will come once. The Court pays well for loyalty, but it pays only at the moment of purchase. Afterward there are only refunds, and refunds are always deducted from something."

* * *

She walked home the long way, through the memory-market district, hood up, hands in her pockets. The stalls were shuttering for the night, the vendors of bottled afternoons and secondhand childhoods packing their wares into lacquered cases. A woman was arguing with a buyer over the price of a summer; Mara heard her say, *it was a good summer, everyone remembers it fondly,* and felt the strange vertigo of hearing her own profession spoken aloud in the street.

At the corner of Saltmonger's Row she stopped, because a child was selling pencils off a tray, and among the ordinary reds and blacks there was one pencil of grey wyrm-glass dust, the kind surveyors used, the kind that wrote on anything including treated vellum and oiled silk.

She bought three.

That night, by candlelight, she copied the map's margin coordinates onto a strip of oilcloth in surveyor's grey, and sewed *that* into the heel of her left boot, separate from the map, separate from the ledger, so that no single discovery could take everything from her at once. Then she opened the private book to a fresh page and drew, at the top, in plain unencrypted characters that she would later cipher, a heading she had never before permitted herself to write about the institution that owned her life:

*Accounts Receivable: Owed to Mara Winterbourne, by the Ninefold Court.*

Below it, the first entry:

*One life, prior. Contents unknown. Value: incalculable. Status: held in escrow against payment of an oath I do not remember swearing.*

She looked at the entry for a long time. Eleven years of training said the entry was madness—keepers did not bill the Court; the Court billed everyone. Eleven years of training also said, in its dry and patient voice, that a debt unrecognized was still a debt, and that the oldest trick of any great house was to convince its debtors they were the ones who owed.

The candle guttered. Outside, far to the west, thunder rolled over rooftops that had never been part of any erased country, and Mara dipped her pen and added one final line to the page, the line she had been circling all week the way the map's roads circled their convergence point:

*Note: The coordinates lead to a door. I am the only person alive who can read them. Whatever waits at the end of that road, the Court fears it enough to buy me, lie to me, and kill strangers in my doorway. Conclusion: it is worth more than my loyalty. Conclusion: it is worth more than my fear.*

*Next entry to be recorded at the door itself.*

She closed the book, pressed the wax seal of plain tallow—no crest, no mark, nothing the Court could read—and slid it behind the hearthstone. Then she sat in the dark with her boots on, ready to run, listening to the rain begin, and did not sleep at all.

Somewhere in the last hours before dawn, she realized she had stopped thinking of herself as the Court's keeper.

The realization arrived without drama, the way most balances shift: quietly, irreversibly, in the space of a single entry. She was not keeping the Court's accounts anymore. She was keeping her own. And her own books said she was owed.

Three streets away, though she would not learn it until morning, a collector named Ilsa Winterbourne was nailing a message to the memory-market's central post, a message addressed to Mara in handwriting that matched their mother's, and the message read: *They've posted your description at every gate. We leave for the salt line tomorrow. Bring the map or don't come—but if you don't come, come anyway.*
