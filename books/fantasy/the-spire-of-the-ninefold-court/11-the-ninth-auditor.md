## 11. The Ninth Auditor

There were nine auditors of the Ninefold Court, and First Auditor Corvane was the ninth.

This was not a fact he shared. The other eight believed themselves complete, a perfect circle of authority, and Corvane had spent thirty years cultivating the pleasant fiction that he stood slightly outside their number—first among equals, but an equal still, a man who chaired their councils without belonging to them. It was a useful arrangement. A circle has no head. A line does. And Corvane had learned, in the long apprenticeship of his ambition, that power prefers to be a line: something with a beginning, something that can be followed, something that ends at a single point.

He sat now in the upper counting-room where the light came down through nine tall windows, each glazed with glass blown from different sands so that the morning fell into the room in nine colors. It was the hour he liked best, before the clerks arrived, before the day's inventory of impossible things began its slow circulation through the ledgers—bottled memories racked like wine, borrowed years decanting in their vials, doors sleeping in their frames. The Court at dawn smelled of ink and cold stone and, faintly, of salt.

Salt. He noticed it every morning and every morning he failed to wonder why. That was the design of it. That was the *craft* of it.

Corvane opened the ledger that was never inventoried, never shelved, never named aloud—the one bound in grey hide that lived in a drawer beneath the floorboards of his own chambers, because there were only two keys to that drawer and one of them had been melted down and cast into a bell that rang over a harbor in a country that no longer existed. He did not remember melting it. He remembered being told that he had. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole architecture of his life.

The ledger opened to the page it always opened to, because the binding clause permitted no other page. He read it as he had read it perhaps four thousand times, and as always the words passed through him like water through a net: *Let the signatory surrender to this instrument all memory of Vessany, its roads, its names, its orchards, its dead; let the surrender be total; let the surrendered memory serve as the seal upon the erasure; let the seal endure so long as the signatory endures.*

His name was signed at the bottom. He recognized his own hand the way a man recognizes his face in a mirror—with familiarity and no recognition at all. The ink had gone brown with age. Beneath the signature, in the same hand, smaller, almost furtive, someone had written a single word:

*Forgive.*

He had studied that word for decades. He had commissioned analyses of the ink. He had interrogated the parchment's provenance until the archivists began avoiding him. And the conclusion, arrived at again and again by every method the Court possessed, was that the word was written in his hand, at the same sitting, in the same ink, by the same man who had signed away a country.

He could not remember writing it. He could not remember what he had wanted forgiven. He knew only that when he read the word—and he read it every morning, because the clause compelled the reading, drew his eye down the page like a hook through water—something behind his sternum turned over slowly, the way a great ship turns, too large and too heavy to be anything but deliberate.

Grief, he supposed. Or regret. The words were nearly synonyms at his age.

* * *

The summons regarding Winterbourne reached him mid-morning, and he read it twice, and on the second reading his hands were not entirely steady.

It was a routine report—he had ordered it made routine, had built the machinery of surveillance so finely that even his own attention arrived disguised as paperwork—but beneath the clerkly prose ran the thing itself: the last swordspeaker had lied to him. In his own counting-room, under the nine-colored light, Mara Winterbourne had looked him in the eye and told him she had never seen the dying stranger, and her pulse had not risen, and her breath had not changed, and every instrument of the Court's scrutiny had found her clean.

Which meant she had learned to lie past the instruments. Which meant she had done it once before. Which meant, though Corvane would not write this in any report, that somewhere inside the Court's most obedient servant there was a self that predated obedience, and that self was awake.

He should have felt alarm. He noted, with clinical detachment, that what he actually felt was closer to relief—a loosening, like a knot cut rather than untied. This troubled him more than the lie itself. He filed the feeling where he filed everything unaccountable: in the drawer, under the floor, beside the grey ledger, in the country-shaped silence at the center of his life.

He sent for the night-wardens' depositions instead. The stranger who had died in the counting-room had been identified—or rather, *dis*-identified, which was rarer and more interesting. His effects included a map that no longer appeared in any inventory. The wardens swore they had searched the body. The search record said the map was found. The search record also said the map was not found. Both statements bore the same clerk's mark, and the clerk, when questioned, wept and could not say why, and by evening had resigned his post and booked passage on a ship whose destination he refused to disclose.

Things were moving. Things that had been sealed for thirty years were moving, and Corvane found himself standing at the window watching the city below with the expression of a man who hears, in the walls of his own house, the sound of water where no pipe runs.

* * *

In the afternoon he went down to the Unmaking Ledgers.

No auditor visited them willingly. The chamber sat below the counting-rooms, below the vaults, below even the old foundations, in rock that predated the city, and the ledgers themselves lay on stone shelves in ranked rows—nine hundred volumes, one for each treaty, each annexation, each quiet administrative disappearance that had built the Court's empire of the impossible. Countries did not vanish on their own. Someone balanced the account. Someone signed.

Corvane walked the rows slowly, letting his fingers trail along the spines. He did this perhaps twice a year and had never asked himself why. The chamber was cold in a way that was not temperature. The air tasted of salt.

At the newest row—the oldest books, though the shelf-labels lied about it out of professional courtesy—he stopped at the volume bound in grey. It matched the ledger in his drawer exactly, because they were halves of one another: the public half and the private half, the erasure and its seal. He had read this one too, four thousand times, and it told him nothing, because it was written in a language that had been erased along with everything else, and the only living translator was a memory he did not possess.

But today, for the first time in thirty years, he took the volume down and carried it up into the light.

He told himself it was procedure. Winterbourne had the map; the map concerned Vessany; therefore review of the relevant instruments was warranted. The reasoning was sound. He was a creature of sound reasoning; it was the load-bearing wall of everything he was. And yet as he climbed the stairs with the grey book under his arm he caught himself walking quickly, like a thief, like a boy late for something, and he made himself slow down, and the slowing cost him something he could not itemize.

In his chambers he set the two ledgers side by side on his desk—the private clause and the public treaty—and looked at them for a long time.

Here was what he knew, assembled from thirty years of careful, oblique research: that Vessany had been a country of orchards and salt-roads and door-craft older than the Court's; that the Court had bought its erasure through a treaty signed by nine hands, eight of whom had sold their signatures cheaply and one of whom had demanded, as his price, that the transaction be *perfect*—total, seamless, leaving not a road nor a name nor a grief behind. That perfection had required a seal, and the seal required memory, and the largest memory available had belonged to the man demanding perfection.

He had engineered his own forgetting. That much the documents made clear. He had stood in some room, in some year, holding a pen over a clause he himself had drafted, and he had chosen to make himself the lock on the door he was closing—because a seal purchased from anyone else could be stolen back, but a seal that *was* the signer, that lived in his blood and endured as long as he endured, was beyond theft.

And then, at the last moment, in the same ink, he had written *forgive.*

Corvane pressed his palms flat against the desk and breathed.

The terrible arithmetic of it was elegant, and he could appreciate its elegance the way a blind man appreciates the proportions of a room he cannot see. He could not remember Vessany. But the clause needed him to *not* remember actively, continuously, with the whole force of his mind—his forgetting was not an absence but a labor, performed daily, unbeknownst to the laborer. Every morning the grey ledger drew his eye and he read it and understood nothing, and that very act of incomprehension was the work that kept a country dead. He was not a man with a hole in his memory. He was a machine for producing a hole, and the machine did not know itself.

And the regret—that was the flaw in the craft, wasn't it. Whoever he had been, that final day, had known that a seal of pure forgetting would not hold, because pure forgetting leaves no reason to guard the door. So he had left himself one thread: the word beneath the signature. Not a memory. Just the *shape* of one. Grief without content. Regret with its object amputated. Enough to make him come down to the chamber twice a year and trail his fingers along the spines. Enough to make him taste salt in the dawn light and fail to wonder why.

He had built himself a prison in which the prisoner was also the lock, and he had left, scratched into the cell wall, a single word he could read but not obey.

*Forgive.* Forgive whom? Himself? The Court? A woman in a burning orchard whose voice he did not know he had heard? There was no way to know. There was no way to know, and the not-knowing was the mechanism, and he loved the mechanism, because the mechanism was the Court, and the Court was the only thing he had ever—

He stopped there. He examined the sentence he had almost thought, turning it over like a coin of uncertain mint. *The only thing he had ever* — what? Loved? Chosen? Been? The thought would not complete itself. It completed itself nowhere except in that place behind his sternum where the great ship turned.

* * *

That evening he summoned the duty-collector and gave three orders, and each order was correct, and each order felt, as he spoke it, like a sentence being handed down from some higher bench to which he had no access.

First: the hunt for Winterbourne was to be escalated, quietly, without the full council's knowledge. She was to be taken alive. He heard himself add the condition—*alive*—and watched the collector's eyebrows rise, because protocol said otherwise, because protocol said that instruments of erasure were to be destroyed or reclaimed, not preserved. Corvane let the eyebrows rise and fall unanswered. He did not examine why he wanted her alive. He was growing expert at not examining things; it was, after all, his life's work.

Second: the memory-markets were to be raided, beginning with the stalls along the Salt Road, and every document bearing the word *Vessany* was to be brought to him personally. *Personally.* Another deviation. Another entry in the invisible ledger of his deviations, which grew longer each year and which he kept, characteristically, nowhere at all.

Third: he was to be informed immediately if anyone spoke to Winterbourne claiming kinship. The report of a collector named Ilsa Winterbourne had crossed his desk three days ago, and Corvane had read the surname four times before understanding why the reading hurt.

Winterbourne. The stranger had died saying a version of the name. The map was drawn in a hand that the graphologists—those he trusted—judged consistent with the swordspeaker's own, dated years before her oath. Sisters, perhaps. Or a family. Or a house with a childhood in it, somewhere in a country that existed now only as the weight behind his sternum and the salt-taste of every dawn.

When the collector had gone, Corvane sat alone with the two grey ledgers and the nine-colored dusk coming through his windows, and he did the thing he did every evening and had never once described to another living soul: he tried to remember.

Not the country. The country was gone; that was the point of him. He tried to remember *wanting* it—to remember the shape of the desire that must have preceded the signing, the man who had stood in that room with the pen and chosen totality. What had that man feared? What had that man hated? You do not erase a country out of neutrality. Somewhere under the clause, under the seal, under the thirty years of flawless service, there was a motive with his name on it, and it was the one piece of inventory the Court had never been able to appraise.

Nothing came. Nothing ever came. The labor of forgetting performed itself smoothly, drawing the memory down below the reach of recall the instant he approached it, and he sat in the failing light feeling the familiar twin motions of his soul: the turning of the great ship, and the smooth machinery working above it, tirelessly, keeping the water clear.

But tonight—for the first time—the machinery slipped.

It was small. It was almost nothing. He had reached for the inkwell and found his hand had already ground the pen against the page in the opening stroke of a letter he had no intention of writing, and the letter began:

*Mara Winterbourne. Before you are taken, you should know that the man who hunts you—*

The pen stopped. The ink dried in the nib. Corvane read the six words he had written and felt the floor of himself tilt, gently, the way a deck tilts.

He did not know how the sentence ended. But his hand, his traitor, his faithful hand—the same hand that had signed and then begged pardon—had known how it *began*, and the beginning was a confession, and a confession implies a crime, and a crime implies a man who committed it, and that man was waiting somewhere inside the machinery like a drowning sailor under clear water, visible on calm days, visible now.

Corvane burned the page in the candle and watched the words curl and go. Then he took out a fresh sheet and wrote, in his coldest clerk's hand, the order that protocol demanded and his deviations had delayed: *Proceed to full mobilization. The swordspeaker is to be stopped by any means.*

He signed it. His signature was steady. Below it, unbidden, his hand paused—hovered over the space where a second word might go—and then withdrew, and the order went down to the collectors clean.

But all that night, in the chamber below the counting-rooms, the grey ledger on its stone shelf lay open to the only page it had, and the word beneath the brown signature seemed darker than it had that morning, and in the nine-colored dark of the upper rooms the First Auditor did not sleep, and tasted salt, and did not wonder why.

By dawn the hunt had begun in earnest. And far to the east, past the border stones, past the salt flats where compasses spun, a frozen country waited with its orchards mid-flame and its villages mid-collapse, holding its breath around the shape of a name that both of them had lost—one by clause, and one by oath—and only one of them, Corvane understood with a certainty he could not source, was going to find hers first.
