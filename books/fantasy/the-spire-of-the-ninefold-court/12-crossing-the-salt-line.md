## 12. Crossing the Salt Line

* * *

Mara had walked out of cities before. She had left the Court at dawn on collection errands a dozen times, her satchel heavy with sealed writs and her face arranged into the particular blankness that made people look away from her rather than at her. But she had never walked *toward* anything like this.

The road ended where Vessany had been erased, and it ended without apology. One moment there was cobbles, then hardpan, then grass gone grey with salt-dust—and then nothing that any mapmaker would consent to draw. The land simply stopped meaning itself. Ahead lay a plain of white so vast and so flat that it hurt to look at, stretching to a horizon that shimmered like the inside of a closed eye.

"It's salt," Ilsa said behind her. "All of it. The whole country."

"I can see that."

"You can't." Ilsa came up beside her, and even now, even after the fire in the orchard door and the smugglers' fire where they'd traded truths neither wanted, Mara found it difficult to hold her sister's face steady in her attention—like trying to remember a word in a language she no longer spoke. "You're seeing a flat. What you're standing on is Vessany. Every street. Every grave. The river Vessa ran through here—the one in the songs. They didn't destroy it, Mara. You can't destroy a country. They *salted the ledger*. They wrote over it until what was written underneath couldn't be read anymore."

Mara crouched and pressed two fingers to the ground. It was crusted, gritty, cold even under the noon sun. And beneath the salt, faint as breath on glass, she felt something. Not warmth exactly. Not movement. More like the sensation of pressing a hand against a door and feeling, on the other side, someone leaning back.

Her compass hung at her belt. She hadn't needed it since the orchard, but she drew it out now, watched the needle spin—lazy at first, then frantic, whirling like a thing trying to escape its own housing. It settled nowhere. It pointed, for one strange instant, directly down.

"Down," Mara said quietly.

Isla nodded. "Everything here is down. That's how preservation works. Nothing moves because everything is being held still from underneath."

They should have camped at the edge and crossed by daylight proper, with rest behind them. But the light was failing, and Mara had learned enough in the past weeks to know that this country did not distinguish between night and day—it was all one hour, the hour of its ending, held forever—and so waiting bought them nothing but time for doubt. They walked out onto the white.

The silence changed within fifty paces. It wasn't the silence of empty places, which is really just quiet. This was the silence of a room where someone has just stopped speaking mid-sentence. Mara kept catching herself turning around, certain she'd heard the last syllable of something enormous.

By full dark the salt glowed. Not brightly—a phosphorescence like drowned stars, rising out of the crust in slow pulses, as though the plain were breathing through a wound. And in that light Mara saw the first village.

It stood perhaps half a mile off their line of travel: rooftops, a bell tower, a well with its bucket still swinging on its rope. Except the bucket was not swinging. It hung at the top of its arc, motionless, defying its own weight. A bird—some small brown thing—hung above the square with its wings half-spread, frozen between one wingbeat and the next.

"Oh," Ilsa said. It was a very small sound for a very large grief.

Mara walked toward the village without deciding to. Her feet knew the way, which frightened her more than the frozen bird did. Her feet turned left at the well, avoided a rut in the road that thirty years of salt had not filled in, and stopped at a doorway where a woman stood with a loaf of bread in her hands, caught forever in the act of pulling it from an oven that no longer held heat or bread or time.

The woman's face was ordinary. Flour dusted her forearms. There was a burn scar on her wrist shaped like a crescent moon.

Mara knew that scar. She did not know how she knew it, and knowing it opened in her chest a pain so specific and so old that she had to put her hand against the doorframe to stay upright.

"We shouldn't touch them," Ilsa said softly, beside her now. "The smugglers said—"

"I'm not going to touch her."

"You were reaching."

Mara looked down. Her hand was indeed extended, fingers curled the way they curled around the hilt of her sword when she was about to speak a form—not grasping, but *addressing*. As if part of her believed that a swordspeaker could cut the salt away and let the woman finish pulling her bread from the oven, thirty years late.

"She'd be my mother's age," Mara said. Or someone's mother. The thought slid sideways and she lost it.

They did not camp in the village. Mara could not have explained why; only that sleeping among the preserved felt like sleeping inside a held breath, and she was afraid of what might happen if the country exhaled while she dreamed.

* * *

Ilsa took first watch, though watch against what, neither of them said.

She sat on a rise of salt-crusted stone with her back to the dead village and looked out at the glowing plain, and she let herself feel, for the first time since the Well of Names, the full weight of what she had done. Corvane's coin was still sewn into her coat lining. She kept telling herself she'd spend it on passage, on food, on Mara's safety—but coins don't care what they buy, and betrayal doesn't care why. She had sold the shape of their route to a man who unmade countries, and she had told herself it was survival, and survival was true, and it was also not the whole truth, and the whole truth was that some part of Ilsa Winterbourne had never stopped believing that the Court was the only solid thing in a world of erasures.

And now here she sat, on the erasure itself.

Below her, Mara slept badly, turning, murmuring fragments. Once she said a name—Ilsa leaned forward to catch it—but it wasn't a name she recognized. It sounded old. It sounded like the name of a river, maybe, or a girl.

Ilsa had been eleven years old when the treaty came through. She remembered almost none of it—that was the cruelty of the thing, the genius of it. The Court hadn't just erased Vessany; they'd erased the memory of Vessany from everyone who owed them anything, and every family that had ever borrowed a year or sold a memory owed the Court something. The forgetting had spread like dye through water. By the time Ilsa was twelve, she could not have told you what she'd forgotten. There was just a shape in her childhood, a room in the house of her mind with the furniture covered in sheets, and she had learned, the way children learn anything, not to open that door.

Mara had opened it. Mara was walking through that house room by room, and every door she opened shook the walls of Ilsa's own memory.

That was supposed to make her grateful. Mostly it made her afraid. Because she had seen what Mara was becoming out here on the salt—seen her hands move in forms that no living school taught, seen the frozen bird turn its head, *actually turn its head*, when Mara passed beneath it—and Ilsa understood that her sister was not walking toward the truth so much as the truth was walking toward her, wearing Mara's body, and it would arrive whether Ilsa helped or hindered.

So help. It was the only choice that left her able to meet her own eyes.

A sound woke her near midnight—or what felt like midnight; time out here moved like syrup. Not a sound, precisely. A repetition. From the direction of the second village, further along their route, something was happening over and over: a crash, a settling, a crash, a settling. Like a house falling down and being caught, falling down and being caught.

"Mara," she whispered, and then, when her sister didn't stir, "Mara. Wake up. Something's wrong with the dying."

* * *

The second village was larger, and it was coming apart.

Or it would have been, once, thirty years ago. Now it fell apart forever: a wall of stone bulged outward, frozen mid-collapse, its bricks suspended in the air like a held breath; below it a cart stood with its wheel cracked, the pieces of the wheel hanging in the exact positions they had occupied at the moment of shattering. The repeated sound was the wind moving through the gaps in the broken things, filling and emptying, filling and emptying, a country trying to scream through a mouthful of salt.

Mara stood at the edge of the square and could not move.

She had prepared herself for horror. She had not prepared herself for *familiarity*. Because she knew this square. Not from memory—she had no memory, that was the entire wound of her life—but from somewhere deeper, from the place her sword-forms lived, the place that knew things her mind had been sold out of. There had been a fountain there, at the center. Children had thrown bread to fish in it. She knew this the way she knew how to stand, how to fall, how to cut.

"There," Ilsa said, pointing. "Do you see—"

At the fountain's dry basin, half-buried in salt, lay three small shapes. Dolls? No. Mara made herself walk closer. Toys, carved from soft wood, painted in colors the salt had not quite eaten: a horse, a boat, and a little figure of a woman holding a sword, its paint worn away at the edges by a child's long love.

Mara picked up the wooden swordswoman.

The moment her skin touched it, the flash came—not the burning orchard this time, not the woman's voice, but a courtyard in summer, dust and sunlight, and a voice saying *again, from the third form, again*, and laughter, three voices laughing, and the smell of—

Of honey. The taste of honey, suddenly, overwhelmingly, in a mouth that had not tasted it since before her oath. She staggered. The toy slipped from her fingers and landed in the salt, and the taste vanished as if a ledger-line had been drawn through it.

"Mara!" Ilsa caught her arm. "What happened?"

"Nothing. I'm fine." She wasn't. She knelt and retrieved the toy and put it inside her coat, against her heart, and told herself she was collecting evidence.

But the compass at her belt had begun to behave strangely again—not spinning now, but trembling, straining downward and slightly east, toward a point on the horizon where the salt-glow gathered thicker than anywhere else, like a bruise under the skin of the world. Toward, she understood without knowing how she understood, the house with their childhood in it.

"The map says the door is four days' walk," Ilsa said, following her gaze. "Past the well-field. Past the—"

"I know where it is." Mara heard her own voice come out flat and certain, a stranger's certainty. So did Ilsa; she saw her sister's eyes widen. "I've always known where it is. I think—" She stopped. Started again. "I think that's what my oath cost. Not one memory. All of them. Poured out into a ledger so the Court could hold the deed to who I was. And everything I've lost since I started speaking the words—every word costs more—it isn't spending. It's *buying back*. Coin by coin."

The wind moved through the ruined village, filling and emptying. Somewhere a wall finished a collapse it had been postponing for thirty years, brick by impossible brick, and neither of them could say afterward whether it had truly fallen or whether the country had simply turned over in its sleep.

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way you speak near a frozen bird. "Then stop speaking the words until you have to."

"I will."

"Don't lie to me, Mara. Not out here. Out here I'll know."

Mara looked at her—at this woman who claimed to be her sister, who had betrayed her, who had saved her from the fire, whose face Mara still could not hold onto from one hour to the next—and she found that the honest answer was already forming, and that it terrified her more than the salt or the collectors or the compass straining toward home.

"I won't lie to you," Mara said. "But Ilsa—when we reach the door, whatever it asks, I need you to remember something for me. In case I forget."

"What?"

"That the woman in the oven was going to feed us." Her voice broke, finally, cleanly, like ice giving way. "Someone was making bread, and we were coming home to eat it."

Far to the east, across forty miles of breathing salt, the glow gathered itself, pulsed once—slow, patient, like a heart remembering its rhythm—and the compass needle swung toward it and lay still for the first time since they'd crossed the line, pointing the way a child points: *there, there, there.*
