## 13. The House With Our Childhood In It

The salt flat ended the way a held breath ends—not gradually, but all at once, with a sound like the world remembering how to be a world.

One moment Mara walked on white crust that crackled beneath her boots and tasted of old grief; the next, the ground softened to dark loam, and grass stood up around her ankles, green as an accusation. Beyond it rose hedgerows, and beyond the hedgerows, half-hidden by thirty years of preserved mist, a lane.

And at the end of the lane, a house.

Mara stopped walking. Ilsa did not.

Ilsa had seen it first—of course she had; Ilsa saw everything first, had spent their whole crossing three steps ahead with her hood back and her face turned toward the horizon like a woman following a smell. Now she broke into a run, boots hammering the frozen road, and Mara watched her go and could not make her own legs obey.

It was only a farmhouse. That was what she told herself. Two storeys, slate roof, a chimney with swifts' nests crusted into its lip like old candle wax. A barn leaning companionably against a shed. An orchard behind it where the trees hung heavy with fruit caught mid-fall, apples suspended in the air like brown notes on a stave. Ordinary. Any country's ordinary. She had appraised a thousand bottled memories of houses exactly like this one, had held them up to the counting-room lamps and noted *provenance: rural, modest, sentimental value high* in her small precise hand.

She had never once wondered why none of them made her feel anything at all.

"Mara." Ilsa's voice came back down the lane, cracked open. "Mara, it's—it's *standing*. Everything else here is falling, burning, mid-collapse—and this is just *standing*."

"I can see that."

"Then come *see* it."

Her legs finally agreed to carry her. The lane passed under a gate whose latch was worn bright by thumbs—the same thumb, some ancestral thumb, pressed ten thousand times—and Mara found her own hand reaching for it before she decided to reach, finding the worn place without looking, fitting herself to it the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Her hands remembered. They had been remembering since the smuggler's fire, since Ilsa had put a practice sword in them and Mara's body had unfolded into forms no one had taught her in this life—*the river parts, the reed bends, the last light asks nothing*—forms with names in a language she did not speak but somehow knew. Her hands remembered everything. It was only ever her mind that arrived late, empty-handed, apologizing.

The front door was unlocked.

Of course it was unlocked. Nothing inside the preservation could change; nothing needed locking. The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound, because sound, like decay, required time, and time had been confiscated from Vessany thirty years ago.

Inside smelled of bread.

That was the thing that undid her—not the dustless furniture, not the clock on the mantel frozen at eleven minutes past four, not the coat rack holding three coats in three sizes—but the *smell*, hanging in the hallway air as thick and warm as if someone had pulled a loaf from the oven a minute ago and stepped out to the well and would be right back. Preservation did not stop at stone and wood. It kept the bread. It kept the warmth of the hearthstone, faint under her palm when she braced herself against it. Somewhere upstairs a bed held, Mara understood without testing it, the exact shape of a sleeping child.

"Oh," said Ilsa, from the parlor doorway, in a very small voice. "Oh, Mara. Come look."

* * *

The parlor held a table, four chairs, a cold stove, and a wall of framed things.

Not paintings. Not quite photographs—Vessany had never trusted the camera, Ilsa had said once, called it a machine for forgetting which half of the truth you were keeping. These were drawings, mostly, in charcoal and ink and the stubborn colored chalks children are given when they cannot be given paints. And they were labeled. Someone in this family had believed in labels the way Mara believed in ledgers—every drawing captioned in a firm slanted hand:

*Mara, age six. The day the geese got out.*

*Ilsa, age four. Asleep in the apple bin.*

*Sela, age nine. Reading to the goats.*

Mara read the third caption twice. Then a third time.

"Sela," she said aloud, and the name sat strangely in her mouth, a coin from a currency that no longer circulated. "There were two of us. You said there were two of us. You've been saying it since the memory-markets—*my sister Mara, my sister Mara*—"

"There *were* two of us," Ilsa said. But her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when a floorboard has begun to creak. She was staring at the middle drawing. At *Sela, age nine. Reading to the goats.* At a girl with a long serious face and ink-stained fingers, drawn with more skill than the others, because Sela had drawn herself.

"Then who," Mara asked, very quietly, "is she?"

Ilsa didn't answer. So Mara began, methodically, terribly, to count.

Three coats on the rack. Three bowls in the cupboard drain-rack, one chipped. Three sets of boots by the kitchen door, sized large, medium, small. Three names cut into the beam over the fireplace with a penknife in a child's wobbling alphabet: *MARA ILA SELA*—no, not ILA. She leaned close. *ILSA.* The carver had run out of patience halfway through the S and simply stabbed it twice.

Three beds upstairs. She climbed the stairs and counted them, though she already knew. The room at the front held two beds and a crib long since converted to a shelf, two sisters' room, and on the wall between them a height-chart of notches with dates climbing toward the ceiling, two columns of names ascending year by year until the chart ran out of wall—

Two columns.

But downstairs there had been three names on the beam, and three coats, and a girl reading to goats, and Mara stood between the two beds—one made with military precision, one a snowdrift of blankets—and understood that she was looking for herself in this house and could not find herself, could not find *which* self, because the house insisted on three daughters and every story Ilsa had told her had room for only two.

She went back down. Ilsa had not moved from the parlor doorway. She looked, Mara thought, like a woman standing at the edge of a frozen lake she had sworn was safe.

"There's a desk," Ilsa managed. "Father's study. There might be—records. He kept records of *everything*, he was worse than you are—"

"I'm not anything," Mara said. "I don't know what I am. I don't know which notch on that wall is mine."

"You're *Mara*."

"That's the name I woke up with! After the oath! Don't you understand—" and her voice cracked, and she hated it, and pressed on anyway, because the numbness she had carried for years in the counting-room had burned away somewhere on the salt flats and what was left underneath hurt. "Don't you understand what this house is saying? Three sisters. One of them chose the Court—Ilsa, *you told me that*, you told me one of us took the Court's coin and sold the rest of us, you said it to explain why you'd tracked me across half the city—"

"I said a sister betrayed us. I didn't say—I didn't know there were *three*—"

"Or you did," Mara said. "And you've known which one you were all along, and you've let me walk into Vessany not knowing whether I'm the sister who was robbed or the sister who did the robbing."

The words fell into the preserved air and stayed there, hanging like the apples outside.

Ilsa's face did something complicated. Mara watched it happen and could read none of it—grief and guilt and something older than both, and underneath it all, unmistakably, fear. Not of Mara. Of the question.

"The study," Ilsa said again. "Please. Before either of us says anything else we'll want to take back."

* * *

Her father's study smelled of pipe smoke that thirty lost years had not dared disturb.

Desk, chair, shelves of almanacs and ledgers—he had been a farmer who kept accounts the way other men prayed, and Mara felt a pang of kinship so sharp it was nearly vertigo. *This is where I learned it,* she thought. *Before the Court taught me the form of it, someone here taught me the love of it.* She ran her fingers along the desk's edge and found, worn into the wood near the drawer, the shallow groove of a thumb-rest, and her thumb settled into it perfectly.

She opened the drawers the way she opened everything: top left first, smallest items forward, oldest documents at the back.

Farm accounts. A deed. Letters from a cousin in a town whose name the map had refused to hold. And then, wrapped in oilcloth at the very bottom of the bottom drawer, a packet tied with string, and on the oilcloth, in that firm slanted hand she was coming to know like her own heartbeat, a label:

*For whichever daughter comes home asking questions. You will know which one you are after. Forgive me for not making it easier. I could not make it safe and easy both, and I chose safe.*

Mara's hands, which did not tremble even when her voice did, untied the string.

Inside: three documents.

The first was a birth record, copied fair in her father's hand. *Sela Winterbourne, born to Aldous Winterbourne and his wife Maren, in the spring, weighing as much as a sack of flour and twice as loud.* Below it, in different ink, added later, cramped: *Apprenticed to the Ninefold Court, age sixteen. Signed the papers herself. Wept at the gate and signed anyway. Said the fee would pay off the farm. Said she'd come home rich. The Court's receipt lists the payment taken: her share of the family's future. What that means, they would not say.*

Mara read it three times. *Signed the papers herself.* The Court did not buy children. The Court did not trick the willing. Every debt in every ledger she had ever kept had been entered freely, that was the whole theology of the place, that was what Corvane's soft terrible voice recited at every audit: *we take nothing that is not offered.*

Someone in this family had offered.

The second document was another record, another spring, another daughter. *Ilsa Winterbourne, born—* and the details blurred, because Mara's eyes had begun to burn, and she made herself finish it: *Stolen from the road at age nineteen, on her way to market. Taken by collectors in payment of a debt not hers. The debt was Sela's. The Court collects where it finds the collateral.*

"Not hers," Ilsa whispered. She had come to stand beside Mara without Mara hearing her, which should have been impossible, and was reading over her shoulder. "Not mine. It was never my debt. I always thought—" Her breath shuddered. "I always thought I must have done something. Sold something. Everyone the Court touches thinks that. It's how they keep us quiet."

"And the third?" Mara asked. Her voice sounded far away, like a voice heard through the preservation's silvered air.

The third document was the shortest.

*A third daughter, born in the autumn, three years after Sela. We named her Mara. When the collectors came for Ilsa, this child hid in the orchard and watched, aged seven, and afterward she asked me what a debt was, and I could not answer her. When she was grown the Court came back—for the balance of Sela's account. They wanted the farm. They wanted the years. And this daughter, my youngest, went to the counting-house alone and paid it. Paid it entire. Paid it with—*

The handwriting stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-word, the pen lifting from the page, and beneath the break her father had written, in a hand gone suddenly old and unsteady: *She made me promise not to write what she paid. She said if it was written anywhere, even in my ledger, they could find it and take the rest. She said, tell them I'm nobody. Tell them I'm a clerk. Tell them I have no past worth mentioning.*

*So I wrote nothing. I am writing this now because I am dying, and a man should leave one honest page.*

*Whichever of you is reading this: the daughter who paid was brave, and the daughter who was stolen was blameless, and the daughter who started it was sorry. I loved all three of you the same, which is to say more than the sky. Do not let the Court's arithmetic tell you otherwise.*

Mara set the pages down on the desk with great care, the way she handled anything whose value could not survive rough treatment.

"So," she said. The word came out level. She was proud of that, in the distant part of her still taking inventory. "Three sisters. One who sold the family's future to the Court. One who was stolen to cover it. One who paid the balance with—" she touched her own temple, lightly, "—whatever this is. Whatever I gave them. My memory. My oath. Myself."

"Yes," Ilsa said.

"And you still don't know which one I am."

"No."

"You *knew* Sela. You grew up with her. You'd know her face—"

"I'd know her face at *nineteen*," Ilsa said, and now the fear in her was naked. "You were seven when I was taken. Seven, Mara. I have a baby sister the size of a memory, and the woman standing in front of me has our father's thumb-groove worn into her habits and our mother's way of counting before she speaks and—" her voice broke entirely, and she steadied it the way soldiers do, by force, "—and Sela was *sixteen* when she signed, and people change in ways a sister can't chart. I can't tell. I have looked at you across a hundred campfires and I *cannot tell*, and God forgive me, some nights I don't want to know, because if you're her—"

"If I'm Sela," Mara said, "then I built the cage. If I'm the youngest, I locked myself in it. And if—" 

"If you're neither," Ilsa finished, "then you're the one they took the most from, and none of us gets to be angry at anyone, and somehow that's the worst of the three."

Outside, through the study window, the preserved sun hung at its fixed angle over the orchard, lighting apples that had been falling for thirty years and would fall forever, never landing, never bruising. Mara looked at them and thought: *that is what the Court does. It takes the moment of your worst decision and keeps it falling, forever, never letting it land, never letting it be finished.*

Somewhere in her, behind the oath's blank wall, a memory pressed its palms against the stone. She almost heard it. *Almost* was the whole cruelty of her life.

"We should go," she said. "Daylight doesn't move here, but Corvane's collectors do. And there's one more thing this house has to show me."

"How do you know?"

"Because Father wrote *for whichever daughter comes home asking questions*," Mara said, "and then he stopped writing. Men who stop mid-sentence in their own studies are hiding something in the room. I've audited enough of them to know." She was already turning to the shelves, running her finger along the spines of almanacs, feeling for the hollow spot, the false back, the weight that didn't match its neighbors. "He couldn't write what I paid. But he wouldn't destroy it. He was a man who kept accounts."

Her finger found it—a gap behind the almanacs, a seam in the shelving—and her heart, which had been steady through the battle on the salt road and steady through the burning orchard-door, began at last to pound.

Because carved into the wood of the hidden panel, small, in a child's wobbly alphabet, in the same knife-strokes as the beam above the fireplace, someone had cut a fourth name.

And Mara did not recognize it at all.
