## 15. Collectors on the Salt Road

They came at the hour when the light went the color of old pewter, and they came the way the Court did everything: in order.

Mara saw them first as a smudge on the western horizon, where the salt flat met a sky that had not decided whether it was evening or something older than evening. Nine columns. She counted them twice because her hands were shaking and she wanted to be sure her eyes were lying. They were not. Nine columns of collectors in grey traveling cloaks, each column flying the pennant of one auditor, moving across the dead country's skin with the patient, terrible discipline of an audit.

"They've been walking since dawn," Ilsa said beside her. She crouched behind a fallen lintel—part of some farmhouse that had been preserved mid-collapse, its roof still sagging thirty years after no fire burned beneath it. "I watched them form up on the ridge. Corvane didn't come himself."

"No," Mara said. "He sent his arithmetic instead."

The battlefield spread out around them like a held breath. That was what this place was—the Court had frozen Vessany at the moment of its unmaking, and the moment it had chosen included the last stand. Here, along the salt road, the border garrison had made its refusal: soldiers caught mid-charge, spears levelled at enemies already dissolved into treaty-language; a standard-bearer with the banner half-raised, the cloth stiff as hammered tin; horses with their manes streaming backward forever. The whole field glittered faintly under the strange light, salt crusted over everything like frost, like sugar, like the rime that forms on a thing kept too long in a cold cellar.

Mara had walked through it all morning and had not gotten used to it. You did not get used to flies in amber. You only learned to stop looking directly at their eyes.

"How many?" she asked.

"I counted two hundred in the first rank. Collectors don't travel with baggage trains—they carry their own bindings." Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Wardens behind them, maybe three hundred more. And the ledgers themselves. Someone's carrying the Unmaking Ledger on a lectern-palanquin. If we're named in it tonight, we're named *tonight*."

"We're named in every ledger they have," Mara said. "That stopped being negotiable when I cut down a warden in front of forty witnesses."

She said it flatly, the way she'd once read account totals aloud, and hated herself a little for how easily the old numbness came back to hand. But underneath it, for the first time in her remembered life, there was something else—not fear exactly, though fear was there, cold and specific in her stomach. It was more like the feeling of standing at a counting-table knowing the sums would not balance no matter how many times you ran them, and having to decide, finally, whose error you were going to confess.

The Court's error. Or hers.

"Then we run," Ilsa said. "The Well is a day east. If we can reach it before they close the ring—"

"And if we can't?"

Ilsa looked at her. In the pewter light her face was all planes and shadows, and for one disorienting heartbeat Mara could not remember why she had ever doubted this woman was her sister. Then the heartbeat passed, and doubt crept back in, familiar as an old coat.

"Then we die on Vessani soil," Ilsa said, "which is more than the Court ever let anyone do."

* * *

They ran.

The salt road was the map's second promise, and it held: a raised causeway of white stone running straight as a ruled line through the preserved fields, built by people who had believed their country would last forever. Their boots rang on it. There was no way to be quiet here; the whole land rang back, a low bell-tone that rolled out ahead of them like a warning given away for free.

Behind them, the nine columns broke formation and began to run too.

Mara heard them coming the way you hear weather—a pressure change, a hush, then a sound like a thousand pages turning at once. The collectors did not shout. Shouting was inefficiency. They simply flowed across the salt, grey on white, and where they passed the frozen soldiers they did not slow, and Mara saw—with a lurch that nearly put her on her knees—one collector reach out without breaking stride and touch a charging spearman on the shoulder. The soldier did not fall. He *unmade*. Folded up like a letter being returned to sender, gone between one stride and the next, and the salt where he had stood stayed perfectly smooth, as if he had never been owed to the world at all.

"They're erasing the evidence," Mara gasped.

"They're tidying," Ilsa corrected grimly. "Same thing, in their books."

A binding came out of the sky.

Mara felt it before she saw it—a tightening behind her sternum, the sensation of her name being written down somewhere by someone who spelled it wrong. She turned in time to see it: a ribbon of grey script, unspooling from a collector's fingers fifty yards back, writhing through the air toward her like a serpent made of clauses. She had spent fifteen years handling such things from the safe side of a counter. Bottled. Filed. Balanced against equivalent consideration.

It had never occurred to her, until this exact moment, that she knew how they flew.

Her body knew. Her body swung left without consulting her, dropped low, and her hand found the sword at her hip—and drew it, clean, in a single motion so practiced it frightened her more than the binding did. The blade came up and she spoke, without deciding to, one syllable of the old tongue that lived in her muscles the way her mother's face no longer lived anywhere:

"*Vael.*"

*Turn.*

The binding bent around her sword-edge like water around a stone and shattered into loose characters that drifted down onto the salt, harmless, already forgetting what they had been ordered to do.

And Vessany stirred.

Mara felt it go through the ground like a pulse through a sleeping limb. The frozen orchard two fields over—the one they had run past an hour ago, trees locked mid-blossom—shivered. Petals moved. For three heartbeats, wind crossed the salt flat, actual wind, smelling impossibly of green apples and woodsmoke, and somewhere far off a dog barked once, a living sound in a country of statues. A woman's voice, thin as memory, called a name Mara almost caught.

Then it stilled. The petals froze again. The wind died. The dog did not bark twice.

But the collectors had seen it. All two hundred of them stopped at once, a single grey organism pausing mid-flow, and the silence that followed was worse than pursuit.

"That," Ilsa breathed, "was not supposed to happen yet."

"What did I do?"

"You *spoke*." Ilsa grabbed her arm, hauling her onward, but her eyes were wide and wet. "Not a ward-word, not a trade-word—an oath-word. First-form. Mara, swordspeakers trained ten years before they were permitted first-form, and even then—" She stopped herself. Started again. "What did it cost? Think. What's missing?"

Mara reached inward, the way she reached into any ledger, expecting to find everything accounted for.

Her mother's face was gone.

She knew this because she knew there had been a face—knew it the way you know a column exists because the total demands it—but when she looked for it there was only smooth blankness, a page washed clean, and above the blankness a small precise note in her own mental handwriting: *payment received, one (1) maternal countenance, fair condition.*

"No," she whispered. She stumbled. Ilsa caught her and they kept moving, because the collectors had begun to move again. "No—I looked at her just—when? When did I look at her last? Ilsa, I can't remember when I—there was a kitchen, there was bread, there was—"

"Mara." Ilsa shook her. "Look at me. That's the price. That's always been the price. Every word takes its toll and the toll is taken from wherever the word thinks it's owed. You gave it your childhood because your childhood is where the sword came from." Her voice cracked. "*Don't spend another one unless you mean it.*"

* * *

They meant to lose them in the broken villages.

The map showed a cluster of homesteads off the road's eastern shoulder—Vessani names she now read fluently, which should have told her something about how much of her the oath had really taken: *Karst Hollow. The Tannery Rows. Little Anselm.* Places that existed nowhere else on any map because the maps had been bought and burned, and here they stood, mid-collapse, roofs bowed like tired shoulders, doorways open onto rooms where supper tables still waited with the patience of the dead.

Mara and Ilsa came off the causeway and into the lanes of Karst Hollow, and the collectors came off the causeway after them, and the running battle became something slower and crueler: a hunt through streets built to the scale of ordinary lives.

Mara fought the way she appraised—economically, precisely, wasting nothing. Her body supplied techniques her mind had never learned; she stopped questioning this sometime around the third warden, when she took a man's blade on her crossguard, turned it, and put him down with the pommel, all in the time it took him to finish reciting the opening clause of her arrest. Bindings came at her out of doorways and off rooftops, and she cut them down, and each time the land shivered and breathed, and each time she came away lighter.

One apple tree, blossoming for three heartbeats. Gone.

The smell of rain on hot slate. Gone—she noticed its absence the way you notice a missing tooth, tonguing the space where a summer afternoon used to be.

The name of the woman who had taught her to hold a quill. Gone, and with it, absurdly, the ability to remember why she had ever loved ink.

She kept fighting. That was the horror of it, she thought, ducking a binding that took a chimney-pot apart overhead—that the spending got easier. Each loss made the next one smaller by comparison. The Court had taught her this without meaning to: that anything can be paid if you pay it in installments.

"LEFT!" Ilsa screamed, and Mara turned and there was Corvane's arithmetic made flesh: a senior collector stepping out of the tannery's shadow with the Unmaking Ledger itself open on a floating lectern beside him, pen already moving, writing her into a sentence designed to end her. Not a binding this time. A *deletion*. She could feel the difference in her teeth.

There was no room to dodge in the narrow lane. No time to turn it aside.

So Mara Winterbourne did the thing fifteen years of bookkeeping had trained her never, ever to do.

She overpaid.

"*VAEL ANSERA,*" she said—*turn, and return*—second-form, the forbidden compound, the one that doesn't deflect a working but unwrites it, sends it home, makes the ledger answer for its own ink. The deletion folded backward into the open book like a snake swallowing its tail. The collector screamed. The lectern cracked down the middle.

And Vessany did not stir this time.

Vessany *rose*.

Every frozen thing on that battlefield moved at once—the soldiers finished their charge, the banner snapped fully aloft, the horses' manes streamed, and thirty years of held breath came out of the country in a single vast exhalation that flattened the collectors like wheat. Mara heard voices, hundreds of them, shouting in Vessani, the last orders of the last stand finally arriving thirty years late, and the ground under her feet was warm and alive and *grateful*, and for three glorious seconds the war resumed exactly where it had left off, and the Court's grey ranks broke.

Then it ended. Of course it ended. The exhalation spent itself; the country sank back into its preservation like a diver returning underwater; and Mara knelt on the warm salt of the road, sword-point down, trying to remember her own name and finding, for one bottomless instant, nothing there at all.

It came back. It came back because Ilsa was screaming it, kneeling over her, shaking her: "*Mara. Mara. MARA.*"

"I'm here," Mara said. Her lips were numb. Her mind was a house with most of the windows dark. She inventoried, because inventory was all she had left: her name, yes. Ilsa's face, yes. The taste of honey—gone. The reason she had become a swordspeaker—gone; there was a sword in her hand and no story attached to it whatsoever, just steel and competence and a hole shaped like a purpose.

What remained was enough to stand on. Barely.

She stood.

Down the ruined lane, the collectors were regrouping, but slowly, badly, shaken by a country that had answered its enemy. And beyond them, at the edge of the failing light, Mara saw the palanquin of the Ninth Auditor set down upon the salt, and a tall figure descend from it—not hurrying, never hurrying—and begin, alone, to walk toward her through the stirring, sighing, half-remembering ruins of Karst Hollow.

Corvane had come after all.

And Mara understood, with the last clear thought of a day that had cost her almost everything soft in her, that he had not come to collect her.

He had come home.
