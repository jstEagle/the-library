## 16. The Price, Itemized

The salt road gave them shelter that night in the shape of a customs house, or what had been a customs house before Vessany died. It stood mid-collapse the way everything here did—roof beams frozen three inches above their sockets, dust hanging in the air like a held breath—and Mara found she could walk beneath those floating rafters without flinching now. Three days in the preserved country had taught her its grammar. The dead villages were not haunted. They were *paused*, and pausing was worse.

She built no fire. Fire belonged to living things, and she felt less and less sure she qualified.

Instead she sat on a customs officer's stool, before a customs officer's desk—someone named, according to the brass plate still nailed to the wood, *HALLWARD, T., INSPECTOR OF GOODS*—and took out her private ledger.

It had begun as a weapon. That was how she had justified it to herself, back in the city: the Court kept accounts, so she would keep an account; every method they used turned in her hands like a knife finding its proper grip. But ledgers do not care what you intend them to be. A ledger is just honesty with lines drawn under it, and tonight the only honest thing left for her to write was a list of what she had already lost.

She uncapped her pen. Her hand was steady. She noticed that and hated it—a swordspeaker's hands did not shake even when everything else did, and some traitor part of her body still considered itself bound to service.

*Item,* she wrote. *My mother's face.*

That one had gone first, though she had not known it at the time. In the battle on the salt road, when the collectors came down on them like weather, she had spoken the word that unmade a warden's shield—the true name of iron, spoken the way iron liked to be spoken—and the word had gone out of her and taken something with it, the way a river takes soil from a bank so gradually the bank never feels the loss until it is standing in the current.

She had known, abstractly, that her mother had a face. She knew it now only as a fact with a hole in it. *I had a mother,* the knowledge said. *Her face fit my memory the way a key fits a lock.* And then nothing. Not darkness—that would have been something, a shape, a grief with edges. Just absence wearing absence's clothing. When she tried to picture her, she got instead a vivid and useless image of the word *mother* written in ink, as if her mind, scrambling, had offered her the ledger entry in place of the woman.

The Court had taught her this trick without meaning to. She had spent eleven years appraising bottled memories, holding other people's mothers up to the light, pricing them by clarity and vintage. She knew exactly what a mother-memory looked like from the inside. Warm kitchen. Flour on hands. The particular gravity of a voice that has said your name ten thousand times. She could describe one better than most people who still owned theirs.

She simply could not have her own.

*Item,* she wrote. *The taste of honey.*

That loss had come at the Well of Names, or rather on the road after it, when she spoke the second word—the word of water, which had answered her by rising out of the salt flat in a slow silver column and turning aside three arrows meant for Ilsa's throat. Words of power were not free. They were paid in the coin of the speaker, and the speaker did not get to choose the denomination. The water had taken honey.

She discovered it that evening, absurdly, over a strip of dried apple Ilsa had shared with her. It tasted fine. Sweet, tart, correct. And underneath it there should have been—an echo, a resonance, honey answering sweetness across her whole history, first tastes, stolen tastes, honey on a thumb offered by someone whose face she also no longer had—and there was nothing. The taste arrived and departed without leaving any trace of having been enjoyed. She had stood in the ruined street chewing and swallowing like a machine being tested, waiting for pleasure that never assembled itself, while Ilsa watched her with an expression she couldn't bear to look at directly.

Ilsa had said, quietly, "What did it take?"

"Honey," Mara said. "Of all the things in the world. Honey."

"It could be worse."

"It could." She had almost laughed. "But it won't be. That's the thing, Ilsa. It will always be exactly as bad as the next word requires."

Now she wrote it down anyway, because writing was the only prayer she trusted. Under *honey* she added the smaller losses she'd been pretending not to notice, the ones that didn't announce themselves but surfaced like drowned things:

*Item. The color of the door of the house where I grew up.* (Though perhaps the map had taken that, not the words.)

*Item. The name of the game we played on the stairs.* (We. Who was we? The question had stopped hurting somewhere around dawn yesterday, and that frightened her more than the loss.)

*Item. Why I became a swordspeaker.*

That last one she wrote slowly, pressing hard enough to dent the page beneath.

Everyone else's reasons were legible to her—she had read enough souls through the Court's inventory to know the taxonomy. Revenge, most commonly. Fear, nearly as often. Love, rarely, and always expensive. Duty, in the ones the Court bred deliberately, oath-bound from childhood, their memories traded away in installments so gradual they never noticed themselves becoming instruments.

She was not that last kind. She was almost certain she was not that last kind. There had been a moment—she clung to it the way a drowning sailor clings to driftwood that might be a shark—there had been a *moment*, on the night the stranger died in her counting-room, when the word *Vessany* entered her and she had felt not confusion but *recognition*. Grief has a shape. Hers had fit Vessany like a hand fits a glove worn soft with use.

So she had chosen the sword once, for a reason, and the reason had been hers, and the words had eaten it, and now she swung a blade through forms her mind had never learned because her hands remembered what her heart no longer could. She was a letter delivered to an address that had burned down. She was still sealed. Someone had wanted her to say something, once. She could no longer remember what, or to whom, or why it had been worth this price.

*Item,* she wrote, and then sat for a long time with the pen hovering.

Because there was one more loss, the largest one, the one she had been circling all night like a tongue probing a broken tooth.

*Item. The certainty that I am worth saving.*

No. She crossed it out. That wasn't a payment; that was just exhaustion talking, the small hours doing their usual forgery. She drew a line through it firmly, the way she'd been trained—no falsified entries, ever, not even against yourself—and then, honestly, wrote instead:

*Item. The ability to believe the loss will stop.*

That was true. That was the true horror of it, the thing she had finally understood on the salt road with the iron-word still ringing in her teeth: the payments did not diminish. There was no exchange rate that favored her. Each word cost what it cost, and the Court had designed it that way—or the oath had, or the treaty, whoever had authored this arithmetic—and the design assumed the speaker would run out of reasons before running out of words. That was the safety mechanism. That was how oaths stayed kept. No one spends their mother's face lightly. You are supposed to stop.

And she had looked at the next word—the one she would need at the door, whenever they reached the door, the word the map's margins promised in her own handwriting—and she had understood that she intended to spend it anyway.

Not bravery. She wanted to be precise about that, since precision was the last faculty she fully owned. Bravery would be spending the word to save Ilsa, and Ilsa was asleep in the inspector's back room, breathing the shallow breath of someone who had sold her sister to Corvane and hadn't forgiven herself and maybe shouldn't. Bravery would be spending it for Vessany, the whole sleeping country, and Vessany was a stranger to her now in the way her mother was a stranger: a fact with a hole in it.

No. What remained was simpler and colder. She intended to pay because the debt existed. Because somewhere inside the erased architecture of her, a woman had signed an oath in her own blood and handwriting, and that woman deserved to have her signature honored even if Mara could not remember signing it. The oath was the last thing the words had not managed to eat, because the oath lived in her hands and her throat, below memory, below appetite, in the place where sword-forms waited like soldiers at attention.

You do not abandon a debt because you cannot remember contracting it. If she believed nothing else—and the list suggested she believed very little else—she believed in the sanctity of entries. The Court had taught her that too, and like everything they taught, it cut both ways.

She heard Ilsa stir in the back room and stilled her pen until the breathing evened again. Some things were not for sharing yet. Perhaps not ever.

* * *

Near dawn—it was difficult to say dawn in a country where the sun hung forever at the angle it had held at the moment of erasure, but her body kept its own clock stubbornly, another small loyalty she treasured—she finished the inventory.

It ran to two pages. She read it through twice, the way she had read ten thousand appraisal slips: checking each item against the evidence, striking nothing, adding one final line.

*Item, undated, ongoing. Whatever the next word chooses to take. To be recorded when discovered.*

Then she folded the pages, and sat with them in her lap, and thought about burning.

Here was the argument for keeping it: a record is a weapon, and she had built this ledger to be a weapon, and the day would come—soon, if the collectors' horns she'd heard at dusk meant anything—when proof of the Court's arithmetic might matter. Show a jury of anyone a list like this and the Ninefold Court's whole trade stood revealed: not commerce but consumption, not keeping but eating. Every bottled memory in the spire's vaults was a line item in someone else's inventory of loss. This little document was the same crime, itemized, committed against herself by herself, and it was evidence, and evidence survives, and survival was the entire point.

Here was the argument for burning: she had watched the Court keep records for eleven years, and she knew what records did to the people who kept them. Records made loss manageable. Manageable loss became routine. Routine became policy. Somewhere in the spire there was a clerk—not evil, probably, probably tired and underpaid and fond of his supper—who had entered the unmaking of Vessany into a ledger between lunch and the evening bell, and had slept well, because the ledger made the country *small*. A country is unbearable to destroy. An entry is easy.

She would not become that clerk. Not even for herself. Especially not for herself.

There was a third consideration, quieter than either, and it was the one that decided her. If she carried the list, she would reread it. Rereading was how the Court honored its debts—by consulting them, balancing them, letting the books define what was owed. But her mother's face was gone regardless of whether she noted its going. The note did not preserve anything. The note was just a scar she would pick open nightly. And there was a difference—she was only now learning it, learning it the hard way, the way you learn everything in this country—between remembering a loss and rehearsing it.

Rehearsal was the Court's whole art. She refused to study at that school anymore.

She rose, went to the cold hearth in the corner—Inspector Hallward's hearth, frozen mid-life like everything else, half-burned correspondence still lying in the grate, letters from a country that no longer existed—and she fed the pages to the last live coal she carried in her kit, banked that morning from ordinary, unpreserved, honest fire.

The paper caught. The ink darkened, curled, went white, went ash.

She watched each item go. Mother's face: gone again, a second time, and this death at least was witnessed, which was more than the first one got. Honey: gone again. The reason: gone again. She watched with a strange ceremonial attention, the attention she had once given to authenticating a duke's bottled wedding-day, and when the last fragment lifted she said, aloud, in the empty customs house, to Inspector Hallward and the hanging dust and whatever paused ghosts listened from their amber:

"Recorded. Paid. Carried forward."

Carried forward. The accounting term. Debts don't vanish when you close a book; they carry forward to the next period, accruing interest. That was what she was now. Not a person with a past—a debt with legs, walking toward the door, and every step added interest, and the door would collect.

It should have been despair. She examined herself for it carefully, professionally, the way she'd examine a suspect gemstone, and found—underneath the grief, underneath the hollowed places where honey and her mother used to sing—something else. Not hope. Hope was a luxury good and she was done trading. It was closer to what a sword feels, if swords feel anything: no opinion about tomorrow whatsoever, total commitment to the edge, perfect readiness to be used for whatever the hand holding it intends.

She chose to go on. Not because going on was wise, or survivable, or owed to anyone living. She chose it because the alternative was to let the Court's arithmetic be the last word, and Mara Winterbourne—whatever was left of her—had spent her whole erased life believing one article of faith above all others:

Every account can be balanced. And balance does not mean the ledger wins.

She banked the coal. She checked the sword at her hip—her hands ran the first form without asking her permission, smooth as water, and she let them, and thanked them silently, the way you thank a dog that remembers you when you cannot remember it.

Outside, in the paused light, Ilsa emerged rubbing sleep from her eyes, and stopped, and tilted her head, reading Mara's face the way sisters do even when they've forgotten how to be sisters.

"You burned it," Ilsa said. "Whatever you were writing. You burned it."

"Yes."

"Was that giving up?"

"No," Mara said, and found that she meant it. "It was refusing to let it be *their* kind of record. Come on. We're a day from the door, and I want to arrive before Corvane does."

Ilsa frowned. "Why would he—"

The horn sounded then. Not behind them, from the direction of the salt flats and the pursuit they'd outrun. Ahead. From the west, from the door, deep and bronze and patient, blown by someone who did not need to hurry because he already knew, somehow, that they were coming to him.

Corvane was already there.
