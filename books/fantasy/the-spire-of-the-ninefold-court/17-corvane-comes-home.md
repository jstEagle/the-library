## 17. Corvane Comes Home

The salt line did not stop Corvane the way it stopped other men.

He had watched it stop them—collectors and wardens who reached the border of erased Vessany and turned back without knowing why, their ledgers fluttering in their hands like birds that wanted to be elsewhere. The Court's own instruments refused to cross. Compasses spun. Ink ran backward up the page. And men who had served the Ninefold Court for thirty years found, at the edge of that white plain, an argument forming in their mouths that they could not finish, and they would turn around and report that the road was washed out, or the season wrong, or simply that they had decided against it.

Corvane crossed on foot, alone, carrying nothing but his seal and a flask of water, and he did not decide anything at all.

That was the thing he could not put into any report, and he had drafted four of them in his head during the walk from the last living village: that his feet were making this journey without him. That some part of him—the part the ledgers had eaten, the part that was now a hole shaped exactly like a country—had taken hold of his body the way a rider takes a horse, and was carrying it home.

*Home.* He tested the word and found it fit nothing. Corvane had no home. He had quarters in the Spire on the fourth floor, appointed by committee; he had a chair in the Hall of Nine that was older than his tenure and would outlast his bones; he had a coat with his office stitched into the collar so that even his clothing knew what he was. First Auditor of the Ninefold Court. Keeper of the Unmaking Ledgers. A man made entirely of titles, like one of those nesting dolls the eastern traders sold, except that when you opened Corvane all the way down, there was no smallest doll inside. There was only painted wood and a hollow where something should have been.

The salt crunched under his boots like old porcelain. Ahead of him the horizon was a smear of white dissolving into a sky the color of skimmed milk, and somewhere past that horizon lay villages frozen mid-collapse, a battlefield holding its breath, and a house with three sisters' childhood still folded inside it like a letter no one had dared to open.

He had sent collectors after Winterbourne. Six parties. All six had come back empty, or not come back at all, and the ones that came back brought reports he read three times each, looking for what they were not saying. *The country answers her,* one warden wrote, and beneath it, in a different hand—his own, he realized, from some meeting whose memory had been paid away—a note: *Of course it does.*

He had not written that note. Or rather: he had written it, and then arranged to forget writing it, which was worse. That was the architecture of the binding clause. Not ignorance. Ignorance would have been a mercy, a clean amputation. No—it was this: knowledge filed away behind a door he had personally locked and then personally swallowed the key, so that every few years he caught himself circling Vessany in treaty discussions, lingering over its name in the margins of other documents, defending its borders in arguments about jurisdictions no one else cared about—and never once asking himself why.

He asked himself why now. Walking across the salt, alone, with the wind coming off the dead land carrying a smell that was almost rain and almost ash and neither, Corvane finally asked himself the question the way you ask a debt to show its face.

*Why do I regret this?*

And the hole where the answer used to be looked back at him, patient as an auditor.

* * *

The first preserved village rose out of the salt like a shipwreck.

Corvane had read the survey reports, of course. He had approved them, signed the requisitions for the preservation teams, initialed the clause that fixed the unmaking at the moment of signature—3:40 in the afternoon, the forty-first day of autumn, a time chosen because the clerks needed a number and the treaty needed a moment and no one involved thought the moment belonged to anyone. He had known, in the way a man knows the contents of a drawer he has never opened, that the villages stood as they had stood at 3:40 on that day.

Knowing was nothing. Seeing was another country entirely.

A woman hung halfway out of a second-floor window, mid-leap, her body arced like a diver's, her mouth open on a word she had been shouting for thirty years. Below her, a cart of apples lay scattered in the street, each fruit suspended a hand's width above the cobbles, frozen at the height of its falling. A dog strained at the end of a rope that had already snapped—the rope's two ends drifting apart like seaweed—and the dog's eyes were fixed on the woman in the window, and there was such devotion in the animal's arrested body that Corvane had to stop walking and stand very still until his throat agreed to work again.

They had called it preservation in the ledgers. *Preserved pending resolution.* As if the country were a jar of fruit. As if someone, someday, might come back with a resolution.

No one had come back. Until Winterbourne.

He walked on through the falling woman's shadow. His reflection followed him in the window glass, distorted, doubled—a fat gray ghost pacing a man-shaped absence—and he understood suddenly why the collectors' reports grew strange near the center of the preservation. This place did not haunt you. It showed you your own shape against everything you had unmade, and let you do the haunting yourself.

* * *

He found the battlefield in the late light, if light it could be called—that sourceless silver glare the salt threw back up at the sky, noon and dusk at once. The armies of the Last Treaty War stood locked in their final charge, thousands of men and horses fixed at the instant before contact, banners stiff as iron, a trumpet player's cheeks swollen around a note that had been screaming for three decades without ever being heard.

And among them, walking with the ease of someone crossing a market square, a woman with a sword at her hip and a ledger-book bound in gray leather under her arm.

Mara Winterbourne saw him before he could arrange his face. She always did. It was one of the thousand small things about her that had made her the best appraiser the Court had trained in a generation—you cannot lie to a woman who reads the truth off hands and eyes the way other people read print.

She stopped twenty paces away and did not draw. That was its own message. *You are not worth the cost of a word,* it said, and Corvane knew now exactly what words cost her, because he had spent the last week reading the intercepted pages of her private ledger, copied by a collector before she burned it, and the inventory of her losses had kept him awake in a way he had not been awake since—

Since. There was no *since*. That was the whole disease of him.

"First Auditor." Her voice was flat as the plain. "You've come a long way to arrest me."

"I haven't." The honesty surprised them both. He heard it land between them like a dropped coin. "I've come—" and here the hole opened under him again, and he stepped into it deliberately, because he was tired, gods, he was so tired, and there was no one left in the world to whom he could say the next sentence except the woman whose life his signature had helped to hollow out.

"I don't know my name."

* * *

She didn't laugh. He had braced for laughter, or contempt, or the sword after all. Instead Mara Winterbourne studied him with the particular attention she gave to objects whose value she was still determining, and said, "Corvane is your name."

"Corvane is my title." He heard how it sounded, and pressed on anyway. "It was given to me with the office. Before that—" He gestured, and the gesture failed, his hand opening on nothing, the universal sign of the Court's accounting: *no entry found.* "There were papers. An appointment. I have read them. They say a man named Corvane was elevated to First Auditor forty-one years ago. They are signed in his hand. But a signature proves ink, not a man. I have audited enough lives to know the difference."

The trumpet screamed its silent note. Somewhere in the frozen ranks a horse's mane streamed backward forever.

"You're telling me," she said slowly, "that the Court erased *you*."

"The Court does not erase people, Auditor Winterbourne. It trades in them." He almost smiled. "I helped write that language. I should know. When we unmade Vessany, the treaty required payment, and the largest single line item was the memory of the signing itself. Every party to the treaty surrendered it. The clerks, the ministers, the nine of us. It was elegant, actually—it still is. You cannot prosecute a crime no one can remember agreeing to commit. The binding clause holds because the guilt holds, and the guilt holds because the grief has nowhere to live. We paid our memories into the ledger, and the ledger became self-sealing." He looked out at the armies, at the boy-soldier nearest them whose spear-hand had been a farmer's hand the year before; he knew this the way he knew the smell of the salt, from nowhere, from the hole. "I remember the war. I remember the famine after. I remember deciding it was necessary. What I do not remember is the day I decided it, or who I was standing next to when I signed, or whether anyone wept. The ledger ate the day itself. And with it, apparently, whatever I was before I was Corvane."

"And you want me to give it back."

It was not a question, and it was not an accusation, and somehow both were in it. He met her eyes. Gray eyes, winter eyes; he had hired her partly for those eyes, though he had told the selection committee it was for her penmanship.

"I want you to tell me," he said, "whether there is anything left to give back. You have walked this country. You have spoken its names and felt it answer. You know better than any living soul what survives an unmaking and what doesn't." His voice did something he did not authorize; it roughened. "You are the last oath-bound swordspeaker. Your order kept the true records, the ones we burned. If anyone alive can look at a man and say *there was a person in there once*, it is you. So I am asking. Not as the First Auditor. The First Auditor is a set of clothes. I am asking as whatever is inside them."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind moving salt dust along the ground in long pale ropes.

Then Mara Winterbourne unsheathed her sword—not to threaten, he understood that immediately, but the way a priest uncovers a relic—and held it flat between them, blade horizontal, hilt offered. An old gesture. He recognized it from nowhere, from the hole, and recognition went through him like cold water.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked.

"No."

"Neither do I. My body knows. That's all I have—my hands remember what my mind paid away. It's a greeting, I think. Or a testing. Swordspeakers touched blades before they trusted words." Her jaw tightened. "Ilsa says I had sisters. Says one of them chose the Court. I found the proof in my parents' house, and I still don't know which sister I was, and I have stopped sleeping waiting to find out. So understand what you're asking me. You're asking the one person in this world with less of herself than you have to go digging in your ruins. Because if I speak your name—if I pull whatever thread is left of you out of this country's memory—something comes loose. In you, or in me. The Well of Names doesn't give change, Corvane. It only takes."

"Yes," he said. "I know. I priced it."

Something moved behind her eyes at that—anger, maybe, or its twin, pity—and then she did the last thing he expected. She laughed, once, short and bleak, and lowered the sword.

"Look at us." She said it to the frozen armies, to the salt, to the whole ambered corpse of a country. "The keeper of the Unmaking Ledgers and the last swordspeaker, standing in the middle of the crime, each asking the other to prove they were ever real." She looked back at him. "You're right, though. That's the worst part. You're right that I'm the only one who can try. And you're right about the other thing too—I've seen it in every village since the salt line. What survives isn't the people. It's the *reaching*. The woman in the window is still reaching for the child inside the house. The dog is still reaching for the rope. Whatever the Court cut away, it couldn't cut wanting." She sheathed the blade. "So. Hold still, and don't fight it, and if it costs me another piece of myself, you'll owe me, and I keep honest ledgers now."

"What will you speak? Do you know it?"

"I know pieces. The map gave me some. The well gave me more. And there's one word I've been carrying since the orchard—" She stopped. Frowned. "The burning orchard. The first flash, before I ever touched your precious map properly. A voice calling a name through the smoke. I thought it was mine." Her hand drifted to the hilt, not drawing, just resting, the way a person touches a wall in the dark. "But I've been saying it to myself for weeks and it never fits right. Like a key tried in the wrong lock."

"Say it," Corvane said. "Whatever it costs."

She drew a breath. And Corvane, who had audited the deaths of nations without flinching, discovered that his hands had begun to shake.

"*Vessan,*" she said.

Not the country. Not the land, not the treaty-name, not the word stamped across the blank space where maps used to have a nation. A name. A small one, a household one, the kind of name called across a courtyard at supper-time, through smoke, through years—and the salt plain took it the way dry ground takes rain. The frozen trumpet-note bent, just slightly, like grass in wind. Along the ranks of the preserved army, heads turned—not moving, nothing so merciful as movement, but *turning*, attention passing down the lines like fire down a fuse, ten thousand pairs of eyes finding the two small figures standing between the armies.

And inside Corvane—inside the hollow where the smallest doll should have been—something knocked.

Once. Twice. Like a fist on a door. Like a man waking in a room he does not recognize and remembering, before anything else, that he is not supposed to be here.

He went to his knees in the salt. He did not decide to. The name had gone through him and taken his legs with it, and out of the hole came—not memory, not yet, the ledger's locks were strong—but the *shape* of memory, the shadow a thing casts through a wall. A kitchen. Smoke that was not war's smoke. A woman's voice, the same voice from the orchard, saying the name again, exasperated, loving: *Vessan, if you break that fence your father will—*

"That's not my name either," he whispered, and heard his own voice crack down the middle. "That's—Winterbourne. That's a Winterbourne name. That's *your* name, that's one of yours—"

Mara had gone very pale. Her hand was still on her hilt. "Ilsa," she said. "Ilsa's full name. Ilsa Vessan Winterbourne. She told me on the salt road and I never—why would that be the word in the orchard, why would I carry *her* name—"

But Corvane was not listening anymore, because the knocking inside him had become a pounding, and through the splintering wood of the binding clause came one image, whole and unbearable and *his*: a young man with Mara Winterbourne's gray eyes, kneeling in a kitchen, swearing an oath over a sword to a woman with Ilsa's face—swearing to protect the Winterbourne girls, all three of them, *especially the one who talks to the Court's men like she was born to it*—and the young man's own mother laughing from the doorway, saying the name that Corvane had been unable to find for forty-one years, saying it plainly, the way family does:

"*Vessan.* Our Vessan. Off saving the world again."

The First Auditor of the Ninefold Court knelt in the salt of the country he had unmade, and began, very quietly, to weep.

Above them the trumpet finally, after thirty years, fell silent.
