## 19. The Reading of the Ledgers

Mara came to the Court at dawn, alone, as the ledgers required.

That was the joke of it, she thought, standing before the great bronze doors of the guildhall while the city behind her still slept in its grey hour. Every rule the Ninefold Court had ever written was a door, and doors opened from both sides if you knew the words. She had spent eleven years learning the words. She had spent three weeks learning what they were for.

She wore no sword. The sword was a formality; the oath was the weapon, and the oath lived in her throat now, awake, hungry, patient. It had been awake since the salt road, since the frozen country had answered her voice like a dog hearing its master's step. Each word she'd spoken out there had cost her something—her mother's face, the taste of honey, the reason—and she had burned the inventory rather than grieve it twice. But the ledger in her blood kept its own accounts. She could feel the balance. She could feel what remained.

Enough. It would be enough.

The doors were warded, of course. Ninefold wards, laid in the founding, keyed to the blood of every sworn member. Mara pressed her palm to the bronze and spoke the opening formula—*by debt acknowledged, by debt discharged, let the counting-house open*—and felt the wards taste her, hesitate, and let her through. The Court had never imagined a member coming home to collect.

The counting-room was empty at this hour, the appraisal benches dark, the memory-vaults sealed behind their triple glass. She walked through it the way she had walked through it a thousand mornings, her boots making the same small sounds on the same worn stones, and the familiarity of it nearly undid her. This had been her whole world. The rules had been her walls and her warmth together. She had been *invisible* here, and had called the invisibility peace.

The guildhall proper rose beyond the counting-room: a barrel-vaulted space like the inside of a throat, ringed by nine balconies where the auditors sat in session. Below them, the Ledger Floor—a sunken circle of black stone where the great books were kept chained to their lecterns, each one the size of a coffin lid, each one bound in the hide of something that had once had a name.

The Unmaking Ledgers stood at the center, on the Ninth Lectern, apart from the others. Three volumes. No one was permitted to read them aloud. No one was permitted to read them at all, except the First Auditor, in the dark, with the doors locked, once a year, on the night the treaty was signed.

Mara crossed the Ledger Floor and laid her hand on the first volume.

The binding was warm. It had been warm for thirty years, she realized—the warmth of a country still burning inside it, preserved mid-flame like the orchard behind the slum door. The Court had bound Vessany's unmaking into these pages, and the pages had never cooled.

"Stop."

The voice came from the balcony. Corvane. He stood at the rail in his grey auditor's coat, and behind him, one by one, the other eight were arriving—drawn by the wards, by the alarm Mara had tripped simply by touching what she touched. She saw them settle into their places like crows onto a fence: Second Auditor Vesh with her ledger-sight that read debts off a person's skin; the twins Ondrel and Ondra, who between them held every contract ever signed; old Auditor Maren, who had sworn Mara's own oath into the book eleven years ago and had not looked at her since.

Corvane came down the spiral stair. Up close, in the dawn light leaking through the high windows, he looked worse than he had on the salt road. Thinner. Like a man who had been remembering things against his will and losing sleep to them.

"Mara." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "You came back."

"I came to collect," she said. "Article One of the Founding Charter. Any sworn member may demand the reading of any ledger before the full bench, if she stands surety with her own name." She had the words by heart. She had written them into her private ledger in her own handwriting, dated before her oath, and her own handwriting had never lied to her. "I stand surety. Read them."

"That article was struck from the Charter a hundred years ago."

"Then strike it from the book in the vault," Mara said, "and show me the page where the strike was witnessed. You can't. I've looked. The Court kept the article alive because the article was *useful*—because it was the one rule that made the Court look like it had a conscience." She turned to face the nine balconies. "You wrote rules so you would never have to be cruel on purpose. Well. I'm invoking the rule."

Silence on the balconies. She felt them weighing it—she had spent eleven years learning to feel what the Court weighed—and she knew that some of them wanted to refuse, and that all of them *could* refuse, and that none of them would. Because refusing meant admitting the ledgers had something in them that could not survive a reading. Because the Court's whole architecture was built on the belief that its rules would hold against anything.

"First Auditor," said old Maren at last, her voice dry as ledger-paper, "the demand is properly made. If we refuse it, we are in breach of our own founding."

Corvane looked at Mara for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—regret, the kind that had outlived its own reasons. "You understand what you're asking," he said. "The reading unmakes the unmaking. Every debt in those books comes due at once. The Court cannot pay it. *I* cannot pay it."

"Then the Court is insolvent," Mara said. "I've balanced accounts my whole career, Corvane. I know what an insolvent house looks like from the inside. It looks exactly like this."

She opened the first volume.

The pages did not want to turn. The book fought her the way the frozen country had fought the thaw—every leaf heavy with the weight of what was written on it. But her hands knew this work. Eleven years of ledgers; eleven years of small debts, borrowed years, bottled memories, entered fair and balanced fair. She turned to the first page.

And she began to read the names.

* * *

She did not read them the way the Court read entries. She read them the way the Well of Names had taught her—aloud, whole, with the breath behind them, the way a name is spoken by someone who loves you.

"Vessany of the Nine Rivers. Called the Orchard Country. Called the Country That Kept Its Word."

The first word went through the guildhall like a bell struck inside a bell. The high windows shivered in their leads. Dust fell from the barrel vault in a fine grey rain, and somewhere far below the foundations, something groaned—stone remembering it had been built on a filled-in riverbed, on a buried thing, on a name.

On the balconies, the auditors flinched. Vesh pressed her hands over her ears and then took them away, because covering your ears does not stop a debt from being read into you.

"Anselm Vessany, cartographer, who drew the map that would not stay erased. He had a wife. He had a daughter who whistled when she was happy. The treaty took his country and the ledgers took his name, and he died in a border town under a false one, and the false one is *also* in this book, and I am striking it."

She turned the page. The guildhall shook. The chains on the other eight lecterns rattled and snapped link by link, the lesser ledgers trying to flee their own contents.

She read the orchard-keepers. The ferry-women of the Nine Rivers. The salt-merchants. The children of the school at Vessa-town, all forty of them, their names entered in a clerk's hand so small it could only have been written by someone refusing to look at what he wrote. She read the false names too—the names the erased had been given to die under—and she struck them as she went, and each strike was a small thunderclap, and the black stone of the Ledger Floor began to sweat as if the building itself were frightened.

Her voice held. That surprised her. She had expected it to break by now, expected grief to take her the way it had taken her at the house with their childhood in it. But this was not grief. This was *work*. This was the only work she had ever been trained for: precision, patience, one line at a time, and no entry left unbalanced.

Corvane stood at the foot of the Ninth Lectern and did not stop her. She glanced at him once, between pages, and saw that he was mouthing the names after her—silently, half a beat behind, like a man remembering a prayer. His own erased memories were the binding clause; Ilsa had told her that; the treaty was nailed shut with what Corvane used to be. Every name she spoke loosened the nail.

She came to the page of the swordspeakers.

* * *

The swordspeakers of Vessany had been nine, and the ledgers had them all: their oaths, their prices, the memories each had paid. She read the oaths aloud and the guildhall rang with them, and she felt her own oath stir in her throat in answer, recognizing its siblings, wanting to be spoken.

And there—third from the bottom, in handwriting she knew, because she had deciphered it on the map's margin, because it was her own—

*Winterbourne, Mara. Third daughter. Price paid: her own name, held in escrow until the debt of Vessany is discharged or the oath is spoken whole.*

Not erased. *Held.* All this time, she had not lost her name. The Court had been keeping it for her, the way the Court kept everything—fair and balanced, in a vault, at interest.

Her eyes blurred. She let them. Then she read the entry aloud.

"Mara Winterbourne. Third daughter of Anselm's line. Who chose the oath so her sisters could choose the door. Whose price was her own name, held against the day the account came due." Her voice cracked on the last word, finally, and she let it. "The day is today. I am calling the debt. I am speaking myself back."

She said her name the way the Well had taught her, the way you say the name of someone you love, with the breath behind it, whole.

The guildhall did not shake this time. It *sang*—one vast harmonic note from foundation to finial, every stone in the building remembering what it was built on, and the high windows blew out all at once in a bloom of glass, and through the empty frames the dawn came in, and with the dawn, faint and impossible and thirty years late, the smell of orchards in blossom.

On the balconies, the auditors were on their feet. Old Maren was weeping without any expression at all, tears running down a perfectly composed face. The twins were holding each other. And Corvane—

Corvane had gone to his knees on the black stone, both hands pressed flat against it, his head bowed. The binding clause was tearing, and she could see it tearing him, the way a knot tears the rope. He was remembering. She could see him remembering, and it was breaking him open, and she did not know yet what he would do when he finished.

The second and third volumes of the Unmaking Ledgers were straining at their chains now, pages fanning themselves, the rest of the debt impatient. There were more names. There were always more names. And the guildhall was coming apart around her in slow, groaning increments, and somewhere beneath the Ledger Floor the buried river had begun, audibly, to run.

Mara turned the last page of the first volume, set her hand flat on the second, and kept reading.

Behind her, in a voice she had never heard him use—rough, young, unguarded, the voice of whoever he had been before the treaty—Corvane began, at last, to speak his own name.
