## 22. The Spire Stands Empty

The village of Lowmarsh had no name for what Mara did with the sword, so they called it *the lesson*, and after a while that was name enough.

She taught on the green behind the mill, where the ground stayed soft and the morning fog came off the river in long gray ribbons. Eight children at first. Then twelve. Then more than she could count without taking off her boot and using her toes, which she did once, solemnly, to great delight. They came because their parents sent them—because a swordspeaker who had unmade the Ninefold Court was worth something even in a place where nothing was worth much—and then they kept coming because it turned out that standing in the cold before dawn, breathing together, learning to hold a wooden stave as if it were the only true thing in the world, felt like being *kept*. Like being a thing someone had decided not to lose.

"Again," Mara said.

Twelve staves came up. Twelve small bodies settled into the third form, the one called *Listening*—though none of them knew it had ever been called anything, because Mara had never told them. Some names you gave away freely. Some you kept in your mouth like stones from a riverbed, smooth and private, warmed by carrying.

"Wren, your elbow."

Wren, who was nine and all elbows anyway, corrected it.

"Bram, don't look at your feet. Your feet know this. It's your eyes that are the coward."

Bram laughed, and lost his grip, and his stave went skittering across the frost-browned grass, and everyone laughed, and Bram went red to the ears and fetched it back, and Mara let the laughter run its course before she said "again," because a teacher who could not bear to be laughed at would teach children nothing but fear, and fear was the one inventory she had sworn never to stock again.

Her hands remembered everything. That was the strange mercy of it. Whatever else the speaking had taken—and it had taken much; she kept the list of absences the way other people kept pressed flowers—it had never touched the forms. Her body was a book the Court had never thought to bind. So she taught from the hands down: here is how the wrist turns, here is how the weight shifts, here is how you stand when the world asks you to stand. And if a child asked *why do we do it this way*, she said, *because someone loved someone enough to make it work,* which was true, and which was all of it she still had.

When the lesson ended she walked the fog-line back toward the village, and the children peeled away one by one to their chores and their breakfasts, calling goodbyes over their shoulders, and Mara stood a moment alone on the green with her breath hanging white in the air, and felt the thing she had learned to recognize and not distrust: peace. Not the flat numbness of her counting-room years. Not the white heat of the salt road. Something with texture to it, like wool. Something you had to earn every morning, the way the miller earned his flour, the way the river earned its going.

Then she went inside, and opened her ledger, and made the day's first entries.

* * *

The ledger sat on the kitchen table of the cottage she rented from Widow Hask, who charged her too little and left bread on the sill too often, and whom Mara had entered into the book under *debts freely owed* with a note: *bread, twice weekly, unasked-for, therefore priceless.*

It was an ordinary ledger. That was the whole point of it, and also the miracle. Calveskin cover, plain. No wards, no locks, no clauses that bit. The paper was cheap and drank ink greedily, and the columns were drawn by hand and slightly crooked, and Mara had come to love the crookedness the way you love a scar: proof that a hand had done it, a fallible human hand, working in the world instead of above it.

In the Court's ledgers, every entry had been a theft dressed as arithmetic. *One memory, appraised. Three years, borrowed against collateral. One country, written out.* She had balanced those books for eleven years with perfect precision and never once asked what precision cost the people in the columns, because the people in the columns had been reduced to line-items, and line-items do not bleed.

This ledger held only three kinds of things, and she had written the rule on the first page in letters large enough that any child who found the book could read them:

*NAMES. BIRTHS. DEBTS FREELY OWED.*

Nothing entered without consent. Nothing entered that the person entered had not agreed to be remembered. When old Tam Fletcher died in the autumn, she wrote his name, and under it the single sentence he had approved while he could still speak: *He mended shoes and argued beautifully.* When Wren's sister was born—a squalling red scrap they named Odile, after nobody, because Wren insisted the girl should get to be the first of herself—Mara wrote the birth, date and hour, and Wren pressed her thumbprint beside it in berry-ink, laughing, and Mara looked at that smudge of purple and thought: *this is what accounting was supposed to be.* A way of saying *you were here.* Not a way of owning that you were.

There were debts in it too, but only the kind people chose. Widow Hask's bread. The cooper's loan of a cart when Bram's father fell ill. Ilsa—

Mara paused over that page, as she paused every morning, though there had been nothing new to write since midwinter.

*Ilsa Winterbourne. Debts freely owed: my life, twice. My name, once. Payment pending, terms unknown, creditor absent.*

That was the honest way to put it. The Court would have written something cleaner. The Court would have written something that closed.

* * *

Ilsa had come back in the spring, or part of her had.

She arrived on a market day, walking up the cart-road with a pack on her back and dust on her boots, and Mara saw her from across the square and felt her whole body go still in the old swordspeaker way, everything gathered and quiet, waiting for the blow. Except the blow didn't come. Ilsa just stopped in front of her, and frowned—the frown of a woman trying to recall a room she'd only visited once—and said:

"You look like someone I was fond of. I can't think who."

And Mara, who had rehearsed a hundred greetings on sleepless nights, who had prepared herself for hatred or grief or the blank soldierly courtesy of a collector returned to duty, heard herself say simply:

"I'm fond of you too. Would you like tea?"

Isla stayed. That was the fragment that remained: not the sisterhood, not the salt road, not the well, not the betrayal or the forgiveness or the door—but the *shape* of affection, like a footprint left in set concrete. She did not remember loving Mara. But she behaved, sometimes, unmistakably, like a woman who loved her. She saved Mara the heel of the loaf without thinking. She stood at Mara's shoulder during lessons and corrected grips in a voice roughened by old habit. Once, watching the fog roll off the river, she said, apropos of nothing, "You used to hum when you were nervous," and then looked startled at her own mouth, and Mara had to walk to the well and draw water for ten minutes before her face would behave.

They did not call each other sister. The word had too many edges. But Ilsa slept in the loft now, and her boots were by the door next to Mara's, and some mornings Mara woke to the sound of her whetting a knife below and thought: *the Court took her loyalty and gave her nothing, and she came home anyway, to the shape of a life she can't remember choosing.* And some mornings she thought darker thoughts—that the fragments might be worse than absence, that a footprint in concrete mocks the foot. But most mornings she thought only: *she's here. Write it down. She's here.*

She never wrote Ilsa's memories back. The Well of Names had been destroyed—or freed, depending on how you read it—when the ledgers unraveled, and whatever names it had held spilled out into the country like seed from a torn sack, taking root where they would. Vessany was remaking itself, slowly, badly, magnificently, in the mouths of people who mostly misremembered it, and Mara had come to believe that was the only way a country could honestly live. What Ilsa had lost was lost the way a field lies fallow. Perhaps something would grow. Perhaps not. It was not Mara's to force, and forcing was the Court's whole grammar, and she had burned that dictionary.

But she kept the ledger open to Ilsa's page every morning, and read the entry aloud, quietly, like a prayer or a promise: *creditor absent, payment pending, terms unknown.* Because a debt freely owed should stay open until both parties agree to close it. That was the whole difference between her book and theirs.

* * *

Vessany itself came back the way a body comes back after fever: unevenly, with aches, with patches of strange color.

The salt flat had broken apart in the exhale—Mara refused, forever after, to call it anything but an exhale—and where the salt had been there was mud, then grass, then, by the second summer, actual rivers finding their old beds with the blind certainty of water remembering. Villages reassembled out of the amber-stasis of their preserved ruins, and the people who stepped out of them were the same people who had frozen mid-collapse thirty years ago, blinking, mid-scream, mid-prayer, alive. Grief came with them intact. The dead stayed dead. There was no clause anywhere in the unraveling that undid dying, and Mara had stopped expecting mercy from the mechanics of the world. You got what the ledger gave you. Sometimes what it gave you was a second chance at ordinary weather.

Lowmarsh sat near the border of the returning country—not in it, exactly; borders had gone soft and embarrassed about themselves since the Unmaking unraveled—and so the village got travelers. Refugees, returners, scholars with bad manners, and occasionally pilgrims of a newer faith that worshipped nothing except the principle that *things erased leave shadows, and shadows can be spoken to.* Mara fed whoever knocked. She had eaten enough meals counted out to the last crumb by someone else's arithmetic to know what a bowl of soup given freely was worth.

Some of them asked to see the Spire.

Everyone knew about the Spire now. The guildhall of the Ninefold Court still stood two weeks' ride south, tall and pale and impossible to look at directly, like a tooth pulled but not discarded. After the reading—after Mara had stood in the hall of nine auditors and spoken name after name after name until the binding clauses cracked and the shelves of bottled years burst and the doors to erased countries blew open all at once like windows in a storm—the Court had simply... stopped. No decree dissolved it. No army sacked it. The collectors drifted home. The auditors aged out or wandered off or, in Corvane's case—

In Corvane's case, he had cut the clause and then he had wept, openly, in front of everyone, remembering all at once why he regretted, and then he had walked north into the returning country and never been seen again. Mara liked to think he'd found a village that needed a meticulous man with terrible regrets. She had entered him in her ledger under names, with the words *he tried, at the end, to pay.* It was more than the Court ever entered about anyone.

So the Spire stood empty. Nine floors of vaulted silence, its impossible inventory spilled or looted or given away, its ledgers ash, its seals broken. Squatters came and went. Birds nested in the appraisal chambers. And on the top floor—the ninth floor, the floor where the Unmaking Ledgers had lived—there was a single door that had not been there before the night the ledgers burned.

* * *

Mara climbed the Spire once a month. She told herself it was inspection; she told Widow Hask it was business; she suspected, privately, that it was closer to tending a grave, or watering a tree she hadn't planted and didn't own but couldn't abandon.

The door stood at the center of the top floor, freestanding, framed in dark wood gone silver with age that it hadn't had a month ago. It opened onto anywhere. That wasn't poetry. She had tested it, carefully, the way she tested everything now—with consent, with witnesses, with her hand on her sword-hilt and her feet ready. Through it she had seen the burning orchard of Vessany, unburned, heavy with fruit. A harbor city whose name she didn't know, all rigging and gulls. A desert under three moons. Once, unbearably, a kitchen: copper pots, a woman humming, a smell of honey—

She had closed that one gently, the way you close a letter you're not ready to answer.

The door opened onto anywhere, but it opened *outward only from the Spire*, and only for someone willing to pass through having chosen what they carried. That was the toll. Not loyalty surrendered, not truth surrendered—those were the false choices the original makers had built into the doors, the ones Ilsa had paid, the ones Mara had refused. This door, grown new from the ruin of all the others, asked a different price: you could take nothing through it that you had not consciously, freely, knowingly chosen to carry. Fear traveled fine. Love traveled fine. Debt traveled, if it was freely owed. Stolen things turned to smoke at the threshold. Lies turned around and walked back through on their own legs, which several smugglers had found deeply upsetting.

Mara had gone through exactly once, in the early spring, carrying nothing but her name—and come back the same evening, homesick for Lowmarsh within the hour, laughing at herself on the far side of the world. She wasn't ready. Maybe she'd never be ready, and maybe that was fine. The point was not the going. The point was that the door existed, and that it asked nothing but honesty, and that she had decided—after a winter of deciding, alone, in the fog, on the green, over the crooked ledger—that such a door should never again be locked.

Doors that locked were how the Court had begun. Everything else had followed from that: the locking, the keeping of keys, the keeping of the keepers, the slow conversion of every person in the building into either a lock or a key. Mara had spent eleven years as a key. She had spent one year becoming a person. She would not preside over the beginning of that story again, not even accidentally, not even kindly.

So she swept the top floor, and oiled the hinges, and left the door unlocked.

* * *

On a bright morning in late summer—the anniversary of nothing in particular, which was the best kind of anniversary—Mara finished the month's ledger entries and packed her satchel for the climb.

"You're going to the tower again," Ilsa said from the doorway. Not a question. Fragments-Ilsa always knew, in that wordless way, the rhythm of Mara's absences, and always stood in the doorway when she left, and always said something at the edge of asking to come.

"There's dust on the ninth floor," Mara said.

"There's dust everywhere. You have a broom here."

"It's a particular dust."

Ilsa snorted. Then, softer, in the voice she used maybe twice a season: "Come back for supper. I'm making the thing with the onions." She didn't know it was Mara's favorite. Mara had never told her. It didn't matter. The knowing was in the making, and the making was in the house, and the house was in Lowmarsh, and Lowmarsh was real, and that was enough. More than.

"Save me the heel," Mara said, and kissed her cheek—Ilsa flinched the way she always flinched, and leaned in the way she always leaned, the contradiction living comfortably in her body—and walked out into the bright.

The road south ran along the new river. Children waved from the barley fields. At the border-marker—just a stone now, just a stone with a word on it that a mason had cut fresh and slightly crooked: *VESSANY*—a family was crossing with a cart, arguing cheerfully about whether the old country would want them back or whether they'd all become strangers in the meantime, and Mara listened to the argument fade behind her and thought: *both. Both is the answer. Both is the only honest answer anyone ever gets.*

The Spire rose out of the afternoon haze, pale and improbable, wearing ivy now on its lower floors like a retired general wearing civilian clothes. Inside smelled of dust and birds and, faintly, impossibly, of rain on hot stone—the smell of somewhere else leaking gently through the unlocked door nine floors up. She climbed. Her legs knew the stairs. Her hands trailed along the rail the way they trailed along a sword-grip, and on the seventh-floor landing she passed a squatter's abandoned kettle and a nest of swallows, and smiled at both.

The ninth floor was full of light. The roof had a hole in it now, tastefully placed, and the afternoon came down in a broad gold column, and motes turned in it like slow bright fish. The door stood in the middle of the light, silver-framed, patient, ajar.

Through the gap she could see—today—a hillside. Long grass moving in wind she couldn't feel. Somewhere beyond the hill, smoke from a chimney, a country going about its business, wanting nothing from her.

Mara swept. She oiled the hinges. She checked the frame for rot and found none, because the door seemed to maintain itself out of sheer willingness. Then she sat down in the column of light with her back against the doorframe—against the door itself, warm as a living shoulder—and took out the ledger, and read the day's entries one more time.

*NAMES:* Tam Fletcher. Odile, born at dawn. Corvane, who tried. Nine hundred and forty-one others, entered since spring, each one consented, each one crooked-inked, each one free.

*BIRTHS:* Odile. Two foals. A rumor of twins in Millbrook, unconfirmed, awaiting permission.

*DEBTS FREELY OWED:* Bread, twice weekly. A cart, returned with thanks. Ilsa Winterbourne—my life, twice; my name, once; payment pending, terms unknown, creditor home.

She read that last line twice. Then she dipped her pen, and beneath it, in her careful hand, she added a word she had been circling for months without landing on, the way a moth circles a lamp:

*Creditor home. Terms: ongoing.*

Not closed. Never closed. Open the way the door was open—not because nothing was owed, but because what was owed was love, and love does not settle accounts; it only keeps them.

Mara closed the book. She sat a while longer in the gold light, listening to the wind on the far side of the door move through grass she might someday walk in, when she chose, carrying what she chose. Her sword lay across her knees—her true sword, the one she'd carried out of Vessany, the one whose oath she had spoken aloud until it shattered into honesty—and she rested her palm on the flat of it, and felt it hum, very faintly, like a sleeping animal dreaming of work.

Below her, faintly, she could hear the village bell ringing the hour. Somewhere down there, twelve children were eating supper and complaining about onions. Somewhere down there, a woman with a footprint-shaped heart was saving her the heel of the loaf. Somewhere down there, a crooked ledger waited on a kitchen table for tomorrow's names.

Above her, around her, through the open door, the world went on being wide.

Mara Winterbourne—who had been a key, and then a weapon, and then a wound, and was now, at last, merely a woman with a broom, a book, and a name she had paid everything to keep—stood, shouldered her satchel, and left the door unlocked behind her.
