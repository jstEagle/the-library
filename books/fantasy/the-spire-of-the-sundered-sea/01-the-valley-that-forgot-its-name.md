## 1. The Valley That Forgot Its Name

The grain caravan moved at the speed of its slowest ox, which meant Tamsin Vane had four days to think, and thinking was the one thing her vows forbade her to do well.

She rode point on the lead wagon with her hood back and her hands loose on the reins, letting the mule pick its own line down the old flood-road. Behind her stretched eleven wagons of barley and millet, forty souls, and the particular smell of the Sundered Valley in late harvest: wet stone, woodsmoke, and river silt that never quite dried. Somewhere ahead, three miles maybe, the road bent around the shoulder of a hill that had been a temple once, before the water took it, before the water gave it back.

Nobody in the valley called it anything. That was the rule here, unspoken but absolute: the valley had no name because names could be found. Maps were burned or never drawn. Travelers who left told nothing, or told lies so thick no one believed them, or — most often — simply never came back, because there was nowhere out to come back from. The Sundered Valley sat in the drowned heart of a kingdom called Mereth, and if you said *Mereth* aloud, an elder would spit and tell you not to invite things in.

Tamsin had lived here twenty years. She knew exactly what could be invited in, and it wasn't much. That was the whole point of the place.

"Vane." Doss Halloway's voice carried up from the third wagon, cracked and cheerful as ever. "You see anything?"

"I see your hat is on fire," she said.

Doss clapped a hand over his pipe, scattering embers into the road dust, and the wagons behind him laughed. It was a good sound. Tamsin let herself hold onto it for the length of a breath, the way she'd been taught — hold it, mark it, let it go — and then went back to watching the treeline.

There was nothing in the treeline. There was almost never anything in the treeline. In twenty years of guarding caravans, shrine processions, tithe-loads and bride-trains through the valley, Tamsin had fought bandits twice, wolves once, and a very confused bear that turned out to be a man wearing a bearskin for reasons he explained at length before anyone asked. The work was easy. The pay was honest. The silence was total.

That was why people hired her, though they didn't know it. Not the sword. Not the twenty years. The silence.

She had sworn herself to Vael when she was nine years old, in a shrine that smelled of ash and cold iron, with an ember the size of a robin's egg sitting in a stone bowl where a god should have been. The priest — the last priest, though nobody used that word anymore — had held her small hands over the ember and said: *He does not speak. He does not answer. He asks nothing and gives nothing, and in return you will give Him your noise.* She hadn't understood then. Twenty years later she understood perfectly. Everyone carried noise inside them — fear, want, grief, the endless chattering argument of being alive — and Tamsin's vows had banked hers down like a fire raked over with ash. She felt things. She simply did not let them speak first.

It made her very good at her job and very poor company, and she had made peace with both.

The caravan crested the hill at midday, and below them lay the flood-plain: terraced fields stitched along the ribs of drowned streets, houses built on the second stories of older houses, church towers standing out of the shallows like gray teeth with laundry strung between them. The village of Lowmark, such as it was — two hundred people stacked vertically because the water kept taking the ground floor and nobody saw any reason to argue about it.

And beside the road at the plain's edge, half-sunk in silt and moss, stood the ward-stone.

Tamsin looked at it the way she always did, without looking at it. It was just a rock. A tall, leaning slab of black granite carved all over with letters in a script nobody living could read, worn smooth on one side where generations of cart-drivers had tied off their lead ropes to it. Children climbed it. Farmers blamed it for bad weather. If you'd told the people of Lowmark that the stone was the only reason their valley existed at all — that it was the keystone of wards laid down in the last terrible year of Mereth, wards that folded the whole valley out of the sight of gods and kings and magic itself — they would have laughed, politely, the way you laughed at madmen and priests.

They didn't know. Almost none of them knew. And Tamsin, keeper of a guttering god-ember and the only person in the valley still under vow, had been ordered by that same dying faith to make sure they never did.

*Let them sleep*, the ember had said, once, in the single word it had given her in twenty years. *Sleep.*

So she did her job, and kept her mouth shut, and collected her silver.

* * *

The trouble started at dusk, and it started, as most trouble did these days, with a lantern.

Not Tamsin's lantern. Hers hung from the lead wagon's frame like it had every night for twenty years — a plain iron cage holding a flame that gave light and no more, because that was all it had ever been asked to give. The other lantern belonged to the surveyor.

Crown surveyors came through the valley perhaps twice a year. They were tolerated the way weather was tolerated: necessary, unwelcome, endured. This one was young, precise, and named Aldery Fenwick, and he traveled with a case of brass instruments, a ledger bound in green leather, and a lantern with a lens of smoked glass that he claimed measured "residual luminescence." He had said this to Doss over supper with the confidence of a man who had never once been laughed at, and Doss had nodded along, having understood perhaps a third of it.

Fenwick was taking his evening readings at the ward-stone when Tamsin walked the picket line.

She heard him before she saw him — the small industrious sounds of a man alone with his instruments, the scratch of a nib, a muttered number. Then a pause. Then the muttering stopped.

She came around the mossy flank of the stone and found the surveyor crouched at its base, his smoked-glass lantern open, its beam laid flat against the carved letters. And the letters were glowing.

Green. Faint as foxfire, faint as memory, but unmistakably green, running along the worn channels of the script like water finding an old channel. It pulsed once. Twice. Then guttered out, leaving the granite black and ordinary, and the night suddenly very loud with crickets.

Fenwick sat back on his heels. His face in the lantern-light was pale and delighted, and delight was the wrong expression entirely, and Tamsin felt her hand move to her sword-hilt without consulting her.

"You saw that," Fenwick said. He wasn't asking.

"The moss," Tamsin said. "Glows sometimes. Wet season."

"It's been dry for six weeks." He was already writing, nib flying. "Residual luminescence, sustained pulse interval of approximately—" He glanced up. "You're the guard captain? Vane?"

"Guard."

"Captain Vane." He wrote that too. "This is remarkable. Do you know how many readings like this I've taken across the crown's territories?" He answered himself before she could refuse the question. "None. Zero. In eleven years. The instrument registers background decay everywhere else — everything's fading, has been since the Sundering, since the old kingdom's magics bled out of the world. But this—"

"This is a rock," Tamsin said, "in a ditch, and my caravan leaves at dawn, and you're welcome to stand in the ditch all night."

Something flickered across Fenwick's face — not offense; calculation. He closed his ledger with a soft clap. "You know," he said pleasantly, "when I was assigned this circuit, my superior told me the valley would be a waste of time. *Nothing there*, he said. *Nothing worth measuring*. Which struck me as odd phrasing for a surveyor. Nothing worth *measuring*. Not nothing to see. Nothing to measure." He stood, brushing silt from his knees, and smiled at her with genuine warmth. "Your valley isn't on any map I've ever seen, Captain Vane. Not the old ones, not the new ones. Do you know how difficult it is for something to be absent from eleven years of surveys? Absence leaves a shape. I've spent my career studying the shapes of absences." He picked up his lantern. "And this is the first absence that's ever glowed at me."

"Goodnight, sir," Tamsin said.

He went back toward the campfire, whistling. Tamsin stayed by the stone a long moment, her breath shallow, her hands very quiet at her sides.

Slowly, deliberately, she knelt and laid her palm flat against the black granite.

Nothing. Cold stone, damp moss, the dumb patience of a thing that had held its post for two hundred years. Whatever had flickered was gone, and might have been nothing — wet-season phosphorescence, a trick of the lens, the surveyor's instruments stirring up static in the air the way a comb stirs up sparks. Might have been.

But she had seen the green run along those letters like blood along a groove, and she had seen it *pulse*, and pulses meant a heartbeat, and heartbeats meant—

*Enough.* She shut the thought down the way she'd been trained to shut down every thought that got loud. She rose, walked the rest of the picket line, checked the watch rotations, checked the axle-grease, checked the tarp on the fourth wagon where the millet had gotten wet, and did all of it with the flawless, empty competence of a woman whose mind was exactly where her feet were.

Only when she reached her bedroll behind the lead wagon did she take out the lantern.

It was small, old, and ugly — dented tin, a door that wouldn't latch, a handle wrapped in leather gone hard as horn. Inside, on a bed of grey ash, sat the ember: a lump the size of a robin's egg, dull as a coal three days dead. It had looked like that for twenty years. It would look like that until it didn't, someday, and then Tamsin Vane would be the former keeper of nothing, free to be loud like everyone else, and some part of her — the part she didn't let speak — dreaded that day more than she feared any blade.

She opened the little door and cupped her hands around the ember, close enough to feel warmth, though there was never warmth. Close enough to listen, though it never spoke.

"Old man," she murmured. "Tell me the wards are fine."

The ember sat in its ash. Grey. Silent. Twenty years silent, and before that, mostly silent, and before *that* —

Well. Before that was the drowning, and the Sundering, and the reason a kingdom full of gifted children had been hidden inside a pocket of dead air, and Tamsin had never been told any of it, because the ones who knew were ash now, and the god who could have told her was a cold egg in a broken lantern.

But tonight — and this she would turn over for hours afterward, examining it like a coin suspected of being clipped — tonight, as she sealed the little door, she thought she felt something. Not a word. Not warmth. Just a shift, deep in the grey, the way a house shifts at night when somewhere far away a door has been opened.

She slept badly, which was rare, and dreamed of water rising over green-lit stones, which was worse.

* * *

They broke camp at dawn, and Fenwick rode with the caravan as far as the crossroads at Millhollow, chattering about drainage gradients while Doss nodded and the drivers rolled their eyes. At the crossroads he shook Tamsin's hand with both of his, thanked her for a pleasant escort, and turned his mule east, toward the pass, toward the crown's roads and the crown's offices and whatever clerk would read his green-leather ledger.

Tamsin watched him go longer than she needed to.

"Trouble?" Doss asked, coming up beside her, pipe already lit again.

"No."

"You've got your listening face on."

"I don't have a listening face."

"Everyone's got a listening face. Yours is just quieter than most." He puffed smoke at the sunrise. "Twenty years I've hired you, Vane. Twenty years, and you've never once lied to me badly enough that I couldn't hear it. So I'll ask different. Is the valley safe?"

And there it was, the question, the whole question, the question under every contract and every harvest and every child sent down to the water-stairs alone. Tamsin thought of the green light pulsing in dead letters. Of a surveyor's delighted voice saying *absence leaves a shape*. Of the shift in the grey, like a door opening somewhere far away.

"Yes," she said.

Doss studied her, and she watched him decide — the way the whole valley decided, every day, without knowing they were deciding — to believe her. To believe in the borrowed safety, because the alternative was to admit they had been living on credit for two hundred years.

"Good," he said. "Because we've got barley to sell."

The caravan groaned into motion, wheels and oxen and forty sleepy voices, rolling north along the flood-road past the ward-stone, past the drowned towers with their washing lines, past the shrine where Tamsin would kneel that evening and find the offering bowl full of ash that had not been ash the day before.

She didn't know that yet. She rode point with her hood back and her hands loose, and behind her the valley lay green and gold and utterly, beautifully blind, and above the eastern hills — so high and thin that only someone who had spent twenty years learning to look at nothing would ever notice it — a single bird crossed the sky that had too wide a wingspan, and flew against the wind, and did not flap.
