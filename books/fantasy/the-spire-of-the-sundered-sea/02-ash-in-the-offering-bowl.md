## 2. Ash in the Offering Bowl

The shrine of Vael stood at the edge of Hollowmere where the last house gave up and the reeds took over, a low stone building with a roof of slate gone green and a door that had never once been locked. Tamsin Vane had been coming to it since she was six years old, when Mother Aldith had first pressed her palm against the offering bowl and told her, *you'll do*. Twenty years later she still didn't know what the old woman had seen in her, only that whatever it was lived in a shallow iron bowl on a stone altar and was dying.

She climbed the path at dusk, boots wet from the caravan road, shoulders still carrying the ache of two days' driving oxen through rain. The grain caravan was delivered, the contract closed, coin in her belt-purse and nothing owed to anyone until the next one came. In the valley that was as close to peace as life offered.

Inside, the shrine smelled of cold ash and old tallow. No incense — Vael's people burned nothing sweet for their god. Silence was the offering. You came in, you sat down, you shut your mouth, and if the god had anything to say to you, it said it in the space you left.

Tamsin knelt at the altar and looked into the bowl.

The ember sat in its bed of grey like a coal someone had forgotten. It had been the size of a fist once — so Mother Aldith claimed, and Mother Aldith had lied about smaller things than gods. Now it was barely an apple's worth of red, pulsing slow, each glow a little weaker than the last. Tamsin had been measuring it without meaning to, the way you measure a sick friend's face. Three years ago it had flared when she entered. A year ago it had warmed. Tonight it did neither.

"Evening," she said, because she always said something, and silence before the god spoke first felt less like devotion and more like eavesdropping.

Nothing. The ember breathed its dull red breath.

She settled onto her knees properly, the way she'd been taught, spine straight, hands open on her thighs. The vows ran through her on their own track, worn smooth as river stones: *I keep what is given me. I speak what must be heard. I spend myself before I spend another.* She'd taken them at seventeen with a knife-prick and a mouthful of ash, and in twenty years of soldiering she had never once found them inconvenient. That was the thing about serving a silent god. He made no demands you could fail. He just watched, or seemed to watch, out of a coal the size of an apple, while you got on with the work of staying alive.

But tonight the watching felt thinner. Tonight the ember looked less like a god and more like a fire that had not been fed.

She reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward a strange dog — and laid two fingers against the bowl's rim.

The metal was warm. Warmer than it should have been, warmer than the room, and under the warmth a tremor, fine as a plucked string. Tamsin went very still. In twenty years the bowl had never trembled. The ember had flickered, had dimmed, had once — the night Mother Aldith died — flared high enough to throw shadows on the rafters. But it had never *moved* like this, like something leaning against the inside of a door.

Then the light guttered. Not down — sideways, dragging itself up over the bowl's lip in a thin tongue of red, and in that tongue Tamsin saw, or heard, or received, one word.

*Door.*

Not a voice. Nothing so kind. It arrived behind her eyes the way a blow arrives, already finished before she knew it had started, and it left the same ringing quiet a blow leaves. She snatched her hand back and knelt there in the dark with her heart going like a drummer boy's.

"Door," she said aloud, testing it. "What door?"

The ember sank back into its bed of ash. The trembling stopped. Whatever had spent itself saying that single word lay still now, dimmer than it had been, and Tamsin understood with a coldness that started in her fingers and worked inward that she had just watched something pay a price. A word like that, from a god this small, cost what a mercenary's last arrow costs. You did not loose it at nothing.

She stayed on her knees a long time. The tallow stub on the altar shelf burned down another finger-width. She turned the word over every way she knew. There was the Door in the old stories — every child raised in the valley knew the stories, the drowned kingdom, the wards, the way in that was also the only way out, though nobody living had ever claimed to have seen it. The elders called the stories comfort-food for the old-timers, the ones who still remembered Mereth before the water. *There are no doors,* Elder Maren had said at the autumn moot, not three months past. *There is the valley, and there is us, and the crown has forgotten us, and that is the whole of our good fortune.*

Good fortune. Tamsin looked at the guttering coal and thought: *he doesn't think so.*

Because that was the other thing, the thing she had carried home from the caravan road like a splinter working toward her heart. The surveyor's lantern. Green light, at the ward-stone, where green light had no business being. She had told herself it was swamp-gas, some alchemist's trick of the glass, the man's own nerves. She had told herself all the way back down the valley, and the telling had gotten thinner with every mile, and now she knelt in front of a dying god who had spent his last strength on a single word, and the word fit the splinter exactly.

*Door.* As in: the way out. As in: the wards were not eternal. As in: the valley's invisibility was a lamp burning oil, and somewhere tonight somebody had seen the flame move.

"I'm listening," she said to the ember. It gave her nothing further. Of course it didn't. Vael was a silent god; even his dying was done quietly. One word was a sermon, from him. One word was a shout.

She banked the altar candle, swept the ash from the bowl's rim with the little brush kept for the purpose, and did the small offices that had no theology in them, only habit. Then she sat back on her heels in the dark and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the ward-stone.

If the wards failed, the crown would come. Not soldiers first — the valley had nothing worth looting and everyone knew it. Witch-hunters first. The proclamation would follow, the registers, the pyres. And the valley was full of the old-timers, the gifted children smuggled in forty and fifty years ago, now grandmothers with bad knees whose gifts had slept so long they'd half-forgotten them themselves. People like — Tamsin stopped the thought there, the way you stop your hand at the edge of a blade.

People like her.

She had never said that sentence to herself in full. She had danced around it her whole adult life. She kept the shrine, she bore the vows, she alone among the valley's mercenaries could feel the ember's moods through the iron — and she had filed all of it under *duty*, under *inheritance*, under anything except the true name of the thing. Mother Aldith had chosen her for a reason. The wards had hidden children for a reason. And the reason had a face, and the face was hers, and she had spent twenty years not looking directly at it.

Now a dying god had spent his last coin on one word, and the word was about exits.

Tamsin rose, knees stiff, and stood a moment in the doorway looking out at Hollowmere's lights strung along the mere like a necklace dropped on dark water. Smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys. Somewhere a fiddle, badly played. Children shouting at some game whose rules existed only tonight. This was the whole world, as far as anyone here would admit. This was the prize. And it was sitting inside a wall that was, by every sign she could read, beginning to crack.

* * *

She went to Elder Maren's house before the fiddle stopped.

Maren answered the door himself, a big man gone soft, wool cap in hand, smelling of lamp oil and boiled mutton. Behind him his wife was putting away dishes, and the clatter of it filled the pauses in everything he said, which was how Maren liked it — pauses full of ordinary noise, so that nothing unusual could get a word in.

"Tamsin Vane." He beamed at her. "Back from the road safe. Come in, there's stew."

"Elder." She stayed on the step. She had learned long ago that conversations went worse indoors, where chairs invited sitting and sitting invited patience, and patience was the one weapon she couldn't afford to hand a man like Maren. "I need the council convened. Tomorrow, or the day after."

His beam faltered. "Convened? Whatever for? The caravan's in, the tolls are paid—"

"The ward-stone on the north road. A crown surveyor put a lantern to it two days ago and the lantern burned green."

The dish-clatter behind him stopped.

Maren's face did something complicated and then settled on the expression he wore for difficult weather and difficult women alike: mild, reasonable, faintly disappointed. "Green light. Lantern-glass does strange things near the stones, girl. Everyone knows that. Refraction, reflection — my grandfather swore the east stone glowed blue every Michaelmas, and here we all still stand."

"It wasn't refraction. The flame bent *toward* the stone. Like it was drinking."

"Like it was—" He laughed, short and unamused. "You've been on the road too long. You see omens in ditches. Come inside, eat some stew, sleep it off."

"There's more." She made herself say it plainly, because dressing it soft would only give him more to refuse. "The ember spoke tonight. At the shrine. One word."

The pause after that was longer. His wife had come to stand behind him now, arms folded, and Tamsin saw the look pass between them — the look that said *she's finally cracked*, the look that had probably been waiting years for its moment.

"The ember spoke," Maren repeated carefully. "And what did our silent god have to say for himself?"

"One word. *Door.*"

Silence. Then Maren sighed the sigh of a man burdened with fools, and it was a masterwork, that sigh, worn smooth from decades of use against precisely this kind of trouble. "Tamsin. You're a good soldier and a better guard, and we're grateful for you, truly. But you keep a coal in a bowl. Coals don't talk. And if they did—" he spread his hands, reasonable to the last, "—what would a god know of doors? He's been dead longer than I've been alive. All that's left is habit. Yours and mine both."

"He spent something saying it. I watched him pay for it."

"You watched a fire gutter. They do that, near the end." And then, softer, with real kindness underneath the dismissal, the kindness that was somehow worse: "You're tired, and you're the last one keeping his altar, and I understand that grief makes the quiet things seem loud. But listen to me. Nobody is coming. The crown forgot this valley before your mother was born. The wards have held for sixty years. They will hold sixty more. Go home, Tamsin. Sleep."

Behind him, his wife said gently, "We could send Marta round with broth tomorrow," as though the matter were settled and the only remaining question was soup.

Tamsin looked at the two of them standing in their lit doorway with their clean kitchen and their certainty, and she understood that she was not talking to cowards. That was the terrible part. Maren had fought at the ford in the Bad Year; his wife had walked three children over the mountains out of Mereth with witch-hunters' horns sounding behind them. These were brave people. They had simply decided, decades ago, that safety was a fact rather than a loan, and a man can live inside a decision like that for so long that the walls become invisible, and then someone comes to the door talking about cracks, and all he can hear is a madwoman insulting his house.

"The surveyor wrote things down," Tamsin said. "In a book with the crown's stamp on it. I saw the stamp."

"Surveyors write everything down. It's what they're for." Maren smiled. "Paper isn't a ward-breaker, girl."

"No," Tamsin said. "It's worse. Paper travels."

She left them to their stew. On the walk back through the village she tried the Hedleys, and got the same wall in a different accent. Old Sef, who had come over the mountains as a boy and still flinched at horn-calls, listened longest of anyone — stood in his yard with his hands deep in his pockets and stared at the ground while she talked, and for one moment Tamsin thought she'd found her ally. Then he said, "If it's starting again, I'd rather not know till it's here," and went inside, and shut his door with great gentleness, as if closing it softly might keep the future out too.

By midnight she had counted the whole town and found no one in it. Denial, everywhere, thick and warm as bedding. And she could not even hate them for it, because she understood it from the inside: she had spent two days telling herself the green light was swamp-gas. The difference between her and Maren was only that she had a coal in a bowl that had grabbed her by the skull and said one word, and Maren didn't, and there was no arguing a man out of a quiet life with anything quieter than a god.

* * *

She went back to the shrine in the small hours, because there was nowhere else that felt honest.

The ember had dimmed again. She could see it now, plainly, the way you see a fever turning in a face — not a flicker but a slope, a direction, a countdown wearing the mask of a steady light. She knelt and put her fingers to the bowl and the iron was cooler than it had been at dusk, and she thought: *how many words left in you? How many did that one cost?*

And underneath that, arriving with the slow horrible clarity of a debt being read aloud, the other thought. The real one.

Her faith had a number on it.

All those years of kneeling, of sweeping ash, of keeping the office of keeper as naturally as breathing — she had thought she was tending something permanent. A god, even a small silent one, even a nearly-dead one, was supposed to be a fixed point. You built your discipline on it the way you built a house on rock. Every vow she'd sworn, every mercy she'd dealt on the road, every time she'd stood a watch she didn't have to stand because *Vael watches* — all of it had assumed the watcher would go on watching. Take away the permanence and what remained? A woman kneeling in front of a cooling coal, guarding a religion with maybe a season left in it, in a valley with maybe less.

Unless the word meant otherwise. Unless *Door* wasn't a warning but an instruction. Unless the god, in spending his last strength, hadn't been crying alarm but giving orders — *there is a way out, find it, open it* — and the countdown wasn't the end of her faith but its final commission.

She didn't know which. That was the worst of it. She knelt there in the cold with her hands around an iron bowl, and the ember breathed its small red breath, and she could not tell whether she was keeping a deathwatch or receiving marching orders, and both readings demanded the same next step:

Find out what the Door was. Where. Whether it had ever been real, or whether it was a story the old-timers told their grandchildren to make the drowning sound survivable. And there was exactly one person in the Sundered Valley who might know — one person who had made maps of the drowned country back when maps of it were legal, one person exiled here with his life's work rotting in trunks, one arrogant, disgraced, half-mad royal cartographer who hated the crown, hated the valley, and hated being asked questions most of all.

Odell Ferrance.

Tamsin had guarded his fence-line twice and collected his tariff disputes once, and all three times he had treated her like a talking boot. Breaking into his archive would cost her. He'd set dogs, or wards of the paper-and-ink kind, or simply lawyers of the verbal kind, and either way she'd come out of it poorer in coin or dignity.

She looked at the ember.

It pulsed once, faintly, like a sleeper stirring. Or like a patient counting the beats he had left.

"All right," Tamsin said to it, to the dark, to herself. "Door."

Outside, far off across the mere, a light moved along the north road where no light had any business moving at this hour — a lantern, swinging steady, going from stone to stone. Green at the edges, when it caught.

Tamsin stood in the shrine doorway and watched it pass, and began, silently, to count.
