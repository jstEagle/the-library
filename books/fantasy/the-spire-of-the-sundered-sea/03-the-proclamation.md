## 3. The Proclamation

The herald came on a grey morning, three days after the lantern on the north road, and he came with soldiers.

Tamsin was in the market square when they rode in — six lancers in crown black, and behind them a man in a herald's tabard, gold thread gone brown with damp, carrying a leather scroll-case and an iron-bound chest that two soldiers hauled between them like it was full of stones. The valley had seen crown men before. Tax assessors, mostly, once a decade, blinking at the fog like cats carried somewhere against their will. They always seemed relieved to leave.

These ones didn't blink. These ones had been told what to expect.

The square emptied the way water drains — slowly at first, then all at once. Tamsin stayed where she was, leaning against the well-post with her arms crossed, because running made you interesting and she had spent twenty years being nobody worth a second look. The herald set his case on the mounting block, cracked the wax, and unrolled a proclamation tall as a door. He nailed it to the market post with four strokes of a hammer that rang across the mere like a bell tolling for something.

Then he read it aloud.

"By order of His Majesty's Grace, and under seal of the Office of Inquiry: let it be known throughout the reclaimed territories that all bearers of the gift, howsoever named — witch-blood, ember-touched, god-sworn, or kindled — shall present themselves for registry within thirty days of this posting. Those registered shall be assessed. Those assessed as harmless shall be taxed and tolled as the crown directs." The herald paused. He was young. His voice wanted to shake and he wouldn't let it. "Those who conceal themselves, or who are concealed by others, shall be accounted enemies of the realm."

A woman near the bakery — Marta Ossel, who sold Tamsin bread every seventh day and had never once overcharged her — said, too loudly, "And if we've none of those here?"

The herald looked at her. Then at the proclamation. "Those found to be concealing," he read, "shall burn with them."

Nobody said anything after that. The hammer-strokes had already said it. Tamsin watched the words settle over the square the way fog settles over the mere — not falling so much as arriving, everywhere at once, inescapable. *Enemies of the realm.* She turned the phrase over. It had teeth in places she hadn't expected. Not *enemies of the crown.* Of the realm. Of everything, then. Of the ground and the road and the rain.

The soldiers began unbarring the iron-bound chest. Inside were smaller copies of the proclamation, printed on cheap paper, dozens of them, and a pot of paste, and brushes. They went down the high street in pairs, nailing paper to posts, to barn doors, to the lintel of the shrine where Tamsin had knelt three nights ago and heard a dying god say one word.

They pasted one to the shrine door itself.

Tamsin felt that one somewhere under her ribs.

* * *

By noon the whole valley knew, which meant by noon the whole valley was talking about it in the particular way people talk about a thing they're afraid of — sideways, in lowered voices, in kitchens with the doors shut. Tamsin did her rounds anyway. That was the job. Guard the caravan, guard the granary, guard the peace. Twenty years of guarding a peace that had held because everyone in the Sundered Valley had agreed, without ever saying it aloud, that there was nothing here worth crowning or burning.

She stopped first at the Widow Corren's house, because the Widow Corren was old enough to remember when Mereth was still on maps, and because old women in valleys full of frightened people are the first thing that gets looked at differently.

The widow opened the door before Tamsin knocked. She'd been watching the road.

"They'll come for the list," she said. No greeting. Her hands were folded tight enough to whiten. "That's how it starts. Registry. Names on paper. And then some clerk reads the names, and finds out which ones are the wrong kind of name."

"You're not on any list," Tamsin said. "There aren't lists here. There's never been—"

"There have always been lists, girl. We just kept them in our heads instead of in offices." The widow stepped back from the doorway, and Tamsin saw the kitchen beyond: hearth swept cold, bags packed, a lifetime stacked by the door in three bundles. "I crossed into this valley when I was nine years old. My mother carried me through the ward-stone with soldiers behind us. You think I don't know what a beginning looks like?"

"The wards held for sixty years."

"And now your god is whispering about doors, isn't he." The widow's eyes were sharp and dry. Old women always know more than they admit; it's the tax on surviving. "Word gets around. The shrine-keeper's daughter stands in the market looking like winter came early. Something's cracking, and everyone can feel it, and along comes the crown with its paper and its paste." She lifted one bundle. "I'm going to my cousin's, down past the salt-farms. Quiet place. Nobody's gift but a knack for weather."

"That knack could get her burned now."

"I know," the Widow Corren said. "That's why I'm going. Someone has to be there when it happens to her."

Tamsin walked her to the cart. Halfway down the lane, a neighbor — Hesk, who farmed the east terraces, whose grain Tamsin had guarded for eleven seasons — came out to watch the bundles loaded, and stood there with his arms folded, and did not offer to help, and did not quite meet anyone's eye.

He wasn't a cruel man. That was the thing Tamsin kept coming back to all afternoon. Hesk wasn't cruel. He was afraid, and fear in a small community doesn't make people wicked; it makes them careful, and carefulness curdles fast. By evening she'd seen it four more times. A pause before a greeting. A glance held a beat too long on someone's hands. Old Pell, who mended nets, finding his mooring line cut and no one owning to it, and everyone knowing exactly what the cutting meant: *we know what you might be, and we'd rather you floated loose.*

The valley had lasted sixty years on a shared fiction: *there is no magic here, there never was, we are only farmers.* The proclamation hadn't broken the fiction. It had just priced it. And prices make neighbors into accountants.

* * *

The elders convened at dusk in the meetinghouse, and Tamsin went because they summoned her, and she went armed because she'd read the same proclamation they had.

Twelve elders. Grain merchants, millers, the ferry-master, old Sorrel who kept the ledgers. They sat around the long table with the proclamation spread among them like a corpse at a wake, and they argued for two hours about whether arguing would help.

"We send a delegation," said Sorrel. "To whoever holds authority here. Point out that the valley has paid its tolls. That it has been neutral—"

"It's not neutral anymore," said the ferry-master. "It was never neutral. It was *hidden*. There's a difference, and the difference just nailed itself to our market post."

"Then we comply," said Marta Ossel, who had spoken up in the square and regretted it since. "Registry. Assessment. Most of us are plain folk with plain blood. Let them count us and go away."

"And the ones who aren't plain?" Tamsin said.

Silence. The good kind of silence, the kind with shame in it.

Marta didn't look at her. "We don't know that there are any."

"You do know," Tamsin said quietly. "You've always known. You've known about the Widow Corren. About old Pell. About half the families on the salt-farms. You knew, and it was fine to know, because knowing cost nothing. Now it costs something. So the question in this room tonight isn't whether there are gifted people in this valley." She let it sit. "The question is whether you're going to hand them over."

An elder named Brack — wool, land, a voice built for complaining about taxes — slammed his palm on the table. "Don't you dare frame it that way. Don't you *dare*. We fed these people. We sheltered them. Sixty years! And what has it bought us? A herald in our square and soldiers on our roads and a promise that we burn alongside them. What would you have us do? Die for strangers?"

"They're not strangers."

"They are to me!" And there it was, finally, the truth underneath, ugly and honest. "My grandfather came here to get *away* from the gifted. From the wars their kind brought down on Mereth. This valley was supposed to be done with all of it. Done with gods and gifts and burning. And now the price of other people's secrets is going to be paid out of my family's skin, and I am telling you plainly: I won't pay it."

"Brack—" Sorrel started.

"No. Let her answer. Vane. You're sworn to some silent god of your own, aren't you? Everyone knows it. The way you stand at the shrine. The way you don't swear oaths like normal folk." He leaned forward. "Are you registered? Are you on the crown's list? Because I'd hate for this valley to be *concealing* anything."

The room went very still. Tamsin looked at him for a long moment, and then around at twelve faces, and understood that the valley had died sometime in the last three days, quietly, the way a person dies — still warm, still recognizable, but past calling back.

"I'm a mercenary," she said. "I take contracts. I keep them. Ask anyone I've guarded whether I've ever brought harm through these gates." She pushed back her chair. "But if you're asking whether I'd hand a child to witch-hunters to save my own roof — no. I wouldn't. And now you know, so write it on your list."

She left. Nobody stopped her. Behind her, faint through the meetinghouse door, she heard Sorrel saying, carefully, reasonably, the voice of a man building a gallows out of good intentions: "Perhaps... a registry of our own. Just names, locally kept. For our protection. If we hold the list ourselves, we control—"

Tamsin walked out into the dark and did not listen to the rest.

* * *

The shrine was pasted over, but paper tears, and Tamsin tore it. Underneath, the old wood of the door, and beneath that, in the cold dark inside, the offering bowl and the guttering thing that lived in it.

She knelt. She didn't light a candle. The ember didn't need light; it gave off less than a coal's worth, a dull red pulse in the ash, and lately it had been pulsing faster, or she imagined it was, the way you imagine a sick friend breathing harder.

"Door," she said. "Last week you give me one word. Tonight I could use a few more."

The ember flickered. Long seconds. Then, faintly, not sound so much as pressure behind the eyes, in the old vow-tongue that lived in her bones:

*Count.*

"I'm counting."

*Faster.*

Outside, across the mere, the north road lay empty. No green-edged lanterns. But far to the south, where the valley narrowed toward the ward-stone and the world beyond, she could see — or thought she could see, or feared she could see — a line of fires. Campfires. Too many for travelers. Too orderly for smugglers.

Soldiers don't camp outside a valley for three days unless they're waiting for something.

Tamsin banked the ember, shouldered her pack, and went to find the mapmaker, because whatever the elders decided about lists, and whatever the crown decided about burning, the countdown had stopped being abstract — and Odell Ferrance owed her an answer about a Door.
