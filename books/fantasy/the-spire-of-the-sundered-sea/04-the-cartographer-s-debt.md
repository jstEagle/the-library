## 4. The Cartographer's Debt

The archive of Odell Ferrance sat at the dead end of Lantern Row, behind a door with three locks and a man who hadn't spoken to anyone in the valley for eleven years. I knew this because I'd been paid to know things. It was most of what mercenary work was: knowing which doors opened, which doors pretended to open, and which doors you went through anyway.

I went through his window instead. The latch was salt-corroded and gave with a whisper.

Inside smelled of ink, tallow, and old paper gone soft as cloth. Shelves climbed every wall, crammed with rolled charts and bound folios, and in the middle of the room a drafting table stood under a hanging lamp, its brass shade polished bright—the only clean thing in the house. Maps covered the table's surface, weighted down with river stones. Maps of the valley. Maps of the valley from angles no surveyor could have taken them.

I had been in a great many houses I wasn't invited into. This was the first one that felt like standing inside someone's chest.

"Third shelf," said a voice from the dark by the stove. "Behind the atlas of the Nine Provinces. There's a knife."

I didn't take the knife. I turned slowly, hand on my own blade, and found him: a narrow man in a nightshirt, gray-stubbled, holding a poker like it was a sword and he'd once known the difference. His eyes went to my face, then to the lantern at my hip—Vael's lantern, the ember guttering behind smoked glass—and something moved through his expression too fast to name.

"Tamsin Vane," he said.

"You know me."

"I knew your mother." He set the poker down with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he wants very badly to throw it. "Everyone in this valley knew your mother. What do you want, mercenary? If you've come to rob me, be quick about it. If you've come to kill me, I'd ask that you wait until morning. I'm mid-chart and the tide-lines won't survive an interruption."

"The Door," I said. "Out of Mereth. Where is it?"

The lamplight caught his face and I watched eleven years of exile rearrange themselves into nothing at all. He was good at that. You learned to read men like maps, in my trade, and Odell Ferrance was a map with the coast torn off.

"There is no Door," he said.

"Then there are no charts in this room either." I lifted one from the table—a coastal survey, exquisite, annotated in a cramped hand. "The crown doesn't exile its master cartographers over nothing. It exiles them over things they drew wrong. Or things they drew right."

He sat down heavily at the table, in the chair worn to the shape of him, and looked at the ember-lantern for a long time.

"Twenty years," he said finally, "that light has been dying in that glass. I used to see it pass my window every festival night. Every year, dimmer." He rubbed his eyes. "You're too late, Vane. Whatever you think is opening, it's closing faster."

"Then tell me where it closed."

* * *

He refused me for three hours. I'll give him credit—he refused well, with digressions about tidal mathematics and the crown's habit of erasing coastlines it found inconvenient, all of it a wall built brick by brick while I stood in his archive with my arms crossed and let him build it. He offered me tea twice. I declined twice. Around midnight he stopped performing irritation and just looked tired, and tired men tell the truth more often than angry ones, so I pressed.

"You drew the valley," I said. "Before the wards. Before it was cut out of every map in the kingdom. You were there."

"I was a servant of the Office of Surveys," he said. "I drew what I was told to draw and unmade what I was told to unmake. That is not the same as being anywhere."

"But you know where the Door is."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a dog barked itself out and the valley's enormous quiet folded back over the street.

"If I knew," he said quietly, "and if I told you, do you understand what happens? The wards are not walls. They are a forgetting. A country-sized act of mercy performed by people who are mostly dead now. You don't find the seam in a forgetting, Vane. You don't pick that lock. You live inside it, and you grow old inside it, and you thank whatever god you still talk to that nobody remembers you exist." He spread his hands, ink-stained, trembling slightly. "That is the debt I owe these people. I helped make their prison. I will not help break it."

"It's not a prison to them. It's the only home they have left."

"Yes." He almost smiled. "That's what makes it unforgivable."

I should have left then. I had made my demand, been refused, and there was nothing in breaking an old man's bones that would get me coordinates. But the ember chose that moment to shift in its glass—not a flicker, not a word, just a lean, the way a dog leans against a leg—and Odell Ferrance saw it and went white.

"That's not possible," he whispered. "It was ordered silenced. I watched the order signed."

"What order? Signed by whom?"

And that was when the sky over the eastern quarter turned orange.

Not lantern-orange. Not forge-orange. Fire-orange, the kind that climbs, the kind that eats roofs. We both went to the window, and down the long crooked line of Lantern Row we saw it: the eastern shrine, the old one, the one where the valley's first refugees had scratched their names into the doorpost because they had nowhere else to put them—burning.

And below it, in the square, horses. Black armor that drank the firelight. The crossed staves of the witch-hunters' livery, here, inside the wards, inside twenty years of safety, as though the valley's invisibility had been a coat and someone had finally come to collect it.

"They shouldn't be able to find us," Odell breathed. "No chart shows this valley. No road leads—"

"No chart shows it because you erased it." My voice came out flat and cold, and I didn't recognize it as mine. "You didn't hide us, cartographer. You blinded everyone else and called it shelter. And now the crown has remembered how to look."

The shrine's bell tower collapsed inward with a sound like a held breath released. Sparks climbed. I thought of the elders' faces two nights past, refusing to believe the ward-stone had flickered. Refusing, refusing, refusing, right up until refusal stopped being available.

Odell was already moving—pulling a folio from the third shelf, behind the atlas of the Nine Provinces, shoving loose charts into a leather case with hands that shook but did not hesitate. Twenty years of careful exile, undone in ninety seconds of arson.

"You said you wouldn't help break it," I said.

"I said I wouldn't help break it." He cinched the case shut. "They're breaking it themselves. There's no virtue in standing in a burning house out of principle." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and furious. "The Door is under the Spire. The Spire of the Sundered Sea, out past the Drowned Mile, in the caldera that isn't on any map because I burned that map myself, twenty years ago, with my own hands, on the Inquisitor Corvan's direct order." He swallowed. "I kept the coordinates. God forgive me, I kept everything. That was always my sin—I can't unmake anything. I can only redraw it."

Outside, boots on cobbles. More than one pair. Moving toward Lantern Row.

"How far?" I asked.

"Nine days' walk, if the flooded roads allow it. They won't." He snuffed the lamp. "They never do."

We went out his back window into the alley, the ember-lantern swinging at my hip, throwing our shadows tall and wavering against the wall—two figures fleeing a country that had already forgotten them, toward a door neither of us believed in yet, chased by fire that had finally learned our address.

Behind us the eastern shrine burned, and I made myself a vow with no god left to hear it:

I would reach the Spire. I would spend whatever it cost.

Starting, if it came to it, with the truth.
