## 5. What the Ward-Stone Kept

Odell Ferrance had spent thirty years making maps, and the last nine of them lying.

Not the ordinary lies of a cartographer—the small mercies of rounding a riverbend, of leaving blank space where blank space was honest. He had made those lies too, in his service to the crown, back when his ink was royal purple and his seal opened doors from the capital to the coast. Those lies were craft. What he practiced now, here in the Sundered Valley, was something closer to sin.

He lied with silence. He lied by omission so complete it had the shape of architecture. Every morning he walked past the ward-stone at the edge of Hollowmere with his surveyor's satchel slung over one shoulder, and every morning he looked at it—really looked at it, the way only a man trained to read stone could—and said nothing to anyone about what he saw there.

Because he was the only one in the valley who could read it.

* * *

The archive was his house, though "archive" flattered it. A squat stone cottage at the end of Tanner's Row, its windows fogged with salt rot, its single room crowded floor to rafters with map chests, folio cases, and the long cedar tubes that had once carried his work to the king's own table. Now they carried nothing anywhere. He was exiled. The edict that stripped his title also stripped his right to leave, and the valley's wards—which he understood better than anyone alive, which was precisely why he'd been sent here—kept him as surely as they kept everyone else.

He told himself it was penance. He told himself a great many things, in the years after the burning of the Cartographers' Hall, when his name went from honorific to curse in the space of a single proclamation. *The mapmaker who sold the coast.* That was what they called him in the capital, before they stopped calling him anything at all and simply erased him, struck his name from the rolls with the same bureaucratic tidiness with which Mereth itself had been struck from every chart.

That was the secret under all his other secrets: Odell Ferrance knew Mereth existed.

The world outside believed the Sundered Valley was all there was—a backwater province, poor and magic-dead, tucked into hills nobody wanted. The world outside did not know that beyond those hills lay a drowned kingdom, cities under green water, roads under silt, a whole country erased not by catastrophe but by decree. The wards hadn't been raised to hide the valley from Mereth.

They'd been raised to hide Mereth from the valley.

And Odell had helped draw the lines.

He had been young then, twenty-six, brilliant, ambitious, the youngest master cartographer the Hall had ever certified. He remembered the commission arriving: sealed orders, eyes-only, a survey of the eastern provinces with instructions so strange he'd assumed they were a test. *Map the boundaries. Do not map what lies beyond them.* His superior, a gray-faced man named Aldous Wren, had watched him work for three days straight without explaining why, and on the fourth day had taken him into a locked room beneath the Hall and shown him the thing that ended his old life.

A child. Six years old, maybe seven. Sitting cross-legged on a stone floor in a circle of chalk, surrounded by men in gray robes, and around her—Odell could still see it, would always see it—the air bending. Light curving like water around a stone. She wasn't doing anything. She was just *there*, and the room couldn't decide how to hold her.

"What is she?" he'd asked, and Wren had answered with the flatness of a man reciting doctrine: "She's a problem."

The gift, Wren explained—the old blood, the thing that had built Mereth's spires and sung its bridges into being—was dying out everywhere else in the world. Burned out by the witch-hunts, bred thin by fear, hunted into hiding. But not gone. Never quite gone. It surfaced still, in children, in the strangest places, and every gifted child born inside the crown's reach was a lit fuse waiting for a mob or an Inquisitor or, worse, a war.

So the crown had done what crowns do. It had gathered them.

"There's a place," Wren said. "East of the mountains. Poor land, forgotten land. We're going to raise wards around it—not walls, wards. Old craft, bought at ruinous cost. Inside those wards, the gift cannot wake. Cannot kindle. A child who would burn a village down at twelve will live her whole life never knowing she carries fire." He'd looked at Odell then, and his voice had dropped, and for one unguarded moment Odell had seen that Wren was not a monster but a tired man performing monstrous arithmetic. "They'll never know what they are. They'll grow up thinking they're ordinary. Is that cruelty, Ferrance? Or is it the only mercy left?"

He hadn't answered. He'd drawn the map instead. The boundary line, the ward-perimeter, the placement of each anchor stone—he'd placed eleven of them himself, chosen the granite, calculated the sightlines, marked where the resonance would pool and where it would deaden. His finest work. His masterpiece. The most precise thing he ever made, and the thing he could never sign.

They called the place the Sundered Valley because the Sundering had broken it, and that was even true, in its way. But the name did double duty, as names commissioned by ministries always do. *Sundered.* Cut off. Divided from itself. The valley's people thought the wards kept danger out.

The wards kept everything in.

* * *

Nine years of exile had taught Odell exactly one method of survival: become boring. Be the odd old man with the maps nobody cared about. Drink modestly, complain about his joints, let the villagers file him under *harmless*. When Tamsin Vane broke down his door on a wet night in late autumn with a lantern in her hand and murder in her posture, part of him felt almost relieved. Nine years of pretending not to know things. Someone had finally come to collect.

She stood in his doorway like a drawn blade. He knew her by reputation—everyone did; the mercenary sworn to the silent god, the woman who'd held the ford at Marrow Creek alone against six bandits—but knowing a reputation and standing in front of it were different experiences entirely.

"You're going to tell me about the Door," she said.

"Am I."

"The shrine burned last night. Eastern ridge. Witch-hunters' work, and don't tell me you haven't heard—they came through the ward-line like it was wet paper, which means the wards are failing, which means somebody in this valley knows more about the wards than any farmer does." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about her; everything stayed level, pressed flat, like water finding its weight. "You were crown. I've seen your seal on the old boundary markers. So you're going to tell me where the Door is, or I'm going to take this house apart board by board until I find it myself."

He should have lied. He'd had nine years of practice. Instead he heard himself say, "There is no Door," and heard, in his own voice, the sound of a man whose lie had finally gotten too heavy to lift.

Her eyes narrowed. "No Door."

"No Door *out*," he corrected, because apparently his mouth had decided honesty was happening tonight whether he liked it or not. "There's a gate in the western ward-line. The founders called it the Door. It doesn't lead out of the valley, Vane. It leads out of the *lie*. There's a difference, and the difference has killed better people than either of us."

Tamsin set the lantern down on his desk, deliberately, the way one sets down a weapon one intends to pick back up. "Then start with the lie."

And Odell Ferrance, who had rehearsed this confession in the dark for nine years without once believing he'd deliver it, pulled the first chest from under his desk and unlocked it.

* * *

Inside lay the master map. Not a copy—the original, drawn in his own hand, the one document in the world that showed Mereth entire: the drowned capital of Cairnlow with its sunken causeways, the flooded Mile where the sea had come in over the old road, the caldera far to the south where the Spire stood half-drowned, and ringing it all, in red ink gone brown with age, the ward-perimeter. Eleven anchor stones. One gate.

Tamsin stared at it for a long time. Long enough that the candle guttered twice.

"That's not the valley," she said finally. "This is—" She put her finger on the perimeter line and traced it, and Odell watched her face as her finger traveled the boundary of her entire life and found it smaller than she'd ever imagined. "I've walked this line. I've *patrolled* this line. Twenty years. This is the valley."

"This is the cage," he said. "The valley is what's outside it."

"Outside it is water."

"Outside it is *Mereth*."

He watched her take that in the way one takes a blow—not all at once, in stages, the knees first, then the hands gripping the table's edge. He had planned to be gentler. He had imagined, in his cowardly rehearsals, easing her toward it, letting her discover the drowned cities on her own terms. But there was no gentle way to tell a woman that her homeland was a kingdom at the bottom of the sea, and that she had spent her whole life guarding the walls of her own prison.

"The Sundering," he began, and stopped, because she was already shaking her head.

"I know the story. Everyone knows the story. The sea rose, the old kingdom drowned, the survivors fled inland and raised wards against the flood-magic, and we've lived here ever since, safe, plain, god-fearing." She said it in the flat cadence of a catechism, and then she said, quieter, "It's not true, is it."

"It's true the way a portrait is true. Right nose, wrong soul." He turned the map so the light caught the annotations along the western edge—his own handwriting, younger, tighter. "The Sundering happened, yes. The sea took the lowlands, the Spire's hearth cracked, the old gods went silent or fled. But the drowning took decades, Vane, not a night. People evacuated. They fought over the high ground. And while they fought, the crown was doing something else entirely, something that never made it into any chronicle."

He tapped the red line.

"They were collecting the children."

* * *

He told her all of it, because once the dam broke there was no stopping it halfway. The surveys. The lists. The midwives paid to report births that came "strange"—children born with the old blood showing, eyes that caught light wrong, cries that made dogs uneasy, infants who healed overnight. In the last years before the wards closed, the crown's riders had swept the eastern provinces with quiet efficiency, gathering every gifted child that could be found and carrying them east, past the mountains, into the poor forgotten land behind the new ward-line.

Eleven hundred children, by the final count. Maybe more. The records were sealed, then burned, then burned again during the purges, until the only surviving tally was the one Odell had memorized and the one carved, in cipher, into the base of the western ward-stone—because the warders who raised the stones had insisted someone, somewhere, keep a list of the names. Even prisons, it turns out, keep records of their prisoners.

"You're one of them," he said. "Your name is on the stone. Tamsin Vane, entered the valley at age four, recorded as 'gift-suspect, class indeterminate.' Your parents weren't refugees, Vane. They were *delivered*. There's a difference, and the difference is a receipt."

She didn't speak. She had gone very still, the way she must have learned to go still in twenty years of soldiery, the stillness that keeps a body from bleeding out. He could see her heartbeat in her throat.

"The wards," she managed at last. "If the wards keep the gift from waking—"

"Then everyone in this valley who was brought here as a child has lived their whole life amputated and never felt the wound." He hated saying it. He said it anyway, because she deserved the whole ugly truth and not a curated portion. "You asked me once—I remember, three winters ago, at the harvest market—you asked me if I ever missed magic. You laughed when you said it. You said a man like me wouldn't know what to do with it. Vane, you have more of the old blood in you than anyone I've catalogued in forty years of looking. If the wards fell tomorrow, you wouldn't miss magic. You'd *be* it. You'd be the brightest thing in this valley by an order of magnitude, and you have spent twenty years as a hired sword because the alternative was never offered to you."

The lantern light moved across her face and he watched the twenty years rearrange themselves behind her eyes. He knew that look. He'd worn it himself, the day Wren locked the door behind him and he understood that his brilliance had been used as a tool of captivity—that every precise line he'd drawn was a bar. The moment when your own competence becomes evidence against you.

"My vows," she said. Her voice had gone hoarse. "Vael. The silent god. I swore—"

"Was founded by the prisoners." He said it gently now, as gently as he knew how, which was not very. "Or by their children. Nobody remembers exactly—the shrine records are older than the second generation. A god of silence, in a valley where the truth could not be spoken. Think about what that means, Vane. Think about what a faith looks like when it grows up inside a locked room. Silence wasn't Vael's nature. Silence was the *condition*. The god your people made was shaped exactly like the wall around them."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "The ember. The ember is real. I've carried it. It spoke to me."

"I believe you." And he did; that was the terrible part, the part that had kept him awake since the shrine burned. "An ember can be real and still be a prisoner. Everything in this valley is real, Vane. That's what makes it a prison instead of a dream. Real grain, real graves, real marriages, real gods. A whole life, fully furnished, with one door nailed shut."

* * *

They sat across from each other until the candle burned down to a stub, and somewhere in the middle hours the conversation changed key—from confession to planning, from what had been done to what might yet be done. Odell found, to his surprise, that he was no longer dreading this. The relief of telling was real, but underneath it was something older and hungrier: thirty years a cartographer, and the one map that mattered, the one correction the world needed, still undelivered.

"The wards are failing," he said. "You're right about that, and the witch-hunters walking through them proves it. Anchor stones don't fail all at once—they fail in sequence, weakest first, and the weakest is always the one nearest running water. The eastern ridge shrine sat above the underground channel from the old aquifer. That's why it burned first. The others will follow, north to south, over months if we're lucky, weeks if we're not. When enough of them go, two things happen at once. The gift wakes in every carrier in this valley—suddenly, all together, untrained, terrified. And the crown's hunters, who have never stopped watching this line, walk in and start registering names."

"And the Door."

"The western gate. The one gap in the line, built into the original design—Wren's order, though he never admitted it in writing. A pressure valve. A way *in* for supplies and warders, and, in the founding documents' own language, 'a contingency for egress.'" He almost laughed at the bureaucratic euphemism. Egress. Eleven hundred children, and the men who caged them wrote *egress*. "It opens onto the Drowned Mile. From there, south along the old causeway to the Spire. Beyond the Spire—" he traced it with one finger, the route he had redrawn a thousand times in his head, in exile, in punishment, in hope, "—beyond the Spire is the living world. Whatever's left of it."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "The elders won't believe any of this."

"No."

"They'll call it sedition. They'll call *me* sedition. I've guarded these people my whole life, Ferrance. Tomorrow I stand in the square and tell them their whole history is a warden's bedtime story." She rubbed her scarred knuckles, a habit he'd seen a hundred times and never once wondered about until tonight. "Why tell me? Why now? You've had nine years. You could have died with it."

He looked at the map between them—at the red line, at the little gate-mark, at the drowned kingdom he had erased and erased and erased until erasing it was the only skill he had left.

"Because the shrine burned," he said. "And because a man can only keep one country in his head for so long before he has to put it back on paper." He closed the chest. "Take the eastern road tomorrow. Talk to whoever's left at the ridge shrine. And then come back, and we'll plan the route properly—because if the wards are falling, Vane, then everything I just told you stops being history."

He paused at the door as she lifted the lantern, and added the thing he should have led with, the thing that had been sitting in his chest all night like a swallowed coal:

"Everything I just told you becomes a countdown."

* * *

She left before dawn. Odell stood in his doorway and watched her go down Tanner's Row, lantern swinging, shadow stretching and folding over the wet cobbles, and he thought about the ward-stone at the valley's edge—the one he passed every morning, the one with the cipher list carved into its base, the one holding up the sky of a lie.

Tonight, for the first time in nine years, he would sleep without locking the map chest.

He only wished he believed that was mercy.
