## 6. The Girl Who Ran From the Fire

The eastern shrine was still smoking when Tamsin reached it.

She had smelled it from a mile out—that wrongness of burning that wasn't wood or grain but something older, something with resin and stone-dust in it, the way a shrine burns when the fire has been fed by hands that know what they're doing. Witch-fire, the old-timers called it. It didn't spread so much as *choose*.

The roof had gone in. The offering bowl lay cracked on the steps, and the ash inside it had been scattered by boot-prints—three sets, maybe four, deliberate, ground into the soot like men stamping out a life. On the doorpost someone had nailed a strip of parchment with the crown's seal dripping wax down over Vael's sign, the closed eye, defacing it with a scrawl: *REGISTERED AND ACCOUNTED*.

Tamsin stood in the ruins of the place where she had kept her silence for twenty years and felt nothing she could name. Not rage. Rage was simple. This was more like standing on ice and hearing the first crack travel under your boots.

The ember was in her lantern at her hip. She'd carried it since the night it spoke—the word *Door*, single and terrible, spoken into her bones through three inches of cold iron. She had told no one but Odell Ferrance, and telling him had been its own kind of surrender.

She crouched at the edge of the ash and read the ground the way Odell read maps.

Four riders, shod heavy, crown-pattern. They'd come from the north road, burned fast and left faster—but one set of prints doubled back toward the tree line before departing, lighter, unhurried. Someone had walked to the trees and stood there a while. Watching the shrine burn, or watching for followers.

And there—pressed into the mud at the base of the steps, half-filled with rain from the night—a footprint too small for any of them. A child's, or near enough. Barefoot. Pointed east, toward the valley's edge.

Tamsin straightened. The lantern at her hip gave a flicker she would have sworn she felt rather than saw.

"You see it too," she murmured.

The ember said nothing. It hadn't spoken since the word. But lately it did things—leaned, if a flame could lean—and Tamsin had stopped pretending she didn't notice.

East. Toward the ward-line. Toward the crack in the sky of the lie, where a surveyor's lantern had flickered green two weeks ago and everything since had been falling.

She went east at a walk, then, when the light thickened behind her and the smoke fell away, at a run.

* * *

She found the girl at dusk, in the drowned orchard below the old toll road, and found her because the girl was trying very hard not to be found and was bad at it.

Tamsin heard the breathing first—quick, shallow, controlled the way soldiers breathe when they're hiding, which meant whoever it was had been taught by someone who hid professionally. Then a sound smaller than breathing: a child whimpering, and a hand clamped gently over mouth to stop it.

Tamsin moved off the path, circled wide through the apple trees gone wild and half-sunk in marsh water, and came up behind a tumbled drystone wall where the shadows pooled deepest.

"Come out slow," she said. "Or don't, and I'll come to you."

Silence. Then, low, furious, pitched to carry exactly as far as Tamsin and no farther: "If you're one of his, I'll scream, and there are people in these hills who'll come running."

"People in these hills," Tamsin said, "are mostly dead, fled, or hiding under their own floors. Try again."

A long pause. Then a figure rose over the wall—one hand raised empty, the other holding a knife held wrong, thumb along the flat, a grip for showing a blade, not using one. A girl of perhaps seventeen, rain-dark hair cut short and badly, wearing a rider's oilskin two sizes too big with the Inquisitor's grey chevrons scraped off the shoulders, leaving pale ghosts of them in the waxed cloth. Behind her, pressed into the hollow of the wall, a boy of six or seven with eyes that were doing something eyes shouldn't do—catching the last grey light and giving it back gold.

Gifted. Freshly, barely, dangerously gifted, the way children were right before the gift either rooted or died, and everyone in the valley knew what the crown did with freshly rooted ones.

"And you?" Tamsin asked. "Are you one of *his*?"

The girl's jaw tightened. "No."

"You're wearing his colors."

"I'm wearing what was in the stable." The knife didn't waver, which took effort; Tamsin could see the effort. "I'm Sefa."

"Sefa what?"

"Just Sefa." A beat. "Sefa Corvan."

The name went through the wet air between them like a coin dropped down a well. Tamsin heard it land. She kept her face still, because twenty years of guarding caravans teaches you that the moment matters most when you least want it to.

Corvan. The Inquisitor. The man whose riders had burned the shrine an hour after dawn. The man whose edict hung nailed to every post from Tanner's Row to the mill, promising registration or fire. The man whose hunt-list Odell had described to her in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant—names, parishes, ages, dispositions. *He keeps a list,* Odell had said. *It's famous among the frightened. He crosses names off personally.*

"You ran," Tamsin said. "From him."

"I ran *with* this." The girl tilted her head at the boy without looking away from Tamsin. "His mother died in the Kettlebridge burning. I pulled him out of the smoke before my father's riders finished the street. We've been walking four days. I was taking him to the valley."

"Why?"

"Because the valley takes people." For the first time, the girl's voice cracked—not with fear but with something worse, the sound of an argument losing. "That's what everyone says. That's what it's *for*. The place the maps forget. You go in and the gift goes quiet and nobody burns." Her eyes narrowed. "You're from there. I can tell. You've got the look—you all have the look, like you're listening for something."

Tamsin thought about the shrine, and the ward-stone, and the surveyor's green lantern, and the word *Door* sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal of her own.

"The valley isn't safe anymore," she said. "The wards are failing. His riders can come in now."

"I know." Flat. "I've known for a month. That's why I'm running ahead of them instead of riding with them."

Behind her, the boy made a small sound. Gold flared in his eyes and guttered. Sefa turned and put both hands on his shoulders, and whatever she whispered to him, it worked; the light sank back down and he buried his face in her oilskin.

Tamsin watched her do it and felt something shift in her chest that she distrusted immediately and completely.

Twenty years of vows. Twenty years of keeping her face smooth while the valley decided, season by season, which neighbors were neighbors. She knew what it cost to be looked at differently by people you'd bled for. And here was this girl—this *child of the hunt*, raised in the shadow of the man whose list had her village's name on it—kneeling in marsh mud at dusk with a stolen boy and a knife she couldn't use, having chosen, alone, at some hour Tamsin would never know, to stop being her father's daughter.

"Can you fight?" Tamsin asked.

"No."

"Good. Honest. Can you run?"

"Obviously."

"Then get up. Both of you. You're coming with me, and if you're lying about anything—if there are riders waiting where you're leading—I'll leave you in the marsh for the crows, and I want you to believe that, because it'll make both our lives simpler."

Sefa stood. She slung the boy onto her hip with a practiced motion that told Tamsin more than any oath could, and looked at her with red-rimmed, level eyes.

"There's one thing you should know first," she said. "Since we're being honest about lies."

* * *

They walked the last mile in the dark, off the roads, through the reeds. The boy—his name was Pell, he informed Tamsin solemnly, twice—rode on Sefa's back with his arms around her neck, and every few hundred yards his eyes would flare faint gold and Sefa would murmur something and it would die down. Tamsin walked ahead with the lantern hooded, feeling the ember inside it pull toward the boy like a tide toward the moon.

Twice she stopped, hood up, hand on her sword, and waited until whatever had made the sound—a heron, a deer, wind in the drowned willows—resolved itself into harmlessness.

At the third stop, Sefa said quietly, behind her: "You keep checking the treeline on the left."

"Habit."

"It's not a habit. It's the same treeline every time." A pause. "There's nothing there. I'd know. I grew up learning how to hide people in trees."

Tamsin didn't answer. But when they moved on, she shifted her route fifty yards south, just to prove the girl wrong about something.

They reached the waystation—an old toll-house on the ghost of the Mereth road, dry-floored, one door, one window, walls thick enough to swallow sound. Tamsin barred the door, lit no lamp, and sat them all in the dark with hard bread and river water. Pell ate like a small animal and slept mid-chew against Sefa's shoulder within minutes.

In the dark, Sefa said: "You wanted honesty."

"I wanted it an hour ago."

"You're getting it now." Tamsin heard cloth move—the girl shifting the sleeping boy's weight, settling. "I took something from my father's tent. The night I ran. I thought—" A breath. "I thought if I knew who he was hunting next, I could warn them first. Get ahead of the list."

"And?"

"And I did. For two towns. And then I got to Kettlebridge a day late, so instead I pulled Pell out of the ashes." Her voice stayed level the whole time, which was its own kind of horrifying. "I still have the list. I couldn't burn it. I told myself it was because it might save someone else. That was half true. The other half is that I'm not ready to burn my father's handwriting. Is that honest enough for you?"

"Show me."

Cloth again. Then a folded paper passed across the dark, and Tamsin unhooded the lantern just enough—a knife-slit of amber light—and opened it.

She had expected a ledger page. What she held was worse: a single sheet, dense, in a small severe hand, names in columns with parish and age beside each, and beside each name a notation in a code she almost recognized—Odell would recognize it, she thought, the cartographer's shorthand for elevation and bearing, repurposed for human beings. *Where to find them. How many to expect. Whether they'll run.*

She read down the column.

Mereth Valley entries. Old-timers' names, names from her caravan contracts, names from the shrine rolls. And at the top of the first column, inked fresh, the notation still sharp:

**VANE, TAMSIN — keeper. Priority. Do not register. Take alive.**

Below it, in the same hand, a line she read three times because the first two times it refused to mean anything:

*She carries it. Confirm before the Spire.*

The Spire. A word she'd never heard spoken aloud, sitting on her father-hunter's list like a destination already decided.

The lantern flickered. Inside the hood, the ember leaned—toward the paper, unmistakably, the way it had leaned toward the boy's gold eyes. Toward the word *Spire*. Tamsin snapped the hood shut and the darkness came back like a lid closing.

"Well?" Sefa said.

"You knew my name was on this."

"I hoped it wasn't. I checked the top of the list every night hoping it wouldn't be yours." The girl's voice didn't waver. "You're the mercenary who guards the grain road. Everyone between here and the ford knows the silent woman with the lantern. When I saw you come through the orchard I nearly ran anyway. I thought—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I thought if the list was right about you, you'd already be one of his. Taken alive. Turned."

"And if I were?"

"Then I'd have screamed for the hills, like I said." A pause, and then, with the blunt courage of someone too tired to dress anything up: "But you're not turned. Turned hunters don't check treelines like they're protecting something. They check them like they're hunting."

Tamsin folded the list along its worn creases and held it a moment longer than she needed to. *Take alive.* Not kill. Corvan wanted her breathing, and wanted whatever she carried confirmed before a place called the Spire—which meant the Inquisitor knew about the ember, knew what it was worth, and had written her name at the top of a page of doomed strangers because she was not, in his arithmetic, a stranger at all.

She was the prize the whole hunt bent around. She had been for years, probably, walking her caravans under a sky that was only ever borrowed.

"Pell," Tamsin said. "Whose is he now?"

"Mine," Sefa said instantly. "If you'll have it that way. If the valley won't take us, then wherever you're going—I heard the mapmaker's house burned his ledgers, I heard you were seen going in and coming out with a lantern that isn't yours—wherever that is, we'll keep up. We're good at keeping up. Four days, and he hasn't cried once at night since the second one." Her hand rested on the sleeping boy's hair. "I know what I am. I know whose daughter. I'm not asking you to forget it. I'm asking you to spend it."

Tamsin sat in the dark and listened to the marsh tick and settle outside, and felt the ember in its lantern doing its slow leaning, and understood that the countdown Odell had named had just acquired a face and a voice and a child asleep against its shoulder.

The enemy's daughter. Running from the fire, carrying the fire's own list, with Tamsin's name burning at the top of it.

"Sleep," Tamsin said finally. "Both watches tonight are mine. At first light we go to Odell Ferrance, and you show him that list, because there's a word on it I need him to read." She paused at the window slit, watching the black line of the drowned hills. "A word I think he already knows."

Behind her, Sefa's breathing slowed toward sleep. Against her shoulder, Pell dreamed, and even in sleep his eyes leaked a thin gold light, like a coal remembering fire.

At the top of the list, in a hunter's careful hand, the ink was already drying toward morning:

*Confirm before the Spire.*
