## 7. Crossing the Drowned Mile

The ward-stone cracked like ice going out in spring.

We heard it from the tree line, half a mile off—Odell had timed the crossing for dusk, when the valley's wards were weakest, and even so none of us expected a sound. Wards were supposed to fail quietly, the way old men die in their sleep. This one failed like a wall coming down.

Tamsin, Sefa said, and her hand found my sleeve. Her father's hunt-list was folded against her heart where she kept it, and I could see by her face that she'd read it again this morning. My name at the top, inked in Corvan's own hand. She touched my sleeve anyway. That was the whole of it: she touched my sleeve.

The green light was still fading off the stone when we moved. Odell went first with his rolled maps strapped across his back like a soldier's pack, which was what they were, though he'd never admit it. Then the two Ferrance apprentices he'd insisted on bringing—Pell and Marrow, fourteen and sixteen, both of them carrying more of the gift than either knew. Then Sefa. Then me, because I'd sworn to walk last, and Vael's ember rode in its lantern at my hip, guttering like a candle in a draft.

Behind us, the valley. Behind us, twenty years of grain contracts and quiet vows and neighbors who had started looking at the old-timers the way you look at a fire you haven't decided to put out yet. The elders had refused us supplies in the end. Old Wenna had slipped me a sack of hardtack through her back fence and told me not to come back. *Don't come back,* she'd said, and meant it kindly, and it had cut worse than any curse.

Ahead of us, the drowned mile.

* * *

You have to understand what the drowned mile is. Every child raised in the Sundered Valley grows up on its edge stories. A league of country where the flood never fully receded after Mereth drowned—a causeway under black water, rooftops showing at low tide like teeth, chapel spires leaning at angles no honest builder would allow. The valley's founders had warded it shut on the far side too, double-sealed, so that nobody inside could look out and nobody outside could look in. For two hundred years it had been the one direction with nothing behind it. Not wilderness. Nothing. The maps Odell had burned—the ones he hadn't burned—showed it as blank parchment with a single notation in faded iron-gall ink: *here the world forgets.*

We crossed at low tide, along the spine of the old causeway, and the water came up to our knees and then our thighs and then, in the deep channel where the road had collapsed, to Pell's shoulders, and we passed the boy hand over hand with his maps held high above his head, and he laughed about it afterward, which is how I knew he was going to be all right.

The water was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. It should have been cold—it was autumn, the wind off the sea had teeth—but it lay over our skin like bathwater left standing, and where it touched the scars on my hands it *tingled*, a sensation I had no word for and did not want to learn. Odell noticed before I said anything. He always notices first; it's the cartographer in him, reading a country through his body the way other men read it through their eyes.

Magic, he said quietly, when we stopped on a rise of broken paving to breathe. It's seeping back. The wards are down on both sides now, and the land remembers what it used to hold.

I looked at my hands. The scars from the marsh skirmish two years past—shrapnel from a crown powder-charge, nothing remarkable—were faintly luminous beneath the water. Not glowing, exactly. More like the way a coal looks an hour after the fire's out, if you know to look.

I put my hands under the surface and made them stop.

* * *

We made the first island by full dark—an old market town called Sel Hallow, if Odell's memory served, though he admitted his memory of Mereth was secondhand, assembled from archives and the testimony of refugees who'd been children when the flood came. The town stood waist-deep in permanent water, its lower floors drowned, its upper windows dark as eye sockets. We climbed in through a granary door thirty feet above the current street and made camp in what had been a merchant's counting room, the ledgers still shelved along one wall, swollen into bricks of pulp.

Sefa got a fire going in a hearth that hadn't seen flame since before her grandmother was born. Pell and Marrow argued about whose turn it was to take first watch and settled it the way children settle everything, with a coin and a grudge. Odell unrolled his working map across the floor and weighted the corners with ledger-bricks, and began, in his small precise hand, to draw the drowned mile as we had just crossed it—the first survey anyone had made in two centuries.

I stood at the window.

Below me, the flooded street. Moonlight on black water, and under the water, shapes. A cart, its wheels intact. A doorway with its lintel carved in the old style, the interlaced knotwork that the valley's carpenters still copied without knowing why. And further out, where the street met what had been the square, a fountain, dry these two hundred years, its basin furred with weed—and standing in the dry fountain, up to its calves in water that wasn't there, a statue of a woman holding a set of scales.

I knew her face.

Not from anywhere. That's what I want to be clear about. I had never seen that statue, never seen any image of it, never been taught the iconography of drowned Mereth because the valley taught nothing of Mereth—the founders had wanted the children to grow up thinking the world began at the ward-stone. But I knew her face the way you know your own reflection in a dark window. The set of the jaw. The way the scales hung slightly uneven, one pan lower, as if she'd been caught mid-judgment and frozen there.

My gift woke properly that night, in that window, looking at that statue.

It didn't feel like waking. It felt like a wound reopening. There was pressure behind my eyes, a heat in my palms that had nothing to do with the fire, and then—this is the part I still can't write cleanly—the water below me began to move. Not with the tide. With *me*. A slow spiral forming around the fountain, around the statue, widening, deepening, as if something in the drowned square had recognized something in me and was answering.

I closed the shutter. The spiral kept turning for another minute—I could feel it through the floor, a pulse in the boards under my feet—and then it stilled, and I sat down against the wall with the lantern in my lap and shook until dawn.

Sefa woke once and found me like that. She didn't ask questions. She sat down beside me, put her shoulder against mine, and stayed until my hands stopped trembling. In the valley we'd had a word for that kind of sitting. We'd called it keeping watch. It was the closest thing to prayer my god had ever allowed me, and she did it without being asked, and I have never told her what it cost me to let her.

* * *

The second day we crossed open water, and that was when the dead began to notice us.

I don't mean ghosts. The valley stories were full of ghosts—lantern-lit processions on the causeway, bells ringing underwater, a woman in white who walked the flooded mile asking travelers for news of her sons. I'd heard every one of those stories my whole life and believed none of them, because I was a mercenary and a skeptic and a servant of a silent god, and silent gods make skeptics of everyone.

What I mean is this: the water remembered people.

At midday, crossing the deep channel where the causeway had fallen, we passed over a district Odell marked on his map as the Weavers' Quarter. The rooftops there stood just proud of the surface, and between them the water ran slow and glassy, and in the glassy places you could look straight down into the streets below—doors, windows, a shrine with its lamp niche empty, a child's ball wedged in a drain grate. Two hundred years down, and the ball still red.

And in those glassy stretches, things rose toward us. Not bodies. Impressions. Shapes in the water like heat-shimmer inverted, like reflections of people who weren't standing there. They gathered under the boat—under *me*, specifically, wherever I leaned over the gunwale—and they didn't speak and they didn't reach, but they *looked*, and the looking felt like standing in a crowd of the starving outside a bakery window.

They're not dead, Odell said. His voice had gone strange. Or rather—they're past dead. What's down there is the shape the city left in the magic when it drowned. Like a footprint in wax. And the magic's waking up, and the footprints are waking with it, and you—

He stopped. He looked at me the way you look at a lit fuse.

You're the loudest thing in the water, Tamsin. Whatever you are, whatever the valley buried in you, this country can hear it. You're not walking through Mereth. You're *announcing yourself* to Mereth.

I thought about the shrine. About the ember guttering in its bowl, giving me one word—*Door*—and nothing else in twenty years. I thought about the ward-stone cracking. I thought about the statue in the fountain, whose face I knew.

Then I thought about Brannoc.

My brother drowned when we were children—not in the drowned mile; in the reservoir above the valley, an accident, a winter thaw, a hole in the ice. I was nine. I carried him out of the water myself and he was already gone, and the valley's healers—who were not healers, only herbalists, because the gift was warded flat in every soul that bore it—could do nothing, and I learned at nine years old what it was to stand in a country that could not save you. I gave his body to the ground and I gave my voice to Vael the same week, and I have sometimes wondered, in the long gray years since, whether those two acts were the same act. Whether I swore myself silent because someone I loved had been silenced forever, and I couldn't bear the asymmetry.

So when the impressions in the water began to gather—not under the boat, but ahead of it, at the prow, in a loose cluster shaped vaguely like a child—I did what any fool would do.

I looked at its face.

* * *

It didn't have a face. That's important. I need you to understand that it didn't have a face, because what happened next has been eating me alive ever since, and I want the record to show that I was not deceived by anything so cheap as a trick of light.

It had a *presence*. A weight in the water, a warmth, a way of holding itself that I recognized the way you recognize a gait from a distance before you can see who's walking. And the longer I looked, the more the water around it organized itself into suggestion—shoulders, a jawline, hair drifting—and the suggestions were wrong in every particular except the ones that mattered. Too tall for a child. Too broad. And yet the *stance* of it, head slightly tipped, watching me watch it—

Brannoc would have been twenty-nine this year. I am thirty-four. When he died he was seven, and I was nine, and I was the one who pulled him out, and I have spent twenty-five years being the older sibling of a seven-year-old, which is a thing nobody tells you about grief: it freezes them. My brother is seven years old forever. He will always be smaller than me. He will always be the one I carried.

The shape in the water was bigger than me.

And I knew, immediately, absolutely, with the cold clarity of a mercenary reading a battlefield, that this was wrong. That whatever gathered at the prow was not my brother, could not be my brother, that the drowned dead of Mereth had no business wearing the face of a boy who had died in the valley's reservoir two hundred years after this city sank. The arithmetic alone forbade it. Grief does not care about arithmetic. Grief looked at that shape and saw my brother grown up—all the birthdays I hadn't watched, all the height he should have reached, the man he would have become—and grief said *yes, that's him, that's exactly him*, and grief was lying, and I knew it was lying, and I looked anyway.

Because that's the thing about the lie. You always know. From the very first moment, you always know. And you look anyway, because the alternative is a world where he stays seven forever, and some part of you would rather be lied to than live in that world one more day.

Sefa saw my face change. She put out a hand—she's learned to read me faster than anyone since Wenna—and said, Tamsin, don't.

I didn't touch it. I want that recorded too. I didn't touch it. I looked, and the looking lasted four seconds, and in those four seconds the shape finished itself—jaw, brow, the small scar over the left eyebrow that Brannoc had earned falling out of Wenna's pear tree, translated upward onto a grown man's face as if grief had hired a sculptor—and then Odell did the sensible thing, the thing I should have done, and threw the boat's lantern-baffle shut, cutting the light, breaking the moment, and the shape in the water came apart like breath off glass.

The water was just water after that. Black, cold now—the warmth had gone out of it the moment the shape dissolved—and full of nothing but rooftops and two hundred years of silt.

Nobody spoke for a mile.

* * *

We camped that night in the belfry of a drowned church, on a platform Odell rigged from pews lashed together, forty feet above the waterline. Below us, through the open bell-frame, the nave lay flooded to the clerestory, and the water was calm, and the calm felt deliberate. Performed. As if the drowned mile were holding its breath to see what we'd do next.

I took first watch. Of course I took first watch. Sefa pretended to sleep beside me with her boots on, which is her way of saying she doesn't trust me alone with myself, and I love her for it, and I have never told her that either.

Around midnight, the lantern spoke.

Not the ember—the ember hasn't spoken since the shrine, since *Door*, since the night my faith became a countdown. The lantern itself. The iron of it, the glass, the little cage of Vael's silence that I've carried for twenty years: it grew warm in my hand, warmer than any flame inside it could account for, and the warmth arranged itself into something almost like a voice, almost like words, and what it said—or what I heard, which may not be the same thing—was:

*He is not your brother. But he is not lying about the Spire.*

I sat with that until my watch ended. I turned it over the way you turn a coin you suspect is clipped. He is not your brother. But he is not lying about the Spire. Which means the shape in the water—the thing that wore my grief like a mask—is real, and has an agenda, and knows where we're going, and wants us to keep going there. Which means somewhere ahead of us, in the deep water or the deep ruins, something is waiting for us to arrive, and it has already invested in the journey. It showed me my brother grown. That was an investment. Lies that elaborate are never free.

I woke Odell at grey light and told him. All of it. The statue, the spiral, the shape at the prow, the lantern's warning. He listened the way he listens—completely, silently, his pen moving the whole time, taking notes on my confession as if my confession were coastline—and when I finished he said one thing.

Then we're being guided. Good. Guided means predictable.

And I hated him a little for that, and loved him more, because he's right, and being right is the only mercy he knows how to give.

We broke camp and took to the boats. Ahead of us the drowned mile opened into open sea—the true Sundered Sea, the caldera country, where according to Odell's recovered coordinates the Spire stands in forty fathoms of water with its crown barely breaching the waves. Three days' sail, if the weather holds. Less, if the thing that wears my brother's face decides to hurry us.

On the second morning out, in water too deep for any rooftop, the warmth came back into the sea around our hull. The impressions returned—not at the prow this time, but alongside, pacing us, a dozen shapes where before there'd been one, and among them, at the front, the tall one. The grown one. Keeping pace. Watching me watch it.

And this time it didn't dissolve when the light changed. This time it held.

This time, as the sun cleared the water, it lifted one hand out of the sea—just the hand, just for a moment, cupped like a man offering something—and in the cup of that hand, glinting, was a shard of dragon-fire glass, the old Mereth road-markers, the kind that once lit the whole kingdom's highways, and it set the shard down on the swell beside our boat and let the current carry it to us, and then it sank, and the others sank with it, and the sea went ordinary again.

Pell fished the shard out. It was warm. It is still warm. It points, very faintly, very steadily, south-southeast, the way a compass needle points north, and Odell has measured it three times and swears the bearing matches the Spire's coordinates to within a degree.

A signpost, Sefa said, from the drowned dead of a country that's been dead two hundred years. To a place only one living person alive knows how to find.

Or a trail of breadcrumbs, I said. Laid by something that wants us exactly where it wants us.

Yes, Sefa said. Both of those can be true.

Both of those can be true. I've been turning that sentence over for a day and a half now, and I think it might be the truest thing anyone has said to me since the ward-stone cracked. Something in the deep water knows my name. Something in the deep water is spending my brother's face like currency. And the lantern at my hip—the cage, the leash, the last fragment of a silenced god—grows warmer every hour, and says nothing, and waits.

Three days to the Spire. The water is getting warmer.
