## 8. The Brother Beneath the Water

The chapel came up out of the water like a confession.

Odell saw it first—he was always seeing things first, that was his curse and his trade—and he stopped poling the barge and said, quietly, "There." Just that. There. As if naming a thing could keep it small.

We'd been two days in the drowned country by then, and I had learned its grammar. The ruins of Mereth did not rise; they *surfaced*, the way a drowned man surfaces, reluctantly, in pieces. A chimney here. A weathervane there, still turning in a wind that had nothing left to turn it against. Streets that ran under black-green water so clear it felt like an insult, like the city was showing off its own corpse.

The chapel stood on a hill that had been a hill once and was now a shoal, its bell tower leaning at an angle that said *I have been patient*. Its roof was gone. Its windows were empty eyes. And through those windows, down in the flooded nave where the light didn't reach, something was glowing.

Orange. Deep orange. The color of coals banked for the night. The color of my brother's forge.

"Sunken fire," Sefa said, from the bow. She'd been quiet since the breadcrumb trail started—since the drowned mile had begun laying out its little lanterns of phosphorescence for us, one every fifty yards, like a host lighting lamps before guests arrive. "That's not natural fire. Natural fire drowns."

"Everything about this place is not natural," Odell muttered. "That's rather the point of it."

I said nothing. I was looking at the glow and feeling the lantern at my hip grow warm against my thigh, warm the way a hand goes warm, and thinking: *the ember knows this place.*

*The ember knows this fire.*

* * *

We grounded the barge on the shoal because Odell wanted to chart it and Sefa wanted to stretch her legs and I wanted—I told myself—to scout the high ground. All three of us knew it was a lie. We went because the fire called us, and we had been in the drowned country long enough to stop pretending we weren't being drawn.

Inside, the chapel was a cathedral of knee-deep water. Pews floated loose, turned every which way, as if the congregation had stood up all at once and never sat back down. The altar was dry—a miracle or an engineering choice, the stone raised on a plinth above the floodline—and on the altar burned the fire.

Not a bonfire. A coal-bed, wide as a hearth, burning without fuel, without smoke, without sound. Orange light moved across the wet walls and made them look like the inside of a living thing.

And standing in the fire, ankle-deep in flame that should have eaten him, was my brother.

He looked exactly as he had at nineteen. Broad shoulders, forge-calloused hands, the crooked grin, the burn scar on his forearm shaped like a comma—the scar he'd gotten pulling me out of the kiln-house when I was seven, the scar I had kissed better with mud and prayer because neither of us believed in doctors. His hair was dark. His eyes were dark. He was dead twenty-two years, drowned in the flood that took half the valley's first settlement, and he was looking at me across a burning altar with an expression of such ordinary, unbearable affection that my knees nearly went.

"Tamsin," he said.

His voice was right too. That was the worst part. Not an echo, not a memory replaying—it was *his* voice, roughened by smoke, warm at the edges, the voice that used to say *Tamsin Vane, you are the stubbornest creature God ever half-finished.*

"Don't," I said. My hand found the hatchet at my belt. I don't know what I thought the hatchet would do. "Don't you say my name in that voice."

"It's the only voice I have." He spread his hands, palms up, the way he always did when he was innocent, which was never. "It's the only face I have. I'm sorry for both. I know what they cost you."

"You're dead," I said. "You drowned when I was twelve. I carried your body out of the water myself. I buried you under the rowan tree at Coldharrow with my own hands, and I put a coin in your mouth, and I sat vigil three nights, and nothing answered. Nothing *ever* answered. So whatever you are, you're not him."

"No," he agreed, gently. "I'm not."

The honesty of it undid me more than any lie would have. I had braced for denial, for the full performance, for the ghost insisting *I am Brannoc, I came back, sister, I came back.* Instead it stood in the fire wearing my grief like a borrowed coat and admitted the coat wasn't mine.

"What are you?" Odell asked. He had positioned himself between Sefa and the altar, which was either protective or tactical, and with Odell it was usually both.

"A fragment," the thing said. "A splinter of dragon-fire, old as the Spire, older than the drowning. When the caldera went cold, I fell out of the hearth like a spark off an anvil. I've been drifting ever since—in chimneys, in forges, in dying fires. Looking for a shape people would follow."

"And you picked that one." Sefa's voice was flat and hard. She hadn't taken her eyes off it since we entered. "Out of every face in the world. You picked hers."

"It picked itself," the spirit said. "I don't choose what I'm made of. I'm fire, and fire is memory. When I drifted near her—when I felt what she carries—" it nodded at the lantern on my hip, and the lantern pulsed, once, like a heartbeat—"I couldn't be anything else. She is the most Brannoc-shaped thing left in the world. I am simply the reflection."

"That's vile," Sefa said.

"Yes," it agreed again. "It is. I won't pretend otherwise. But I can get you to the Spire, and no one else in this drowned country can say the same."

* * *

We argued on the shoal while the barge rocked at its mooring line and the chapel glowed behind us like a lamp in a skull.

"He's lying," Odell said. "Or it's lying. Whatever. It's lying, Tamsin. Spirits of fire are spirits of appetite. It wants something, and the something is you, and specifically the something is that lantern. Everything it says is shaped to walk you to the altar with the ember in your hands."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you haven't spoken since we came out of the chapel, and you have your hatchet in your hand, and you're holding it wrong, which means your hands aren't working properly, which means your judgment isn't either."

"I know it's lying," I said. "Or I know it might be. I know everything you know, Odell, plus one thing you don't."

"Which is?"

I looked at the chapel. At the fire in the window, moving like breath.

"When it said my name," I said, "for one moment—one moment—I wasn't afraid. First time since the ward-stone cracked. First time since the proclamation, since the shrine burned, since the marsh, since any of it. For one moment I was twelve years old and my brother was alive and the world was a place with him in it." I made myself look at Odell. "You want me to weigh that against suspicion? Fine. But weigh it honestly. Because I've been afraid for twenty years, Odell. Twenty years of silent vows and borrowed safety and a god who wouldn't speak. One minute of not-afraid. That's what it offered. That's the price of the ticket."

Odell took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his shirt hem, which he did when he was losing an argument and wanted to look like he was consulting maps instead.

"The Door," he said finally. "Say it needs the ember. Say the spirit leads us true, all three days, and delivers us to the Spire with our throats uncut and our map intact. Say it does everything right." He put the spectacles back on. "At the end, at the altar, it will ask for something. Fire always asks. What will you do then?"

"I'll decide then."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is. You taught me that—you and your breadcrumbs. Both things can be true. It can be a liar *and* the only guide. It can be using me *and* mean it, whatever 'it' means for a thing made of memory. I can't refuse it, Odell. Don't ask me to. Ask me anything else."

He studied me for a long moment. Behind him Sefa watched the water line, hand on her knife, doing the sentry work none of us had ordered and all of us needed. Somewhere out in the drowned streets, a fish jumped, and the splash sounded almost like a word.

"All right," Odell said at last. "But we set terms. It walks ahead, never behind. It doesn't touch the lantern. It answers one question truthfully each day—we choose the question—and if we catch it in a lie, we leave it on the first dry rock we find, and you don't argue."

"And if it saves our lives in the meantime?" Sefa asked without turning around.

"Then we renegotiate," Odell said. "I'm a cartographer. Everything is negotiable. That's what borders are."

* * *

The spirit accepted the terms with a gravity that would have been funny from anyone else. From a dead man's face it was just another wound.

It walked ahead of the barge—not in the water, but *on* it, or slightly above it, its bare feet dimpling the surface without breaking it, leaving ripples that spread outward in perfect rings. Where it passed, the phosphorescent breadcrumbs brightened, and I understood suddenly that they had never been laid for us at all. They were its trail. It had been walking this route for years, maybe decades, pacing the road to the Spire like a dog pacing a fence line, waiting for someone worth guiding.

At dusk of the first day it crouched on a half-drowned quay and waited for our question.

Sefa went first. "What does Corvan know? Is he behind us?"

"He is behind you," the spirit said. "Two days, maybe less. He has a gunship and a tide-reader and he has stopped following the map, which frightens him more than you can know. He is following *you*. Or following the ember. With Corvan it was always hard to tell the difference."

Second day, Odell's question: "Is there another way into the Spire than the main causeway?"

"There is a way through the foundry levels," the spirit said, "under the sea-gate. It is flooded to the third stair. Your girl can swim it; the rest of you will need air and nerve. And it is watched by something that was a warden once and is now mostly hunger. I would call it honest odds. Half and half."

"Half and half of what?" Odell demanded.

"I said truthful, not comforting," the spirit said, and grinned Brannoc's crooked grin, and I had to look away at the water.

My question I saved for evening, when the others slept and the spirit kept its watch at the barge's prow, sitting with its knees drawn up like a boy on a dock. I sat across from it with the lantern between us. It glowed in response to the fire in the fire, two coals recognizing each other.

"One question," it said. "Choose well."

I had a list. I'd been composing it all day. *What happened at the drowning? Why did Vael go silent? What waits at the Spire? What are you, really? What do you want?*

Instead I heard myself ask: "Did it hurt him? At the end. In the water. Did it hurt long?"

The spirit was quiet for a moment. The rings of its passage spread out around the barge, soft as breathing.

"I don't know," it said. "I wasn't there. I only wear what she remembers, and she wasn't there either. No one who loved him was there. That's the cruelty of drownings—they happen in company, but never the right company."

I nodded. I found I wasn't disappointed. It was the truest answer I'd had in weeks.

"That was a poor use of a question," the spirit said.

"No," I said. "It was the only one I actually needed answered."

* * *

On the third day the water grew warm.

Not sun-warm. Forge-warm. We poled through streets where the current ran lazy and hot, where steam rose off the surface in the morning cold and hung over the rooftops like the city trying to remember how to breathe. The lantern at my hip was nearly painful now, and twice I felt it pulse in rhythm with something far ahead—something vast and slow, a heart the size of a mountain beating once a minute, deep under the horizon.

The Spire. We were close enough to feel it.

The spirit stopped at midday on the crest of a drowned bridge, its feet on the balustrade, and pointed south.

"There," it said.

Far off, at the edge of sight, something rose out of the sea. Black against grey sky. Too big for the eye to take in whole, so the mind kept shrinking it and failing. A tower, if towers could be mountains. Light—faint, guttering, orange—played around its crown like a halo having second thoughts.

"The Spire of the Sundered Sea," the spirit said. "The hearth is nearly cold. You can see it from here—see how the light stutters? Three days ago it would have been steady. Whatever sleeps in that caldera is running out of time, and so is everything bound to it."

"Including you," Sefa said.

"Including me." It turned to me then, and the light of the far-off Spire caught its face, and for a moment—just a moment—the face flickered. Like a candle in wind. Underneath, for less than a heartbeat, I saw something else: not a face at all, but a brightness, a pressure, a heat given outline. Then Brannoc's grin reassembled itself, warm and crooked and terrible.

"Sister," it said, and the word dropped into my chest like a coin into a well. "When we reach the altar, there is something I must ask of you. Something I should ask now, while there's still time to hate me for asking."

"Then ask now," I said.

It shook its head. "No. You'd refuse, and you'd be right to, and then you'd refuse again at the altar anyway, and I'd rather have the refusal once, at the place where refusals still mean something." It looked south again, at the stuttering light. "Ask me tomorrow's question tonight. Spend it early. Because after tonight, Tamsin, I may not be able to answer anything truthfully at all."

The lantern flared against my hip—hot, insistent, urgent as a fist knocking—and I understood, with a certainty that arrived whole and unwelcome, that the ember and the spirit had been speaking to each other all along. In the language of fire. In the silence my god had worn for twenty years.

And somewhere behind us, faint as a rumor, came a sound that did not belong to the drowned country at all: the deep-throated cough of a gunship's engine, carrying flat across the warm water.

Three days had become two. Maybe less.

"Tell me what you'll ask at the altar," I said to the spirit. "Tonight. Before the question-price runs out. Tell me what you want, and let me spend my sleepless night deciding whether to give it."

It smiled at me with my brother's mouth, and the smile held more sorrow than any dead man's face should carry.

"Oh, sister," it said. "What I want is to go home."
