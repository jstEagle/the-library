## 9. The Inquisitor's Mercy

Sefa had learned to read her father's face the way other children learned letters.

There was the face he wore for the crowds—grave, sorrowful, lit from within by the terrible beauty of duty. There was the face he wore for his riders—flat, efficient, a tool being sharpened. And there was the third face, the one almost no one saw, that came out only in the small hours when he thought she slept: not grief exactly, but something adjacent to it. The look of a man counting coins he hated to spend.

For eleven years she had believed the first face was the true one.

She had been nine years old the day of the Hollowbrook mercy, and she remembered it with the terrible clarity that children keep for the moments that break them. A village in the hill country, three days' ride from anywhere, accused of harboring a gifted child. The Inquisition's wagons rolled in at dawn with their white banners and their iron censers, and the whole village turned out in the square because they were made to turn out, farmers and millers and one bent old grandmother who kept looking at Sefa like she wanted to tell her something.

The child's name was Pell. He was six.

Sefa stood at her father's side on the courthouse steps, as she always did, in her good coat with the silver clasp, and watched Corvan come down among the people like a shepherd walking into his own flock. She heard him say the words she would carry for the rest of her life, words the balladeers would later get wrong because the truth was worse than any song:

*"The gift is not the sin,"* her father said, lifting the boy onto the wagon step so all could see him, small and sniffling in a shirt too big for him. *"The crown does not burn children. It teaches them. This child will be taken into care, and this village will be spared, because mercy given publicly binds us all to be merciful privately."*

The crowd wept. Sefa wept. Pell waved at her over his father's shoulder as the Inquisition's carriage took him away toward the capital, toward the "schools" everyone pretended not to wonder about.

That night, camped two miles east where the road dipped behind a ridge, Sefa woke to the smell of smoke.

Not cookfire smoke. Wet-thatch smoke. Burning-hair smoke.

She climbed out of the tent and walked toward it because she was nine and did not know yet what her own legs were doing. Over the rise she saw Hollowbrook burning—not the square, not the courthouse, but the houses at the far end, the poor end, the end where the bent old grandmother lived. Riders moved between the flames with torches, unhurried, methodical, working their way up the street house by house while the people who ran from the fire were caught at the well by men with hooked poles.

Her father stood apart, at the edge of the light, watching.

He wasn't smiling. That was the thing Sefa held onto longest, the thing she used to defend him even to herself. He wasn't smiling. His face was grave and sorrowful and lit from within by the terrible beauty of duty, the same face he'd worn on the courthouse steps, and he watched his riders burn thirty-one people alive with the expression of a man witnessing an execution he had personally ordered, which was exactly what it was.

*The gift is not the sin,* he'd said at noon.

At midnight, the sin was the bloodline, and bloodlines lived in houses, and houses burned.

A rider noticed her—a sergeant named Vask, whose beard smelled of tallow—and grabbed her arm hard enough to bruise. "Girl, you shouldn't—" and then her father's voice, quiet across the firelight:

"Let her look, Sergeant."

Corvan crouched down to her level. Up close, the smoke had gotten into the lines around his eyes, and later—in the years after—she would understand that he had let her see on purpose, that this too was a kind of teaching, though whether he meant to teach her cruelty or truth she never decided.

"This is what mercy costs," he told her. "Remember it. The ones who forget what it costs become monsters. The ones who can't pay it become dead." He touched her cheek with a hand that still smelled of the censers. "You will pay it better than I do. I've seen to that."

Then he sent her back to bed, and in the morning the ash of Hollowbrook was already falling behind them like gray snow, and Pell's empty seat in the carriage was explained as *transferred ahead*, and Sefa lay against the leather and understood, in the way of children, without words or evidence, that she had watched a performance with two audiences. The village got mercy at noon. The crown got obedience at midnight. And she got both faces, so that someday she would wear them too.

She had been nine. She had believed him anyway, for a while longer, because believing was easier than the alternative, and she was nine.

She was twenty now, and she was done.

* * *

Now she crouched in the reeds above the causeway road with Tamsin Vane's knife in her boot and her father's hunt-list folded against her heart, and she watched the lanterns of the Inquisition come up the drowned mile through the rain.

Six riders, no—the lantern-light split and doubled in the water, eight, maybe ten. Behind them, heavier shapes: the supply wagon with its iron-banded chest, and on the wagon's bench, wrapped in an oilcloth cloak, the tall straight silhouette she would have known blindfolded by the way it sat a horse, the way it sat everything, like the world was furniture arranged for his use.

Her father had found the trail faster than Odell predicted. Of course he had. Odell calculated in maps; Corvan calculated in daughters. He knew Sefa's habits the way she knew his faces. He would have guessed the smuggler's market, guessed the causeway, guessed the marsh crossings—and then simply ridden to the place where all guesses converged, because he had spent twenty years learning the geography of other people's desperation.

"They'll reach the ford in a quarter hour," Odell whispered beside her, flattening his long body into the reeds. His hands were shaking; she could hear the map case rustling against his back like a nervous animal. "We can't fight ten. We can't outrun them on this ground. Tell me you have a thought, girl, because I'm out."

"I have a thought," Sefa said.

It was a bad thought. All her thoughts lately were bad thoughts, each one a stone she picked up knowing it would sink them. But she had stopped pretending otherwise somewhere between the whale-carcass town and here, somewhere between Brannoc's beautiful lying face and the night she'd copied her father's hunt-list by candlelight and seen, in her own handwriting, the name **TAMSIN VANE** at the top, underlined twice in her father's red ink with a note in the margin: *the keeper. take alive if possible.*

Alive if possible. Her father's mercy again. Two audiences.

"Tamsin," she breathed, and felt more than saw the mercenary shift in the reeds to her left, silent as the god she served, which was to say completely, which was to say like something that had made a discipline of not existing. "They're going to offer terms. My father doesn't burn first. Not anymore, not with witnesses. He offers. It looks better in the reports."

"And?" Tamsin's voice was low water over stones.

"And you should hear them before you refuse them." Sefa swallowed. "Because I know how he sounds when he means the opposite of what he says. I grew up on it. Let me do the listening."

Below them, the lanterns reached the ford and stopped. Rain needled the flooded mile, turning every reflection into broken glass. A horn sounded once, twice—a parley call, old-fashioned, almost courteous—and a voice carried across the water with the trained carrying power of a man who had spoken from courthouse steps his whole life:

"Tamsin Vane! Keeper of the ember! I am Corvan, Inquisitor of the Sundered Marches, and I hold a warrant of grace signed by the crown itself! Walk out with the lantern, walk out alone, and no hand will be laid on those who follow you! They are sheep led astray; you are the wolf! Spare them your fate!"

Beside her, Odell made a small disbelieving sound. "Grace. Signed by the crown. Does he think we were born yesterday?"

"He thinks," Sefa said quietly, "that some of us were born nine years ago, in a square in Hollowbrook, and never stopped believing." She watched the lanterns below, watched her father sit his horse in the rain with his face turned up toward the dark reeds where his daughter hid, and she knew—he could not possibly know, and yet he knew—that she was there. He had known since the market. Maybe since the shrine fire. Maybe since the day she stole the list.

He raised his voice again, and this time it changed. Softer. Almost gentle. The third face, the midnight face, the one for counting costs.

"Sefa." Just her name, carried flat across the water, no title, no audience-performance in it—or rather, the deepest performance of all, the one performed for an audience of one. "I know you're there, little bird. Come home. Whatever you think you saw, whatever you think you owe these people—come home, and none of it will ever be counted against you. You have my word. My word has never been broken to you."

And that was the moment, kneeling in the freezing reeds with the rain running off her chin, that Sefa finally understood the shape of her whole life.

His word had never been broken *to her*. Because she had never been the one he made promises to. She had been part of the payment. The spared child at Hollowbrook, the pardoned villages, the registered instead of burned—all of it was currency, and she was minted from the same metal. Even this. Even *come home*. It was not love offered; it was a coin displayed, gleaming, before an audience of reeds and rain and one silent mercenary, so that when Sefa refused, her refusal would be on the record, witnessed, another entry in the great ledger where Corvan recorded what mercy cost and who refused to pay.

He was giving her a role in the performance. Refusal was the role. He needed her to refuse, loudly, publicly, so that the report could read *daughter corrupted by the keeper, pursued with heavy heart.*

Well.

She had learned from the best.

Sefa stood up out of the reeds.

"Sefa, don't—" Odell hissed, and Tamsin's hand closed on her ankle—not pulling her down, just anchoring, just saying *I'm here*—and Sefa shook them both off gently and stepped to the open bank where the lantern-light could find her.

"Father!" she called, and her voice cracked once on the word and then steadied, and she hated that crack and was proud of it in the same breath. "You want me to come home?"

The lanterns swung toward her. Ten riders, motionless in the rain. Her father's face, pale above his collar, wearing—for just one unguarded instant, one frame torn out of the performance—the third face. The real one? No. She knew better now. There was no real one. There was only the ledger.

"Here is my answer," Sefa said. "Hollowbrook. Say the word, Father. Say *Pell*. Say what happened to the boy you lifted onto the wagon step while I cried. Say it out loud, to your riders, to the crown, to God, and if you can say it—if you can stand there in front of everyone and say *we took him to the schools and the schools burned him in the winter of the gray fever because gifted children die in the schools*—then I will walk down this bank and put myself in your hands, and I won't run again, I swear it on Mother's grave."

Silence. Rain on water. Somewhere far off, thunder counting to itself.

Her father sat his horse, and she watched him do the arithmetic she had watched him do her entire life: the riders listening, the reports waiting, the ledger balancing. Thirty-one people in a burning street versus one word spoken aloud. She watched him weigh his daughter against his record, and she watched the scale settle, and it took less time than a heartbeat.

"You are ill," Corvan said at last, loudly, clearly, for the record. "Taken in by heretics. Grieved past reason. We will discuss your treatment when you are in custody." He turned his head slightly, addressing the darkness beyond her. "Vane. The offer stands until moonrise. After that, I burn the marsh, and whatever runs out of it, runs out burning."

"That's the answer, then," Sefa said, and her voice didn't crack this time at all.

"That's the answer," her father said, and turned his horse.

She stood on the bank and watched him go, ten lanterns shrinking up the drowned mile, and she did not cry. Crying was for girls who still believed the first face.

Behind her, Tamsin rose out of the reeds without a sound, rain streaming off her, the lantern with the guttering ember hanging at her hip throwing its faint gold light up between them. For a long moment neither spoke.

"Moonrise is two hours," Tamsin said finally. "The salt marsh south of the causeway floods at high water. If we cut across it in the dark—"

"You'll drown," Odell said, limping up, gray-faced. "Or the horses will. Or both."

"Or we vanish," Tamsin said, "and a man who calculates in daughters loses his map of us." She looked at Sefa, and something passed over the mercenary's scarred face that on anyone else might have been tenderness. "You could still walk down that bank, you know. He'd honor it. Some of it. Enough."

"I know," Sefa said. And she found, examining herself, that it was true—she could, and he would honor enough of it, and she would spend the rest of her life as a coin in her father's purse, gleaming, spent carefully, spent *publicly*.

"No," she said.

Tamsin nodded once, as if a contract had been signed. Then she unslung the lantern and held it up between them, and in its dying light Sefa saw the ember pulse—once, weakly, like a heart remembering what hearts were for—and felt, for the first time since she'd stolen the list, that she was standing on the right side of a line, even if the line was drawn in floodwater and the far bank was on fire.

"South, then," Tamsin said, turning toward the black water. "Through the marsh. Keep to my tracks and don't stop moving, whatever you hear behind us."

Sefa took one last look north, at the drowned mile where her father's lanterns had vanished, at the country she had crossed and recrossed her whole life without ever once seeing.

Whatever you hear behind us, Tamsin had said.

What Sefa heard, as they waded out into the dark, was very faint and very far away: the sound of her father's horn, sounding the signal she had known since childhood, the one that meant *the marsh will be fired at moonrise.*

They had less than two hours, and the water was already rising.
