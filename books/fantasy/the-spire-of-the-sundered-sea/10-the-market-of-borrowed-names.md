## 10. The Market of Borrowed Names

The town was called Gullet by the people who lived there and the Whale by everyone else, because it had been built inside one.

It had beached itself on a mudflat at the drowned edge of Mereth two generations ago—a leviathan, gray-blue and mountain-sized, its bones gone to ivory scaffolding, its ribs arched overhead like the vaulting of a cathedral that had never been finished. Someone had stretched sailcloth between the ribs. Someone else had hung lanterns from the vertebrae. Now three hundred people lived in the hollow of the dead thing, and called it home, and charged travelers for the privilege of walking through its open mouth.

"Charming," Sefa said.

She said it flatly, the way you'd say *rat-infested* or *on fire*, but her eyes were moving everywhere—counting exits, counting blades, doing the arithmetic her father had taught her before she ran from him. Tamsin had learned to read that arithmetic. It meant Sefa was afraid and intended not to show it.

"Don't touch anything," Odell said. "Don't buy anything. Don't tell anyone your name."

"You told me that already."

"I'll keep telling you until it works."

Tamsin adjusted the strap of her pack and looked down the throat of the whale. Lantern-light pooled amber along the rib-arches. Somewhere deep inside, a fiddler was playing a tune with too many notes in it, the kind of music smugglers liked because it couldn't be hummed back to a magistrate. The smell hit in layers: brine, tar, cooked fish, lamp oil, and underneath it all the sweet rot of the beast itself, sixty years dead and still giving.

Brannoc walked beside her.

He wore his face well these days—the drowned boy grown into a drowned man, water still beading in his hair no matter how dry the air, his smile easy, his eyes the green of shallow sea over sand. He had been walking beside her since the sunken chapel. She had stopped flinching when he spoke. She hadn't stopped watching him. She suspected she never would.

"You've been here before," she said quietly.

"Once." Brannoc tilted his head back, looking up through the gap where the whale's blowhole had been, at a sky going bruise-colored with evening. "When I was alive. My captain sold salt here." A pause, small as a knife turned over. "Different then. Fewer lanterns. More teeth."

"Everything has more teeth now," Odell muttered. "Come on. The passage-broker keeps an office in the fourteenth vertebra. If we reach him before full dark we might sleep tonight without holding our knives."

They went in through the mouth, past the gate-tolls—a woman with a whalebone cudgel and a ledger, who took Odell's coin and didn't ask names, which was the whole point of Gullet—and down the esophagus-road into the body of the town.

* * *

The Market of Borrowed Names opened around them like a wound.

Stalls crowded the space between the ribs, two stories of them in places, ladders and rope-bridges stitching the levels together. Everything was for sale. Salt and gunpowder. Prayer-candles stamped with the faces of gods who were dead or exiled or both. Bottles of seawater labeled with kingdoms that no longer appeared on any chart. A caged bird that repeated, in a perfect court accent, *"I never saw them, I never saw anything"*—a confession-eater, Odell whispered, worth more than the ship it would eventually buy someone's silence with.

And names. Names above all. That was what the market traded best.

There was a stall where a one-eyed scribe sold new identities written on slips of vellum: three coppers for a name, five for a name with a history, ten for a name with a grieving widow to swear to it. Beside him, a woman sold the opposite service—for a fee she would take your old name off your shoulders and carry it away in a lead box, so that no divination, no witch-hunter's art, no debt-collector's curse could find you by it. The box rattled when she shifted it. It sounded like it held a great many names, all of them trying to get out.

Sefa stopped walking.

"Tamsin." Her voice had gone thin. "That list. The hunt-list my father carries. How does he find people?"

"Names," Tamsin said. "True ones. That's why the gifted change them, why they run to places like this." She watched the lead box rattle. "And why he burns the markets after."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. Then she bought nothing, and touched nothing, and kept walking, and Tamsin understood that something in her had made a decision she wasn't ready to say out loud yet. Fine. There would be time. Or there wouldn't, and it wouldn't matter.

They found the fourteenth vertebra.

The passage-broker was a fat man named Quill whose flesh had gone the same gray as the whale around him, as though the beast were slowly reclaiming its tenant. His office was a cage hung from the rib-ceiling, reachable only by a rope-ladder, which told Tamsin everything about how much trust existed in Gullet. He sat behind a desk bolted to the floor, weighing a brass astrolabe in one pudgy hand like a man deciding whether to eat it.

"Passage," Odell said, climbing up. "West, past the Shattered Teeth, to the caldera coast. Quietly."

Quill laughed. It was a wet sound. "Everyone wants quiet, mapman. Quiet costs double." He set the astrolabe down and steepled his fingers. "You want the caldera coast. Nobody wants the caldera coast. The Spire's shadow scares off honest sailors, and dishonest ones know better than to ask what's out there. I can sell you a boat. I can't sell you a crew willing to point its bow that way."

"I can pay for the unwillingness."

"With what?" Quill's good eye—it was hard to say whether either of them was the good one—drifted to Odell's pack. "Word came down the coast about a cartographer traveling with a soldier and a girl. Word says he carries the Ferrance surveys. Originals. Charts of waters that haven't been charted since the kingdom drowned." He smiled with too much gum. "Word travels faster than boats, friend. Especially here."

Odell's hand moved, very slightly, toward the flap of his pack. Tamsin saw it and didn't stop it. This was the price they had agreed to pay before they ever reached Gullet, and they had agreed to it in the dark of a campfire, in low voices, while Brannoc sat apart pretending to watch the water. Odell's life's work. Forty years of surveying a drowned country, every sounding and salvage-mark and secret channel inked in his own cramped hand. They needed passage more than paper.

"The western charts only," Odell said. "The Door approaches. Nothing east of the Teeth."

Quill's fingers drummed. "The Door approaches," he repeated, tasting it. "Now that's a phrase I haven't heard since—" He stopped himself, and his face did something complicated, and Tamsin realized with a small cold shock that the broker was afraid. Not of them. Of the word itself. Of what came after it.

"Half the charts now," Odell said. "And the boat. And a crew that doesn't ask questions."

"Three-quarters, and I keep my questions."

"Half, and you don't even think them."

They haggled while the lanterns below burned lower. Tamsin stood at the rail of the cage with Sefa and watched the market turn over beneath them, and let the haggling wash past her like weather. This was Odell's part. He had been born to men who talked money across desks; he had the cadence of it in his blood even disgraced and half-starved. She watched instead for other things. Watched the gate-woman glance up at their cage twice, and speak once to a man in oilskins who then left quickly. Watched a boy climb the rope-bridges with a message folded small in his fist, moving with the particular urgency of someone paid by the word.

Watched Brannoc slip away down a ladder while nobody was looking at him except her.

* * *

She found him at the tail of the town, where the whale's vertebrae dwindled to a last knob of bone sticking out over the mudflats, and the market noise fell away behind them like a tide going out.

He was talking to someone.

A woman—or a shape of a woman—stood at the rail of bone, wrapped in a fisherman's slicker with the hood up. Sea-water ran off her hem in a steady trickle, pooling, spreading, though the boards were dry everywhere else. She was not quite the right size for her coat. She was not quite the right number of joints for her hands, which stayed folded in front of her and did not move, not once, in all the time Tamsin stood watching.

Brannoc's voice was low. Tamsin caught fragments.

"—not asking you to lie to her. Just... delay. Two days is all. Let the tide do the work—"

The hooded figure made no sound. But the water at her feet spread another inch, and Brannoc nodded as if he'd heard a whole speech.

Tamsin stepped out of the shadow of the vertebra.

Brannoc turned. For half a heartbeat—one heartbeat, no more—the face he wore was wrong. Not Brannoc's face. Something older underneath it, patient and vast and terribly tired, looking out through her brother's eyes like a light behind frosted glass. Then it settled, and he was her brother again, smiling, easy, guilty.

"Tamsin."

"Who is she?"

"A harbor-warden." He shrugged. "Local spirit. They're thick out here—the whale drew things to it for miles when it died. I'm arranging us a fair current past the Teeth. Saving us days."

"That isn't what you were arranging."

"Tamsin—"

"She doesn't talk," Tamsin said. "I watched. Her mouth never moved and her hands never moved and the water under her moved like it was listening. That's not a warden. Wardens have parishes. That thing has orders." She came closer, boots ringing on bone, and Brannoc stepped back and there it was again, the flicker, the wrongness, gone as fast as it came. "What did you promise her?"

"Nothing that matters."

"What. Did you promise."

He looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment she thought he might tell her the truth—that some buried fragment of whatever he truly was might surface and speak. His jaw worked. The sea-smell of him sharpened, the way it did when his control slipped.

"A delay," he said finally. "That's all. Two days of fog off the Teeth. It buys us a clean crossing. You want a clean crossing, Tamsin. Everyone wants—"

"Who pays for it?"

Silence. Out beyond the mudflats the tide was coming in, filling the channels, whispering against the whale's dead flank. Down in the market the fiddler had stopped playing. In the quiet, Tamsin heard the hooded woman's water dripping, drip and drip, patient as anything that has been ordered to wait.

"Brannoc," she said softly, using the name like a key, "my brother drowned when he was fourteen. He was kind and he was stubborn and he lied badly, exactly the way you're lying now. Whoever you are—you wear him well enough that I let myself forget. I won't forget tonight." She stepped in close enough to see her own reflection in the wet dark of his eyes, and hated what she saw next in them: fear. Hers. "If your bargain costs my people, I will spend whatever I am to unmake it. Tell me now, or find out."

For a long moment the spirit said nothing. Then, very quietly, in a voice that had slipped halfway out of Brannoc's cadence into something deeper:

"The ember wants to go home, keeper. So do I. We are the same thing wearing two shapes, and we are both so tired." The hooded woman's water dripped. "Two days of fog. That is all I asked for. Time. Because when we reach the Spire, everything ends—her, me, you, all of it—and I wanted..." He stopped. Started again, human again, brother-shaped again, miserable. "I wanted a little more of this first. The walking. The company. Being almost real."

Tamsin stood very still.

She wanted to believe it. That was the trap, and she could see its jaws laid bare in front of her, and she wanted to walk into it anyway, because the alternative was admitting that her brother had been dead twenty years and this thing had never been him at all, and she had crossed half a drowned kingdom talking to it in the dark, telling it things she'd never told the living.

"Two days," she said at last. "Fog. Nothing else. And if I learn you've bargained one hour more than that—if the *ember* has bargained one hour more—I'll put out your lantern myself, and I'll do it kindly, because you wear a face I loved. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," said the thing wearing her brother.

She turned and walked back toward the light of the market, and did not look at the hooded woman, and did not hear her leave, and knew anyway that the mudflats were empty behind her, and that the drip-drip-drip of waiting water had followed her farther than it should have.

* * *

They got their boat.

Quill delivered at moonrise as promised: a black-hulled ketch named the *Charity*, forty feet of stolen grace, with a crew of five who took Odell's coin and Odell's charts and asked nothing, which in Gullet was the highest form of honesty. Odell handed over the western surveys roll by roll, and each roll cost him something visible—he flinched, each time, like a man donating blood. When the last one was gone he stood on the dock with empty hands and said, mostly to himself, "Well. Now I'm just a man who used to know things."

"You still know the way," Sefa said.

"That I do, girl. That I do." He managed something like a smile.

They loaded in the dark: water, hard bread, the lantern with the ember sleeping in it, Tamsin's blades, Sefa's father's hunt-list folded small against her heart like a splinter she'd decided to keep. Brannoc helped with the lines, passing ropes through his own insubstantial chest when it amused him, and the crew pretended not to see, because crews out of Gullet had seen worse and been paid to forget it.

At the gangplank, Tamsin paused and looked back at the whale-town glowing in its own lantern-light, three hundred borrowed lives stacked inside the ribs of a dead god of the deep. Somewhere in there was a woman with a lead box full of surrendered names. Tamsin found herself hoping the box was heavy. Found herself hoping every name in it belonged to someone running from a fire.

"Captain says we make the Teeth by tomorrow night," Sefa said, coming up beside her. "With luck."

"Luck," Tamsin said, "or fog."

Sefa glanced at her. "You felt it too? The air's gone strange since sundown. Heavy. Wet." She frowned out at the black water. "My father taught me weather before he taught me scripture. Fog like this doesn't come natural off a warm coast. It comes *sent*."

Tamsin said nothing.

They cast off. The *Charity* slid out on the turning tide, Gullet sinking behind them into the dark, and the fog was already waiting offshore—not banked, not drifting, just standing on the water in a wall from sea to sky, silent as congregation, exactly where a ship bound west would have to pass through it.

Odell came aft, squinting, and his face went gray. "I charted these waters for thirty years," he said slowly. "I never once charted fog standing still."

Brannoc stood at the bow, hair beaded with spray, watching the wall come closer. He didn't turn around. But Tamsin saw his hands, hanging at his sides, curl slowly closed—as if holding something he'd been given, or gripping something he was afraid would be taken back.

The fog swallowed the bow. Then the deck. Then them.
