## 11. Storm Over the Sunken Road

Odell Ferrance had spent forty years drawing lines on paper, and he had learned one truth above all others: a line was a promise. A river line promised where the water ran. A road line promised where the ground held firm. And somewhere in his chest, older and more stubborn than any of those promises, was the line he had drawn around Mereth itself—the border of a country that no longer existed anywhere but in his ink.

He felt it now, riding the causeway through the rain, that old promise tightening like a wire.

The Sunken Road had been the grand processional way of the capital once. Odell knew this because he had drawn it, at nineteen, from a surveyor's notes so old the original draftsman's hand had crumbled to dust against the vellum. The road ran straight as a rule for six miles across what was now open water, its paving stones drowned by the Sundering but still holding their course beneath the flood, close enough to the surface that at low tide the crown of each arch broke the waves like the spines of sleeping animals. On either side, the drowned city of Vaelmar stretched away under gray-green water: rooftops, bell towers, the ribs of bridges. A country lying face-up in its own grave.

And above it, coming up the wind with a sound like tearing cloth, flew the gunship.

"Odell." Sefa's voice, tight, from behind him. "Tell me you see it."

"I see it." He did not turn. Turning meant taking his eyes off the shape in the storm, and Odell Ferrance never took his eyes off a thing he intended to beat.

The ship was a crown corvette—*Kestrel*-class, if the silhouette held true—twin rotors beating the rain into mist, hull slung low with the weight of the swivel-guns mounted fore and aft. He had drawn those too, once, in the fat years before his disgrace, when the Office of Roads and Wonders had still trusted him with military surveys. *Kestrels* carried eight men and enough powder-shot to strip the roof off a manor house. They were not built for weather like this. That was the only mercy the storm offered, and Odell intended to spend every coin of it.

Tamsin Vane rode up beside him, hunched against the rain, the lantern swinging at her saddlebow. Inside it, behind smoked glass, the ember of her dead god burned without flickering—as steady as a nail driven through the world. Odell had stopped trying not to look at it. It looked back sometimes. He was almost sure of that.

"How long since it spotted us?" she asked.

"It spotted us an hour ago. It's been herding us since." He pointed with his chin down the causeway, where the paved way curved gently toward the ruins of the Tollgate Arch—a great broken loop of stone rising out of the water ahead, half-collapsed, framing the storm like a crooked door. "See the arch? There's exactly one gap wide enough for us to pass, and it sits directly under the best firing line in six miles of drowned city. Whoever flies that ship has read my maps."

"You gave the crown your maps," Brannoc said. The spirit wore the drowned brother's face the way a coat hangs on a hook—accurate, empty. Rain went through him sometimes, when he forgot himself. Today he remembered. "Every mapmaker serves somebody."

"I served geography," Odell snapped, and immediately hated how much it sounded like a lie. Because it was one. He had served the crown, gladly, for thirty years, and the crown had used his beautiful lines to build beautiful engines of beautiful violence, and when he had finally objected—not even loudly, just once, in a memo—he had been exiled to the very valley whose existence his own maps were ordered to omit. A cartographer erased along with his country. The symmetry was not lost on him. It had never once been lost on him.

"The arch," Tamsin said. "Can we make it before them?"

"We can make it. We can't make it *through* it. Not if they hold the far side." Odell pulled his oilskin hood back and let the rain hit his face, because thinking required discomfort; comfort made him lazy. Sixty years of fieldwork had taught him that. "But they won't hold the far side. They'll hold the near side. They want us stopped on the causeway with water on both flanks, nowhere to run. Standard interdiction. I wrote the manual section myself, gods rot me."

Sefa laughed—one sharp, unhappy note. "Of course you did."

"I was very good at my job." He said it flatly, without pride. It was simply inventory. "Which means I know exactly how they'll deploy, which means I know exactly where the flaw is. There is always a flaw. Usually it's me."

Ahead, the Tollgate Arch grew. Below the causeway, the water churned black over the sunken road, and here and there a chimney or a weathervane stood out of the flood like a drowning man's raised hand. Somewhere under all that water was the palace district. Somewhere was the Street of Inkmen, where Odell had bought his first pen. Somewhere was his house, with its slate roof and its garden drowned forty feet down, where his wife's roses had gone on blooming for a season in the dark before they understood.

He had mapped all of it. Every street. Every door. Forty years, eleven thousand sheets, and one master map—the master map—drawn at a scale of one inch to two hundred yards, containing every road, every ford, every cellar, every secret passage of the kingdom of Mereth as it had been on the last morning before the Sundering. It lived in the oilskin tube strapped across his back, wrapped in waxed linen, and it was the single most dangerous object in the drowned world. With it, the crown could raise the valley's wards, drain the flooded quarters, dig out every gifted child the Sundering had hidden, and burn the past clean to the bedrock.

Without it, the crown had guesses. Guesses cost ships. Guesses cost men. Guesses bought time.

That was the arithmetic he had been doing for three days, ever since the gunship first appeared on the horizon like a bad thought. And the arithmetic kept arriving at the same ugly answer, the one he kept turning over and finding facedown, hoping it would change.

*The map buys the escape. The map is the escape.*

"There," Tamsin said.

The gunship came out of the rain squall to the north, low over the water, rotors flattening the sea into a white scar. It swung wide, elegant as a hawk, and settled into position directly over the causeway a quarter mile ahead of them—between the company and the arch. Its shadow slid along the wet paving stones toward them, long and cold.

Swivel-gun crews scrambled into the mounts. Even at this distance, in the rain, Odell could see the orange gleam of slow-match being blown to coal.

"They're signaling," Sefa said, squinting. "Lamps. Three flashes, pause, three flashes."

"Hail and parley," Odell translated. "Standard first contact. They'll demand surrender of persons and documents. Then, when we refuse—and we will refuse—they'll fire one warning shot across our heads. Then—" he shrugged inside his oilskin— "they'll stop warning."

Brannoc drifted alongside, rain beading on a dead boy's face. "There are seven of us and one of it."

"One of it is enough." Odell counted his companions without looking: Tamsin, silent and grim, her scarred hands white-knuckled on the reins. Sefa, seventeen years old and already carrying more treason than most smugglers manage in a lifetime. Brannoc, who was not a person. The twins Pellam and Jorey, ex-soldiers both, who had signed onto a grain contract two months ago and somehow ridden all the way into legend. Old Maren, the cook, who had refused, absolutely refused, to be left behind, and who was currently muttering a prayer to a god who couldn't hear her, because prayers didn't work in the valley and everyone prayed anyway, out of habit, out of love.

Seven. Against a warship.

"Options," Tamsin said. Just the word. She had a mercenary's genius for reducing a catastrophe to a single syllable.

Odell reined in. The rain drummed on his hood. Behind them, the causeway ran back six miles to the shore of the valley. Ahead, a gunship. Around them, a drowned kingdom, and under everything, everywhere, his life's work strapped to his spine like a debt come due.

"Option one," he said. "We surrender. They take the girl, they take the ember-bearer, they take the map, and they take all of us to Corvan's tribunal, where we will each receive individualized, thorough, and permanent attention. Option two, we fight. Pellam and Jorey are good men but they are not good enough to shoot down a *Kestrel*, and the moment its guns find the causeway we are fish in a barrel with the barrel also on fire. Option three—"

His voice caught. Not from fear. From something worse: clarity.

"—option three, we split their attention."

Tamsin turned in the saddle. She looked at him the way she looked at everything, with those flat gray eyes that had watched twenty years of the valley's slow suffocation, and Odell understood that she already knew. Mercenaries always knew. They did arithmetic for a living too.

"What do you need?" she said.

*What do you need.* Not *what are you proposing.* As if the plan were hers to serve rather than his to confess. Something in Odell's chest cracked slightly, painlessly, like ice at thaw.

"The gunship's weakness is its guns," he said, and found his voice steadier than he expected. "A *Kestrel* holds station over a target by hovering, and it hovers nose-on to the greatest threat. If we give it a threat on the causeway, it faces the causeway. Its stern swings toward the open water to the south. And in the water to the south—" he drew a breath— "there is a submerged tower. The Harbormaster's Spire of Vaelmar. Top floor is twenty feet under the surface. Its foundation stones rise to within four feet of the waterline. If a ship swings its stern low over that foundation at hover-height—"

"It clips it," Jorey said. The big twin had gone very still. "Rotor strikes stone. Ship comes down."

"Ships don't come *down*, they come apart," Odell said. "But yes."

"So we need the gunship facing the wrong way," Tamsin said slowly, "at exactly the wrong place. What makes it face the causeway?"

"A target it cannot ignore." Odell reached back, and his fingers found the waxed linen of the tube, and rested there. "A document it has crossed a hundred miles of storm to recover."

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence. Out on the water, the gunship's lamps blinked their patient triple-flash, and the slow-match glowed, and the drowned city lay quiet beneath them all, keeping every secret it had ever been given, except the ones written down.

"No," Sefa said. "No. You've carried that thing since before I met you. You sleep with it. You talk to it."

"I talk *to* it because it doesn't answer," Odell said. "It's the only honest relationship I have left." He tried for humor and heard it come out as gravel. "Listen to me, all of you. That map is forty years of my life. It is the last complete record of a country the crown erased and then pretended had never existed. Every street of Vaelmar. Every name of every parish. My wife's house is on sheet nine. My daughter's grave is on sheet eleven." His voice did not shake, though he gave it permission to. "I know precisely what I am holding."

"Then why—" Maren began.

"Because a map is a promise," Odell said, "and I have spent my whole life promising other people's countries to powerful men. This is the first time I get to break one on purpose."

He unslung the tube. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"The plan is this. I ride ahead on the open causeway, alone, carrying the map visibly. They will not fire on the man holding the object of the entire expedition—they'll want it intact, and they'll want me alive to explain its cipher-locks. The gunship rotates to cover me. Its stern swings south. When it does, Tamsin takes the rest of you off the causeway onto the submerged paving—we can walk it, the water's waist-deep on the low arches—and around the flank while their attention is on me. If the wind cooperates, the ship drifts over the Harbormaster's foundation and solves its own problem. If it doesn't cooperate, you use the confusion to reach the arch and vanish into the ruined quarter, where no rotor can follow and no gunner can see. Either way, you live. Either way, the Door's coordinates stay where they belong." He tapped his own temple. "In the only archive the crown can't raid."

"And you?" Tamsin asked.

Ah. There it was. The question underneath the question.

"I am sixty-one years old," Odell said, "exiled, disgraced, rheumatic, and the finest mapmaker the kingdom of Mereth ever produced, which is a sentence I have wanted to say out loud for thirty years. I will negotiate with the gunship. Cartographers are useful. They'll keep me useful. And if they don't—" he shrugged— "then I die on the Sunken Road of my own country, in sight of my own house, having burned my own work to save the last of its children. There are worse pages for a story to end on. I've read most of them."

"No," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin—"

"*No.*" It wasn't loud. It was final, the way a ward-stone is final. She leaned forward in the saddle. "You don't ride alone. You ride ahead, yes. You show them the map, yes. But the second that ship turns, you get off that causeway and into the water with the rest of us, or I will come back for you, and I swear on the silent god whose corpse I'm carrying, Odell—I will come back for you, and I will ruin your beautiful plan."

For a long moment the rain fell between them. Then Odell Ferrance, who had not laughed honestly since the Sundering, laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound.

"You know," he said, "in thirty years of service, not one officer ever offered to ruin a plan for my sake."

"Get used to disappointment," Tamsin said. "Move."

* * *

They moved.

Odell rode out alone down the wet spine of the causeway, the map-tube held high in his right hand like a torch, like a flag, like a confession. The rain hammered it. Behind him, muffled by storm, the others slipped off the causeway's southern edge into the flooded shallows, walking the drowned road's shoulders where the old parapets still stood chest-high, ghosts wading through a ghost city.

Three hundred yards. Two hundred. The gunship loomed, vast and dripping, its rotors beating the storm into circles, and Odell saw the gunners tracking him, saw the officer on the deck rail raise a speaking-trumpet, and he stood in his stirrups and shouted into the wind:

"Ferrance! Royal Office of Roads and Wonders! I carry the Master Survey of Mereth!"

The trumpet lowered. A pause, long enough to contain a whole career. Then, faint through the rain, a voice: "*Stand fast and be boarded!*"

"Come and take it!" Odell bellowed—and threw the tube.

Not at them. Into the air. High, spinning, end over end, waxed linen flashing white against the gray—the single most dangerous object in the drowned world tumbling free above the Sunken Road—and he watched the entire gunship flinch toward it, every eye, every gun, the whole ponderous ship rotating on its hover-thrusters with a groan of shifting ballast, stern swinging south, swinging south—

—directly over the Harbormaster's Spire.

The sound, when the rotor struck the foundation stone four feet below the waterline, was not like anything Odell had ever heard, and he had heard the Sundering itself. It was a tearing, a grinding, a scream of metal finding the bones of the old world, and then the *Kestrel* lurched sideways like a struck animal, trailing smoke and one whole rotor-blade, wallowing down toward the causeway with its guns swinging wild.

Odell wheeled his horse, already off the paving, already sliding down the parapet into the black water where six shapes rose to meet him out of the rain—Tamsin first, one scarred hand reaching, hauling him under the surface of the storm.

Behind them, the burning ship came down across the causeway like a falling star, and ahead, through the smoke and the rain, the Tollgate Arch opened its crooked door onto the drowned quarter of Vaelmar.

Odell's hands were empty. For the first time in forty years, his hands were completely empty.

And in his head, word for word, line for line, the whole country stayed.
