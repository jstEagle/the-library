## 12. The Ember Speaks

The lantern had not spoken in three days.

Tamsin carried it anyway, slung from the staff she'd cut on the drowned mile—a length of black cypress that had once been a chapel doorpost, still faintly warm to the touch even now, even here. The ember sat behind the shuttered glass like a coal wrapped in wool: dim, sullen, breathing in a rhythm too slow to be fire and too regular to be anything else.

She had talked to it at first. In the flooded chapels, in the salt marshes, in the long grey hours of walking with Odell's coordinates held only in his head since the burning of his maps. She'd told it about Brannoc's face rising out of the water. She'd told it about Sefa's father's handwriting, her own name at the top of a hunt-list in a neat clerical script. She'd told it things she hadn't said aloud in twenty years of silence, because you could say anything to a god who couldn't answer.

Then it had answered—one word, *Door*, back in the valley—and gone quiet again, and she'd felt like a fool for every prayer she'd ever wasted.

Now they made camp in the ribs of a beached river-barge, half-buried in dune grass a day south of the salt marsh. Sefa was on watch. Odell slept sitting up against the barge's hull with his hands folded over his empty map-case, and even in sleep his fingers twitched, drawing lines on nothing. The two hired spears they'd picked up at the whale-market—Maren and Doss—snored in the lee of the stern.

Tamsin sat apart, as she always did, and opened the lantern's shutters.

"Talk," she said. "Or don't. But I'm done guessing."

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. And then—

*You were never guessing,* it said, and the voice was not what she expected. Not the deep bell-tone of scripture. It was small and dry and old, like paper turning, like her grandmother's voice before the drowning years took her. *You were obeying. There is a difference, and I am sorry for it.*

Tamsin's hand closed around the staff until the cypress creaked.

"You've been sorry for twenty years," she said. "Say something new."

*New.* A pause, the pulse stuttering. *Yes. You deserve new. Listen, then, keeper, and hate me if you must, but listen to all of it before you decide how much.*

The wind moved through the dune grass. Sefa glanced over from her watch; Tamsin shook her head slightly, and the girl turned back to the dark, though her shoulders stayed tense. She'd heard the lantern whisper before. Everyone had. Nobody had heard it *speak*.

*I did not die,* the ember said. *That is the first lie to bury. Vael did not fade, did not gutter out, did not abandon Mereth to the sea as the priests of the crown preached after. I was silenced. There is a word for what was done to me and your tongue has it: imprisoned.*

"I know." Her voice came out flat. "Corvan's order. The Spire. You told me—"

*I told you a piece, in fragments, when I could force a syllable through the binding. Now I will tell you the whole, and the whole is worse than the piece.* The ember flared, sank, steadied. *Your vows, Tamsin Vane. Silence. Service. Surrender. You think you chose them. You think some part of you heard a dying god and knelt. You did not. The vows were written into you when you were nine years old, by men wearing my name, and they are not prayers. They are a leash.*

The world went very still. Somewhere down the beach a night-bird cried and got no answer.

"What do you mean, written into me?"

*The valley was not a refuge. Odell Ferrance will have told you that much by now—he strikes me as a man who tells truths badly and late. Your gift was sealed behind wards before you could walk. But seals decay, and children grow, and a sealed gift leaks. So the wardens needed a second lock. Something the child would carry inside herself, everywhere the wards could not reach.* The pulse slowed, deliberate, like a man choosing each step on rotten ice. *They gave you a god to serve who could never speak. They taught you that your silence was devotion. Every vow you kept was a bar across your own door, and you built them gladly, and you called it faith.*

Tamsin stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand.

"That's a lie," she said.

*It may be,* the ember said gently. *Test it. When have you ever prayed and been answered? When, in twenty years of service, have you felt me? Truly felt me—not the absence you named peace, not the quiet you called holy?*

She wanted to throw the lantern into the surf. Her arm actually drew back.

And then she remembered the shrine at Alder's Ford, the offering bowl full of ash that never blew away. She remembered kneeling there as a girl, bleeding from her first contract, asking for one sign—one—and feeling nothing, absolutely nothing, and being told by the elder that the silence itself was the answer. That faith without answer was the purest faith.

She had believed that. She had *built her life* on that.

Her arm came down slowly.

"Why," she said, and her voice cracked on it, "why tell me now?"

*Because the leash is fraying. Because Corvan spent twenty years tightening it and three weeks chasing us has burned through the last of his binding-words. And because what remains of me cannot survive what comes next unless you hear it freely chosen.* A flare, brief and pained. *I have so little left, keeper. What sits in this lantern is not Vael. It is what was scooped out of Vael when they put him down—an ember raked from a hearth and locked in iron so the hearth could be declared cold. Everything I say costs me. I have been saving up these words for twenty years, spending them drop by drop, and I am nearly empty. So hear me while the saying is cheap.*

Tamsin sat back down. Her legs simply stopped holding her.

"The Inquisitor," she said. "Corvan. He didn't kill you."

*He cannot kill a god. No one can. He did something more efficient. He tried me.* The ember's light turned strange then, reddish at the core, like coals under ash. *Your crown was drowning, keeper. The Sundering was eating the coast, the gifted were blamed for it, and the people wanted someone to burn. And Vael—the true Vael, the whole of him—stood in the great temple and demanded justice. Not order. Justice. He named the lords whose levies had stripped the flood-defenses. He named the ministers who had sold the seawalls' stone. He demanded they be given to the water they had sold, and the crown would not survive that truth, and so the crown reached for a man who understood that gods, like witnesses, can be silenced.*

"Corvan."

*Corvan was young then. Younger than you are now. He wrote the Rite of Stillness himself—he was the finest liturgist of his generation, and he turned his genius to building a cage. Iron, salt, and law. The law was the strongest part.* A dry sound that might have been laughter. *They always forget that. Gods are bound by law more surely than by iron, if the law is written well enough and sworn by enough throats. My silence was not an act of violence. It was an act of legislation.*

Sefa had drifted closer. Tamsin saw her out at the edge of vision, standing with her arms wrapped around herself, listening. She didn't send the girl away. Sefa had the right to hear her father's work described.

"And the altar," Tamsin said. "At the Spire. You said—"

*Only the Spire's altar can unmake what the Spire's rites created. The Stillness was anchored there, in the dragon-fire hearth, because dragon-fire burns through any lesser chain and they needed a fire that would hold a god. The same hearth can free me—or end me. Both doors open from the same hinge, and which one swings depends on the hand that turns it.* The pulse quickened, and Tamsin realized with a lurch that the ember was afraid. *Understand me, keeper. If we reach the altar and the binding is broken whole, everything I am comes back into the world at once. Twenty years of a god's silence, released. Magic returns to Mereth like a tide. And the Door opens—for everyone, for all of them, the old-timers and the children and Odell's apprentices and every soul the valley borrowed its life from.*

"And the cost?"

The ember was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Tamsin thought it had spent itself into silence.

*The cost,* it said finally, *is that I do not know what I am anymore. Twenty years in iron changes a thing. I remember demanding justice and I remember being caged for it, and somewhere between those two memories I may have become small, or cruel, or mad. You would be opening a door for something you cannot see. That is the cost. Not your vows—they were never yours to lose. The cost is trust.*

Tamsin looked at the flame. Really looked, for the first time, not as a god or a mission or a countdown, but as a prisoner in a box, talking to the one person in the world raised to carry it.

"My vows," she said slowly, "were a leash."

*They were my leash, threaded through your throat. Forgive me. I had no other way to keep a fragment of myself near enough to someday reach the altar. I chose a child and I let her love me and I never told her why, because telling her would have let Corvan hear it through the binding. Of all my sins, keeper, that is the one I would take back if the world offered.*

The dune grass hissed. Down the beach, Maren turned over in her sleep. Tamsin found she was crying, which was absurd, which was twenty years late, and she let it happen without wiping her face because there was no one she needed to be hard in front of except a lantern.

"All those contracts," she said. "All those years guarding people who'd burn me if the edict reached them first. I thought I was serving you."

*You were serving you,* the ember said. *Every life you saved was a brick in the road to this shore. Do not retroactively make it chains. A leash and a road can look alike from underneath, and you walked forward the whole way. That was never obedience. That was you.*

From the shadows by the hull, Sefa spoke. "My father wrote that rite."

The lantern flickered toward her voice. Tamsin didn't move.

"He was nineteen," Sefa said. "There's a copy of the Stillness in his field-kit. I've read it a hundred times trying to understand him. It doesn't read like law. It reads like grief." Her jaw worked. "He loved Vael. That's the thing nobody says. Half the hymns in the rite are Vael's own words, turned backward. He didn't build a cage because he hated the god. He built it because he couldn't survive what the god was asking for."

*Yes,* the ember said softly. *Tell her that, daughter-of-Corvan, because I cannot. The tragedy of your father is not cruelty. It is that he loved order more than justice, and he was honest enough to know the difference, and he chose anyway. He will meet us at the Spire believing he is merciful. He will offer bargains. And every bargain he offers will be a smaller cage, beautifully made.*

"How do you know he'll be at the Spire?" Tamsin asked.

*Because he has chased the ember for twenty years and we are finally walking it to the one place it can end. Where else would a hunter stand?*

Odell stirred against the hull, blinked awake, and took in the scene—Tamsin on the sand with wet tracks on her face, the lantern blazing brighter than it had in days, Sefa standing witness. The old cartographer's eyes narrowed.

"It's talking," he said. Not a question.

"It's confessing," Tamsin said.

"Then it's worse than I thought." He hauled himself upright, joints cracking, and came to sit across the fire-ring from her. His gaze on the lantern was the gaze of a man looking at a document he'd hoped was forged. "Ember. Tell her the rest. The part about what happens if the binding breaks and the Door opens *without* the altar."

The ember's light shrank.

*Ferrance.*

"You've had twenty years," Odell said. "She's had three weeks. She deserves the arithmetic."

*The arithmetic,* the ember said, and the dry old voice trembled, *is this, keeper. The Door predates the Stillness. It was built as an exit from Mereth when the Sundering began, and it runs on godfire—any godfire. If the binding breaks at the altar, I can pour myself through it entire and buy passage for a thousand souls. But if the Door is forced open by lesser means—by a cartographer's coordinates and a stubborn woman's will—it will take what godfire it can find. It will take me in scraps. It will spend me blind and hungry, and it will not stop at me. It will drink the gift out of anyone standing close. It will leave the valley's children hollow.*

Silence on the beach. Even the surf seemed to hold off.

"So we do it right," Tamsin said. "Altar first. Then Door."

*Altar first,* the ember agreed. *And pray, keeper—pray to whatever you have left—that we reach it before Corvan does. Because there is a third arithmetic, and I have hidden it even from myself until this moment.* The flame bent low, almost to darkness. *If Corvan reaches the altar first, he will not break the binding. He will renew it. And a renewed Stillness will not merely cage me. It will seal the Door forever, and Mereth will not be a drowned country with an exit. It will be a tomb with the lid nailed shut. Him inside with us.*

Tamsin looked east, where the land fell away toward the drowned caldera, toward a spire she'd never seen but had been walking toward her whole life without knowing it. Two days, maybe three, if the marshes stayed kind. They wouldn't.

"Then we walk tonight," she said.

She closed the lantern's shutters—but not all the way. A finger's width of emberlight escaped, steady now, like something that had finally set down a weight it had carried alone for twenty years.

Behind her, Odell was already rolling his bedroll, muttering coordinates. Sefa was kicking sand over the fire. And Tamsin Vane, unsworn for the first time since she was nine years old, stood up with no god's permission and no god's silence, and found that her legs held.
