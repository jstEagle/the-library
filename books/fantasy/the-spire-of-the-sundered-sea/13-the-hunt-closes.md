## 13. The Hunt Closes

The salt marsh announced itself by smell before sight—a low tide-rot sweetness that crept under doors and into dreams. Sefa had grown up in a house that smelled of ink and lye, where her father scrubbed his hands until they cracked, and she had thought she knew what corruption meant. The marsh taught her otherwise. It was honest corruption. Nothing hid here. Everything rotted in the open, and the reeds grew tall and green out of the ruin of it.

She stood at the edge of a drowned field with Brannoc's borrowed face beside her and Odell's apprentice Tam hunched over the last surviving chart, and she understood that they were trapped. She had known it since dawn, when the egrets went up all at once from the eastern flats, a white rag of birds torn loose against the grey sky. Birds fled gunships too.

"They'll come up the causeway," Tam said, tracing the old road with a cracked fingernail. "It's the only dry line through. Everything else is knee-deep or worse."

"Then we don't take the causeway," Sefa said.

"We can't swim the channels with the lantern. If Vael's ember touches brine—"

"I know." Everyone knew. Tamsin had said it once, quietly, the way she said everything that mattered, and then hadn't slept for two nights watching the lantern like a mother watches a fevered child. "I'm saying we don't be on the causeway when they arrive. We let them pass. We cut south through the peat."

"The peat will swallow us."

"The peat doesn't have guns."

Tamsin came back from the high ground then, moving with that flat economy of hers, and Sefa watched her read the sky the way other people read scripture. Twenty years in the valley. Twenty years of contracts and silence and a god who spoke one word a season, if that. And still she moved like someone who expected to be hunted, because she always had been; she just hadn't known it.

"They're a day behind us," Tamsin said. "Maybe less. Corvan travels light and fast. He'll want to catch us in open ground before the Spire's approaches."

"He won't catch us in open ground," Sefa said. "He'll catch us here. That's why he's driving us this way—the causeway funnels straight into the marsh. He knows these maps better than we do. Better than Odell did."

Nobody answered that. Nobody liked answering things about Odell anymore. His name sat among them like a debt.

* * *

They made camp in the shell of a peat-cutter's hut, walls of stacked turves gone soft as bread, roof half-caved but enough to break the wind off the flats. Sefa took first watch because she couldn't sleep anyway, and because watching was the only prayer she'd ever been taught.

Her father prayed too. She'd seen him at it, kneeling in whatever room they gave him, hands folded not like a penitent but like a man holding something very small and very alive between his palms so it wouldn't escape. She used to think he was praying for the gifted. For mercy. For the children whose names crossed his desk and whose fates he decided with a stroke of red ink.

She had watched him spare a girl in Harrow Fen, three years ago. Publicly, in the square, before the whole assembled village: *This child bears no true taint. The crown's mercy is vast.* He had lifted the girl's chin and smiled, and the village had wept with relief, and Sefa had glowed with pride until she saw him that night, riding out with twelve men to a hamlet called Ashgrave that wasn't on any list, and she had followed, and she had watched the fires go up against the stars, and she had understood that mercy was a costume he wore in daylight and burned off after dark.

Ashgrave had held nine people. Two of them were children. Her father came back smelling of smoke and washed his hands until they bled, and knelt, and prayed, and Sefa had lain awake listening to the water sounds of his washing and understood that she loved a monster, and worse—that the monster was not a monster at all, which would have been simpler. He was a man who believed. Belief made him kind to his horse, generous to his men, gentle with her, and absolutely without limit toward anything that frightened him. And magic frightened him more than death, because magic had killed her mother—or so he told himself every night on his knees, though Sefa had long ago stopped believing the story, if he ever truly believed it either.

A reed sighed. Not wind. Weight.

She was moving before she finished deciding to move, hand on Tamsin's shoulder, and Tamsin came awake the way she did everything—completely, instantly, no transition. Around them the others stirred. Brannoc's face appeared in the dark, lit faintly from within, ember-glow behind the eyes, and even now, even knowing what he was, Sefa's chest clenched at the sight of him. That was the cruelty of it. Whatever the spirit was, it wore grief's own face, and grief disarmed you every time.

"How many?" Tamsin breathed.

Sefa listened. The marsh had its own language: water moving under reed, bird, insect, the far groan of settling peat. Underneath it, rhythmic, patient: hooves. Many. Spread wide in an arc, closing.

"All sides. They've been circling since dusk."

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, and with it came the riders.

They didn't charge. That was the terrible thing—they simply arrived, materializing out of the reed-banks in ones and twos until there were thirty of them, maybe forty, arranged in a loose crescent around the hut with the ease of men who had done this a hundred times and never lost. Grey cloaks, black lacquer armor beneath, the crowned-eye sigil of the Inquisition stamped on their breastplates. Their horses stood hipshot and calm in the shallow water, trained for marsh work, and Sefa recognized the lead mare and hated herself for recognizing it.

Corvan rode forward alone.

He looked older than she remembered. That was absurd—he'd left her only months ago—but age had gotten into him somewhere along the hunt, settled around his eyes and mouth like silt. He wore no helm. His hair, going grey at the temples the same way hers someday would, was tied back plainly. He carried no drawn weapon. He never needed to draw first.

"Sefa." Just her name. He said it the way he'd said it over breakfast a thousand mornings, and her body betrayed her with a flood of memory: him teaching her to ride, him binding her split lip, him standing in the doorway of her room at night when she pretended to sleep, checking, always checking, love expressed as surveillance.

"Father." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"You're well." Not a question. He looked her over as if confirming inventory, and something in his face eased, and that easing was the worst part, because it was real. He was glad to see her alive. She could not doubt it and she could not be consoled by it, both truths sitting in her chest like stones in a bag. "You're thin. You've been eating?"

"There's food on the road."

"There's food everywhere if you know how to take it." A ghost of the old humor. Then it went out of his face, banked like a lamp turned low, and he looked past her at the company ranged in the hut's broken doorway. Tamsin with her scarred hands loose at her sides. The apprentices, pale and clutching each other. Brannoc's spirit, glowing faintly, wearing a dead man's patience.

And the lantern. One of the riders had told him, of course. Word traveled ahead of the Inquisition the way rot traveled ahead of meat.

"That shouldn't exist," Corvan said quietly. "That light was put out twenty years ago. I made certain of it myself."

"You didn't put it out," Sefa said. "You caged it. There's a difference, and I think you know the difference, and I think it's been eating you."

His eyes came back to her. For a moment the Inquisitor fell away entirely and there was only her father looking at his daughter across a drowned field, and she saw how tired he was. Genuinely, humanly tired. She wanted, horribly, to cross the water and touch his face.

"You don't understand what you're carrying," he said. "None of you do. You think it's a god. It's a wound. Mereth was a wound in the world, and everything that poured out of it—everything your cartographer died protecting, everything that child—" his gaze flicked to little Wren among the apprentices, and Sefa saw Tamsin shift, subtly, placing herself between Corvan's attention and the boy— "everything like that is infection. I am not cruel, Sefa. I am a surgeon. Surgeons are hated until the fever breaks."

"Ashgrave wasn't surgery."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt—she'd learned to distinguish them. Guilt would have meant he thought he'd done wrong. This was pain at being seen, which was different, and in its way more damning.

"Ashgrave was necessary," he said, and his voice did not waver, and she understood that he had rehearsed even this, alone, on his knees, holding his small living faith between his palms. "Every hard thing I have done has been necessary. That is my burden and I carry it so that softer people needn't."

"Softer people." Sefa laughed, and heard how ugly it sounded over the water. "Father. You're talking to me. You raised me. Do you really think I'm one of the soft people you do your hard work for? Or am I just the person you come home and wash your hands in front of?"

The riders shifted. Somewhere down the crescent, a horse blew and stamped. Corvan lifted two fingers—not a threat, just a stilling gesture, the same gesture he'd used to quiet her nightmares—and the line went calm.

"I didn't come here to argue theology with my daughter," he said. "I came to offer terms."

"There it is."

"There it is." He gestured, and a rider brought forward a document case, oilcloth-wrapped, official. "Full pardon. Yours and the apprentices'. Registered, protected, placed under my personal guarantee—you know what that means in the capital; no one touches what I guarantee. Schooling. Work. Lives." He paused. "And for you specifically: restoration. Your name cleared of association. You could come home."

Home. The word landed in her like a hook set deep. She thought of the house that smelled of ink and lye. Of the window seat where she'd read while her father worked late, comforted without knowing it just by the sound of his pen.

"And the price," she said.

"Tamsin Vane." He said the name without hatred, which was somehow worse than hatred. "And the ember. Both delivered to me, unharmed, and the Door never opened. That's all. That's everything. You walk away clean and everyone you've gathered walks away with you—except one woman who has been dying since the day she picked up that lantern, and a god that should have stayed dead."

Sefa looked at Tamsin.

Tamsin met her eyes, and did not plead, and did not look away, and did not say *don't do it*. She just looked at her with those valley-flat grey eyes that had watched twenty years pass without blinking, and gave her the smallest nod, as if to say: *this is yours to decide, and whatever you decide, I release you from it.* As if her life were a debt Sefa might honorably default on.

That was the moment Sefa understood the full shape of the offer. Not the pardon. Not the school or the name or the home. The shape of it was this: her father had priced her loyalty exactly, and the price was one life, and he genuinely believed she might pay it, and he was right to believe it, because some cold practical part of her—the part that had survived Ashgrave, the part that kept watch while others slept—was doing arithmetic. Four lives saved for one. A surgeon's math. His math, in her head, already.

"No," she said.

Corvan closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, the father was gone and the Inquisitor stood in his place, and she realized with a strange calm that the two had never actually been separate; the Inquisitor was just the father with the lamp turned up.

"Sefa—"

"No. Listen to me, because I'm only saying this once." She stepped forward, into the shallows, so the water pulled at her boots and the space between them shrank to ten feet of brown water. "You taught me that mercy performed in public and cruelty practiced in private is hypocrisy. You taught me that a debt must be named before it's paid. So here is the debt, named: you burned Ashgrave. Nine people. You wrote the list that put Tamsin Vane's name at the top, and you did it knowing she was a child when the wards closed—knowing she was one of the children you claim to be saving. You caged a god and called it surgery, and you pray every night with your hands bleeding because some part of you knows exactly what you are."

"Sefa." His voice cracked, just slightly, on her name. "I am trying to save you."

"You're trying to save yourself. You want the ember because it's evidence. Because if Vael speaks again—if the Door opens—if anyone learns what the wards really were, then twenty years of your life becomes a crime instead of a duty, and you cannot survive that. Neither can the crown. I understand it, Father. I understand it completely. And I refuse it."

The marsh had gone very quiet. Even the water seemed to hold itself still.

"You'd choose her," he said. "Over your own blood."

"I'm choosing everyone," Sefa said. "That's the difference between us. You choose order and call it everyone. I'm actually choosing everyone—including you, you stupid, brave, ruined man, including you, which is why I'm telling you to stand your riders down and go home and open your ledgers and read them honestly for the first time in twenty years."

For a long moment he said nothing. The water ticked against the horses' legs. Down the crescent, a rider's gauntlet creaked on a rein.

Then Corvan sighed, and it was the sigh she knew from a thousand evenings, the sigh of a man setting down a heavy ledger at the end of a long day, and she knew, with sick certainty, what came next, because she had grown up watching him set down burdens and pick up harder ones.

"I offered," he said, mostly to himself. Then, louder, to his riders, in the flat command voice that brooked nothing: "Take them. The daughter is not to be harmed. The rest—" he paused, and she watched him weigh it, watch the scales move behind his eyes, and she knew he was weighing whether mercy here would cost him more than cruelty. "The rest are to be taken alive if possible."

"If possible," repeated his captain, and the crescent began to close.

* * *

What happened next, Sefa would remember in fragments for the rest of her life.

Brannoc stepping forward onto the water—actually onto it, the surface dimpling but holding under him, fire remembering it had once been a dragon's and dragons answer to no law of buoyancy. His borrowed face turned to Tamsin, and he said something Sefa couldn't hear, and Tamsin's hands came up, and the scars on them lit.

Not metaphorically. Lit—ember-orange seams splitting the skin of her palms and wrists, light pouring out of her like heat out of a cracked furnace door, and the marsh water around her began, impossibly, to steam. The riders' horses screamed and reared. Somewhere a man shouted about sorcery and another shouted to loose, and arrows came out of the grey morning, and Sefa threw herself over little Wren as the world broke into noise and steam and burning light—

—and through it, one image fixed itself in her: her father, in the chaos, not advancing. Standing his mare still amid the panic, watching Tamsin burn with an expression she had never seen on him before, and would spend the rest of the journey turning over and over in her mind.

It wasn't triumph. It wasn't fear.

It was recognition. The look of a man seeing, finally, at close range, the thing he had spent twenty years insisting was an infection—and finding, to his horror, that it looked like a person. Looked, in fact, like his daughter, standing in the shallows with her arms around a child, lit from behind by another woman's impossible fire.

The light swallowed everything.

When it faded, the riders were scattered, unhorsed, blinded; the company was running south into the deep peat with the lantern swinging wild; and Sefa, sprinting, lungs raw, glanced back once and saw her father on his mare in the steaming shallows, motionless, watching them go.

He didn't order pursuit.

Not yet.

And somehow that was the most frightening thing of all.
