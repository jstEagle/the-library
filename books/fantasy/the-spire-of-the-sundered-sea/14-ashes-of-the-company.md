## 14. Ashes of the Company

The salt marsh announced itself by smell before sight — brine and rot and something older underneath, like a grave that had been left open on purpose. Tamsin reined up at the edge of it and let her horse blow.

"Bad ground," Halvard said beside her. Twenty years in the Sundered Valley had turned him grey and patient, a man who'd fought in every border skirmish the crown pretended never happened. He chewed a stalk of marsh-grass like it was a pipe he'd given up. "Knee-deep mud, no cover, one causeway through. If Corvan's riders want us, this is where they take us."

"Then we don't stop," Tamsin said. "We're across before dark."

Dree snorted from behind. She was the youngest blade in what remained of their company, all elbows and opinions, a crossbow she named after every man who'd disappointed her currently loaded with her third-favorite name. "The Inquisitor's been three days behind us since the storm. He'll be six by now. That gunship burned his best riders' nerve."

"The gunship didn't burn anything of Corvan's," Sefa said quietly. She rode at the rear where she always rode, close enough to flee, far enough not to be trusted. Her father's hunt-list was folded against her heart like a knife she kept sharpening. "He's not chasing us. He's herding us."

Nobody answered that, because nobody wanted it to be true. The causeway ran out ahead of them, a spine of ancient stone barely wider than a cart, vanishing into fog that lay on the water like a sheet pulled over a face. Somewhere under that water was old Mereth — streets, temples, the bones of the country the maps refused to remember. Odell had gone quiet when they reached the marsh, quieter than usual, his fingers tracing shapes on his saddle-bow that Tamsin recognized as coastlines.

They crossed in single file. The fog swallowed sound. Hoofbeats became suggestions. Once, far off to the left, something vast moved beneath the surface — not a fish, not a beast, just a displacement of water too large and too deliberate — and Brannoc's voice came out of the air near Tamsin's shoulder, mild as morning.

*That would be the cathedral district.*

She flinched. She was still not used to hearing him. She was never going to be used to hearing him, and some treacherous part of her hoped she never got used to it, because getting used to your dead brother's voice felt like a second kind of dying.

"You could have told me that before," she muttered.

*You didn't ask.* He drifted alongside her horse, translucent as breath on glass, wearing the face he'd had at seventeen before the flood took him and the ember took his shape. *Also, you should know there are riders on the causeway behind us. Six. Maybe eight. They've stopped moving.*

Tamsin raised a fist. The column halted. In the fog behind them, nothing moved, which was itself an answer.

"Halvard," she said.

"I see it." He spat the grass out. "Dree, back fifty paces and look. Everyone else — forward. Fast and quiet."

It went wrong the way things go wrong: quickly, and then all at once.

The first bolt came out of the fog before Dree even shouted. It took Halvard high in the chest, punched him backward off his horse into the black water, and Tamsin heard herself scream his name in a voice she didn't recognize. Then the fog bloomed with torchlight — they'd carried shuttered lanterns, crown-issue, the kind that showed nothing until you opened the slats all at once — and the causeway lit up like a wound.

Eight riders. Crossbows. And at their center, dismounted, walking calmly through the melee with his coat buttoned to the throat: not Corvan. A lieutenant. Younger, hungrier, the kind of man who'd been promised his master's chair if he brought back the last keeper of Vael.

"TAKE THE WOMAN ALIVE!" he roared. "The rest are forfeit!"

What followed took less time than it takes to tell. Tamsin put a knife into the first rider's thigh as he swung past. Dree killed the lieutenant's horse and then the lieutenant's attempt at dignity, rolling off her own mount into the shallows and coming up shooting. Sefa — Sefa moved like someone who'd grown up watching witch-hunters drill and had memorized every mistake they made; she caught a rider's wrist and used his own momentum to fold him into the water.

And Odell Ferrance, sixty years old and built like a folded map, stood in the middle of the causeway with his hands raised and did the only magic available to him: he lied. "REINFORCEMENTS ARE COMING UP FROM THE SOUTH ROAD!" he bellowed, in the parade-ground voice of a man who'd once briefed generals. "SOUND THE HORN!"

For three full seconds, the crown riders believed him. Three seconds was enough for Tamsin to reach Halvard.

He was sitting up in the shallows, which meant he was still alive, which meant there was still something to save. The bolt had gone through below the collarbone. His hands were pressed around it and his blood was coming out between his fingers pink with salt water, and his eyes found hers with the flat calm of a man who had done this arithmetic before, on other battlefields, over other bodies.

"Don't waste it on me," he said.

"Shut up." She got her arms under him. "Shut up and lift."

"Tamsin." He coughed. Water ran pink off his chin. "Twenty years I watched you guard people who couldn't see what you were carrying. Best soldier I ever served with. Worst liar." His mouth twitched toward a smile. "Get them through. That's the contract now. Get them through."

Behind them Dree screamed — not pain, fury — and Tamsin turned in time to see a rider drag the girl half out of the water by her hair, crossbow leveled at her face at the length of an arm. Tamsin threw her knife. It was a good throw. It wasn't good enough. The bolt loosed wild and the rider fell and Dree scrambled free, but another had come around the flank of the fight, and Petch — quiet Petch, who'd joined them at the market town because Odell owed him a debt nobody ever explained — stepped into the arrow's path without hesitating, the way you'd step in front of a child, because that is what Petch was doing, because Dree was nineteen and Petch had decided somewhere on the drowned road that she counted as one.

The bolt took him through the throat. He sat down in the water like a man tired of standing, and did not get up.

Afterward, Tamsin could never assemble those seconds in order. What she remembered was heat.

Not fire-heat. Ember-heat. It started in her sternum where the lantern-gift lived, where twenty years of silent vows had banked it down to coals, and it came up her arms like water rising in a locked room. The fog around her glowed. The salt water at her knees steamed. Every scar she'd ever earned lit pale gold, and the nearest rider's crossbow simply came apart in his hands, its iron limbs unwinding like wire.

"No," she said, to herself, to the gift, to whatever of Vael was looking out through her eyes. "No — not here —"

But the gift did not answer to words anymore. It answered to grief.

Halvard was dying in her arms. Petch was dead in the water. And something in Tamsin tore along a seam twenty years old, and the light came out of her in a ring.

It was not an explosion. Explosions are fast and blind. This was slow, and it saw everything. The light rolled outward across the marsh and where it passed, the crown riders' weapons died — not broke, died, iron going soft as tallow, bowstrings crumbling to ash — and the horses screamed and plunged free of their riders and the riders themselves were thrown down, not burned, not killed, just pressed flat against the causeway by a weight that had nothing to do with gravity and everything to do with judgment. The fog burned away in a circle a hundred yards wide. Underneath them all, briefly, impossibly, the sunken roofs of old Mereth showed through the clear black water: streets, doorways, a chapel bell still hanging in its tower, waiting.

And in the center of it stood Tamsin with her hands raised, and her hands were burning.

She felt it happen the way you feel a brand. The ember-light sank into her palms, into her fingers, drawing lines there — fine gold fissures, branching like frost on a window, like lightning frozen mid-strike, glowing faintly through the skin as though her bones had become lanterns with the shutters open. There was no pain, which frightened her more than pain would have. Pain means the body is arguing. This was her body agreeing.

When the light guttered out, the fight was over. Four riders fled into the fog on foot. Two lay breathing shallowly and would live. The lieutenant was gone — fled early, Tamsin realized, which meant Corvan would know everything by tomorrow.

The cost was written on her hands.

She looked at them for a long time. The golden fissures pulsed gently, dimming and brightening with her pulse, beautiful in the way that wounds are beautiful when they're fresh. She closed her fists. The light leaked out between her fingers anyway.

"Well," said Brannoc softly, at her ear. "That's going to be hard to hide."

* * *

They buried Petch on a hummock above the waterline, because the marsh would not hold a grave and none of them would put him in the water, not after seeing what the water held. Dree dug most of it herself and would not let anyone help, and when it was done she knelt there so long that Sefa finally knelt beside her and put an arm around her shoulders, and Dree let her, which surprised everyone including Dree.

"He stepped in front of me," Dree said finally. Her voice was scraped raw. "He didn't even think about it."

"That was the whole of him," Odell said quietly. "The rest was commentary."

Halvard lasted until midnight. They laid him on dry planking salvaged from a drowned barn, and Sefa did what she could with the wound, which everyone knew was nothing, and Halvard knew better than anyone and was kind enough not to say so. Near the end he gripped Tamsin's wrist — careful, always careful, even dying, to avoid the marks on her hands — and looked past her at the stars coming out over the marsh.

"Get them through," he said again. "That's the contract."

"I will," Tamsin said.

"Swear it proper." His mouth curved. "Silent god. Silent vow. Do it right, Captain."

So Tamsin Vane bowed her head over a dying man in a drowned country and gave Vael's vow — the vow she'd spoken at seventeen with the ember warm in her chest and her whole life ahead of her — and this time she spoke it aloud, into the dark, with no god left to hear it except the fragment wearing her brother's face, and maybe that was enough, and maybe it was everything.

Halvard died between one star and the next, without fuss, the way he'd done everything.

* * *

Dawn found them on the far side of the marsh, on a shelf of higher ground where the drowned road climbed toward hills that were only hills again — real stone, real soil, Mereth's spine rising toward the caldera. Behind them the fog had closed over the graves. Ahead, when the mist thinned, they saw it for the first time: a smudge on the horizon like a tooth, like a needle, like a candle-snuffer set down over the world.

The Spire.

Tamsin sat apart from the others, working a whetstone along a blade that didn't need it, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. The gold fissures caught the sunrise and threw it back in tiny constellations. Dree watched her from across the fire-pit and did not look away, and did not flinch, and Tamsin loved her a little for both.

"It doesn't hurt?" Dree asked at last.

"No."

"Does it scare you?"

Tamsin considered lying. She'd spent twenty years lying — about the vows, about the lantern, about what she was — and the lying had bought the valley its borrowed safety and had bought her nothing but solitude. Halvard had called her the worst liar he'd served with. He'd been wrong. She'd been good enough. That was the problem.

"Yes," she said.

Dree nodded slowly, as if this were the correct answer to some exam. "Good. Petch would've liked that you said it."

Brannoc appeared at the edge of the firelight, insubstantial, his borrowed face turned toward the Spire with an expression Tamsin couldn't read — hunger, or homesickness, or something older than either. When he spoke, only she heard.

*You're spending yourself faster now,* he said. *Every time it wakes, the ember burns lower. You feel it, don't you? Like a candle measured in days.*

She did feel it. She hadn't told anyone.

*The altar can still save it,* Brannoc went on, softer. *Save it, or spend it. But you have to get there before it gutters, Tamsin. Before you do.* His eyes — Brannoc's eyes, brown and quick and seventeen forever — lifted to her scarred hands, then to her face. *Three days, maybe four. After that, the choice stops being yours.*

Across the fire, Sefa unfolded her father's hunt-list one more time, checked the route markings, and frowned. "There's a valley pass ahead," she said. "Single road. If he wants to end this, it's there."

"Then we don't sleep long," Tamsin said, and got up, and led them toward the Spire with her hands full of light.
