## 15. The Spire of the Sundered Sea

They smelled it before they saw it.

Sefa said it first, standing in the bow of the smuggler's skiff with the wind pulling her braid sideways. "Do you smell smoke?"

Tamsin did. Not woodsmoke, not peat. Something older and sweeter, like a hearth that had burned for a thousand years and was now burning down to nothing. It came across the water on a wind that shouldn't have been there—the sea around Mereth had been still as glass since they'd passed through the ward-line three days ago—and it carried with it a warmth that had no source.

Then the fog lifted, and there it was.

The Spire rose out of a drowned caldera like the last tooth of some enormous beast, black stone veined with channels of dull red that pulsed—slowly, faintly, like a heartbeat heard through too many blankets. It must once have blazed. Even dead and dim, even choked with centuries of ash, you could see where the dragon-fire had run through its walls like blood through veins, could imagine the whole structure lit from within, a pillar of flame visible from every coast of the old kingdom. Now those veins glowed the color of coals raked over at night. Almost dark. Not quite.

"That's the hearth," Odell said quietly. He stood at the tiller with his ruined hands wrapped in wool, and he hadn't looked away from the Spire since it appeared. "The dragon-fire hearth. Every map I ever drew of this place, I drew from secondhand accounts. Sailors' rumors. Priests' lies." His voice caught. "It's smaller than I thought."

"Everything true is smaller than the stories about it," Brannoc said.

He sat in the stern, wearing her brother's face the way a man wears borrowed clothes—correctly enough to fool the eye, wrong enough to unsettle the heart. He had been quiet for two days. Since the marsh. Since Tamsin's gift had torn loose from her and burned the Inquisitor's riders back into the water, scarring her hands with lines of ember-light that no glove would cover. He had watched her hands ever since, she realized. Watched them the way a man watches a wound he caused.

Or the way an ember watches a fire it is about to join.

The skiff slid down into the caldera's throat. The water here was impossibly clear—you could see the drowned city below them, streets and stairways and the arches of a great temple, all furred with white coral, all perfectly preserved, like a kingdom holding its breath underwater. The Spire's roots went down through all of it. Somewhere beneath that sunken city, she knew, was the altar. Somewhere beneath everything was the Door.

Nobody spoke as they tied off at a shelf of black rock that had once been a landing quay. There was nothing left to say that the Spire itself wasn't already saying better.

* * *

Inside, the Spire was warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a bed is warm after someone has just left it. The heat came up through the stone floor and pressed against their boots, and the red veins in the walls brightened slightly as they climbed—as if the building noticed them, remembered what visitors were, tried its best to be hospitable with almost nothing left in the pantry.

They climbed for hours. The stair wound upward inside the Spire's hollow core, past galleries where the ash of ages lay ankle-deep, past alcoves where offerings had been left and never collected: a child's shoe. A soldier's medal gone green with salt. A jar of honey, sealed, its wax unbroken. Gifts to a god who had stopped answering, left by people who kept leaving them anyway. That, more than anything, made Tamsin's chest ache. She knew that kind of faith. She had lived it for twenty years, kneeling before a bowl of ash, calling silence holy.

Sefa walked behind her with the hunt-list rolled in her sash and her father's signet ring on a cord around her neck, and she didn't touch either one. Odell counted steps under his breath, mapping as he went, because a man like Odell Ferrance could not help himself even now, even here, even with his life's work burned on a causeway. And Brannoc—

Brannoc climbed like someone climbing toward something. Not with them. Ahead of them, always a few steps ahead, his borrowed face turned up toward whatever waited at the top, and when the light from the dying hearth touched him, Tamsin saw that it didn't cast a shadow.

She had known, she realized. Some part of her had known since the sunken chapel, since the moment the water opened and gave her back her brother's smile. You don't get your dead back. Everyone in the Sundered Valley knew that; it was the first lesson grief taught and the last lesson it repeated. But knowing a thing and being forced to say it aloud are different countries, and she had been living in the first one for a long time, and the stair was carrying her steadily out of it.

The stair ended at a doorway tall as a cathedral's mouth.

Beyond it, the heart of the Spire: a vast round chamber open to the sky, floored in black stone worn smooth by ten centuries of feet, ringed by pillars carved with dragons mid-flight. At its center, sunk into the floor, was the hearth—a basin wide as a threshing floor, and in it, the last of the dragon-fire.

It was nearly cold.

What remained was a skin of embers spread thin across the basin's bottom, glowing a deep bruised orange, breathing so slowly that between one pulse and the next you could believe it had finally died. The heat that rose from it was the heat of a thing remembering warmth rather than possessing it. Ash drifted up from the basin in slow spirals and hung in the air without falling, as if even gravity had grown gentle here out of respect.

And in the middle of the embers, small and stubborn and unmistakable, burned a single point of light that was not dragon-fire at all.

The ember. Vael's ember. The god-ember that had guttered in an offering bowl in a shrine in the Sundered Valley and spoken a single word—*Door*—and set all of this in motion. It had traveled in a lantern at Tamsin's hip for a hundred leagues, and now, forty feet above the basin, it strained against the lantern's glass like a moth against a window, leaning toward the hearth, toward the altar beneath it, home.

"It knows where it is," Sefa whispered.

"Of course it does," Brannoc said. "It's been coming here longer than any of us."

* * *

Tamsin walked to the edge of the hearth alone.

The others stayed back—Sefa with her arms crossed against a chill she wouldn't admit to, Odell with his hands pressed flat against the warm stone as though taking its pulse, memorizing it. Tamsin knelt at the rim of the basin the way she had knelt at a hundred shrines, and unhooked the lantern, and held it up, and for a long moment simply looked at the little light inside it.

Twenty years. Twenty years of silence and vows and a god who never answered, and here at the end of everything, the silence turned out to have been a prison cell, and the god a prisoner, and she its unwitting guard. Corvan's order had done that—she knew it now, had carried that knowledge since the lantern first spoke. The Inquisitor hadn't killed Vael. He had locked it away, and let the world believe the lock was a grave.

"I'm here," she said aloud. Her voice sounded very small in the vast chamber. "I got it here. Whatever happens next—I want you to know I got it here."

The ember flared, gently, the way a hand squeezes yours.

"Tamsin."

Brannoc's voice. Close. Too close—he had come up beside her without her hearing, which should not have been possible, which had never once happened in twenty years of soldiering. She didn't turn. She watched the light in the lantern instead, and the light watched her, and between them Brannoc stood with her brother's face full of a sorrow that no living face of his had ever worn.

"You should sit down," he said.

"I've walked four days through flooded ruins. I'll stand."

"Tamsin." He said her name again, softer, the way he'd said it when they were children and she'd scraped her knee and refused to cry in front of the older kids. "There's no easy way to tell you this. So I'll tell it plain, the way you'd want."

The ash spiraled. The embers breathed. Somewhere far below, under the drowned city, the Door waited in its dark.

"The chapel," he said. "When the water took me. You think you pulled me out and I slipped away anyway. You've carried that—the half-second, the fingers slipping through yours. You've built twenty years on that half-second."

"Don't," she said.

"He drowned, Tamsin. Clean and quick, the way drowning goes. There was nothing to save and nothing that suffered. What came out of the water to find you afterward—what has walked beside you these last weeks—" He stopped. Started again. "I am not Brannoc. I never was."

The words landed the way a blade lands. No pain at first. Just the strange cold fact of it, the geometry of the wound opening.

"What are you?" she asked. Her voice came out level. Soldier's voice. Later there would be room for the other voice, but not yet.

"I'm the last fragment of the ember." He lifted his hands—her brother's hands—and looked at them as though seeing them clearly for the first time, or admitting he had always seen them. "When Corvan's order imprisoned Vael, the god broke apart. Most of it went into the lantern-bowl. Into exile, asleep, wordless. But one piece tore free. One piece stayed awake, and hid, and spent a century learning how to take shape. And the only shape it could learn—the only shape with enough love in it to hold a spirit together—was a memory. Yours. Your grief for your brother. I wore it because it was the strongest thing in you, and I needed strength to walk this road."

"You needed a guide," Tamsin said. "You told us the way to the Spire."

"I *am* the way to the Spire." He smiled, and it was Brannoc's smile, and it broke her heart cleanly, like ice cracking. "Don't you understand yet? Look at the hearth. Look at how cold it is. Dragon-fire doesn't die of age, Tamsin. It dies of hunger. The hearth needs feeding. The altar needs a key. The Door needs an ember entire—and I am the piece that's been missing. I led you here because I had to come home. All of me. Even this piece. Even the piece wearing your brother's face."

The lantern trembled in her grip. Inside it, the ember burned steady and low, and she understood suddenly that it had known—that it had known since the chapel, since the beginning, that the spirit walking beside them was its own lost flesh, and it had said nothing because what would she have done? Turned him away? Left a fragment of her god to wander the ruins alone?

Would she have refused her brother's face, even knowing?

"No," she said, and then louder, to the chamber, to the hearth, to the god in the lantern: "No. I won't do it. I didn't carry him across a country just to burn him in a furnace. Find another way. Take my gift, take my years, take—"

"There is no other way," Brannoc said gently. "There was never another way. That's why I couldn't tell you sooner. You'd have turned back. And the valley can't turn back—it's dying, Tamsin, you know it is. The wards are failing, the hunters are inside, the crown has put your neighbors' names on lists. The Door is the only mercy left in Mereth, and the Door costs what it costs."

"And what does it cost?" Her voice cracked at last, and she let it. "Tell me exactly."

He looked at the hearth. At the lantern. At her scarred hands, the ember-light in them pulsing in time with the little flame, in time with him.

"Everything that burns," he said. "Me. The ember. The hearth. The whole of what's left of Vael, poured through the altar at once. One fire, spent entirely, to open the Door and hold it open until everyone is through." He met her eyes. "And after that, nothing. No god. No ghost. No brother. Silence—not the enforced kind, not the prison kind. Just... rest. Real rest, at the end of a long fall."

Tamsin knelt there at the rim of the dying hearth and felt twenty years rearrange themselves inside her. The silent god. The empty offering bowl. The brother who slipped through her fingers—all of it, every silence in her life, turning out to be one silence, one long falling darkness, and she had been walking beside a piece of it this whole time, loving it, unable to stop loving it even now.

Especially now.

"When I judged the ember," she said quietly, "at the trial—when I asked it whether it abandoned Mereth—it showed me the drowning. It showed me the children it couldn't reach. Do you remember what it said after? Its last words before it went quiet again?"

"I remember," Brannoc said.

"It said: *the debt is mine.*" She looked up at him, at the face the dark had borrowed to soften its own ending. "So this is how a god pays its debts. With itself. And it sent the payment wearing the face I'd trust most."

"I chose the face," Brannoc said softly. "That part was mine. If I was going to spend my last hour breaking your heart, I wanted—" He faltered, the first time she had ever seen him falter. "I wanted to break it as someone you loved. It seemed kinder than arriving as a stranger."

Behind her, boots on stone. Sefa, close now, her hand hovering near Tamsin's shoulder without landing. Odell farther back, hat in his hands, an old man bareheaded before the last fire of a dead kingdom.

"Tamsin," Sefa said. "The water. Look at the water."

Tamsin turned.

Far below, at the caldera's rim, lights were moving along the shore of the drowned city. Lantern-light, many lanterns, arranged in the disciplined grid of a military column. And cutting through the evening stillness, faint but unmistakable, a sound she had hoped never to hear again so soon:

A hunting horn.

Corvan had found them. The Inquisitor had followed the smell of smoke all the way to the end of the world, and he was climbing.

Brannoc looked at the lights, and then at the hearth, and then at Tamsin, and her brother's face settled into something calm and ready and terribly young.

"Well," he said. "Best not keep the Door waiting."
