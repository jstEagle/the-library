## 16. Corvan at the Threshold

Inquisitor Corvan had burned one hundred and six heretics in his thirty years of service to the crown, and he had never once enjoyed it.

That was the first thing Sefa had never understood about him, and perhaps the last thing he would ever be able to teach her: that the fire was not a pleasure. It was a ledger. Every flame was an entry, every entry a debt, and the crown's debt to the world was so old and so vast that no honest accounting could ever balance it. He had simply agreed, long ago, to keep writing in the book.

Now he stood at the edge of the drowned caldera with his cloak heavy with salt spray and his boots sinking into black sand that remembered being a mountain, and he looked up at the Spire of the Sundered Sea for the first time in twenty years.

It had not gotten smaller. That was the trouble with the things you tried to forget. They waited, and they kept their size.

"Riders hold here," he said.

His captain, a broad woman named Hesk with frost-scars on both cheeks from the northern campaigns, glanced at the caldera rim, at the narrow causeway descending into the bowl of the drowned sea, at the Spire rising out of the gray water like a needle someone had driven through the world's heart to pin it shut.

"You're going down alone," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd served him nine years; she knew when the order was an order.

"I am."

"The girl—"

"Sefa is not yours to take." He said it flatly, without heat, because heat would have invited argument. "If any hand is laid on her, I will know whose, and I will deal with that hand personally. We wait until dawn. If I have not returned by dawn—" he paused, weighing what came after, and found there was no honest way to finish the sentence. "If I have not returned by dawn, withdraw to the garrison at Corrow Ford."

Hesk studied him a moment longer than discipline strictly allowed. Then she saluted, fist to breastplate, and turned back to the line of riders huddled against the salt wind among the rocks.

Corvan went down alone.

* * *

The causeway had been a pilgrim road once. You could still see it under the silt and the salt crust: the wide flagstones, the low pillars where lanterns had hung, the grooves worn into the stone by ten thousand knees and feet. Mereth had come here to worship. Mereth had come here to ask its god for rain, for harvests, for mercy, and the god had answered through its ember in the Spire's high hearth, and the country had believed itself loved.

Corvan had been a young man the last time he made this walk. A junior inquisitor with a new commission and a head full of law, climbing these same stones toward an audience with a god.

He had been so certain then. That was the thing about certainty nobody warned you about—it didn't feel like arrogance from the inside. It felt like clarity. It felt like finally seeing the shape of the world whole.

He reached the Spire's base as the light failed, and found the door open.

Of course it was open. The Door inside was sealed; the door outside had never needed to be. Vael had wanted no barriers between itself and its petitioners. *Come all ye who carry,* the old liturgies said, *for silence hears everything.*

He climbed.

The stair wound upward through the Spire's hollow gut, past galleries furred with salt-bloom and bird-nests, past niches where votive lamps had guttered out centuries ago and never been replaced, because the last keeper had died and no one came after. His breath fogged. The air grew warmer as he rose—not stove-warmth, not fire-warmth, but something older, a residual heat like a forge gone cold that morning rather than a year ago.

At the top, the altar chamber opened around him like a held breath.

They were already there. All four of them, arranged around the cold hearth as if they'd rehearsed the tableau: the mercenary woman Tamsin Vane standing closest to the altar with her scarred hands loose at her sides, the ember-light in those hands dimmed but unmistakable; the runaway daughter beside her, arms crossed, chin up, wearing her defiance like armor two sizes too big; the old cartographer leaning on a staff near the wall, watching Corvan with the flat appraisal of a man pricing a horse he intended to buy cheap; and the fourth—

The fourth stood half in shadow, and wore Brannoc Vane's face, and Corvan felt his stomach tighten at the sight of it even though he had known, had always known, what lived in the deep places of the Sundered Valley and what masks it might wear. Dragon-fire did not die. It only changed hands.

"Inquisitor," Tamsin Vane said. Her voice carried in the great chamber without effort. Twenty years of silence had taught her how to make small sounds into large ones. "You've come a long way to burn us. The hearth is cold. You'll have to bring your own wood."

"I haven't come to burn anyone."

Sefa laughed—a short, hard sound with nothing in it but hurt. "That's a new one. What's the sermon, then? Repentance? Registration? One last chance to kneel before the crown forgives us with fire?"

"Sefa." He said only her name, and watched it land, and hated himself for how well he still knew where to strike. "I'm not speaking to you yet."

"Then who—"

"To her." He inclined his head toward Tamsin Vane. "Keeper of the ember. Last sworn of the silent god. I've come to bargain."

The cartographer shifted against the wall. The brother-thing in the shadows did not move at all, which was worse.

"Bargain," Vane repeated.

"You have something the crown wants. I have something you want. I thought we might trade like civilized people, for once in this miserable history." He unslung the case from his shoulder—the long leather case he had carried across three hundred miles of drowned country—and set it on the altar step. He did not open it. Not yet. "But first, you should understand what you're holding. All of you. Because none of you do. Not even you, Keeper. Especially not you."

"We know enough," the cartographer said. "We know your edicts. We know your hunt-lists. We know what you did to the valley."

"Do you?" Corvan looked at the old man steadily. "Do you know why?"

Silence. The good kind, he thought grimly—the kind that meant he had their attention. He had spent thirty years learning to use silence as a blade. It seemed fitting, now, to use it in service of the truth.

"Then let me tell you a story," he said. "Since we've all come such a long way."

* * *

"I was twenty-three years old," Corvan said, "and a junior inquisitor attached to the Chancery of Gifts, when I first read the Drowning Reports. Do any of you know what the Drowning Reports were?"

No one answered. Sefa's jaw was set. The cartographer's eyes had narrowed.

"No. Of course not. The crown buried them within a decade, and buried the burying-men after that." He began to walk, slowly, along the curve of the altar chamber, because stillness made men defensive and motion kept them listening. "Mereth was not drowned by accident. That much, I think, even the valley's stories got right. But the stories say the sea rose in wrath, or the wards failed, or the gods turned away. The truth is simpler and far more damning. The truth is that the sea rose because the gifted of old Mereth pulled it up—to drown the war they couldn't stop, and the weapons that wouldn't stop burning, and the plagues the chancellors kept deploying after the healers begged them to stop.

"They saved the world," Corvan said, "by drowning their own country. And then they raised the wards over the last pocket of dry land, hid it from every map and every eye, and filled it with their children. The gifted children. So that when the world forgot magic—and the world was determined to forget it, the world has never forgiven magic for the war—you see, the gift would survive somewhere. Seeded. Sleeping. In hiding."

"And the crown?" the cartographer asked quietly.

"The crown learned the truth forty years ago. A surveyor's error. A green flicker at a ward-stone, the same omen your Keeper saw last spring." He nodded at Tamsin. "Yes. We've been watching the valley since before you took your first contract, Vane. And when the Chancery understood what the valley was—who was in it—they faced a choice. Expose it, and watch every frightened village in nine kingdoms demand the burning of every gifted soul in it. Or contain it. Quietly. Permanently."

"So they sent witch-hunters," Sefa said.

"I sent witch-hunters," Corvan said. "I wrote the containment doctrine. I built the registry. I designed the edicts you've spent this whole journey cursing." He stopped walking. "Because I was the one who found out what Vael wanted."

The name landed in the chamber like a stone dropped down a well. Even the brother-thing stirred in its shadows.

"The ember spoke to me," Corvan said. "Once. Directly. Through a dying priestess I was interrogating—I won't soften that word; she was dying whether I questioned her or not, and the answers mattered to more lives than hers. And Vael came up through her like water through a crack, and it looked at me with whatever a god uses instead of eyes, and it told me what justice required."

He let the pause stretch.

"It told me the drowning was unfinished. That the war's weapons still slept under the sea, that the plagues still breathed in the deep trenches, that the drowning had been a surgery and the surgery had been left half-done—and that a god of silence owed the world the rest of the operation. It wanted me to help it wake the sea again. Finish the work. Drown the coastlands. Nine kingdoms' worth of shore, tens of millions of souls—because a god's arithmetic does not count people, Keeper. It counts debts. And in Vael's ledger, the living world still owed a debt for what Mereth's chancellors did, and silence does not forgive."

Tamsin Vane's voice, when it came, was very quiet. "You're lying."

"I have never lied about Vael," Corvan said. "Not once in thirty years. Ask yourself, Keeper. Ask the thing you've prayed to your whole life. Ask it why it never answered you. Ask it why your vows felt like a leash. Ask it why a god of silence needed a mercenary to guard grain caravans while it slept in a shrine bowl." He saw the blow land—he had spent his life reading faces across interrogation tables, and hers was no harder to read than any other, grief and faith tearing along the same seam. "Your god was not silenced by my order, Vane. Your god was *contained*. By me. Because I was the only man in the Chancery who understood that killing it would free it—that an ember cannot be extinguished, only moved or caged—and that letting it act would end nine kingdoms. I built the cage. I called it silence. I have kept it locked for thirty years, and I have signed death warrants for a hundred and six people to keep anyone from ever opening it, and I would sign a hundred and six more tomorrow, and I will burn in whatever hell keeps accounts for men like me, and it will have been worth it."

The words rang off the salt-white walls and faded.

Nobody spoke. The wind found some high aperture in the Spire and moaned through it, a low note like a struck bell heard underwater.

* * *

It was Sefa who broke it, and of course it was Sefa, because she had inherited his inability to leave a silence unfilled.

"The child," she said. "The gifted child in Marrow's Bend. The one you spared on the scaffold steps in front of everyone. I saw it. I was eleven. You picked her up and you carried her out of the crowd and you told them the gift was a sickness and she was in remission." Her voice shook. "Was that real? Was any of it real?"

Corvan closed his eyes.

"Marrow's Bend," he said. "Yes. I remember." He opened his eyes and looked at his daughter across the width of the altar chamber, across the width of everything that lay between them now. "Her name was Ilse. She was six years old. Her gift was weather-sense—she could taste storms coming three days out. Worthless, militarily. Harmless, entirely. The local magistrate wanted her burned because the crowd wanted her burned, because fear wants feeding, and a magistrate who doesn't feed it gets eaten." He exhaled slowly. "So I performed mercy. Publicly. Because a public sparing buys credibility, and credibility buys restraint, and restraint—my kind of restraint—is the only reason there are not pyres in every square in nine kingdoms. I have spent thirty years performing a cruelty so thorough that it looks like mercy from the inside, Sefa. And I have spent thirty years performing just enough mercy to keep the cruelty from devouring everything. Both performances were true. That's what no one understands. Both were true."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "You can't just—both can't be true."

"They can," Corvan said gently, "when the world is broken badly enough. That is the first lesson of power, and I am sorry, truly sorry, that you learned it from me instead of from a book."

He turned from her, deliberately, and faced Tamsin Vane, because the bargain had to be offered to the Keeper and he would not dishonor it by offering it sideways.

"Here is the truth I've brought to trade, Vane. The full truth. The Drowning Reports, complete. The names of the ward-raisers. The location of every sleeping weapon under the Sundered Sea. The Chancery's contingency plans—including the one that activates if the Spire's hearth relights, which involves three gunships and enough fire-oil to glass this caldera. And the one thing no document contains, because I am the only man alive who carries it: the binding-word. The word that holds Vael's cage shut." He rested his hand on the long leather case. "All of it. Yours. Freely given."

"And in exchange?"

"The ember entire. Delivered to me, sealed, and surrendered to the Chancery's deep vault at Corrow Ford, where it will sleep until the crown itself crumbles. No relighting. No trial. No altar. No Door." He held her gaze. "Or refuse, and I withdraw, and the gunships come at dawn anyway, and you learn whether a god's arithmetic counts gunships in its ledger. Those are the terms. I won't pretend they're generous. I'll only swear they're honest."

"The Door," Tamsin said. Her hands had curled at her sides, and the ember-light in them pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat deciding. "You know that's what we mean to do. Open it. Let the valley's people out. End the hiding forever."

"Yes."

"And you'd stop it. Cage the ember. Keep the valley hidden. Keep my people buried in a country that isn't on any map—for their own good, the way you've kept everything, for everyone's own good, for thirty years."

"For everyone's survival," Corvan said. "Yes. That is exactly what I would do. And I will tell you the last truth, Keeper, the one beneath all the others, the one I have never said aloud in thirty years." He drew a breath, and found it heavier than expected. "I am tired. God help me, I am so tired. I have held this cage shut with my bare hands for half my life, and I have watched my daughter grow up afraid of me, and I have burned a hundred and six people whose only crime was carrying a spark of the thing I'm protecting them from, and every year the cage grows weaker and the cost grows higher and the arithmetic gets worse. If you can show me another path—one that saves the valley without waking the sea—then in the name of every dead priestess and every burned child, show me. But if the price of your Door is Vael loose in the world again, then no. I will pay anything, do anything, become anything, to stop it. That is my confession. Judge it how you like."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind in the high aperture and the sea breathing against the Spire's roots far below.

Then the brother-thing stepped out of the shadows.

It moved like a man, but the firelight from Tamsin's hands passed through its edges wrong, the way light passes through smoke, and when it spoke in Brannoc Vane's warm river-dredged voice, the words came out with a patience no living man owned.

"He offers you a cage, little sister," it said. "And he calls the cage honesty. Ask him what happened at the shrine at Coldharbor. Ask him what he burned there, and why the ash was still singing when the rain came."

Corvan went very still.

Because there had been no witnesses at Coldharbor. There had been no survivors at Coldharbor.

He had made sure of it himself.
