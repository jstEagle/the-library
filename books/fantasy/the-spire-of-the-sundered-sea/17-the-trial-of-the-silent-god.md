## 17. The Trial of the Silent God

The altar was a slab of black glass, and it remembered fire.

Tamsin felt that memory through her boots as she climbed the last stair of the Spire—a heat that wasn't heat, an old insistence in the stone like a scar that still ached when weather turned. The caldera below breathed cold. The dragon-fire hearth at the Spire's heart had been dying for twenty years, maybe longer, and its death had soaked down through the rock until even the stairs were the temperature of grief.

Behind her came the others. Sefa's breath was quick and shallow; she kept one hand on the knife at her hip out of habit, though there was nothing left up here to fight. The two apprentices Odell had saved from the marsh—Pell and young Annet—clung to each other near the doorway, too frightened to come closer, too stubborn to leave. And Brannoc—what was left of him—walked beside her with his dead brother's easy stride, his face lit from within now, faintly, like paper held over a candle.

He had confessed on the hearth-stone below. *I am the ember's last fragment. I wore your grief because grief is the only door you never locked.* She had not forgiven him yet. She had not refused him either. Both of those debts could wait.

The lantern in her hand did not wait.

It had been speaking since the marsh, since Corvan's order had been burned off it like paint stripped from iron, but here, at the altar, it spoke the way a river speaks when the dam breaks—all at once, all of it, everything it had swallowed for two decades.

*TAMSIN.*

Vael's voice filled the dome of the Spire. Not loud. That was the horror of it—it didn't need to be loud. It simply occupied the space the way a king occupies a throne room, the way silence occupies a shrine. Her knees wanted to bend. Twenty years of vows pulled at her spine like hooks set deep in the meat: *kneel, lower your eyes, be small before the god.*

She stayed standing.

"I'm here," she said. "You've got my attention. Say what you brought me up a drowned mountain to say."

*YOU WERE SWORN TO ME.*

"I was sworn to something," Tamsin said. "I'm starting to wonder if it was ever you."

The light in the lantern guttered—not in fear, she thought, but the way a man shifts his weight before a hard conversation. Around them the black-glass altar began to warm. Faint lines woke across its surface, hairline veins of amber, spreading outward from the center like roots, like roads, like the faded ink of a map Odell once showed her—the drowned kingdom drawn in fire instead of ink.

And then the altar began to speak back.

***

It came as images first, pressed flat against the inside of her eyes.

Mereth whole. Mereth alive. A country of green terraces stepping down to a silver sea, of towers built around living flame, of children born with light under their skin who were not hidden, not hunted, not registered—cherished. She saw the Spire in its fullness, the caldera breathing warmth instead of cold, the great hearth roaring with dragon-fire that had been tended, generation upon generation, by keepers in gray who took no name and made no sound.

Keepers like her. Sworn like her. Silent like her.

Then the water came.

Not a wave. A slow catastrophe, a sea deciding to move inland over the course of a single terrible season, magic gone wrong somewhere far away—she couldn't see the cause, only the consequence, villages drowning by inches, refugees climbing toward high ground that shrank daily. She saw the crown of Mereth send riders to the Spire demanding the dragon-fire be spent to hold back the sea. She saw the keeper of that age kneel before the altar and ask the god.

She saw the god refuse.

*THE SEA WAS NOT MY WORK,* Vael said, and the voice was defensive now, and defense is a thing gods should not need. *MY FIRE WAS FOR THE ALTAR. FOR THE DOOR. FOR THE COVENANT BETWEEN MERETH AND THE WORLD BEYOND IT.*

"And the people?" Tamsin heard herself ask. Her voice sounded very small in the dome, and very hard. "What covenant covered them?"

*THE CROWN CHOSE TO SPEND THE FIRE ON WARDS. TO HIDE THE GIFTED CHILDREN. I CONSENTED. THE CHILDREN WERE SAVED.*

"The children," Tamsin said slowly, "were walled into a valley with no name, where they grew old farming grain and hiring themselves out as caravan guards, and every one of them died without ever seeing the sky their mothers were born under." She lifted the lantern. "That was the mercy. I've walked through it my whole life. It smells like wet wool and pig shit."

Sefa made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a sob.

*THE ALTERNATIVE WAS DROWNING.*

"There were other alternatives." Tamsin's hands were shaking. She let them shake. "Odell told me about the Door. The Door was always there. You sat on this mountain with enough fire to open it—for anyone, for everyone—and you gave it to nobody. You let the crown raise wards instead. You let a country drown quietly so you wouldn't have to spend yourself."

*I AM ONE EMBER. THE DOOR TAKES ALL OF IT.*

"Yes," Tamsin said. "So does a god's pride."

***

The amber veins in the altar flared, and the images changed.

Now she saw what Vael had not chosen to show her. She saw the years after the drowning—the valley sealed, the wards humming, the god reduced to a whisper in a shrine-bowl because the crown's new order, hungry and afraid, had decided that a god who would not serve the crown was a god to be managed. She saw Inquisitor Corvan, younger, harder-faced than Sefa had ever painted him, standing in a burning shrine with a warrant written on vellum made from the hide of something Tamsin didn't want to identify.

She expected to see cruelty. What she saw instead was procedure. Corvan hadn't hated Vael. He had filed Vael away, the way a clerk files a debt he cannot collect—*the god demands justice for the drowned; the crown cannot survive justice for the drowned; therefore the god must be silenced.* She heard him speak the words aloud to his officers, weary, almost apologetic: *"We do not burn the god because it lies. We burn it because it tells the truth, and the truth is unaffordable."*

Beside her, Sefa went white. "That's his voice," she whispered. "That's exactly—he used to say things like that at supper. Like apologies counted as reasons."

"They don't," Tamsin said. But her own certainty had developed a crack in it, and she hated Corvan a little for that, too.

Because here was the wound beneath the wound: Vael had been silenced for demanding justice. And Vael's justice had been—what? A word dropped into an offering bowl after twenty years of silence. *Door.* One word, wrung out through the cracks of its prison, while the people it had abandoned to borrowed lives knelt before its bowl and gave it the best of their grain.

A god silenced unjustly was a tragedy. A god that had earned its silence was something else entirely.

The altar seemed to arrive at the same thought. The amber veins pulsed once, twice, and settled into a steady glow, and the presence in the lantern drew itself up the way a prisoner draws himself up before a tribunal he did not expect to face.

*WHAT WOULD YOU DO WITH ME, KEEPER?*

The question rang in the dome. Tamsin realized, with a strange floating clarity, that this was the trial. Not some rite written in old books, not a formula of candles and blood—the ember freed from Corvan's leash had climbed its own mountain and put itself, deliberately, in front of the one person in the world with the most reason to hate it and the most authority to forgive it. The wounded rendering judgment on the wounder. No law above her. No crown, no ward, no vow.

Just her. Just twenty years of kneeling.

"You want me to judge you," Tamsin said.

*I WANT SOMEONE TO. CORVAN JUDGED ME WITHOUT LISTENING. THE CROWN JUDGED ME WITHOUT COURAGE. YOU LISTENED. YOU HAVE THE COURAGE. AND YOU ARE THE LAST OF MY KEEPERS, WHICH MAKES YOU THE ONLY COURT I HAVE LEFT.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was smaller. *I HAVE BEEN SILENT SO LONG THAT I NO LONGER KNOW IF WHAT REMAINS OF ME DESERVES TO SPEAK.*

Tamsin looked at Brannoc.

His borrowed face was unreadable—of course it was; it was a fragment wearing a mask wearing a memory. But his eyes, which were her mother's eyes, which had watched her across a kitchen table when they were children, were fixed on her with something that looked desperately like hope. He was part of this. Whatever she decided about the god, she was deciding about him.

She thought about the valley. About Marda Ellery, who had taught her to gut a fish and never once mentioned the light that used to live under her skin. About old Tam Broadhurst, who paid mercenary wages in eggs and honest gratitude, who had been one of the gifted children, who had spent sixty years believing the valley was sanctuary because the truth—that it was a cage built as a kindness—would have broken him. About the herald's proclamation nailed to the meeting-house door, and the neighbors looking at the old-timers like inventory.

About the Door. About what it would cost.

"Here's my judgment," Tamsin said, and the dome carried her voice down into the cold caldera, down to the dying hearth, out over the drowned ruins of a kingdom that no map remembered. "You failed us. Not by being weak—one ember against a sea, fine, I understand arithmetic. You failed us by choosing survival over service. You kept your fire safe in a mountain while your people drowned by inches, and then you let a crown bury you rather than testify to what it did, and the people who kept faith with you anyway—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—the ones who knelt at your empty bowl for twenty years, they got nothing. Silence. You gave us silence and called it holiness."

The lantern flickered low.

"But," Tamsin said, and the word cost her more than any vow ever had, "you were also the only god in this whole rotten story who ever asked to be judged instead of worshipped. Corvan silenced you to protect a crown. You're handing me the knife to judge you fairly. That's not nothing. That might be the first honest act any power has performed in this country since the water came in."

She set the lantern on the altar. The amber veins rose to meet it like tide to a mooring post.

"So here is what I render. I do not restore you. I do not worship you. I will not rebuild your shrines or re-swear your vows or teach another generation to kneel to a silence." She heard Pell gasp behind her; the boy had grown up on stories of the silent god, everyone in the valley had. "But I do not extinguish you either. You asked for justice, and justice isn't vengeance. Justice is being seen clearly and given exactly what you've earned—no more, no less. What you've earned is to be *spent*. All of it. On the Door. On the people you should have saved the first time."

Silence in the dome. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

*AND IF I REFUSE?* the ember asked. There was no menace in it. Only exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion, something that sounded terribly like relief.

"Then you stay in the lantern until the lantern rots, and you watch from a shelf while the world gets along without gods," Tamsin said. "Your choice. It's the only one you get. I'm done taking orders from altars."

Brannoc stepped forward. Up close, the light under his skin moved like something swimming. "Tam," he said, in her brother's voice, and she flinched. "Ask it. Ask it what it wants. Not what it deserves."

She looked at the lantern. At the little guttering point of gold behind the glass, smaller now than a candle-flame, smaller than anything that had once commanded a nation's kneeling.

"What do you want?" Tamsin asked. "Truthfully. Last time. No performance."

The flame bent toward her, the way a flame bends toward breath.

*REST,* Vael said. *OH, KEEPER. I HAVE WANTED REST SINCE BEFORE YOUR VALLEY HAD A NAME. EVERY PRAYER WAS A HAND SHAKING ME AWAKE. I STAYED LIT BECAUSE DYING FELT LIKE FAILING THEM AGAIN. BUT IF THE FIRE CAN BUY THEM A DOOR—* the flame steadied, and the voice with it, calm as deep water— *THEN LET IT BURN DOWN TO NOTHING, AND LET ME SLEEP IN WHAT IT BUYS.*

Tamsin closed her eyes. Twenty years of vows slid off her like a coat she'd outgrown—*be silent, be small, be the god's hand*—and underneath them she found, to her surprise, not emptiness but space. Room to stand up straight.

"Then it's agreed," she said. "Court adjourned."

***

They came down from the altar into the cold heart of the Spire, and that was when Sefa stopped dead on the stairs.

"Tamsin." Her voice had gone thin. She was looking past them all, down through the Spire's broken eastern wall, across the black water of the caldera. "Riders. On the causeway. A lot of them."

Tamsin followed her gaze. Lantern-light strung along the drowned road like beads on a wire, moving fast, disciplined. And at the head of it, a standard she knew now—the crowned eye of the Inquisition, its lid half-lowered.

Corvan had come to the Spire. Of course he had. The ember was free, the wards were ash, and whatever else Corvan Hale was—monster, clerk, father—he had never once in his life left a debt uncollected.

"He can't reach the altar before we do," Pell said, but his voice shook.

"No," Tamsin said. She picked the lantern up off the altar stone, and it was lighter than it had ever been, as if judgment had already begun to spend it. "But he doesn't have to. He just has to be standing between us and the Door when we try to open it."

Below them, across the black water, the first rider reached the foot of the Spire and raised a horn to his lips.

The note that came out of it was not a war cry. It was, Tamsin realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, a parley call.

Corvan wanted to talk.
