## 18. What the Door Costs

The altar room at the top of the Spire was smaller than Tamsin had imagined. Twenty years of dread had built it into a cathedral in her mind—a vaulted hall of black stone where gods were judged and kingdoms ended. Instead it was a round chamber no wider than a granary floor, its walls streaked with salt-bloom, its ceiling open to a sky the color of pewter. The caldera breathed below them, and through that breathing came the smell of deep water and old iron.

At the center stood the hearth: a basin of dragon-forged bronze, wide as a wagon wheel, cold for so long that frost ferns had grown up its flanks like ivy. The dragon-fire that had once filled it—fire that did not burn but *remembered*—was down to a single coal, crusted grey, sunk in ash.

And around the coal, in a lantern of green glass, sat Vael's ember.

Tamsin set the lantern on the altar's rim with both hands, the way she'd carried it across half a drowned country. Her palms were still scarred from the marsh—ember-light burned into her skin in branching lines, like lightning frozen under glass. They ached now, constantly, quietly, the way old wounds ache before rain. She had stopped hiding them. There was no one left to hide them from who mattered.

Sefa stood at the chamber's eastern arch, watching the causeway below through Odell's cracked spyglass. The apprentices—Maren and little Tobin and the twins—huddled against the far wall with the last of the valley folk who'd made the climb: eleven souls out of two hundred, the rest strung along the Spire's spiral stair like beads on a fraying cord, waiting for word.

Waiting for her.

* * *

The ember spoke first. It always spoke first now; it had earned that much.

*Tamsin.* The voice came from the lantern, low and many-layered, a chord of voices rather than one—the god's voice, or what remained of it after the trial. After she had sat in judgment on the thing she'd served for twenty years and found it guilty. *You know what is asked.*

"I know," she said aloud, because some things deserved to be said into open air, not just received in the skull like a stone dropped down a well. "Say it anyway."

*The Door requires the ember entire. Not a portion. Not a spark spent in passing, as I have been spent for twenty years on small mercies. All of it. Every fragment gathered home—including the one that wears your brother's face.*

Brannoc stood near the hearth. He had stood there since they'd arrived, not moving much, the way he'd never moved much even alive—leaning against things, patient, watching the world with those grey eyes she'd have known anywhere, anywhere, in any crowd, in any afterlife. He looked at her now and did not speak. He had confessed already, three nights ago in the dark below: that he was not her brother at all, only the ember's last loose fragment, wearing her grief like a coat it had found washed up on a shore. That everything he'd said to her on the drowned road, every memory he'd offered, every joke about how Brannoc used to cheat at dice—all of it had been assembled from her own remembering, a mirror polished until it looked like a person.

She had wept when he told her. She was not weeping now. She didn't fully understand why, except that grief, like water, finds its level eventually.

"The Door opens both ways?" she asked the ember. "Once it's open, it stays open?"

*Until the last of your people passes through. Then it closes behind them, and Mereth is sealed—not hidden, not imprisoned. Finished. At rest.*

"And you?"

A pause. For twenty years she had believed the silent god could not lie—that silence was truth's last fortress. Then she had learned the silence itself was a prison, laid by Corvan's order, and she had learned that gods could be caged by men with ledgers and law, and something in her faith had cracked in a way that never quite mended but also never quite broke. It had become something else. Something more honest.

*I end,* the ember said. *That is what entire means. Gathered whole, spent whole. A god is not a hoard. It is a fire, and fire is only ever borrowed.*

"Then I'm asking you plainly," Tamsin said. "Is this what you want? You could refuse. You could gutter out here in this bowl, unspent, unjudged, done. No one would call it cowardice. I wouldn't."

*I would,* the ember said, and there was something almost wry in it, the ghost of a humor she'd never known it had, because it had never been allowed to speak. *I watched you judge me, Tamsin Vane. You weighed my silence against a drowning country and you did not flinch. Do you think I can do less?*

She closed her eyes. "Then we spend you."

*We spend me.*

* * *

It should have been simple after that. It was not simple. Nothing in Mereth had ever been simple; that was the country's whole disease.

Odell Ferrance would have known how to sequence it—the cartographer's mind, the love of ordered steps—but Odell was ash on a causeway fifty miles back, his master map burned to buy them an hour, his coordinates living now only in Sefa's careful head and Tamsin's scarred hands. So it fell to Sefa, seventeen years old, daughter of the man who had silenced a god, to stand at the hearth and recite the working from memory.

"The Door sits beneath the caldera's throat," she said. "The coordinates are a bearing and a depth, and the bearing is—" she checked the wax tablet she'd copied from Odell's dying dictation, hands steady, because Corvan's daughter had been raised steady whether she liked it or not, "—north-northeast, four points off true, measured from the Spire's shadow at dusk. The depth is ninety fathoms below the waterline. The Door is a seam in the world, and the seam opens when the ember is poured into the hearth and the hearth remembers being a furnace."

"And the people?" Maren asked. She was fourteen, one of Odell's apprentices, ink-stained, brave past all sense. "How do they cross?"

"They walk down," Sefa said. "The stair becomes a road. That's what the Door is—not a gate, a road. Odell said the old kings built it as an escape route for exactly this: the day the country died. Nobody ever used it." Her voice caught, very slightly, on *nobody ever used it*, and then steadied. "Nobody ever had anything worth spending to open it."

"We do," Tamsin said.

"You do," Sefa corrected, gently, and Tamsin heard the truth in the correction and let it stand. Two hundred lives. One god. The arithmetic was not difficult. The arithmetic had never been difficult. What was difficult was that the god had a face, and the face belonged to her brother, and she had spent twenty years talking to a coal in a lantern because it was the closest thing to prayer the valley allowed.

* * *

They began at dusk, because the bearing required the Spire's shadow, and the Spire threw a long finger of darkness across the caldera's western rim as the sun went down behind the drowned hills.

Tamsin carried the lantern to the hearth's edge. The others withdrew to the walls—Sefa with the children, the twins holding each other's sleeves, Tobin too young to understand and old enough to feel the weight of the room anyway. Eleven souls plus the ones on the stairs, plus the two hundred below in the camp among the whale-ribs and the salt-killed orchards, all of it balanced now on a green-glass lantern and a woman with lightning scars on her hands.

Brannoc came to stand beside her.

Not the ember beside the hearth. Beside *her*. Close enough that if he'd been alive, if he'd been real, she could have taken his arm the way she used to when they were children walking the levee road, before the flood year, before everything.

"You don't have to watch," he said. His voice was Brannoc's voice. Of course it was. It had been assembled from her memory of Brannoc's voice; there was no other voice it could have.

"Yes I do," she said. "You're the part that hurts most. You go last, or I go first. Those are the options."

He smiled. It was a good smile. It was her smile, actually—she could see that now, finally, the way you can see a forgery's brushstrokes once someone shows you where they are. Brannoc had laughed bigger than that. Brannoc had grinned like a door kicked open. This was Tamsin's own tight, guarded smile, reflected back at her for twenty years, and she had loved it because it was familiar, and familiarity had done the rest.

"I lied to you," he said. "On the road. About the chapel. About the way the light came through the windows. You remembered it wrong and I kept your wrongness because it was warmer than the truth."

"I know."

"I'm going to say things at the end," he went on, "when the ember gathers me in. Fragments surface. Old words, maybe. Maybe words that aren't mine at all—maybe the god's, maybe other keepers', everyone the ember ever touched. Don't listen for him in it. Don't look for Brannoc. There's nothing of him left but what you lent me, and you'll want it back, and wanting it back will hurt worse than losing it did."

"Thank you," she said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it, because thanking a lie for its honesty was the most Mereth thing she had ever done.

He put out his hand—not touching hers, hovering above it, the way spirits do, the way heat hovers over a flame—and beneath his fingers her scarred palm warmed, and for one moment the ember-light in her skin answered the ember in the lantern, and the two glows recognized each other like travelers from the same lost city.

Then he stepped back, and it was time.

* * *

She broke the lantern.

It took three strikes against the hearth's bronze rim. Green glass is tougher than it looks—it has to be, to hold what it holds—and on the third strike it split along a seam no one had ever seen, falling open like a seed pod, and the ember rose.

Not flew. *Rose.* Slowly, the way smoke rises, the way grief rises when you finally stop pressing it down. A coal the size of a fist, glowing not red and not white but a color she had no name for, a color that lived between the others, the color of the space where magic met the world and neither won. It hung above the basin. The frost on the hearth's flanks began, very slowly, to weep.

"Vael," Tamsin said. Last time. She said it the way she'd said it ten thousand times in twenty years of silence—no, not ten thousand. She had never said it aloud. The vows forbade speech. She had thought it, silently, daily, hourly, a name held in a closed mouth like a coin under a tongue. Now she said it aloud, once, into the open air of a chamber at the top of a drowned country, and the sound of it was strange and small and enormous all at once. "Vael. I release you from my service. I release myself from yours. Whatever happens next, we're both free to choose it."

The ember pulsed. And then it poured.

There was no other word. It tipped, the way water tips, the way light tips through a crack under a door, and it ran down into the basin in a slow bright stream, and where it touched, the hearth remembered. Frost flashed to steam. Bronze woke from grey to the deep orange of a living forge. And the coal at the bottom—the dragon-fire's last fragment, the one that had slept in the ash for a century—cracked open like an egg and gave up what it had been keeping.

Fire climbed the hearth's walls. Not ordinary fire. Remembering-fire. Tamsin felt it in her scars first, a warmth running the branching lines of her palms like spring thaw running a river's delta, and then she felt it everywhere, in her teeth, behind her eyes, in the old break in her left ankle that had ached every winter since she was nineteen and suddenly, briefly, did not.

The chamber filled with voices.

This was what Brannoc had warned her about. Fragments surfacing. The ember gathering itself home out of every place it had been spent—every mercy, every ward, every quiet word pressed into a dying keeper's ear across two hundred years—and each fragment brought its moment with it. She heard a midwife's blessing in a dialect dead before her grandmother's grandmother. She heard a soldier's last prayer on a field whose name no map remembered. She heard children. She heard so many children, the gifted ones the wards had hidden, their small voices calling names of people long gone, and every one of those voices was a thread pulling home, and every thread made the fire brighter, and the brighter it burned the more it sounded less like a god and more like a country.

Mereth, speaking. All of it at once. The drowned and the burned and the forgotten, saying *we were here.*

Tamsin stood at the rim and did not flinch. She had judged this god once already. She could bear its testimony.

Below the caldera's throat, ninety fathoms down, something began to answer.

* * *

Sefa felt it first through the floor—a shudder, rhythmic, like a heartbeat coming up through stone. Then the stair. The great spiral stair that wound down the Spire's inner wall, the one the refugees had climbed for six days with their bundles and their babies and their goats, began to change. Each step lit faintly from within, a line of ember-light spiraling downward into the dark, marking a road where there had only been a descent.

"The Door's waking," Sefa called up. "Tamsin—it's waking!"

"Send them down," Tamsin said. "All of them. Now. While it holds."

They sent them down. Maren took Tobin's hand. The twins went together, the way they did everything. One by one, then two by two, then in strings of ten and twenty, the people of the Sundered Valley stepped onto a road of light inside a mountain at the bottom of the sea, walking toward a seam in the world that a god's whole life was being spent to hold open.

From the rim of the hearth, Tamsin watched the fire in the basin begin to fail.

It failed the way a candle fails—slowly, then suddenly. As each fragment came home and was spent, the voices thinned. The midwife's blessing finished and faded. The soldiers' prayers burned down to embers of sound. The children's names were called, and answered, and called no more. The fire sank in the basin like a tide going out, and with every inch it sank, the Door below opened a little wider, and through that widening Tamsin could feel—could *taste*, on the air rising up the stair—the living world. Rain that wasn't salt. Soil that grew things without argument. Magic, loose and lawful and everywhere, the way air is everywhere.

Her people were walking into it. She counted them by the lights on the stair, and the count went past a hundred, and past a hundred and fifty, and the fire sank lower, and she did not move from the rim.

"Tamsin." Brannoc's voice, beside her again. Fainter now. The ember was spending him too—he was its last fragment, he'd said so himself, and last fragments go last. "It's nearly time for me."

"I know."

"When I go—if there's anything of the god left in what I say, don't take it as absolution. You judged him fairly. Fair judgment doesn't need the defendant's agreement."

"I know," she said again, and this time she added, because she was her father's daughter and her brother's sister and she had earned the right to say hard things plainly: "And if there's anything of Brannoc in you—anything real, anything that isn't mine reflected back—I want you to know I'd have loved him anyway. Even knowing. Even now."

For a moment the spirit was silent. And in that silence, for the first time in the whole long deception, his face was not quite her brother's and not quite her own reflection. It was something between. Something that belonged to neither of them alone. Something that had become real the way all borrowed things become real: by being needed long enough.

"That," he said, "is the kindest thing anyone has said to me in either of my lives."

Then the fire reached him.

* * *

She felt it leave the way you feel a tooth come out—shock, then relief, then the strange nakedness of the gap. One moment he stood beside her, leaning against nothing, grey-eyed, patient. The next, the ember-light that had worn his shape drew up out of it like a tide withdrawing from a footprint, and what remained was a silhouette of smoke that held his outline for three heartbeats and then let it go.

The last of the voices surfaced as he dissolved. She didn't try to stop herself from listening. She'd promised him she wouldn't look for Brannoc in it, and she didn't—but she listened, because listening was not looking, and because she owed the dying her attention.

What came up out of the fire was not a god's voice and not her brother's. It was older and plainer than either. It sounded like the country itself, at the very end of its strength, saying the only thing left to say:

*The door is open. Walk through. Be free of us.*

*Be free of me.*

And then the hearth went dark.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a life goes out, the way a country goes out. The bronze cooled from orange to black in the space of a breath, and the frost began to return to its flanks, and the voices stopped, and the chamber was silent—truly silent, for the first time in Tamsin Vane's twenty years of sworn silence, silent in a way that contained no god at all.

She stood at the rim of the cold hearth with her empty lantern and her scarred hands and she felt—nothing. That was the truth of it. Not peace, not grief, not triumph. A clean and terrible nothing, the nothing of a debt paid in full, the nothing of a room after the funeral guests have gone home.

Then, from below—from the stair, from the Door, from the seam in the world—came a sound.

Voices. Living ones. Not fragments, not memories, not echoes spent from a dying fire. Real voices, her people's voices, rising up the spiral road: laughter, a baby crying, someone singing—badly, joyfully, a harvest song from the old country sung in a new key by someone who'd never seen a harvest. Maren's voice, shouting something delighted to Tobin. The twins arguing about which of them had seen the sea first.

They were through. They were *through*, into the living world, and the Door was holding, and the fire that had bought it was ash in a bronze bowl, and none of them would ever know the price, and Tamsin decided in that moment that they should never have to.

She turned to Sefa, who stood at the eastern arch with the spyglass trained down the causeway, and Sefa lowered it slowly, and her face, when Tamsin read it, was not the face of a girl reporting good news.

"There's dust on the road," Sefa said. "Riders. A lot of riders. Coming fast."

Corvan. The Inquisitor. Her father. Coming up the Spire's causeway with whatever force the crown had left, arriving—as he had arrived at every turning of this story—at the exact moment when everything was already decided, to see what kind of ending he'd been given.

The Door was open. The people were crossing. And the man who had silenced a god was climbing the stairs to find out what his daughter and a mercenary had done with the silence.

Tamsin set the empty lantern down on the cold hearth, picked up her sword, and went to meet him.
