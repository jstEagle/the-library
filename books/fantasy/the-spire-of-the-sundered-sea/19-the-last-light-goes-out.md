## 19. The Last Light Goes Out

Sefa had spent her whole life learning to read her father's silences.

There was the silence he kept before a verdict — flat, patient, a silence like a drawn blade. There was the silence after, which was different, heavier, the silence of a man stacking another stone on a wall he'd been building for twenty years. And there was the silence of the road, when he rode at the head of his riders and said nothing at all, because nothing needed saying; the warrant did the talking.

She heard that third silence coming up the causeway before she heard anything else. Hooves on old dragon-stone, muffled by salt mist. Thirty riders, maybe fewer — the crown had bled itself thin hunting ghosts in a drowned country, and even Corvan's reputation could not conjure an army out of what remained. She stood at the Spire's threshold with Tamsin beside her and felt her own heartbeat do something strange: it didn't quicken. It settled. Like a hand smoothing a page flat.

"He'll want to talk," Sefa said. "He always talks first. The burning is the punctuation."

"You don't have to be there," Tamsin said. Her hands were still bandaged where the ember-light had scarred through them, and she held her sword loosely, the way she held everything now — as if grip were a thing she'd finally stopped needing to prove.

"Yes, I do."

Behind them, the Door stood open in the Spire's hearth-wall. That was the only word for it, though *door* was too small a thing to say about it. It was an absence shaped like a doorway, a tall rectangle in which the world simply declined to exist, and through it came wind that smelled of rain on warm stone and woodsmoke from cookfires and, faintly, impossibly, bread. Living-world smells. The refugees of Mereth went through it one family at a time, blinking, weeping, holding children up so they could see the sky on the other side — a sky with weather in it, real clouds moving under their own power, unregulated by wards.

The ember had gone into the Door entire. Sefa had watched Tamsin pour it out of the lantern like a woman emptying the last of a wine bottle she'd sworn never to open, and the light had gone up into the frame of the world and become passage. What remained of Vael was whatever lived in the going-through. What remained of Brannoc was nothing at all; the spirit had come apart as the Door opened, and Tamsin had not looked away while it happened, and had not spoken for an hour afterward.

Now the riders crested the last rise of the causeway, and Sefa saw her father dismount.

* * *

Corvan had aged since the salt marsh. That was the first thing. He'd always seemed carved rather than born — a man assembled out of verdicts and grey wool and that terrible patient voice — but somewhere between the marsh and here, the carving had begun to erode. He walked up the Spire steps alone, waving his riders to hold at the base, and his boots rang on stone worn smooth by feet that had climbed here three hundred years ago to feed a hearth that no longer burned.

He stopped six paces away. Close enough for conversation. Far enough for a sword-draw. He'd taught her that distance himself, once, when she was nine: *This is how far you stand from a stranger, Sefa. This is how far mercy stands from judgment.*

"Daughter," he said.

"Father." Her voice came out steady. She'd rehearsed this moment in a hundred nightmares and in all of them she'd screamed at him, or wept, or run. In none of them had she sounded calm. Calm, it turned out, was what you got when you stopped hoping the man in front of you would be someone else.

His eyes went past her, to the Door. To the people passing through it. An old woman was crossing just then, carrying a bundle of bedding and nothing else, and on the other side — Sefa saw it clearly — someone caught her elbows and steadied her and laughed, and the sound came back through the absence like light through water.

"So it's done," Corvan said. "The ember is spent."

"It's spent."

"On the Door." He said it without accusation. That frightened her more than anger would have. "I spent eleven years trying to find that Door, and I would have sealed it. You spent four months and opened it." He looked at her. "Which of us loved this country more, I wonder. Or is love the wrong measure entirely?"

"You didn't come to measure things," Sefa said. "You came with thirty riders and a warrant. I can smell the vellum from here. It smells like the ones on our kitchen table. Like every kitchen table we ever had."

"The warrant is for the mercenary." His gaze flicked to Tamsin, who hadn't moved. "Vane. For the destruction of a registered relic of the crown — that being your god, madam, though I doubt the clerks in the capital will ever phrase it so honestly. And for the unlawful passage of two hundred and forty-one subjects across a border that officially does not exist." A pause. Something moved behind his face like a fish under ice. "The list does not include my daughter. I made certain of that."

"How generous of you," Sefa said, "crossing me off the list of things you intend to destroy."

"I've never intended—" He stopped. Breathed. When he spoke again the verdict-voice was gone and what was underneath it was worse: tiredness. Real tiredness, the kind she'd only seen once before, years ago, the night after he burned the village at Harrow Fen and sat in their kitchen until dawn with his hands wrapped around a cold cup, saying nothing, doing nothing, being the second kind of silent. "Sefa. Step aside. Let me take Vane, and let me report that the Door collapsed behind them, unrecoverable. The valley stays erased. Mereth stays erased. The crown keeps its clean map and its clean conscience, and everyone standing on this stone lives."

"And the two hundred forty-one?"

"They're gone. Past any border I can name. Past any law I'm required to enforce." His jaw tightened. "That is the largest act of mercy I have ever performed, and it will cost me everything I have left, and you are looking at me as if I offered you poison."

"Because you did," Sefa said. "You're offering me a story. *The Door collapsed.* You've told that story your whole life. The gifted child you spared in front of the crowd at Duncarrow — you told everyone you released her, and then you rode three days and burned her village anyway, because the crowd needed mercy and the ledger needed blood and you never once let the two meet where anyone could see." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated that he heard it. "You're not offering mercy, Father. You're offering me a version of you that I can live with. And I already tried living with him. He doesn't exist."

For a long moment Corvan said nothing. Wind off the caldera stirred the hem of his coat. Behind Sefa, the crossing continued — a farmer, two sisters, Odell's youngest apprentice hauling a crate of maps that smelled of smoke and salt. Somewhere below, the last ward-stone in the valley cracked with a sound like a distant door, and nobody flinched anymore. They were all done flinching.

"You sound like your mother," he said finally, and it wasn't cruelty. It was surrender wearing cruelty's clothes, because it was the only uniform he owned.

"She'd have opened the Door too."

"Yes," he said quietly. "She would have. Long before you. And I would have reported that it collapsed." He looked down at his own hands, gloved, scarred at the wrist-bones where a witch's binding-cord had bitten him thirty years ago — the first arrest of his career, the one they still put in the histories with his name spelled correctly. "Do you know why I silenced Vael? Truly? Not the report. Not the history."

"Tamsin says the god demanded justice the crown wouldn't survive."

Tamsin shifted slightly beside her. Sefa hadn't known she was listening. Perhaps she hadn't been, until now.

"That's the shape of it," Corvan said. "Twenty-two years ago the god began speaking again — through embers, through dreams, through any vessel weak enough to carry it. And what it said, everywhere, in every mouth, was the same thing. *Mereth was drowned on purpose. Name the names. Open the archives. Give the dead their verdicts.*" His voice flattened into recitation; he'd said this to himself many times, alone, probably, in the dark, practicing a confession no one had ever asked for. "Do you understand what that means? Not the crime. The *names*. Half the great houses of the capital built their fortunes on the drowning — the evacuation contracts, the land grants, the salvage charters. The crown's legitimacy sits on top of that flood like a lid on a pot. If Vael had its trial, the kingdom doesn't survive the verdict. So the crown gave me a choice, dressed up as an honor. Silence the god, or watch it burn the country down to its foundations — and take every gifted child in every village down with it, in the purges that would follow."

"So you chose the lid."

"I chose the pot," Corvan said. "A pot with people in it, boiling slowly, forever. Yes. I have had twenty-two years to learn the difference and it has not gotten easier to say aloud." He straightened, and for a moment the Inquisitor reassembled himself out of habit, spine and shoulders and grey wool, and Sefa understood that this was the last time she would ever see him wear himself. "But the ember is spent. The god cannot testify. The Door is open and the witnesses are leaving, one family at a time, and by winter there will be no one left in this valley who remembers being Mereth." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Sefa had ever seen on a human face. "The lid holds. The pot goes cold. History will call it peace."

"And you?" Tamsin said. Her voice was rough. She'd been quiet so long that the words came out like something excavated. "What does history call you?"

"A servant of order," Corvan said. "It will be wrong, and it will be kind, and I will not be alive to correct it."

* * *

He turned to go. That was the part Sefa hadn't prepared for. All her nightmares had ended in fire or in chains or in shouting, and instead her father simply turned, the way he turned from a verdict rendered, mission complete, and started down the steps toward his riders and his warrant and his twenty-two years.

"Wait," she said.

He stopped. Didn't turn around.

"You said the list doesn't have my name on it. Where does it end, then? What happens to you when you ride back with no prisoner and no Door and two hundred forty-one people unaccounted for?" She took a step down toward him, close enough now to see that his hands weren't steady. "You know what they do to witch-hunters who fail. You've *been* what they do to witch-hunters who fail. You built the procedure."

"There are procedures," he agreed mildly, "for everything."

"Then stay."

The word came out of her before she'd decided to say it, and once it was out she couldn't take it back and found she didn't want to.

"Through the Door. Come through with us. Leave the warrant on the step and the riders at the bottom and walk through into — into rain, Father. Real rain. Cookfires. There's a town on the other side of wherever this comes out, Odell thinks, a harbor town, and nobody there knows your name or your history or your procedures. You could be—" She faltered. She didn't actually know what he could be. She'd never seen him be anything else. "You could be a man with a daughter. That's all. That's all it costs."

Corvan turned around then. He looked at her for a long time, and Sefa searched his face the way she used to search it as a child, reading silences, looking for the father inside the Inquisitor the way you look for the shipwreck under clear water.

And she found him. That was the unbearable thing. He was in there. He had always been in there — the man who'd carried her on his shoulders through the harvest fair at Duncarrow, who'd taught her the stand-back distance and the reading-of-silences and every other skill she'd turned against him. He was in there, and he was tired, and he was afraid, and he was going to refuse.

"I appreciate the offer more than you can know," he said. "And I will decline it, and you will spend some years hating me for declining, and then some further years understanding it, if I've raised you half as well as I fear I have." He held up a hand before she could speak. "No. Hear the arithmetic. Through that Door I am a fugitive with a famous face and a head full of state secrets, and the crown's reach is longer than your harbor town's welcome. Every year of my freedom would be a year the crown hunted harder — for me, and then for the trail that led to me, and the trail leads to you. My staying buys nothing except the pleasure of my company, and I am, as you have so accurately catalogued, mostly procedures." A breath. "And there is one other matter. One debt."

"The god," Sefa said.

"The god." He glanced past her, into the Spire, at the cold hearth where the lantern sat empty. "Vael is not entirely spent. Whatever the ember poured into that Door, some residue remains in the altar-stone — I can feel it from here, and I have felt it since the marsh, getting quieter by the day. A god dying is not like a man dying. It takes longer. It takes *witnesses*." His voice dropped, and for the first time since the causeway he sounded like something other than a man managing an outcome. He sounded like a penitent. "Someone should sit with it. Someone who knew what was taken from it, and who owes it. The crown sent me to silence a god, and I did it, and in twenty-two years I have never once asked whether the god deserved silencing. I think the answer is no. I think the answer has been no the whole time." He met Sefa's eyes. "Let me pay that. It's the only coin I have left that isn't counterfeit."

"You could write it down," Sefa said desperately. "The truth. The names. Put it through the Door—"

"And start the war I spent twenty-two years preventing, in a country full of people who've only just learned to stop flinching?" He shook his head. "No. Some truths need to rest before they can be borne. Let them sleep with the country. Let the next generation dig them up, if they must, with soft hands." 

He descended one step. Then another. At the third he paused, and did not turn fully around — only his profile, etched against the mist coming off the caldera.

"Sefa."

"Yes."

"When I'm dead — and it won't be long, a dying god makes poor company and I've earned poor company — someone will ask you about me. Clerks, historians, your own children someday. They'll ask what manner of man Corvan the Inquisitor was." His voice held level, but barely. "They'll offer you two stories. The butcher of the gift, and the servant of order who did hard things in hard times. Both are true. Neither is sufficient. So I ask only this, and it is the only thing I have ever asked of you: remember me kindly. Not accurately. Kindly. Accuracy is the crown's job. Kindness is yours."

"Daddy—"

"Don't." The word broke in half coming out of him. He mastered it. He mastered everything; it was his single talent and his single curse. "If you say it, I'll want to believe I can come back from it, and I can't, and I've lied to you enough for one life."

Then he bowed to her — her father, bowing, low and formal, the bow of a subject to something higher than a crown — and turned, and went down the long stairs to his riders.

* * *

Sefa stood at the top of the steps and watched him go.

At the bottom, he mounted, and spoke briefly to his lieutenant, and the riders wheeled — away, back down the causeway, back toward a valley that was forgetting its own name with every hour, back toward a capital that would receive either a prisoner or a report and had, in the end, received only a report. *The Door collapsed behind them. Unrecoverable. The valley remains empty. Mereth remains erased.*

The lie he'd tell with his last breath, to keep the lid on the pot, to keep the people in it from boiling. She would hate him for it. She already knew she would hate him for it, and then understand him, exactly on the schedule he'd predicted, because he'd raised her half as well as he feared.

The riders shrank. The mist took them. The sound of hooves on dragon-stone faded to nothing, and then there was only the wind, and the creak of the Spire, and the small sounds of the crossing — a baby crying on the far side of the Door, and someone singing to it, badly, in a language Sefa didn't know yet but would.

Tamsin came and stood beside her. For a while neither of them said anything. Below, the caldera's dark water lapped at the drowned streets of a kingdom that had maybe an hour left of having anyone in it.

"He asked me to remember him kindly," Sefa said.

"Will you?"

Sefa thought about it honestly, because lying to herself was the family disease and she meant to die of something else.

"I'll remember him tired," she said at last. "Tired, and stubborn, and standing in the exact right place at the exact wrong time for twenty-two years. And on the anniversary, maybe, I'll be kind about it." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way he'd taught her not to cry — quickly, efficiently, without surrendering the field. "Come on. The old woman with the bedding — her grandson crossed ahead of her and won't stop asking for her. Someone has to walk her through."

They turned back to the Spire together. Behind them, deep in the altar-stone, something faintly glowed and guttered — a god dying slowly in the company of the only witness left willing to sit with it — and ahead, through the absence in the wall of the world, the smell of bread and rain and morning grew stronger with every soul that passed.

Two hundred and forty-one had crossed. The count was climbing. And Sefa Halloran took the first step of the rest of her life through a Door that had cost a god, a country, and a father, into a morning with no map for it — where, weeks later, in a harbor town smelling of tar and rain, a girl with her father's grey eyes and none of his silences would sit down with fresh paper and begin to draw.
