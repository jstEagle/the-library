## 21. A Map With No Valley On It

The town was called Greywater, which was a lie twice over — the water ran brown with river silt and the town itself was the color of old rope. Tamsin loved it immediately.

It had no walls worth the name, no wards at all, and nobody looked twice at strangers. Half the harbor were strangers. Fishers from three coasts, salt-farmers inland, a colony of Merethi refugees who had come out of the Door in ones and twos over the years, drawn by rumor like gulls to a gutting table. They had a saying here that Sefa taught her the first week: *Greywater takes anyone who can pull an oar.* Tamsin could pull an oar. That turned out to be enough.

She took work guarding grain again, because some things you do not stop doing just because the world ends once. The caravans here went along the coast road instead of through a drowned valley, and the merchants paid in coin instead of silence, and there were no ward-stones to watch flicker green. She kept catching herself checking anyway. Twenty years of habit does not drown easy. She would find her eyes drifting to the roadside stones at dusk, waiting for a light that never came, and then she would remember: nothing is hiding anymore. Nothing needs to hide.

It should have felt like losing something. Mostly it felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying.

* * *

Sefa drew maps on the floor.

Not on paper — paper cost money they didn't have — but on the wide floorboards of the rented room above the chandlery, in charcoal, working from memory and Odell's notes. Odell had kept everything in his head those last miles, after the master map burned, but he'd talked the whole way down the mountain, dictating to Sefa while Tamsin walked rearguard with her scarred hands wrapped against the cold. Bearings. Distances. Where the causeway bent, where the marsh drained, where the whale-town had been beached since before any of them were born. Sefa had filled three notebooks on the road and now, in Greywater, she was turning them into something permanent.

Tamsin came up the stairs one evening with bread and smoked fish and found the whole floor covered in lines.

"You're drawing the valley," she said.

"I'm drawing what's *under* it." Sefa sat cross-legged in the middle of the floor, charcoal behind her ear, ink to her elbows. She was fifteen and had stopped flinching when people said her father's name, which had taken most of those weeks. "The route. Door to coast. Odell always said a map of a place that doesn't exist is worthless, but—" she shrugged, "—he also said the only worthless thing is an unrecorded truth. So."

The valley was there, rendered small and careful: the caravan roads, the shrine at Harrow's Bend, the archive where Odell had lived his exile. The flooded Mile. The caldera, with the Spire rising out of it in fine ruled strokes. And at the eastern edge, past the last of the old country, a gap in the coastline, and then Greywater, drawn slightly larger than its actual size because Sefa had a cartographer's vanity about destinations.

There was no label on the valley. Just the shape of it, empty, the roads running through blankness like veins under skin.

"No name?" Tamsin asked.

"What would I write? 'Mereth' is a dead kingdom's name. 'The Sundered Valley' is a prison's name." Sefa tapped the empty space with her charcoal. "Odell says leave it blank. He says the people who came out of it get to name it, not the crown, not the maps, not us. We just record that something was here, and that we left."

Tamsin stood looking at it for a long time. Her whole life fit inside that blank space, and on this map it was smaller than the room they slept in.

"Put the shrine in," she said finally. "Harrow's Bend. And the little one on the east road, the one that burned. People will want to know where their dead are buried."

Sefa wrote the labels small and neat. Then, without being asked, she drew one more mark: a tiny lantern symbol at the valley's edge, right where the Door had been, and beside it a single word — *light kept here.*

"That's not accurate," Tamsin said.

"It's true, though," Sefa said, and went back to her coastlines.

* * *

The school started in a boat shed, because everything in Greywater started in a boat shed.

Odell Ferrance had been in the town four days before he'd begun complaining about the state of local geography — which was to say, nonexistent — and nine days before he'd cornered the harbormaster and demanded space. What he got was a leaky shed at the end of the pier with a hole in the roof and a view of the mackerel boats. He declared it adequate in the tone of a man announcing his own funeral, and then he spent two weeks making it magnificent.

He hung charts on every wall. He built desks out of broken dory planks. He made Sefa's notebooks into primers, copying them out in his precise surveyor's hand until his fingers cramped and Sefa took over half the copying herself. And then he opened the doors and announced, to the harbor at large, that any child who wished to learn the shapes of the world could come and learn them free, and any adult likewise, and if anyone thought that was a strange ambition for a disgraced royal mapmaker they could take it up with the tide.

They came. At first just Odell's own apprentices — Pell and Maro and little Annet, the orphans who had crossed with them and clung to him ever since, calling him Master Ferrance with a solemnity he pretended to hate. Then dock kids, curious, staying for the pictures. Then a Merethi woman in her forties who had never been allowed letters in the valley, because the valley hadn't believed in teaching the gifted anything but fear, and who sat in the back row among the children and learned her alphabet with her jaw set like a woman breaking stone.

Tamsin came by sometimes, in the gaps between contracts. She told herself it was to check security, which was nonsense; nobody in Greywater burned schools. Really she went to watch Odell teach.

He was different here. In the valley he had been a caged animal, sharp and bitter, hoarding his knowledge like a dragon hoards gold it can never spend. Here he spent it constantly, lavishly, wastefully — drawing coastlines on the shed floor in chalk, telling the story of the gunship on the causeway, letting the children hold his surviving instruments with wet hands and only wincing a little. He never mentioned the burned map. But sometimes, when a student got a bearing exactly right, he'd go still for a moment, looking at the chalk line as though it were the last living member of a family, and Tamsin understood that he had rebuilt his country one lesson at a time, and that this was how a mapmaker survived burning his life's work: he made another.

One evening he caught her standing at the door and beckoned her in.

"You're going to make yourself useful," he said. "Sit down."

"I don't teach."

"You guard things. Guard this." He put a slate in her hands. On it, in a child's wobbly hand, someone had drawn the Spire — all wrong, too tall, dragons circling it like flies — and beneath it: *where the lady killed the god.* "Half the harbor thinks that's what happened. Half the other half thinks you're a witch who ate the valley. You might as well tell them the truth before it curdles into something worse."

"The truth is complicated."

"The truth is *always* complicated. That's why we have schools." He looked at her over his spectacles, and for a moment she saw the man who had broken into his own archive rather than let witch-hunters have it, the man who had fed his masterpiece to a fire to save six lives and called it a fair trade. "Tell them the simple part, then. There was a valley. It kept us safe and it kept us prisoners, and both things were true. A god died so we could leave. Now draw your own map forward and stop looking over your shoulder."

Tamsin held the slate. A child had drawn her, at the bottom corner, stick-thin with a lantern, standing outside the door.

"All right," she said. "But I'm charging you in fish."

* * *

The crossroads was a mile inland from Greywater, where the coast road met the drovers' track, and there had been a shrine there once. Everyone agreed on that much. A low stone table under a wind-bent hawthorn, moss-eaten, older than anyone's grandmother. Older than the town's memory of what it had been *for*. The old gods of the coast had gone wherever gods go when people stop needing them — quietly, without vengeance, leaving their furniture behind.

That was what decided her, in the end. A shrine with no god in it. Not a stolen altar, not a contested one. Just a place where people had once knelt, and where the stone had kept kneeling long after everyone else stood up.

She carried the lantern out on a grey morning in the last week of autumn, alone. She could have brought company — Sefa would have come, Odell would have grumbled and come anyway — but this was the one errand she needed to do by herself, the way you bury someone.

The lantern was cold. It had been cold since the Spire, since the ember went entire into the Door and the light that had guttered twenty years in a bowl of ash went out all at once, clean, like a breath released. Vael's last fragment had worn her brother's face to reach the altar, and Brannoc's face had smiled at her at the end — or the ember had smiled with it, she still couldn't untangle which, maybe never would — and then there was no face, no voice, no god. Only a lantern with a burnt-out wick and a door standing open onto the living sea.

She had carried it across the water anyway. Some weights you keep by choice.

At the shrine she set the lantern on the stone table, in the shallow worn hollow where offerings had once sat, and found she didn't know what to say. All her vows were spent. *Keep the ember. Keep the silence. Keep the valley* — all paid out, all honored, all done. For the first time since she was seven years old, kneeling in a shrine much like this one while an elder lit a coal in a bowl, she was speaking to no one. Or speaking to everyone. She hadn't yet learned the difference.

"You were a bad god," she said. The wind took it sideways. "You let the country drown. You hid us instead of saving us, and you called it keeping, and you made a girl carry you for twenty years because you were too proud to die alone." She paused. "And you gave me Brannoc's face at the end, when I needed it, even if it was only a mask. Even if it was a lie you told me because it was kinder than the truth. Maybe that cancels some of it. I've decided it cancels some of it."

A gull screamed somewhere over the hawthorn.

"I'm not putting you here to be worshipped," she said. "Nobody's coming to kneel. I'm putting you here so people remember that somebody tried. That's all a shrine is, maybe. Proof that somebody tried." She touched the lantern's cold handle once, lightly, the way you touch a grave-marker. "Rest. Both of you."

Then she walked away and did not look back, because she was done being a woman who looked back.

She told no one where the lantern had gone, but Sefa found out within two days, because Sefa found out everything. She appeared at the chandlery table that evening with her charcoal and a fresh sheet of the good paper Odell had traded for, and drew, without comment, a small lantern at the crossroads on the new map. Under it she wrote: *a light was kept here. it is finished.*

"That's a strange inscription for a map," Tamsin said.

"It's an accurate one." Sefa blew the dust off the ink. "Maps used to record what kings owned. Odell says the next kind records what people survived." She hesitated, then, in the smallest voice Tamsin had heard from her since the marsh: "Do you think it worked? The Door. Do you think they're — all right? Out there?"

Tamsin thought of the valley's people scattered along the coast like seeds off a threshing floor — the old gifted children finding each other in port towns, farmers learning tides instead of weather-watching, Odell's apprentices reciting bearings in a leaky school. Thought of the Door standing open behind them until the ember was spent, and then simply a passage in the rock, ordinary, walkable, leading nowhere but onward.

"They're pulling oars," she said. "Greywater takes anyone who can pull an oar. I think that's what the whole world is, now. Places that take anyone who can pull an oar." She reached across and ruffled Sefa's hair, which the girl tolerated with the martyred expression of her father, and which meant more between them than either would say aloud. "Go finish your coastlines."

* * *

Winter came soft to Greywater, the way winters came to places that had never been afraid of anything worse than storms. The school moved indoors to the harbormaster's hall when the boat shed became uninhabitable, and doubled in size, and Odell began complaining about class sizes in the tones of a man describing his greatest triumph. Sefa's map went up on the hall wall, all eight feet of it, the first chart of the route out of a country erased from every other map, and travelers stood in front of it and pointed and asked questions, and Sefa answered with a confidence that Tamsin recognized as armor she had once worn herself.

On midwinter's eve, the whole town lit lamps along the seawall — an old custom, older than anyone's reason for it, lamps against the dark just because the dark comes anyway. Tamsin stood among them with her scarred hands in her pockets and watched the lights run out along the water, and thought: this is what it looks like when people keep a flame without needing it to be divine. Small. Numerous. Lit by hands.

She was almost home when she passed the notice board by the chandlery and saw the new paper nailed up among the fishing prices.

Not a crown edict. Not a hunt-list. Just a letter, copied out fair in a careful hand and posted by someone with good intentions and no idea what they were posting:

*A scholar of the coastal roads seeks accounts of the drowned kingdom of Mereth, its peoples and customs, for a history now in preparation. Generous payment for testimony. Inquire at the sign of the Anchor & Chain.*

Tamsin read it twice. Somewhere south, in the cities, the crown was arguing about what to do with a few thousand refugees and one burned province's worth of awkward truth, and meanwhile the rest of the world was doing what the rest of the world always did: getting curious. Historians. Scholars. People with generous payment, asking gentle questions about a place that had spent twenty years being invisible and one terrible year becoming famous.

She thought of Sefa's blank space on the map. *The people who came out of it get to name it.* But that wasn't quite true, was it. The people who came out of it would get to *tell* it — or the historians would tell it for them, tidied and footnoted, the valley a curiosity, the god a footnote, the Door a legend. Stories did not stay blank just because you wanted them to. Someone was always coming to write on the empty spaces.

Tamsin stood in the lamplit street a long while, watching her breath fog.

Then she unpinned the letter from the board, folded it into her pocket, and walked home to show it to the others — because whatever came next, whatever questions the world was going to ask about the country they had carried out on their backs, they would answer together or not at all.
