## 22. Emberlight

The year turned the way years do in a coastal town—by salt and weather, not by bells. One morning the fishing fleet went out under a sky like hammered tin, and Tamsin Vane stood at her window watching it and thought: *one year since the Door.*

She had counted without meaning to. That was the trouble with anniversaries; they arrived whether you invited them or not.

The lantern sat on her table where it had sat for most of that year, after she'd carried it out of Mereth's drowning dark and set it down in a town called Harrowgate that appeared on maps now—real maps, drawn by living hands, with the Sundered Valley absent from them entirely, as if the sea had finally been allowed to forget. The lantern was cold. It had been cold since the night the ember went out of it, spent whole and entire on the Door, and Tamsin had learned to look at that coldness the way you look at an old scar: not with grief, exactly. With acknowledgment. *You happened. I happened. We are both still here.*

She dressed, strapped no sword—it still felt strange, a year later, to leave the wall empty—and took the lantern down into the street.

* * *

Harrowgate woke around her the way towns do when they believe in their own tomorrow: shutters banging open, a baker arguing with his apprentice about the proofing, gulls staging their daily robbery of the fish market. Children ran past her legs chasing a hoop, and one of them—the Ferrow girl, maybe six, all elbows and opinions—skidded to a stop to stare at the lantern swinging from Tamsin's hand.

"That's the dead god," the girl said, with the frank cruelty of children who have only ever heard stories.

"It's a lantern," Tamsin said. "Gods are heavier."

The girl considered this, decided it was either very wise or very stupid, and ran on after her hoop.

Tamsin smiled. A year ago she would not have known how to answer a question about gods except with silence. Now she had a whole small arsenal of jokes, and she deployed them like a woman disarming traps she'd once set herself.

The crossroads shrine stood where the coast road met the market way—a squat stone thing older than Harrowgate, older probably than Mereth, its niche empty for as long as anyone living could remember. When Tamsin had first planted the lantern there, in the weeks after the crossing, old women had crossed themselves at it and young men had spat, and everyone had asked her whose god it was, and she had told them the truth every time:

"No one's. That's rather the point."

Now the niche held the lantern, and around its base someone—Sefa, she suspected, though Sefa denied it with the specific energy of the guilty—had planted sea-thrift, which bloomed pink and stubborn through salt wind and neglect. The stone was worn smooth where travelers touched it. Not praying, Tamsin thought. Just touching. There was a difference, and the whole world was learning it.

She set the lantern in its place and stood back, and did what she had come to do instead of pray: she remembered. Brannoc, aged nine, holding a crab aloft like a trophy before it pinched him. Odell Ferrance, ink-stained to the elbow, saying *a map is just a promise the land hasn't broken yet* two days before he burned his life's work to buy them an hour. The marsh, and Marta going down with her axe still swinging. Corvan, staying behind in the failing light, asking only to be remembered kindly—which Tamsin had done, grudgingly, the way you honor a debt you didn't agree to but paid anyway.

A year of remembering, and it didn't gut her anymore. That was the miracle nobody sang about: grief gets lighter not because it shrinks but because you grow around it.

"Talking to the lantern again?"

Sefa came up the coast road with a roll of paper under her arm and charcoal on three fingers, as always. She was nineteen now and carried herself like a ship's mast—all straight lines and purpose—and she'd stopped flinching at her father's name sometime in the spring, which had taken longer than learning to draw again, and had cost more.

"Someone has to keep it company."

"It's a lantern, Tamsin."

"Gods are heavier." She nodded at the paper. "New chart?"

Sefa unrolled it against the shrine's stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. The coastline of the Sundering Reach, drawn fresh: Harrowgate, the ferry points, the drowned Mile marked now not with warnings but with depths, useful depths, depths a salvager could work with. And inland—blank. Deliberately blank. Where the valley had been, Sefa had left clean white space, and in the corner, in her small precise hand: *Here lived people. They went home.*

"The guild master wants me to fill it in," Sefa said. "Trade routes. He says a blank space on a chart is an invitation to disaster."

"He's right, generally."

"I told him some spaces should stay blank so we remember we chose them." Sefa rolled the chart away, briskly, before the moment could get heavy. "The school wants you today. Odell's lot. They're arguing about projection methods and they've decided you're the tiebreaker, which means they've decided you'll lose."

"They remember I burned half a library?"

"They remember you were there when it mattered." Sefa shrugged. "Turns out that counts for more than accuracy."

Tamsin laughed. Somewhere in the last year she'd become someone who laughed easily, and she still caught herself marveling at it, the way you marvel at a limb that heals stronger than it broke.

* * *

The school occupied a net-loft above the fish sheds, and by afternoon it held eleven students and smelled of lamp oil, salt, and ambition. Odell Ferrance had owned nothing when he died—not his maps, not his name, not even his country—but he had owned knowledge, and knowledge, unlike maps, could not be burned. His apprentices had scattered from Mereth with their heads full of his teachings, and in Harrowgate they had built the thing he'd never dared dream of: a school of cartography that taught the land as it was, not as the crown wished it remembered.

Tamsin stood at the back while two of the eldest argued latitude against bearing, chalk dust flying, and let the noise wash over her. This was what she'd bought, she thought. Not with the ember alone—with twenty years of guarding grain caravans through a valley that forgot its own name, with Marta's blood in the marsh, with a brother's face that had never truly been his. All of it, everything, poured into this: children arguing loudly about the shape of the world, in a room with no wards on it, in a country with no Inquisition in it, safe enough to be wrong.

Mira, the youngest, tugged her sleeve. "Vane. Is it true you talked to a god?"

"It's true a god talked at me. Mostly it complained."

"What did it say? At the end?"

Tamsin crouched to the girl's level. Around them the argument roared on, unheeding.

"It said thank you," she said. "And then it said rest. And I think—" she chose the words carefully, because children carry words a long way— "I think those might be the same thing."

Mira frowned, filed it away, and ran back into the argument, which had escalated to someone drawing a rude map of the guild master on the slate. Tamsin straightened, and her hands ached faintly—the old scar-light, the ember-burns from the marsh, pale silver now instead of gold, visible only when the sun hit them right. Her gift, such as it remained, lived in her hands like banked coals. Small. Hers. Enough to warm soup and light candles and, once, to seal a leaking hull until the boats reached shore. She used it for nothing grander, and it asked nothing grander of her.

That, too, was the point.

* * *

Evening came in gold off the water, and the town emptied toward the shrine the way it had begun to do, without any edict or observance, on this particular evening each season—though tonight was the one that mattered, the one Tamsin hadn't announced to anyone and suspected everyone knew anyway.

They gathered loosely: Sefa with her charts rolled under her arm, Odell's apprentices still chalky, fishermen with their caps off and their skepticism intact, old women who had crossed out of the valley on Tamsin's left and right and now grew tomatoes in soil that had never known a ward-stone. Brannoc's name was spoken twice, quietly, by people who'd known him only as a story. Marta's once. No speeches. Nobody had wanted speeches; the whole town had developed, in its first free year, a deep allergy to being preached at.

The lantern sat cold in its niche among the thrift flowers.

It was Mira who stepped forward. Tamsin saw it happen almost in slow motion—the girl slipping loose from the crowd, walking to the shrine with the terrible certainty of the very young, and stopping before the lantern with her small hands opening at her sides.

"Mira," Sefa started, gently.

But the girl had already breathed in, the way the apprentices taught each other to breathe—in through the nose, slow, *like drawing a line you can't erase*—and cupped her hands around the lantern's dead glass.

Light kindled between her fingers.

Small. Unsteady. A candleflame's worth, no more, trembling like something newborn. Tamsin felt her own chest tighten—not fear, not anymore, but the old vigilance rising and finding nothing to fight, and sitting back down inside her, confused, disused.

Mira fed the light through the glass with the concentration of a child threading a needle, tongue between her teeth. The wick caught.

The lantern held flame.

Not green. Not the sick ward-glow of the surveyor's lantern a lifetime ago, not the cold gold of Vael's dying ember. Plain orange fire, ordinary fire, fire that would gutter if the wind rose and need relighting tomorrow and the day after. It threw warm light across the crossroads and the faces gathered there, and it flickered, and it stayed.

Nobody knelt. That was the thing Tamsin noticed, the thing she knew she would carry: nobody knelt. They stood, in a loose ring, in the last of the sunset, and watched a six-year-old's small gift hold flame in a dead god's lantern, and they smiled the way you smile at a neighbor's good harvest.

The future lit by hands. Not handed down. Lit.

Mira turned around, ablaze with triumph, and found Tamsin's eyes across the crowd. "It stayed," she said. "Did you see? It *stayed.*"

"I saw," Tamsin said.

"Was yours ever like that? When you were little?"

Tamsin considered lying, considered the truth, and gave the child the better gift, which was honesty shaped kindly: "Mine was bigger. But it wasn't mine, not really—it belonged to vows, and to a god, and to a country that drowned. Yours is just yours. That's rarer. Take care of it."

Mira nodded gravely, the gravity of six, and ran back to her mother holding her hands up like trophies, and the crowd began to break apart into the evening—into suppers and lamplit windows and arguments resumed and lives, plain lives, continuing.

Tamsin stayed a while. Sefa stayed with her, shoulder to shoulder against the shrine's stone, while the lantern burned its small stubborn orange between them and the stars came out over a sea that had swallowed a kingdom and finally, mercifully, let it sleep.

"You could go back," Sefa said eventually. "To the Reach, I mean. The salvagers found the Spire's crown last month. They say the caldera's gone quiet. Cold all the way down." She glanced sideways. "You could see it. What's left."

Tamsin looked at the lantern a long time.

Somewhere beneath that cold water lay an altar where she had judged a god and found him wanting, and a Door she had opened with everything she loved, and a brother-shaped absence she still missed with a toothache ache on certain nights. Somewhere down there was the whole of Mereth, sleeping at last, unwitnessed.

"No," she said, and heard how easy it was. "I already know what's left." She tapped the lantern glass, once, lightly. "This is what's left. Us. The rest can rest."

Sefa nodded and said nothing, because she'd learned—from Odell's ghost, in a manner of speaking—that some spaces should stay blank so you remember you chose them.

They walked home together through streets that smelled of bread and tar, past the school with its lamps still burning, past doors that locked against thieves and nothing else. Behind them the lantern held its flame at the crossroads, tended by no god, sworn to no vow, waiting for tomorrow when some small pair of hands would light it again—or wouldn't, and that would be fine too.

Tamsin Vane, unsworn, unbowed, unafraid, slept soundly until morning, and dreamed of nothing but the tide.

*The end.*
