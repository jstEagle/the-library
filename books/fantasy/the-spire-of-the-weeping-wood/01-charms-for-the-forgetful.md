## 1. Charms for the Forgetful

The bell above Tamsin Vane's shop had been enchanted once, long before they owned it, and now it did nothing but ring. That was the way Tamsin liked their magic: small, deniable, and mostly broken.

They opened at half past the morning bell, as they always did, after checking the street in both directions through the gap in the shutters. Corvane woke slowly on market days, the fog off the canal burning down to a grey haze that made the slate roofs look like drowned ships. The lamplighter was snuffing the last of the night-lamps along Chandler's Row, and two of the Office's grey-coated wardens were walking their circuit past the fountain with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no.

Tamsin counted them without meaning to. It was a habit so old it had become part of them, like the scar on their thumb or the way they flinched at chalk marks. Two wardens, walking east. Not stopping. Not looking at the little shop with the painted sign — *VANE'S CHARMS: LUCK, SLEEP, SWEET WEATHER* — wedged between a dyer's and a coffin-maker's, which was, Tamsin thought, a suitably honest neighborhood for what they sold. Luck, sleep, sweet weather. Nothing there to hang a person for.

Not anymore.

They unlocked the door, propped it open with the iron boot-scraper shaped like a sleeping dog, and began the morning ritual that was less commerce than liturgy. The luck knots went on their hooks by the window — braided cords of red wool and river-reed, each one tied by hand, each one *just cord*. The sleep salts went into their little blue jars, ground from valerian and hops and lavender, smelling honestly of what they were. The weather-charms were glass beads on leather thongs, and if a bead fogged before rain, well, glass did that; everyone knew glass did that.

Tamsin had spent eleven years making sure everything in this shop could be explained by someone who wanted very badly not to be hanged.

"Morning, Mistress Vane." Old Pell the dyer leaned in his doorway across the lane, hands purple to the wrist, nodding at the sky. "Rain by evening?"

"Fog till noon," Tamsin said. "Then it can't make up its mind."

He laughed, satisfied, and Tamsin felt the small cold satisfaction they always felt — because the fog would hold until noon, would break apart exactly when Tamsin's borrowed breath of will unknotted it, and no one in Corvane would ever know that the weather over Chandler's Row bent slightly, gently, around the wishes of the charm-seller. It was the smallest possible magic. A whisper instead of a word. Eleven years ago, whispering had kept them alive, and they had never learned how to stop.

*You have perfected it,* they reminded themselves, arranging the knot-cords one more time, though they'd already arranged them twice. *This is what perfect looks like. It looks like nothing.*

* * *

The first customer came at midmorning: a young woman with a baby on her hip and shadows under her eyes deep enough to drown in.

"Sleep salts," she said. "For me, not the babe. He sleeps fine. I'm the one lying awake listening to him breathe."

"New mothers do that." Tamsin scooped salt into a twist of paper with the practiced economy of long habit. "A pinch in warm milk, last thing. If it doesn't work, come back and I'll give you your coin back."

"And if it does?"

"Then it was worth a copper."

She smiled, paid, and paused at the door. "My gran used to say you were lucky yourself, Mistress Vane. Said she saw you once, years back, walk out of a fire on Tanner's Lane without a mark on you."

The shop went very quiet around Tamsin. The kettle on the brazier hissed. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere a gull screamed at the canal.

"That must have been another day entirely," Tamsin said pleasantly. "I burn easily."

The woman laughed, embarrassed, and left, and Tamsin stood with their hand flat on the counter until their heartbeat came down out of its gallop. *Eleven years.* The fire on Tanner's Lane. They barely remembered it themselves — which was, in Corvane, a mercy. Memory was the first thing the Pale Office taught people to surrender. Forget a spell, forget a face, forget why you flinched at chalk. The city forgot things the way a body forgets pain once the wound closes: completely, and on purpose.

That was the bargain of Corvane. Magic had been outlawed for twelve years — outlawed, erased, *unmade*, though no one liked to say what exactly had happened the year the law came down, only that something large had been cut away and the whole country had agreed, in the bone-deep way countries agree to things, to stop looking at the empty space where it had been. The maps ended at the eastern hills now with a polite blank space and the words *UNSETTLED LANDS*, and schoolchildren were taught that nothing had ever been there worth remembering.

Tamsin had arrived in Corvane eleven years ago with no memory worth keeping and a talent worth hiding, and had built a life out of the space between those two facts.

* * *

Midday brought the routine customers. A fisherman wanting a luck knot for his nets — third one this season, he lost them like gloves. A clerk buying sweet-weather beads for his sweetheart's garden. A boy of perhaps ten who lingered too long by the shelf of salts, turning a jar in his hands, and looked up at Tamsin with an expression that made the hair rise on their arms: not curiosity exactly. *Recognition.*

"You're the charm-witch," the boy said.

"I'm the charm-*seller*." Tamsin took the jar gently from his hands. "Witch is a hanging word, love. Don't spend it."

"My mam says the Office burned a woman in Harrowgate last month. For charms." He said it the way children say terrible things, flatly, watching your face for the crack. "Real ones. She made a horse out of smoke."

Tamsin's hands stayed steady. Eleven years of practice lived in that steadiness. "Smoke horses," they said, "are worth exactly one burning, then. Here—" they pressed a luck-knot into the boy's palm, free, the red wool bright against his grime— "for your mam. Tell her it's just cord."

He ran, and Tamsin watched him go, and the fear sat down in their chest like an old dog finding its place by the fire. *Harrowgate.* That was three days' ride north. The Office was widening its net again. Every few years the sweeps came closer, the questions got quieter, the gallows got busier, and every charm-seller in the city checked whether their knots were really just cord.

Tamsin's knots were really just cord. They had checked a hundred times. The magic was elsewhere — folded into the arrangement of the shop, the angle of the light, the faint pressure of goodwill they laid over every customer like a second coat of paint. Harmless. Invisible. Perfect.

So why did they go to the window and watch the boy all the way to the corner?

*Because he knew,* said the small voice they kept locked in the cellar of themselves. *He looked at you and he knew.*

"Nonsense," Tamsin said aloud, to the empty shop, which was another habit eleven years old.

* * *

Closing time came with the fog returning off the water, thickening the street into smears of lamplight. Tamsin banked the brazier, counted the day's coppers — a modest, forgettable sum, always modest, always forgettable — and was reaching for the shutters when they heard footsteps.

Not the warden's tread. Wardens walked in rhythm, two pairs of boots, bored. These steps were wrong — dragging, uneven, the sound of someone spending their last strength on forward motion.

Tamsin's hand went to the shutter bar and stopped.

Every instinct they had, every craft-honed reflex, said *shut the door, blow out the lamp, be nobody*. That was the whole religion of their life. Be nobody. Sell cord and salt. Let the world pass by your little window without catching on anything.

But the footsteps stopped outside, and through the fog came a sound Tamsin hadn't heard since childhood — a sound they had no memory of hearing, and yet knew, the way you know a language you've forgotten you speak.

Someone was humming. Low, broken, wet with blood or exhaustion. A lullaby. In a tongue that had no business existing inside the borders of Corvane, in a tongue that the maps said had never existed anywhere at all.

The latch lifted. The door swung open on its own — Tamsin would swear afterward it opened on its own, that their gift moved without their permission for the first time in eleven years — and a man fell across the threshold into the lamplight.

He was older, grey-bearded, dressed in travelling clothes gone dark with mud and worse than mud. There was a wound under his ribs that had soaked through his coat, and his eyes found Tamsin's with the desperate clarity of a man racing his own death to finish a sentence.

"You," he rasped. "It had to be you. I looked thirty years and it was always going to be—"

"Sir, you need a surgeon, I'll fetch—"

His hand shot out and caught their wrist, shockingly strong. Into their fingers he pressed something folded small and hard — paper, oilcloth, the weight of it strange, heavier than paper should be.

"Take it. Take it *east*." His mouth twisted around the next word like it hurt. "Vesperholt."

And the name went through Tamsin like a hook through cloth — *Vesperholt* — because it meant nothing, it was nothing, it was a sound from nowhere, and yet some buried room in them lit up at it, lamps coming on one after another behind doors that should have been sealed.

The man smiled. Blood at his teeth. He said, softly, almost kindly:

"You'll remember. That's the cruelty of it. You'll remember everything."

Then his eyes rolled back, and the strength went out of his hand, and the stranger from nowhere died on the floor of Vane's Charms with the fog pressing against the windows and the lullaby still hanging in the air like smoke.

Tamsin knelt beside him for a long moment, the map heavy in their fist, their heart slamming against eleven years of careful, perfect safety.

Outside, far up Chandler's Row, a lamp flickered. Footsteps — two pairs of boots, unhurried, rhythmic — turned onto the cobbles and started toward the little shop with the painted sign.

And Tamsin, without knowing why, reached out and blew out the light.
