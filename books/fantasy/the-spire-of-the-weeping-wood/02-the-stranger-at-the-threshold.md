## 2. The Stranger at the Threshold

Closing time in Corvane had its own liturgy, and Tamsin Vane performed it every evening with the devotion of the truly frightened.

The shutters went up first—the outer ones, plain pine painted the grey of old dishwater, indistinguishable from every other shutter on Latch Row. Then the lamp was trimmed to a single wick, low enough to suggest a shopkeeper winding down, not so low as to seem furtive. Furtive drew eyes. Tamsin had learned that in their first year: nothing in Corvane attracted attention like the appearance of avoiding it. Then came the inventory, counted aloud in a voice pitched for any listening ear—*eleven luck knots, four sleep-salts, two courting charms, one weather-glass, cracked*—each item harmless as bread, each one a small lie told with a straight face.

The luck knots were genuine, more or less. Braided hemp and a whispered intention to keep a fisherman's line from snagging; there was no law against wishing a man well. The sleep-salts were lavender and hops and nothing else, though Tamsin charged triple what the apothecary did, because people trusted a charm-seller's salts to *work*, and belief was half the cure anyway. The courting charms were pure theatre—a dried rosebud, a ribbon, a rhyme off the top of the head—and they sold beautifully to girls who wanted an excuse to be brave.

None of it was magic. That was the point. None of it could hang them.

The true thing—the gift Tamsin had carried since before memory, the warmth behind the breastbone that answered when called—stayed folded down inside them like a moth with its wings closed, day after day, year after year. Twelve years in Corvane and they had never once let it open. Not when the fever took half of Latch Row and old Mistress Corbie begged on her knees for *anything, anything you have, girl*. Not when the flood came up the Vane and Tamsin could feel the water wanting, wanting, wanting to be told where to go. They had stood on the levee with their hands jammed in their pockets and their teeth gritted and let the river do its worst, because the Pale Office's wagons came through afterward, always, and chalked doors, and asked questions in soft voices, and the ones who answered wrong were gone by morning.

Safety was a craft. Tamsin had apprenticed to it the way other children apprenticed to coopering or dye-work, and after twelve years they believed—had permitted themselves to believe—that they had mastered it.

It was raining when the stranger came, which mattered later, though Tamsin didn't know it yet. Rain kept honest folk indoors, and it washed the streets clean of the Office's foot-patrols, who hated wet chalk. The bell above the door gave its cracked half-ring at the hour past dusk, when the lamp was already out and Tamsin's hand was on the inner bolt, and the stranger fell through the doorway rather than entered it.

He was tall, or had been. He came apart as he crossed the threshold, shoulder first against the counter, then sliding, leaving a dark stripe along the wood like a snail's trail made of night. Tamsin caught him under the arms—lighter than he looked, terribly light, the lightness of things already mostly gone—and lowered him onto the floorboards between the counter and the shelf of sleep-salts.

"Shop's closed," Tamsin said, which was absurd, and both of them knew it.

The stranger laughed. It cost him something terrible; his whole face clenched around it. He was perhaps fifty, grey-stubbled, dressed as a drover or a tinker, clothes soaked through and stained everywhere with the same dark wet that was spreading now across Tamsin's floor. There was a wound under his coat somewhere high on his side. His hands were a workingman's hands, cracked and rope-scarred—and then he moved one of them, and Tamsin saw the fingers, quick and precise even now, moving in patterns no drover's fingers knew. Sigils. Half-formed, dying, but unmistakable.

Every instinct Tamsin owned screamed at once, a nest of startled birds.

"Don't." The stranger's hand shot out—not a sigil this time, just grip, astonishingly strong—closed around Tamsin's wrist. His eyes were pale, almost colourless, and very clear. "Don't heal me. Listen. There isn't time to waste it on me."

"I don't know what you—"

"You do." He said it without accusation, almost gently, and pulled something from inside his coat with his free hand. Folded paper, soft with handling, creased into eighths and creased again, as if it had been opened and refolded ten thousand times over many years. He pressed it into Tamsin's palm and folded Tamsin's fingers over it, both of his hands around theirs now, warm and shaking. "You'll know what it is. You'll know better than anyone living."

"I'm a charm-seller," Tamsin said. "I sell knots. I sell salt. I don't—"

"*Vesperholt.*"

The word went through Tamsin like a needle finding the gap between ribs. Not pain, exactly. Recognition of a place pain might live. Their mouth shaped the syllables back without permission—*Ves-per-holt*—and the word fit their tongue like a key fits a lock cut for it, and that was the most frightening part: not that they'd never heard the name, but that some muscle deep in them had heard it *before*, long before, and remembered how to say it.

"There's no such country," Tamsin whispered.

"No," the stranger agreed. Blood was at his lips now, bright against the grey stubble. "There isn't. That was the whole idea." His laugh again, worse than before, a ruin of a sound. "They'll tell you east of the Marle there's only marsh and the marsh goes on forever. Look at the map. Look at whose hand drew it."

Tamsin looked down at the folded paper in their fist. They didn't open it. Some animal part of them understood that opening it here, now, over a bleeding stranger, would be the last ordinary moment they would ever have, and they wanted to stand in that moment a while longer, the way you stand in shallow water knowing the drop-off is just ahead.

The stranger saw the refusal and nodded, as if Tamsin had said something wise.

"When I'm done," he said, "move me. Don't let them find me in your shop. And whatever comes to mark the door—" his grip tightened, "—don't touch the chalk. Chalk remembers hands. You understand? Chalk *remembers*. Let it dry and sweep it into running water, and say nothing, and be nobody, and burn nothing—"

"Burn nothing?"

But his eyes had gone elsewhere. Past Tamsin's shoulder, past the wall, at some middle distance where dying men keep their appointments. His breathing changed, shortened, became a series of small deliberate efforts, like a man climbing stairs one at a time on his knees.

"My daughter," he said. "Sera. If she finds you—if she *hunts* you—let her. She has cause. Tell her the map chose right, even if I didn't. Tell her—" A breath. Another. The stairs were getting steeper. "Tell her I didn't pay. Tell her why."

"Tell her why *what*? Who are you? What's your name?"

He smiled, and it transformed him, briefly, into someone Tamsin felt they ought to know—an ache, a shape, a coat once worn. "Ilves," he said. "And the rest of it—" his gaze drifted to the paper in Tamsin's fist, and something in his face was almost tender, almost pitying, "—the rest of it you already know. You've known since you were small. You just weren't allowed."

Then he whispered it again, *Vesperholt*, softer, like a lullaby's last line, and stopped.

* * *

Afterward, Tamsin sat on the floor beside the body for a length of time they could never account for. The rain ticked against the shutters. The gutter outside ran loud. Somewhere a dog complained and was silenced.

Then the craft took over, because the craft was all there was. Move him. Don't let them find him in your shop.

He weighed nothing going up the back stairs, nothing at all, as though most of him had already departed and left instructions for the remainder. Tamsin got him over the rail of the yard, into the strip of dead ground behind the tannery where the drainage ran toward the river, and laid him down in the blackberry tangle with what dignity remained available, which was little. His pale eyes stayed half-open. Tamsin reached out and closed them and felt the gesture land somewhere in their own chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling a long way before it hit.

Back inside, the blood on the floorboards. Sand from the bucket, scrubbed, scrubbed, scrubbed until the wood showed bare and honest. The stripe on the counter too. The coat he'd bled through—into the stove, bit by bit, fed to the flames between customers' hours tomorrow, slow, unremarkable. Every trace. Every seam of him sewn shut.

The paper stayed in Tamsin's pocket. Tamsin did not look at it. Looking at it felt like standing at the top of the drop-off.

It was nearly midnight, the lamp relit and burning low, when Tamsin finally unbolted the front door to dump the scrub-water—running water, he'd said, chalk remembers—and saw the sigil.

Chalk. White as bone, drawn on the door itself, on the grey unpainted pine at eye height, in a hand so fine and confident it might have been engraved. Tamsin knew it the way everyone in Corvane knew it, the way children knew it before they knew their letters: three vertical strokes crossed by a horizontal, and beneath them a circle with a line through it, like an eye that had been refused its sight. The mark of the Pale Office. The mark they chalked on doors of interest. On doors being watched.

On doors that would be knocked upon.

Tamsin's first thought, absurdly, was that they hadn't heard anyone approach—twelve years of craft and they hadn't heard a single footstep, and the chalk was fresh, still proud of the board, not yet touched by drizzle. Their second thought was colder and arrived with total certainty: *no one drew this.*

No one had come up the street. The rain held no footsteps. But the sigil was there, crisp as morning, marking the door where a dying man had fallen through, and it had not been there at dusk.

The paper shifted in Tamsin's pocket, weightless and enormous.

Burn nothing, Ilves had said. But Tamsin's hands, without any instruction from the sensible part of them, had already drawn the map out and unfolded it halfway, enough to see the ink, enough to see the coastline of a country that officially did not exist, rendered in careful, loving strokes—

—in handwriting Tamsin knew better than their own reflection.

Because it was their own. Every loop, every crossbar, the particular impatient slant of the *l*s, the way the *t*s were crossed in single sweeping strokes—it was the hand Tamsin saw whenever they signed a receipt or priced a knot, copied out in a steadier, older version, as if the writer had once written this way and would again.

Tamsin stood in the doorway with the rain coming down and the Office's blind eye staring at them from their own door, and understood, with the calm that arrives when fear exceeds its container, that the stranger had been right about everything.

Someone was going to knock.
