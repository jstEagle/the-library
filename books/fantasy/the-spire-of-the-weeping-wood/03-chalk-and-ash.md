## 3. Chalk and Ash

The dead man's chalk mark stayed on Tamsin's door for three days.

Tamsin scrubbed at it twice with lye and once with vinegar cut with rue-water, the way you'd treat any sigil that might be watching back. The mark wouldn't lift. It was a pale circle bisected by a single vertical stroke — the Office's eye-and-blade, drawn not in chalk at all, Tamsin realized on the second day, but in something finer and grayer, like ash pressed into wood grain. It had gone *into* the door. It was part of the door now, and the door would wear it like a brand until the door itself was burned or rotted away.

*They know someone came here. They know he died here.* That was the arithmetic Tamsin did each night before sleep, lying rigid beneath the wool blanket in the room above the shop. *They know. The question is only how much more they know.*

So Tamsin did what Tamsin always did: performed ordinariness. Opened the shutters at the usual hour. Set out the luck-knots in their usual jumble of red and river-clay thread. Ground sleep-salt coarse for the insomniac wives of the Tanner's Row, fine for the merchants who wanted their dreams sweet. Smiled the small, unremarkable smile of a person who had never once been interesting.

Three days of it. And on the third day, an hour past closing, when the light had gone the color of dirty pewter and the rain had begun its evening argument with the roof tiles, a man came through the shop door without knocking.

He was tall in the way that made doorways seem apologetic, gray at the temples, wearing a long coat of undyed wool that had been chosen precisely because no one remembers coats like it. His boots were wet but clean. He carried no lantern. In Corvane, after dark, that meant one thing only: he didn't need one, or he wanted people to think he didn't.

"Closed," Tamsin said, from behind the counter. Voice level. Hands visible. Heart doing something frantic and private beneath the ribs.

"I can see that." The man looked around the little shop with the mild attention of a buyer appraising goods. His gaze moved across the luck-knots, the jars of salt, the bundles of dried feverfew and St. John's wort — all of it harmless, all of it *certified harmless*, stamped with the Office's lead seal that cost Tamsin two silver a year and bought exactly as much safety as two silver could buy. "Charming place. Very honest."

"We keep to the law." *We*. A good instinct; hedge-witches alone sounded defensive, hedge-witches with neighbors sounded established.

"Do you." He produced, from inside his coat, a folded square of paper, and unfolded it with the care of a man handling something either precious or filthy. "Merrick Hale. Office of Pale Inquiry. This is a warrant of questioning. You are not under arrest." He let that sit in the air between them like a coin balanced on its edge. "Yet."

Tamsin read the warrant. The letters swam. *TAMSIN VANE, CHARM-SELLER, TANNER'S ROW* — written in a clerk's neat hand — *required to answer questions pertaining to the death of one ALDRIC ILVES, found deceased upon these premises.*

Aldric Ilves. So the stranger had a name after all. Tamsin had spent three days trying not to give him one, because names were hooks and hooks caught things, and already Tamsin's nights were full of him: the blood spreading black across the floorboards, the map pressed into Tamsin's hands with fingers that had gone cold too fast, and the whisper — *Vesperholt* — a word shaped like nothing, meaning nothing, belonging nowhere Tamsin knew.

"Found deceased," Tamsin repeated. "On my premises."

"He walked a mile and a half with his stomach opened to reach your door, Mistress Vane. Or Master Vane. The records are admirably vague." Hale's voice never rose or fell; it was a flat road going somewhere. "That interests me. People don't crawl dying through the rain toward charm-sellers. They crawl toward surgeons, or priests, or home. He crawled toward *you*." His eyes were pale gray, almost the color of the mark on the door. "Why?"

"How should I know? I'd never seen the man."

"No," Hale agreed mildly. "I don't imagine you had. And yet." He stepped closer to the counter, close enough that Tamsin could smell rain and something underneath it, cold and mineral, like water off stone. "When my men searched your shop this morning — you noticed the disturbance, I trust, we tried to be tidy — they found scorch marks in your hearth. Recent. And in the ashes—" he paused, delicately, "—fragments of vellum. Map-vellum. Sealed with wax I have not seen since I joined the Office twelve years ago."

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. Tamsin kept both hands flat on the counter where he could see them.

"A letter," Tamsin said. "From my aunt. In Varrow. Family matters." It came out too fast. Tamsin heard it come out too fast, heard the words stumble over one another like drunks leaving a tavern late, and watched Hale hear it too.

"Your aunt," Hale said.

"Yes."

"Burns her correspondence?"

"She's a careful woman."

"Mm." He folded the warrant away. For a moment he simply stood there, and the silence stretched until Tamsin could hear the rain ticking against the shutters, the shop's own timbers settling, the sound of a city holding its breath. Then Hale said, quietly, almost conversationally:

"The man who died on your floor was called Aldric Ilves. Before that, for eleven years, he was called Anchor-Keeper Third Class, and before *that* he was a citizen of a country that does not exist. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Mistress Vane?"

"That he was mad."

"That he was *escaped*." Hale's pale eyes didn't blink often enough. "There is a place the world forgot. Not metaphorically. Not lost, not ruined, not renamed. Forgotten — by treaty, by edict, by an act of the Pale Office older than my service and larger than my understanding. Every map amended. Every record burned. Every mind that ever held it, emptied of it, gently, so gently most never felt the loss. Twelve years ago there was a country east of the Greywater. Today there is a blank space and a treaty line, and if you ask anyone what lies east of the Greywater they will tell you *nothing*, and they will believe it, because believing it is the only thing keeping the peace of the realm."

His voice dropped lower.

"And Ilves was one of nine keepers set to watch the door of that forgetting. Eight are accounted for. He was the ninth. He ran. And he ran *to you* — a hedge-witch with a sealed license and a spotless record and burn marks in her hearth — and he died pressing something into your hands." Hale straightened. "I am required by statute to inform you that harboring materials relating to the Erasure carries a penalty of hanging. I am also permitted—" and here something flickered across his face, fast, gone again, something that in another man might have been called hesitation, "—to exercise judgment regarding what I report upward. So I will ask you once, plainly, and I advise you to consider that I will know a lie because I have heard ten thousand of them and they all taste the same."

He leaned in.

"What did Aldric Ilves give you?"

And Tamsin — who had lied to tax assessors and Office inspectors and a jealous lover and their own reflection for fifteen years, who had built an entire life out of plausible dullness the way a mason builds a wall, stone by patient stone — heard themself say:

"Nothing. He was a beggar. He collapsed. I couldn't stop the bleeding. There was nothing."

The lie tasted of copper.

Hale held their gaze for a long, long moment. Rain ticked. Somewhere down the row a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

"Well," he said at last, stepping back, smoothing his coat. "Then there's nothing further. I'll leave a watchman on your street — routine, following a death. You'll not notice him." At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "For what it's worth, Mistress Vane — and it is worth very little — I've read what remains of Ilves's file. In his last recorded interview, eleven years ago, asked whether he regretted his office, he said: *'I regret that the door has to be kept by anyone at all.'*" A pause. "Goodnight."

The door closed. Tamsin stood in the dark of the shop for a long time, listening to the rain, counting breaths, waiting for the shaking to start. When it started, Tamsin rode it out standing, palms flat on the counter, the way you ride a fever.

Then Tamsin went upstairs, knelt at the hearth, and took out the map.

* * *

It was hidden where it had been hidden for three days: sewn into the ticking of the mattress, wrapped in oilcloth, cold as a caught fish. Tamsin carried it to the fire on knees that didn't want to cooperate and told it, aloud, in the empty room, the truth that couldn't be said to Hale:

"I'm sorry. Whatever you are. Whoever he was. I can't carry this. Hanging is hanging, and I didn't choose any of it."

The copy — the copy Tamsin had made on the first night, tracing every line by candlelight because some instinct older than sense had demanded a backup — went into the flames first. Cheap paper, common ink. It caught at once, curled, flared orange, and was ash inside a minute. Tamsin watched it go and felt nothing except the beginning of something that might, with time and distance, become safety.

Then the original.

It went into the fire and lay there.

The flames licked along its edges and slid off. Tamsin fed it kindling. The fire climbed, roared, threw heat against Tamsin's face — and the map lay in the heart of it, white and whole, untouched, as though it were lying on a table of glass. The flames parted around its borders like water around a stone. Where fire touched the vellum directly, the surface merely gleamed, faintly, with a light like moonlight on snow.

Tamsin snatched it out with bare fingers. It wasn't even warm.

"No," Tamsin said. "No, no, *no*—" and thrust it back in, held it down with the poker, piled on coal until the hearth howled. The map emerged from the inferno pristine, and now Tamsin could see what terror had kept hidden before: the ink lines were moving. Faintly. Slowly. The river on the map flowed, a hair-thin shimmer traveling its length, west to east, against every river Tamsin had ever known. And along the margins, in handwriting that Tamsin had spent three days refusing to examine closely — because examining it meant admitting it — the same hand that had written *Vesperholt* on a dying man's lips had written notes, annotations, a whole private language of arrows and stars and one phrase repeated in four places, small and urgent as a heartbeat:

*the anchor lives. the anchor lives. the anchor lives.*

Tamsin sat down hard on the hearthstone with the unburnable map in their lap, and the shaking that came now was not fear, or not only fear. It was the shaking of a foundation tested and found hollow. Because Tamsin knew that handwriting. Had known it since the first night, had known it the way you know your own face in a mirror and flinch. Those looping ascenders. That impatient crossbar on the t's. Tamsin had written shopping lists and price signs and forged licenses in that hand for fifteen years without once asking why it felt less like writing and more like remembering.

*The anchor lives.*

Outside, down the row, a watchman's lantern swung its slow arc — routine, following a death, invisible. Inside, the fire burned on harmlessly around a piece of a forgotten country that had chosen Tamsin's hands, Tamsin's hearth, Tamsin's life, as its keeper.

Staying meant the gallows — tomorrow, next week, whenever Hale's judgment ran out or his superiors grew curious about the fragments of vellum in the ash. Running meant the unknown, the treaty line, whatever waited east of the Greywater in the blank space where a country used to be. Tamsin looked from the door to the fire and understood, with terrible clarity, that the choice had already been made — had perhaps been made twelve years ago, by whoever had cut away a childhood and left this hollow-handed witch in its place.

Tamsin packed by candlelight: salt, thread, the feverfew, a knife, bread. Left the seals and ledgers and the whole careful architecture of the invented life stacked neatly on the counter like a suit of clothes no longer worn.

At the back door, the map in a satchel against their spine, Tamsin paused once, looking at the gray mark burned into the front door's wood.

"They'll hang me for what I am anyway," Tamsin told it softly. "Better they chase what I actually did."

And slipped out into the rain, east, toward the river and the blank on every map — while somewhere behind, in the dark of the empty shop, the unburnable page lay warm in the ashes, and the words *the anchor lives* glowed faintly, like a lamp lit for someone coming home.
