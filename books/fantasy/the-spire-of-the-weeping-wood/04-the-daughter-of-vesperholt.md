## 4. The Daughter of Vesperholt

Tamsin left Corvane before dawn, when the city was still the color of ash and the lamplighters had not yet come down from their ladders.

They went out through the tanner's gate, where the watch was thinnest and the toll-keeper slept in his booth with a jug of something amber cradled against his chest. Tamsin did not use magic to pass him. That was the discipline of twelve years, the whole architecture of their survival: never a spell where a coin would do, never a charm where a lie would serve. They set three coppers on the sleeping man's ledger, lifted the gate's crossbar with their own two hands, and slipped through into the grey morning like water finding a crack.

Behind them, chalked across their shop door in strokes that no rain would ever wash away, burned the Office's mark: a circle bisected by a vertical line, the sigil they painted on the doors of the condemned. Merrick Hale had not arrested them. That was worse than arrest. That was a door left open so that a frightened animal would run, and run toward what it loved.

So Tamsin ran east.

The map rode against their chest inside their coat, folded along creases worn soft as cloth. It could not burn—Tamsin had proven that twice in the night, once in the hearth and once with a candle held until the wax drowned the flame—and it seemed to know where it wanted to go. Not literally; Tamsin was not such a fool as to believe paper had wants. But every time they stopped at a crossroads and consulted it, their hands unfolded it already turned to the east, already tracing the river road with a finger that moved without instruction, sure of the way the way you are sure of your own name.

*Your own name.* Tamsin flinched from the thought. Names were dangerous now. Hale knew them. The city would have it posted by noon: *TAMSIN VANE, CHARM-SELLER, WANTED FOR QUESTIONING IN THE MATTER OF HIGH TREASON.* Treason. As though loving a country were a crime. As though a person could betray a thing they could not even remember.

The road out of Corvane followed the Aldern for six miles, then climbed away from it through orchards going over with late blossom, and Tamsin walked with their hood up and their shoulders aching and told themselves a story about an inn two days east called the Ferryman's Rest, where they would take a room under another name and decide what kind of life could be built from the wreckage of this one. It was a thin story. Stories had been thin since the stranger died on their floor with blood on his lips and a word in his mouth like a stone he'd carried too far.

*Vesperholt.*

The name sat in Tamsin's chest like a swallowed coal. They had searched their memory for it all night while pretending to sleep—the word meant nothing. No country called Vesperholt appeared on any map Tamsin had ever seen. And yet the map pressed against their heart was drawn in their own handwriting. They had checked. Three times, by candlelight, holding the stranger's map beside letters they'd written to a cousin in the south, comparing the way both hands made the letter *l* with a little backward hook, the way both crossed their *t*s low and impatient. Identical. A thing with no explanation except the ones that ended everything.

The afternoon came on warm and golden, criminally beautiful, the hedgerows loud with birdsong. Tamsin was descending through a stand of birches toward a ford where the map showed the road crossing a stream, one hand on a trunk to steady themselves—

—and the world flipped.

A boot caught their wrist. The birch-trunk spun. Tamsin hit the leaf-litter hard enough to knock the sky loose, and by the time the trees righted themselves there was a knee on their sternum and a knife resting against the soft place beneath their jaw, and above the knife was a face.

A young woman, perhaps five years younger than Tamsin, with hair the color of wet slate cut raggedly at her jaw and eyes so pale a grey they were nearly white. Her cheeks were hollowed. Her knuckles, wrapped around the knife, were split and scabbed. She had been traveling hard and sleeping badly and eating worse, and she looked at Tamsin the way people look at the thing that killed their family.

"You're the charm-seller." Her voice was hoarse, scraped raw. "The one from Corvane. The one my father died reaching."

"I don't—" Tamsin's mouth was full of dirt and fear. "I don't know your father."

"No." Something moved behind those pale eyes, some grief so old it had gone strange, almost calm. "You wouldn't."

She shifted the knife a fraction, and Tamsin felt a bead of their own blood slide down their throat. "He came to your shop bleeding," the woman said. "You let him die on your floor."

"He was dying *when* he arrived! There was nothing—I bound what I could, I'm not a physician, I'm a *charm-seller*, I sell luck knots and sleep salts, I couldn't—"

"You could have saved him if you'd been what you actually are." The words landed like stones dropped from a height. "Instead you played shopkeeper and he bled out among your little trinkets. Don't insult me with the act. I've watched your shop for four days. I saw the wards woven into your window-frame, old ones, older than anything the Office teaches. You're no hedge-witch playing at charms. You just hide it well."

Terror rose in Tamsin like floodwater. This woman knew. Which meant the Office might know, which meant— But beneath the terror, absurdly, indignation. "I hid because hiding is how you stay alive. If I'd torn open his wounds and worked craft over him in a city that hangs people for less, the Pale Office would have taken us both, and he'd have died anyway, only sooner and louder. What I did—what I *didn't* do—was the only mercy available."

The knife didn't move. The woman's breath came fast and shallow. "He gave you the map."

"Yes."

"And he said Vesperholt."

Tamsin went very still. "How do you know that?"

"Because he's been saying it for twelve years." For the first time the voice cracked, and the calm over the grief split open, and underneath was a girl of maybe nineteen who had followed her father halfway across a continent watching him die by inches. "In his sleep. In his fever. He taught me the word before he taught me my letters. He drew maps of a country that isn't there and made me memorize rivers that don't exist and swore to me that someday—" She stopped. Swallowed. Pressed her lips white. When she spoke again the knife-hand had begun, very slightly, to shake. "And then he found you. The last anchor. And he died on your threshold with the word finally out of his mouth, and I wasn't quick enough to stop the Office's man from following him in."

"His name," Tamsin said quietly. "Tell me his name."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, suspicion, the reflexive tightening of someone who has learned that questions are traps. Then, grudging, watching Tamsin the whole time: "Ilves. Edrik Ilves. Keeper of the western anchor, third of the nine."

*Third of the nine.* The phrase passed through Tamsin like a cold wind through a house, rattling doors they hadn't known were there. Somewhere far below thought, below memory, something answered the words—a resonance, a bell struck in a cellar. Their face must have shown it, because the woman's eyes narrowed.

"You felt that."

"I felt nothing."

"Liar," she said, but without heat now. Almost wearily. She took the knife away from Tamsin's throat and stood, backing off a step, keeping the blade angled between them out of habit rather than threat. "Get up."

Tamsin got up slowly, brushing leaves from their coat, keeping their movements deliberate and visible. Up close the woman was taller than she'd seemed, and thinner, with the particular gauntness of someone running on purpose. Dirt streaked her cheekbones. Under her travel cloak, Tamsin glimpsed the edge of something inked onto her collarbone—a symbol, half-hidden, a circle bisected by a line.

But inverted. The line broken. The Office's sigil, unmade.

"My father marked me with it when I was small," she said, following Tamsin's gaze. Her chin lifted, defiant. "It's a keeper's ward. It hides what I am from the Spire. From *their* spire-finders." She spat the word. "It's why I've stayed alive when every other keeper's child is dead or forgotten."

"Forgotten," Tamsin repeated. "You keep using that word like it's literal."

"It is literal." She looked at Tamsin for a long moment, and whatever she saw in their face made her sigh, and lower the knife entirely, and rub her free hand over her eyes as though suddenly, crushingly tired. "You really don't remember. Twelve years, and they cut it that clean." She said it half to herself. Then, briskly, as though deciding something: "There's a shrine off the ford road, half a mile north. Old, pre-Office, nobody tends it. We can talk there without being seen from the road. Or I can leave you here and you can walk back to Corvane and hang." She raised an eyebrow. "Choose."

Tamsin chose.

* * *

The shrine was a ring of standing stones furred with moss, roofless, its central altar long ago cracked by frost. Wild garlic grew in the shadows between the stones, and somewhere unseen water dripped with a patience like breathing. Sera—for that was her name, Sera Ilves, though she offered it reluctantly, like a coin pried from a fist—built no fire. She sat on the altar itself, which Tamsin suspected would have horrified whoever raised the stones, and pulled bread and dried plums from her pack and divided them with a precision that was not generosity so much as accounting.

"Eat," she said. "You'll want your strength. And listen, because I'll only say this once, and then you're going to tell me exactly what my father said, word for word, and exactly what the Office knows, and we're going to argue about whose fault he is—was—and then we're going to walk east whether you like it or not."

"That's quite an itinerary," Tamsin said, and bit into the bread because it smelled wonderful and because arguing on an empty stomach was a losing game.

"Twelve years ago," Sera began, without preamble, "there was a country east of here. Between the Aldern's source and the sea-cliffs. Fertile land, old cities, a language of its own. Its capital was called Vesperholt, and at the center of the capital stood the Spire—a tower grown, not built, alive, the root of everything. The Spire held the country's memories. Every birth, every death, every song. It remembered *for* the nation, the way a granary holds grain. Nine anchors kept it—nine people born tied to it, living conduits. My father was one. And I think—" her eyes flicked to Tamsin, sharp and unreadable, "—I think the ninth was a child. Smuggled out the night it happened. Lost."

The plum in Tamsin's mouth had gone to stone. "What happened?"

"The Pale Office happened." Sera's voice went flat and hard, a blade laid on a table. "The Office wasn't founded to police magic, whatever their charters say. It was founded *by* the erasure—to guard the crime. They fed the whole country to the Spire. Every mind in Vesperholt, every memory, every name, poured into that tower and locked there. The land didn't burn. It was *unwritten*. Where Vesperholt stood there's now a stretch of empty moor that travelers avoid without knowing why, and maps show nothing, and no one asks, because asking requires remembering, and there's nothing left to remember *with*. Except—"

She held up her hand and counted, folding fingers down. "The Spire itself. The Wood around it—we call it the Weeping Wood, the trees that grew from the spilled memory, each trunk holding what wouldn't fit inside. The nine anchors, wherever they are—or were. And the keepers' children. Me." She unfolded the last finger and looked at Tamsin. "And whatever you are. Because the Office thinks you're important enough to chase, and my father thought you were important enough to die for, and the map he carried for twelve years—the map he guarded with his life—is written in your handwriting. Explain that, charm-seller. Take all the time you need. I've waited twelve years already."

The silence filled with dripping water and birdsong and the sound of Tamsin's own pulse. They looked down at their hands, at the ink stains that never fully washed out, at the fingers that had unfolded the map unbidden, eastward, homeward.

"I can't explain it," Tamsin said at last, honestly. "I woke up fourteen years ago in a ferry-town south of here with no history and money in my pocket and a lullaby in my head in a language I couldn't speak. I built myself from scratch. Charm-seller. Careful little life. I was *good* at it." They laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And now a dead man's map says I've always known the way east, and a woman I've never met carves a broken circle into her skin to spite the Office, and I can feel it, Sera. Whatever you're describing—I can feel it under my ribs, like a room in my own house I was never allowed to enter."

Sera studied them across the ruined altar, and Tamsin watched something shift in her face—not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of one soldier seeing another's uniform and understanding, unwillingly, that they serve the same battered flag.

"My father used to say," she said quietly, "that the anchors weren't chosen for power. They were chosen for endurance. Because carrying a door in your chest hurts." She stood, shouldering her pack, and looked east through the standing stones, where the sun was dropping toward a horizon Tamsin had never once wondered about. "We walk tonight. The river runs backward at dusk—that's how the map finds the way, past all the villages where nobody knows what lies east. Two days to the moor. Maybe four to the Wood."

"And the Office?"

"Already walking." She tossed Tamsin the rest of the bread. "My father bled for six hours in your shop, charm-seller. Do you really believe a man like Merrick Hale spent those hours drinking tea?"

Tamsin caught the bread and did not answer, because through the gap between the standing stones, far off across the darkening fields, they could see it: a smudge of torchlight on the eastern road, moving with the unhurried certainty of something that has never once lost its quarry.

Sera saw it too. She pulled her hood up over the broken-circle ward, and her pale eyes glinted.

"They're not following the map," she said. "They're following *you*."
