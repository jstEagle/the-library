## 5. What the River Remembers

Sera Ilves had grown up on stories of the Weeping Wood, which was a strange thing to say of a country that no longer existed. Her father had told them in the low voice he saved for things too precious to say loudly: how the trees wept amber sap that held memory like flies hold light; how the river that fed it ran backward at dusk, returning to its source instead of leaving it; how Vesperholt had been so beautiful that the world had grown jealous and unmade it. She had believed every word until she was nine, when her mother sat her down and explained, gently, that there was no such place. That Papa's homeland was a story he'd invented to survive something Sera was too young to understand.

She had hated him for it for years. Then he died bleeding against a charm-seller's counter, whispering *Vesperholt* like a prayer, and Sera understood that he had never been lying at all. He had simply been the last man alive who remembered.

Now she walked beside the charm-seller—the anchor, whatever that meant—along a river that did not exist on any map, following a map drawn in handwriting that should have terrified her more than it did.

The river was called the Lume by the villagers downstream, though none of them could tell her why. It ran gray-green and slow through country that grew poorer and quieter the further east they went. And at dusk, as her father had promised, it turned. There was no violence to it, no reversal anyone could point at; the water simply began moving the other way, patient as a tide, carrying leaves and silt back toward mountains that stood somewhere ahead of them in a haze the color of old pewter.

"It's wrong," Tamsin said, watching it. They had stopped at the bank where the water curled around a fallen alder, flowing now toward the hills instead of away from them. "Rivers don't do that."

"Everything east of here is wrong," Sera said. "That's rather the point."

Tamsin hugged their arms around themselves. Three days out of Corvane and the hedge-witch still moved like someone expecting a hand on their shoulder—checking doorways, flinching at hoofbeats, keeping their hood up even under a sky that held nothing but rain. Sera had spent the first day of their acquaintance wanting to shake them. She was beginning, reluctantly, to want something else instead. To ask what it cost, living like that. To learn whether the price had ever been paid willingly.

"The map says we follow the Lume to the second ford," she said, unfolding the paper again. In daylight, away from the terror of that first night, she could study it properly: the fine confident lines, the little annotations in a language she couldn't read but could almost taste—something rounded, something sung. "There'll be a village. The mark means a house that will shelter us."

"You trust it?"

"I trust his hand." She folded it away before Tamsin could see her fingers tremble. "I don't trust anything else."

They walked on. Behind them, the river carried the evening home.

* * *

The village was called Brant's Hollow, or had been once; the sign over the well had weathered past reading. It was the kind of place the Pale Office never bothered to visit because there was nothing left to take—a dozen houses, a shrine with its saint's face worn blank, fields that went up to the tree line and stopped as if scolded.

The woman who owned the marked house was old and nearly blind, and she took them in without questions, which was itself a kind of answer. She set out bread and hard cheese and weak beer, and asked only, "You're going east, then," in the flat voice of someone stating weather.

"We're going to the Spire," Sera said.

The old woman's hands paused on the loaf. "Never heard of it."

"There's a wood east of here. Trees that bleed."

"I've lived here seventy years." She said it kindly, the way one corrects a child. "There's forest east, same as everywhere. No trees bleed. No spire. Nothing east but the fells, and beyond them—" she frowned, groping after something, her clouded eyes going distant— "beyond them there's..."

Her mouth stayed open. Her hands resumed their work. Whatever lay beyond the fells was a room in her mind whose door had been painted shut so long ago she no longer saw the paint.

"They can't remember," Tamsin said quietly, when the woman had gone to fetch more bread. They were sitting close together at the table, and Sera noticed they had angled their chair between her and the door. Old habit, or new protectiveness; she couldn't tell which unsettled her more. "It's not that they won't. Ask them what's east and it's like asking them to see behind their own heads. The Office didn't just erase Vesperholt. They erased the shape of the hole it left."

"How do you know that?"

Something crossed Tamsin's face—too quick to name, gone before it landed. "Everyone knows the Office burns books and hangs witches. Nobody asks why whole villages can't say what's over the next hill. You start asking and you stop being able to sleep."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Tamsin agreed, and looked at their hands. "It isn't."

Later, in the loft above the kitchen, Sera woke to singing.

It came soft through the floorboards, half-drowned under the banked fire's crackle. A woman's melody—or not quite a woman's; a voice caught between registers, young and old at once, rising and falling in a pattern that made the hair on her arms lift. Not Corvani. Not any language she knew. But her father used to hum something like it, wordless, when he thought she was asleep, and she had forgotten she remembered that until this moment, lying rigid in the dark, listening to a stranger sing her father's ghost down the stairs.

She crept to the loft's edge and looked down.

Tamsin sat by the hearth, wrapped in the blanket the old woman had lent them, eyes closed, swaying slightly. The song poured out of them like water finding a crack in a wall—unhurried, unconscious, grief-shaped. As Sera watched, words surfaced in it, syllables in that rounded, sung language she had almost tasted on the map, and Tamsin sang them cleanly, fluently, the way people only sing what they learned before they learned anything else.

Then the voice caught. Stopped. Tamsin opened their eyes and stared into the fire with an expression Sera recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone who has just heard themselves say something impossible.

She should have announced herself. Instead she lay back in the dark and listened to Tamsin not-sleep for the rest of the night, and told herself the cold in her stomach was only the draft.

* * *

In the morning she tested them. She told herself it wasn't cruelty—it was cartography. You charted a coastline by sailing along it.

"The song you were singing," she said, as they packed up to leave. The old woman pressed a heel of bread into Tamsin's hands and would take no coin. "What language was that?"

Tamsin went very still. "I don't know what you mean."

"Last night. By the fire. You were singing."

"I don't sing." The denial came fast and flat, a door slammed on a hinge. "I've never sung. I don't—I'm tone-deaf, actually. Ask anyone in Corvane."

"Corvane isn't here."

"Then believe me." But their hands, knotting the strap of their pack, had gone clumsy, and the knots came out wrong twice, and Sera watched a person watch themselves come apart and pretend not to notice.

She let it go. For an hour.

They walked the river road east through drizzle, past the edge of Brant's Hollow's fields, past the shrine with the blank-faced saint, and the whole way Sera felt the question pressing against her teeth like a toothache. At the second milestone—an old stone, older than the road, carved with characters no one in these parts could read anymore—she stopped and pulled out the map and held it up beside the stone.

The characters matched. Of course they matched. She had known they would.

"Read it," she said.

"I can't read it."

"Read it, Tamsin."

And Tamsin looked at the stone, and their lips moved, and sound came out of them in that rounded sung tongue—three words, quiet, like a greeting or a farewell—and then they clapped a hand over their own mouth as if something had bitten them.

"What did it say?" Sera asked. Her heart was hammering and she hated it, hated how much she wanted the answer, hated her father a little for dying before he could give it to her himself.

Tamsin lowered their hand slowly. Their eyes were wet, and full of a terror that had nothing to do with the Pale Office. "'The water remembers the way home,'" they said. "It's—it's a blessing for travelers. You say it when you set out on a journey. My grandmother—" They stopped. Blinked. "I don't have a grandmother. I mean, I must have had one, everyone does, but I don't know her name, I don't know anything about her, I never—"

They sat down abruptly on the wet grass, right there at the roadside, and put their head in their hands.

Sera crouched beside them. Up close she could see the trembling, fine as a plucked string, running through their shoulders.

"When I was small," Tamsin said, muffled, "there was a lullaby. I've always known it. I assumed I learned it somewhere—Corvane, a market, some singer. But it's not Corvani. I checked, years ago, quietly, because knowing foreign songs is suspicious when magic is illegal. And I couldn't find it anywhere. Anywhere. So I decided I'd imagined it. I decided that, and I believed it, for eleven years, because believing it kept me safe." They lifted their head. Their face was blotchy and furious and frightened all at once. "It's the same language, isn't it. The map. The stone. The song."

"Yes," Sera said.

"And your father wrote this map. In this language. In—" Tamsin laughed, a broken sound with no humor in it. "In my handwriting. Sera, I watched him write it. I stood in my shop and I watched a stranger write a map in my own hand, and I thought it was a trick, a forgery, some Office game. But it's not, is it. It's mine. Somewhere, sometime, I wrote it. I just don't remember writing it, because I don't remember—" Their voice failed. They tried again. "Who am I? What am I? What did they cut out of me?"

Sera had come east for vengeance. She had rehearsed it on the road out of Corvane: this charm-seller had drawn her father to their shop, had gotten him killed, had burned his copy of his life's work while an inquisitor watched. She had imagined hating them cleanly.

Instead she knelt in the wet grass beside a stranger who was grieving a self they'd never been allowed to meet, and found that the hatred had nowhere to stand. Her father had spent thirty years carrying a country alone. If this person had once belonged to it—if some piece of Vesperholt had survived inside them all these years, leaking out in lullabies and blessings like light under a door—then her father hadn't died alone either. He had died passing it on. To an anchor. To the last door.

To this frightened, stubborn, lying-to-themselves hedge-witch who couldn't tie a knot straight.

"Your name," Sera said slowly. "Tamsin Vane. Is that real? Do you remember being given it?"

Tamsin was silent a long moment. Rain dripped off the milestone onto the old carved letters, filling them, making them shine.

"I remember choosing it," they said finally. "Or—I remember telling myself I chose it. I remember being fifteen and deciding that Tamsin Vane sounded like nobody, like smoke, like a person the Office would walk past. I remember practicing it in mirrors until it fit." A breath. "I don't remember anything before I was fourteen. Nothing. There's just a wall, and on this side of the wall is a girl called Tamsin Vane selling luck-knots, and on the other side is..." They gestured helplessly at the stone, the river, the east. "Whatever this is."

Sera stood. Held out her hand.

Tamsin looked at it, then up at her, wary as a wild thing. "Where are we going?"

"East," Sera said. "All the way. You're going to walk up to those trees that bleed, and you're going to touch one, and you're going to find out what's on the other side of your wall. Because I need to know if you're the person my father trusted. And you—" she said it gently, and meant it as a wound and a gift both, "—you need to know who's been living in your body for the last twelve years."

Tamsin took her hand and rose. Their grip was cold and did not let go quickly.

They walked on east, and the river walked with them, running home.

Near dusk, when the light went pewter and the water turned, Tamsin began humming again—helplessly, the way a wound hums—and this time they didn't stop when they noticed. And far ahead, where the fells should have shown white against the sky, there was instead a darkness like a wall of trees, and from it, faint on the wind, came a smell Sera had known all her life from her father's coat collar and had never been able to name.

Amber. Sweet resin. Something like tears.

Tamsin stopped walking. The humming stopped too.

"Sera," they said, in a voice gone strange and young. "I know those trees."
