## 7. The Weeping Wood

They smelled it before they saw it.

Tamsin had been telling themselves for two days that the sweetness in the air was rot—fallen fruit somewhere, a dead deer in the brush—but Sera stopped so abruptly at the tree line that Tamsin walked into her back, and the look on her face said otherwise.

"That's not rot," Sera said.

The forest rose ahead of them like something drowned and then raised again. The trees were enormous, older than any species Tamsin could name, their bark pale as candle-wax and veined with slow-moving color: amber, rust, a green so deep it was nearly black. And from every branch, from seams in every trunk, a thick liquid wept in long glistening ropes, catching the grey light and holding it, so that the whole wood seemed hung with beads of glass.

"It's crying," Tamsin said, and hated how small their voice came out.

"It remembers," Sera corrected. She had not slept properly since the river; there were shadows under her eyes like bruises, and her father's map was wrapped around her wrist now, bound there with cord as if she feared it might walk off on its own. "That's what my father called them. Tears. He said—" Her voice caught, steadied. "He said each one is a memory. A stolen one. The trees grew up drinking everything the Office cut out of us."

They stood at the edge a long moment. The wood made no sound of wind. There were no birds. But beneath the silence Tamsin heard something—not with the ears, exactly. With the place behind the breastbone where their suppressed gift lived, the thing they'd spent twelve years pretending was nothing. It pulled toward the trees the way a thirsty tongue pulls toward water.

"We should go around," Tamsin said.

"There is no around." Sera pointed east, where the canopy thickened into a wall of pale trunks going on past sight. "My father drew the road through it. Through it or not at all."

* * *

Inside, the light changed.

It came down through the high canopy already ambered, already sweetened by the sap, so that walking under the trees felt like walking through honey held up to the sun. Every few paces another rope of sap hung at eye level, trembling but never falling. Tamsin learned quickly not to touch them—the first time their sleeve brushed one, an image detonated behind their eyes: a kitchen, bread on a table, someone laughing in another room—and they staggered into a trunk and pressed both palms against its wax-pale bark to steady themselves.

The bark was warm.

Not sun-warm. Warm the way skin is warm. And beneath their hands, faint as a pulse through a wrist, something moved.

"Tamsin." Sera had turned back, hand on her knife out of habit rather than threat. "Don't—"

"I know." They took their hands away too fast, as if from a stove. "I felt it. It felt *me*."

They went on more carefully after that, threading between the trunks, keeping their arms close. But the wood did not need touching anymore. As they walked deeper, the memories began to come loose on their own, drifting across the path like pollen. Tamsin would catch a scent—woodsmoke, rain on hot stone, some spice they had no name for—and with it a flash of knowing that belonged to no life they had lived. A woman braiding a child's hair by lamplight. A bridge over white water with seven arches, though Tamsin had never seen such a bridge. A word spoken over and over in a language they did not know and yet understood completely, the way you understand your own name:

*Vesperholt. Vesperholt. Vesperholt.*

"Stop," Tamsin whispered, and didn't know if they meant the forest or themselves.

Sera watched them with an expression Tamsin couldn't read—pity and suspicion fighting for the same ground. "You keep doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Flinching. Like something's being handed to you and you don't want to take it."

Tamsin opened their mouth to deny it and found nothing there to say. The truth was that it felt exactly like that: a gift pressed insistently into their hands by someone who refused to be refused.

"My father said the Wood chooses," Sera said quietly. "When we were small—he told me this once, when he was drunk, which was the only time he told anything—he said if you ever got inside it, it would give you back whatever piece of you the Office took. If you wanted it or not."

"And you believe that?"

"I believe my father spent thirty years not coming here." She looked up at the weeping branches, and for a moment her face was young and unbearably sad. "So yes. I believe it."

* * *

They made camp at dusk in a hollow ringed by four great trunks, and Tamsin sat awake while Sera slept, watching the sap-beads glow faintly above them like lanterns in a house where everyone has gone to bed.

It was one particular tree that kept calling them.

It stood apart from the others, slightly smaller, its bark smoother, younger—and Tamsin had noticed it hours ago because it had been noticing *them*. Every time they passed it, the warmth beneath the bark quickened. Now, in the dark, it glowed very softly along one seam, a thin line of gold like a door left open on purpose.

Tamsin knew they shouldn't. Knew it the way they knew all their survival-craft, the careful invisibility they'd built brick by brick in Corvane: don't look twice at anyone, sell only harmless charms, never let the gift rise past the surface of the skin. Twelve years of *don't*, and every instinct they owned said *don't* now.

But the tree was singing. Not sound—a pull, low and patient, in the same voice as the lullaby that had been leaking out of them since the river. A lullaby in a dead tongue that Tamsin had hummed without knowing, and Sera had gone white when she heard it, and neither of them had spoken of it since.

Tamsin crossed the hollow. Their feet decided before they did.

Up close, the seam of gold ran the height of the trunk, and within it something shifted like a figure moving behind frosted glass. Tamsin raised one hand. Hesitated. The tree waited—it had clearly been waiting a very long time—and the waiting itself was unbearable, worse than any answer could be.

They laid their palm flat against the bark.

The world folded.

* * *

Night. Firelight. A room with low beams and a smell of tallow and wet wool, and outside the window a city Tamsin did not know and knew absolutely: spires, a harbor, banners of deep violet stitched with a white star. Vesperholt. The name arrived whole, the way your own name arrives, without translation.

Someone was carrying them. Strong arms, a coat that smelled of smoke and camphor, and the person carrying them was running. Tamsin saw the runner's face from below, from a child's angle: a gaunt man with grey in his dark beard, jaw set hard—

*Sera's father.*

"You're safe," he was whispering, to the child, to himself, to the night. "You're safe, little anchor. Hold on. Whatever happens, hold on."

And behind them—Tamsin twisted in the memory, the child's body twisting, looking back—the city was *coming apart*. Not burning. Worse than burning. Whole streets were going soft at the edges, like ink touched by water, like a drawing breathed upon. People stood in their doorways watching their own houses fade and did not scream, because even their screaming was being taken first. Above it all, vast beyond sense, a pale shape rose into the sky: the Spire, drinking, drinking, drinking.

A woman ran alongside them briefly—dark-haired, breathless, pressing something into the child's hands, folding small fingers closed over it. Her face swam close and Tamsin's heart slammed against their ribs because he knew her, knew her the way you know a face from a mirror, the same narrow chin, the same crooked brow—

*Mother.* The word rang through the memory like a struck bell, and grief came with it so total that twelve years of careful, crafted safety shattered around it like ice.

"They're cutting the road behind us," the woman said to the man carrying Tamsin. "There's no way back for either of us. But the child—the child can live outside it, if the Office thinks the anchor died with everything else."

"The cut will take everything," the man said. "Their name. Their years. All of it. No child should have to pay that."

"No one should have to pay any of this," she said, and her hand touched the child's hair once, lightly, the way you touch something you are already mourning. "But they'll live. That's what I'm buying with the rest of it. Promise me you'll watch for them. Promise me you'll find them when it's time."

Then white. Then a pain like a door closing through the middle of a life. Then—

* * *

Tamsin came back to themselves on their knees in the loam of the Weeping Wood, both hands still pressed to the trunk, sobbing without having decided to, without any memory of beginning.

Sera crouched in front of them, gripping their shoulders, saying their name—saying *Tamsin, Tamsin*—and the name sounded suddenly foreign, borrowed, a coat someone else had sewn. Because it wasn't theirs. It was the false one, the invented one, the label pinned to a child whose real self had been carved out and fed to a tree in a country that no longer existed.

They were from Vesperholt.

They had *always* been from Vesperholt. Born under violet banners, rocked to sleep by the woman with the crooked brow, carried bleeding-out-of-memory through the night by the gaunt man who had spent thirty years searching and finally found them dying in a charm-shop in Corvane—and had given Tamsin his map instead of his warning, because there hadn't been time for anything longer.

The handwriting on the map. Their own handwriting. Of course it was. It was the handwriting they'd had before the cut, buried deep enough to survive, rising up through their fingers whenever they drew.

"I remember," Tamsin heard themselves say. Their voice was wrecked. "Sera—I remember the night it was erased. I was there. I was *born there*." They looked up, and the tears on their face felt like the sap on the trees, like the whole weeping wood was crying through them. "Your father carried me out. I was the anchor. I *am* the anchor. That's why the gate needs me—that's why it's always needed me."

Sera had gone very still. In the dim glow of the sap-lanterns her face worked through shock, through fury, through something rawer than either—and landed, at last, on recognition, the same terrible kind Tamsin had just been handed.

"Then my father didn't die for nothing," she said slowly. "He got you here. He got you *back*."

Above them, all through the Weeping Wood, the ropes of sap began to tremble—thousands upon thousands of them, stirring in a wind that did not blow—as if the trees themselves had understood, and were turning, in their slow rooted way, toward the Spire.

Toward home.

And far off, at the wood's southern edge, thin and clear across the silence, came the sound Tamsin had been dreading since Corvane: hoofbeats, many of them, and the high cold note of Office horns.
