## 8. The Gate That Should Stay Shut

The Spire rose out of the Weeping Wood like a wound that had learned to stand.

Sera Ilves had imagined it a hundred times in the twelve years since her father first whispered the word to her—*Vesperholt*—and every imagining had been wrong. She had pictured towers of white stone, of glass, of bone. What stood before her now was none of those things. The Spire was grown, not built: a column of living wood so dark it drank the dawn, its surface seamed with channels where sap ran slow and glistening, weeping down its flanks as though the whole structure were grieving on behalf of everyone who had forgotten to.

At its base, half-swallowed by roots older than any nation Sera could name, was the door.

It was small. That was the cruelest part. All this—the erased country, the stolen millions, the map drawn in Tamsin's own unknowing hand—and at the end of it, a door no taller than a woman, plain as a cottage gate, banded in silver that had not tarnished because silver does not forget what it guards.

Tamsin stood before it and did not touch it. Sera watched them from ten paces back, arms crossed against the cold that pooled around the Spire like water around a shipwreck. There was something terrible in the way Tamsin looked at the door—not longing, exactly. Recognition. The look of a person reading their own name carved somewhere they had never been.

"You can feel it," Sera said. Not a question.

"Like standing inside a bell when someone else is ringing it." Tamsin's voice was rough. They had not slept; none of them had, not since the river, not since the trees began to bleed memories into the clearing air. "Every memory in this Wood is pressing against me. Like they know what I am."

"What are you?" Sera asked, and hated how much the question cost her. She had spent six days hating this hedge-witch charm-seller who wore her father's death like an inherited coat. Six days of walking beside them, of watching suppressed songs leak out of them at dusk, of listening to a lullaby in Vesperholti rise from their throat unbidden—a lullaby *her mother* used to sing, before Vesperholt stopped being anywhere at all.

Six days, and she still did not have an answer she could hate them for.

"I don't know," Tamsin said. "But I think it knows."

They reached the door together. Up close, the silver bands were engraved—not with words, but with names. Thousands upon thousands of them, ranked in columns fine as spider-silk, wrapping the frame from threshold to lintel. Sera traced one line with her fingertip and felt something answer beneath the metal: a warmth, a pulse, faint and far away, like pressing an ear to a wall and hearing life on the other side.

*These are the anchors,* she thought. *Or they were.*

And there, near the bottom, a name that made her breath stop: **ILVES, MAREK**.

Her father's name. Scratched through, deliberately, by a hand that must have been his own.

"He crossed this out," she whispered. "Why would he cross himself out?"

Tamsin didn't answer. Their hand had risen toward the door's latch—hesitated—and then the forest spoke.

Not with words. With horns.

* * *

The sound came rolling through the Weeping Wood from the west, three notes long and wrong, the kind of sound that makes birds go silent and sap run backward up the trunks. Sera knew it instantly, the way you know a voice from a nightmare. She had heard it once before, from across a market square in Corvane, the day the Pale Office took a charm-seller away in irons and nobody looked up.

"They found us." Tamsin turned, and Sera saw something she had not seen on their face in all their days together: not fear. Fury. "How—"

"The map," Sera said grimly. "Your copy burned, but mine—" She touched the satchel at her hip and understood, all at once, with a lurch of sickness. Her father's original had never left her body since Corvane. And it was warm. Warm as a fevered brow. "It's calling to the Spire. Or the Spire is calling to it. Either way, we've been ringing a bell since we crossed the treeline."

Through the bleeding trees, figures emerged. A dozen at first, then more—white-robed, chalk-pale, moving between the weeping trunks with the unhurried confidence of people who owned everything they walked through. At their center rode a tall figure on a grey horse whose hooves made no sound on the forest floor.

Sera knew that silhouette too. Inquisitor Merrick Hale. The man who had come to Tamsin's shop with questions like knives. The man who, by some arithmetic Sera still could not perform, had let them both live long enough to get here.

The riders halted at the edge of the clearing. Hale dismounted. He did not approach. Instead he raised one hand, and behind him the robed figures began, with dreadful calm, to unpack their instruments: braziers, black jars, long iron rods tipped with sockets that glowed faintly even in daylight.

"What are they doing?" Tamsin breathed.

Sera's stomach dropped. She had grown up on the fragments her father let slip, on the stories he told only after wine, when his grief loosened its grip on his tongue. She knew those jars. She knew what went into them.

"They're going to burn the Wood," she said. "All of it. Every tree. Every memory." Her voice cracked on the last word. "That's what the Office has always done. That's what the erasure *is*, Tamsin—it isn't just forgetting. Someone has to feed the forgetting. Twelve years of cutting memories out of the world and feeding them here, and if the anchor dies or the door opens wrong—" She swallowed. "If Vesperholt comes back wrong, or half-back, or screaming—the Office burns the evidence. All of it. Every last stolen soul in these trees."

The braziers were lit. Even from this distance Sera could smell the fuel—something sweet and wrong, like honey poured over rot.

And then Merrick Hale did the thing Sera would spend the rest of her life trying to understand.

He drew his rod, turned, and struck the nearest brazier from its stand.

* * *

What followed happened fast, the way catastrophes do, each moment arriving before the last one finished.

Hale moved through the Office line like weather. Sera saw him fell two acolytes before they understood their inquisitor's rod was pointed inward rather than outward; saw him hurl a black jar into the undergrowth where it burst and hissed; heard him shouting in a voice stripped raw of all its Office polish—

"The founding crime! Do you know what you're feeding? Do you know what *we* are? Go and look at your own ledgers—look at the first page—"

A senior acolyte, faceless behind a chalk-white mask, raised both hands. The air around Hale thickened, whitened, like frost climbing glass. Sera saw him fight it—saw him plant his feet and tear one arm free and point, not at the acolytes, but at the door. At Tamsin. At Sera.

"*Run!*"

The word cost him everything. The whiteness closed over him mid-breath, and where Inquisitor Merrick Hale had stood there was now only a shape of pale dust holding, for one impossible moment, the outline of a man with his arm still extended—pointing home—before the wind that moves through the Weeping Wood took even that.

The masked acolytes did not pursue. They regrouped around their braziers instead, methodical, patient, and began feeding the fire that would eat the world's grief. They had time. They believed they had all the time there was.

Sera grabbed Tamsin's sleeve and hauled them toward the door.

"No," Tamsin said—but weakly, already stumbling after her, tears freezing on their cheeks whether from grief or terror neither of them could say.

"He bought us minutes," Sera snarled. "We will not waste them arguing."

* * *

The door's latch was warm as a held hand.

Up close, Sera could see that the silver bands were not merely engraved. The names *moved*—slowly, imperceptibly, the way stars move if you watch long enough—flowing upward along the frame like sap, like breath, like a queue waiting at a border that had been closed for twelve years.

Her father's scratched-out name sat at the bottom of the flow. Below it, blank space waited. Below that, another name, half-formed, shimmering: letters assembling and disassembling, unable to settle.

Tamsin's name. Trying to be written.

"Why won't it finish?" Tamsin whispered.

And then Sera's father's voice was in her ears, though he was four years dead and had died refusing exactly this. She remembered the night he told her—the only night—wine-raw and weeping, gripping her wrists hard enough to bruise:

*"The gate doesn't take blood, little bird. It doesn't take lives. It takes worse. It takes a whole self—an anchor's complete memory, everything they ever were, poured out to fill the mold of a nation. My name was on that door because I was born to it. I crossed it out because I looked at you and chose to be a coward. I died a traitor to my country rather than leave my daughter alone in a world with no country in it. Forgive me. Forgive me. I could not pay."*

She had thought, all these years, that he meant he couldn't bear the dying. Now she understood. Dying was nothing. Dying was easy; her father had done it in a gutter in Corvane with a Pale Office knife between his ribs, and dying had kept every scrap of himself intact.

The gate wanted something no one survives having taken.

"It needs a whole memory," Sera said aloud, and her voice sounded like someone else's. "An anchor's entire self. Everything you are, Tamsin. Your name. Your childhood. The shop. The charms. Every song you've leaked, every dream, every grudge, every kindness—you'd pour it all into the door until there was nothing left standing where a person used to be." She was shaking. "My father refused. He crossed out his own name. He let them hunt him across twelve years and die in the street rather than pay it."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. The Spire wept above them. Behind them, far off, the braziers roared, and somewhere in the burning dark, millions of stolen memories were beginning to scream.

"That's why the map was in my handwriting," Tamsin said softly. Not a question. They had worked it out days ago, Sera realized, on the backward river, and had simply been waiting for the courage to say it. "I wasn't given your father's map. I wrote it. Before they cut me. Before I was smuggled out as a child with a hole where my country used to be." They looked down at their own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "I'm not the last anchor by accident, Sera. I'm the last anchor because everyone else paid."

"And you'd do it." Sera's rage arrived all at once, hot and blinding, aimed everywhere—at the Office, at the Spire, at her father, at Tamsin, at herself most of all. "You'd walk in there and let them hollow you. You'd give them *everything*—"

"For a country?" Tamsin turned to her, and their eyes were wet and steady and utterly without self-pity. "For two million people who don't remember they exist? For villages full of mothers singing lullabies in a language the world forgot? Yes, Sera. In a heartbeat. Wouldn't you?"

"Yes!" The word tore out of her. "*Yes*, of course—that's what breaks me open, don't you see? It's not a sacrifice anyone could refuse. That's what makes it monstrous. Love shouldn't come with a price tag like this. Love should—" Her voice broke. "My father loved me more than his country and they called him a coward for it. You love a nation you can't even remember and you'll call it duty. There's no version of this where somebody doesn't lose everything."

Tamsin reached out and took her hand. Their palm was warm. Ordinary. A charm-seller's hand, calloused from knot-work and salt-craft, that had never done anything braver than survive.

"Sera." Quietly. "Hold the door for me."

"Not yet." She gripped their fingers hard. "Not yet. There has to be another way. There's always—"

"There isn't. You know there isn't. Your father knew. Hale knew, at the end—he pointed at this door with his last breath." Tamsin smiled, small and heartbreaking. "Let me choose it. Don't you dare take the choosing away from me too."

The firelight was growing behind them. The names on the silver bands flowed faster now, urgent, a tide pressing at a lock. And below them all, Tamsin's half-written name flickered—waiting, patient as the Spire itself—for the hand that would finish writing it.

Sera put her other hand flat against the door. It beat beneath her palm like a heart remembering how.

"One condition," she said, and her tears fell freely now, freezing as they left her. "Before you go in. Before it takes you. You tell me your name. Your real one—the one you had before they cut it out of you. Because someone has to carry it. Someone has to say it after. Otherwise they win twice."

Tamsin stared at her. And slowly, like dawn coming up over a country that had been dark for twelve years, understanding dawned in their face—alongside something that might, in another life, have become joy.

They opened their mouth to speak it.

Inside the Spire, something vast turned over in its sleep—and began, at last, to listen.
