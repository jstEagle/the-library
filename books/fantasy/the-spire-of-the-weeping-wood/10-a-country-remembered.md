## 10. A Country, Remembered

Sera Ilves did not hear the door open so much as feel it.

She stood at the threshold of the Spire with her father's map crushed in her fist and Tamsin's name still warm on her tongue—she had spoken it into the dark of the gate, *Tamsin Vane, Tamsin Vane*, like a coin pressed into the hand of a departing traveler—and then the world behind her exhaled.

It began as a sound. Not thunder, not a roar; something gentler and far more terrible. It was the sound of ten million people remembering where they had left their coats. It was the sound of a language uncoiling in the throats of farmers who had spent twelve years humming a tune they could not name. The river that ran backward at dusk shuddered along its whole length and turned, obedient once more, toward the sea it had always known. And east—east, where for a generation the villages had ended their roads in shrugging fields and a fog of not-thinking—east filled up.

Sera felt it fill because she was standing in the doorway when it happened, one foot in the world and one foot in Vesperholt, and so she was the hinge. Light came through her like water through a lock-gate. She saw the hills take their names back: the Emberfell Ridge, the Saltmother Coast, the long grey spine of the Vane Mountains, which she had never known were called that until the knowledge arrived in her like a letter postmarked twelve years ago. She saw cities stitch themselves into the mist—Vesperholt the capital first, its spires rising out of nothing the way grief rises, all at once and already old. She heard bells. Bells that had not rung in twelve years because no one remembered why they hung there, ringing now, cracked and joyful and slightly out of tune.

The map in her hand moved. She watched, weeping without deciding to, as the blank eastern half of her father's careful ink bloomed with coastline and forest and road, the lines drawing themselves in handwriting that was not his and somehow always had been. At the center of it, where the Spire stood, a small mark appeared: not an X, not a star. A door, drawn open.

* * *

The villages remembered what lay east the way a body remembers a wound.

In Lowmarsh, where Sera and Tamsin had slept in a hayloft while the ferryman's wife sang a lullaby in a dead tongue without knowing what language it was, the woman dropped her bread in the yard and stood very still for a long time. Then she said, to no one, "My brother went east." She had said those words before, but they had been empty, a stone rolled around the mouth until it lost its edges. Now they had weight. Her brother had gone east, and he had died there—or lived there, or married there—but either way he had gone somewhere real, and she sat down in the mud and cried for him with her hands over her face, and her neighbors came out of their houses already crying too, because everyone in Lowmarsh had someone who had gone east.

In the market town of Corvane itself, three days' ride west, the Pale Office's chalk sigils began to fail. Inquisitors found their own records unreadable—pages that had recorded, in careful bureaucratic loops, that no such country existed, that the eastern territories were simply empty crown land, that the erasure had been necessary, had been merciful, had been *founded upon*. Merrick Hale's superiors burned files through the night, but paper remembers better than people do, and by morning the Office's founding crime sat in the open like a corpse in a square. There would be trials. There would be years of them. Some of the inquisitors would hang, and some would write memoirs, and none of it would give back the twelve years.

But Sera did not see any of this yet. She saw only the Spire's door closing softly behind her, and the wood of it going smooth and anonymous under her palm, as if the door had never been a door at all but merely the tallest tree in a forest that had finally stopped weeping.

* * *

The Weeping Wood had changed overnight.

Where the trees had bled sap in long amber ropes, their bark now gleamed dry and pale as birch. Sera walked among the trunks and put her hand against one—the same trunk whose memory Tamsin had touched, the night of the erasure, the night a child anchor was smuggled out with its past cut away—and felt nothing but wood. Cool, living, ordinary wood. The memories were gone from the trees because they had gone home: into the minds of the grieving, the emptied, the half-remembering, flooding back along invisible channels like water finding an old riverbed.

Except one memory. One small, stubborn remainder that had nowhere to return to, because the person it belonged to had given it away entire.

She found Tamsin—or what had been Tamsin—at the base of the Spire, sitting on a root with their hands open in their lap, watching the light come through the new leaves.

They looked up when she approached, and their face was Tamsin's face, and it was not. The wariness was gone—the flinch Sera had learned to read across a hundred miles of forced company, the constant low calculation of a person who had made safety a craft. In its place was something simpler and stranger: attention. The person looked at her the way a keeper looks at a visitor, kindly and completely, holding nothing back because there was nothing left to guard.

"Hello," they said. Their voice was Tamsin's voice, but lighter, unburdened. "You've been crying."

"You gave it away," Sera said. "All of it. Your name, your mother, your—" She stopped. Her throat closed. "You didn't even hesitate. I held the door and you walked in and you didn't hesitate."

"It wasn't walking in." They considered, head tilted, and for a moment the old cleverness flickered through the new calm like a fish under ice. "It was more like... setting down something heavy at the top of a hill and letting the river have it. I remember deciding. I don't remember what I decided *about*. Is that strange? It doesn't feel strange. It feels like morning."

"Your name was Tamsin Vane."

"Tamsin Vane," they repeated, tasting it. Nothing moved behind their eyes. "That's a good name. Whose is it?"

"Mine now," Sera said, before she knew she was going to say it. "I'm keeping it for you. That's how this works—I figured it out on the walk here. You can give a thing away, but if someone carries it, it isn't erased. It's just... elsewhere." She knelt so their eyes were level. "So I'm going to carry it. Every time I come here, I'm going to say it, and you're going to listen, and someday maybe a piece of it will grow back. And if it doesn't—" her voice broke and she let it— "if it doesn't, then it lives in me, and my children after me, and that's enough. That has to be enough."

The keeper looked at her for a long moment. Then they reached out and touched her wet cheek with two fingers, gently, the way Tamsin had once touched a sleeping bird they'd rescued outside Lowmarsh—a gesture with no history left in it, and yet unmistakably the same gesture.

"You loved them," the keeper said. Not a question. Keepers perceive things.

"I hated them," Sera said. "For most of the road. I blamed them for my father dying on their floor. And then somewhere between the backward river and the bleeding trees I stopped being able to tell the difference, and I think—" She laughed, wrecked and surprised. "I think that's what love is, maybe. When the difference stops mattering."

"They sound worth carrying," the keeper said.

"They were," Sera said. "*Are.*"

* * *

She stayed three days, because there was much to do. Vesperholt needed witnesses—people who had stood at the door, who could testify to what the Office had done and what it had cost. Sera wrote letters until her hand cramped, dictated testimony to the first provisional council assembled under the Emberfell Ridge, argued with cartographers about the true course of the border rivers (the maps, redrawing themselves, had gotten the Saltmother's mouth wrong by two leagues, and she corrected them with her father's map flat on the table, the ink still settling). She found her father's grave—no, not his grave; his house, still standing outside the capital, dusty and untouched, with his coat on the hook and her childhood drawings still pinned above the hearth. Twelve years of absence folded up like a fan. She slept in her own bed and woke at dawn disoriented by the smell of home, and lay there letting the tears come, because grief and joy had stopped being separate things and she suspected they always had been.

On the fourth day she packed a bag, kissed the doorframe of her father's house—for luck, for memory—and rode west, back toward the Wood, back toward the Spire. She carried bread, salt, a flask of plum brandy, and, wrapped in oilcloth, a small book she had begun keeping: a record of everything she could learn or reconstruct about a hedge-witch named Tamsin Vane, charm-seller of Corvane, last anchor of the gate. It was a thin book. It would grow.

The lullaby she carried without needing cloth or paper. The ferryman's wife had taught it to her properly on the second visit, and Sera's voice fit it the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

* * *

The Spire's keeper was waiting at the tree line when she came, as though they'd known the hour, which perhaps they had. The Wood was quiet around them, dry-barked and green, and beyond it the country went on forever—smoke from honest chimneys, the far glint of the capital's bells, a hawk turning over the Emberfell Ridge with the afternoon under its wings.

"I brought offerings," Sera said, holding up the bag. "Bread. Salt. Brandy. Also gossip, if you want it. The Office is falling apart, the ferryman's wife sends her regards, and the hawk over the ridge has nested in the same tree four years running, which apparently matters to someone on the council."

"I want all of it," the keeper said, smiling, and took the bag.

They sat together on the root while Sera talked, filling the silence with the small bright debris of the restored world, and the keeper listened with that total, unguarded attention, asking questions, laughing in the right places. When the sun went low and gold through the trees, Sera paused, took a breath, and opened the little book.

"Now," she said. "Hold still. This part matters."

And she said the name. *Tamsin Vane.* Once for the child smuggled out of a burning country. Once for the charm-seller who perfected the craft of being unseen. Once for the person who set down everything they were so that millions could be anything at all. And once, softly, just for herself, because some payments are made in the direction of the heart and no ledger records them.

The keeper closed their eyes while she spoke. Something crossed their face—not memory exactly, not yet, but the place where memory might someday take root, like a shadow of a shadow moving over still water.

"That's a good name," they said again, opening their eyes. And then, with a small wondering frown, as though hearing it from very far away and very deep: "Say it again tomorrow."

"I will," Sera promised, and meant it for every tomorrow there was.

Far off, past the ridge, the evening bell of Vesperholt began to ring—cracked, joyful, slightly out of tune—and in the doorway of the Spire, drawn faintly in the grain of the living wood, the mark of an open door seemed, just for a moment, to shine.
