## 1. The Voices in the Ivory

The dead man's name was Corvin Hale, and he had been a liar in life, a smuggler by trade, and — if the bone was telling me true — deeply disappointed about his own funeral.

I know how that sounds. Give me a moment.

I sat at my workbench in the back room of the shop on Tanner's Row, lamplight pooling yellow over the piece I'd spent three weeks carving: a memorial rod of whale-ivory, six inches long, meant to sit on a mantel and say *a man lived here and mattered*. Corvin's widow had paid half up front and the other half in a look that suggested she'd pay the rest when she felt like it. Greyhollow economics. Everything is negotiable except the tide.

The ivory had been good stock — dense, pale, old enough to have forgotten being part of anything. That's what people don't understand about bone-carving. You're not shaping the material. You're negotiating with it. Bone remembers being alive, and some of it remembers more than that. My job was to find the shape already sleeping inside the piece and convince it, gently, to wake up wearing a name.

So I carved. Ribs for the border, a wave motif because Corvin had drowned — tasteful, I thought, though maybe too honest — his initials knotted into the crest. And all the while, the way I always do, I listened with the other ear. The one that isn't an ear.

Mostly I hear nothing. Whispers, maybe. A pressure, like weather coming. The sense that a piece of bone is *occupied*, the way a chair is occupied after someone's just left it warm. Nine years of listening, and that's most of what I've gotten: warmth in empty chairs. Enough to make a living. Not enough to hang me.

But lately the whispers had been getting louder. Lately the chairs weren't just warm. Lately they'd started talking back.

I set down the graver, blew the dust from the final line, and held the finished rod up to the lamp. It caught the light along its length, and I felt it settle — that little click behind the sternum, the piece deciding to be itself.

And then Corvin Hale said, clear as a bell through a door: *"You've spelled the wave wrong."*

I dropped the memorial. It rolled off the bench and under the stool, and I sat there with my heart doing something undignified while the voice — patient, faintly amused, very much dead — waited.

"That's not possible," I told the empty room.

*"It's spelled H-A-L-E,"* said Corvin Hale. *"You've cut H-A-I-L. Like weather. My mother will haunt you for that, and unlike me, she has the temperament for it."*

I fished the rod out from under the stool with two fingers, the way you'd pick up something that might bite. Held it to the light again. He was right. Three weeks of work, and my hands had cut the letters they'd always cut, muscle memory overriding the ledger page I'd copied the name from. Hail. Like weather. Wonderful.

"Nobody will notice," I said, to a piece of bone, in an argument I was already losing.

*"His widow will,"* Corvin said. *"She notices everything. It's why I married her and also why I occasionally took jobs out of town."* A pause, and then, quieter, with something underneath the humor that made the hair on my arms stand up: *"You can actually hear me. Nine years I've been dead, and you can actually hear me."*

"I hear impressions," I said. "Feelings. Suggestions."

"You heard a full sentence with correct grammar and a joke in it," said the dead man, and I couldn't argue, because I had.

Here's the thing you need to understand about Greyhollow, and about the Thornfell crews that run it: we are a practical people. We deal in goods that cross borders quietly, in tolls paid in silver and silence, in the fine art of not asking where things came from. We are superstitious only in the ways that are useful. Knock twice before entering the counting house. Never whistle on a night run. Don't take salt from strangers.

And above all, above everything, stitched into every rule the clans have: *nothing touched by the old magic.* No relics. No god-stones. No bone that sings. The last time the Sundered Sea's gods were awake and answering, the wars burned three generations of smugglers off the mountains, and our grandmothers built Greyhollow in the ashes specifically so their grandchildren would never have to choose between faith and dinner again.

A carver who hears the dead? That's not a trade secret. That's kindling.

I wrapped Corvin's memorial in oilcloth, misspelling and all, and I did not sleep much.

* * *

Dario found me at dawn, which was Dario's way. Most people wait to be invited; Dario treated doors as suggestions and my kitchen as common ground. He let himself in, put a kettle on without asking, and looked me over with the expression of a man inspecting cargo for damage.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I've been working."

"You've been working *badly.* There's ivory shavings in your hair. You only leave shavings in your hair when you've been arguing with a piece." He poured two cups, pushed one at me. Dario Okonkwo — no blood relation, but the name stuck when Odile took us both in, and neither of us ever scraped it off. Broad as a doorframe, face like a friendly cliff, and eyes that missed nothing, which was the problem. "What piece?"

"A commission."

"Xanthe."

"A memorial rod. Corvin Hale's."

Something moved behind his eyes. Corvin Hale had run the eastern passes for twenty years; everyone knew him; everyone knew how he'd gone under. Dario sat down across from me slowly, the way you sit down when you're buying time to think. "And?"

"And it's done. It'll be delivered today."

"And?" he said again, softer, and that softness was worse than shouting, because it meant he already suspected and wanted to give me room to lie. Dario was generous like that. He'd been giving me room to lie since we were children, and I'd been taking it since then too, and we'd built a whole brotherhood on top of the space between what I said and what was true.

"It's fine," I said. "Good piece. Clean lines."

He watched me drink tea for a long moment. Outside, Greyhollow woke up around us — cart wheels on wet cobbles, the fish-gulls screaming their eternal grievance, someone down the lane swearing at a mule in two languages. A normal morning. A morning with nothing in it but living people making living noise, and God, how I loved it and how insufficient it suddenly seemed.

"The crews are saying things," Dario said at last. "Little things. Pell says the compasses have been wandering near the coast road. Wren says the chapel steps below the point were dry yesterday, all the way down, which they haven't been since the flood took them. Old Marek swears he heard singing off the water and I told him it was gulls and he agreed too fast." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, precise, deliberate. "The Thornfell families are nervous, Xanthe. When the families get nervous, they start looking for reasons. Reasons with faces attached."

"I'm not a reason. I'm a carver."

"You're a carver who talks to his work."

"I talk *to* my work. Everyone talks to their work. You talk to your rigging."

"I swear at my rigging. It doesn't swear back." He leaned forward. "Xanthe. Brother. Whatever's changing in you — and something is changing, don't give me the face, I've known you thirty years — whatever it is, you bury it deeper. Not because I doubt you. Because I know exactly what the clans do with gifts like yours, and I have buried friends for less."

"Dario—"

"*Deeper,*" he said, and there it was, the cliff-face under the friendliness, the boy who'd fought dock-rats for me when we were nine and would fight armies now. "No pieces that sing. No commissions from anyone who prays. If a client's bone starts whispering, you finish the job, you deliver it, and you forget it. Promise me."

And here's the miserable arithmetic of a found family: they raised you, they fed you, they taught you every skill worth having, and in exchange you owe them the truth — except the truth is the one thing that could burn the whole house down. So you promise. You promise with a straight face and steady hands, and you hate yourself a little more each time, and you tell yourself it's protection. Protection of them. Protection of the family. Protection, protection, protection — the word does a lot of work if you never examine it closely.

"I promise," I said.

Dario studied me, sighed the sigh of a man who has decided to believe a thing because the alternative is unbearable, and stood. At the door he paused. "Odile wants you at the table tonight. There's a run being planned. Big one, through the high pass." His mouth went crooked. "She wants her best guide."

"Tell her I'll be there."

He left. The kettle ticked as it cooled. And I sat alone in my kitchen with my tea going bitter, doing the thing I do instead of praying, which is listening to the quiet and hoping it stays quiet.

It didn't.

Because from the back room — from the shelf where I kept the odds and ends, the offcuts and failed pieces, the little graveyard of experiments no client would ever want — came a sound I had never heard before and will hear until the day I die.

A fragment of grey stone, no bigger than my thumb, a chip of covenant-rock I'd bought years ago as a curiosity, was humming. And threaded through the hum, thin and far away and unmistakable, was a voice I knew better than my own.

My sister's voice.

Sefa had been dead nine years, drowned in the same sea that swallowed Corvin Hale, and in nine years she had never once spoken to me — not through bone, not through stone, not through dreams or grief or the desperate midnight bargains I'd offered every power I could name. I'd made my peace with it. I'd told myself the dead don't answer, that hearing was just hearing, that silence was the natural state of things.

The stone hummed her name.

And beneath Greyhollow, far below the chapel steps that had mysteriously dried, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the water in my cup trembled in rings.
