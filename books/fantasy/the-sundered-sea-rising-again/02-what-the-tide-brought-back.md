## 2. What the Tide Brought Back

The drowned chapel below Greyhollow had been drowning for two hundred years and showed no signs of giving up the habit. Twice a day the tide came up through the sea caves and filled its nave to the second arch, and twice a day it withdrew again, leaving behind weed, brine, and the particular silence of a place where nobody had prayed in earnest for a very long time.

I went there the morning after finishing Master Corvel's memorial bone, because I hadn't slept, and because when I don't sleep the voices get louder, and because — this is the part I'd have lied about, if anyone had asked — a dead smuggler named Tam Voss had told me to.

*Go down where the water prays*, he'd said, through the ivory, in that thin whistling way the dead have of speaking, like wind through a keyhole. *Something answered me last night. Something that isn't one of us.*

I'd told him dead men don't know anything I don't. He'd laughed. It is a strange thing, being laughed at by a bone you carved yourself. It does wonders for the ego.

So: low tide, grey dawn, boots ruined as always. I climbed down the cliff stair behind the salter's sheds, past the racks of fish that smell the same whether they're fresh or three days dead — much like certain clients of mine — and slipped into the chapel through the broken seaward door.

Inside, the air was cold and mineral, tasting of stone that has given up on ever being dry. The pews were gone; salvaged generations ago, no doubt, or rotted into the silt. The altar remained, half-submerged in a pool that never drained, and above it the old fresco of the Sundering had faded to a smear of blue and gold, like a memory someone else was trying to describe to you.

I stood at the edge of the water and felt it before I heard it.

You should understand something about my gift, since you're going to hear a great deal about it. The dead speak to me through worked bone. Not through air, not through dreams, not through any of the respectable hauntings. Through the material itself — the grain of it, the memory of the animal, whatever passes for a soul in a shinbone. Carve a name into ivory and the man who wore that name can lean against it from the other side, and if he pushes hard enough, I hear him.

What I felt in that chapel was nothing leaning against anything. It was the water itself, humming. A note too low to be a sound, felt in the teeth rather than the ears, and threaded through it — faint as a coin at the bottom of a well — a voice saying the same thing over and over:

*Answer us. Answer us. Answer us.*

Not one voice. Many. Layered, patient, ancient. Prayers going out, and something on the far side of them going *yes.*

I am not a brave man. I want that on the record. Bravery is what other people call it afterward, when you survived. What I actually did was stand in a flooded ruin at dawn with my heart going like a drum-crew's payday and think, very clearly: *Xanthe, walk back up the stairs, make tea, and never tell anyone, including yourself, that this happened.*

Then the pool moved.

Not waves. Not tide. It drew back from the altar like a held breath, sliding off the stone in sheets, and beneath it — revealed, gleaming wet in the first real light through the eastern windows — lay a fragment of pale grey stone about the length of my forearm, veined with lines that might have been carving and might have been cracks, except they were too regular for cracks and too wrong for any chisel-work I knew.

Covenant-stone. I knew it the way you know your own name — not learned, just *there*, some knowledge sunk so deep in the bone-carvers' line that it surfaces without permission. Every apprentice in my mother's line heard the stories: the stones that once stood at every crossing of the realms, the oaths cut into them, the gods who kept the terms. And then the Sundering, and the sea rising, and the stones drowned or shattered, and the gods going silent, and everyone agreeing, with enormous relief, to pretend that had been the natural order of things all along.

Nobody had heard an old god in nine generations. That was the whole foundation of Greyhollow, honestly. Free roads, free trade, no divine bookkeeping.

And here was a piece of one, lying in a tidal pool, humming like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

I crouched at the pool's edge. I did not touch it. I want credit for that too — I crouched there for what felt like a full minute, exercising a caution so profound it deserves its own ballad.

Then the fragment spoke, and every scrap of caution I owned went out of me like heat from an open door.

"*Little brother.*"

Nine years. Nine years since Sefa Okonkwo walked into the Thornfell fells with a survey crew and didn't walk out. Nine years of carving bones for strangers' dead, listening to their griefs, doing the work cleanly and well and never once hearing the one voice I would have sold both hands to hear. Nine years of telling myself she was simply beyond reach — past the veil, past the working, wherever the dead go when they're done with the world.

Her voice came up out of that stone like she'd only stepped out of the room to fetch something.

"You're supposed to be dead," I said. My own voice sounded absurd in that vaulted dark, small and cracking. "That's generally how this works."

A laugh — her laugh, quick and low, the one that used to get us both in trouble with Odile. "*Oh, I'm dead. Dead as the tide's uncle. But the veil's wearing thin down here, little brother, and the old ones are waking, and things are getting through that haven't gotten through in a long, long time.*"

"Things," I repeated. "Vague. Good. I love vague."

"*Come closer.*"

"I'm fine here."

"*Xanthe.*" And there it was under the wryness, the thing that made my chest hurt: worry. She was worried about me, dead nine years, and somehow that was worse than everything else. "*Please.*"

I came closer. Of course I came closer. You can dress it up as courage or curiosity or professional interest in anomalous geological phenomena, but the truth is that when your dead sister says *please*, you move.

Up close, the fragment's veins resolved into script — dense, interlocking, older than any alphabet I could read — and the hum in the water rose until I felt it in my back teeth. When I reached toward it, the surface of the pool shivered though my hand hadn't touched it yet.

"What are you?" I asked it. "What *was* it? This whole thing?"

"*A covenant,*" Sefa said, and the playfulness dropped out of her voice entirely, and what replaced it frightened me more than anything the drowned god-hum had done. "*An oath between the realms and the powers under the sea. The stones held it. The carvers wrote it. Then men broke it, little brother — deliberately, carefully, on purpose — and drowned the wardens, and sank the stones, and built a town on the wreckage where no god could reach.*"

"Greyhollow."

"*Greyhollow.*" A pause. In the pause I heard, beneath her, the layered chorus again, that patient *answer us, answer us*, but closer now, as if it had noticed me noticing. "*They're waking anyway. The Sundered Sea is rising again, and the veil between here and there is tearing like wet cloth. And there's a stone on the high fells — the keystone, the one they couldn't fully break — and it's calling for a carver. Only a carver of our line can reforge it.*"

"Reforging it does what, exactly?"

"*Binds the realms together again. Seals the veil. Quiets the gods.*" Another pause, and I hated pauses by then, because I was learning that Sefa's pauses were load-bearing. "*And closes the free roads forever.*"

I sat back on my heels. Cold water soaked through my trousers and I barely registered it. The free roads. The passes through Thornfell that Odile's crews had run for three generations, the routes my family bled and bribed and buried people to keep open. The whole architecture of my life — the found-family, the warm kitchen above the chandlery, Dario teaching Wren knots, Pell cursing at maps — all of it standing on the fact that the border between realms was broken, and profitable, and staying that way.

"And if nobody reforges it?"

The silence that followed was long enough that I looked up, half-sure the vision — or whatever it was — had ended.

"*Then the veil tears all the way,*" Sefa said quietly. "*And everything that's been pressing against it comes through at once. Not just the kind dead, little brother. The drowned ones. The hungry ones. The things the founders broke the covenant to keep OUT as much as to keep their freedom IN.*" Her voice frayed at the edges, and for a moment she sounded less like a ghost than like a woman shouting across a widening gap of wind. "*It's already starting. You'll see it before the week's out. And Xanthe — they've noticed you. The ones waking. They know a carver lives in Greyhollow, and they know your name.*"

"That's not comforting."

"*It isn't meant to be.*" The light through the east windows had strengthened, laying bars of gold across the pool, and the fragment beneath the water seemed almost to glow. "*I have to go. Holding this shape costs more than I—* No. Don't do that face. Listen. The high fells, north of the Split Crown. Follow the voices when they start clamoring — they'll lead you right, even when nothing else makes sense. And little brother—" 

"Yes?"

"*Don't tell them. Not yet. Not Odile, not Dario. Promise me.*"

"Why? They're my family."

"*Because I remember what happened the last time a bone-carver told the clans what the gods wanted,*" she said, and there was something old and grieved in it that had no business belonging to my sister, some knowledge that wasn't hers and shouldn't have been, and then the pool sighed shut over the stone, the hum faded, the cold rushed back in, and the chapel was only a chapel again — flooded, abandoned, ordinary.

I sat there until the tide turned and began creeping back up the nave, licking at my boots like it wanted them, and finally climbed the cliff stair with salt in my hair and my hands shaking in a way I intended to attribute to the cold.

Here is what I decided on the stairs, one step at a time: I would say nothing. I would carve my commissions, run my guides, eat Odile's stew, and keep my mouth shut, because a dead woman I trusted more than anyone living had asked it of me, and because — let's be honest with ourselves, Xanthe, nobody else will — because the alternative meant watching Odile look at me the way the crews look at cursed cargo.

It was a good plan. It was a lie I could live inside for a while, maybe.

It lasted four days.

* * *

On the fourth night, Wren came pounding up the chandlery stairs white as bleached ivory, still in her market cloak, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with the climb.

"There's a woman praying in the square," she said. "Down on her knees, right in front of the tide-bell. Begging. Her boy's lost at sea." She swallowed. "And Xanthe — something's answering her. I can hear it. Everybody can hear it."
