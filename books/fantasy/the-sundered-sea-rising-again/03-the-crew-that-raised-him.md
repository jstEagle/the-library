## 3. The Crew That Raised Him

Odile's kitchen smelled of black tea, burnt sugar, and the particular staleness of a house where too many secrets had been kept too long. I sat at the long table with my hands around a chipped mug and let the warmth of it soak into me like an apology I hadn't asked for.

Nine years in this house. Nine years since Odile Okonkwo found a half-starved boy with bone dust under his nails crouched in her smuggler's cellar, and instead of slitting his throat for trespassing — which was the traditional Greyhollow welcome — she'd fed him stew and asked if he could read a map. I could. I could also do things no one in Thornfell would forgive, but she hadn't asked about that, and I hadn't offered.

"You're staring at your tea like it owes you money," Pell said from the far end of the table, where she had spread three maps and was weighting their corners with whatever came to hand: an inkpot, a knife, a dead man's compass, and half a honeycake she was clearly saving for herself.

"It does," I said. "It's been giving me nothing all morning."

"That's because you drink it like a coward. Water down the leaves twice." She flicked the honeycake out of Wren's reach without looking. Wren, fifteen years old and built entirely of elbows and grievance, withdrew the hand slowly, as though this were a tactical retreat rather than a defeat.

The table filled around us the way it always did before a run: Dario sliding into the bench beside me with the heavy grace of a man who carried other people's cargo for a living, Odile last, taking the head seat not because anyone assigned it to her but because gravity worked that way in Greyhollow. Things fell toward Odile. Debts. Loyalties. Occasionally knives.

"Right," she said, and the room went quiet the way it only goes quiet for her, or for snowfall, or for the moment before something breaks. "The Vesper run."

Pell tapped the middle map. The pass she indicated — Kestrel's Notch, high on the Thornfell shoulder — looked ordinary enough in ink: a narrow throat of stone between two ridges, the old smuggler road threading through like a stitch through scarred skin.

"Vesper silk," Pell said. "Twelve bolts of it, dyed in colors that don't legally exist anymore. Buyer on the far side pays in silver that hasn't been debased since the Sundering. One crossing. Two nights' travel each way. Profit margin that'll keep us fed until spring and drunk until summer."

"And the catch," Dario said. It wasn't a question. There is always a catch; in Greyhollow, the catch *is* the product.

"The catch," Pell agreed cheerfully, "is that the pass is singing."

I kept my face still. My hands were wrapped around my mug, so nobody saw them tighten.

"Singing," Odile repeated flatly.

"Humming. Buzzing. Take your pick." Pell shrugged, which for Pell was an entire aria of discomfort compressed into one gesture. "Scout came back Tuesday. Wouldn't go past the lower cairns. Says the air up there tastes like a struck bell and his teeth have been ringing ever since. He's sleeping with wool packed in his ears and swearing he can still hear it."

Wren leaned forward. Of course Wren did. Wren had never met danger she didn't want to arm-wrestle. "Singing how? Like voices?"

"Like the mountain remembering it used to be something else," Pell said, and then seemed embarrassed by her own poetry, and covered it by stealing the honeycake after all.

Everyone looked at Odile. Odile looked at me.

That was the arrangement, unspoken these nine years: when the roads turned strange, they looked at me. I read stone and weather and the moods of passes the way Pell read stars. I knew which scree slopes lied about being stable and which caves breathed. I was the crew's quiet miracle, and I had built that miracle on a foundation of omission the size of a cathedral.

Because here is what I actually knew, sitting there with my tea going cold and my sister's voice still lodged behind my ribs like a fishhook: the pass wasn't humming because the mountain remembered. It was humming because the drowned gods of the Sundered Sea were waking up, and their rising was thinning the veil between what-is and what-was, and last night in a flooded crypt beneath Greyhollow, my dead sister had spoken to me through a fragment of covenant-stone and told me the world was coming apart along seams the founders of this very town had cut on purpose.

And here is what I said:

"There's old magic in the high fells. Pre-Sundering work. It's been dormant." I took a sip of coward's tea. "Dormant things wake angry. But the pass itself will hold. We go low, we go quiet, we don't touch anything carved, and we're through in two nights. It's doable."

Silence, while everyone weighed it.

Then Odile nodded once, and the matter was decided, because that's how decisions worked at this table, and I had just told the first real lie of my adult life to the only people who had ever chosen to keep me.

* * *

Afterward, Dario caught me in the yard, where I was pretending to check the mules' hooves. Mules are patient accomplices. They don't judge you for lying; they just want oats.

"You hesitated," he said.

"I considered the route carefully."

"You hesitated *before* you answered. You never hesitate. You look at a ridge and tell us which side will kill us, no pause, no flourish." He crossed his arms. He was bigger than me and always had been, broad where I was knotted, but he wore his size gently, like borrowed clothing. "What did you hear?"

"Nothing." The word came out smooth. Smoothness, I've learned, is what guilt sounds like when it's been practiced. "Stone doesn't talk, Dario."

"No," he said quietly. "But you do, when you're scared. You talk more." He didn't push. He never pushed. That was the cruelty of Dario — he simply stood next to the truth and waited for it to come to him, and eventually it always did. "Whatever's up in those fells, Xanthe — whatever's making the scout's teeth ring — if it's got anything to do with you, with your—"

"With my *what*?" I said, sharper than I meant to.

He held my gaze. Brown eyes, steady, hurt in a way he didn't yet have words for. "With whatever you carry," he said finally. "You don't have to name it. Just don't let it spend us."

He walked back toward the house, and I stood with a mule lifting its hoof obediently for inspection I wasn't doing, and I hated myself in a fresh and interesting way.

Here is the arithmetic I couldn't stop running: tell them the truth, and Odile — whose grandmother helped shatter the covenant, though I didn't know that yet, though Sefa's voice had only gotten as far as *the stone was broken on purpose* before the crypt water closed over it — Odile might put me out, or worse, might decide I was a problem to be solved, because gifts like mine had started wars, everyone said so, everyone whispered it. Or say nothing, and lead them into a pass where the veil was bleeding, where the dead walked visibly now, where something vast and hungry was waking with its mouth already open toward the small warm lives I loved best in the world.

Protecting them by deceiving them. It's remarkable how noble that feels from the inside.

* * *

Evening brought Wren to my workshop, which meant evening brought trouble, because Wren only visited the workshop when she wanted something or when the house felt too full of other people's histories and not enough of her own.

She sat on my workbench, which was forbidden, swinging her heels against the drawer fronts, which was doubly forbidden, and watched me carve.

"What are you making?" she demanded, in the tone of someone who has decided the answer will be disappointing in advance.

"A memorial token. Widow Ashgrave's husband." I turned the little piece of sheep femur in the lamplight. A lark, mid-flight, wings just breaking free of the grain. Simple work. Honest work, mostly. "So she has somewhere to put her grief that isn't the dinner table."

"People really believe bones hold the dead?" Wren asked. She asked it carelessly, the way children ask about God, wanting to be told either answer is fine.

"Some do." I set down the graver. "Some believe salt keeps ghosts out. Some believe the sea takes memories as payment. Belief is cheap in Greyhollow. Everything else costs."

"Do *you* believe it?"

There it was, sitting in the lamplight between us, small and terrible. I thought about Sefa's voice saying my name in the dark water. I thought about the widow Ashgrave, who deserved her lark. I thought about Dario standing next to the truth, waiting.

"I believe," I said, "that a good carving gives grief a shape. And shaped grief is lighter than the other kind. Whether anything listens—" I shrugged. "That's above my pay grade."

Wren studied me for a long moment with those too-old eyes of hers, the ones that had already catalogued every exit in every building she'd ever been thrown out of. Then she nodded, satisfied or pretending to be, and hopped off the bench.

"Pell says if the pass sings loud enough, we might hear the old prayers again. Like — actual answers. People are lighting candles at the drowned chapel. Did you know that? Down below the tide wall. Old women, young men, everybody. Praying to gods that are supposed to be dead." She grinned, fierce and delighted, the way only Wren could make catastrophe look like a festival. "Wouldn't that be something? To ask the dark for something and have the dark actually *answer*."

"Wren," I said.

But she was already at the door, silhouetted against the hallway lamp, all elbows and certainty. "First candle I get my hands on," she announced, "I'm asking for a name. Something big. Something worth having." And she was gone, her boots hammering down the stairs, leaving the workshop door swinging.

I stood in the lamplight with the lark half-finished in my hands, and cold walked up my spine on small feet.

Below the tide wall. The drowned chapel. Candles lit over flooded graves, prayers spoken into thinning water, and Wren — bright, reckless, unblooded Wren — intending to offer her name to whatever woke on the other side of the veil.

In the old stories my grandmother's grandmother supposedly told, names were currency in the dark country. Names were claims.

I set down the carving. From the shelf above my bench, among the finished pieces waiting for delivery, a small whale-ivory charm hummed — very faintly, very sweetly, like a struck bell heard through fathoms of water — and I realized I could hear it even though I hadn't touched it, and that it was not humming alone. Every relic in the room had begun, softly, to sing.

Somewhere beneath Greyhollow, something vast was listening to a girl who had promised it her name.
