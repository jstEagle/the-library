## 4. A God Answers Wrong

I want it on the record that I did not pray.

That matters. In Greyhollow, prayer is a transaction, and transactions have terms, and I had never signed anything. I'd grown up in a village where the priestess charged by the syllable — a copper for "O gods who sleep," two for "hear us," and if you wanted an actual miracle you'd best bring your goat. So when the old gods drowned in the Sundered Sea, my mother's generation shrugged and said good riddance to bad debt. I inherited that shrug along with her second-best boots and her talent for running.

Which is why what happened at the drowned chapel was not my fault, no matter how Odile tells it later.

* * *

It was market day, which in Greyhollow means everyone lies to everyone with extra cheer. I was minding Dario's stall of "antiques" — his word; mine would be "things pulled out of bogs" — when the woman came through the crowd like a needle through cloth. Thin as a fishbone, gray at the temples, hands wrung so tight they'd gone white at the knuckles. She stopped in front of me and looked at me the way people look at doors.

"You're one of Odile Vane's," she said. Not a question. Everything about Greyhollow is Odile's, so being one of hers narrows it down only slightly, but she said it like a pedigree.

"I'm one of hers," I agreed. "Wren."

"Wren." She said my name slowly, tasting it, and something in my stomach went cold without asking permission. "They say the Vane crew can get things done. Things the church won't touch."

"Depends on the thing."

"My son." Her voice dropped to a whisper that still carried, because grief has volume whether you want it or not. "Fever. Four days. The new priests pray to nothing and the old ones are dead and drowned, and someone told me — someone told me there's a chapel below the cliff, under the waterline, where the old god still listens. That if you go down at slack tide and you *ask*—"

"Ma'am." I set down the chipped idol I'd been holding. "Whatever someone told you, the chapel's flooded. Has been for a hundred years. People drown there. Regularly. It's practically a hobby."

"He's eleven," she said.

And that was the whole argument, wasn't it. Eleven years old, four days of fever, a mother with nothing left but a rumor and a name she could offer up. She pressed a coin into my hand — an actual coin, worn smooth, stamped with a wave pattern older than the town — and said, "Just tell me it's true. Tell me someone still listens."

I meant to say no. I want that on the record too. But my mouth, which has betrayed me since birth, said: "Slack tide is at dusk. Don't go past the third arch. And don't give it your name."

She blinked. "Give what its name?"

"That's the thing about prayers," I said, hearing my mother's voice come out of my own face. "Somebody always answers. The trick is making sure it's somebody you can afford."

* * *

Here's the part where honesty compels me to admit I followed her.

Not because I believed in drowned gods. Because I believed in drowning villagers, and I've seen what desperation does to people's arithmetic. I told myself I'd watch from the rocks, keep her out past the third arch, and be home in time for Pell's stew, which is terrible but reliably terrible, which in Greyhollow passes for comfort.

The tide was out further than I'd ever seen it. The whole bay floor lay naked and glistening, kelp like spilled hair, and the chapel rose from the mud black and patient — a broken shell of a building with its bell tower snapped off like a snapped finger. The woman picked her way down the stones with her lantern held high, and I slunk along the rock shelf above, feeling like a fool and doing it anyway, because that's most of what courage is.

She reached the door. She stood in the flooded nave, water to her ankles, and she prayed.

Now. I've heard prayers. They're mostly lists. *Heal him, forgive me, let the crop come in, let him come home.* This was not a list. This was a woman emptying herself out onto wet stone, all of it, every fear and bargain and blame, and the words didn't matter anymore than the shape of a keyhole matters. What mattered was the opening.

The air changed. That's the only way I can put it. The pressure dropped the way it does before a summer storm, and the lantern flame bent sideways toward the chapel, toward the dark water pooling in the apse, and something in that water turned over.

I felt it notice her.

I felt it notice *me*, a heartbeat later, the way you feel a stranger's eyes between your shoulder blades.

"*Name,*" said the dark water, and the voice was vast the way the sea is vast, not loud so much as everywhere, coming up through the mud and the rocks and my bootsoles. "*A name is offered. A name is claimed.*"

"It's not—" the woman started, and I was already moving, scrambling down the rocks, because I knew — some animal part of me knew — that she'd brought more than a coin tonight. She'd brought me. My name, given freely in the market, in daylight, to a stranger with grief in her hands. A name is offered.

"*Wren,*" said the water, tasting it. "*Small bird. Small bird, bright throat. Come down. Come be remembered.*"

"Nope," I said, aloud, to a god, which is either very brave or very stupid, and the mud sucked at my boots as I grabbed the woman's arm. "We're leaving. We're leaving now. Ma'am, your son needs a living mother, walk."

The water was rising. Slack tide turning early, or the sea simply deciding it wanted its chapel back. Black water slid up the pillars, and shapes moved in it — not fish. Shapes like hands, like faces half-remembered, like the idea of a congregation drowned a century ago and still showing up out of habit.

"*Claimed,*" the dark said again, and the word landed on me like a hand on my shoulder, heavy and cold and proprietary, and I understood with total clarity that somewhere in the ledger of old things, my name had just been written down.

That's when Xanthe came out of the dark like vengeance with a satchel.

I never heard him approach — I never do; the man moves like smoke — but suddenly he was between us and the apse, and he had something in his raised hand: a small carved token, bone-white, no bigger than a thumb. He'd been carrying it, I realized later. He carried them the way other men carry knives.

"Wrong name," Xanthe said to the water. Calm. Conversational, almost. Like he was correcting a merchant short-changing him at market. "You'll want to strike that entry."

"*Who speaks?*" The whole bay seemed to lean in.

"A carver," Xanthe said, "on behalf of the unclaimed." And he threw the token into the rising water.

What happened next, I will be describing badly for the rest of my life. The token hit the surface and the water *screamed* — not loudly, worse than loudly, intimately, a sound inside the ears and behind the eyes — and every drowned shape in the flood turned toward that little bone carving like flowers toward sun. The pressure lifted off my shoulders so suddenly I staggered. The woman made a sound like a hinge and sat down hard in the shallows.

And Xanthe stood there in the wash, soaked to the knees, listening — actually listening, head tilted, to something none of the rest of us could hear — and whatever he heard made his face do something complicated and sad before he schooled it back to blank.

"Go home," he told the woman gently. "Boil willow bark. Honey and hot cloth. He'll break the fever by morning." And then, quieter, to the receding dark: "And you. No claims. Not tonight."

The water sank. The lantern stood up straight. The bay exhaled.

Xanthe turned around and found me staring at him, and the woman staring at him, and behind us both — because of course, because the universe has comic timing — the rest of the crew coming down the rocks with lanterns of their own, drawn by the wrongness in the air like moths.

Dario first, blade already out. Then Pell, whose curses are famous in three provinces. Then Odile.

Oh, hell, I thought. Oh, hell and taxes.

* * *

Odile Vane is not tall, but she has the gift of standing in a room as though the room were built around her, which technically this one had been, centuries ago, before the sea took it. She looked at the woman — who fled up the rocks clutching her lantern like a stolen child — then at me, dripping, then at Xanthe, standing in the ebb with a satchel full of bone tokens and nowhere left to hide.

"Someone," Odile said, in the voice she uses when the news is going to be expensive, "explain why the drowned chapel was singing."

"The Maren woman's boy is sick," I said. "She prayed. Something answered. It — took my name. Claimed it. And then Xanthe—" I gestured helplessly at him. At the token. At the whole impossible evening.

"Xanthe what?"

He didn't flinch. I'll give him that. He reached into his satchel, slow and deliberate, and drew out another carving — a little figure no longer than a finger, a woman with folded wings — and held it up in the lantern light.

"They talk," he said. "The dead. In the bones. They've talked to me since I was a child. I hear them when I carve. I heard the god come, and I heard what it wrote down, and I broke the claim before it could collect." He swallowed. "I'm sorry. I know what you'll say. But the alternative was Wren."

The silence that followed had texture. I could have cut strips off it and sold it as leather.

Pell broke it, because Pell always breaks things: "You're telling us you've got the *hearing*. The old blood. The thing the Thornfell crews used to drown people for."

"I'm telling you I carve names into bone and the names answer," Xanthe said. "Yes."

Dario's face was doing something I'd never seen on it — his brother-face, the easy one, cracking down the middle. "How long?"

"Since Sefa died."

"Nine years," Dario said. "Nine years, and every run, every pass, every time we walked through something old and humming and you said *it's fine, it's nothing, keep moving*—"

"It usually was fine."

"That's not the point!"

"No," Odile said, and the point arrived in her voice like a nail driven flush. "The point is that gifts like yours started wars, boy. Before the Sundering. Whole mountainsides burned because some carver heard a dead king whisper and decided it was scripture. My grandmother crossed the sea to get away from men who spoke for gods." She looked at him for a long moment, and I watched something close behind her eyes, a door shutting softly. "You should have told me."

"And you'd have kept me?" Xanthe asked quietly.

"I'd have *decided*," Odile said. "That's the difference. That's the whole difference." She turned away, cloak snapping, and paused at the foot of the rocks. "Back to Greyhollow. All of us. We'll speak on it when the tide's right." Which is Odile for *you are in trouble*, and also Odile for *you are still mine*, and I couldn't tell which weight was heavier.

Dario walked past Xanthe without looking at him. That was the worst part. Worse than the god, worse than the screaming water. Nine years of brotherhood, and he walked past like Xanthe was a stranger who'd lied beautifully.

Xanthe stood alone in the ebb, lantern light catching the little winged figure still in his hand, and I saw his lips move — just slightly — answering something none of the rest of us could hear.

"What did it say?" I asked him. "The god. Before you broke the claim. What did it want?"

He looked at me, and for once he didn't hide any of it, and that was somehow scarier than everything else.

"It said the others are waking too," he said. "All of them. And there's a stone in the high fells that's supposed to hold them — and it's broken." He closed his hand around the carving. "And it thinks I'm the one who can fix it."

Above us, far up the black shoulder of Thornfell, lightning flickered inside a cloudless sky — once, twice, patient as a knock at a door.

Nobody said anything. Nobody had to. We all felt it: the mountain, humming, waiting to see what we'd do next.
