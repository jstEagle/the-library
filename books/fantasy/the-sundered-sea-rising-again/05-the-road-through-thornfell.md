## 5. The Road Through Thornfell

The pass had a smell before it had a sound. That was the first wrong thing.

Thornfell in autumn smells of pine rot and cold iron and, if the wind comes off the Sundered Sea, a faint brine that makes the mules sneer. This morning the brine was there, all right, but underneath it sat something sweeter and older, like incense burned in a room nobody had opened for a hundred years. I said nothing about it. I'd gotten very good at saying nothing about things; it was practically my trade, alongside bone-carving and lying to customs officers with a straight face.

"Wind's turning," Pell announced from the front, because Pell announced everything, usually twice, usually as though she'd invented weather personally. "We make the high cut by noon or we camp in the open like idiots. Your call, Odile."

"My call is we don't camp in the open like idiots," Odile said. She rode her mule the way she did everything: as if the mule should be grateful for the arrangement. "Xanthe. The road."

The road. Right. That was my job — reading stone and scree and the little cairns the first carvers had left, finding the safe line through Thornfell where no safe line officially existed. Nine years of guiding smugglers through passes that appeared on no map, and I'd never once been lost. I'd also never once heard the dead this loudly.

Because they were loud now. They'd been getting louder since the drowned chapel, since Sefa's voice rose out of that fragment of covenant-stone and turned my careful, manageable secret into something with a pulse. In Greyhollow I could still pretend it was weather. Out here, under the peaks where the veil between realms had gone thin as wet paper, the dead came off the mountainside in waves.

*The oath,* they said. *The oath, the oath, you who carve, you who remember, the oath is broken—*

I kept my eyes on the trail and my hands loose on the reins and thought about toll schedules.

* * *

By midmorning the veil started bleeding through in earnest, and there was no pretending otherwise, because Wren saw it too.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Wren asked, pointing with the entire arm, the way children point. Eighteen years old, three months escaped from somewhere bad, and still carrying the unhelpful honesty of someone who hadn't yet learned which questions get people killed.

The *that* in question hung over a shelf of granite like heat-shimmer, except it was silver, and it moved against the wind. Where it touched the rock, the rock remembered being something else — I watched a boulder flicker into a standing stone carved with faces, then back again, like the mountain couldn't decide which of its histories was true.

"No," Dario said flatly. He'd been flat all morning. Dario did flat the way other men did shouting. "Nothing about this run is supposed to do anything. That's why we're paid triple."

"We're not paid triple," Pell said. "We're paid double and promised triple, which is different arithmetic entirely."

"It'll be triple when we deliver," Odile said, "if we deliver. Which depends on our guide." She looked at me, and it wasn't an accusation, exactly. It was worse. It was trust, offered plainly, in front of everyone, so that refusing it would be its own confession.

"The high cut is still clean," I said. "The shimmer stays off the trail. Keep to the cairns and nothing touches us."

It was mostly true. The dead didn't want to touch the living; most of them had forgotten how. But they wanted something from me, and they were getting less polite about asking, and every step deeper into Thornfell turned their murmur up another notch until it felt like walking through a market square built entirely of grief.

*Carver.* That word again, passed hand to hand down the centuries. *Carver, carver, you who took up the chisel — your order swore. Your order swore and the stone was shattered and the sea has no law now—*

"I need a minute," I said, and swung down before anyone could object, and walked ten paces off the trail, and pressed my palm against a cairn-stone so old its carving had worn to suggestion.

The voices came through like water through a cracked dam.

They told me what I'd half-known since the chapel, in a hundred overlapping registers — some whispering, some shrieking, one old man reciting it in what sounded suspiciously like verse. The first bone-carvers hadn't just guided travelers. They had been wardens. Every safe road through Thornfell, every pass that let clans move without waking what slept beneath, existed because the carvers' order had sworn an oath at a great stone set in the highest fells — an oath that bound the realms in balance, that kept the drowned gods dreaming and the living roads honest. And then the founders of Greyhollow had shattered the covenant-stone on purpose, and the free roads had bloomed like weeds in the crack, and the carvers had died out or gone quiet or learned, like me, to say nothing about things.

*You are the last who hears,* said a voice near my palm, young and tired. *You are the last, and you walk past our graves hauling contraband.*

"Xanthe." Dario's voice behind me. Not loud. Dario didn't need loud. "Odile wants to know why you're talking to rocks."

I hadn't realized my lips were moving. Nine years of practice, undone by a mountain full of the insistent dead.

"Just checking the cairn marks," I said. "Some of them have shifted."

"Some of them," Dario said, "have been shifted by you, maybe. Because you've been strange since the chapel, brother. Stranger than usual, which for you is saying something. You hear things out here the rest of us don't. Don't think I haven't noticed. Don't think *she* hasn't noticed."

"Dario—"

"We're family," he said, and his voice cracked on it, just slightly, the way good stone cracks — along a fault line somebody put there long ago. "That's the whole religion we've got. You remember that, whatever else is going on in your skull."

I remembered it. That was precisely the problem.

* * *

The realm-storm caught us an hour past noon, and after that there was no more pretending for anybody.

It came off the high fells without warning — one moment silver haze, the next a wall of it, roaring down the pass like a tide given legs. Odile shouted the order to shelter under the great leaning slab the crews called the Parson's Hat, and we made it — barely, Wren last, dragged in by the collar by Pell while the shimmer closed around us like a fist.

And inside the storm, the dead became visible.

I had seen them my whole life the only way I could — heard, never seen, voices without bodies. Now the veil was so thin that even the living got my affliction at a discount. They stood among the rocks in their hundreds: fishermen crusted in salt, clan-mothers in dress that predated Greyhollow by a thousand years, soldiers whose wounds were still fresh after however many centuries. They weren't attacking. That was somehow worse. They were *waiting*, all facing the same direction — up-trail, toward the high fells, toward whatever remained of the covenant-stone — and murmuring their single word like a congregation.

*Oath. Oath. Oath.*

Wren made a small sound and grabbed my sleeve. "Xanthe. Xanthe, they're looking at us — no. No, they're looking at *you*."

They were. A few dozen of the nearest had turned, salt-white faces fixing on me with terrible recognition, and the murmur changed. *Carver,* they said, aloud now, audible to everyone, because the veil didn't care who heard anymore. *Carver. Last of the order. The oath, carver. Restore the oath.*

"What," said Pell, very quietly, "is a carver?"

The storm held us for two hours. Two hours of the dead murmuring, of Wren shaking, of Odile sitting her mule with her arms folded and her face doing absolutely nothing at all, which any child raised in Greyhollow knew was the most frightening thing a person could do. When the shimmer finally thinned and rolled back up the fells, the dead faded with it, all but their voices, which stayed with me like ink under skin.

We rode on in silence for a mile. Then Dario hauled his mule across the trail in front of mine.

"You knew," he said.

"Dario—"

"*You knew.* You stood in that storm and they called you by name — by *title*, whatever that is — and you're going to stand there and tell me this route was just a route? That you picked the high cut for the toll schedule?" His voice climbed. Dario never climbed. "Those things — those *dead* — they want something and they want it through you, and you've led your family straight into the middle of wanting it. Admit it."

"Dario," Odile said, "enough."

"No, let him answer." He wasn't even looking at her. He was looking at me with nine years of brotherhood and something new behind it, something that hurt worse than anger. "Answer me, Xanthe. Are you guiding us, or are you delivering us? Because there's something up that mountain that talks to you like you owe it, and I'm starting to think we're the payment."

The dead, helpfully, chose that moment to murmur through my skull — *the oath, the oath, go up, carver, go up* — and I watched Dario see it happen. Watched him read my face, the tiny involuntary turn of my head toward the fells, the way a compass needle can't help itself.

"There," he said softly. "There it is. Gods' sake, Xanthe. Or rather — *their* sake."

"Dario, that's enough." Odile's voice this time had frost in it, but her eyes, when they touched me, had the frost too.

"They called him *last of the order*," Dario said. "Orders have purposes. Purposes have costs. And I'd like to know, right now, before we go another step, whether the cost is going to be paid in us."

I opened my mouth to lie. I was good at it; it was practically my trade.

What came out instead was: "Not here. Tonight. At camp, I'll tell you everything. All of it. I swear it on the crew."

Dario held my eyes a long moment. Then he wheeled his mule around. "Tonight, then," he said, and the words landed like a debt being entered in a ledger.

We made the far side of the pass by dusk, alive, cargo intact, trust bleeding out slowly onto the scree behind us like something we'd wounded and couldn't stop up. Wren wouldn't look at me. Pell hummed tunelessly, which was her version of screaming. And Odile rode beside me the whole last mile without saying one single word, which from Odile was a sentence longer than any she'd ever spoken.

At camp, while Dario built the fire with more force than fire required, Sefa's voice found me through a chip of covenant-stone I'd carried from the chapel, hidden in my coat pocket against my heart like a guilty thing.

*Xanthe,* she said, gentle as tide over sand. *Tomorrow you reach the stone. Tomorrow everything changes. Tell them tonight — but tell them gently, love. Some truths you have to carry like eggs.*

Above us, in the dark where the stars should have been, the shimmer of the veil pulsed once, slow, patient, like something breathing in its sleep.
