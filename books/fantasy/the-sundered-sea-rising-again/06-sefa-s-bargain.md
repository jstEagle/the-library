## 6. Sefa's Bargain

The dead, I have learned, do not knock.

They simply arrive, the way damp arrives in Greyhollow — through the walls, under the doors, into your boots when you aren't looking. So it should not have surprised me that Sefa came to me at three in the morning, while I sat in my workshop pretending to carve a whale-tooth amulet for a client who had never once, as far as I could tell, been near a whale.

"Brother," she said, from the covenant-fragment wrapped in oilcloth on my bench. "We need to talk."

I dropped the burin. It clattered off the table and rolled somewhere under the shelving, which is where all my tools go to die.

"Nine years," I said. "Nine years of nothing, and now you want to *talk*."

"You were busy." A pause with her old teasing in it, the ghost of a ghost. "And I was dead. Neither of us is blameless here."

I pressed my palms flat against the bench and breathed the way Odile taught us — four counts in, six out, because panic makes smugglers sloppy and grief makes them worse. The fragment sat there like a shard of grey glass, no larger than a fist, humming faintly against the wood. Through it I could feel her the way you feel a fire through a closed door: warmth, presence, the shape of someone on the other side.

"What happened to you?" I asked. "After the flood. After—" I couldn't say *after I failed*. "Sefa."

"After the water took me? I went where the drowned go. Down into the Sundered Sea, into the long dark where the old gods sleep. And then, brother, they started waking up." Her voice tightened. "And everything down there began to move again. Including the oaths."

"The oaths?"

"The covenant." She let the word sit between us like something heavy being set down carefully. "You've heard them talking about it. All those voices in the passes, clamoring about broken promises. You thought they were mad, didn't you?"

"I thought the dead were generally chatty and rarely coherent. Like sailors."

"They are coherent." And now she wasn't teasing at all, and the warmth through the door became heat, became something urgent. "Xanthe, the covenant-stone wasn't shattered by accident. It wasn't worn down by weather or cracked by an earthquake. It was broken. Deliberately. With hammers, with iron, with intent. By the founders of Greyhollow."

I stared at the fragment. Outside, the town was doing its pre-dawn business — gulls screaming, carts rattling over cobbles, Pell swearing at his mule in two languages. Ordinary sounds. Sounds built on top of something else entirely.

"When?" I managed.

"A hundred years ago. Your Odile's grandmother was there. Her name is carved into the breaking, along with eleven others. They called themselves the Free Roads Compact." Sefa's voice went strange — not angry, exactly. Sad the way stone is sad, which is to say patiently, for centuries. "They broke the covenant so the gods' law would stop following their people across the borders. Before the breaking, every road was watched. Every bargain witnessed. You couldn't steal what was sworn to another, couldn't cross a ward without paying toll to powers older than mountains. The clans chafed under it. So they broke it."

"And the sea rose instead," I said slowly. "And the realms drifted apart. And the veil thinned everywhere except—"

"Except along the roads the founders kept for themselves. The smuggler roads. The free roads." She laughed, and it was a bitter sound, which was unlike her. "They didn't free the world, brother. They freed one road and dammed the rest. Every soul that drowned when the Sundered Sea swallowed the lowlands — every village, every chapel, everyone who prayed to the old gods and got silence — that silence was purchased. Paid for by the founders so their grandchildren could move contraband unwatched."

I thought of Odile's hands, knotted and steady, teaching me to read currents. Of the stories she told about her grandmother, the great Maren Okonkwo-Vale, who "held the clans together through the hard years." Held them together. Over a broken god-stone. On a foundation of drowned prayers.

"How do you know this?" I asked. "You've been dead nine years."

"I've been *down* there nine years. Among the drowned. Among the founders, too — they're all dead now, and the dead keep excellent records. Guilt is very durable, brother. It outlasts flesh." A pause. "There's more. There's always more. The reforging — if you reforge the stone, the covenant returns. All of it. Watched roads. Witnessed bargains. Divine law laid back over every pass in Thornfell like frost over grass. The free roads end. Forever. No crew crosses without paying the old tolls. Greyhollow as it exists — smuggling, freedom, the whole ragged glorious mess of it — ends."

* * *

I did not sleep. Instead I sat in the workshop until the light came up grey through the salt-fogged window, turning it over, looking for the flaw in it, the way I look for the flaw in a piece of bone before I commit the knife. Bone always tells you where it wants to break. Truth does too, usually. This truth wanted to break right through the middle of my life.

At noon, Odile sent for me.

Her house sat above the harbor like a gull above a fish-market, all angles and advantage. Inside smelled of tea and pipe-smoke and forty years of decisions. She was at her table with maps, and Dario stood behind her with his arms crossed, and Wren perched on the windowsill trying very hard to look like furniture. Pell lounged by the fire, sharpening a knife he had sharpened twice already that morning, which meant he was nervous.

"Sit," Odile said.

I sat.

"Dario tells me you've been hearing things." Her voice was level, which from Odile was worse than shouting. "Voices in the pass. Voices since the tide changed. And that you took something from the flooded chapel below Greyhollow without telling anyone."

"It's a memorial piece," I said. "For a client."

"Xanthe." Just my name, and the weight of it settled on my shoulders like a pack full of stones. "I have known you since you were eight years old and bit a customs man. I have lied for you, bled for you, buried friends beside you. Do not lie to me now."

So much for the flaw-finding. The bone broke where it wanted to break.

"There's a fragment of the covenant-stone in my workshop," I said. "My sister speaks through it. And she's told me why the veil is thinning, and why the dead are loud, and why the passes hum like struck wire." I made myself meet Odile's eyes. "Greyhollow's founders broke the covenant. On purpose. To free the roads. Your grandmother was one of them."

The silence afterward had texture. Pell stopped pretending to sharpen. Wren's feet went still.

"My grandmother," Odile said finally, "was a woman who made hard choices in hard years, and I will hear no slander of her from a bone-carver who talks to ghosts."

"It's not slander. It's the reason the lowlands drowned. It's the reason half the coast prays to gods who can't answer. Someone bought your family's freedom with a hundred years of other people's silence."

"And you believe a dead girl's word over mine?"

"I believe my sister," I said quietly, "who has been silent herself for nine years, and has no reason left to lie to me."

Odile rose. She walked to the window, and for a long moment she looked out at the harbor, at the boats, at the grey water that had given her family everything.

"Here is what happens next," she said, without turning. "If what you say is true — if the covenant comes back — then the roads close. Not just for us. For every clan, every crew, every child we feed out of the margin we scrape between kingdoms. You'd end the only life these people have ever known because the dead asked nicely."

"Not because they asked. Because it's owed."

"*Owed*." She turned, and her eyes were flint. "Choose, then, Xanthe. Here, now, in front of all of us. The crew or the gods. The living or the dead. Because I'll not carry a man through Thornfell who's listening to instructions from the other side."

Wren made a small sound. Dario shifted, and I caught his eye, and what I saw there wasn't anger. It was fear — fear for me, dressed up as anger, the way it always was with him.

"The stone can be reforged," I said. "Only a carver can do it. Only one who hears the dead. Which means—"

"Which means it's you," Odile said. "Of course it's you. The gods always find their instrument and dress it up as a choice." She picked up her tea, drank it cold, set it down. "So choose."

I looked around the room. At Pell, who had taught me stars. At Wren, who reminded me of me at fifteen, all sharp edges and nowhere to put them. At Dario, who had carried me out of a burning warehouse when I was twelve and never once mentioned it. At Odile, who had found a starving boy in the reeds and decided, against all evidence, that he was worth feeding.

Every road I had ever walked had been walked with these people.

"I'm sorry," I said.

Odile nodded once, as if I'd confirmed an order of goods.

"Then you walk alone," she said. "Out of Greyhollow by dawn. If you take the high road to the Sundered Stone, you don't come back down it. That's not cruelty, boy. That's arithmetic. I have thirty mouths depending on roads that stay open, and I'll not gamble them on your ghosts."

"Odile—" Dario started.

"Quiet." But her voice cracked, just slightly, on the word, and I understood that this cost her more than she would ever let any of us see. "Dawn, Xanthe. Take what you need. Leave what you love."

* * *

I packed by lamplight: carving knives, oilcloth, dried fish, the fragment wrapped soft against my chest where it murmured to me in a voice like water over stones.

"You could stay," Sefa said gently. "You know that. Live small and quiet. Let the world sort its own debts."

"You're the one who woke me up."

"Yes. And I've regretted it every hour since, watching what it costs you." Her voice softened further, until it was barely distinguishable from memory. "Brother. When you get to the stone — whatever it asks of you — remember that you were loved first by the living. Don't trade them for me."

"I'm not trading anyone," I said, and slung the pack over my shoulder, and stepped out into the fog-grey street where Greyhollow lay sleeping on its stolen freedom, unaware that dawn was coming to collect.

Behind me, light as breath, I heard footsteps fall in with mine in the dark — and stop, and turn away.

Almost, I turned back.

Almost.
