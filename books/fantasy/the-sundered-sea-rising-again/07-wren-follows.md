## 7. Wrefollows

That's a joke. It should read:

## 7. Wren Follows

Wren had stolen three things in her life worth bragging about: a silver compass from a tax collector who deserved it, a week of freedom from the workhouse that raised her, and — as of two hours past midnight — a look at Xanthe Okonkwo's face when he thought no one was watching him leave.

It wasn't a good look. That was the trouble.

She'd expected guilt, maybe. Guilt she could work with; guilt was just fear wearing a nicer coat. But what she'd seen, lit by the single lamp he allowed himself on his way out of Odile's house, was something worse. Resolve. The kind of calm people get right before they do something stupid and permanent, like confessing to a crime or marrying into nobility or walking alone into the high fells where the veil between worlds had gone thin enough to see through.

He hadn't even taken the good rope.

* * *

The thing about following a bone-carver through Thornfell at night is that bone-carvers are, professionally speaking, quiet. Xanthe moved up the goat-track above Greyhollow like a man who'd been apologizing to the mountain his whole life for being on it. Twice Wren lost him entirely and had to guess at his path from the way the gorse bent. Once she lost her footing altogether and spent a long, undignified moment hugging a boulder while loose stone rattled down into darkness, and she said several words Pell would've charged her for.

The moon wasn't helping. It hung low and swollen over the peaks, the color of milk going off, and the higher she climbed the more it seemed less like light than like an eye deciding whether to open all the way.

She caught up with him at dawn, at the mouth of a pass she only knew from Pell's maps — the Hollow Stair, marked on every chart the crew owned with three little strokes meaning *don't*. He stood with his back to her, looking up at walls of rock that hummed. Not wind-hummed. Hummed the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have stopped.

"You're loud," he said, without turning around.

"I'm *deliberate*," Wren said. "There's a difference, and I'll thank you to respect it."

He turned. Up close, in the grey first light, he looked like he hadn't slept, which pleased her meanly, because she hadn't either. "Go home, Wren."

"No." She came to stand beside him, mostly so he couldn't loom. He wasn't tall enough to loom, but he managed it anyway, the way quiet people did. "Odile sent Dario out the west road to cut you off, which is a waste of a perfectly good brother, because here you are, east. And I'm not going back to tell her I lost you. Do you know what she does to messengers? She doesn't shoot them. She makes them *say it twice*."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile's poor cousin. "You shouldn't be here. This pass isn't safe anymore."

"Neither's Greyhollow. Neither's anywhere, if half of what the dead are saying is true—"

"You don't hear the dead."

"No," Wren said, and stepped closer, jabbing a finger up at the humming walls. "*They're* audible. To everyone. Which means whatever's happening up there, it's not your secret anymore, carver. So either you tell me why you're marching alone toward a god's front door, or I follow you anyway and get us both killed, and then you'll have my death on your conscience along with everything else you're carrying, and honestly? You don't strike me as having the room."

He looked at her for a long time. Wind came down the pass and it smelled wrong — salt, deep-sea salt, though the Sundered Sea lay days away and below them, drowned under its own old magic.

"Fine," he said finally. "Walk. But stay behind me, and if I say run, you run, and you don't ask toward what."

"See, that's the spirit of partnership I was promised when I joined this crew."

"It isn't a partnership. You're baggage."

"Luggage," she corrected. "Baggage has feelings."

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the world got thinner.

Wren had heard the phrase *veil between realms* her whole life, dropped by smugglers and priests with equal vagueness, and she'd assumed it was one of those things people said the way they said *honor* and *interest rates* — words that stood for something real but that nobody wanted to examine closely. She examined it now. Because by midday the air itself had begun to go translucent at the edges, and through the translucence, things were visible.

A second landscape. Laid over theirs like wet silk on a table.

Hills beneath the hills. A sea beneath the sky. And on the slopes of that other country, figures — hundreds of them, thousands, standing still as fenceposts, all facing the same direction. Facing the covenant-stone. Facing wherever Xanthe was going.

"Don't look at them," Xanthe said quietly.

"They're looking at me."

"Don't answer, either. Some of them will ask you things. Names, mostly. Never give your name up here."

"Too late. Something already took it." She said it lightly, but his head snapped around fast enough that she regretted the joke instantly. "What? When?"

"First night. A villager prayed at the drowned chapel and something answered wrong, and it asked for names, and I—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "I gave mine. It was going to take Xanthe's otherwise. It seemed fair trade at the time."

Xanthe stared at her, and Wren watched him arrive at the arithmetic — that she'd traded a piece of herself for him without telling anyone, without telling *him*, and that he'd been carrying his own secrets so long he hadn't noticed hers. His face did something complicated. She looked away first.

"The storm's coming," he said instead. "We need shelter before dark. There's a cave ahead, an old watch-post. We'll make it if we hurry."

"What kind of storm?"

"The kind where the dead stop being a metaphor."

* * *

The realm-storm hit at dusk, and Wren learned that everything she'd seen during the day had been the storm warming up.

It didn't come from any direction. It came from *between* — pressure first, a squeezing behind the eyes, then light that fell upward, and then the second world tore fully loose from the first and poured across the pass like water finding its level. The hills beneath the hills became the hills. The sea beneath the sky became the sky, vast and inverted and full of drowned stars.

And the dead walked.

Not figures anymore. People. They came down the slopes in their hundreds, and they were *close*, close enough to touch, close enough that Wren could see the details the daylight ghosts had hidden — a fisherwoman with net-marks scarred into her palms, a boy in a soldier's coat three sizes too big, an old man whose lips moved endlessly around words no living throat had spoken in centuries. They didn't seem to see the living. They walked through boulders. One passed through Wren's shoulder and left a coldness there that took minutes to fade, like a handprint made of winter.

She pressed against the cave wall beside Xanthe and discovered she was shaking. "This is what you hear," she said. "All the time. All of *this*."

"All of this," he said. "Quieter. Usually."

"How do you stand it?"

He was quiet for a while. The dead streamed past the cave mouth, patient as weather. "You don't stand it," he said finally. "You get very good at pretending you're somewhere else. I've been somewhere else since I was nine years old."

"Wren," said a voice.

Both of them went rigid. At the edge of the cave's light stood the boy in the too-big coat, and his eyes had found her now, fixed and hungry, and his mouth shaped her name again. "Wren. Wren. You gave it. You gave it and we kept it. Come and be remembered. Come and be *kept*—"

Xanthe moved faster than she'd ever seen him move. His hand went into his coat and came out with something white — a carved token, small as a coin, and he pressed it into her palm and closed her fingers over it hard. "Hold this and say nothing. Whatever it offers. Say *nothing*."

The boy-thing drifted nearer, and the cold rolled off it in waves, and Wren clenched the token until its edges bit and kept her teeth shut so hard her jaw ached. It hovered there a long moment, wanting. Then, frustrated or called elsewhere, it thinned and shredded apart into the storm-wind.

She let her breath out in pieces. "What is this?" She opened her hand. The token showed a knotwork pattern she recognized from his memorial-work, except this one was unfinished, deliberately open at one side.

"A door that closes," Xanthe said. "I've been making them for years. For clients. For—" He stopped.

"For yourself," Wren said. "For Sefa."

His whole body flinched around the name. And in the storm-light, with the dead streaming past and nowhere left to hide, Wren watched Xanthe Okonkwo decide — visibly decide — that the pretending was over.

"She died nine years ago," he said. "Drowned off the Sundered coast. I never heard her voice again. Not once, in nine years, when I hear everyone else — every stranger, every murderer, every beggar who ever died within a day's ride of my carving bench. And then three weeks ago the gods woke up, and the first voice out of the flood was hers. Asking me to reforge the covenant-stone." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Do you know what the crew thinks? That I betrayed them. That I led Dario into that pass for the gods' sake. Odile thinks my gift is the seed of the next war. And the truth is stupider and worse: the dead want me to fix a border that keeps our family fed, and I can't refuse them, because one of them is my sister, and I would burn the free roads and every road besides for nine seconds more of her voice. That's the truth. That's the whole ugly ledger."

The storm howled. Wren turned the token over in her fingers.

"Odile told us once," she said slowly, "that you were found on the tide-line. Nine years ago. Same year."

"Sefa washed ashore near Greyhollow. Dead. I followed her body inland and never left."

"And you never told anyone why."

"Why would I? Grief's private. And the crew—" He gestured vaguely at the storm, the dead, the thin bright ruin of the world. "Crews survive on not-knowing. I learned that early. Every secret I kept was a wall between the dead and all of you. If they'd known what I am, what I hear, they'd have started watching me. Fearing me. Or worse, *using* me. I kept silent because silence was the only way I knew to keep you safe from my life."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan I had!"

"Well, it's a terrible one!" Wren was shouting now, over the storm, over the dead, and she found she didn't care who or what heard. "You built walls and called it protection and let us think you were cold, and Dario called you a traitor, and Odile looked at you like a weapon that might go off, and meanwhile you were just — *grieving*, you idiot, grieving alone in a house full of people who love you, because you decided love meant lying to us! That's not protection. That's just loneliness with good intentions!"

The words rang off the cave walls and out into the storm, and for one absurd moment Wren imagined the dead stopping to listen.

Xanthe stared at her. Rain that fell upward beaded in his hair. "You followed me," he said at last, "into a realm-storm, to shout at me."

"Somebody had to. Nobody else in that family shouts. It's a health hazard, all that politeness." Her voice cracked halfway through and she hated it. "You could have told me, Xanthe. Of all of them. I gave a *name* for you. I'm owed at least one truth."

He crossed the cave in three steps and pulled her into a hug that was less embrace than collapse — the hug of a man setting down something he'd carried too far. She held on. Around them the dead walked and the seas hung wrong-side-up in the sky and the world came apart at its seams, and none of it, somehow, was as frightening as how much she'd been afraid for him.

"If you die up here," she said into his shoulder, "Odile will make me say it twice."

"I'll try not to die."

"Try harder than that."

* * *

The storm broke near midnight, and the two worlds settled back into their uneasy layering, and the dead receded up the slopes like a tide going out — all but the ones nearest the summit, Wren noticed. All but the ones gathered at the broken stone.

They climbed through the small hours, and as the sky paled, Xanthe stopped so suddenly she walked into his back.

Ahead, on the ridge against the bruised horizon, stood the covenant-stone — shattered into five great leaning shards, glowing faintly, humming the note she'd been hearing in her teeth since yesterday. And between the shards, filling the gap like a hand in a glove, stood something vast. It had shapes where a person would have features, and the shapes kept changing, and every version of it was looking directly at Xanthe.

Beside it, smaller, human, unmistakable even at distance — Dario. Axe in hand. Waiting.

"Oh good," Wren breathed. "Everyone's here."
