## 8. The Sundered Stone

I had imagined, on the long climb, that I would arrive at the covenant-stone with some kind of plan. Carvers are planners by nature — you don't set chisel to ivory without knowing where the grain runs. But the stone had other ideas about grain.

It stood in a bowl of bare granite at the top of the world, or what passed for it in Thornfell, and it was shattered the way a promise is shattered: not broken clean, but splintered into a dozen leaning shards, each one taller than Dario, each one humming. Not with sound. With *attention*. The air around it tasted like the moment before lightning decides where to strike, and every hair on my arms stood up and introduced itself to its neighbors.

"Well," I said aloud, because talking to yourself is the first sign of wisdom or ruin, and I'd never been accused of the former. "Hello again."

The shards answered. Of course they did. They'd been answering since Greyhollow, since Sefa's voice rose out of that drowned crypt fragment and cracked nine years of silence wide open. Now it wasn't just her. It was hundreds — no, thousands — of the dead, layered like sediment, all of them pressing against the inside of my skull with variations on a single theme: *finish it. finish it. carver, finish it.*

"Patience," I muttered, unpacking my tools on a flat shelf of rock. Bone-carving tools, yes, but also the heavier ones I'd bought off Pell with three weeks' wages and a lie about a commission in Lowmarsh. Chisels meant for stone. For covenant-stone, if the old texts Sefa used to read aloud were to be believed — and she'd been dead nine years, so her sourcing couldn't be fact-checked, which made her the most reliable scholar I knew.

The largest shard bore script. Old script, pre-Greyhollow, the kind that predates the smuggler clans deciding that gods were bad for business. I ran my thumb along the grooves and let myself listen the way I let myself do nothing else in life: completely.

*We swore,* said a voice like wind through a rib cage. A woman's voice, very old. *Bone for boundary. Breath for binding. Let no hand unmake what hands together made.*

"And then someone's hands unmade it anyway," I said. "Grandmother Odile, as it turns out. Lovely family tradition."

The dead did not laugh. The dead, in my experience, have a terrible sense of humor — which is ironic, given that they're the only audience guaranteed to get every joke eventually.

I set the first chisel against the great central shard, found the grain, and drew back to strike.

That was when the sea arrived.

There is no sea on top of Thornfell. There hasn't been since before the Sundering, since before the old gods went down with their drowned chapels and their flooded crypts and their patient, patient silence. But I heard it anyway — heard it the way only I could hear anything anymore, which is to say with bones rather than ears — a vast rolling pressure, a tide coming in across stone that has never once been wet. The humming of the shards changed key. The dead went quiet, all at once, the way birds go quiet.

Then something looked at me.

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left. It didn't arrive. Arrival implies distance traveled. This simply *was*, the way a headache is, the way grief is — suddenly present and impossible to argue with. It filled the bowl of granite without displacing any of it. If my eyes had been permitted to work properly, they might have reported something like a figure, something like a crown of coral, something like an expression. Instead they reported light bending wrong and my own heartbeat, visible through my shirt, keeping time with a tide that wasn't there.

***CARVER.***

Its voice didn't come through the bone-sense. It came through everything. Granite rang with it. My teeth sang with it.

"You're loud," I said. Wry narration, remember. It's a coping mechanism. "For a god who's been underwater for nine hundred years."

***PAYMENT WAS SWORN. THE COVENANT DEMANDS ITS DUE.***

"I know. I've read the fine print." I hefted the mallet. "Problem is, everyone who signed it is dead, and everyone who broke it is rich, and I'm just the guy holding the chisel."

***THEN YOU UNDERSTAND.*** The pressure of its attention narrowed, focused, became almost gentle, which was infinitely worse. ***A DEBT MUST BE PAID. AND YOU HAVE COME ALONE TO PAY IT.***

*Alone.* That word landed harder than any mallet stroke. Because I had chosen alone. I had walked out of Odile's kitchen with her ultimatum hanging behind me — *the crew or the gods, boy, and if you choose wrong, don't come back* — and I had chosen, and I had told myself the choice was clean.

Nothing about it had been clean. Nothing about me had ever been clean. I was a man built out of other people's leftovers: a dead sister's books, a smuggler family's charity, a gift nobody wanted.

I raised the mallet.

***WAIT.***

And then the god did the cruelest thing I have ever experienced, and I include in that ranking the day Sefa died, because at least that day had the decency to be honest about what it was taking from me.

***I CAN GIVE HER BACK.***

The mallet stopped being in my hands. Or my hands stopped being around the mallet. One of those. The world contracted to a single point, and in that point —

"Sefa."

Not the voice-in-the-bone version. Not the echo-from-a-crypt-fragment version. Her actual voice, nine years older than the last time I'd heard it, because the dead don't age but this would not be the dead. This would be *her*. Standing on the granite. Frowning at me the way she frowned when I overworked a piece, arms crossed, one eyebrow up, alive and annoyed and *mine*.

***THE STONE STAYS BROKEN, AND SHE WALKS OUT OF THE SEA AT DAWN. BREATHING. CARVING. ARGUING WITH YOU ABOUT YOUR TOOL MAINTENANCE. ALL OF IT.***

My knees did something unsanctioned. I caught myself on the great shard, and its humming ran up my arm like a confession.

Here is the thing you need to understand about temptation: it doesn't lie. That's what makes it temptation instead of threat. Everything the god offered was true. Leave the stone broken, keep the roads open, keep the clans free, keep Odile's family whole and Wren safe and Dario employed — and get Sefa back. Every single thing I loved, preserved, in exchange for the one thing I'd been asked to give up. It wasn't even a hard bargain, mathematically. Any accountant in Greyhollow would have signed it with both hands.

But Sefa had spent her whole life reading the old texts aloud while I carved, and there was one passage she always read twice, tapping the page: *A covenant reforged is worth more than a wish returned, because the wish serves one heart and the covenant serves all of them.*

She'd written that in the margin herself, actually. In ink that was probably contraband.

"She'd hate this," I said. My voice came out rough. "You know that? She'd absolutely despise being used as currency. She'd lecture you for a week about the ethics of necromantic leverage."

***ALL SOULS ARE CURRENCY, CARVER. YOURS MOST OF ALL.***

"Yeah," I said. "See, that's exactly the theology she hated."

I reached for the mallet.

Which was when Dario hit me.

* * *

To be fair to him, he announced himself first — a bellow of "XANTHE, DON'T" from the edge of the bowl, roughly two seconds before his shoulder met my ribs and carried us both sprawling across the granite. The mallet skittered away. My chisel rang off a shard. And my brother, my enormous, loyal, catastrophically literal brother, rolled to his feet between me and the stone with his knife already drawn.

"Odile sent me," he said. "Stop at any cost, Xanthe. Those were her words. I'm asking you — please — make the cost small."

The god's attention swung toward him like a lighthouse beam, and Dario flinched — everyone does; it's involuntary, like blinking — but he held his ground, knife up, legs braced, protecting a piece of rock from a man holding a chisel. That was Dario in miniature. He'd have fought the Sundered Sea itself with a boat hook if he thought it was threatening family.

"Dario." I got my feet under me. My ribs were going to file a complaint. "You can't stop this with a knife."

"Wanna bet?" He was crying, actually. He'd deny it later, but he was. "You've been strange for weeks. Since the chapel. You hear them, don't you? The dead. You've *always* heard them, and now they've got you climbing mountains to fix their broken rock, and Odile says — Odile says gifts like yours started the Godswars, Xanthe, she says—"

"Odile's grandmother *broke* the stone, Dario." I said it quietly, because shouting it would just make him dig in. "Her own blood. To free the clans from divine law. The free roads exist because of a shattered oath, and every run we've ever taken, every coin we ever earned, grew in that crack."

His knife arm dipped. Just a fraction. "That's a dead woman talking."

"It's several thousand dead people talking, actually, and they're remarkably consistent." I stepped sideways, toward the mallet. He tracked me. "Brother. Please. Move."

"No." Fresh tears, furiously blinked back. "You move. You walk away, and we go home, and we never speak of this mountain again. That's the offer. That's the *only* offer, because I am not — I cannot—" His voice broke clean through. "I already lost one sibling on a mountain road. I will not lose another one to a *god*."

Nine years ago, Dario had carried Sefa's body down out of Thornfell. Nine miles, they said. Alone. He'd been seventeen.

I picked up the mallet anyway.

***INTERESTING,*** rumbled the god, with what I can only describe as relish, ***AND NOW THE OTHER OFFER STANDS OPEN TO HIM ALSO.***

Dario stiffened. I saw it happen — saw the god's attention pour into him the way water finds a crack, saw my brother's eyes go wide and fixed as the offer landed: whatever Dario wanted most, dangled like bait. I didn't know what it was. That was the horror of it. After twenty years of sharing a bunk and a trade and a family, there was a want in my brother big enough for a god to grip, and I hadn't known it existed.

"Dario," I said. "Whatever it's showing you — it's real, that's the trick. It doesn't lie. It just makes sure the truth costs somebody else."

He turned to look at me, and his face was terrible and young and drowning. "Xanthe. It says if I stop you — if I break your hands — it'll give her back too. Both offers. Either of us. It says—"

"I know what it says."

***CHOOSE,*** the god said, gentle as deep water. ***BROTHER AGAINST BROTHER. ONE OF YOU WILL SERVE ME GLADLY. THAT IS ALL COVENANTS HAVE EVER ASKED.***

And Dario raised his knife at me, weeping, hating it, and I raised my mallet at him, heartsick, certain, and above us both the shards began to sing.

Somewhere below, faint on the wind off the fells, I heard — with ears, actual ears, not bone-sense — a sound I knew far too well.

Wren's whistle. Three notes sharp. The clan signal for *incoming, and she's mad.*

* * *

The storm broke over the bowl of granite like a dropped jug — realm-light everywhere, the dead stepping visibly out of the air, and cutting up the slope through all of it, small and furious and impossibly alive, came Wren, with Pell's spare coat flapping and murder in her eyes.

"Oh good," I breathed. "Reinforcements."

Dario lowered his knife half an inch. "Whose side is she on?"

"Historically?" I reset my grip on the mallet. "Mine. Loudly. Against everyone's advice."

The god turned its attention toward the small furious shape climbing toward us, and I felt, with absolute clarity, the exact moment it decided to make one more offer.

I swung at the stone instead.
