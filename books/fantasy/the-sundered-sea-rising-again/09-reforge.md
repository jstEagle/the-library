## 9. Reforge

The god made its offer the way all the best liars make theirs: gently, and with my own face on it.

*She can come back,* it said, in a voice like water finding the lowest room of a drowned house. It stood at the edge of the shattered covenant-stone, vast and half-shaped, wearing — I am not making this up — Sefa's smile stretched across something that had no business having a mouth. *The stone stays broken. The roads stay open. The girl walks home to Greyhollow by morning. Everyone wins.*

"Everyone except every soul between realms," I said. "But sure. Let's lead with the dead sister. Very persuasive."

Behind me, Dario's boots crunched on frost-bitten gravel. He had come up the fells with a knife and Odile's orders, and he was breathing hard in that way he did when he'd run the last mile rather than admit he'd been afraid for me. I didn't turn around. Turning around would have meant seeing his face when I said what I was about to say, and I am many things — bone-carver, liar, smuggler, brother — but I am not brave enough for that.

"No," I told the god.

*Xanthe.* My name in Sefa's voice. It reached into my chest and squeezed like a fist around an old wound.

"I know," I said quietly. "I know exactly what you're doing. You found the shape of her in the stone fragments, the way I find shapes in ivory. You carved her from my grief because grief is soft material." My hands were shaking. I let them shake. "It's good work. Honestly. Better than mine."

The god's borrowed smile flickered. Gods hate being complimented by their marks.

I knelt at the covenant-stone.

Nine years I had carried Sefa's silence like a stone in my boot — small, familiar, always there when I walked. And now here was the cruelest arithmetic of my life: the reforging demanded a carver give up the very sense that made the work possible. My hearing of the dead. Every voice in every relic, every whisper in whale-ivory and river-bone, gone. Including hers. *Especially* hers.

Sefa had died nine years ago in a flood that took half of Greyhollow's lower quarter, and I had never once heard her since. Not through any bone, any relic, any prayer. The other dead came to me like gulls to a fish cart. She never came at all. For nine years I told myself she was at peace, or beyond reach, or — in my worst hours — that she had chosen not to speak to me.

Then the old gods woke, the Sundered Sea rose again, and her voice rose out of a fragment of covenant-stone like a cork surfacing after a drowning. And the first thing she'd done was guide me here.

"You could still take his offer," Dario said behind me. His voice was rough. "Xanthe. Look at me. You could have her back. Odile would forgive — eventually. Pell would grumble. Wren—"

"Wren followed me up a mountain into a realm-storm because I lied to her family for her own good," I said. "Wren doesn't get a vote in this. That's the problem with collecting strays; they develop opinions."

I set my palms against the stone.

It was warm. That was the wrong thing for mountain granite to be, in the wind, in the dark, with snow coming sideways off the high fells, and it was warm the way a fever is warm — heat without comfort. Under my hands I felt the fracture lines running through it like veins, and along those lines, faint as breath on glass, I heard them: the first bone-carvers, dead four hundred years, still apologizing.

*We broke our oath,* they murmured. *We broke it and the world paid interest ever after.*

Four centuries ago, the carvers who made the covenant between the realms were also the ones who shattered it — bribed, or frightened, or simply tired of gods having opinions about mortal roads. Greyhollow's founding families, Odile's grandmother among them, had built an empire of smuggler roads in the crack. And the crack had been widening ever since, and things were starting to fall through it, and the drowned gods of the Sundered Sea were waking up cold and furious and hungry for someone to hold accountable.

That someone, it turned out, was a bone-carver with bad knees and a talent for hearing things he shouldn't.

Typical.

*I will help you,* Sefa said. Her voice came from everywhere now, from the stone itself, from the storm, from the place under my ribs where I kept her. *Hold the pieces. Speak the oath. I'll carry the words.*

"Sefa." I closed my eyes. "When it's done. When I can't hear you anymore. Will you—"

*I was never yours to keep.*

The words went through me like a chisel through green ivory — clean, deep, final.

"I know," I whispered. "I've known for nine years. I just liked pretending the silence meant something else."

*It meant everything else. It meant I trusted you to live.*

Gods. Even dead, my sister could gut me with one sentence. It ran in the family. She'd learned it from watching me lie to Dario for a decade.

I began to carve.

Not with tools. There was no tool fine enough, no blade that could cut a covenant back together. I carved with the thing itself — my hearing, my gift, the sense that had made me strange and useful and lonely my whole life. I reached into the fracture lines and pulled the broken edges toward each other the way I'd pull a splintered antler tine true, feeling for the grain of the original oath, following it down into the stone's deep structure. The dead helped. All of them — the first carvers, the drowned sailors, the nameless thousands whose voices had crowded my skull since the veil began to thin. They sang the old words into my hands and I set them into the stone like inlay.

And with every word I set, I lost something.

First the small voices went — the muttering background hum of the near-dead, the gossip of recent ghosts. Then the far ones, the deep ones, the chorus I'd lived inside since childhood. It wasn't pain. That surprised me. It was quietness, spreading outward from my own ears like ice forming across a pond, and underneath the quietness, a grief so clean and enormous it barely registered as feeling. More like weather than sorrow.

*Almost,* Sefa said, and her voice was thinner now, more echo than person. *Last line. Say it true.*

"The realms bound, balanced, held," I said. My voice cracked on *held*. "By carver's hand and carver's cost. So sworn. So sealed. So—"

The god hit the stone like a wave hitting a sea wall.

The world went white and roaring. I felt the covenant shudder under my hands, felt the last seam straining, felt my hearing tear loose all at once — a soundless snap, deep in my head, like a rope parting — and then the god was *in* the space where the voices had been, filling it, screaming its fury into the exact hollow it had spent my whole life trying to occupy.

*YOU GAVE UP EVERYTHING FOR NOTHING, CARVER. THE STONE—

—and then Dario was there.

I saw it happen in the strobing light of the realm-storm: my brother, who had crossed the fells to stop me, stepping between me and the god with his arms spread wide and his knife out — useless, absurd, a paring knife against a hurricane — and shouting, in the flat Greyhollow accent we shared:

"He's my brother! Whatever he broke, whatever he owed, whatever your drowned law says — you don't touch him! You want payment? Take it from the ones who broke it first! Take it from the founders! Take it from *us*, but you don't touch him!"

He was crying. He'd deny it to his grave. He was absolutely crying.

And the strangest thing happened, which is that the god stopped.

It loomed over us both, that vast half-remembered shape with Sefa's smile dissolving off its face like frost in sunlight, and it looked at Dario — really looked, the way you look at something unexpected — and the fury in it banked down to something almost like confusion. Almost like recognition.

*A brother shields a carver,* it said slowly. *As in the old days. As in the oath's first telling.* It turned its attention to me, and there was no bargain left in its voice, only a kind of grim, ancient fairness. *The cost is paid. The covenant holds. Go home, bone-carver. You will not hear us again. Pray that is mercy.*

The light went out of the storm. The pressure lifted. And in the sudden ordinary dark, with snow falling straight and quiet as sifted flour, I knelt before a whole covenant-stone and listened to nothing at all.

No dead. No whispers. No Sefa.

Just the wind, and my own ragged breathing, and Dario's hand landing on my shoulder, heavy and warm and shaking.

"It worked?" he asked. "Xanthe. Did it work?"

I opened my mouth to answer and realized I couldn't hear myself either — no, that was wrong, I could hear *myself*, my own voice bouncing back off the stone, thin and human. What I couldn't hear was anything else. The world had gone down to its bones: wind, snow, breath, my brother's heartbeat when he pulled me up and gripped my arms too hard.

"It worked," I said. My voice sounded enormous in the silence. Everything sounded enormous. "It's done. The road through Thornfell is closed, Dario. The passes are closing. Odile's grandmother's empire ends tonight."

He stared at me. Snow gathered in his eyebrows. Somewhere below us, faintly, the first light of dawn was finding the lowlands, and I thought I could see — or maybe I imagined it, maybe I would always be imagining things now — the grey line of the Sundered Sea, risen again, calm as glass.

"You gave up your gift," he said. Not a question. "You gave up *her*."

"She was never mine to keep," I said, and it hurt less than I expected, saying it out loud. Almost everything does, once you finally say it out loud.

Dario looked at me a long moment. Then he laughed — a wet, broken, furious laugh — and hauled me into a hug that cracked a rib I'm fairly sure was already cracked, and said into my hair, "Odile is going to kill you. If the god doesn't first. She sent me with a knife, Xanthe. A *knife*. Against *that*."

"So you're defecting, then."

"I'm defecting," he agreed. "Come on. We've got a long walk down, and you need to tell me every single lie you've told me since you were twelve, in order, so I can decide which ones to stay mad about."

We started down. But halfway to the treeline, I stopped, because far below us in the pre-dawn dark, small figures were climbing up through the snow — three of them, moving fast, one short and stubborn and unmistakably carrying a lantern she had absolutely stolen from Odile's stores.

Wren had said she'd follow me. I should have known better than to doubt it.

What I couldn't have guessed — what made me grab Dario's arm and point, heart hammering in the new silence — was who walked beside her.

Because the third figure climbing toward us through the snow, wrapped in Odile's own storm-cloak, was Odile herself. Matriarch of Greyhollow. Keeper of her grandmother's secrets. Coming up the mountain in person, at dawn, after sending a knife.

And I had a terrible, wonderful suspicion about why.
