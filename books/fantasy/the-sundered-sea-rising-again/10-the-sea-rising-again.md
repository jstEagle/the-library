## 10. The Sea Rising Again

Silence, it turns out, is not one thing.

I had assumed — foolishly, the way I assume most things — that losing the dead would leave a hole where they'd been. A cavity in the skull, a ringing absence. What I got instead was simply quiet. The ordinary kind. The kind everyone else had been living inside all along, like fish who never think about water and then wake up one morning as air-breathing idiots with a headache.

The last voice I heard was Sefa's, and I will not cheapen it by repeating it here. Some words are load-bearing. You don't take them out to show people.

After that: nothing. Dario's shield-arm across my chest, the god's fury breaking over us like weather, Wren screaming something I couldn't hear — all of it arrived as motion and light without sound's meaning, and when the stone sealed itself under my bleeding hands with a shudder that went up through my wrists and into my teeth, the world snapped shut around me like a door in winter.

Then there was just the mountain. And my brother, breathing hard beside me. And a boy with soot on his face asking me over and over if I could hear him, and me, for the first time in thirty years, having to say no.

* * *

The walk down from the fells took four days. It should have taken two, but Dario insisted on setting the pace of a funeral procession, which was thematically appropriate but practically exhausting.

I learned things about silence on that walk. I learned that Wren hums when he thinks he's alone, badly, some smuggler ballad about a mermaid with poor judgment. I learned that Dario cracks his knuckles exactly twice before speaking when he's working up to an apology, and that his apology, when it finally came on the third evening, was eleven words long and contained the word "brother" twice, which for Dario was practically an oratorio.

"You could've told me," he said. That was the whole of it, more or less.

"I did tell you," I said. "In chapter five. You called it a trap."

He grunted, which I heard perfectly well. Grunts travel at any volume.

Wren filled the rest of the road the way water fills a hull — constantly, cheerfully, and with a growing sense of inevitability. He narrated everything. The color of lichen. His theories about whether the god we'd bound still had opinions about him specifically. Whether I was going to be strange now, deaf-strange, the way old Pell was deaf in one ear and compensated by shouting opinions at people from across rooms.

"I'm not going to shout," I said.

"You're already shouting," he said. "Inside your heart."

"The gods' sakes, Wren."

But here is what I didn't tell either of them, walking down through Thornfell with the covenant humming somewhere behind us like a struck bell too low for ears: I wasn't afraid of the quiet. I was afraid of Greyhollow. Of Odile, and the accord she'd have to sign, and the look on her face when the roads she'd spent sixty years keeping open closed like a hand making a fist.

You can survive a god. Gods are honest about wanting to kill you. It's family you have to watch.

* * *

Greyhollow smelled the same when we came down to it — tar and brine and the frying onions that perpetually haunted the Lantern Row — but it looked wrong, the way a face looks wrong after crying. The drowned chapel stood open to the sky now, its flooded crypt drained, the sea pulled back from it like a tongue retreating. People stood around it in clusters, touching the old stones the way you touch a scar you've decided to stop being ashamed of.

Prayers were being answered openly in the streets. Not the vast, half-remembered horror that had taken Wren's name back on the coast — those were small gods, local ones, the kind that lived in chimney smoke and tide-pools and asked very little beyond being remembered correctly. An old woman pressed her palm to a wall and thanked it for her grandson's fever breaking. Two fishermen argued amiably about which of the Ninefold Tide had actually returned their boat, each convinced it was his patron.

The border between realms wasn't gone. It was *settled* — thickened into law instead of wound, a membrane instead of a hemorrhage. Things crossed when they were meant to cross. That was the whole of the new covenant, carved in my own hand into stone older than any nation: balance, consent, return. The Sundered Sea rising again, but this time within banks of its choosing.

And the roads — the free roads, the smuggler passes that Odile's grandmother had bought with a broken oath three generations ago — those were closing. Not violently. They were simply filling up with *meaning*, which is fatal to smuggling. A pass that hums with divine attention is a pass with customs officials made of light.

Which left Odile.

She received us in the long room above the chandlery, sitting behind the same scarred table she'd sat behind since before I could carve, and she looked at me for a long moment with eyes the color of a storm deciding.

"So," she said. "The bone-carver who couldn't keep his mouth shut even after the gods took his hearing."

"Deaf, not mute," I said. "Unfortunately for everyone."

Pell snorted from the corner. Wren beamed at me like I'd come home from war instead of from a mountain, which, honestly, close enough.

Odile rose. She crossed the room slowly, the way she did everything that mattered, and she stopped in front of me, and for one terrible moment I thought she was going to strike me, and for one stranger moment I thought I might deserve it.

Instead she put her hand on the side of my face, the way my mother must have done once, before both our mothers became stories.

"You gave up your gift," she said. "For a stone. For *their* law."

"For balance," I said. "And because someone had to. Your grandmother broke it. Someone Whitlock-adjacent ought to fix it."

That earned a sound from her that in a younger woman would have been a laugh. Then she turned away, and picked up the document lying on her table — the accord, drafted in the weeks since the sealing, brought down from the fells by intermediaries who spoke for the waking gods and for the coastal towns and, apparently, for me, since my name was on it in a hand that pretended to be mine.

She read it standing. We all watched her read it. Nobody breathed except Wren, who breathed constantly and audibly, like a bellows with anxiety.

"They close the high passes," she said at last. "All of them."

"Yes."

"And in exchange the clans keep their ships, their harbors, their names, and their necks."

"Yes."

"And you signed this."

"You'll notice," I said, "that my name appears to have been forged by someone with better penmanship than me. I'm told that's how accords work. Someone has to be the respectable fiction."

Odile set the paper down. Took up the pen. Held it over the signature line for a duration I could have carved a memorial plaque in, and then — muttering something in the old harbor cant that I am legally certain was a curse on my entire lineage — she signed.

"There," she said. "The Okonkwo boy out-negotiated the Okonkwo matriarch using a piece of paper he didn't write. If you ever tell anyone I hesitated, I'll deny it and also drown you."

"She loves you," Pell stage-whispered to me. "That's her loving voice."

"I know," I said. "It's very similar to her threatening voice."

"That's how you know it's real."

* * *

The house on Lantern Row hadn't changed. Of course it hadn't; houses don't, that's their whole trick. Same crooked stair, same smell of wood-shavings and tallow and the ghost of every meal Dario had ever burned. My workbench sat where I'd left it, dusted — dusted, someone had been dusting my tools, which meant Wren, because Dario considered dust a form of weather and Odile considered my tools beneath her notice.

I stood in the doorway of my own workshop for a stupidly long time, holding a lamp, listening — the habit of a lifetime, listening — to nothing.

No dead murmuring in the ivory. No sister's voice rising out of the offcuts in the scrap bin. No ancestors critiquing my technique from whatever bone happened to be nearest. Just the house settling, and the sea a few streets away doing its enormous patient work, and upstairs, faintly, Wren humming his terrible mermaid song while Dario told him to stop in a tone that had never once worked.

I picked up a rib of whale-ivory, old stock, beautiful, and held it to my ear like a shell.

Nothing.

I put it down and, after a moment, began carving anyway. Not a memorial. There was no one to carry a message for anymore; the dead and I had settled our accounts. Just a shape — a wave, maybe, or a bird, or whatever the ivory decided it wanted to be when it grew up. For the first time in my life the work was only work, and I found, somewhat to my disgust, that it was enough.

They came down one at a time, the way families do when they're pretending not to check on you.

Dario first, leaning in the doorway, arms folded, radiating brotherly surveillance. "You eating?"

"At some point."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer of a man holding a chisel."

He accepted this, because he had to, and stayed anyway, pulling up the stool he'd sat on since we were boys, saying nothing, needing nothing said.

Then Pell, who inspected my half-carved wave-bird, pronounced it "adequate, for a man with no mystical advantages," and stayed to argue with Dario about shipping lanes under the new accord, loudly, with gestures.

Then Wren, who didn't pretend anything at all. He just came and sat on the floor against my bench like a dog claiming a hearthstone, and said, "So what do you hear now?"

"Nothing," I said. "Everything. You, mostly. You're very loud."

"Good." He grinned up at me, soot still faintly in the creases of his smile, and I understood that he'd asked the question on purpose, to give me the chance to say the answer out loud. That boy was going to be dangerous in about six years. Possibly to me specifically.

Odile came last. She didn't sit. She stood in the lamplight looking at the four of us crammed into my workshop like the world's least profitable crew, and her mouth did something complicated, and then she said:

"Ships still need provisioning. Harbors still need runners. The accord closed the passes, not the trade." A pause. Her eyes found mine. "There's work, if a carver wants it. Honest work." Another pause, heavier. "With us. If he wants."

It cost her. I watched it cost her, every syllable, and I loved her completely for spending them anyway.

"I want," I said.

"Well." She straightened, brisk again, armor back on. "Then someone should tell this crew we sail on the morning tide, and someone else should stop carving birds and start carving cargo manifests, because the gods may be reasonable now but harbormasters never have been—"

"*Matriarch,*" Pell said, "breathe."

Outside, the sea rose and fell against the seawall, steady as breath, bound and balanced and answered-to, and the drowned chapel bell rang once across the water — though whether anyone rang it, or whether the tide itself had learned to, I couldn't say and no longer needed to ask.

The dead were quiet. The living were loud, hungry, arguing about dinner, and waiting on me to finish the damn bird.

I picked up my chisel, and I got back to work.
