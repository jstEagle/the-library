## 2. A Country Off the Map

*Odile*

They walked out of the valley at dawn, when the mist lay thick enough to hide them from anything with eyes.

Odile had lived in the valley for ten years and had never once looked back at it. Now, at the tree line where the dampening field ended and the world began, they felt the change like a door opening — a faint hum in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes. Magic could not follow past this point, Cassian had said. But Odile's gift had stirred anyway, two nights ago, when the hunters burned the village at the valley's edge. That was the thing they hadn't said aloud: if the field was fraying, nowhere was safe.

"Stay off the roads," Cassian murmured. He walked half a step ahead, as he always did, as if the ground might betray them otherwise. "The crown's men think in lines. Roads, rivers, borders. We move between them."

"And where do we sleep tonight?"

"There's a town called Merrow's Ford." He said it slowly, testing the name like a rotten plank. "Or there was."

"*Was?*"

He didn't answer. Ahead of them, the farmland opened up — wheat gone to seed, orchards heavy with unpicked fruit, a farmhouse standing open to the sky with its door swinging in no wind at all. The silence had a texture to it. Not empty. Abandoned in a hurry, or taken away.

Odile stopped at the farmhouse gate. A child's toy sat on the porch step, a wooden horse with one leg chewed by some long-dead dog. Dust lay over everything except the road, which was swept clean by wind and use.

"When did you last come through here?" Odile asked.

"Four years ago, mustering levies." Cassian's jaw worked. "There were three hundred people in that house alone. Two hundred more down the lane."

"And now?"

"Now it isn't on any map I've seen." He said it flatly, but his hands weren't steady, and Odile watched them not be steady, and filed it away.

* * *

*Cassian*

Cassian had signed an order once. He thought about it constantly now, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. A single page, a wax seal, a line of script in his own hand: *The Villalobos family is hereby removed from the registers of the Crown and conveyed beyond the borders of the realm.* He had been twenty-six years old and certain he was saving them. He had never learned whether he'd been right, because he'd requested the posting farthest from the capital and told himself distance was penance.

Now the woman who had grown from the child in that order walked beside him, calling him by a rank he no longer held, trusting him with her life because she had no one else. And every mile he led her further from safety, the order weighed heavier against his ribs.

Because the truth was worse than she knew. The truth was that the purge wasn't new. The crown had been erasing people for years — whole towns, whole bloodlines, struck from maps and records and memory until even their neighbors couldn't say why the lane was empty. The Villalobos family hadn't been the first storm-callers exiled. They'd been among the last whose exile was written down at all.

And Cassian had signed it knowing what came next for everyone else.

"Here." He crouched at a crossroads where four lanes met under a dead elm. Someone had nailed a signpost to it years ago; someone else had since pried every board off. Just stubs remained, pointing nowhere, naming nothing. "This used to say Merrow's Ford, south fork, six miles."

"You're certain?"

"I marched through it twice a year." He stood. "Which means either my memory is going, or—"

"Or the country is." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and it chilled him worse than shouting would have. She looked down the south fork. "Someone swept this road recently. Cart tracks. Fresh."

"People still live out here, then."

"People always live somewhere. Maps just stop agreeing about it." She started walking before he did, which bothered him more than he could say. She was supposed to follow. She was supposed to need him. If she didn't need him, then what exactly was he for, out here, besides a witness to what he'd helped begin?

* * *

*Odile*

Merrow's Ford existed. That was the strange part.

It sat where Cassian said it would, hunched along a brown river, maybe sixty houses, smoke rising from a dozen chimneys. Living people moved in its lanes. But when Odile asked at the well for directions east, toward the coast, the woman drawing water frowned as though Odile had spoken in tongues.

"East of what?" she said. "This is the edge of things. Past the river there's just the downs, and past the downs the sea, and nobody goes."

"The sea has a name," Odile said carefully.

"Not one anyone uses." The woman's eyes slid away, and Odile saw the fear in her, the trained fear of someone who had learned that names attract attention. "You'll want to move along. Market day's done."

In the inn, over stew neither of them tasted, Odile spread the map Cassian carried — a real one, army-issue, inked before the erasures began. Whole provinces sat printed across the interior. And stamped diagonally across the coastal march, in faded red official ink: *SURVEY WITHDRAWN. NO RECORD.*

"They didn't erase the land," Odile said quietly. "They erased the paperwork. And then they went after anyone who remembered the difference."

"Yes."

"How long have you known, Captain? That it wasn't just me. That it wasn't just my family."

Cassian set down his spoon. For a moment Odile thought he would lie — he had the face for it, the officer's smooth composure — and something in Odile's chest coiled tight, ready.

"Since before I met you," he said. "Since before your family was—" He stopped. Started again. "Since the first purges. I was posted to a survey unit. We unmade villages. On paper first, then in person."

Odile kept their voice level with an effort that cost them everything. "Unmade how?"

"It doesn't matter how. What matters is the Sundered Sea. It's the one place the erasure can't reach — the tide keeps its own records, the old priests said. If there's a way out of this country that the crown can't close, it's through the sea-door at the vanished coast." He leaned forward. "I know the route. Every unmade road between here and the water. You need me to get there."

*I need you alive,* Odile thought. *That isn't the same as needing you.*

Because Odile had seen the signature. Three days ago, in the burning village, in the clerk's strongbox a fleeing magistrate had abandoned — a bundle of old orders, copies, the paper trail of the first exiles. And there it was in a young officer's careful hand: *Villalobos.* Signed, sealed, conveyed beyond the borders.

The man across the table had written the end of Odile's childhood. Had sent their mother into the magic-dead dark. Had done it — the order's marginal note suggested, in another hand, *at the request of Capt. C. Veyle, who accepts full responsibility* — for reasons Odile didn't yet understand.

Odile closed the map. Smiled the smile that had kept them alive for ten quiet years.

"Then we leave at first light," they said. "Captain."

* * *

*Cassian*

They made eight miles before the bells started.

Behind them, back down the road toward Merrow's Ford, a sound rolled across the fields like weather — church bells rung wrong, fast and arrhythmic, the alarm pattern Cassian had taught garrison troops a lifetime ago. Then a glow above the tree line. Orange, flickering.

"They found the town," Odile said. No question in it.

"They followed our tracks from the crossroads." Cassian's stomach turned. His fault, his route, his tracks. "We cut east. There's a drover's path through the downs — no records, no roads, nothing for their surveyors to read. It runs all the way to the drowned capital."

"To the seawall."

"To the seawall." He glanced at her. In the firelight reaching across the fields, her face was unreadable, and that frightened him more than the bells. She'd been watching him for two days with a patience that felt like a blade being honed. "Odile. Whatever you're carrying, whatever you think you know—"

"I know the way is long," she said pleasantly, "and that you're the only one who knows it."

She walked on. Cassian stood a moment longer, watching the village burn behind them, watching the woman walk ahead of him into the unmade country, and understood with perfect clarity that she knew. Not everything. But enough.

Enough to keep him alive only as long as the road required.

He followed her east, toward the sea that remembered everything, and prayed — for the first time since he'd signed his name — that it would remember mercifully.
