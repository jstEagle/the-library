## 3. What the Tide Remembers

The sea came back to Odile before the ruins did.

They smelled it first — salt and rot and something older underneath, the way a storm smells before it has a name. Then the road tipped downward through dunes gone soft with moss, and there it was: the drowned capital of a country that no longer existed, rising out of the shallows like bones out of skin.

Towers stood to their waists in grey water. A cathedral spire leaned at an angle that defied its own weight. Streets ran beneath the tide, visible in strips where the light hit, cobbled and patient, waiting for feet that would never come. Gulls wheeled overhead, crying in a dialect no living person spoke.

"This is Vhalen," Cassian said quietly behind them. "Was Vhalen. The old capital."

"I know what it was." Odile's voice came out flat. Everyone knew the story — the capital swallowed by the Sundered Sea in a single night, the crown's shame, the reason the maps had begun to lie. What the stories didn't say was why the water here looked wrong. Too still. Too remembering.

Cassian limped past them down the shingle, his bandaged shoulder dark at the edge. Three days since the valley burned. Three days of unmade roads and farmhouses whose owners had stared through them like glass. His limp had worsened overnight, and he was pretending it hadn't.

"The tide pools," he said. "That's what the deserters called them, before I deserted. Where the country bled out of the world, the memories didn't go with it. They pooled." He nodded at the still water between the towers. "You can fall into one. Come out someone else. Or not come out at all."

"Then we walk around them."

"We can't. The seawall gate is through the middle of the city." He turned, and his eyes were steady in a way that had begun to infuriate Odile — steady as if steadiness were something he owed them. "And the door you need is beyond the seawall."

Odile said nothing. They climbed down onto the first drowned street, boots splashing ankle-deep, and tried not to look at the water.

It didn't work. The water looked back.

* * *

The first pool took them without asking.

One moment Odile was stepping over a submerged cart axle; the next they were standing in a marble hall lit by a hundred candles, ten years younger, watching their mother argue with a man in a grey officer's coat.

The hall wasn't real. Odile knew that the way you know you're falling. But the memory had weight, and texture, and the smell of beeswax and cold iron, and their mother's voice — gods, her voice, which Odile hadn't heard since the exile, since the valley, since silence —

"—the order stands," the officer was saying. "The Villalobos line is condemned. Storm-callers cannot be permitted—"

"Then condemn me," said the officer's own voice, from inside Odile's own memory of Cassian's face, younger, unscarred, standing before a desk with a parchment on it. "But this order names three children. Three. I won't sign a document that sends children to the pyres."

"You'll sign what the crown commands, Captain."

A pause. The candle flames bent, though there was no wind indoors. Young Cassian looked down at the parchment for a long time. When he looked up, something had been decided behind his eyes, something that cost him visibly.

"Yes," he said. "I'll sign."

The memory lurched. Now Odile was in a stable yard at night, rain hammering the roof, watching their mother shove a travel pack into their teenage arms while young Cassian held a lantern and spoke fast and low: "The order says exile to the valley — the magic-dead valley, do you understand? Not execution. I altered it. The crown ordered your family burned, and I changed the word. *Burned* became *exiled*. It will be discovered. You have days, maybe hours. Go now. Don't stop. Don't write to anyone."

Their mother's hand closed on Odile's wrist, hard enough to bruise. "Say nothing," she whispered. "To anyone. Ever. If you know what he did, they can make you tell it."

Then the pool let go, and Odile was on their knees in six inches of grey water in a dead city, gasping, salt burning their throat, ten years of hatred rearranging itself so violently they could hear it grind.

He hadn't condemned them. He'd rewritten the sentence. He'd spent his career, his honor, his name buying them a decade of quiet — and then walked into the valley himself to guide them out when the quiet failed.

Odile knelt in the tide pool and shook, and the water around them trembled in rings, and somewhere far above, thunder answered from a sky that should have been clear.

They got up. They didn't look at Cassian, who was watching them from twenty paces off with an expression like a man reading his own death warrant, and who said, very softly, "How much did you see?"

"Enough," Odile said. Their voice cracked down the middle. "Why didn't you *tell* me?"

"And have you decide to hate me less?" He almost smiled. "You needed the anger. It's kept you alive. I wasn't going to take it from you until you didn't need it anymore."

"That's not your choice to make."

"No." He looked away, toward the seawall, black against the horizon. "It stopped being my choice the day I made it theirs. Come on. We're close."

* * *

The seawall gate was a wound in a wall meant to hold back the Sundered Sea itself, and the hunters reached it thirty seconds before they did.

There were six of them — crown-grey cloaks, faces wrapped, the lead one carrying a censer that smoked with the sweet metallic stink of null-iron. Odile felt it like a hand closing over their sternum, pressing the gift down, and understood with sudden clarity that these were not ordinary soldiers. These were the ones sent for people like them.

"Run for the gate," Cassian said. "Now."

"Cassian—"

"They want the storm-caller, not the traitor. I'm worth nothing to them except as bait." He was already moving forward, drawing his sword with his bad arm, and the absurdity of it — the man who had saved them twice now spending himself a third time — hit Odile like a wave. "I signed my name to your life once. Let me do it properly."

"I know what you did," Odile said. "I saw it."

Something crossed his face — grief and relief tangled past separating. "Then you know I never once regretted it." He turned. "GO."

He met the hunters at the mouth of the gate, and Odile ran — three steps, four — and then stopped, because their body refused to obey, because some part of them that had spent ten years learning to survive alone had finally, catastrophically learned something else.

They turned back.

The null-iron smoke was thickening. Two hunters had already fallen; Cassian fought like a man settling accounts, economical and furious, holding the narrow gate mouth so they couldn't flow past him toward Odile. But there were too many, and his arm was wrong, and Odile watched the censer-bearer circle wide through the shallows, watch the smoke curl toward Cassian's face, watch him stagger—

Grief went through Odile like lightning through a mast.

Not grief for the family they'd lost. That grief was old, worn smooth, almost comfortable. This was new grief, hot grief, grief with a blade in it — and it transmuted as it passed through them, the way pressure makes diamond, the way lightning makes ozone. Fury. Clean and total and *theirs*, no longer borrowed from a decade of exile but minted fresh in this moment, in this drowned city, for this man.

Odile walked back into the smoke.

The gift rose to meet them like a tide answering the moon. Ten years of suppression cracked open all at once, and the Sundered Sea heard it — the whole grey expanse of it shuddering, the drowned towers groaning, clouds boiling up over a horizon that had been clear an hour ago.

The censer-bearer turned. Behind the cloth wrapping, Odile saw their eyes widen.

"You should have run," Odile told them, and raised their hands, and the sea stood up.

* * *

Lightning took the censer first, then the man holding it. The thunder that followed rolled out across the water and came back changed, deeper, as if the drowned city were answering. Two more hunters broke and fled into the ruins; the storm found them anyway, or the tide did — Odile would never know which, and would carry not-knowing like a stone.

Through the smoke and the sheeting rain, Cassian lay crumpled at the gate mouth, breathing, bound, alive. Alive. Odile dragged him up with arms that shook and a heart that hammered fury still, and behind them, out on the open water, something vast was stirring — a darkness beneath the waves arranged in a shape far too deliberate to be natural, a seam in the sea beginning, slowly, to glow.

"There," Odile gasped. "The door. I can feel it — it's *listening* —"

"Then answer it." Cassian's voice was a ruin. He managed half a smile through the blood on his face. "Finish what I started."

Odile looked out at the glowing seam, and felt the storm gathering in their chest like a debt come due, and knew — with the certainty of the tide, which remembers everything — that the sea would exact a price for what they were about to ask.

They raised their hands anyway.
