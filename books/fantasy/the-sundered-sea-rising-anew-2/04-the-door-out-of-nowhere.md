## 4. The Door Out of Nowhere

The pyre was already lit.

I saw it from the seawall, through the smoke of burning watchtowers: a scaffold of black timber on the drowned capital's last dry square, and Cassian lashed to it, and the fire climbing his legs like something hungry that had waited years for the invitation. Hunters stood in a ring around him, gray-cloaked, patient. They weren't watching him burn. They were watching the crowd — a few hundred erased people, the unrecorded remnants of an unrecorded country, herded in to witness what happened to those who helped the gifted.

They wanted me to come. That was the whole architecture of it. A storm-caller does not let her own burn.

Good, I thought. Let them want it.

I walked down out of the ruins with my arms open and the sky already answering.

It had been ten years since I'd felt it properly — not the frayed tingling of the valley's edge, not the half-remembered ache, but the full weight of weather leaning into my hands like a horse that knows its rider. The sea gave it to me first. The Sundered Sea had no name left on any map, but it remembered itself anyway; I could feel its memory rising through my boots, salt and patience and old grief. Then came the clouds, peeling off the horizon in long gray banners. Then the pressure, dropping so fast my ears popped and the crowd below flinched as one body.

*There you are,* the storm said, in the voice of every storm I'd ever called. *There you are.*

I had thought I would be afraid. I wasn't. That was the terrible part — the relief of it, the way my whole body unlocked like a door rusted shut finally kicked open. Ten years of holding my gift underwater, and now I let it surface, and it was enormous, and it was mine, and it wanted nothing except to be used.

Below me, someone shouted my description. A hunter turned, squinting up at the seawall where lightning was beginning to pool along the stones like water finding its level.

I called the first strike down into their command standard, and the night turned white.

* * *

What follows, I will tell plainly, because there is no one left to tell it slantwise.

I went down into the square through rain that fell sideways, thunder walking ahead of me like a vanguard. The hunters were trained for storm-callers — they had iron nets and dampening chains and prayers stitched into their collars — but they had never trained for this, for a caller standing in the open with nothing left to protect, nothing left to lose, and meaning it. Iron nets conduct. Dampening chains ground. Every weapon they brought against me became part of my circuitry. I took their lightning and gave it back doubled.

I reached the pyre and put it out. Not gently. The fire died screaming, steam erupting off the blackened wood, and Cassian hung in the ropes with his eyebrows burned away and his eyes open, and when he saw me he did something worse than thank me.

He wept.

"Odile," he said. "Your family — the order — I never—"

"I know," I said.

Because I did know now, had known since the drowned ruins showed me everything, the memories pooling in the flooded streets like tide-water: Cassian at his desk with the exile order, the true order, which named my mother and sister for the pyre and me for the towers. Cassian's pen hovering. Cassian falsifying it — altering the record, softening the sentence, buying us exile instead of death, knowing it would end his career and very nearly ended his life. All those years I had carried his name like a stone in my chest, and the stone had been hollow, and inside it was the only kindness anyone in power ever offered us.

"You signed it," I said, cutting the ropes. "You signed us out of the world."

"Somebody had to," he rasped, "or nobody would have."

The hunters were regrouping. I could feel them at the edges of my storm like teeth at the rim of a wound. And I could feel the other thing too, the thing I'd been circling since the ruins: the Sundered Sea's hidden door, somewhere under this drowned city, sealed behind magic older than the crown and keyed to a price.

Every door wants payment. I had learned that in the valley, watching it forget things slowly — a well here, a wedding song there. Doors eat. The question was only what.

I put my hand flat against the wet flagstones and asked the sea.

It answered honestly, which is the cruelest thing about the sea. It showed me the price the way a merchant shows coin: the valley. My mother's voice — not the memory of her voice, but my ability to hold it, the sound itself, gone to static. Home. All of it. Every path I'd walked barefoot, the smell of bread in our kitchen, my sister's laugh, the exact blue of the sky over the hills the morning we were driven out. Burned away as fuel. I would stand on foreign sand and know that I had loved a place without being able to find it in myself.

And I would still say yes. I knew that before the vision finished. That was the worst discovery of the night — not the cost, but how little the cost moved me, now, with Cassian's rope-burns under my fingers and the hunters closing and my gift singing in my blood like a struck bell.

"Odile." Cassian had gotten his feet under him, barely. He was looking at my hand on the stones, and he understood doors; he'd spent his life opening them for the crown. "Whatever it's asking—"

"It can have it," I said. "Hold on."

* * *

I have tried, since, to describe the working, and every attempt comes out wrong, so I'll give you the shape of it only.

A storm-caller does not make weather. We negotiate with it. So I negotiated — with the sea beneath the ruins, with the sky above them, with the old drowned magic that had swallowed a capital and kept its secrets. I opened myself as the channel and let everything pour through: ten years of suppression, a decade of small safe days in a valley that forgot itself by inches, the fury of a burned village, the grief of a family erased twice over — once by the crown, once by paper. I called down every mile of sky I could reach, and the Sundered Sea rose to meet it.

The square split. Not exploded — *split*, along a seam no eye had ever seen, a hairline of light running out toward the horizon across the water's skin. Behind the light, the sea bent upward like a page being lifted. And in the gap was not water.

It was a coast. Foreign sand, pale under a strange sun, dunes running down to a different ocean entirely. A door out of nowhere, onto a shore that had never heard of our country because our country no longer officially existed anywhere but in the heads of the few hundred people huddled in the square behind me, watching.

"Go!" I screamed at them, and some did — the quick, the young, the ones who hadn't given up on living — stumbling through the seam while the hunters' arrows fell around them and my storm ate the arrows out of the air.

Cassian didn't go. He stood beside me with his burned hands half-raised, useless and stubborn, holding the line of the doorway open with his body where the magic needed anchoring. Because that was the other thing the price hadn't mentioned: a door this size needs two. One to call the storm. One to stand in it.

He'd known. Of course he'd known. He'd been reading crowns and doors his whole life.

"Hold," I told him, and he held.

The cost came due all at once, the way tides do. It didn't hurt. That surprised me most — it was warm, like standing too close to a hearth. I felt the valley go first, the green of it lifting out of me like heat-shimmer. Then my mother's voice, mid-word, some ordinary sentence about bread or weather or love, dissolving before I could catch the end of it. Then home entire — the paths, the kitchen, the sky. I stood in the middle of my own erasure and watched the country leave me, and I kept calling, and I did not stop, because behind me people were still crossing, and the door stayed open exactly as long as I did.

The last of them went through. Cassian grabbed my wrist.

"Now," he said.

We stepped through together, and the Sundered Sea closed behind us like a mouth.

* * *

Foreign sand. Pale, fine, unfamiliar grain by grain. A sun that sat wrong in the sky. Gulls crying in a language I didn't know, though I understood suddenly that I wouldn't have understood gulls at home either, because there was no *at home* anymore. I searched for it — reflexively, desperately — the valley, the kitchen, her voice. I found smooth places where they had been, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth.

I sat down in the sand and, to my own surprise, laughed. One broken note of it. Cassian lowered himself beside me, singed and swaying, and looked at the stranger I had become to myself.

"Do you remember—" he started, and stopped, careful. "Do you know who you are?"

"My name is Odile," I said. It was the one thing the sea had left me, or maybe the one thing I'd paid for with everything else. "I'm a storm-caller. There was a country. I think I loved it."

"That's more than most of them got," he said quietly, nodding back at the shore, where the rescued were coming down the dunes in twos and threes, blinking at their new ocean, carrying each other, carrying nothing.

I looked at him — this man whose face I knew and whose crime I could no longer fully weigh, because the scales themselves had burned. Whatever he'd done, whatever he'd saved us from, lived now in a country I couldn't picture, signed by a hand I couldn't see. Only one thing remained legible: he had held the door.

"Walk with me," I said.

We walked. Behind us the dunes filled with the unrecorded, the erased, the formerly mapped — a whole nation washed up on a beach that had never asked for us. Ahead, past the far lip of the sand, I could see lights. A town. Roads. A world that would never know what any of it had cost, and would get to keep not knowing, because I had made it so.

The wind came in off the strange sea, and it touched my hands, and it was listening.

"Well," I said to Cassian, and to the wind, and to whatever came next. "Let's see what this place forgets."
