## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The border of the Stillwater Valley was not a wall, or a river, or even a line on the land. It was a feeling — the moment when the wind stopped tasting of iron and the birds went suddenly, stupidly tame.

Kestrel Sterling felt it cross her like a cold sheet being drawn over a body.

She had been walking for eleven days. Her boots had given up pretending to be boots somewhere past the second ridge, and her feet had opinions about every stone between there and here. Behind her, somewhere in the greening hills of the Marchlands, Maren was surely awake by now, standing at the door of the crooked little house with its bundles of drying yarrow and its jars of things that whispered, reading the absence of her apprentice the way other people read weather. *Gone south. Gone toward the water. Fool girl.*

Let her read. The one thing the Stillwater Valley did not permit, they said — the fisherfolk said it with a shrug, as if reporting a dull fact about tides — was magic. Nothing followed you in. Nothing reached you from outside. Whatever you carried that was not flesh and blood, you left at the border like muddy boots at a door.

Kestrel stopped at the top of the last hill and looked down into the valley, and made herself do the thing she had rehearsed for eleven days on the road: she let go.

It should not have been possible. Magic was not a coat; you could not simply shrug it off and hang it on a peg. Maren had spent nine years teaching her that the gift was not something she *had* but something she *was* — that the small warm current under her skin, the one that made cut herbs knit faster and dying lambs breathe again, was woven into her the way color was woven into her eyes. You cannot put down your own blood.

But the valley asked anyway. And to Kestrel's everlasting horror, her magic *obeyed*.

It went out of her quietly, without pain, like a breath released after being held too long. She felt it drain down through her feet into the sweet grass, felt the current gutter and still, felt a silence open up inside her where there had always been sound. Nine years of Maren's voice in her head, nine years of the low singing hum of power that lived beneath everything — gone. Not suppressed. *Absent.* As if someone had reached into the instrument of her and lifted out one string, leaving the rest of the harp intact and useless.

She sat down hard in the grass.

*Good,* she told herself. *This is what safety feels like.*

It felt, in truth, like erasure.

She stayed on the hilltop until the shaking stopped, because she had learned on the road that if she let herself feel the whole shape of what she'd done all at once, she would turn around and walk back to the Marchlands and hand herself over to Maren's disappointment, which was worse than Maren's anger, which was considerable. So instead she catalogued, the way Maren had taught her to catalogue herbs: item, property, use. Item: one runaway apprentice, twenty years old, no money worth mentioning, a name she could no longer keep. Property: frightened, footsore, free. Use: unknown.

The name was the second thing to go.

Sterling was not her true name — nothing so dramatic as that — but it was the name Maren had given her, the way masters gave apprentices names the way farmers branded cattle, and it sat on her now like the collar of a shirt buttoned by someone else. In the valley she would need a name that fit a net-mender, a herring-gutter, a nobody. She tried several against the inside of her teeth as she walked down into the valley: *Maren* was unthinkable. *Wren*, too close to home. At last, near the bottom of the hill, where a stream crossed the road and she stopped to drink and wash eleven days off her face, she settled on the plainest thing available.

*Kess.*

Just Kess. A name like a pebble. A name with no hooks in it for anyone to catch.

* * *

Lowmark announced itself by smell before sight: tar, brine, woodsmoke, and under it all the green-rot sweetness of the marshes at the valley's southern edge. Then the village itself came around a shoulder of dune grass — forty houses maybe, gray-shingled and salt-stained, huddled along a tidal inlet like old men leaning together against the wind. Nets hung from long drying frames in rows, great brown curtains of them, and the masts of small fishing boats scratched at a sky the color of an unwashed window.

Kestrel stood at the edge of the village green and listened, the way she couldn't help listening even without her magic — habit outlasting the thing that shaped it. She heard hammers. She heard a dog. She heard children shrieking happily about something near the tide gate. What she did not hear, anywhere in Lowmark, in any house or heart or hedge, was the faint subterranean hum she had grown up inside. No wards. No charms. No witch-bottles buried under doorsteps, no horse-shoes hung point-down to spoil ill-wishing.

Nothing. The whole village lay open to the sky like a palm with no lines in it.

*Safe,* she thought. And then, immediately, treacherously: *Empty.*

An old woman with arms like ship's cable was re-hanging a torn net on the nearest frame, cursing at it with the fluency of long practice. Kestrel watched her work for a while, then made herself walk over.

"You'll be wanting help with that," she said. "I mend nets."

The old woman looked her up and down with the frank inventory of a woman who had priced fish and men all her life. Took in the ruined boots, the road-thinness, the hands — Kestrel saw her look at the hands, because everyone who mattered looked at the hands, and net-work leaves its signature there, callus and scar and the particular flat-topped nails of someone who has spent years pushing needles through knotted twine.

"Where'd you learn that?"

"My mother." It came out smooth. It wasn't even entirely a lie; Maren had taught her knots alongside charms, insisting the two arts were sisters, and some of those lessons had happened in a fishing village very much like this one, in another life. "She's dead."

"Aren't they all." The old woman jerked her chin at the frame. "Net's called the *Patience*. Tore on the bar last week. If your fingers are as good as your story, there's bread and a bunk in it. Brine-house has room since the Aldous boy drowned." She said it the way people in Lowmark said everything, Kestrel would learn — flatly, without ornament, grief filed under weather. "I'm Gull. Everyone is, eventually."

"Gull," Kestrel repeated, and then remembered herself. "Kess. I'm called Kess."

"Called," Gull said, with the smallest lift of an eyebrow, as if she had heard the word *called* and filed it away in whatever drawer such women kept for things they noticed but did not pursue. "Well then, Called-Kess. Needle's in the basket. Don't make it worse."

Kestrel picked up the needle.

And here was the strange mercy of it, the thing she had not dared hope for: her hands knew what to do. All through the first day, and the second, and the weeks that folded into each other after, her fingers moved through the brown twine with a fluency that needed nothing from the part of her that had been scooped hollow. Sheet-bend, bowline, the mending-stitch that ran the tear closed like a seam of scar tissue. The work was endless and repetitive and required exactly enough attention to crowd out thinking, and Kestrel came to love it the way you love anything that keeps you from yourself.

The brine-house bunk was narrow and smelled of salt and old herring, and she slept there like the dead, dreamless, ten hours a night, and woke each morning to gulls screaming their one argumentative note, and ate bread and smoked fish and drank small beer, and was, by every measurement anyone in Lowmark cared to apply, safe.

* * *

Safety, she discovered, had a texture, and the texture was wool.

Not her own thoughts only — everything in the valley was slightly muffled, slightly blurred at the edges, like a tapestry viewed through fogged glass. She noticed it first in small ways. The seasons seemed to arrive late and leave early. The villagers' faces, friendly enough, were oddly difficult to hold in memory; she would part from someone at noon and by evening struggle to recall the exact color of their eyes. Even her dreams were thin gruel, grey and forgettable, whereas on the road she had dreamed vividly, terribly, of drowning towers and a voice calling a name she refused to hear.

In Lowmark she dreamed of nothing but nets.

Once, walking the shingle at dusk, she passed a young man gutting mackerel who whistled a tune that snagged at something deep in her — six falling notes, over and over, like water dropping down a stairwell — and she stood there in the cold pink light with her heart hammering before she realized she had no idea why. The tune meant nothing. It was a fish-gutting whistle. She walked on. But that night she caught herself humming it, and stopped, appalled, the way you stop when you find your hand reaching for something in your sleep that you swore you'd thrown away.

*You wanted this,* she reminded herself, lying awake in the brine-house dark. *You chose this. Every hour you are not found, not used, not spent — this is what it cost, and it was cheap.*

Cheap. She turned the word over. It didn't ring true, but she made herself say it again anyway, like a prayer said against evidence, because the alternative — that she had traded something irreplaceable for mere survival — was a ledger she could not bear to open.

The villagers were kind in their flat way. Gull paid her in coin and bread and never asked questions, which Kestrel understood was the local currency of respect. The priest at the low stone chapel — a chapel with no icons, she noted, no saints, no relics, nothing to hang a prayer on, just four walls and a roof and a bell — nodded to her on Sundays and asked nothing. Children stared at her newness for a week and then absorbed her into the landscape like a rock. By the end of the first month, when the fish-wives gossiped on the dock, they spoke of her as *the Kess girl* as though she had always been there, as though she had been born between two tides like everyone else, and Kestrel found this simultaneously the most comforting and most frightening thing that had ever happened to her.

*They forget,* she thought, watching the harbor lights come up one evening, gold coins dropped one by one into black water. *Or rather — there is nothing here to remember with. The valley takes the sharp edges off everything. Off grief, off joy, off me.*

She tested it once, carefully, in the privacy of the brine-house. Sat cross-legged on her bunk, closed her eyes, and reached — gently, the way you test a broken arm — for the place inside her where the current used to run.

Silence. Not even silence, really; silence implies an absence of sound, and this was more thorough than that. It was as if the question *is there magic here?* could not properly form. She tried to visualize Maren's face and got a blur, a suggestion of features, like a portrait left out in rain. She tried to recall the exact words of the spell — *not a spell, a craft, we don't call them spells, Kestrel, spells are for people who don't understand what they're doing* — the words of the craft Maren had used to seal a wound on her forearm when she was thirteen, and could not. She remembered that it had happened. She remembered the white scar. The knowledge itself had gone soft as wet paper.

Something in her chest clenched so hard she had to press her fist against her sternum and breathe.

*That's not forgetting,* she thought, staring into the dark. *Forgetting is losing something. This is having it taken and being made comfortable about the loss.*

And yet — and yet she stayed. That was the humiliating truth of it. Week slid into week. The *Patience* was mended and tore again and was mended again. She learned which houses leaned east and which leaned west, learned that the baker watered his ale and the ferryman cheated tourists and the Aldous family still set an extra bowl at supper for a son the sea had kept. She became, stitch by stitch, a person who belonged to Lowmark, and every night the person she had been got a little thinner, a little smoother, a little more like a stone worn pleasant by water.

Some nights she almost managed not to dream of drowning towers at all.

* * *

It was on an ordinary morning — a morning so aggressively ordinary that afterward she would remember it as the last morning of her old life, the final bead of the rosary — that the numbness cracked.

Later she would try to reconstruct it and fail. There had been nothing to mark the day. Gray light, the smell of tar, Gull swearing at the weather. Kestrel was at the drying frames working a stubborn knot out of the *Patience*'s shoulder when she felt it: a single wrong note struck through the valley's soft silence, faint and far off, like a hairline crack running through a bell.

She went still, needle suspended.

There it was again. Faint. Directionless. But *sharp* — the first sharp thing she had felt in two months — and underneath it, threading through the numbness like blood through water, something that felt horribly, unmistakably like pain. Not hers. Not the valley's. Something coming from outside, dragging its wound across the threshold, and the valley's gentle wool parting around it because pain like that did not ask permission.

The knot slipped from her fingers.

Down by the tide gate — the old timber gate where the inlet met the sea, where children had shrieked happily on her first day — a small crowd was gathering. From this distance she could see only that something lay on the planks. Something that had not been there moments ago. Something that people were backing away from.

Kestrel stood with the net needle trembling in her hand, and every instinct she had — the old instincts, the buried ones, the ones the valley was supposed to have sanded smooth — screamed at her to put the needle down, walk back to the brine-house, eat her bread, mend her nets, stay wool-wrapped and safe and erased until she died comfortably of old age in a village that could not quite remember her name.

She dropped the needle on the frame and ran.

The crowd parted for her without knowing why — the fisherfolk would say afterward that the Kess girl had a way of appearing where trouble was, and none of them would think it strange — and she reached the front of it, and looked down.

A man lay on the tide-gate planks, half-drowned and burned, his clothes crusted with salt and char, his skin the color of candle wax. He was breathing in shallow, rattling pulls, and clutched against his chest with both blackened hands was a leather map case, gripping it the way a drowning man grips driftwood.

His eyes found hers across the gap of three feet and the entire width of the world.

He smiled. It was the worst thing Kestrel had ever seen, that smile — relieved and terrified and certain, all at once.

"Warden's daughter," he said.

The words went through the valley's wool like a knife through canvas. For one terrible heartbeat the numbness cracked clean open, and Kestrel felt *everything* — the hum of the world outside the borders, the weight of the hills, the taste of iron on the wind, the map case burning in the dying man's grip like a coal — and beneath it all, rising from somewhere deeper than memory, a voice that was not the man's and not hers and older than both:

*At last. At last, at last—*

The man's hand opened. The map case tumbled from his fingers onto the planks between them, rolled once, and came to rest against her boot.

"Warden's daughter," he said again, softer, the smile still fixed, his eyes already going glassy. "It has to be blood. Only blood will—"

The tide sighed against the gate. The gulls screamed. And the valley's numbness, moving like a slow white wave, began closing over the crack in the world — but too late, far too late, because Kestrel was already kneeling, and her hand was already reaching for the leather case, and the ink inside it, she would swear to the end of her days, was already moving.
