## 2. A Stranger at the Tide Gate

The tide gate at Lowmark was old before anyone now living could remember, a squat arch of grey stone straddling the channel where the sea crept in twice a day to fill the flats, and crept out again like something ashamed of itself. Kestrel had been mending nets beside it for eleven days when the stranger came through.

She heard him before she saw him — or rather, she heard the gulls change their minds. They had been wheeling over the mudflats all morning, lazy and quarrelsome, and then all at once they lifted as one body and swung inland, screaming, as though something had walked beneath them that they wanted no part of.

Kestrel looked up from the net across her knees, her needle stilling mid-stitch, and saw a man standing in the mouth of the gate.

He should not have been standing. That was her first thought, and it stayed with her long after everything else that happened that day had blurred together. He stood the way a fence post stands after a storm has leaned on it for years — out of habit more than strength. His clothes were the ruin of fine ones; she could see the ghost of embroidery along a sleeve that was now mostly char, and one boot had burned away entirely below the ankle, leaving the foot beneath blackened and weeping. The left side of his face had been scalded by something worse than water. The skin there was tight and shining, pulled into new shapes, and where his eyebrow should have been there was only a smooth scarred curve like a wave drawn in wax.

And he was wet. Not tide-wet, not rain-wet. *Sea*-wet, soaked to the marrow in a way the Stillwater Valley's timid sea never managed, dripping water that left a faint gleam on the stones that did not look like salt. It looked, though she would not let herself finish the thought, almost like light.

He saw her. She knew it because his whole ruined face changed — not with pain, which he must have carried everywhere in him by then, but with recognition. With relief. With a joy so naked and so terrible that Kestrel felt her chest tighten around it, because no one had ever looked at her like that, and no one should have looked at a stranger that way unless—

Unless they were looking past her. Unless they were seeing someone else.

"No," she said aloud, to him, to the day, to whatever was about to happen. "No, you've got the wrong—"

He took a step. Then another. Then his legs remembered what had been done to them and went out from under him, and he came down on the stones of the tide gate like a dropped bundle, and the sound he made was small and human and awful.

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to lie awake and take the day apart piece by piece — Kestrel would wonder why she ran to him. She had spent eleven days teaching herself not to run toward anything. In the valley, running toward things got you noticed, and being noticed got you questions, and questions were the one currency she could not afford. Her whole survival rested on being nobody: the quiet new girl at the net-menders' bench who kept her sleeves down and her eyes lowered and her hands empty of anything but twine.

But her body had not learned the lesson yet. Her body still belonged to the girl she'd been before Stillwater — the girl who had once pulled a drowning lamb from a flooded culvert without a thought, who had once put her bare hand between a neighbor's child and a kettle of boiling fat. So she ran, dropping needle and net, skidding on weed-slick stone, and knelt in the spreading shine of that too-bright water with his head cradled against her arm before she'd decided to do any of it.

Up close he smelled of smoke and brine and something underneath both, something old, like the air inside a house that has been shut for a very long time.

"Water," he rasped. His good eye found hers. "Please."

She fumbled the skin off her shoulder and held it to his lips. He drank the way drowning men drink — desperately, wastefully, half of it down his chin — and when she took the skin away he made a sound that might have been thanks or might have been laughter.

"You're her," he said. "I knew it. I *knew* it, even this far—" A cough took him, deep and wet, and when it passed he lay panting against her arm, and she saw blood on his lips, dark and frothed.

"I'm not anyone," Kestrel said. "You're mistaken. There's nothing here but fishermen and me."

"Warden's daughter." He said it gently, the way you'd correct a child who insisted the moon was made of bread. His scorched hand moved — slowly, with the care of someone handling a wound — to the leather case strapped across his chest. It was long and slim, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, and the strap had cut a groove into his shoulder that had bled and dried and bled again. "Warden's daughter," he said again, and smiled with the half of his mouth that could still smile. "Do you know how far I've carried that? Do you know what I gave up to bring it the last mile?"

"I don't know what you're talking about." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Underneath the steadiness her heart was slamming like a door in wind. *Valley,* she reminded herself. *Stillwater. Nothing follows you here. Nothing can.* The valley's numbness pressed around her like deep water, that blessed dullness she had swum into eleven days ago and clung to ever since, the place where magic thinned to nothing and hedge-spells died an inch from your fingertips. Safe. She was safe. This man was dying on the stones and it was terrible, but it was *not hers.*

"Of course you don't." Another smile flickered. "They sealed the valley so well. Ninety years of forgetting. My grandmother used to say the Stillwater didn't keep magic out — it kept memory out. Same thing, maybe." His eye drifted past her, up the channel toward Lowmark's rooftops, its smoking chimneys, its nets hung out like grey laundry. "Pretty place. I'm glad it was pretty, at least. Somewhere to end."

"Don't talk like that. I'll fetch the herb-wife—"

"She'll only tell you to make you comfortable." He said it without bitterness. "I've seen the burn that killed me. It was on the other men first. I watched it work." His hand found the case again, and this time he began working at the buckle with fingers that did not want to obey. "Help me. Please. Help me get it open. It has to be in your hands, not mine. It has to *choose* you."

"Nothing chooses me." The words came out harder than she meant. "Nothing picks me for anything. I made sure of it."

His eye came back to her face and studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy some question he'd been carrying across half the world, because he nodded slowly, painfully, as if she had confirmed something in his favor.

"That's why it's you," he said. "Don't you see? Anyone who *wanted* it — anyone who reached for it — it would have slipped through their fingers like water. But you ran from it. You ran all the way into the one place it couldn't follow." He laughed, and the laugh cost him a rattle of blood. "It followed anyway. It followed *you*. Ink remembers, warden's daughter. Ink is the most patient thing in the world."

Her hands, without any instruction from her, had already begun helping him with the buckle.

* * *

The case opened with a breath of air that smelled of cedar and lightning.

Inside, rolled tight and bound with a cord braided from three colors of thread — gold, silver, and a red so dark it was nearly brown — lay a map.

Kestrel had seen maps. Maren had owned two, cracked and foxed things of the Sundered Sea coast, and Kestrel had spent long evenings copying their coastlines by candlelight, learning the shapes of places she would never go. She knew what maps looked like. She knew what maps were: ink on skin of calf or sheep, lines and names, a flat argument about a round world.

This was not that.

Even furled, even before she touched it, the map was *warm*. She felt the warmth before her fingers made contact, radiating off the rolled vellum like a hearthstone at evening, and when her thumb finally brushed its edge the warmth jumped to her skin and climbed her wrist like a living thing taking hold of a branch.

"Oh," she said. Small and stupid. It was all she could manage.

"Unroll it," the man whispered. "Just the top corner. Just enough to know."

"I shouldn't—"

"*Unroll it.*"

She told herself afterward that she had meant only to humor a dying man, that kindness had overridden sense, that any decent person would have done the same. But the truth, which she kept in the locked room at the back of her mind where she kept all her truths, was simpler and more shameful: she *wanted* to. Some door in her that she had nailed shut eleven days ago rattled on its hinges, and her hands obeyed the rattling.

She loosened the three-colored cord. She unrolled the top corner of the map against her knee.

Ink crawled.

That was the only honest word for it. The lines on the vellum moved — not shimmered, not shifted in the way cheap illusionist charms shift, but *crawled*, deliberate and slow, like ants discovering a sugar trail. Coastlines refined themselves before her eyes. A harbor grew piers, cranes, the tiny suggestion of ships at anchor. Towers rose from the page in strokes of ink so fine they seemed drawn by something smaller than a hand. And at the center of it all a mountain took shape, and within the mountain, rendered in a single point of burning red that seemed somehow deeper than ink, a heart.

A sleeping heart of fire.

And then the map showed her things that were not on the page.

* * *

She was standing on a quay at dusk, and the air smelled of roasting chestnuts and brine and lamp-oil, and the towers of Vael went up around her like organ pipes, pale gold stone shot through with veins of rose quartz that caught the last light. People moved past her — laughing, arguing, a woman scolding a boy with a stolen apple — and none of them saw her, because she was not there, because this was memory, because this was ninety years drowned. Music came from somewhere, strings and a drum, and a child ran by trailing a paper kite on a string, and above it all, above the highest tower, the mountain stood with a thin ribbon of smoke rising from its crown like incense, like a lit wick, like a promise.

*We are held,* said a voice that was not a voice, that came from the ink itself, from the patience of ink. *The fire holds. The wardens hold the fire. And the sea, which hates us, waits.*

Then the dusk turned wrong. The ribbon of smoke thickened, darkened, bent sideways in a wind that should not have blown from that quarter. Bells began — not alarm bells, not yet, first just the hour bells, ordinary and golden, and then other bells joining them, faster, uglier. On the quay the chestnut-seller abandoned his cart. The woman snatched up her apple-thieving son. And far out past the harbor lights, where the sea met the sky, the water *rose* — not a wave, waves had shape, this had none, this was the sea simply deciding to be taller, climbing and climbing against every law —

*A door,* said the ink-voice, colder now, and underneath the cold something that might have been grief. *Someone opened a door.*

The memory shattered. Kestrel came back to herself on her knees on the tide-gate stones with the map corner still pinned under her hand and her own name caught somewhere behind her teeth, unsaid, because for one heartbeat she had forgotten it — forgotten Kestrel Sterling, forgotten Lowmark, forgotten eleven days of careful nothing — and known herself instead by another name entirely, older and heavier:

*Warden.*

And in that heartbeat, the valley's numbness cracked.

She felt it go like ice splitting on a spring pond — a single jagged line through all that blessed dullness, and through the crack poured everything the valley had been holding back: the taste of power on her tongue like copper coins, the pressure of the world's hidden currents against her skin, her own blood suddenly loud in her ears, *singing*, answering the map's red heart note for note. For one terrible heartbeat she was not numb, not safe, not nobody. She was a struck bell.

Then the valley closed over the crack, sealing it, smothering it, and the numbness rushed back in like cold water filling a footprint in sand — but not perfectly, not completely. Something remained. A hairline fracture. An itch under the skin of the world.

The map's ink had stopped crawling. The top corner of Vael lay quiet against her knee, innocent as any dead thing.

Except that now, faintly, in the webbing between the fingers of her right hand, there was a mark. A small spiral, fine as a spider's line, dark as tattooed ink, that had not been there that morning. It did not wash off. She tried, later, with seawater and river water and a scrap of pumice. It only seemed to settle deeper, patient, content.

* * *

The man was dying faster now. She could feel it under her hands the way you feel a fire going out — less heat, less insistence, the great effort of him easing toward surrender. His breathing had gone shallow and whistling. When she bent over him, his good eye took a long time to find her.

"Did you see it?" he whispered.

"Yes."

"Then it knows you. Too late to pretend otherwise, warden's daughter." He tried to laugh; it became a cough; the cough brought blood. "My name was Ede Rell. Cartographer, third rank, archive of... it doesn't matter. The archive burns. Everything burns, back there. I carried this out of the fire because the librarians said — they *said* — the last warden's line wasn't ended. Just scattered. Hidden. One daughter, taken east as a child, apprenticed to a hedge-witch..." His eye fixed on her. "Maren. Is that the name? Maren of the fen-edge?"

Kestrel's whole body went still. The word came out of her before she could stop it, small and flat: "How do you know that name?"

"We know lots of names. We've had ninety years to learn them." His scorched hand lifted, trembling, and pressed the map case into her grip, folding her fingers over the leather with a strength that surprised her, that must have come from somewhere below the dying. "Take it. Take it east, take it north, take it anywhere but give it to no one who asks for it. If someone asks for it, that's how you'll know they're the enemy." A bubble of blood popped at the corner of his mouth; he wiped it absently, like a man wiping ale-foam. "The Ember Court is coming. They smell death and inheritance. They'll ask sweet questions. Don't answer sweetly."

"I don't understand any of this. I don't understand what a warden *is*."

"You will." His eye was drifting now, losing focus, sliding toward the grey water moving in the channel. "Ink remembers. Blood remembers. You're the last of the door-wardens, girl. The last key. The fire is guttering and the sea is rising and there is one door out of the drowning, and it opens—" His breath caught. Held. "—it opens for you."

"Stay with me," Kestrel said, uselessly, the way people always say it. "Stay with me, help is coming, the village is right there—"

"The valley," he murmured. His gaze had settled on the channel water, on the slow green-grey tide sliding through the gate, and something in his ruined face eased, gentled, like a man seeing home at the end of a long road. "It's true, then. Nothing follows you in. I made it. I actually made it." He smiled. "Tell my grandmother's people I got it home. Tell them Ede Rell carried it the last mile."

His hand went slack in hers.

Kestrel knelt there a while, holding fingers that no longer held back, listening to the tide move and the gulls complain somewhere inland, feeling the map warm and patient against her knee like a sleeping animal. She thought about calling out. The village was five minutes' walk; the herb-wife would come; there would be a shroud and a hole in the churchyard sand and a priest mumbling over a stranger with no name anyone could give. That was the ordinary path. That was the Lowmark path. That was the path of a girl who mended nets and kept her sleeves down and her hands empty.

Instead, very carefully, she rolled the map closed, bound it with its three-colored cord, slung the case across her own shoulder — where the strap settled into the groove of her collarbone as if it had been measured for her — and sat back on her heels in the shining water.

Between her fingers, the little spiral pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat. Like an answer.

Far off down the coast road, where the valley met the world and the world began, a dust-plume rose against the afternoon. Horses. More than one. Moving fast.

Kestrel looked at them for a long moment, and then she stood, and picked up her mending basket, and walked toward Lowmark with a dead man's inheritance hidden under her arm and dread sitting in her stomach like swallowed stone — walking, for all the world, like a girl who was only bringing the day's nets home.
