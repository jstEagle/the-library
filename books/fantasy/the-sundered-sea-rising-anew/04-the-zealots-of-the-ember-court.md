## 4. The Zealots of the Ember Court

The sea had a smell for death. Serah Vane had learned that in her first year of service, walking the drowned quays of Merrow's Reach with the old inquisitor Dannel, who could stand at a harbor mouth and tell you whether a body lay beneath the water and how long it had been there, and how it had died — by blade or by drowning or by fire, each leaving its particular sweetness on the salt wind. *Death is honest,* Dannel used to say, tapping his ash-gray censer against his palm. *It is the living who lie about everything else.*

Three days out of the last Ember waystation, following the coast road north through country that grew steadily greener and more indifferent to her presence, Serah caught that smell again. Not a corpse this time — something older, stranger. A death that was still happening. It came off the land like heat off a forge: burnt paper and drowned wood and, beneath it all, a thread of something that made her teeth ache, like biting silver.

She knew what that thread meant. Every inquisitor did, though none spoke of it above a whisper. It was the scent of warden-blood. The scent of Vael.

She crested the last hill at dusk and saw Lowmark below her: a scatter of gray houses huddled between the valley wall and a brown estuary, nets strung like funeral banners between poles, boats drawn up on the shingle with their bellies showing. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight, untroubled columns. Nothing about the village suggested magic. Nothing about it suggested anything at all. It was the most deliberately forgettable place Serah had ever seen, and she had spent six years learning to notice deliberate things.

The Stillwater Valley. Even the Court's oldest maps marked it with a blank space and a warning: *Here nothing answers.* No ley ran through it, no charm held, no scrying glass showed anything but fog. The founders of the Ember Order had called it God's Blind Spot, and they had argued for two centuries about whether that made it holy or abominable.

Serah dismounted at the edge of the shingle and walked her horse in. Her boots found the sand with a sound like slow applause.

* * *

They gathered without being asked, the way villagers always did — first the children at a distance, then the men with their arms crossed, then the women standing just behind their husbands' shoulders but looking at Serah directly, which she noted, and filed away. In most places the women watched the ground. Here they watched *her*. She wondered what kind of place trained its women to look an inquisitor in the eye.

She let them look. She stood by her horse in her travel-grays — she wore no insignia into a strange village, not at first; the ash-colored coat invited either reverence or stones, depending on the parish — and she unlaced her satchel slowly, letting curiosity do her work before fear did.

"I'm looking for a girl," she said, when the crowd had thickened enough. "Sixteen, perhaps seventeen. Dark-haired. She'd have come through here within the past month, alone, on foot. She'd be tired. Watchful." She paused. "She may have given a name. Whatever name she gave, it won't be hers."

A man near the front — broad, sunburnt, the calluses of a net-mender on his hands — shifted his weight. "Lots of girls come through," he said. "Season work. We take anyone willing to mend nets."

"This one wouldn't stay willing," Serah said. "This one would watch doors. Count exits. Sleep light." She let her gaze move over the crowd, gentle as oil on water. "You'd remember her because she never quite belonged, even while she worked harder than your own daughters."

Silence. The good, dense silence of people deciding, together, what not to know.

Serah smiled and reached into her satchel and brought out the thing she always brought out second: a small brass censer on a chain, cold and unlit. She didn't swing it. She simply held it, and let every soul in that square remember that the Ember Court had other tools besides questions.

"Perhaps I should speak with whoever keeps order here."

"The reeve's up at the tide gate," said the net-mender. His jaw was tight. "But we don't hold with burning in Lowmark. Whatever she's done, whatever you think she's done — we don't hold with it."

"We don't burn people anymore," Serah said, and it was almost true. "We burn *lies*."

* * *

The reeve was an old woman named Marget Cole, and she received Serah in a house that smelled of tar and dried kelp, and she lied to her with such flawless, placid incompetence that Serah nearly applauded.

"A dark girl? Might've seen one. Might not. We get travelers." Marget poured tea that tasted of seawater and sat with her hands folded like a woman waiting out weather. "Valley folk keep to themselves, inquisitor. You'll forgive me if I don't know much about anybody."

"You know everyone," Serah said pleasantly. "Villages like this are small enough to memorize. You know who snores, who drinks, whose cow wanders. So you'll understand why I find it interesting that no one in this entire settlement can recall a single stranger."

Marget sipped her tea. "Bad memory, maybe. Salt air."

"Yes," Serah said. "Salt air."

She set the censer on the table between them, and Marget looked at it the way one looks at a snake in a basket — knowing exactly what it was, refusing to flinch anyway.

"Do you know what the Ember Court is, reeve?"

"Fire-fanciers. My gran said you lot started as chimney sweeps who got ideas."

Serah laughed despite herself, a short, real laugh that surprised them both. "Your gran wasn't far wrong. But the idea matters. May I tell it to you? You can refuse to believe any part of it."

Marget gestured: *get on with it.*

"Once, the world burned too much," Serah said. "Dragon-fire under the mountains, hedge-craft in every cottage, ley-fire running through the land like blood through flesh. And the sea — the sea was pushed back, shoved aside, partitioned, *imprisoned*, so that men could build where water ought to be. The Sundered Sea isn't a sea, reeve. It's a wound that was never allowed to close. Whole countries were drowned to make harbors safe for shipping lanes. Vael went under with forty thousand souls, and the wardens lit their great fire to keep the water *there*, held back by force, forever, so no one would ever have to admit what had been done." Her voice stayed level, soft, the voice of recitation. "The Court teaches that every flame must gutter in its time. That the sea has waited ninety years behind a door of stolen fire, and that the debt comes due whether we pay it gladly or screaming. All fire must gutter, reeve. So the sea can be whole. So the wound can close. That's the creed. I've said it every morning since I was nine years old."

"And you believe it."

"With my whole self," Serah said, and meant it, and heard in her own voice the faint hollow note that meaning-it sometimes carried. "Which is why I need the girl. Because there is a bloodline, reeve — the wardens' bloodline — and if it survives, the fire can be fed again. Another century of stolen water. Another century of drowned countries waiting to drown more. Unless she dies unblooded, unmapped, unfound, the sea stays sundered, and everyone who ever drowned in it stays unforgiven." She leaned forward. "I am not hunting a child. I am hunting the lock on a door that should have been broken decades ago. Help me break it."

Marget Cole looked at her for a long moment. Then the old woman said, mildly, "There was a girl mended nets down by the third shed. Quiet. Good hands. Left two days ago, toward the hills, I'd guess. Or maybe toward the marshes. Hard to say." She drank her tea. "Salt air."

It was a lie wrapped inside a truth wrapped inside another lie, and both women knew it, and Serah rose and thanked her for the tea and did not push, because pushing a village only taught it to hide better. Better to leave the censer's memory sitting on their tables. Better to walk the fish sheds herself at first light.

* * *

She did not go at first light. She went at midnight, alone, without lanterns, moving through Lowmark like smoke finding its own level.

The fish sheds stood on stilts above the tideline, smelling of brine and old blood, and it was in the gap between the third and fourth shed — the third shed, *reeve*, you careless, loyal woman — that Serah stopped, because she felt it.

Not smelled. Felt. Like standing near a door with a draft coming through.

Someone was in the fourth shed. Someone breathing very carefully, the way people breathe when they've been holding breath for hours. Serah stood in the dark and listened to that careful breathing and felt something she hadn't felt in six years of service: doubt, arriving cold and unwelcome as a wet hand on the neck.

*She's seventeen,* said a voice in her head that sounded disgracefully like her younger sister. *She mended nets. She ran from someone.*

*She's the warden's heir,* said the voice that sounded like Dannel, dead these four years. *And the fire is a theft, and thefts must be returned.*

From inside the shed, very softly, came a sound like a page turning. Then silence. Then, unmistakably, a whisper — a girl's voice saying words in a language Serah didn't know, except that she did, because every Ember inquisitor learned the Old Vaeli liturgy before they learned to write their own names. Words for *door*. Words for *keep*. Words for *hold fast until I come*.

Serah closed her eyes. Ninety years, the Court taught, the sea had waited. Ninety years of rising tides gnawing at coasts, of harbors dying, of the Sundered Sea swelling fat and hungry behind its dam of dragon-fire — and the Court said the answer was to let the fire die and the waters take back what was theirs, all of it, fairly, finally. Let the wound close. Let the ledger balance.

But the girl in the shed was whispering *hold fast until I come*, in the language of the drowned, and it sounded less like theft and more like grief.

Serah opened her eyes. She had a duty. She had a creed said every morning for fifteen years. She had a censer on her belt and orders in her satchel bearing the seal of the Ninth Flame itself.

She also had, she realized with genuine irritation, a conscience, and it was currently taking notes.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would search the sheds properly, with witnesses, with the reeve standing beside her in uncomfortable honesty. Tomorrow she would do her duty in daylight where duty belonged.

Tonight she turned from the fish sheds and walked back through the sleeping village, and she did not report what she had heard, and she told herself the delay was tactical.

It was half true. Half truths were the Court's native currency; she was merely spending some.

Behind her, in the dark above the fourth fish shed, a girl with warden's blood watched her go, and understood, at last and completely, that the world outside the valley had not forgotten Vael after all.

It had been waiting for her.
