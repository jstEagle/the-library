## 7. Salt in the Blood

Tam Wicker had been mending nets beside his grandmother since he was six years old, and in all that time he had learned exactly two things about her: that she could find a tear in a mesh faster than anyone in Lowmark, and that she never, ever spoke of the sea.

Not the way other villagers did. The Stillwater Valley sat forty miles inland from any coast worth the name, but the men at the fish sheds talked about the Sundered Sea constantly — its tides, its temper, the price of salt-fish hauled up from its shallows. His grandmother, Sela, would listen with her hands busy and her face still, like a woman hearing weather reported from a country she'd been exiled from. Once, when Tam was small, he'd asked her why she never went down to look at it. She'd said, *I looked at it long enough for one lifetime,* and had gone back to her net-mending, and he had been too young to hear what was buried under the words.

He was not too young now.

Now he stood in the doorway of the net-shed at dusk, watching Kestrel Sterling — the quiet girl with the scarred hands who'd taken work at Lowmark three months ago and asked no questions about anything — slip out the back past the drying racks, moving fast, moving guilty, with something wrapped in oilcloth tucked against her chest the way you carried a thing you didn't want to be seen holding.

She'd told him to stay out of it. She'd told him that two days ago, standing by the stone arch in the barley field with her palm flat against the humming rock and her eyes gone wide and strange, and when Serah Vane's voice came cutting across the field — sharp, cold, asking after a runaway girl — Tam had lied for her without thinking twice. He was good at lying. He'd learned it young, watching his grandmother do it whenever anyone asked where she was from.

*"You'll want answers,"* she'd said afterward, gripping his sleeve hard enough to bruise. *"I know. But I can't give them to you. Stay home, Tam. Please."*

He'd promised. He was bad at keeping promises made to people who were drowning; he could see it in her, plain as a rip in a net. Something had hold of that girl and was pulling her under, and she was swimming toward it on purpose.

So when he saw her leave the village at moonrise, heading east toward the lake with the wrapped thing pressed to her chest, he did the only honest thing left to him.

He followed.

* * *

The night was warm for the season, and the valley breathed around him — cut hay, woodsmoke, the green rot of the reed beds going down toward the water. Tam kept to the hedgerows the way he'd kept them all his life, hunting rabbits and avoiding the gamekeeper, and it struck him somewhere past the second mile how strange it was that he knew this land so well and had never once wondered why the valley felt the way it did.

Because it did feel a way. Every valley child knew it without naming it. The Stillwater Valley was quiet inside your chest. Other places had a hum to them — his grandmother had once let that slip too, that there were places where the air *itched*, where hedge-witches could pull things out of nothing if they had the knack — but here nothing itched. Here your hands stayed just hands. Here, if you were born with a knack, it slept in you like a stone at the bottom of a well, and most people never even knew they had one.

His grandmother called it the valley's mercy. He'd always thought that was just a saying.

Kestrel moved ahead of him along the lake path, and as the trees thinned near the shore, she stopped. Tam dropped into the reeds, heart hammering, and watched her unwrap the oilcloth.

Even at fifty paces, even by moonlight, he saw it: a map, spread across her knees, and the map was *moving*. Not fluttering in the wind — the wind had died an hour ago. Moving the way ink moves when it's alive, crawling across parchment in slow rivers, drawing coastlines where no coastlines should be, towers rising off the page like smoke given architecture.

And then the light reached her properly and Tam saw that the ink wasn't only on the map.

It was on her.

Dark lines crawled up her forearms beneath her rolled sleeves, delicate as frost ferning across a window, and where they touched her skin she shivered — but she didn't pull away. She leaned closer to the page like a woman leaning over a well to hear an echo come back up.

Tam should have run. Every sensible instinct said run. Instead he found himself creeping closer through the reeds, close enough to hear the map whisper — because it whispered, a dry papery sound like pages turning themselves — and close enough to see what the ink was showing her.

A city. A whole city drawn in lines of living black: harbors crowded with ships whose rigging was rendered stroke by stroke, terraced hillsides, a great mountain at the city's back with a spiral road climbing it, and at the mountain's crown, a mark like a star with a hole burned in the middle of it.

"Vael," Kestrel said softly. Her voice cracked on the word. "Show me Vael."

The ink obeyed. It bloomed outward from her wrist, and Tam watched the drowned city assemble itself in the air above the parchment — faint, translucent, a ghost of light — streets and stairways and a harbor gate shaped like two clasped hands. And underneath it all, far below everything, deep in the mountain's heart, something pulsed. Slowly. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Like something sleeping down there in the dark, breathing fire, and getting tired.

Kestrel made a sound Tam had never heard a person make — half sob, half laugh — and pressed both hands to the map, and the ink rushed up her arms to her shoulders and she *let it*, head tipped back, mouth open around a wordless cry that was not entirely pain.

Tam stood up out of the reeds before he knew he was moving.

"Don't," he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Whatever it's doing to you — don't."

She whirled, ink-lines blazing faintly along her jaw, and for a heartbeat she looked at him like she didn't know him at all. Then the strangeness drained out of her face and left behind something worse: exhaustion, and fear, and a terrible kind of loneliness.

"You followed me." Not a question.

"You're covered in—" He gestured helplessly at all of her. "What is that? What is it doing?"

"It's showing me my inheritance." She laughed, and it was the worst laugh he'd ever heard. "That's the joke, Tam. Everyone wants me to inherit something. The Ember Court wants me dead for it, my old master wants to use it, and this—" she held up her arm, the dark lines shifting like eels under ice "—this wants me to carry it. Apparently I don't get a say."

"The arch," he said. "In the barley field. It hummed when you touched it."

Her head came up sharply. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've walked past that arch my whole life and it has never once hummed for *me*." He stepped closer, over the reeds, into the moonlight, done with hiding. "But my grandmother flinched every time we passed it. Every single time. She'd cross herself with salt — an old habit, she said, from her mother's people. And she never said who her mother's people *were*."

Kestrel went very still.

"What was your grandmother's name?" she asked. "Her true name. Not the one she used here."

"Sela," Tam said. Then, because the question unlocked something in him, something that had been rattling around behind his ribs since the barley field: "But her mother called her Selaveth. I found it carved inside her sewing box, after she died. *Selaveth, daughter of the house of Mirren, Vaeli-born.* I thought it was nonsense. I thought it was just — old words. Pretty ones."

The ink on Kestrel's arms had stopped moving. All of it, every line, turned toward him — he couldn't explain how he knew that, but he knew it, the way you know someone is looking at you — and Kestrel's expression broke open into something between horror and hope.

"House of Mirren," she repeated. "Tam. The door-wardens of Vael had five houses. Mirren was one of them. They kept the *harbor gates*." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "The map remembers them. It would remember their blood."

"That's not possible," Tam said, and heard how hollow it sounded. "We're net-menders. We've been net-menders for ninety years."

"Ninety years." Kestrel rose, wrapping the map slowly. "That's how long ago Vael drowned. That's how long the refugees have been hiding in this valley — the one place magic can't follow, the one place the Ember Court doesn't look. Your grandmother didn't flee the sea, Tam. She fled a *drowning*." She hesitated, then held out her hand, ink-dark fingers open in the moonlight. "Give me yours."

"Why?"

"Because I think the valley isn't numb for you. I think it's been holding its hand over your mouth your whole life, and I think you deserve to hear what your own blood sounds like."

Every prudent part of him said no. He took her hand anyway.

* * *

The moment their palms met, the world rang.

There was no better word for it. Sound poured up through the ground itself, through his boots, through the bones of his legs, a deep bell-note like the arch in the barley field but vaster, older, as if the whole valley floor were a drumhead and something beneath it had finally been struck. The reeds bowed though there was no wind. Across the black mirror of the lake, fish rose in silver rings, dozens of them, as if called.

And in Tam's chest, in the place where the valley's silence had lived all his life — that quiet he'd never questioned, that mercy — something *woke up*.

Salt. That was the first thing. A taste of salt flooding his mouth though he hadn't eaten any, salt and cold and the groan of timber under strain, and for one blinding instant he was standing on a deck that pitched beneath his feet while the sky rained ash and the water came over the seawall in a wall of its own, black and endless, and voices screamed in a language he shouldn't have known but *did*, every word of it —

*Selaveth! Take her hand! SELAVETH —*

Then it was gone. He was on his knees in the mud of the lake shore, gasping, and Kestrel was crouched beside him with her hands hovering, afraid to touch him again.

"I saw—" His voice came out shredded. He swallowed and tried again. "I saw the water come. Over the walls. Someone was shouting a name — my grandmother's name — her real one. She was a *child*." His hands were shaking. He looked at them like they belonged to a stranger. "Kestrel. What was that?"

"That was your blood remembering." She sat back on her heels, and the ink-lines on her skin had dimmed to something almost gentle. "The map shares. I didn't mean for it to — or maybe I did. I'm sorry." She wasn't, quite, and he could tell, and strangely that helped; pity would have been unbearable. "Your family weren't just refugees. House Mirren kept the harbor gates of Vael. When the city sank, they held the last boats open until the boats were full. That's what the warden houses *did*. They stood in doorways so other people could get through."

Tam knelt in the mud and laughed, or cried, or some ruinous combination of both. Ninety years of nets. Ninety years of mending mesh in a valley that smelled of hay instead of brine, while his grandmother's hands — quick, clever, always finding the tear — remembered gates instead.

"She used to tie knots I'd never seen anywhere else," he said. "Gate-knots, she called them. Said they'd hold a door shut against anything." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "She taught me all of them. Made me practice till my fingers bled. I thought it was just — her way of being difficult."

"Nothing about her was 'just' anything." Kestrel pulled the map from its oilcloth and laid it between them on the mud, and this time Tam didn't flinch from the crawling ink. Up close he could see it clearly now: the five great houses marked along Vael's harbor wall, and at the third gate from the sea, a small device stamped in gold that had faded to brown — two interlocked rings, a knot with no beginning and no end.

His grandmother's gate-knot. Exactly. To the twist of it.

"Oh," Tam said. Very quietly. It was the smallest word he'd ever spoken and it contained everything.

"Neither of us is ordinary," Kestrel said. She was looking at the mountain on the map, at the pulsing mark at its crown, and her face had gone grim. "I thought I was running from my master. Then I thought I was running from the Court. Now I know I was running from this. And you—" she glanced up at him, and for the first time since he'd known her, she smiled, small and real and exhausted. "You were mending nets next to the last daughter of House Mirren and neither of you knew it."

The bell-note had faded from the ground, but the lake hadn't settled. Far out from shore, past where the reeds gave way to deep water, the surface was moving — not rippling, *swirling*, slow and deliberate, a rotation wide as a millwheel. And beneath the swirl, faint as memory, something glowed. Green-gold. Patient.

Like a lamp lit in a window of a house where someone had been waiting ninety years for company.

Kestrel unrolled the map fully, and the ink surged toward the lake, lines streaming off the parchment and sinking into the water, drawing downward, drawing *stairs*.

"There's a door under there," she said. "Isn't there. Your grandmother's knot opens doors. My blood wakes them." She stood, already pulling off her boots, already committed — and God help him, Tam realized he was pulling off his own.

"If we drown, I'm haunting you," he said.

"Warden's honor," she said, and stepped into the water.

Behind them, far back down the valley road, a lantern flickered — small, distant, wrong. Lanterns didn't travel the east road at midnight. Lanterns didn't travel the east road at all, not since the toll bridge washed out two winters past.

Tam looked back once, marking it, and followed Kestrel down into the glowing dark.
