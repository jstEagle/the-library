## 12. The Guttering

The first sign was the gulls.

Kestrel noticed it on a grey morning three weeks after the arch in the barley field, standing at Lowmark's tide gate with a mended net heavy over her arms. The gulls had always been a quarrelsome, greedy congregation — they screamed over the fish sheds at dawn, fought over offal, wheeled in their hundreds above the harbor like a living weather. But that morning they were flying inland. All of them. A river of white wings pouring east over the rooftops, away from the sea, and not one of them looked back.

She stood very still with the net dragging her arms down and watched them go.

*The valley forgets,* she reminded herself. *The valley is safe.* She had said it so many times now that it had worn smooth as a river stone, and she touched the thought the way Tam touched the lucky coin in his pocket — not because it proved anything, but because the habit steadied him. But the map case sat against her ribs beneath her coat where it always sat, warm as a sleeping bird, and through its leather she could feel something she had no name for yet. Not pain. Not fear. Something older than either. A slow dimming, like a lamp behind a thick curtain being turned down one careful notch at a time.

"You feel it too," Tam said beside her. He'd come up without a sound, the way he did, his hands jammed in his pockets, his eyes following the gulls. He'd stopped pretending he wasn't watching her every waking hour since the arch. She hadn't decided whether to be grateful or furious about that.

"I don't know what I feel."

"Grandmother would have called it the hush before the high water." His voice was flat, but his knuckles were white inside his pockets. "She used to say the sea doesn't attack. It just comes back for what's owed."

The net slipped in her grip and she let it. Down the harbor, old Brannick was shouting at his deckhands to look west — and when Kestrel turned and looked west, past the headland, past the gray line where Stillwater Bay met the open Sundered Sea, she saw what had emptied the sky of birds.

The horizon glowed wrong.

Not sunrise-wrong; the sun was well up and pale behind cloud. This glow lived low along the waterline itself, a bruised amber smear stretching north and south as far as she could see, pulsing — she pressed her palm flat against her own sternum to be sure the pulse wasn't hers — pulsing like a heartbeat with too much time between beats. Like a fire seen through smoke. Like a great lung drawing breath slower and slower and slower.

"It's beautiful," Tam said quietly.

"It's dying," Kestrel said, and did not know how she knew, and knew anyway, the way the ink under her skin knew things. "That's the dragon-fire. That's what holds the whole Sundered Sea back. And it's going out."

* * *

By noon, the village square smelled of fish brine and panic.

Word traveled the way word always did along a coast — faster than any rider, carried by boatmen and gossip and the particular animal instinct of people who live by water. The herring fleet had come back early, half-empty, and their captains stood in the square talking over each other: the water off the Sedge Islands had risen two feet overnight, swallowing the low rocks where boys fished all summer; a floating beacon at Gullwatch had gone under whole; and far out, where the drowned towers of Vael were supposed to lie beneath forty fathoms, fishermen swore they could see pale shapes beneath the surface now. Rooftops. Spires. A country coming up for air.

Or the sea coming down to drown everything else.

"The Ember Court will fix it," someone said, and someone else laughed, ugly and short. "Fix it? Half of them are saying this is holy. The fire guttering is the beginning of the sea made whole, they've been preaching it for years —"

"They can preach underwater for all I care —"

"— my sister's family is in Saltmere, on the coast, if the sea rises there —"

"If? Look at the horizon, woman, *look at it*—"

Kestrel stood at the edge of the crowd with Tam's shoulder brushing hers, and felt every eye that swept past her snag, briefly, like a hook in cloth. They didn't know what she was. But Lowmark was a small place, and small places remember strangers, and she had been strange from the day she arrived — quiet, watchful, flinching from nothing so carefully that it looked like flinching from everything. And now the sky was the color of an ending and the sea was eating the world, and people who are afraid need somewhere to put their fear.

"There's the girl," said Mother Anselm, not unkindly, from the steps of the salt house. "Kestrel. You came from outside. What's happening beyond the valley?"

Every face turned to her. She felt the map case press hot against her ribs, felt the ink stir beneath her skin like something waking, and understood with terrible clarity that she stood at a crossroads made entirely of words. She could shrug. She could lie. She could say *I'm only a net-mender, I don't know anything* and go back to her cot above the cooper's shed and pull the blanket over her head, and the valley's numbness would fold around her again like deep water, soft and lightless and safe.

Three weeks ago she would have done exactly that.

"The fire that holds the sea is failing," she heard herself say. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "It has held for ninety years, kept alive by people who gave everything they had to keep it burning. It's failing now because there's no one left to feed it." She paused. The square had gone silent enough to hear the harbor lapping. "There may be one person left. And she's standing here."

Tam made a small sound beside her — not surprise. Confirmation.

"And the valley?" Mother Anselm asked. "Is Stillwater safe?"

And there it was. The question Kestrel had built three weeks of quiet mornings and mended nets around. *Safe.* She thought of the numbness, the way magic slid off the valley like rain off oiled wool, the way she'd shed her apprentice name and her witchcraft at the border stones like wet clothes and never once missed them. Safe. Yes. For her. For perhaps a few thousand souls tucked into this green fold of land while the coasts drowned.

But the map had shown her the arithmetic of the Sundered Sea, and the arithmetic did not stop at the valley walls.

"No," she said. "Not if the fire dies. The valley isn't protected by its stones, Mother. It's protected by the same fire that holds the sea everywhere else — the valley's stillness is just the shadow of the flame falling across it. When the flame goes out—" She stopped, because she could see it then, clearly, horribly: the Stillwater Valley as a cup, and the Sundered Sea as the ocean leaning over its rim. "When the flame goes out, there are no safe places. There's only higher ground and lower ground, and the sea climbing."

* * *

They started arriving that evening.

Kestrel saw the first of them from the headland path, where she'd gone with the map open across her palms — actually open now, in the open air, which she had promised herself she'd never do, but the ink had grown restless since morning and she'd needed to look. The coastline of Vael shimmered on the leather, and beneath the drowned streets she could see the mountain, Mount Vael, and the vein of fire at its heart drawn in ink that flickered. Flickered. Ink should not flicker. Ink should not gutter like a candle in a draft.

She was staring at it, willing it brighter, when Tam caught her arm.

"Look south."

On the coast road, a thin dark thread was crawling toward the valley: carts, donkeys, families on foot, bundles tied in blankets, children carrying smaller children. Behind them, further south along the shore, another thread joined it, and another, converging the way streams converge. Hundreds. By tomorrow, thousands. People from Saltmere, from the fishing villages of the Sedge Coast, from towns Kestrel had only ever seen as smudges on Maren's old trade maps — all of them moving inland, moving toward the one place any story or rumor or grandmother's tale said the sea could not follow.

"They're coming here," Tam said. "To Lowmark. To the valley."

"I know."

"Lowmark has four hundred people. The valley maybe three thousand, in a bad harvest year. There's barley for us and not for ten thousand more. There's—" He stopped, jaw working. "My grandmother came here the same way. Ninety years ago, out of Vael, when the sea took the city. The valley took her in. Barely. She told me stories about the winter — how they ate the seed grain, how half the Vaeli died anyway, how the villagers who shared their bread were heroes and the ones who didn't were only human." He looked at her, and in the last light his face looked suddenly very young and very old at once. "It's starting again. Except this time the sea won't stop at the valley wall, will it? This time there's nowhere the threads end."

Kestrel closed the map. The ink pulsed once against her palms through the leather, faint as a moth's wing.

"No," she said. "Nowhere."

* * *

Night, and the cooper's shed, and she could not sleep.

She lay on her narrow cot listening to the village below — voices, torchlight, the creak of carts being unloaded in the square, Mother Anselm's clear voice organizing bread and floor space with the brisk mercy of a woman who had buried a husband and three sons and knew exactly what grief required, which was soup and work. Refugees slept stacked in the salt house and the drying lofts. More came hourly. Somewhere a child cried with the flat, exhausted persistence of a child too tired even to sob properly.

Kestrel got up, lit no candle, and unwrapped the map by feel.

In the darkness the ink glowed faintly — it had begun doing that days ago, and she had told no one, not even Tam. The coastline of the Sundered Sea lay before her, and along every coast, in fine creeping strokes, the ink of the sea was advancing. Not drawn. Advancing. She watched it take a harbor town she didn't know, a river mouth, the long peninsula of the Sedge Coast — watched the little marks of houses vanish one by one under the rising line, and understood that she was not looking at a map of what was. She was looking at a map of what was *becoming*, updating itself with every beat of the failing fire, hundreds of miles of coastline drowning in real time while she lay safe in a valley full of borrowed bread.

Her hand drifted, as it always drifted, to the mountain. Mount Vael. The heart of fire at its crown, drawn in that flickering ink.

*I am the warden's daughter,* the dead man had said, pressing the case into her hands with the last warmth of his burned body. *The door answers blood. Only blood.*

She had spent three weeks telling herself she had time. Time to learn, time to prepare, time to decide. Cowardice wears patience's clothing beautifully; she knew that better than anyone, having learned it at Maren's knee. But the map under her fingers was ticking down like a clock, and tonight, watching the sea eat the world stroke by patient stroke, she finally understood the shape of the trap her own heart had built.

There was no version of this where she stayed.

If she ran deeper into the valley — there was no deeper, the valley was a cup, and cups fill. If she hid the map, the map would still burn against her skin like a coal in a pocket. If she gave it away, it would crawl back to her, because it was bound to her blood, because inheritance is not a thing you can set down like a basket. Every road led to the mountain. Some roads simply pretended otherwise, and those were the most dangerous, because you could walk them for years believing yourself free.

A soft knock at the shed door. Tam, wrapped in a blanket, holding two cups of Mother Anselm's terrible barley tea.

"You're leaving," he said. Not a question. He'd read it in her face before she'd read it in herself, probably.

"The fire's dying, Tam. The sea's taking the coasts right now, tonight, while we stand here. Thousands of people are walking toward a valley that can't hold them and won't protect them, and they're walking toward it because it's the only direction left." She wrapped the map closed. "I'm not leaving *you*. I'm leaving the lie. The lie was that I could stay."

He set the cups down on the workbench, untouched, and looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the amber stain on the western horizon pulsed — she could see it even through the window, even at night now, the guttering visible against the stars.

"When do we go?"

"*We* —"

"My grandmother was Vaeli. My blood is in this whether you want it or not. And besides—" the ghost of his crooked smile, the one that had gotten him through every scrape of his childhood and would probably get him killed someday, "—someone has to carry the tea."

Kestrel wanted to laugh. What came out instead was closer to grief, brief and sharp, and she let it, because the night was already full of worse sounds.

Far to the west, over the drowned horizon, the glow stuttered — actually stuttered, a full heartbeat of darkness, long enough that every refugee in the square below went silent at once, thousands of faces turning west together like iron filings snapping toward a magnet. Then the glow returned, weaker.

But in that heartbeat of darkness, Kestrel felt the map leap against her chest like a struck bell, and heard — not with her ears, in the marrow of her — a sound she would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life:

A roar, vast and ancient and drowning, from the heart of a mountain at the bottom of the sea.

The dragon was calling its wardens home.

"We go at first light," Kestrel said.
