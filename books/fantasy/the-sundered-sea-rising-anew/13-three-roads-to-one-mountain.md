## 13. Three Roads to One Mountain

### I. Kestrel

The mountain had been visible for two days before Kestrel admitted she could see it.

It rose from the drowned coast like the last tooth of a broken jaw — Mount Vael, black-basalted and scarred, its caldera wreathed in a haze that was not cloud. The haze glowed. It had been glowing wrong for a week now, ever since the night in the flooded archive when the coral librarian had pressed a shard of dragon-glass into her palm and said, *the fire gutters, warden, and you are late.* Now the light above it pulsed like a wound breathing: gold, then grey, then gold again, each pulse fainter than the last.

Kestrel walked at the head of their small, impossible company and did not look back, because if she looked back she would see Tam limping, and Serah Vane riding ten paces off with her hand never far from her sword-hilt, and the whole fragile architecture of their truce would feel as thin as it was.

They had left the valley through the stone arch in the barley field three days ago. Behind them, the Stillwater Valley's numbness had begun to crack — she had felt it go like ice leaving a pond, magic seeping back into hedgerows and well-water, hedge-witches waking from ninety years of ordinary sleep with their old arts tingling in their fingers. She had not stayed to see what that waking looked like. There hadn't been time. There was never going to be time again.

"You're favoring your left side," she said without turning around.

"I'm *resting* my left side," Tam called back. "There's a difference. One of them is dignified."

She smiled despite herself. That was Tam's whole talent, she was beginning to understand — making the unbearable briefly bearable, one joke at a time, while his grandmother's Vaeli blood sang in him quiet and unrecognized. He had crossed half the Sundered Sea coast with her now, through refugee roads and Ember Court checkpoints, and he had never once asked her to slow down or to stop. He only asked, sometimes, in the dark before sleep, whether she was still warm. Because since the archive she *was* warmer than a person ought to be, the map's ink settled into her skin like a second circulatory system, and they both pretended not to know what that meant.

Behind them both, Serah Vane rode her gaunt grey horse in silence, wearing no armor and no colors, which somehow made her more frightening, not less. A week ago she had turned her blade on her own brothers-in-faith in the drowned archive's wake. She had burned her orders rather than deliver them. She had said exactly eleven words about why: *"I was taught the fire was theft. I was lied to."* And then she had asked, in the flat voice of a woman dismantling her own faith plank by plank, whether she could come with them.

Kestrel had said yes because refusing would have cost more than trusting. She was not yet sure that was the same thing as forgiving.

The road climbed away from the coast through terraced hills gone wild — old Vaeli vineyards swallowed by broom and gorse, stone walls crumbling in patterns too regular to be natural. The map-memory stirred under Kestrel's skin as they passed. She closed her eyes for half a stride and saw it: these same slopes green and ordered, women with copper bracelets singing the vines awake, the mountain behind them breathing slow and golden. Her great-great-grandmother's hands in the dirt.

"You're doing it again," Tam said softly, catching up beside her.

"Doing what?"

"Walking in two places at once." He glanced at her forearm, where the map-ink showed faintly through her sleeve, silver lines moving like something alive. "Does it hurt? You can tell me if it hurts."

"It doesn't hurt," she said. That was almost true. It didn't hurt; it *weighed*. Every day the memories of Vael sat a little deeper in her, and every day she understood better what the librarian had meant about wardens being doors — doors don't get to be only on one side of the wall.

Ahead, where the road forked around a burned-out waystation, three figures stood waiting among the crows.

Kestrel stopped so fast Tam walked into her back.

The tallest figure raised a hand — not a threat, a greeting, and the voice that carried down the slope was dry and familiar and made Kestrel's stomach drop through the road itself.

"Well," said Maren. "You look terrible, child. Have you been eating?"

### II. Tam

Tam had heard stories about Maren the way you hear stories about weather — Kestrel's voice going flat whenever the name came up, all the windows shut. He had built a picture of a monster: a hedge-witch with hooks instead of hands, someone who had wanted to carve Kestrel open for a map.

The woman leaning on a staff at the waystation was sixty-ish, sunburnt, travel-stained, with laugh lines around eyes that were currently very much not laughing. She looked like someone's grandmother. She looked, Tam thought uneasily, a little like his own grandmother, dead four years, who had hummed songs in a language nobody else in Lowmark knew.

"Maren," Kestrel said. Her voice had gone cold and careful, a door locked from inside. "How did you find us?"

"You leave a trail a blind man could follow. The valley's numbness broke — thank you for that, by the way, my knees have ached for a week — and every hedge-witch between here and the Stillwater felt it like a bell ringing. Then the horizon started glowing wrong and everyone with any craft at all started walking toward the mountain. I just walked faster." Maren's gaze moved past Kestrel, catalogued Tam in a heartbeat, then fixed on Serah Vane with an expression of genuine, unguarded loathing. "And you brought *that*."

"She burned her orders," Tam said. "Saved our lives in the archive."

Maren snorted. "Inquisitors burn things. It's practically their trade."

"Maren." Kestrel stepped between them. "Why are you here?"

And there it was, Tam thought. The moment. He watched the old woman's face do something complicated — grief and hunger wrestling behind the laugh lines — before she settled on warmth, which fit her like an ill-tailored coat.

"To help you, child. To carry what I can of this burden, since I'm the one who put it on your shoulders. I was wrong, how I went about it. I've had a long road to know it." She spread her hands. "But the fire is guttering, and I am the finest hedge-witch alive, and you'll need every scrap of craft on that mountain. Even mine. *Especially* mine."

Tam watched Kestrel's jaw tighten. He watched her want to believe it — he could see the wanting, the tiredness underneath, the part of her that had run from this woman and missed her every single day since. He moved closer to Kestrel without quite deciding to.

"We camp here tonight," Kestrel said finally. "We walk at dawn. Nobody makes decisions after dark."

"That," said Maren, "sounds wise."

But later, when the fire was low and Kestrel slept, Tam lay awake and saw, across the dying embers, Maren sitting up with her staff across her knees, watching Kestrel breathe. Watching her the way Tam had once seen a man watch a purse he intended to lift. Not hatred. Something worse. Something patient.

He closed his eyes and did not sleep.

### III. Serah Vane

Serah Vane had spent nineteen years believing that doubt was a disease and conviction the cure. She had memorized the Ember Catechism at seven, taken the ash-mark at fifteen, and buried her first heretic at twenty-two — a fishwife who'd kept a forbidden hearth-charm, nothing more, and Serah had done it because the creed required it and the creed was the sea-wall against chaos.

Now she sat apart from the others' fire, sharpening a sword she was no longer certain she had the right to carry, and rehearsed for the hundredth time the sentence that kept her up nights:

*The Ember Court engineered the drowning of Vael.*

Not suspected. Not feared. *Found*, in the sealed dispatch-case of her own superior, in the archive's antechamber: orders dated ninety years past, countersigned in ash-and-salt, authorizing the "permanent extinguishment of the Vael door and the inundation of the recalcitrant population." She had read them twice. Then she had burned them, and burning them had not helped, because the words were in her head now, printed in the Court's beautiful formal script, and they would not smudge.

Her brothers-in-faith were coming. Of course they were. Inquisitor Dain Corvath had survived the archive — she'd left him breathing, a mistake she was revising hourly — and Corvath did not forgive, did not forget, and did not travel slowly. Somewhere behind them on these roads, a remnant of the Court was marching toward the mountain with extinguisher-irons and salt-casks and the serene certainty of men who had never once doubted anything. They would reach the caldera. They would try to kill the fire while it was weak enough to kill.

And she was sitting at a fire with the people trying to save it, sharpening her sword, wondering which side of her own blade she would end up on.

"The edge is fine," said Maren, appearing at her shoulder with the soundless tread of a woman who had crept up on frightened apprentices for forty years. "You're stalling."

Serah did not look up. "Go away, witch."

"You hate me. Good. Hate keeps the mind clear." Maren sat down uninvited, which was apparently something she simply did. "You're calculating distances, inquisitor. How far behind us Corvath is. Whether you could reach the mountain first, warn them, arm them — or whether it would be kinder to slip away tonight and let the girl climb alone."

Serah's whetstone stopped.

"I told you," Maren said gently, horribly, "hate keeps the mind clear. But guilt keeps it sharp. Don't confuse the two."

"I should have let you hang in Lowmark," Serah said.

"Yes, probably. But you were busy having your entire soul rearranged, so I'll forgive the oversight." The old witch leaned closer, and her voice dropped to something almost kind. "Here's what your catechism never taught you, Vane: the fire isn't a weapon and it isn't a theft. It's a promise. Ninety years of wardens keeping it, one life-thread at a time. Your Court broke that promise and drowned a country for it. Whatever you decide to do when Corvath comes — make sure it pays the debt, and not the interest."

She rose and shuffled back toward the fire. Serah sat in the dark a long time, sword across her knees, listening to the mountain breathe its failing gold light over the ridge, and tried to remember the exact wording of the vow she had taken at fifteen.

She found she could only recall the seal.

### IV. Kestrel

Dawn came up bruise-colored, and the mountain was closer.

They walked. The road gave out an hour past the waystation and became a pilgrim track, and the pilgrim track became a goat path stitched along the shoulders of old terraces, and all the while the glow above them strengthened and sickened. By midday Kestrel could feel the dragon-fire directly, without the map's help — a vast slow heartbeat under the rock, each beat weaker, intervals stretching like a sleeper struggling toward a surface that kept receding.

*Two days,* the map-memory murmured against her bones. *Three, if the wind stays kind. Then the sea takes back everything.*

They were not alone on the path. Refugees moved ahead of them and behind them, a thin scattered stream of the displaced — coastal families with carts and goats and children carrying cats, all walking inland, all walking nowhere, because inland was drowning too now, slowly, tide by stubborn tide. Some of them recognized what Serah had begun calling Kestrel's *marking* — the silver lines that showed at her wrists and throat when she sweated — and one old woman pressed a dried fish into her hands and whispered *warden* like a prayer, and Kestrel carried the fish for an hour before she could eat it, because it weighed more than fish should weigh.

At a spring in the shadow of the fourth terrace, they rested, and Kestrel took stock of her strange company and did not like the arithmetic.

Tam, filling waterskins, humming his grandmother's tuneless song. Faithful, funny, and hopelessly mortal — she had watched him cough dust on the climb and hated herself for counting his breaths.

Serah, standing watch on the high rocks, sword bare across her shoulders like a yoke. An enemy three weeks old, an ally of uncertain depth, and the only person among them who knew how Corvath fought.

Maren, dozing against a wall with her staff in her lap. Skilled beyond anyone living. Sorry, perhaps, in whatever way Maren was capable of being sorry. And hungry — Kestrel had seen it now, plain as the laugh lines, the hunger that had driven everything: to hold the map, to hold the bloodline, to hold *her*. Love and appetite braided so long they'd become one rope.

Four people. One mountain. And somewhere behind them, an inquisitor with extinguisher-irons, and somewhere ahead, a dying god of fire waiting to ask its question.

*Kestrel Sterling,* she thought, using the name she'd made for herself in the valley, the one that belonged to nobody's daughter and nobody's apprentice. *Look what you collected.*

"Kes." Tam crouched beside her at the spring, voice pitched low. "Can I say something you won't like?"

"You usually do."

"Your master. Last night, when you were sleeping—" He hesitated, jaw working. "I saw her watching you. And I've been thinking about how she *found* us. About how she always seems to know which road we'll take before we take it." He met Kestrel's eyes. "I think she's not following the mountain, Kes. I think she's following *you*, and I think she has a plan, and I don't think we're in it except as scenery."

Kestrel looked across the spring at her old master — small, grey, sun-worn, dozing like innocence itself.

"I know," she said quietly.

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the waystation. Maren doesn't do anything without a plan, and she doesn't make plans that don't end with her holding the important thing." She stood, brushing off her skirts, and felt the map shift along her arms like a living thing settling closer to its keeper. "But she's also the best chance we have of getting up that mountain alive, and I can't cut her loose and I can't trust her. So until one of those stops being true—"

"—you walk the knife-edge," Tam finished. "That's not a plan, Kes."

"No," she agreed, looking up at the caldera's failing glow. "The plan is higher up. This is just the road."

They climbed through the afternoon into country where the terraces were carved with faces — the wardens, the librarian had told her, though Kestrel had known it the moment she saw them: every statue a person who had given their life-thread to the fire, weathered now to suggestion, their mouths worn soft as if they'd finished speaking long ago and were waiting for her answer.

Near dusk they reached the saddle below the final ascent, a shelf of flat ground sheltered by a ring of standing stones, and made camp because there was no farther to go tonight. The fire-light pulsed overhead, gold-grey, gold-grey, slower now. Kestrel could count the beats between her own heartbeats.

She woke once, deep in the smallest hour, to voices.

Maren's, low and urgent, at the edge of the stones. And another voice answering — a voice Kestrel knew, ash-dry and precise, a voice from the archive, a voice that should have been days behind them.

Corvath.

Kestrel lay perfectly still in the dark, her blood gone cold despite the fire in her veins, and listened to her master bargain with the enemy by starlight — and could not hear a single word of the price.

### V. Maren

Maren had outmaneuvered kings, guildmasters, two archmages, and one extremely persistent tax collector, and she had never yet lost a negotiation she chose to enter.

She stood now in the standing stones' long shadows, facing Inquisitor Dain Corvath — thinner than the archive, burnt along one jaw, his extinguisher-irons wrapped and slung like a pilgrim's relics — and told herself the familiar litany: *he needs me more than I need him. The girl cannot light that fire without a bridge-mother's craft. I am indispensable.*

"My terms stand," she said. "The girl reaches the caldera untouched. Your people withdraw to the lower terraces. Once the fire is rekindled, you may argue theology with it all you like."

"And your payment?" Corvath's voice was mild, which she distrusted; mildness was what zealots used instead of patience. "You said you wanted payment."

"The map's craft. The warden lore. What's in the girl's blood, written down properly, in my keeping." Maren smiled her best reasonable smile. "I've spent forty years chasing this inheritance, inquisitor. I mean to hold *something* when it's done."

Something flickered behind Corvath's eyes — something that was not agreement, was barely even interest, was in fact a kind of pity, and Maren's litany stumbled for the first time in forty years.

"Done," Corvath said. "At dawn, witch. Bring your people up the eastern stair. We'll be waiting."

He melted into the dark, and Maren stood alone among the stones with the mountain's failing heartbeat overhead, and told herself, firmly, in the voice she used on frightened apprentices:

*You are indispensable. You are always indispensable.*

Above her, the fire beat once — gold — and then, for the first time in ninety years, skipped.

Somewhere in the dark below, the sea sighed against the terraces, one wave higher than the last.
