## 14. Maren's Bargain

Maren had always believed that love was a thing you could plan.

She had planned it when she took Kestrel in — a scrawny child with salt-burned eyes and no family worth the name, left on the chapel steps at Greyharrow like an unclaimed parcel. She had planned it through the years of lessons: how to read the weather in a cow's temper, how to charm a fever out of a sickroom without charming the fever into herself, how to hold power the way one held a bird — firmly enough that it could not fly off, gently enough that it did not die of fright. She had planned it even now, walking the pilgrim road toward Mount Vael with frost in her joints and a lie rehearsed so many times it had worn smooth as river stone.

*You are doing this for her*, she told herself, and the words fit so well she had stopped noticing they were a cage.

The mountain was two days ahead, maybe three if the passes were drifted with snow. Behind her, half a day back along the road, the Ember Court remnant moved like a wound through the countryside — forty zealots in ash-grey cloaks, their armor painted with the sigil of the guttering candle, led by a man named Corvane whose face Maren had seen once, years ago, across a burning market square, and had never managed to forget.

She had spent the morning calculating.

That was what she did. That was who she was. While other hedge-witches trusted to instinct and prayer and the slow wisdom of herbs, Maren had made herself a student of leverage. Every soul had a weight, and every weight could be moved if you found the right place to stand. Corvane wanted the fire extinguished; he also wanted the girl who carried the map. Those desires could be set against each other like two stones on a balance beam, and Maren had walked half the night turning the problem over until she found the fulcrum.

Herself.

It was almost elegant. It was certainly the kind of solution Kestrel would have hated, which was, Maren admitted in her darker hours, part of its appeal. The girl had run from her rather than be used, had chosen cowardice — no, Maren corrected herself, *prudence*, though the word tasted like a kindness she didn't believe — rather than complicity. But there was a difference between using someone and spending yourself on their behalf, and Maren intended to make that difference very clear to the universe before this road ended.

She crested a rise where the barley fields of the valley gave way to scree and old pine, and looked back the way she'd come.

They were visible even at distance: a dark ribbon of movement on the pale road, unhurried, inevitable. They did not need to hurry. Zealots never did. They had the patience of people who believed time itself was on their side, because they believed the world was ending and therefore every moment was already paid for.

Maren pulled her cloak tighter and considered, not for the first time, simply walking away.

There were roads south. There were roads west, down to the Sundered Sea proper, where a woman of her skills could vanish into any of a hundred port towns and live out her remaining years drinking bad wine and telling fortunes to gullible merchants. No one would know. No one would ever have to know that Maren of Greyharrow, who had once been called clever by people who mattered, had turned her back on the last heir of Vael and let the sea take whatever the sea was going to take.

But then she thought of Kestrel's face at nine years old, feverish with chickenpox, gripping Maren's hand and whispering, *don't leave, don't leave, don't leave,* and Maren knew — with the cold certainty of a woman reading her own bones — that she would walk into that grey-cloaked column if that was what it cost.

Love, she thought bitterly, was the one spell that never worked the way you cast it.

* * *

She let them find her at dusk, at a shrine to some forgotten river god that stood where the road bent around a shoulder of rock. The shrine was older than the Court, older than the Sundering, maybe; its god's face had been worn blank by centuries of rain, which seemed fitting. Maren built a small fire in the lee of the stones, boiled tea, and waited with the composed air of a woman expecting company.

Corvane arrived an hour after full dark, with six men and no lanterns. His zealots spread out around the shrine in a practiced arc, and Maren noted their spacing, their silence, the way their hands stayed near their blades without resting on them. Well-drilled. The Court had lost its Grand Inquisitor and half its hierarchy in the purges two winters past, but the remnant had kept its discipline. Discipline was the one thing fire couldn't burn away.

"Maren." Corvane said her name the way he might identify a specimen. He was younger than she remembered — or perhaps the burning had aged everyone else while he alone remained fixed, preserved in conviction like a fly in amber. "The hedge-witch of Greyharrow. We've been following your scent for nine days."

"I made it easy," Maren said. "I wanted this meeting."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not surprise exactly, but the recalibration of a man who had expected a chase and received an invitation. He gestured, and his people eased back a step, though not out of blade-reach. He lowered himself onto the opposite stone with the unhurried grace of a man who owned the ground he sat on.

"Talk, then."

"The girl." Maren poured a second cup of tea and set it on the stone between them, an old trader's courtesy that cost nothing and softened everything. "Kestrel Sterling. You want her."

"We want the map she carries."

"She *is* the map, now. You must know that. Your own diviners will have felt the ink take her blood. The parchment is a dead skin she's shed; the living chart is written on her veins. Kill her and you lose Vael forever — the door-wardens' line ends, the arch seals, and the drowned country stays drowned whether the fire gutters or not." Maren sipped her own tea, letting the silence do work. "You can't have both the extinguishing and the ending. You'll have to choose which failure you can live with."

"You presume to instruct me—"

"I presume to *trade*." She set down the cup. Her hand, she was pleased to note, did not shake. "Here is my offer. I am the only person alive who knows the warden bloodline's rites — not the theory, not the archive-scholar's guesswork, but the actual working. I taught myself twenty years ago from fragments your own Court burned in the Greyharrow purges, because I understood even then what was coming. Without me, the girl stumbles up that mountain carrying an inheritance she doesn't understand and dies on the caldera with the fire half-lit. With me—" she met Corvane's eyes, and put every ounce of her considerable skill for seeming into her voice, "—I can deliver her to you *before* the caldera. Before the fire. Before anything is lit or sealed or saved. You extinguish the flame cleanly, with the last door-warden alive to watch her country stay dead. And in exchange, she lives. Unharmed. Free to walk away afterward, wherever she likes, and you never touch her."

The wind came down off the mountain and stirred the ashes of her little fire.

"And what do you ask for yourself?" Corvane asked softly.

"Nothing you'd value. My freedom too. And the satisfaction of knowing the job was done properly."

He studied her for a long moment, and Maren watched him watching her, and thought: *he believes me.* Of course he believed her. It was the oldest truth of her craft, older than charms and wards and the cunning use of herbs — people believed what flattered their understanding of the world. To Corvane, all magic-users were self-serving creatures who would sell their own students for passage; Maren was merely confirming his cosmology. A zealot's faith was a fortress, but it had this one gate, and she was riding straight through it.

"You understand," Corvane said, "that if this is a ruse—"

"If it were a ruse, Inquisitor, I'd have invented a better one." She smiled, thin and dry. "I'm old. I'm tired. I want this finished with as little blood as possible. Isn't that what your creed promises? A whole sea, a quiet world, the long pain ended? I'm offering to end it *gently*. Take the offer. Gently is rare."

He took the tea after all. Drank it. And said, "Walk with us, then, witch. Tomorrow we climb."

* * *

That night, wrapped in her cloak at the edge of the zealots' camp, Maren allowed herself the luxury of review.

It had gone well. Better than well — Corvane had accepted terms that any competent commander should have refused, because the offer confirmed everything he already believed about her kind. She would walk at the front of the column tomorrow. When they found Kestrel — and they would find her, the girl was making for the mountain as surely as water made for the sea — Maren would position herself between the zealots' blades and her student, and in the confusion of the confrontation—

And here, in the privacy of her own skull, the plan grew honest.

Because the truth was that Maren had no intention of handing Kestrel to anyone. The bargain was a key cut to fit one lock: get close enough to protect the girl when steel came out. Forty zealots against a hedge-witch was poor odds in open battle, but Maren had spent thirty years learning the thousand small magics that turned odds — the word that soured a man's grip at the crucial instant, the charm that made torchlight lie about distances, the hex, cheap and vicious, that filled a lung with the memory of drowning. She needed only minutes. Minutes to break Kestrel loose and send her up the mountain ahead of them all, buying the girl the hours she needed to reach the caldera while Maren settled accounts with the Court once and for all.

Whether she survived those accounts was a question she declined to examine. There were things a planner was permitted not to plan.

She looked up at the stars, sharp and cold above the black shoulder of Mount Vael, and felt something she almost never allowed herself: uncertainty. Not about the plan — the plan was sound, the plan was *hers*, and plans were the one country where she had never been defeated. Uncertain about the girl. About whether Kestrel, seeing her master walk into the enemy's camp, would understand. About whether understanding would even matter.

*I fled being used,* Kestrel had screamed at her, that last terrible morning in Greyharrow, and the words had followed Maren down every mile since. *You don't want an apprentice, you want an instrument.*

"No," Maren murmured to the stars, an argument eleven months stale and no less urgent for its age. "No, child. An instrument does what it's told and breaks. I wanted you to *choose*. There's all the difference in the world."

But she had never let Kestrel choose, had she? That was the splinter under the skin of it. Every lesson had been a corridor with one door open. Every confidence had been baited. She had loved the girl the way she loved everything — by managing it — and management, given enough years, becomes indistinguishable from captivity, and the captive runs.

Well. There was one choice left that Maren could give honestly, without corridors, without bait. One clean gift, bought with the only coin she had never spent.

She slept, briefly, and dreamed of a kitchen in Greyharrow, and a girl with salt-burned eyes asking why the sea was sad, and woke with wet cheeks she blamed on the frost.

* * *

The column moved at dawn, and Maren learned within the hour that she had made one error, and errors on this road were expensive.

It was a small thing, at first. Corvane rode beside her and asked questions — artless-seeming questions, the questions of a man making conversation: How long had she known the girl? What rites precisely? What did the caldera require? She answered each one smoothly, feeding him truths braided with omissions, and it was only when he asked, idly, *and the life-thread, witch — does it kill the warden, or bind her?* that Maren felt the trap close around her ankle like a snare-line drawn taut.

Because she had answered before she thought, and her answer was true, and true answers were the most dangerous currency she carried.

"It binds," she had said. "It doesn't kill. The wardens don't die at the caldera — worse luck for them. They become the door. Forever."

Corvane had nodded slowly, and looked at the mountain, and said nothing more for three miles.

It was at the noon halt, eating hard bread among men who would not meet her eyes, that Maren finally saw it — laid out in her mind's eye with the terrible clarity she brought to other people's schemes but so rarely her own. Corvane had never believed her. Or rather: he had believed her *exactly*, and belief was not the same as trust. He had accepted her bargain because the bargain served him either way. If she was honest, she delivered Kestrel to him bound and helpless. If she was false — and he had watched her face when she spoke of binding, had heard the mother's grief she'd failed to fully bury — then he knew she would turn at the critical moment, and he would let her turn, and let her commit, and take her in the act with justification sanctified by her own treachery.

Either way he won. Either way the girl was taken. The bargain was not a bridge. It was a net, and she had walked into it smiling, proud of her own cleverness, and behind his eyes Corvane had been patient the way zealots were patient, because the world was ending and every moment was already paid for.

Maren sat with her bread going stale in her hand and laughed — quietly, hopelessly, genuinely — at the elegance of it.

*Outmaneuvered by a man who thinks strategy is a sin,* she thought. *Because he doesn't need to outthink me. He only needs to out-wait me. Faith has no flank to turn.*

There was, she reflected, a certain justice in it. All her life she had moved people like weights on a balance beam, certain that love was a thing you could plan, and here at the end of everything she discovered the beam had been standing on her chest the whole time.

Very well, then. The plan was dead. Long live the truth.

* * *

They came upon Kestrel's trail at dusk of the second day — boot-prints in the snow-melt, fresh, climbing, and beside them a second set, broader, a boy's stride. Maren's heart clenched to see them: the girl so close, so nearly safe, walking into the teeth of forty zealots with nothing but an inheritance and a farm boy.

Corvane raised his hand and the column halted. Torches were passed down the line and lit, one by one, until the mountainside glimmered with a procession of small flames, and Maren understood with perfect clarity what the display was for. Not light. Theater. The Ember Court announcing itself to the night, to the mountain, to God.

"They're close," Corvane said. "Witch. You'll walk at the front."

"Of course."

"And when we find them—" he paused, and turned, and for the first time since the shrine his composure cracked just enough to show the fanatic underneath, bright and burning, "—you will call out to her. You will tell her the Court keeps its bargains, and she may come down safely, and no harm will touch the boy. And she will believe you, because she loves you, and love believes. Won't she?"

The trap, sprung at last, in plain view. He wasn't going to wait for her treachery. He was going to *use* her treachery — her love, the one thing she had never managed to manage — as the instrument itself. All those years of planning, and in the end she would be the lure hung over the snare.

Maren looked at him. She looked at the torches, at the dark road climbing toward the terraces of dead Vael, at the faint wrong glow on the horizon where the dragon-fire guttered and the sea leaned hungrily against the world. She thought of Kestrel at nine years old, feverish, gripping her hand. *Don't leave, don't leave, don't leave.*

"Yes," Maren said. "She'll believe me."

She began to walk, at the head of the column, into the dark — and behind her eyes, swift and silent, she began to build her last plan, the one with no leverages and no balances and no way out, the one that consisted entirely of stepping between the blade and the girl, and staying there.

Somewhere above, on the mountain, the fire was dying. She meant to be the reason it didn't matter.
