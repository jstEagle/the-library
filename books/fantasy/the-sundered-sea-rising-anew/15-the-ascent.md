## 15. The Ascent

The mountain had been waiting for her.

Kestrel felt it in the soles of her feet before she saw the terraces — a hum rising through the broken road like a heartbeat heard through a wall, patient and enormous and old. Behind them, the drowned archive's lake lay still and gray in the morning light, and above them, Mount Vael climbed into clouds that glowed from beneath, as though the sky itself were lit by coals raked under ash.

She stopped at the foot of the first terrace and looked up. The road switchbacked through what had once been vineyards — Vaeli wine, the librarian's memories whispered in her blood, sweet as honey and dark as garnets — and every terrace wall was cracked and blackened, veined with lines of dull red light that pulsed in slow rhythm. The dragon-fire, guttering. Even here, miles below the caldera, she could feel it faltering: a great breath drawn and held for ninety years, now shuddering toward its end.

"It's warm," Tam said beside her. He had his pack straps cinched tight and his grandmother's copper charm knotted around his wrist, and he was looking at the glowing veins in the stone with an expression she couldn't read — awe, or fear, or some braided thing made of both. "The whole mountain's warm. Like standing next to a hearth."

"Like standing inside one," Kestrel said. "Eventually."

He glanced at her. She hadn't meant to say it aloud, and she didn't take it back.

They climbed.

* * *

The first statue stood at the third turning of the road, where the terraces widened into what had been a plaza, its paving stones burst apart by roots that had no business surviving ninety years underwater and yet had — pale and boneless things, salt-bleached, curling back into the cracks now that the sea was receding.

Kestrel knew the figure at once, though she had never seen it. She knew it the way she knew the map's coastlines, the way she knew the names of harbors that had drowned before her grandmother was born.

It was a woman, carved taller than life, standing with her arms flung wide. Not triumphant. Open. Her face was worn smooth by water and time, but her posture said everything the eroded features could not: *here I am, take it, I give freely.* From the statue's chest, a channel had been cut into the stone, running down through the plaza and up the mountainside, and in that channel — faint, almost invisible — a thread of light still moved. Red-gold. Slow as honey. A life-thread, woven into stone, still burning after ninety years.

"She gave everything," Kestrel said quietly. Her hand rose without her permission and touched the statue's worn fingers. Warm. The stone was *warm*. "And it's still not enough anymore."

"Who is she?"

"Ilset Vane." The name came out of her mouth before she knew she possessed it, surfacing from the map's memories like a fish rising to feed. "Third warden of the fire. She held it alone for forty-one years after the drowning, because there was no one left to relieve her." Kestrel swallowed. "The statues are all wardens. Every person who ever gave their thread to keep the fire alive."

Tam looked up at the woman with the open arms for a long moment. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"Statues. How many wardens?"

Kestrel closed her eyes and let the map answer. Numbers unspooled behind her eyelids, patient and terrible. "Two hundred and six," she said. "Ilset was the sixty-first."

Neither of them spoke for a while after that.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the terraces grew stranger.

The vines came back — impossible, salt-killed things budding green along the blackened trellises wherever Kestrel passed, as though her presence were a season unto itself. The air thickened with heat and the smell of sulfur and something sweeter underneath, like resin, like the inside of a violin. And the statues kept coming.

One at every turning. Sometimes two or three clustered together, as though the road had passed through generations of them in a single stride. A fisherman still holding his net. A child — a *child*, no older than Tam's little sister, carved with her fists clenched at her sides and her chin raised in defiance of whatever had asked this of her. An old man seated on a bench that had been part of the stone, his hands folded, his face serene, as if he'd simply fallen asleep on a warm afternoon and the mountain had honored the pose forever.

Each one fed its thread into the channels in the rock. Each channel ran upward. Two hundred and six rivers of given life, all flowing toward the caldera, all thinning, dimming, failing together as the fire they sustained guttered toward its end.

Kestrel found herself walking slower. She found herself reading the names, because the map put them in her mouth whether she wanted them or not. *Bren of the Lower Harbors. Maren Ostreth —* she flinched at that one, some other Maren, ninety years dead, but her body remembered her own Maren anyway, the woman who had caught her when she was nine years old and feverish, who had taught her to read smoke and mend a broken ward, who had wanted her blood for this mountain. *Sela Vael herself,* the first, at the very top, where the road narrowed and the heat pressed down like a hand.

"Why do you keep stopping?" Tam asked gently, somewhere past the hundredth statue.

"Because they're people." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Not symbols. People who had names and favorite foods and someone who loved them, and they walked this same road knowing exactly what waited at the top, and they did it anyway." She touched the shoulder of a statue — a young man, broad-built, his jaw set like Tam's sometimes set when he was frightened and refusing to show it. "I can feel them. Through the map. It's not memory, exactly. It's more like — an echo of an echo. But they're *there*, Tam. All of them. Still giving."

"And you think you'll join them."

It wasn't a question. She looked at him. His face was streaked with sweat and ash from the climb, his eyes red-rimmed — not from grief, she realized, but from the sulfurous air, or perhaps from holding back the thing he clearly needed to say and had been carrying since the lake, since the crossroads, maybe since Lowmark's fish sheds.

"Not join," she said. "That's the thing the map keeps trying to tell me and I keep failing to understand. Ilset didn't die here. None of them died here. They *changed*. The fire doesn't eat you. It—" she searched for the words, feeling for them the way she'd once felt for the shape of a spell in Maren's smoke. "It takes the thread and leaves the rest. You stay. You just stop being separate from it. Forever."

"Forever," Tam repeated.

"Forever," she agreed. "Warm to the touch. Glowing a little. Hearing the whole country's heart beat through your own." She tried to smile. "You'd never be cold again."

"That's not funny."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a shrine halfway up the sixth terrace ring, a circular room whose roof had half-fallen and whose walls were lined with niches, each holding a small clay lamp. Kestrel lit three of them with a touch — her fingertips sparked now, faintly, gold-red, and had been doing so for two days, and neither of them remarked on it because remarking would make it real.

The heat from the mountain kept them warm enough that they didn't need a fire, and so they sat in the lamplight with dried fish and hard bread and the vast silence of a country holding its breath around them.

Through a crack in the ruined roof, Kestrel could see the caldera's glow, far above — a smudge of dying orange against the stars, pulsing slower now. Slower every hour. Somewhere beyond the mountain, hundreds of miles away, the sea was climbing beaches it had surrendered days ago, and refugees were flooding toward Stillwater Valley, and Serah Vane was somewhere on the mountain roads ahead of or behind them with a blade and a broken faith, and Maren—

She shut the thought away. There would be time for guilt later, or there wouldn't, and either way the mountain didn't care.

"Kestrel."

Tam's voice was different. Quiet in a way she'd learned to recognize over these weeks — the quiet he used when he was about to say the true thing instead of the easy thing.

She turned. In the lamplight his face was very young and very certain, and her chest tightened with a dread that had nothing to do with dragons or fire.

"When we get to the top," he said, "and you do this thing — whatever it actually is, whatever it costs — I need to have said it out loud first. So that afterward, however long afterward lasts, I'll know I didn't leave it unsaid."

"Tam—"

"I love you."

Three words, plain as bread, plain as rain. No poetry, no hedging, no asking. He said it the way he did everything — stubbornly, with both feet planted, as if daring the world to argue.

Kestrel sat very still. The lamps flickered. Far above, the mountain breathed its slow, failing breath, and she felt it pass through her like a tide, and she understood suddenly why the wardens' statues all stood with their arms open rather than raised in farewell: because there was no leaving, and no being left. Because the ones who loved them had to stand at the bottom of this road and watch them walk up it, and the ones who walked it had to carry that love up every step like a stone in the chest, and none of it — none of it — fit inside the word *duty*.

She loved him too. She had known it since the barley field, maybe since the fish sheds, maybe since the moment a burned stranger called her *warden's daughter* and the valley's numbness cracked and she understood that she had never once in her life been safe, only postponed. She loved him with a fierceness that scared her, and she could not say it, because to say it and then climb this road would be cruelty; to say it and then refuse the road would be to spend two hundred and six lives and every coastline in the world for the warmth of one man's arms.

So she said nothing. She reached across the space between them and took his hand, and laced her fingers through his, and held on. Her skin glowed faintly where they touched, gold-red light bleeding through her knuckles, and he didn't pull away. He turned his hand and gripped hers harder.

"You don't have to answer," he said finally, his voice rough. "I'm not asking for one. I'm just — putting it down. Like a marker on a map. So you know where it is."

A laugh broke out of her, wet and helpless. "That's a terrible metaphor. You're a fisherman's son."

"My grandmother drew maps," he said. "Vaeli maps. Maybe it's inherited."

They sat like that until the lamps burned low, hands joined, saying nothing more, and the mountain hummed beneath them, and the stars turned slowly over a country that was learning, street by drowned street, to be dry again.

* * *

Near dawn, Kestrel woke to a sound that did not belong to the mountain.

Voices. Many voices, distant but descending — coming *down* the terraces from above, from the direction of the caldera, moving with the grim cadence of soldiers. She was on her feet before she was fully awake, her hand on Tam's shoulder, her glow doused and hidden, listening.

At the seventh turning, torchlight bloomed against the dark stone. White-and-copper cloaks. The sigil of the guttering flame.

And walking at their head, unhurried, her hands bound in ceremonial chains she wore like jewelry, gray-haired and upright and smiling a small, terrible, loving smile —

Maren.

"They let me come up to see you, dear one," her old master called across the dark, her voice carrying the way it always had, straight into the marrow. "We should talk before you reach the top. Before you do something we'll both regret."

Behind her, the zealots fanned out across the terrace, cutting the road both ways, and their torches threw shadows like bars across the path Kestrel had to climb.
