## 16. The Last Dragon

The mountain breathed.

Kestrel felt it before she saw it — a slow, vast rhythm in the stone beneath her boots, like the pulse of something sleeping just under the skin of the world. Each exhalation rose through the terraces as heat-shimmer, and each inhalation drew the air cold and sweet toward the caldera's rim, as if the mountain itself were hungry for what she carried.

She had left Tam at the last terrace. He had not wanted to. He had stood there among the blackened vines with his fists clenched and his grandmother's salt-ring glinting at his throat, and he had said, *I'll be right here. However long it takes. Right here.* And she had let him believe that being right here would matter, because the truth was a thing she could not make her mouth say yet: that she did not know how many of her would walk back down.

The statues had ended an hour ago. The last of them — Warden Ilsa Vael, hands open, face serene — had watched Kestrel pass with eyes that were not stone at all but old glass, worn cloudy by centuries of ash-fall. Every warden on those terraces had stood where Kestrel stood now. Every one of them had climbed this final stretch of broken road alone.

That was the part no ledger said plainly. She understood it now. The ascent was meant to be solitary, because what waited at the top was not a thing you could share.

The caldera opened before her like a cupped hand.

It was smaller than she had imagined, after everything the map had shown her — barely a mile across, its walls streaked with mineral bloom in colors that hurt to look at too long: sulfur-yellow, oxide-red, a blue so deep it seemed less a color than a wound. The floor was cracked basalt veined with light, and the light moved. It pulsed. It breathed. It gathered in the center of the caldera around a shape that Kestrel's eyes refused, at first, to resolve.

Then it moved, and she stopped trying to make sense of it and simply looked.

The dragon was dying.

There was no other way to say it, no gentler architecture for the truth. It lay coiled in the caldera's heart like a river that had forgotten how to run, and it was vast — its body could have filled the harbor of Lowmark three times over — but every scale that should have burned was dark. Not black; *dark*, the way embers are dark, holding a memory of heat rather than heat itself. Between the scales ran channels of dim orange light, thinning now, guttering, the way a candle gutters when the wax is nearly gone. Its wings were folded against its flanks, and where the wing-membranes stretched they were translucent as old parchment, shot through with veins of failing fire.

Its head rested on the basalt directly ahead of her. One eye opened.

Kestrel had faced Maren's wrath. She had faced Serah Vane across a burning field. She had faced the sea itself, in dreams, rising. None of it prepared her for that eye, because the eye was not angry or ancient or terrible. The eye was *tired*. It was tired the way her grandmother must have been tired, if she had ever had one; tired the way the librarian had been tired, waiting ninety years in the drowned dark for someone to come; tired the way a lamp is tired at the end of a long night, when everyone it was lit for has gone to bed.

"Oh," Kestrel said. It was a small sound, absurd against all that ruin. "Oh, you're — you're not what they said."

*What did they say?* The voice did not come through the air. It came up through the stone, through the soles of her feet, through the marrow of her, resonant and low and roughened by ages of disuse. *That I was a beast? A weapon? A punishment?*

"That you were fire," Kestrel said. "That you were the fire."

*I am what remains when fire has loved too long.* The great head shifted, and scales the size of doors ground against basalt with a sound like a ship coming apart on rocks. *Sit down, warden's daughter. Your legs will want to fail you soon, and I would rather you chose the moment.*

She sat. It seemed important, suddenly, to do as she was asked — not out of fear, though the fear was there, humming along her spine — but because no one had spoken to her like that in a long time. Like someone who might be allowed to choose things.

"I'm not a warden yet," she said.

*No.* The eye regarded her. In its depths, the failing light turned slowly, like a storm seen from very far above. *You are the question, walking. That is what wardens are. Everything else is answer, and answers are cheap.*

* * *

The map's ink had shown her this place once, in the early days when it still crawled across her skin at night: a mountain with a sleeping heart of fire. She had thought, then, that the heart was metaphor. She had thought a great many things were metaphor.

"Tell me what you are," she said. "All of it. The archive showed me the drowning, but it showed me from the outside. From below. I never — no one ever told me what the fire actually *is*."

The dragon exhaled. The breath washed over her, hot and dry and smelling of iron and old rain, and where it touched the basalt the veins of light brightened briefly, gratefully, then dimmed again.

*Before your country had a name,* the dragon said, *I was already old. I flew when this valley was a lake bed and the Sundered Sea was a hundred small seas that had not yet learned to reach for one another. I have outlived nine languages. I did not come to Vael. Vael was built around me.*

Kestrel's throat went tight. "Built around you."

*A young people came over the mountains, fleeing a war their grandchildren would not remember. They found a dying dragon in a caldera and did not run. Do you understand how rare that is? Everything runs from dragons. These fools sat down beside me and began to sing.* Something moved in the ruined face — not quite a smile, but the scar tissue of one. *They sang to keep me alive. Their songs became prayers. Their prayers became a craft. And when singing was not enough, a woman named Ysolt Vael discovered that a life-thread, woven willingly into my failing flame, could feed me for a generation.*

"And she gave hers."

*She gave hers.* The words came flat and heavy as stones dropped down a well. *And her daughter gave hers, and her son, and so on, down nine hundred years. Sometimes two generations between. Sometimes only one. There was a warden named Corin who lasted forty years because he was stubborn and mean-tempered and refused, on principle, to be consumed efficiently. I liked him best.*

Despite everything — despite the terror coiled in her stomach and the image of Tam waiting on the terrace below — Kestrel laughed. It came out ragged, half a sob, but it was real.

"What happens," she asked, "when no one gives theirs?"

The dragon was silent for a long time. The light in its veins thinned. Somewhere far below, deep in the mountain's roots, Kestrel heard the sea — heard it pressing, patient, against the gates that this dying furnace held sealed.

*Then I die,* the dragon said at last. *And when I die, the gates die. And when the gates die, the Sundered Sea remembers what it was always reaching for.* Its eye closed, then opened again, as if even blinking had become expensive. *Your drowned archive. Your drowned streets. All of it drowns twice — first Vael, then everything the wardens bought with their lives. The Stillwater Valley. Lowmark. Every coast you sailed past to get here. The sea is not cruel, child. It is only large, and it does not know how to stop.*

* * *

"So it's simple," Kestrel said, when she could speak. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, small and scraped. "Either I give you my life-thread, or the world drowns."

*Simple,* the dragon agreed. *Yes. That is exactly the lie every warden was fed, and I am tired of feeding it.*

Kestrel looked up sharply.

*Listen to me.* The great head lifted — an effort that made the whole caldera groan, made veins of light flare and sputter — and the dragon's eye held hers with a ferocity that had nothing of kindness in it, and all of honesty. *Ysolt Vael did not die. Do you understand? She did not die. She is still here. She is in the terraces outside — that statue with the open hands — and she is also here, in the weave of my flame, and she has been burning inside me for nine hundred years without end. Every song they sang to keep me alive, I heard. Every prayer. Every warden's last thought, every warden's last love, every name they called out in the dark of their own dissolving — I carry them all. I am not a beast, Kestrel Sterling. I am a reliquary. And I am full.*

The caldera seemed suddenly very small, and very loud, though nothing moved.

"You're telling me not to do it," Kestrel said slowly.

*I am telling you that no one can tell you not to do it, and that is the cruelest thing about it.* The light guttered, steadied. *I will not refuse you. If you give me your thread, I will take it, because the alternative is the drowning of the world, and I have grown too old and too fond of small singing creatures to permit that. But I will not pretend it is a gift. You will not die. Dying would be mercy. You will become fuel that remembers it was once a person. You will feel the sea press against the gates for centuries. You will hear every song. And you will not be able to leave.*

Kestrel wrapped her arms around her knees. Below her, distantly, she could feel the mountain breathing, could feel the whole wounded architecture of Vael holding itself together on the strength of this one exhausted heart.

"There's a boy on the terrace," she said. "Tam. He loves me. He doesn't know I know." Her voice cracked. "And there was a woman named Serah who died on these slopes so I could climb them, and she never got to doubt anything again. And my master Maren traded herself to the Ember Court to buy me this road, and I don't even know if she's alive. Everyone paid to get me here. Doesn't that count for something?"

*It counts,* the dragon said gently. *It counts enormously. It is also not yours to spend. Their gifts purchased your arrival. What happens next is yours alone, which means it is the first truly free choice you have ever made, and free choices are the most expensive things in any world.*

The eye closed. When it opened again, the storm-light in it had gone very quiet.

*So I will ask you the question, warden's daughter, and I will ask it the way it deserves to be asked — not as a demand, not as a debt, but as the only honest question there is:*

The mountain inhaled. The whole caldera leaned in. Far below, the sea pressed, and waited, and pressed.

***What will you pay?***

* * *

Kestrel did not answer immediately. She made herself sit in the silence, because she had spent her whole life running from silences — from Maren's meaningful pauses, from the valley's numbed hush, from the space where an answer should be. This one she would stay inside until it told her the truth.

She thought about the valley that forgot. About how safety had felt like erasure, how mending nets in Lowmark had felt like being slowly unpicked stitch by stitch until nothing of her remained. She had called that cowardice. She had called it wisdom. It had been neither. It had been the refusal to choose, dressed up in either name depending on the hour.

She thought about Maren, saying *let me carry this burden for you* with hunger wearing love's face. About how Kestrel had finally understood, on this long road, that Maren had never wanted to spare her — Maren had wanted to *be* her, to own the choosing without paying the price. And how Kestrel had walked away from the comfortable cage anyway, and how much that had cost, and how glad she was, in some unbreakable place beneath the fear, that she had.

She thought about the coral librarian, ninety years patient in the flooded dark, keeping books that breathed. *The dead country is not dead,* the librarian had said. *It is holding its breath.* Holding its breath for her, in the end. For this hour. For this question.

And she thought about Tam, waiting on the terrace. *Right here. However long it takes.* He had followed her against her wishes and stayed against his own good sense, and his grandmother had been Vaeli, and his blood had known this mountain before his mind did. If she wove herself into the fire, she would feel the sea press for centuries. But she might also feel him — standing at her threshold in whatever years he had, singing, if he chose, the way the first foolish refugees had sung. Fuel that remembered it was once a person. Or perhaps: a person who remembered, forever, that she had been loved.

The dragon watched her think. It did not hurry her. It had waited nine hundred years for wardens; it could wait out one girl's silence.

"My master used to say," Kestrel said at last, "that hedge-witchery is just paying attention until the world pays attention back." Her voice was steady now. Steadier than it had any right to be. "Everyone keeps asking what I'll pay. The Court wanted the sea to have everything. The valley wanted it to have nothing. Maren wanted me to pay so she wouldn't have to. And the whole time—" She laughed, wet and unsteady. "The whole time, the only person who never asked me to pay anything was the boy on the stairs. So maybe that's the answer. Maybe the payment isn't the giving up. Maybe it's the *choosing*, done with your eyes open, when running away was right there the whole time."

She stood up. Her legs shook. She let them.

"I'll pay attention," she said to the dying dragon, older than the country, full of every warden's last song. "For as long as I last. I won't pretend it isn't terrible. I won't call it a gift. But I'll pay it — willingly, the way Ysolt did, the way Corin did, mean-tempered and stubborn if I have to be. And in exchange—"

*In exchange?* Something flickered in the ruined face. Hope, maybe, or the memory of hope, worn smooth as sea glass.

"In exchange, you rest," Kestrel said. "When my thread is woven and the fire stands on its own — my fire, fed by my choosing — you sleep. Truly sleep. Nine hundred years of carrying everyone's last thoughts is enough. Whatever wardens come after me, they tend the flame. They don't feed it *you*."

The caldera was silent for the space of three heartbeats. Then the dragon laughed — a sound like the first crack of spring ice on a frozen lake, vast and grating and, underneath it all, unmistakably glad.

*Nine hundred years,* it rumbled, *and the little warden negotiates.* The great head lowered until its brow nearly touched the basalt before her, until she could see, deep in the failing veins, the tiny bright shapes of every soul that had ever kept this fire alive — and room, she realized, with a lurch that was half horror and half homecoming. Room. There was room in the weave. *Come here, then, Kestrel Sterling. Come and be the door. But first—*

The dragon's eye fixed on something beyond her shoulder, at the caldera's rim, and its voice went flat and hard as slate.

*First, decide quickly. Because the ones your Serah died stopping have found another road up — and they are not alone. Someone is leading them who knows your name, and knows your thread, and has been walking behind you since the day you were born.*

Kestrel turned.

At the caldera's rim, torches were kindling in the ash-dark — one, five, twenty — and descending.
