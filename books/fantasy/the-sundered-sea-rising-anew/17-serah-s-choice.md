## 17. Serah's Choice

The mountain had been burning for three days, and Serah Vane had stopped praying.

Not because her faith had failed — that would have been a mercy, a clean severing she could name and grieve. No, the prayers simply would not form. Every time she knelt inside herself and reached for the old liturgies of the Ember Court — *all fire must gutter, all flame must fade, so the sea may be whole* — the words came back to her changed, transposed into a minor key she did not recognize, until they sounded less like doctrine and more like an apology she had no right to make.

She climbed behind the remnant instead, and counted her sins like beads.

There were eleven of them left. Eleven white-cloaked figures ascending Vael's terraced slopes through ash that fell soft as grey snow, past orchards turned to charcoal sculpture, past statues that rose out of the burned terraces at intervals like sentinels frozen mid-stride. Serah had studied the statues longer than was wise. They were wardens — every one of them, though the Court's histories never used the word. Women and men carved in the act of reaching toward the summit, their faces worn smooth by centuries of wind and heat, and each one wore the same expression: not terror, not regret. *Readiness.* As if they had walked up this mountain on purpose, knowing what waited at the top, knowing what it would cost, and had chosen it with open eyes.

Inquisitor Dain Callow led them now, since Inquisitor Halvard's lungs had filled with ash two days ago and he had died coughing red. Dain was young for his rank, handsome in the way a drawn blade is handsome, and he had recited the Litany of Extinguishment every night around their small smokeless fires in a voice like poured honey. *The sea remembers what the land forgets. The sea forgives what the land denies. We are the mercy of the deep.*

Serah had once believed every word.

She thought about the archive beneath the lake — the drowned stairway, the breathing books, the coral librarian who had waited ninety years in the dark for someone to come down and *ask*. She thought about the sealed orders she had found in Halvard's satchel after his death, the ones bearing the Ember Court's own seal, dated ninety-one years past. *Proceed with the sinking of Vael. The warden line must be severed at its root. The fire, unattended, will gutter within a century; when it does, the sea will take the rest, and the Court will stand blameless before a world that never knew there was anything to blame.*

She had burned those orders on the shore of the lake, page by page, watching the paper curl and blacken, and she had felt nothing like peace. She had felt like a woman standing in a house whose foundations she had just discovered were made of bones.

Now she climbed, and said nothing, and kept her hand near her sword.

* * *

They made camp on the ninth terrace as the light failed, in the lee of a warden statue whose face had eroded entirely blank. Dain called the remnant together and unrolled a map of the caldera — stolen, Serah knew, from the same archive they had tried to burn in Lowmark, because hypocrisy was the one doctrine the Court practiced flawlessly.

"The door-warden girl is already above us," Dain said. His voice carried the gentle certainty of a man reading scripture. "With the boy, and possibly the hedge-witch. It doesn't matter. What matters is that she does not reach the heart before we reach *her*. Brothers. Sisters. Ninety years ago our founders gave everything so that this fire could die on its own schedule. Tomorrow we finish their work. Tomorrow we let the sea come home."

Eleven voices murmured assent. Ten, really — Serah's lips stayed closed, and she watched Dain's eyes flick to hers and away, cataloguing, patient. He knew. He had known since the lake. He was waiting to see which way she would break, and Serah understood, with the terrible clarity that had followed her since she burned those orders, that he intended to be holding the blade either way.

She took first watch. She always volunteered for first watch now; sleep had become a country she could not enter.

The stars over Mount Vael were wrong. She had noticed it the first night and told herself it was ash haze. But the sky here seemed *thinner*, as if something vast beneath the rock breathed heat up through the stone and stretched the heavens over it like skin over a fever. To the west, far off across the drowned lowlands, the horizon glowed a color she had no name for — not sunset, not fire, but the sick amber of a lamp running out of oil. The dragon-fire, guttering. The sea, rising along coasts she would never see, taking towns she would never learn the names of.

*All fire must gutter,* the litany whispered in her memory, sweet as rot.

A pebble clattered on the terrace below. Serah's hand went to her sword before her mind caught up — and then relaxed, fractionally, when the shape resolved into the hedge-witch, Maren, climbing alone out of the dark with her grey braid singed at the ends and her hands raised in surrender long before Serah demanded it.

"You," Serah said quietly.

"Me." Maren lowered herself onto a rock, unhurried, as if they were two neighbors meeting at a well. Up close she looked older than the mountainside, and tired in a way that went down to the marrow. "You're the one who turned her cloak at the lake. I heard about it from a fishwife in Lowmark who heard it from a shepherd. Small valley. Everything travels."

"I haven't turned anything."

"No?" Maren smiled without warmth. "Then why aren't you waking your brothers-in-faith? I'm an enemy combatant sitting ten feet from eleven sleeping zealots, and you're chatting."

Serah's sword cleared its scabbard an inch. "Give me one reason."

"Because in about six hours, Dain Callow is going to drive a quench-iron into the heart of that mountain, and everything from here to the Sundered Sea is going to drown, including the village where my apprentice is mending nets no more, and the only person standing between him and the caldera besides me is you." Maren's voice didn't rise at all. That was the frightening thing about her; Serah had interrogated enough fanatics to know that volume meant nothing, but stillness like this meant everything. "So. One reason. Take it or leave it."

"Why?" The question tore out of Serah before she could dress it in inquisitor's composure. "Why would you warn me? You ran from the Court. Your kind despises us."

"My kind." Maren laughed softly, and there was real grief in it. "Girl, I spent thirty years teaching a child to hide her bloodline so that people like you wouldn't burn her for it. I know exactly what your kind does. I also know that Dain doesn't intend for you to leave this mountain. He told Halvard as much in Lowmark, back when Halvard was alive to hear it. A witness who's seen the founding orders is a loose thread. He's been waiting for you to pull yourself."

The wind moved through the burned terraces, carrying the smell of hot stone and something older underneath — sulfur and salt and, impossibly faintly, roses. Serah looked east, toward the summit hidden in the dark, where a girl she had hunted across half the Sundered Sea was walking toward a death that was not a death, and she looked west, at the drowning horizon, and she found that the scales had already tipped, had perhaps tipped long ago, and she had simply refused to look at the pan that had sunk.

"What do I have to do?" she asked.

Maren's smile faded into something almost tender. "Nothing yet. Just climb faster than grief does."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of a wound.

They reached the caldera rim as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and Serah saw the heart of Vael for the first time and forgot, for one full breath, every catechism she had ever memorized.

It was not a crater. It was a *throne room*. The caldera floor spread below them like a bowl of hammered bronze, and at its center rose the dragon.

She had expected a beast. The Court's oldest texts spoke of the fire-source as a worm, a devil, a wound in the world that righteous hands must finally close. But the thing lying in the caldera was ancient beyond malice — vast and dim, its scales gone the grey of banked coals, its ribs rising and falling with the slow tectonic patience of a mountain deciding to breathe. Its eye opened as they came over the rim. One eye, the size of a chapel window, and in it Serah saw not hatred but a weariness so profound it looked like love, the exhaustion of a creature that had held back an ocean for ninety years on borrowed lives and knew exactly how many it had left.

And beside it — small, absurd, blazing — stood Kestrel Sterling.

She was changed already. Even at this distance Serah could see it: a faint luminescence under her skin, as if the map's ink had finished its migration from parchment to blood and was now writing itself in light. Tam stood behind her, gripping a stave of charred oak, positioned between her and the rim with the hopeless courage of a boy who has decided where he stands and will not be moved.

Kestrel saw them. Saw the white cloaks. And Serah watched her face do the arithmetic — eleven against three, the end of every road arriving at once — and watched her set her feet anyway.

"Wardens of the door!" Dain's voice rang off the caldera walls, gorgeous and hollow. "Daughter of the line that drowned a nation! In the name of the deep, we come to close what your family opened!"

"That family didn't drown anyone," Kestrel called back, and her voice cracked on the words but held. "Your founders did. I've read the orders. I've stood in your archive's ashes. Ask her—" and her arm swung, and every white-cloaked face turned to Serah.

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Serah counted them.

"She speaks the truth," Serah said.

It came out steady. She was almost proud of that. She stepped forward, out of the line of the remnant, and drew her sword — not at the girl, but turning, slowly, until its point faced the men and women she had marched beside for nine years. "I burned the founding orders myself. Halvard carried them. The Court sank Vael. There was no righteous extinguishing. There was murder, and there was a lie, and we are the lie's last keepers."

"Serah." Dain's voice stayed gentle, which was worse than shouting. "Sister. Whatever poison the witch fed you—"

"Nothing she fed me. I read it in your own seal."

He sighed, and it was the sigh of a man setting down something heavy, and Serah knew — she had always known, since the lake, since the first night she'd failed to pray — that this was how it would end. "Brothers," Dain said softly. "Sisters. The sister Vane has fallen. The deep requires her."

Ten blades came free of ten scabbards, and the morning filled with the sound.

What happened next, Serah would remember afterward — in whatever fragment of afterward was granted her — as a series of instants separated by eternities.

Dain moving first, fast, beautiful, his blade arcing for her throat with the economy of a man who had trained beside her for a decade and knew every habit she owned. Her parry coming not from skill but from the single truth she had left: *he expects me to fight like the woman I was yesterday.* She let his blade slide, turned inside his reach, and drove her shoulder into his chest, and they went down the inner slope of the caldera together, tumbling through ash, and somewhere in the fall her sword found the soft place under the arm of Inquisitor Breck and Breck screamed and the scream was the first crack in the remnant's perfect formation.

That was all she could give them. Not victory — she had known since Maren's whisper that there would be no victory, only *time*. So she bought time the way the poor buy bread: piece by piece, at ruinous cost.

She got between the remnant and the caldera floor, and she made them come through her.

Breck, wounded, hung back. Two others faltered — she saw doubt move across their faces like weather, and she shouted at them while she fought, shouted the numbers, the dates, the names from Halvard's orders, shouted *your founders murdered a country*, and one of them — young Ferren, who had wept at his mother's funeral and shared his rations with strays — actually stopped, actually lowered his blade, and Dain cut him down for it without breaking stride, and Serah learned in that instant that she had never truly known the man she'd followed.

After that there was no more talking. After that there was only steel and ash and the enormous slow breathing of the dying dragon filling the world, and Serah Vane fighting with her back to a stranger she had hunted, buying seconds at the price of her own blood.

They marked her early — a slash across the thigh that turned her leg treacherous, a puncture below the collarbone that made every breath a negotiation. She stopped counting wounds after the fourth. She planted herself on the narrow path down to the caldera floor, the only approach wide enough for three abreast, and she made the path a ledger: each stroke parried, each second paid, *this one for the librarian in the coral dark, this one for the drowned streets, this one for Ferren falling, this one for the prayer I cannot say.*

"Fall aside, Serah." Dain again, bleeding now too, from the gash she'd opened along his jaw. His beauty had curdled; he looked like what he was. "The sea takes us all eventually. Why bleed for a stranger?"

"Because she's not a stranger." Blood filled Serah's mouth; she spat it pink into the ash. "Because inheritance isn't blood, Dain. I learned that too late. It's what you *carry* for people. And I'm carrying this."

Kestrel's voice came from behind her, raw and horrified: "Move! Please — you don't have to—"

"Yes," Serah said, and did not turn around, because if she saw the girl's face she might lose the cold bright thing that was keeping her upright. "I do. This is the only offering the Court ever taught me that was worth making."

She heard, rather than saw, what happened behind her — the sound of Kestrel turning away, turning *toward* the dragon, footsteps quickening on bronze. Good. Good girl. Climb.

Dain came at her one last time, and Serah met him with everything remaining, and it was nearly enough. Her point opened his sword-arm to the bone; his opened her side, deep, deep, a gate swinging wide. They broke apart, both swaying, both spent — and then the other four were moving past her, around her, because she could no longer cover the whole path, and she understood with perfect calm that the arithmetic had run out.

So she spent the final coin.

She caught Dain's cloak as he passed. He turned, startled — he had expected her dead already — and she pulled him close, forehead to forehead, the intimacy of the last moment, and drove her dagger up beneath his sternum with the strength of nine years of faith redirected entire.

He looked at her, genuinely astonished, and said, "Sister," and fell.

The others hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and that hesitation was the gift. Above them, at the caldera's heart, the dragon's eye flared like a sunrise remembered, and Kestrel Sterling stepped into the fire.

Serah slid down against the warm stone. The remnant scattered — broken, leaderless, two fleeing downslope and two simply standing in the ash, staring at the light pouring out of the mountain's crown like something being born rather than dying. She found she was smiling. Her side was very warm, warmer than the stone, and the warmth was spreading, and it did not feel like losing.

Footsteps, quick and desperate. Tam's face above her, the boy kneeling in the ash, pressing his hands uselessly against the wound as if a boy's hands could argue with a blade. And behind him, haloed in new fire, Kestrel — transformed, glowing, *alive* in some way that made the air itself lean toward her — dropping to her knees in the ash, saying her name, saying *stay, stay, stay*.

"There's no staying," Serah managed. It was getting hard to make the words go in order. "But listen. Listen. I carried the lie for nine years. Someone has to carry the truth the same length. Tell them. Tell everyone. Write it down — write it where it can't burn."

"We'll write it," Kestrel said, tears cutting clean lines through the ash on her face. Her hand, when it closed over Serah's, was warm — warm as the mountain, warm as the fire, and Serah thought: *oh, so this is what we were trying to put out.* "I swear it. On the archive. On the ledger. On everything."

"Ledger," Serah whispered. The light was everywhere now, or maybe that was just her eyes. "Good. Put... put my name in it. Not as a warden. Just... as a witness. Someone should..."

The mountain hummed beneath her, vast and patient and newly fed, and the sea, miles away, began — though no one living saw it yet — to draw back from the drowning shores.

Serah Vane closed her eyes on the sight of the fire rising, and the last thing she felt was not the cold she had been promised all her life, but warmth — passing through her like a tide going home, like an inheritance arriving late, like a hand letting go.

* * *

Far below, in the drained channels of the lower slopes, water that had crept inland for ninety years paused, shivered, and turned.
