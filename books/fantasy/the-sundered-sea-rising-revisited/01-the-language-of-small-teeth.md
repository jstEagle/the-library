## 1. The Language of Small Teeth

The rats came at dusk, as rats always did, and Cyrus Oyelaran was waiting for them on the third landing of the Widow's Stair, a bottle of sour wine open beside him and his boots dangling over the drop into the smuggler's cut below.

There were eleven of them, though he could only see six in the failing light. The others waited in the shadow of the stair's broken balustrade, whiskers twitching, holding their distance the way their kind always held distance from men. But these were not here to be afraid of him. These were here to do business.

"You're late," he said.

*Not late,* said the eldest, a scarred female with a torn left ear who had been running the Thornfell warrens since before Cyrus had learned to walk upright. *The long way. There is a thing in the tunnel under the granary steps. A snake. Fat as your arm.*

"There is no snake under the granary steps."

*There is now.*

Cyrus sighed and took a pull of the wine. It was bad wine, the kind he could afford, the kind that tasted like vinegar and regret, and he drank it mostly because holding a bottle made a man look like he was doing nothing, which was the safest thing a man in Thornfell could look like. "Fine. A snake under the granary steps. I'll tell Marta to move her flour stores. What else?"

The old rat sat back on her haunches and groomed one paw with the deliberation of a merchant preparing to name a price. Cyrus had known her—known *of* her—for twenty years, and in all that time she had never given him a name. Rats did not use names among themselves; they used smells and histories and the memory of bloodlines, things that could not be spoken in any language made of air. He thought of her as Grandmother, privately, because she was old and sharp and because she treated him with exactly the mixture of contempt and affection that a grandmother would.

*Rockslide,* she said at last. *On the Low Road, past the Split Stone. Two days ago, in the rain. The mountain shed its skin. Big rocks. Many rocks. Your mules will die there, and the men who drive them, if they go the usual way.*

Cyrus set down the bottle.

This was the part of his life no one knew about—not Sera, not the harbormaster whose bribes he arranged, not the mule-drivers who trusted him with their cargo and their necks. This was the part that existed only in the spaces between words, in the whisper-language of teeth and tails that no man had ever heard and no man must ever hear.

He spoke to beasts. That was the whole of it, the whole impossible secret of his twenty-six years. Not commanded them—that was a thing out of fireside stories, the Gift of kings and monsters, the gift that got villages burned. He *spoke*, and sometimes, when the beast saw reason in what he said, it listened. There was no power in it. Only patience, and attention, and the willingness to sit on cold stairs at dusk trading information with creatures the world considered beneath notice.

Which was, Cyrus had long ago decided, precisely why it was safe. No hunter would ever burn a man for talking to rats. You'd have to believe him first.

"How big a slide?" he asked.

*Big as this stair,* Grandmother said. *Bigger. The road is gone. Buried under the shoulder of the hill. My people went through it—the space between the rocks—and came out the far side. There is water moving under the new stones. The hill is still settling. It will settle more.*

"When?"

*When it wants to. The hill does not ask us.*

He almost smiled at that. The hill does not ask us. Twenty years of conversations with animals had taught him that most of what they said was some version of that sentence: the world does not ask us, we endure it, we go around, we survive in the cracks. It was a philosophy that suited Thornfell perfectly.

"Show me," he said. "Tomorrow. First light. Show me where the road ends and how far around the slide goes."

*We will show you,* Grandmother said. *And you will pay.*

"I always pay."

*You pay in grain. Good grain, not the grey stuff from the harbor barrels. And you will tell the mule-men to camp well away from our nurseries this season, as you promised last season and did not do.*

"That wasn't me, that was Doss's crew—"

*Men all smell the same.* She licked her paw again, ending the negotiation. *Grain. Distance. And the small ones say there is cheese in the caravan coming up from the coast, hard yellow cheese in wax, and they want you to know they know about it.*

"They can't know about it, it hasn't even—" He stopped. Of course they could know about it. The rats had probably ridden half the way from the coast inside the wagon itself, listening to everything, tasting the air through the crates. "One wheel of cheese. One. Tell them if I find gnaw-marks on anything else, the deal's off and I'll salt the warren entrances myself."

*You would not,* Grandmother said, with the serene confidence of someone who has watched a man fail to keep a threat for two decades. *But yes. One wheel. We are agreed.*

She turned and flowed down into the dark, and the other rats followed her like spilled ink running backward, and Cyrus sat alone on the Widow's Stair with his terrible wine and felt the particular satisfaction of a job done before anyone knew there was a job to do.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole architecture of his life: every disaster rerouted before it arrived, every danger dissolved into whispers, so thoroughly and so quietly that no one—including, most days, himself—could see the shape of what he actually was. To Thornfell he was Cyrus Oyelaran, fixer, route-man, the quiet fellow who always seemed to know which passes were washed out and which toll-collectors had been bought and which warehouses had roof leaks over the silk. Lucky, people called him. Careful. A man who listened well.

They were right about the last part. They just didn't understand what he listened *to*.

*

He found the mule-train camped in the yard of the Broken Cistern, an old water-shrine gone to ruin that the smugglers used as a waystation. Six wagons, forty mules, and nine men who looked at him with the flat deference that Thornfell showed to anyone who could keep them alive and didn't ask why he could.

Doss was there, chewing a straw, and he spat when he saw Cyrus coming. "Route-man. We leave at dawn. Road's clear, you said."

"The Low Road's dead," Cyrus said. "Slide past the Split Stone. Whole hillside came down in the rain."

Doss stared at him. Behind him, two of the drivers exchanged glances. "I rode that road three weeks ago. Solid as my mother's—"

"It's dead now. Rain took it." Cyrus kept his voice bored, kept his eyes somewhere over Doss's shoulder, because the worst thing he could do was seem too certain, too invested. Certainty without visible cause made men curious, and curiosity was the one animal in Thornfell that could not be bargained with. "Take the Shepherd's Cut instead. Adds half a day. Adds a toll at the narrows—I've already paid it, the marker's on the third cairn. Go around or go through and lose your wagons and probably your lives, I don't much care which."

"And how'd you come by this, then? Slide happens two days back and you already know?"

"A bird told me," Cyrus said, and let it hang there just long enough to be a joke, and Doss laughed because it was a joke, because everyone knew Cyrus talked in riddles, because nobody in the history of the world had ever looked at a lean, unremarkable smuggler leaning against a wall and thought: *there is a man having a conversation with the rats in that wall right now, and the rats are complaining about the cheese.*

"Shepherd's Cut," Doss said finally. "Half a day. Fine. But if the Low Road's open when we come back through—"

"It won't be."

It wouldn't be. By the time Doss's train came back, the hill would have settled twice more, and the Low Road would be a memory and a warning. The rats would guide Cyrus's people along paths no scout could find, around dangers no scout could see, and the caravans would arrive, and Thornfell would say *lucky Cyrus* again, and the secret would stay sewn shut inside him like a coin in a coat lining—heavy, valuable, and never once shown to the light.

He walked the mule-lines while the drivers made ready, checking hooves and harness out of habit, and a dun mule with a lazy eye turned her head and blew warm air into his palm.

*Tall one,* she said. Mules always sounded vaguely aggrieved, as if the world were a service that had failed them personally. *Tall one, the grey stallion bit me again.*

"I'll have them separate you tonight."

*You never separate us. You say words and the words do nothing.*

"Words are my whole trade," Cyrus told her. "Tonight. I promise."

She snorted, unconvinced, but she leaned her forehead against his chest anyway, briefly, the way mules did when they had decided a man was theirs, and Cyrus stood in the smell of hay and dung and leather and felt the familiar ache that he had no better name for than *Tuesday*: the ache of being loved by creatures who could never tell anyone they loved him, in a life where no human being knew him at all.

Comfortable loneliness, Sera had once called it, laughing at him across her kitchen table. *You've built yourself a lovely little cage, Cy, and you polish the bars every morning.* She hadn't meant it unkindly. She had meant it the way friends mean true things—as a tease, as nothing.

He thought about it now, watching the mules stamp in the cold, and decided, as he always decided, that a polished cage was still a fine place to live if you were the only one who could open it.

*

The rats met him at first light at the mouth of the Shepherd's Cut, as agreed, and led him up along the ridge on paths that were barely suggestions—goat-ways, drainage runs, the secret grammar of the mountain itself. Grandmother rode on his shoulder for the steepest stretch, which she pretended was a great concession and which both of them knew was simply faster than making her climb.

Below them, the new slide was visible from a mile off: a raw brown wound across the face of the hill, boulders tumbled like knucklebones, the Low Road vanished entirely beneath it. Water seeped and ticked somewhere underneath. Even from here Cyrus could feel the mountain deciding what to do next.

*Your mules would have died there,* Grandmother said, satisfied. *The tall soft ones, screaming, and the wheels breaking, and the men under the stones. It would have been loud.*

"Loud's the one thing I can't afford."

*Loud,* she repeated, in the tone of a creature examining a strange object. *You live in loudness. Men are loud. Their fires are loud. Their dogs bark all night at nothing.*

"Their dogs bark at me," Cyrus said. "Mostly."

*Because you smell of us,* she said simply. *All of us. Fox and crow and rat and mule. The dogs cannot name it, so they bark. Someday a dog will bark at you that can name it, tall one. Then you will be loud whether you wish it or not.*

He laughed, because there was nothing else to do with a sentence like that, and scratched her behind the torn ear until she bit him gently, which was rat for *enough*.

They spent the morning marking the detour—cairns adjusted, a ribbon tied where the trail forked, a word dropped to the goats who grazed the Cut so they'd keep off the false path and not confuse the mules. Small work. Invisible work. By noon it was done, and the caravan would pass safe, and no one would ever know that the mountain itself had been negotiated with on their behalf by a man carrying a sack of grain and one wheel of hard yellow cheese in wax.

At the top of the ridge, Cyrus sat and ate bread and shared crumbs with sparrows, who gossiped at him about a cat on the far side of town that had learned to open latch-doors, and he laughed at that too, alone, his voice strange to himself after a night of silence.

That was the rule, his first rule, older than all the others, carved into him at seven years old when his mother pressed her hand over his mouth and whispered *never where men can hear, never, never, they burned the Vell family for less:* 

**Speak only to the beasts. Never where men can hear. Never where anyone can see.**

For nineteen years he had kept it. For nineteen years the gift had stayed folded up small inside him, a hidden wing, a coin in the lining, and his life had worked the way a lock works—quietly, precisely, opening only for those with the key.

He should have known. Later—much later—he would lie awake in far places and try to remember the exact day the pattern broke, and he would decide it was this one: an ordinary gray morning, a sack of grain, a wheel of cheese, a rat on his shoulder telling him that someday a dog would bark that could name him.

Because when he came down off the ridge toward Thornfell, dust rising off the low road, the first thing he saw was a crowd gathered tight around the tollgate, silent in the wrong way, the way crowds are silent when something is being nailed up.

And the second thing he saw was the royal seal, red wax, glinting on a fresh-posted proclamation.

And the third thing he saw, at the edge of the crowd, was Sera—his oldest friend, his fence, his keeper of half his secrets—standing very still, looking at the paper, with her face gone white as flour.
