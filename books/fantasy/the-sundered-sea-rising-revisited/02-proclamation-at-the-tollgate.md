## 2. Proclamation at the Tollgate

The morning the decree came to Thornfell, Cyrus Oyelaran was three miles up-slope, negotiating with a family of pine martens over the price of safe passage through their territory.

*Two hens,* the marten matriarch insisted, her whiskers bristling with the particular indignation of a creature who has been lowballed before. *Two hens and the left boot.*

*One hen,* Cyrus said, keeping his voice below the threshold of human hearing, a skill so practiced it had become as natural as breathing. *And I'll leave the boot where your kits can play with it. You know they only want the laces anyway.*

The matriarch considered this with the gravity of a merchant queen. Her kits tumbled in the bracken behind her, all teeth and tail and appetite for destruction. *The laces are the best part,* she admitted, which was as close to agreement as martens ever came.

Cyrus smiled—small, private, the smile of a man who had spent twenty-six years perfecting the art of being unremarkable—and moved on up the trail, leaving a hen-feather token at the boundary stone as was proper. The martens would hold the pass for three days. The caravan coming down from the high villages would reroute through the old drover's path, around the rockslide that had closed the main road, and no one on either end of the transaction would ever know why.

This was his economy. This was his life, built grain by grain across two decades: routes negotiated with badgers, warehouses guarded by rat-councils who took payment in grain and secrets, messages carried by pigeons who asked nothing but safety for their nests. He never spoke where men could hear. He never let anyone watch him listen too long. He kept his gift folded small inside him like a knife worn against the ribs, sharp and hidden, and in exchange he had carved out something rare in Thornfell—a life that worked, that belonged to no lord and no ledger, that was perfectly, invisibly his own.

He was thinking about this, feeling the particular quiet pride he allowed himself perhaps twice a year, when he heard the bell.

* * *

The tollgate bell rang only for proclamations. It hung from an iron bracket above the customs house, green with age, and it had rung perhaps six times in Cyrus's living memory: twice for royal deaths, once for a coronation, once for a tax levy that had emptied half the valley's purses, and twice for things the older smugglers still wouldn't talk about.

He came down the mountain road at a walk, because running drew eyes. That was the first rule, older than any other: *never hurry, never linger.* A man who moved at the speed of ordinary life was furniture. A man who hurried was a story someone might tell later.

Tollgate Hollow clustered around the crossing where the mountain road met the river—the customs house, a shrine with a peeling saint, Denna Marsh's bakery, the stables where Cyrus kept a rented stall under another man's name, and the tollgate itself, a timber arch wide enough for four carts, its beam scarred by decades of cargo and weather. By the time Cyrus arrived, perhaps sixty people had gathered in the muddy square, breath steaming in the early cold. Farmers with market baskets. Two caravans held up mid-crossing, drivers leaning on their yokes. Children perched on the fence rails, sensing holiday.

At the center stood the crier.

He was not what Cyrus expected, though Cyrus couldn't have said what he'd expected. Not a fat town-bellower in livery, but a lean man in travel-gray with the crown's stag worked small on his collar, the way soldiers wear insignia they've stopped being proud of. Beside him stood two others: a clerk with a portable writing desk strapped to his back like a snail's shell, and a third figure who did not stand near the notice board at all, but apart, hands clasped behind him, watching the crowd the way a fisherman watches water.

That one worried Cyrus before the man spoke a single word. Hunters moved like that. Not looking at faces—looking at *hands*, at throats, at the places where people touched each other without thinking. Looking for tenderness.

The clerk unfurled a scroll. The crier lifted his voice—not shouting; it was worse than shouting, it was pleasant, measured, the tone of a man reading scripture.

"By decree of Aldric, fourth of that name, King of the Sundered Throne and Warden of the Marches—" the title was new, Cyrus noted distantly. *Warden of the Marches*. Kings didn't rename themselves over nothing. "—let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of the Old Speech, the communing of men with beasts, hereafter called the Gift, is declared contrary to the peace and dignity of the crown."

The words went into the crowd like cold water into milk. Cyrus heard the change in breathing, sixty chests going shallow at once.

"From this day, the harboring of Gifted persons is a felony against the crown. The possession or training of animals in the Old Speech is evidence of sedition. All persons having knowledge of such practices are bound by law to report them." The crier paused, and here he did something Cyrus would remember for the rest of his life, though he didn't know it yet. He looked up from the scroll, directly at the crowd, and softened. "Neighbors," he said gently. "You have nothing to fear if you have nothing hidden. The crown asks only honesty."

Beside him, the watching man smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes.

The clerk nailed the decree to the notice board with four strokes of a hammer, each one precise as a coffin nail. The crowd did not disperse. Crowds don't, after executions and proclamations—they stand there absorbing the shape of the new world, testing whether it will hold their weight.

It was then that the goat-dog barked.

* * *

Everyone in Tollgate Hollow knew Brindle. He belonged—or rather, attached himself—to Widow Marsh at the bakery, a shaggy gray brute of indeterminate breeding, part goat-herd dog, part something larger and more patient, who had appointed himself guardian of the hollow's children for as long as anyone could remember. He was old now, gray-muzzled, slow to rise. Cyrus had spoken with him perhaps two hundred times over the years, usually about the quality of the scraps behind Denna's ovens, occasionally about the weather, once, memorably, about grief, after the widow lost her son to the fever winter.

Brindle pushed through the crowd's legs toward the notice board, drawn—as dogs always were—by the smell of the hammer-man, the clerk, the stranger. He sniffed at the hunter's boots. And then, in the innocent way of old dogs everywhere, he sat down beside the man's leg, leaned his weight against it companionably, and looked up at the crowd with his tongue out, as if to say, *look, I've made a friend.*

The hunter looked down at him.

What passed between them in the next three seconds, Cyrus could not hear—Brindle made no sound, made no sign, offered nothing but ordinary dog-warmth. But the hunter's head tilted. His nostrils flared slightly, the way Cyrus's own did when he listened past hearing. And then the hunter said, quietly, to the crier:

"This one."

The word dropped into the square like a stone into a well.

"He's just a dog," said Denna Marsh, pushing forward, flour still on her forearms. She was sixty and built like the door of her own oven and afraid of absolutely nothing. "He's fifteen years old and he sleeps on my step. What exactly do you imagine he's *saying*?"

The hunter crouched. Cyrus watched him take Brindle's muzzle in one hand, gently, almost tenderly, and turn the old dog's face toward his own. And Brindle—loyal, trusting, stupid with age and affection—licked the man's wrist.

"There," the hunter said softly, standing. "You see? No fear of me at all. Trained animals lose their fear of handlers." He turned to the crier. "Evidence."

"It's a *dog*," Denna said again, and now there were voices behind her, murmurs, the crowd finding its feet beneath itself. Someone shouted that they'd owned Brindle longer than the king had owned his throne. Someone else laughed, high and wrong.

"Madam," the crier said, with terrible patience, "the decree is clear. Animals showing unnatural tameness, trained response, or bond beyond kind is grounds for seizure and examination. The animal will be taken to the assize at Greywater. If examination finds no fault, the owner will be compensated."

"And if it does?" someone called from the back.

No one answered that. The hunter produced a rope. Brindle went with him the way Brindle went with everyone—happily, tail low and wagging, glancing back at the crowd as if this were a game, as if at any moment someone would laugh and call him home. Denna followed them to the edge of the square, arguing, pleading, and then simply walking alongside until one of the crown's escort put a hand flat on her chest and stopped her, and she stood in the mud watching her dog go down the river road, and Brindle watched her back over his shoulder the whole way, certain, right up until the road bent, that she was coming too.

Cyrus did not move. Cyrus had learned twenty-six years ago that moving was death, and he stood in the crowd with his face arranged into the mild blankness of a bystander, and inside himself something was falling, floor after floor after floor, down through the dark of the building that had been his life.

Because he understood what everyone else was only beginning to grasp: it wasn't about guilt. It wasn't even about proof. The hunter hadn't heard Brindle say anything, because Brindle hadn't said anything. He'd seen a dog be *friendly*, and he had decided that friendliness was contraband.

Which meant that every route Cyrus had ever negotiated was now a crime scene. Every rat-council that guarded a warehouse, every pigeon that carried a message, every marten paid in hens—each one was evidence, and each one could be read by a man like that hunter the way a clerk reads a ledger. His whole economy of silence ran on animals being unafraid of him. And unafraid, as of this morning, was probable cause.

His clients would scatter or sell him. His routes would close. And somewhere in the crowd around him, sixty neighbors were doing arithmetic of their own—who among us has ever spoken carelessly in front of a beast? whose dog is too gentle? whose mule too clever?—and Cyrus could feel the arithmetic turning toward him like weather.

He began, very slowly, to edge toward the alley beside the shrine.

* * *

"You're white as flour," Sera said that evening, sliding onto the bench across from him in the back room of her fence-house, where the lamp burned low and the shutters were double-barred. She pushed a cup of wine at him. "Drink. You look like a man pricing his own funeral."

"Sera." He kept his voice down, though they were alone. He always kept his voice down; it was the one habit he'd never broken. "The hunter at the tollgate today. The one who took Brindle. What did you hear about him?"

She shrugged, and something in the shrug was already wrong—a half-beat too quick, her eyes going to the shutter before coming back to him. Twenty years of friendship, and Cyrus knew every rhythm she had. "They're calling him Voss. Captain Rell Voss. He's working the mountain circuit with a writ straight from the capital. They say he can smell the Gift on a person." She laughed, but it came out thin. "Superstition. Man's got good dogs and better ears, is all."

"Mm." Cyrus drank. Outside, rain had begun, soft against the shutters. "Sera. If it came to it—if they leaned on you—if your children were the price on the other side of a question about me—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her sharper than she meant it, he could tell, because she flinched at her own voice and glanced at the ceiling, where her boys slept. When she spoke again it was barely above breath. "Don't ask me that, Cyrus. Don't make me answer questions nobody's asked yet."

"I'm not asking you to answer. I'm telling you what I'm going to do. I'm gone within the week. The high routes, the ones I've never shown anyone. And I need—" he hesitated, hating the sentence even as he built it—"I need you to move my strongbox to the river cache before then. Quietly."

Something crossed her face then, fast, like a bird across a window—too quick to name, gone before he could swear it had been there at all. Grief, maybe. Or arithmetic.

"Of course," Sera said. "Of course. Whatever you need."

Later—much later, on the wet walk home along the river path, with the tollgate bell hanging silent and enormous above the dark water—Cyrus would try to remember whether he'd seen that flicker on her face, whether he'd known even then. But that night he only felt the cold, and the enormity of the silence where Brindle used to bark at the bakery carts, and the knowledge settling into him like silt: that the life he had built, grain by grain, whisper by whisper, was not his anymore. That silence itself, the one commodity he had traded in all his life, had been revalued overnight—from currency to contraband.

Three days later, a boy came running through the rain to Cyrus's door, breathless, terrified, sent by no one he trusted and everyone he loved.

"Cyrus," the boy gasped. "*Sera sold your name.*"
