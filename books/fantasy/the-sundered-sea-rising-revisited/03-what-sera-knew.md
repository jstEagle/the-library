## 3. What Sera Knew

The rain came down on Thornfell like a debt being collected.

Cyrus had known storms his whole life—the mountain kind, sudden and mean, that turned the smugglers' paths to rivers in an hour—but this was a lowland rain, patient and grey, the kind that seeped under doors and into ledgers and would not stop until everything soft had rotted. It had been falling for two days. On the third day, Sera sold his name.

He did not know it yet. He knew only that the rats had stopped coming to him.

That was how he learned most things worth knowing: through beasts. For twenty-six years the warrens of Thornfell had spoken to him in their small voices—rats with their quick gossip of grain and grievance, mules with their patient complaints, the goat-dogs chained in yards who told him which houses had new locks and which had new men. He had built an entire life out of what they saw and he alone could hear. A caravan rerouted around a rockslide. A warehouse emptied before the tax-men came. A rival's route poached because a magpie liked the shine of Cyrus's copper bits and told him where they'd been buried. All of it arranged in whispers no man had ever witnessed, all of it hidden so deep that even Cyrus sometimes forgot it was there, like a bone grown into the flesh.

And now the rats of Sera's street would not look at him.

He stood at the mouth of Tanner's Row in the failing light, hood up, rain running off his chin, and watched a rat cross the cobbles three feet from his boot without so much as pausing. Rats always paused for him. Rats had opinions about him—they found him useful, mostly, occasionally infuriating—and they always, always acknowledged him, a flick of the whiskers, a half-second of attention, the animal equivalent of tipping a hat.

This one looked straight through him.

Cyrus crouched, slow, and let his gift unfurl the way he did when he was alone, that loosening behind the breastbone, the world suddenly crowded with voices. *Little sister,* he said to the rat, gentle as he'd say anything. *Why do you pass me by?*

The rat stopped. Its whole body went tight, ears flat, and Cyrus felt something from it he had never felt from a rat in his life.

Fear. Of *him*.

*They said not to speak to you,* the rat said, its voice thin and fast as a dropped stitch. *The tall woman's man put down sweetened meal along the walls, and where the meal is, the listening-ones gather, and they said your name is a burning thing now. They said if we carry your words we burn too.* The rat backed away, one step, two. *I'm sorry, whisper-man. I'm sorry.*

It fled into a drain, and Cyrus stayed crouched on the wet stone for a long time while the rain wrote its grey arithmetic on everything.

*A burning thing.* His name. His careful, hidden, never-spoken-aloud-to-any-living-soul name.

There were perhaps four people in Thornfell who knew it. Three of them were dead or gone. The fourth had children.

He walked to her house.

* * *

Sera Vane lived above her fence-shop in Coppersmith's Close, in rooms that smelled of tallow and boiled wool, with a view of nothing but the wall of the dye-house next door. Cyrus had been climbing to her window since they were nine years old, back when she was the miller's daughter with flour in her braids and he was the warren-boy nobody claimed, and the climb was written into his body so deeply that his hands found the mortar-cracks without his eyes.

He crouched on her sill, soaked through, and looked in.

She was at the table with her ledger, her reading-glasses pushed up into her greying hair, her lips moving as she counted. Lamplight made her face gold. She was forty-one years old and tired in the way that women who fed five mouths on a fence's margins got tired, and she was chewing the end of her pen the way she had since they were children, and Cyrus felt his chest crack open along a seam he hadn't known was load-bearing.

"Sera."

She didn't startle. She set down the pen very carefully, squared it against the ledger's edge, and took off her glasses. When she looked up at him her eyes were dry and steady and terrible.

"I wondered how long you'd take," she said. "Two days. You've gotten slower."

"Open the window, Sera."

"Why?" She spread her hands. "So you can come in and shout at me where the children sleep? So the neighbors hear the whisper-man's voice through my shutters? No. Say what you came to say through the glass, like a ghost. That's fitting enough."

He pushed the window open anyway, because twenty-six years of hiding taught you when rules were armor and when they were just fear wearing armor's clothes, and climbed over the sill into the warm room that smelled of every childhood he'd ever pretended not to have. Rain drummed on the roof. Somewhere below, a cart groaned past through the flooded lane.

"How much," Cyrus said.

"Cyrus—"

"How much did they pay you for me?"

She looked at him then, fully, and did not flinch, and that was the worst part. Sera Vane had been lying to tax-men and toll-clerks since before her first husband died, and she was good at it, and here she was choosing truth with both hands like a woman setting her arm on a chopping block.

"Nothing," she said. "That's what should frighten you. There was no bag of coin. There was a man named Corvess, a Gift-hunter with a warrant-book and a smile like a fish, sitting in my shop with his gloves on, telling me about the decree. Telling me that aiding a concealed Gifted person is now treason. Treason, Cyrus. Not smuggling. Not evasion. *Treason.* And that the penalty for the traitor is the same as for the Gifted one." She folded her hands on the ledger, and he saw that they were shaking, and that she was pressing them flat to make them stop. "Then he asked me, very politely, if I knew anyone. And he left his card. And he said he'd be back next week."

"So you gave him my name to buy time."

"I bought my family." Her voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it; she spoke the way she haggled, level and precise, each word weighed. "You want me to have thrown the card in the fire and sung la-de-da? You want the heroic version? The heroic version ends with Corvess coming back, finding my books, finding my debts, finding the letters you sent me in your own hand, and taking Tam and little Wren in for questioning anyway. Questioning by Gift-hunters, Cyrus. Do you know what that means? Have you ever seen what comes out of those wagons?" She finally raised her voice, and it cracked in the middle. "I have. Two winters ago, the Kettering boy. Eleven years old. They carried what was left of him home in a sack and told his mother she should be grateful they'd been thorough."

The room was silent except for the rain.

"They came to me," Sera continued, quieter now, "because everyone knows everyone in Thornfell, and a hunter with a warrant-book doesn't need proof, he only needs leverage, and I am *made* of leverage. My debts. My dead husband's unpaid duties. My brother's old crimes. And now you." She pulled the ledger toward her and closed it, gently, like shutting a coffin lid. "So yes. I sat across from that smiling fish of a man and I said, I know someone. And I watched him write your name in his book. And I will live with it, because the alternative was watching them write my children's names in it instead."

"You could have warned me first," Cyrus said. His own voice sounded far away, underwater. "One note under the door. One crow. You know I'd have been gone by dawn. You could have sold my name and saved me both."

"And if he checked? If he had watchers on this house—which he does, by the way, the man in the blue cloak across the close, he's been there since yesterday—if he had watchers and saw me warn you?" She shook her head. "No. The only safe betrayal is a complete one. I'm sorry. I'm not sorry. Both of those are true, and you can hate whichever one you need to."

"I don't hate you." It came out of him before he could weigh it, and it was true, and he hated that it was true. "Gods help me, Sera, I understand. That's what makes it unbearable. If you'd done it out of greed I could curse you and walk clean away. But you did it because you're a mother, and because the crown has built a machine that turns mothers into informants, and you fed it what it asked for because the alternative was feeding it your children. I understand so well it makes me sick."

"Then understand this too." She stood, and crossed to him, and took his face in her cold hands the way she had when they were children and he'd come to her window bleeding from some fall or fight, and she looked at him with those steady terrible eyes. "I didn't sell everything. I sold your name. Your name is already dead—you've been dead to Thornfell since the proclamation went up; every soul who ever traded with you is rewriting their ledgers tonight. What I kept is your road." Her thumb traced the old scar on his cheekbone, from the winter they were twelve. "Corvess asked where you'd flee to, and I told him west, toward the salt passes, because that's what any fool would guess. You'll go anywhere but. Won't you."

It wasn't a question. Twenty years of friendship, and she still read him better than his own gift read beasts.

"South," Cyrus said hoarsely. "Down off the mountain. Toward the lowlands."

"Then go tonight, before the rain stops and the roads fill with his kind. And Cyrus—" her grip tightened, "—there's a knight. Left for dead past the charcoal-burners' track, in the hollow under the old watch-stone. The beasts have been crying about her for a day and a night; you think I don't know why the ravens circle there? Whatever you decide you are, whoever you decide to save—you should know the crown threw away one of its own knights for refusing to burn a village full of children. That's the country you're running from. Remember its shape."

He stared at her. "You knew. About me. All these years."

"I've known since we were eleven and you talked the mill-cat into walking the beam over the flood-channel to fetch my doll." A ghost of the old smile crossed her face and was gone. "I never said, because saying it would have endangered us both, and because it wasn't mine to say. Just like none of this—" she gestured at the ledger, the lamplight, the whole fragile edifice of her life, "—was mine to keep safe. We do what we can with what we're given. Tonight I was given a choice between your future and my children's, and I chose, and I'd choose it again tomorrow, and I will grieve you like a dead man either way. Now get out my window before the watcher changes shift."

At the sill he stopped, one leg over the wet frame, and looked back at her—at the gold lamplight, the ledger, the whole small stubborn kingdom of her survival.

"The Kettering boy," he said quietly. "Eleven years old."

"Yes."

"And if they come for Tovi-types here, someday—children like—" He couldn't finish it. He hadn't yet met the child who sang to crows; that was still ahead of him, down in the dark of the unwritten days. But he had been that child once himself, muting his own gift, whispering to beasts in cellars, and he knew exactly what a sack carried home to a mother looked like.

Sera understood anyway. She always had.

"Then I hope," she said, "that somewhere south of here there are people braver than me. Now go. And Cyrus—" her voice followed him out into the rain, rough as gravel, "—don't die where I can't bury you. I haven't the money for the journey."

* * *

He went south.

The rain walked with him all night, and by grey morning Thornfell was a smudge behind him and the mountain paths had given way to the long scree-falls above the charcoal-burners' track, and Cyrus Oyelaran, smuggler, ghost, burning thing, crouched at the lip of a hollow beneath a leaning watch-stone and listened.

Below him, half-drowned in bracken and rainwater, lay a woman in broken armor. Big, broad-shouldered, her tabard stripped of every badge so that only pale rectangles remained where cloth had been unpicked thread by thread—a knight erased rather than merely killed. Ravens stood around her in the wet grass, solemn as mourners, and when Cyrus let his gift loosen they turned as one and fixed him with their flat dark eyes, and their voices came to him layered and urgent:

*Warm one. Warm one. She is not done. She is not done.*

Cyrus thought of Sera's hands shaking flat against the ledger. Thought of a sack, and a mother, and a machine that turned mercy into treason. Thought of every rule he had lived by for twenty-six years, the rules that had kept him breathing, and heard them creak like ice on a thawing river.

*All right,* he told the ravens, and began to climb down. *Show me.*
