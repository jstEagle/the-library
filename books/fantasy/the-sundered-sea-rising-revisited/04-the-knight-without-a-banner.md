## 4. The Knight Without a Banner

The crows found her first.

Cyrus heard them a mile before he saw anything—three of them, ragged-winged and frantic, wheeling above the tree-line in the broken pattern that meant *hurt, hurt, something hurt, come*. He had learned long ago to tell the difference between the crows' alarm-cries: the sharp staccato that meant men coming, the low rolling croak that meant carrion, and this third thing, this insistent, almost pleading rhythm that he had never once been able to ignore, though ignoring it had kept him alive for twenty-six years.

He was three days out of Thornfell with nothing but a stolen mule, a sack of dried plums, and every rule he'd ever made for himself strapped to his back like ballast. Stay off the roads. Never sleep twice in one place. Speak to beasts only in whispers, only alone, only when the wind was wrong for human ears. And above all, above everything, carved into him so deep it might as well have been bone:

*Never stop. Never help. Never be seen.*

The crows did not care about his rules. They dove at his head, wheeled, dove again, screaming their plea into his skull until it rang like a struck pot.

"I hear you," he muttered, hunching low over the mule's neck. "I hear you, and I'm telling you no."

The lead crow—a female missing an eye, an old companion from the Thornfell rooftops who had followed him out of the burning town without being asked—landed on the mule's rump and screamed directly into his ear.

"Damn it all," Cyrus said, and turned the mule off the game trail.

* * *

She lay in a clearing where the trees had been cut back for a woodcutter's camp long abandoned, half-hidden under a drift of leaves as if she had tried to bury herself. She wore armor—the good kind, crown-issue, though someone had taken a hammer to the sigil on the breastplate until the king's sunburst was a crater of ruined metal—and there was so much blood on her that at first Cyrus could not tell which wounds were hers and which were other people's.

There had been other people. That much was obvious from the clearing itself: scorch-marks on the trunks, the Gift-hunters' signature, and three bodies in the treeline wearing the black-and-scarlet of Voss's hunters. They had died hard and recently, maybe six hours past, by the smell. The crows had been at them already. One of them had lost an arm to something with an edge, not fire.

"She killed them," Cyrus said quietly. The one-eyed crow croaked confirmation. "Three hunters. Alone. With a hole in her."

Because there was a hole in her. A spear-thrust under the left arm, where the plates didn't meet, deep enough that when he knelt beside her he could hear the wet whistle of her breathing. She was maybe forty, gray going on white at the temples, a face like a cliff that had argued with the sea and lost a few arguments. Her sword lay an arm's length away, still sheathed, which told him something: she hadn't died fighting wild. She'd gone down standing over something.

He followed the drag-marks in the leaves ten feet into the brush and found what she had been standing over—or rather, what remained of it. A woman. A pyre built from broken fence-rails and lit badly, burned down to ash and one unburned thing: a wooden charm, a little carved fox, clutched in a hand whose fingers had fallen away from it. The woman had worn no armor. She had been Gifted, or had looked Gifted, or had been in the wrong place when the wrong people came through.

Cyrus stood over the ashes for a long moment, the charm's small eyes staring up at him, and felt the floor of his life tilt again—that same lurch from the tollgate, from Sera's rain-wet face, the sensation that the world he had built out of silence was being dismantled plank by plank while he watched.

He went back to the knight. He told himself he was only checking whether she was worth robbing. Armor like that would feed him for a season.

Her eyes opened when he knelt. Gray eyes, shock-pale but focused, fixing on him with the flat assessment of a soldier deciding whether the thing in front of her was a threat. Her hand crawled toward the sword.

"Don't," Cyrus said. "You'll die faster."

"Then it's a fair trade," she rasped. Her voice was wrecked, smoke and gravel. "For them."

"For the hunters?"

"For failing." Her gaze slid past him, toward the pyre in the brush, and something moved behind the flatness—grief, huge and unsoldierly. "I was ordered to burn the village at Millbrook. Ordered to burn it *with the people in it*, because the decree changed and nobody sent word. I said the decree was cowardice dressed as law. Captain Voss said traitors don't get to have opinions." A breath that whistled. "So here I am. No banner. No order. Nothing."

"You killed three of his men."

"They were going to do to that woman what they did at Millbrook. I—" She stopped. Looked at him properly for the first time, taking in the smuggler's drab clothes, the mule, the total unremarkability of him. "Why are you here? Nobody comes up this trail. I chose it because nobody comes up this trail."

Cyrus should have lied. He was good at lying; it was most of what he did. But the one-eyed crow had landed on the knight's breastplate, on the ruined sigil, and was making a sound he had never heard from a crow before—a low, warbling coo, the sound they made to their own chicks, their own mates. And below the crow, beneath the leaf-litter, the earthworms had begun to stir, hundreds of them rising through the soil toward the wounded woman like iron filings toward a lodestone, and the mule had crept close and was nuzzling at her gauntlet, and everywhere around the clearing the small hidden lives of the world were leaning toward this dying stranger as if she were a hearth.

*Help her,* said the crows. *Help her,* said the worms, who could not speak but managed it anyway. *Help her,* said the mule, with the enormous simple certainty of its kind.

And Cyrus Oyelaran, twenty-six years old, keeper of a perfect life, heard himself say: "You're going to live. I'm sorry about that. It's going to hurt."

* * *

Dame Odile Vasseur had been dying comfortably for about four hours, and she resented being interrupted.

Not that she would have said so. What she said, mostly, was nothing, because consciousness came and went like tide and between tides there was pain of a purity she had not known since her first year in training yards, and beyond the pain, strangely, a kind of peace. She had refused an order. She had drawn steel on the men she'd served beside for eleven years. Whatever came next, she had chosen it with her whole chest, and there was a terrible freedom in that. Knights spent their lives being pointed like weapons. For one afternoon she had pointed herself.

Then the smuggler came.

She catalogued him the way she'd been trained to: young, dark-skinned, Thornfell-born by his vowels, hands soft in the wrong places and calloused in the right ones—rope, reins, locks, not blades. He moved like a man who had practiced being invisible until invisibility became a kind of grace. He cleaned her wound with spirits that smelled of plums, packed it with moss that numbed better than any field-surgeon's paste she'd ever used, stitched her with a boatmaker's neatness, and never once asked her name.

He also talked to the animals.

She noticed it the second day, fever-blurred though she was. He crouched by the fire and murmured at the mule, low and continuous, the way other men talked to wives or gods, and the mule cocked an ear and answered with little huffing sounds, and Odile, drifting, thought: *there it is.* There was the reason the crown was burning villages. There was the crime worth three corpses in the brush. Not the woman in the ashes—the woman was only fuel. This was the crime. This quiet man and his quiet conversations, this whole secret nation living under the kingdom's floorboards, speaking to rats and crows while the crown walked overhead pretending the floor was solid.

He must have known she'd worked it out. He watched her watching him, and she saw the exact moment the shutters came down behind his eyes—saw him decide to run, saw him decide not to, saw the decision cost him.

"If you're going to report me," he said, "wait until I'm over the ridge. I've had a bad month."

"I can't walk," Odile said. "Reporting anyone is beyond my abilities." She flexed her bandaged side experimentally and paid for it in white light. "Also, I no longer have anyone to report to. You may have noticed my career ended somewhat abruptly."

"Voss doesn't forgive desertion."

"It wasn't desertion." She stared into the fire. "Desertion is leaving. What I did was worse. I stayed, and I said no, and I made them kill me for it in front of each other." A pause. "They didn't finish the job. Sloppy work. I'll have to file a complaint."

Something cracked at the corner of the smuggler's mouth—not quite a smile, more like a smile testing the ice to see if it would hold. "What do I call you?"

"Dame Odile Vasseur, formerly of the King's Third Purge Company." She said it plainly, the way you'd show a scar. "Which means you should probably leave me in these woods to die, and we both know it. Your kind has reasons."

"My kind," Cyrus repeated softly.

"The hidden ones. The ones my company burned out of Millbrook." Her voice stayed level, which took everything. "Eleven years I enforced the decrees. I told myself it was law. Then the law stopped even pretending to be just, and I discovered the only thing holding me upright was habit." She turned her head and looked at him fully, gray eyes steady despite the fever. "So. Knight without a banner, at your mercy. Do your worst or your best. I've earned both."

The smuggler was silent a long time. The one-eyed crow sat on his shoulder, and the mule stood behind him, and Odile had the strange, vertiginous sense of being judged by a tribunal that included everyone except humans.

"You want to know why I helped you?" Cyrus said finally. "I asked myself that all last night. I have rules. Good ones. They've kept me breathing since I was nine years old. Rule one is *never stop*. Rule two is *never help*. You are a walking violation of everything I am." He fed a stick to the fire. "But the beasts begged. Crows, worms, the mule—all of them, all night, begging me not to let you die. And I've spent my whole life listening to them, because they're the only ones who never lie." His jaw tightened. "So apparently the animals think you're worth saving. Congratulations. You've been endorsed by vermin."

Odile laughed, and the laugh pulled at the stitches, and she decided it was worth it.

"And what do you say?" she asked. "The animals have voted. What does the man say?"

The smuggler looked into the fire for a long moment, and Odile watched the armor of him—not steel, the other kind, the kind that never dented—watched it take a blow somewhere near the heart and hold, barely, visibly groaning.

"The man says," Cyrus said at last, slowly, like a man stepping off a cliff he'd stood at the edge of for twenty-six years, "that there's a cartographer half a day south who owes me a favor, and a cave behind his workshop, and that if you tear your stitching I will leave you for the crows after all." He stood, and offered her his hand—rough, steady, terrified. "Can you ride?"

"No," Odile said honestly, and took his hand anyway.

* * *

They went south along the dry wash, slow, the mule bearing double. By midday Odile could stay in the saddle without holding on, which she announced like a military achievement and which Cyrus ignored with the dedication of a man guarding the last door of his life against a siege.

It was near dusk when the crows came back—one-eyed leading, three others strung out behind, flying the urgent pattern, the *come-come-come* pattern.

"Not again," Cyrus said. "I just did this. I'm retired from this."

The crow screamed at him and wheeled north.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

"Trouble." He squinted along the birds' flight line and felt his stomach drop, because he knew that heading, knew it the way you know the direction of home. North was the high pass. North was the road the hunters would use moving between villages. And north, at the end of a goat-track nobody sane would take, was the cliffside workshop of a man named Pell, who owed Cyrus money and secrets in roughly equal measure.

"They're headed for Pell's," he said. "Or there's a village burning. Either way—" He stopped. Closed his eyes. Heard the crows' insistence layering over the memory of Sera in the rain, saying *they have my children, Cyrus*, hearing himself answer, cold and clean, *then you made your choice.*

He was so tired of choices that only ever went one direction.

"Cyrus," Odile said, reading him with a soldier's eye. "Whatever you're deciding—decide faster."

He opened his eyes. Turned the mule north.

"Rule three," he muttered, to no one, to the crows, to the whole listening dark, "is never go back."

The one-eyed crow settled smugly on his shoulder, and together they rode toward whatever the fire had left standing.
