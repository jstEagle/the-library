## 5. Nine Fingers and a Map

The storm found them an hour after dark, and the cliff path found them an hour after that, and by the time Cyrus saw the light burning in the rock face ahead of them, he had stopped believing in either.

Rain came off the sea in horizontal sheets, salt and cold enough to make his teeth ache. Behind him, Dame Odile Vasseur moved like something carved from wet granite—she had not spoken in six hours, not since they'd left the burned-out shepherd's hut where she'd pulled a crossbow bolt from her own shoulder with a knife, a strip of linen, and no sound at all. Cyrus had watched her do it and thought: *this is what I saved.* He still didn't know if the thought was pride or dread.

*Left,* said the gulls, wheeling above the white water far below. *Left, left, the warm one, the dry one.*

He trusted the gulls. Gulls lied about food but never about shelter.

"There." He had to shout over the wind. "In the cliff. There's a door."

Odile squinted through the rain, hand going to the sword she'd carried nameless for three days. "That's a seam in the rock."

"That's a door," Cyrus said. "There's smoke coming out of it sideways."

They climbed down a stair cut into the living stone, each step worn into a shallow bowl by decades of feet, until the sea noise fell away behind them and there it was: a low iron-banded door set flush into the cliff, with a lamp burning in a niche beside it, its flame bent sideways as though the wind had followed them down out of spite.

Cyrus knocked the way the rats had told him to knock—three slow, two fast—and the door opened on a chain before he finished.

One eye. Grey, sharp, ringed with sleeplessness. It went from Cyrus to Odile to Odile's sword and back.

"Smuggler," the eye said.

"Cartographer," Cyrus answered. "Pell."

"I don't take visitors."

"You took mine anyway. You opened the door."

A long pause. Rain drummed on the lintel above their heads. Somewhere inside, a kettle whistled and was taken off the fire.

"The knight can't come in armed," Pell said at last.

"She can't come in unarmed either," Cyrus said. "We're being hunted. Both of us. If you want us gone by morning, fine. But you'll feed us first, because I've been talking to your goats for the last mile and they say you haven't eaten properly yourself in two days."

Another pause. The eye narrowed.

"You spoke to my goats."

"They speak very highly of your cheese."

The chain rattled. The door swung wide.

* * *

Inside, the workshop was larger than the cliff had any right to contain—a cave system hollowed and shored with timber, lamplit, warm, and so crowded with paper that it looked as though the mountain itself were molting. Maps hung from every surface: coastlines Cyrus knew, coastlines he didn't, coastlines that could not exist, drawn in ink the color of dried blood. Rolls of vellum stood in barrels like sheathed swords. A goat—the source of the cheese, presumably—regarded them from a corner with the smugness of an animal whose intelligence report had been believed.

And everywhere, everywhere, the same shape repeated. In the big map on the drafting table. In the small ones pinned to the beams. In a charcoal sketch nailed above the cold hearth, drawn by a child's unsteady hand and kept, clearly, for years.

It was a spiral. Or a coastline shaped like one. A sea curled inward on itself, closed, swallowed.

Pell was not what Cyrus expected. He was younger than his hands—forty, maybe, with a lean, weathered face and hair going grey at the temples—but his right hand ended at the knuckles of his last two fingers, the stump smooth and old, healed wrong or healed deliberately. He moved around his workshop with the economy of a man who had relearned every task one-handed and then some, pouring tea with his left, setting out hard bread and harder cheese without asking whether they wanted any.

"Eat," he said. "Then tell me why a smuggler and a king's knight are knocking on my door in a storm, and why the knight looks like she's been dead for a week."

"Not a king's knight," Odile said. Her voice came out rusted. "Not anymore."

"No," Pell agreed, studying the mud on her boots, the blood on her sleeve, the absence of any sigil anywhere on her. "I can see how the king would want you back."

She flinched. Cyrus watched her flinch and understood, suddenly, that this man knew things. Cartographers always did. They were the crown's eyes when the crown wanted roads measured and harbors sounded—and its memory when the crown wanted villages erased.

"You drew the purge maps," Cyrus said quietly.

Pell's good hand went still on the teapot.

"Thornfell highlands, three years ago," Cyrus pressed. "New roads where the villages used to be. I've seen them. The surveyors' marks were fresh. Someone walked those valleys *after* and made them look like they'd never held anything but trees."

For a moment Cyrus thought the man would throw them out into the rain. Then Pell sat down heavily on his drafting stool, and the anger went out of him like air from a punctured lung, and what was underneath looked older and much more tired.

"They took the fingers for refusing," he said. "Not the drawing. The *refusing*. Surveyor-corps regulation: refusal to certify is mutilation, second offense is the rope. I certified the next four maps." He looked at his ruined hand as though it belonged to someone else. "I told myself paper wasn't stone. That ink wasn't fire. That as long as I never drew another village being deleted, I was clean." His mouth twisted. "Then I started drawing this instead. At night. Where nobody could see."

He rose, crossed to the great map on the drafting table, and turned it toward them.

Cyrus had seen the kingdom's maps all his life. Every one of them showed the realm bounded east by the Graywater hills and beyond that, nothing—blank vellum, or a decorative border of waves and sea-serpents, the cartographer's traditional way of saying *here be nothing, stop asking.*

But under Pell's pen, the blankness had been filled in. Not with land. With water. An immense, spiraling bay, drowned and deep, its shores traced in dotted lines that ran beneath the mountains, beneath Thornfell itself, beneath everything. And at the center of the spiral, marked with a symbol Cyrus didn't recognize—an open circle, a doorway seen edge-on—two words in a cramped, obsessive hand:

*THE SUNDERED SEA.*

"There's no such place," Odile said. But she leaned closer as she said it, and Cyrus noticed.

"There's no such place *anymore*," Pell said. "Which is different. Which is the whole point." He tapped the dotted shoreline with his stump, gently, the way you'd touch a scar. "Every old chart I've ever stolen, bought, or copied—and I have forty-one of them, some predating the crown by six hundred years—shows a civilization here. Roads. Harbors. Bell towers. Then, within a single generation across every source, the charts go blank. Not gradually. All at once. Do you understand what that means? A whole realm, erased between one map and the next. Not conquered. Not migrated. *Erased.* By decree, if the marginalia is honest—and one of them is, a monk's hand in the margin of the Verelant chart: *the waters were called up and the way was shut, and it was written that no map should show it again.*"

"And you believe a margin note from a dead monk," Odile said.

"I believe forty-one independent sources," Pell said, "and I believe my grandfather, who heard bell-song under the ice on Lake Verre in the winter of his boyhood, and I believe—" he hesitated, and for the first time looked almost embarrassed, almost human, "—I believe the wells. Salt in the wells, in the lowland farms, places salt has no business being. The water table doesn't lie. Something beneath us is *rising*, ser. Has been for years. The sea they drowned isn't done drowning."

The rain hammered the cliff outside. The lamp flames leaned. Cyrus became aware that he had stopped eating, that his hands had gone still around the bread, that some part of him was leaning toward the map the way a starving man leans toward a lit window.

He hated that part of himself. He knew exactly what it cost, hope. Hope was a debt collected with interest.

"A door," he said. His voice came out flat, careful. "You wrote *door*. On the map. What door?"

Pell's eyes came up and fixed on him, and Cyrus realized the man had been waiting for that question the whole night—had perhaps built this entire cliffside life, the goats, the lamp by the door, the knock-code the smugglers' routes carried in whispers, all of it as a kind of trap for exactly this conversation.

"The circle at the center," Pell said softly. "In the oldest chart—the Verelant one—it's not decoration. It's labeled. *Porta Vetula.* The Old Door. The monks who wrote about the Sundering mention it twice: once as the thing that was shut, and once—" his voice dropped, "—once as the thing that *opens*. *The way was shut against the Gifted, and the Door was sealed, and only a full surrender shall swing it wide.*"

Silence. Even the rain seemed to hold its breath.

"Surrender of what?" Odile asked.

Pell looked at Cyrus.

Cyrus felt it like a hand closing around the back of his neck. Twenty-six years of rules—*never speak where men can hear, never let them look too closely, hide it, mute it, bury it*—all of them screaming at once. This stranger knew. Had known since the goats, probably. Since the door.

"How," Cyrus said, very evenly, "did you know to ask me that."

"Your goats," Pell said, "told me you were coming."

* * *

Later—much later, when Odile had finally, finally slept, curled on a pallet with her sword in reach even in dreams—Cyrus stood at the great map while Pell trimmed a lamp wick and pretended not to watch him.

"It's a story," Cyrus said. "A beautiful story. Drowned kingdoms and magic doors. People have died for less beautiful stories. People are dying right now for uglier ones."

"Yes," Pell said. "Probably."

"You'd lead us into the empty east on the strength of a margin note."

"I'd lead you into the empty east on the strength of forty-one charts, a rising water table, my grandfather's bells, and the fact," Pell said, "that the crown is burning the highlands *now*, tonight, in the same pattern, the same order, the same decrees that erased this place six hundred years ago. History doesn't rhyme, smuggler. It repeats. And I have read enough maps to know when I'm standing on the page where it happens."

Cyrus looked at the little open circle at the heart of the spiral. *Only a full surrender shall swing it wide.*

He thought of Sera's face in the rain. Of the hounds he hadn't met yet, sleeping somewhere in the king's kennels. Of Tovi, whom he had not yet rescued, whose crow-song was at this moment still just a rumor in a village he'd never heard of. Of Odile breathing slow and wounded ten feet away, trusting him with her back because the beasts had begged him not to leave her.

He thought about what it would mean to be *full*. To be emptied.

"It's a story," he said again, but quieter, and Pell heard the difference, because Pell smiled—small, cracked, nine-fingered, hopeful—and began, without another word, to roll up the map.

Outside, far below, the sea ground against the cliffs, patient as a debt. And somewhere in the dark water over the drowned streets, faint and impossible and unmistakable, a bell rang.
