## 6. The Child Who Sang to Crows

The smoke reached them before the village did.

Cyrus smelled it first as a wrongness in the wind—pine pitch and thatch and something sweeter underneath, hair or wool or worse—and he stopped so suddenly that Odile nearly walked into his back. Pell, a half-dozen paces behind with his map-roll clutched to his chest like an infant, came up short and swore.

"That's Thornfell burning," Pell said. "Or it was. That smoke's a day old."

"It's not Thornfell," Cyrus said quietly. "Thornfell's smoke is black. This is gray." He had spent twenty-six years learning the vocabulary of fire the way other men learned scripture: which woods burned hot, which roofs wept tarry tears, which villages smoldered for days and which went up like dry grass. "This is a small place. A poor one."

"Small places burn first," Odile said. Her voice was flat in the way Cyrus had learned meant she was holding something down with both hands. She still moved like a knight even without her banner—shoulders square, chin level—but there were mornings when he caught her looking at her own hands as if they belonged to someone else. He knew that look. He wore it every time he remembered Sera's face in the rain.

They crested the ridge at dusk, and the valley below told them everything the smoke had promised.

It had been a hamlet of perhaps thirty homes, tucked into the crook of a stream that ran silver through the last of the light. Now it was a scatter of black teeth against the earth. Roof-beams jutted from ash heaps like ribs. The crown's soldiers had come and gone—their hoofprints were stitched across the fields in patterns Cyrus recognized from the tollgate, from the warrens, from every bad thing the kingdom did efficiently—and whatever they had taken, they had taken days ago.

But the crows had stayed.

They rose off the ruin in a slow black spiral when the three fugitives appeared on the ridge, dozens of them, maybe hundreds, wheeling against the bruised sky. And Cyrus, who had spent his whole life keeping his gift folded small and silent inside him like contraband, felt them *notice* him.

It was not words. It never was, not with birds. It was pressure and attention, grief and hunger braided into a single dark knot that pressed against the edges of his mind. *Dead things here. Cold things. Come and see. Come and see.*

"No," Cyrus said aloud, before he could stop himself, before he remembered that Odile and Pell were watching. His voice cracked on it. "No, I'm not—I don't do that anymore. I can't."

"Do what?" Odile asked, though by the set of her jaw he thought she already suspected. She'd seen him speak to the mule-train of rats outside Thornfell. She'd seen what he could do. She'd never once asked him to use it since, and he understood that too—understood it the way you understood a scar, without needing the story.

"They want me to look," he said. "At what's down there." His hands had gone cold. "There might be survivors."

"There might be soldiers waiting for survivors," Pell said practically, but he was already unrolling a scrap of hide, already sketching the valley's shape with his ruined hand, the two missing fingers leaving a gap in his grip that he'd learned to work around the way a river works around stones. "Three ways in, one way out. If anyone's alive down there, they'll be in the root cellars. The stone ones hold heat signatures differently—" He caught himself, glanced at Cyrus. "Sorry. Old habit. I used to map purge sites for the crown. Before."

"Before they took your fingers," Odile said.

"Before they took my fingers," Pell agreed, and rolled the hide away.

* * *

Cyrus went down alone.

It wasn't bravery. He wanted to be clear about that, even in the privacy of his own skull. It was the opposite of bravery—it was the same cold arithmetic that had governed his whole life: if I go alone and die, no one else is slowed. If I go alone and find nothing, no one else has to carry the sight. He had built his entire existence out of this kind of math, this careful accounting where he always paid the cost himself because it was easier than trusting anyone else to hold the ledger.

The crows followed him down the slope in a ragged escort, settling on fence posts and broken eaves, watching him with eyes like wet obsidian. They were not threatening him. They were not thanking him either. They were simply *present*, the way carrion birds are present, and their attention sat on his shoulders like a second cloak.

He found the first body near the well. An old man, face-down in the trampled mud, an iron poker still gripped in one hand. He had died facing the direction the soldiers had come from. Cyrus knelt beside him for a moment—not praying; he'd never learned how—but acknowledging. *You stood. That counts for something, wherever you are now.*

The root cellar door was hidden under a collapsed woodpile, exactly as Pell had predicted, and Cyrus heard the breathing before he saw the cellar: shallow, rapid, terrified. More than one person. He lifted the door-slab aside and looked down into the darkness.

A woman lay curled around a smaller shape, and both of them were so still that for a terrible moment Cyrus thought he'd found corpses after all. Then the smaller shape moved—a flutter, a twitch—and the woman's eyes opened in the dark, and they were the eyes of an animal at bay.

"Don't," she whispered. "Please. Please don't."

"I'm not with them." Cyrus kept his voice low, kept his hands visible. "I'm nobody. I'm passing through."

"You're lying." But her voice had shifted, sharpened. "You're lying because you have to be. Because if you're not lying then—" She stopped. Started again. "They took my daughter. Two days ago. The ones in gray-and-gold. They took her because she sang."

Cyrus felt the world tilt slightly, the way it did sometimes now, ever since the decree. "Sang."

"To crows." The woman began to laugh, and it was the worst sound Cyrus had heard in weeks, worse than Sera's silence, worse than the knight's confession by firelight. It was laughter with nothing left inside it. "She sang to crows, and the crows came, hundreds of them, and the soldiers said no child should be able to do that unless she was Gifted, and they took her, and I hid in a hole in the ground while they did it because I couldn't stop them, couldn't even try—"

She was crying now, great racking sobs that she strangled with her own wrist pressed against her mouth, and Cyrus understood with sudden horrible clarity that she was not crying about her daughter. She was crying because she was alive, and her daughter was gone, and being alive was the unbearable part.

"The soldiers," Cyrus said carefully. "Which way did they go?"

"East. Toward the garrison at Harrow's Ford." She wiped her face with a shaking hand. "But you won't get her back. Nobody gets them back. You've seen the tollgates. You know what happens."

"I know what happens," Cyrus agreed. He stood, slowly, and looked east toward the gathering dark. Somewhere out there was an eight-year-old girl who sang to crows, and somewhere out there were the men who had taken her, and Cyrus Oyelaran had spent twenty-six years perfecting the art of not getting involved, of staying small and quiet and alive, and every single rule he had ever made for himself said *walk away, walk away, walk away.*

"What was her name?" he asked.

The woman looked up at him, startled out of her grief. "*What?*"

"Your daughter. What was her name?"

"Tovi." The word seemed to surprise her, coming out of her own mouth. "Her name is Tovi."

* * *

Odile didn't argue when he told them. That frightened Cyrus more than argument would have.

Pell argued. Pell argued for two hours, pacing the shelter of a burned-out barn, laying out the logistics with his ruined hand and his good one, mapping escape routes that all pointed away from Harrow's Ford. "We're three fugitives with no supplies and one sword between us," he said, jabbing a finger at Odile. "You want to assault a garrison?"

"Not assault," Cyrus said. "Infiltrate. Or better—intercept. Garrisons don't keep Gifted children long. There's a bounty protocol. They'll be moving her to the capital within the week, probably with a small escort, probably by road." He had spent years listening to smugglers plan routes around tolls and checkpoints; the architecture of the kingdom's cruelty was no different from the architecture of its commerce. Everything moved along roads. Everything could be ambushed on roads.

"And you know this how?" Pell demanded.

"I listen." Cyrus met his eyes. "To things you don't hear."

Pell stopped pacing. For a long moment the only sound was wind through the barn's broken rafters, and somewhere far off, a crow calling.

Finally Odile spoke. "I'll go with him."

"You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." She said it simply, without drama, the way she said most true things. "I refused one purge order. I didn't refuse the forty that came before it. This is—" She paused, and Cyrus watched her search for the word, watched her fail to find it, watched her settle for the truth instead: "This is mine to do."

* * *

They caught the convoy at dawn on the second day, on a stretch of road where the forest pressed close and the escort had grown lazy with routine. Four soldiers, a wagon, a driver. Cyrus counted them from cover and felt his stomach drop—four was manageable, four was survivable, four was the kind of odds that Odile Vasseur, disgraced or not, could handle with her sword-arm tied behind her back.

The plan was simple, because simple plans survived contact with reality. Odile took the rear guard. Cyrus took the driver. Pell, who had refused to stay behind ("I mapped ambushes for the crown for six years; I know how they think"), created a diversion on the forward flank—a false trail-sign, a snapped branch arranged just so, the suggestion of movement in the trees.

It worked. It worked almost perfectly. It worked until Cyrus opened the wagon.

The girl inside was small and filthy and chained at the wrists, and when the light hit her face she didn't cry out or shrink back. She looked up at Cyrus with eyes that were red-rimmed but steady, and she said, in a voice hoarse from disuse:

"You're the one the crows were talking about."

Cyrus went very still. "What?"

"The crows." She tilted her head, birdlike, studying him. "They've been following me since the village. They said someone was coming. Someone who talks quiet." She frowned. "You talk loud, though. In your head. Like shouting."

"I don't—" Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "I don't shout."

"You do a little." But she said it without accusation, without fear, with the devastating honesty of a child who has already lost everything and therefore has nothing left to protect. "It's okay. I shout too, sometimes. When I'm scared."

Odile appeared at the wagon's edge, blood-spattered but unbloodied, and cut the chains with two strokes of her knife. Tovi rubbed her raw wrists and looked at the knight, at the cartographer, at Cyrus, with the assessing gaze of someone much older than eight.

"Are you taking me home?" she asked.

Cyrus opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Odile, who looked at Pell, who looked at the road behind them—at the smoke still rising faintly over the valley, at the kingdom that had burned a village for the crime of containing one singing child.

"We can't," Cyrus said finally, and hated every word. "Not yet. But we're going somewhere they can't reach you. I promise."

Tovi considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she nodded once, sharply, as if a bargain had been struck, and climbed down from the wagon on her own.

* * *

They traveled north that day, away from roads, away from garrisons, deep into hill country where the map thinned into guesswork. Tovi walked between them without complaint, eating hardtack soaked in stream water, asking occasional questions with the relentless precision of childhood: *Why is your hand missing fingers? Why does the knight walk like she's carrying something heavy? Why do you smell like rats?*

("Trade secret," Cyrus said, and Tovi laughed, and the sound was so bright and unexpected that all three adults flinched.)

It was evening when Cyrus found her at the edge of camp, sitting on a fallen log with her face turned up to the sky, singing.

It was not a song with words, exactly—or rather, the words were secondary, a rhythm beneath the melody, a heartbeat under the blood. The melody itself rose and fell in patterns that made Cyrus's skin prickle, patterns he recognized in his bones, patterns that were *language*, though not any language men spoke. She was singing to the crows.

And they came.

They poured out of the darkening sky like a black tide, dozens of them, settling in the branches above her, hopping along the log beside her, cocking their heads to catch each note. She sang, and they answered—harsh, beautiful calls that wove through her melody like counterpoint—and Cyrus stood frozen at the camp's edge with his heart hammering against his ribs, unable to move, unable to breathe.

Because he had never done that. Not once, not ever, in twenty-six years.

He had whispered to rats in cellars. He had murmured to mules in empty lots. He had kept his voice below the threshold of human hearing, kept his gift folded up tight and small and *hidden*, because hidden was safe and safe was alive, and he had told himself for twenty-six years that this caution was wisdom. But watching this child sing openly to the darkening sky, unashamed, unafraid, *loud*, Cyrus felt something crack open inside him that he hadn't known was sealed.

It was terror. Of course it was terror. Every instinct screamed that she was painting a target on them all, that the crows' answering calls carried for miles, that somewhere out there Rell Voss or hunters like him were following the sound like a beacon.

But underneath the terror, shamefully, unmistakably, was envy.

She sang, and the sky answered, and she had never once in her eight years of life thought to be ashamed of it—until men with gray-and-gold livery taught her otherwise. And even now, even chained in a wagon, even orphaned by fire, she sang. Openly. Loudly. As if the gift were not a curse to be concealed but a birthright to be exercised, as if the whole point of having a voice was to use it.

Cyrus thought of his mother, dead these fifteen years, who had taught him the rules in a whisper: *never where men can hear, never where lamps can see, never, never, never.* He had thought she was teaching him survival. Standing in the blue dusk with a child's song washing over him and the crows wheeling overhead like a benediction, he wondered for the first time whether she had been teaching him shame.

The song ended. The crows settled into a soft muttering chorus, preening and shuffling, and Tovi turned and found him standing there, and smiled—gap-toothed, unguarded, radiant.

"They like you," she said. "The crows. They say you smell like safety." She patted the log beside her. "Sit. I'll teach you the evening song. Everyone should know the evening song."

Cyrus sat. His legs felt strange, unsteady, as though the ground beneath him had become unreliable. "My mother taught me to be quiet," he said, before he could stop himself. "About—this. About all of it. She said quiet was how we stayed alive."

Tovi considered this with enormous seriousness, the way only children and judges consider things. "Was she right?"

"I don't know." The admission scraped coming out. "I used to think so."

"My mama said my singing was a *gift*," Tovi said. "Like from the old stories. She said people used to sing with beasts all the time, before, and nobody thought it was strange." She picked up a pebble and turned it over in her small fingers. "Then the crown decided it was strange. Mama said crowns do that. Decide things are strange so they have a reason to take them."

"She sounds wise," Cyrus managed.

"She's dead," Tovi said, with the flat simplicity of a child who has not yet learned to cushion truth. Then, softer, turning the pebble over again: "The crows say she's not gone, though. Just far. They say the dead go where the songs go." She looked up at him. "Do you believe that?"

Cyrus looked at the crows roosting above them—dozens of dark shapes against the first stars—and felt the weight of twenty-six years of silence pressing down like a stone. He thought about all the whispers he'd never raised, all the voices he'd never joined, all the songs he'd never sung. He thought about a door out of the kingdom, and whether doors worked the same way for gifts as they did for bodies.

"I don't know what I believe," he said finally. "But I think—" He stopped. Started again. "I think maybe quiet isn't the same as safe. Maybe I got those mixed up a long time ago."

Tovi nodded solemnly, as if he'd passed some test. Then she began the evening song again, softly this time, teaching rather than performing, and the crows leaned in to listen, and Cyrus—Cyrus who had not sung since childhood, who could barely remember the melodies his mother had hummed to him in the warrens—opened his mouth.

Nothing came out at first. His throat had forgotten how. But Tovi sang the phrase again, patient, unhurried, and the crows waited, and something in Cyrus's chest unlocked—something old and rusted and half-remembered—and he followed her, haltingly, badly, a grown man croaking out a child's melody to a committee of unimpressed birds.

It was the loudest sound he had ever made on purpose.

When it was over, the crows rustled approvingly, and Tovi beamed at him with fierce pride, and Cyrus sat on the log in the dark with his heart pounding and his cheeks wet and could not, for the life of him, have said whether he was grieving or rejoicing.

Across the campfire, Odile watched him with an expression he couldn't read, and Pell had stopped pretending to study his maps.

It was Pell, later, spreading his charts by firelight, who broke the comfortable silence. "There's something I haven't told you both," he said quietly. "About where we're going. About why I really know the way." He smoothed the hide flat with his maimed hand, and his finger traced a line west, past the hills, past the salt-flats, to the edge of the map where the parchment simply... stopped. "There's a sea under this kingdom. Drowned. Older than the crown. And I think—" His voice dropped. "I think there's a door in it."

Cyrus looked from the map to Tovi, asleep against Odile's shoulder with a crow feather clutched in one small fist, and felt the evening song still echoing in his chest like a bell that had finally been allowed to ring.

"Tell us," he said.
