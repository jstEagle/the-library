## 7. Ash Road

The road out of Thornfell burned behind them for two days.

Odile Vasseur had seen a hundred fires like it. She had set eleven of them herself—villages with names she could still recite in order, the way other soldiers recited battle honors. So when she looked back from the ridge at dusk and saw the smoke columns leaning east on the wind, she did not need to guess at the method. Three columns, spaced evenly. That was the standard pattern: burn the outlying farms first to drive the population inward, then the mill and the granary so no one could winter in place, then the village proper at dawn when the wind was calmest and the fire would sit down on the rooftops like a patient animal.

She had written the manual on the spacing. Not personally—not her hand on the page—but her campaigns had been cited in it, back when her name was still spoken with something other than contempt. *The Vasseur Method,* some junior clerk had called it in a dispatch, and the phrase had stuck, and she had worn it for six years like a decoration she could never take off without showing the wound beneath.

"You're doing it again," Cyrus said beside her.

He said it quietly, the way he said most things. He had a voice built for cellars and confidences, low and roughened, and he never wasted it. In three days of flight he had spoken perhaps four hundred words aloud, but Odile had begun to notice that he was talking constantly anyway—a murmur under his breath, a soft click of the tongue, a sound like water over stones directed at the mule, at the crows crossing overhead, once, memorably, at a viper they'd nearly stepped on in the pass. The animals answered. That was the part she still couldn't make her eyes believe: the world simply leaned toward him, attentive, as if it had been waiting years for someone to ask it anything at all.

"Doing what," she said.

"Counting." He nodded back toward the smoke. "You count them every time you look."

Odile said nothing. She adjusted the strap of Pell's map-chest across her shoulder—the cartographer rode in the cart now, feverish, his stump of a hand wrapped in linen—and kept walking. Behind her, Tovi slept in the cart's bed among the sacks, one arm flung over the little gray crow that never left her, its head tucked under its wing like a stone in a stream. The child had not cried since the rescue. That frightened Odile more than screaming would have. She had seen it before, in villages after the riders came through: children who went silent not because they were stunned but because they had understood, instantly and completely, exactly what kind of world they lived in.

The road descended through black pine toward a river valley. Above them the sky was the color of a healing bruise. Somewhere far to the north, horns sounded—one long note, repeated, patient as dripping water. Search pattern. She knew that too.

"They'll sweep the valley tomorrow," she said. "Two columns through the gorge, one along the high road. They're not hurrying because they don't think we're this fast."

"How do you know?"

"Because I trained the men who are doing it." It came out flat, matter-of-fact, and only after she heard it did she realize what she'd admitted. Cyrus glanced at her. He didn't ask. He had a way of not asking that was worse than any question.

They made camp an hour past dark in the lee of a fallen fir, off the road, close enough to hear the river running cold and quick below. No fire, at first. Then Tovi woke, whimpering once at the dark, and Cyrus looked at Odile and said, "Small one. Low. I'll watch the wind," and she built it before she remembered that building fires was a habit from another life, another woman, when fire had been a tool she carried in iron pots on wagons.

The flames caught the pitchy wood and settled into a red murmur. Odile sat with her hands open toward the heat and watched them shake, just slightly, and hated them for it.

* * *

Cyrus came back from the treeline an hour later and sat down across the fire without a word, which was itself a report: nothing moving, or nothing that mattered. He fed a strip of dried mutton to the mule, who stood at the edge of the light like a sentry. Then he looked at her across the flames with those dark, level eyes, and said, "Tell me about the eleven."

Odile's breath stopped. "I never told you the number."

"You talk in your sleep." He turned the meat in his fingers, unhurried. "Not words. Numbers. You count up to a number and stop. First night it was nine. Last night it was ten. Tonight I'd guess eleven." He raised his eyes. "I listen for a living, Dame Odile. It's the only gift I've got that doesn't frighten people."

"It should frighten them."

"Everything frightens everyone now. That's rather the crown's whole project."

The fire popped. Down in the cart, Tovi stirred and the crow made a small sound like a hinge, and settled. Odile found that her hands had closed into fists, and she opened them deliberately, finger by finger, the way she'd been taught to open them before a confession—though the last time she'd knelt for absolution she'd lied through all of it, and the priest had given her penance anyway, three psalms and a fast day, and she had walked out lighter and known herself damned.

"Cyrus Oyelaran," she said. "Do you know why they call me Dame? Because I earned it. Twelve years in the king's service. I held a commission signed in ink and sealed in gold, and I believed—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I believed the Gifted were a wound in the kingdom. That's how we were taught to see it. A wound, or a rot. Something that had gotten into the body of the realm and would kill it if it wasn't cut out. Nobody said *people*. You can cut out a rot. You can burn a wound clean. The language does half the work before you ever draw your sword."

"And the other half?" Cyrus asked softly.

"The other half is doing it once." She watched the fire. "After the first time, it's arithmetic. Village has twelve Gifted souls, say. Two old women who charm goats, a boy who talks to bees, a midwife who—well. Who knows things about unborn children that no one should know. Twelve. And the village is four hundred, and the decree says harboring is death, and if you leave them alive, the hunters come next season and burn everything anyway, but slower, and worse, because by then the villagers have spent a year being afraid of their own neighbors. So you do it clean. One morning. You post the list, you take the ones who come quietly to the wagons, and you burn the houses of the ones who run." Her voice stayed level. She was proud of that and disgusted by it in the same breath. "Clean. That was the word we used. As if there were a dirty version that someone else did, somewhere else, and ours was different because we folded the lists neatly afterward."

Cyrus had gone very still. Not frozen—she knew the difference between stillness out of fear and stillness out of attention. He was listening the way he listened to beasts, she realized. Whole-body. As though the words were coming from somewhere deep and easily spooked.

"Nine villages my first three years," she said. "Then two more. The last was Kellin's Hollow, and it was the smallest, and there was only one name on the list, and it was a girl of fourteen who sang to the river so it wouldn't flood the barley fields. She'd saved that village three harvests running. Everyone knew it. The headman tried to give me his own daughter instead—told me the girl had died the previous winter, brought out a shroud, wept beautifully. I found the real girl under the floor of the mill in four hours, because I had done this eleven times and I knew where people hide their miracles." She swallowed. "We took her to the provincial seat for judgment. She was sentenced on a Tuesday and executed on a Thursday, and on Wednesday night she sang through the wall of her cell—not loudly, just to herself, the way children do—and every guard on that corridor requested transfer by morning. Including me. I put in the request. It was denied. And I stood in the yard on Thursday and watched, and I saluted, because that was the procedure, and then I went back to my quarters and wrote a letter resigning my commission, and I did not send it. I wrote it eleven times over five years, Odile, I wrote it every time, and I never sent it once, until the twelfth time, when the order came down for Thornfell province and I looked at the maps and saw the names of places I had drunk in and danced in and I finally—*finally*—"

Her voice broke. She let it. There was no one left whose opinion of her could get worse; there was a strange freedom in that, a bare hillside where nothing would grow.

"I ran," she said. "Twelve years late. And they stripped my rank and named me traitor and put a price on my head that would buy a good farm, and every part of that was deserved, and none of it was justice, because justice would have been the yard at the provincial seat on any of eleven Thursdays, with me on the wrong side of the wall."

The fire had burned low. Cyrus fed it a stick, slowly, considering.

"Tovi," he said at last. "The village she came from. Marrowdale. Did you—"

"No." The word came out too fast, and she made herself slow down and be exact, because exactness was the only currency she had left. "No. I never worked Thornfell province. That's true, Cyrus, I swear it on whatever you hold sacred. But I know the officer who did. Captain Brann. My student. I taught him the spacing." She looked east, where the smoke columns would be visible even now, above the pines, faint as scars on glass. "Whatever happened at Marrowdale, I built the shape of it. When I look at that child asleep in the cart, I'm looking at the output of my life's work. That's the arithmetic I can't finish."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. The river ran. Somewhere upstream an owl called twice, and Odile saw Cyrus tilt his head toward it, just slightly, listening, and then turn back.

"When I was nine," he said, "my mother drowned in a flood that the river-gifts of two villages might have prevented, except both the singers had already been taken. I didn't know that then. I learned it later, from rats, actually—from old rats, who remember everything and tell you only when they feel like it." His mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "For twenty-six years I've hidden what I am so well that sometimes I forget it's there. I've told myself it's wisdom. Caution. The cleverness of small creatures. But sitting here listening to you, I think maybe it was always shame wearing caution's coat. I hid because some part of me believed the decree. Believed I was a wound." He looked at her across the embers. "And you hid your guilt the same way. Carried it alone, counted it alone, in your sleep, in the dark, where no man could hear."

"Yes," Odile said. It was the easiest word she'd said all night.

"That's the trick of it, isn't it," Cyrus murmured, almost to himself. "Shame and secrets keep the same rules. Never speak where anyone can hear. And they both eat you the same way, slowly, from inside, until there's not enough left to stand up with." He poked the fire. "My mother used to say grief shared is grief halved. I thought it was sentimental nonsense. I've been carrying mine twenty-six years and it weighs exactly what it weighed on the first day."

"What are you saying, smuggler?"

He looked up, and in the firelight his face was young and old at once. "I'm saying you counted ten last night and eleven tonight, and I heard both, and I'm still here. I'm saying the child in that cart will grow up someday and learn what the uniform means, and when she asks you—and she will, children always ask—I'd rather she ask a woman who said it out loud once, to a friend, by a fire, than one who swallowed it her whole life and poisoned quietly." He shrugged, awkward, unaccustomed to tenderness and handling it like something breakable. "That's all. Burden shared. My mother again. She was full of sayings."

Odile found that her eyes were wet. She let them be. There was no one to see but a smuggler, a sleeping child, a crow, and a mule—and she was beginning to suspect the mule understood more than any creature had a right to.

"Thank you," she said, and meant the whole impossible weight of it: thank you for hearing, for not flinching, for not absolving her either—he hadn't, notably, hadn't said *it wasn't your fault* or *you did what you could*, hadn't cheapened it with comfort. He had just taken hold of one side of the thing and lifted.

Cyrus nodded once and lay back against his pack, hat over his eyes. "Sleep, Dame Odile. I'll take first watch. The owl says the valley's quiet." A pause. "Mostly quiet."

She slept, and for the first time since Kellin's Hollow, she did not count.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of wet ash. They were striking camp when the crow went up off Tovi's shoulder all at once, shrieking—not alarm exactly, Odile thought, watching Cyrus's face as he listened to it, but something structured, deliberate, a message with grammar.

Cyrus stood motionless in the middle of the clearing, head cocked, and Odile saw the color leave him.

"What is it?"

"Crows from the high road." His voice had gone thin. "Dogs. War dogs, a full pack, moving down the gorge toward us. Big ones. Trained." He swallowed. "And Odile—they're not barking like trackers. They're singing. Under the skin, under the sound. The way Tovi sings." He turned to her, and his eyes were haunted. "Those dogs are Gifted. Leashed and collared and hunting their own kind."

Behind them, far up the valley, a horn sounded—closer now, no longer patient.

And down in the cart, Tovi sat up with her eyes wide open, already humming, already answering.
