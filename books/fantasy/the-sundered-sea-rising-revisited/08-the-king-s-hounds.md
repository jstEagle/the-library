## 8. The King's Hounds

The gorge had a name once, Cyrus supposed. Most things in Thornfell did, before the crown decided names were dangerous. Now the maps called it nothing at all—a brown scar cut through the highlands, walled in slate and pine, with a stream running shallow over its stones like a vein that had forgotten what it carried.

They came into it an hour past dawn, five of them and a mule: Odile limping on her bad knee, Pell muttering compass-bearings to himself the way other men prayed, Tovi asleep against the cartographer's back with her arms looped around his neck like a child's version of a garrote. Sera's children were not with them. That thought still arrived some mornings like a stone dropped down a well—Cyrus never heard it land.

He walked at the rear because that was where he'd always walked. Rear of caravans, rear of conversations, rear of every life he'd ever brushed against. From the rear you could watch the whole procession and no one watched you back. It was a discipline twenty-six years in the making, and he was good at it, and this morning the discipline saved his voice for what was coming, though he didn't know that yet. What he knew was simpler: the birds had gone quiet.

Not fled. Fled was normal—fled was hawks, or weather, or the crack of Odile's knee on wet rock. Quiet was different. Quiet was the forest holding its breath around something walking through it, and Cyrus had spent a lifetime learning the difference between an empty wood and a listening one.

"Pell." He kept it low. "Stop."

Pell stopped. The mule, whose name changed depending on which of them was talking to it—Odile called her Bastard, Tovi called her Buttercup, and she answered to both with equal contempt—stopped too, ears swiveling forward.

"What is it?" Odile's hand went to the sword she wasn't supposed to have anymore, the one she carried anyway, wrapped in sacking across her back like a corpse she couldn't bury.

"Listen."

Tovi woke without being shaken. That was the thing about her that frightened Cyrus most—not the crows, not the open singing, but the way she surfaced from sleep whole and instant, like something born in a country where sleeping deeply got you killed. She was eight years old and she already knew what silence meant.

"Dogs," she said.

And then they heard them too. Not barking—barking would have been almost kind. This was the sound of many bodies moving fast through undergrowth with the noise deliberately pressed out of them, a trained silence, a *hunted* silence turned inside out, coming up the gorge from the south in a long gray line.

"Off the trail," Odile said. "Now. There was a scree slope half a mile back—"

"No," said Cyrus.

Everyone looked at him. He almost never contradicted Odile outright; their arrangement ran on her giving orders about survival and him giving orders about everything else, and the two jurisdictions rarely overlapped. But he was staring down-gorge now, head tilted, listening to a language only he could hear, and his face must have shown something, because she didn't push.

"They're not hunting us," he said. "I mean—they are. But that's not what I'm hearing."

"Cyrus." Pell shifted Tovi higher on his back. His missing fingers made a hook of his right hand, and when he was frightened he tucked it against his chest like a bird folding a broken wing. "If you're about to tell me the dogs are friendly—"

"They're not friendly. They're—" He searched for it. The words for it existed in the beast-tongue but not properly in any human mouth, and translation was always a small violence. "They're *leashed*. Not rope-leashed. Something else. Something inside them."

The hounds came into view then, and the sight of them stopped even Odile's breathing.

There were nine. They moved down the gorge trail in formation, which was wrong—dogs don't do formations—and they were enormous, gray-and-black, heavy through the chest and skull, with the deep-set eyes of animals bred for one purpose and given no others. Their handlers rode behind them: six men in the king's charcoal livery, crossbows resting easy in their saddles, and at the head of the column a rider on a black mare who sat differently from the rest. Straighter. Quieter. Like a man attending his own funeral and finding the service satisfactory.

"Down," Odile breathed, and they dropped behind a fallen slab of slate, and Cyrus did the last thing in the world he wanted to do.

He opened himself all the way.

* * *

Here is what speaking to beasts costs, and why Cyrus had built his life around never paying retail: it is not words. Words are the smallest part. To speak truly to an animal you must open the door of yourself and let it look in, and whatever it sees looking back, that is the conversation. Twenty-six years of whispering to rats had taught him exactly how much of himself to show—two fingers' width of soul, cracked like a shutter, enough for *rockslide, go around, there's grain in it for you* and nothing more.

To reach dogs at three hundred paces through stone, he had to take the shutters off entirely.

What poured into him nearly folded him double.

Nine minds, and every one of them screaming. Not pain—pain he knew how to hold, pain was just another dialect—but *wrongness*. The sensation of being two creatures at once. The hounds were Gifted. All nine of them. Born with the same door in them that Cyrus had been born with, the same thin place between their selves and the world's voices—and someone had found it, and instead of killing them or leaving them wild in the hills, someone had built a harness for it. A leash made of their own gift, knotted so that the very sense that let them hear the world also delivered the world straight into their handlers' hands. They could smell lies. They could smell fear, and grief, and the particular scent of a Gifted mind working—which is how they'd tracked Cyrus's band across half of Thornfell, because every night when he spoke to the mule, he lit up in their noses like a bonfire.

And underneath all of it, under the training and the leash and the terrible competence, they were lonely. Lonely the way he had been lonely for twenty-six years, except they had never known anything else, had never once in their lives spoken to another creature without a handler's fist closed around the conversation. One of them—the eldest, gray-muzzled, moving at the point of the formation—had a memory that recurred like a sore tooth: a hillside, wind, a litter of pups, and herself as a pup among them, before the men in charcoal came. In the memory she could hear the wind *saying* things. In the memory the world talked to her constantly, warmly, indiscriminately, the way it talked to Cyrus.

In the memory she was happy, and she could not have told anyone why the memory hurt.

Cyrus came back to himself gasping, forehead against the slate, Tovi's small hands gripping his shoulders.

"Mister Cyrus?" Her voice was very steady, which is how he knew she was terrified. "You went somewhere."

"The hounds," he managed. "Tovi, the hounds are—"

"I know." She said it simply, the way children say the unbearable. "I've known since yesterday. They sing wrong. Like birds in a cage that learned the cage is part of the song." Her grip tightened. "You can't fix it. Can you?"

He looked at her—at this eight-year-old who had lost her village to the same machinery now bearing down on them, who sang to crows in full voice because no one had ever taught her shame—and lied to her the way you lie to children, which is to say badly.

"Maybe," he said.

* * *

The gorge narrowed half a mile on, exactly as Odile remembered, pinching down to a slot barely wide enough for the mule, walled in slickstone. It was a perfect place for an ambush, which is precisely why the hunters weren't worried about one; the hounds would announce any trap hours before it mattered. Odile laid out their options with the flat economy of a woman reading her own death warrant: fight six crossbows in a killzone and die; scatter into the high scree and be run down piecemeal by animals that could smell Gift-work at a thousand yards; or keep moving and meet the column head-on somewhere worse.

"There's a fourth option," Cyrus said.

Every face turned to him. Behind him the mule stamped, and he felt her opinion arrive in his mind unbidden, blunt as a thrown rock: *the tall one has a plan, the plan smells like blood.*

"It costs us time we may not have," he went on. "And it might not work. And if it works, we lose our best advantage, because after today they'll know exactly what I am and what I can do."

"We're already fugitives with a price on every head," Pell said dryly. "Our advantages are largely theoretical."

Cyrus explained it in four sentences, and Odile stared at him the way she had stared at him only once before, the night she'd confessed to the purges by firelight—like a woman recalculating the size of the world.

"You want to *talk* to them," she said. "The king's hounds."

"I want to talk *with* them. And there's a difference, Dame Vasseur, and it's the whole difference."

* * *

They set it in the narrows. Pell took Tovi and the mule up a goat-path only he had spotted, high on the eastern wall. Odile positioned herself with her unwrapped sword across her knees behind a boulder, because she was Odile, and a plan that depended entirely on talking was, in her professional judgment, a plan with a sword-shaped hole in it. Cyrus walked out into the middle of the gorge trail alone, sat down on a flat stone like a man waiting for a ferry, and began, quietly, to speak.

Not to the hounds. Not yet. He spoke first to the gorge itself—to the stream, to the ravens roosting in the pines above, to the pikas in the talus slopes, asking, requesting, *not commanding*, because command was the hunter's grammar and he had sworn off it before he was ten years old. The ravens lifted in a ragged black sheet and began to scream. The pikas started whistling from a hundred hiding places at once. The stream, asked politely, found a new channel over loose shale, and the noise of it doubled.

By the time the column rounded the bend, the gorge was deafening.

The captain reined in. Up close, Cyrus saw him properly for the first time: a lean man of perhaps fifty, weathered to the color of old rope, with a face that had been handsome once and had since been used hard for purposes unrelated to beauty. His eyes were pale gray and extraordinarily tired. On his left wrist, under the cuff, Cyrus could see the edge of something metal.

"Hold," the captain said, and his voice was quiet and carried perfectly, the voice of a man accustomed to being obeyed by things that could not argue. Nine hounds fanned out around Cyrus in a crescent, hackles up, teeth showing—and Cyrus felt them *hesitate*. Felt the leash strain against what they were smelling, because what they were smelling was not fear.

What they were smelling was *hello*.

"You're Oyelaran," the captain said. "The rat-speaker of Thornfell. My hounds say you're the loudest thing in this province." A pause. "They also say—" and here something crossed his face, quickly, like a fish under ice, "—something I'm told I should report rather than repeat."

"Then don't repeat it," Cyrus said. "Captain...?"

"Voss. Rell Voss." He dismounted. That was unexpected. He walked toward Cyrus slowly, crossbow-men tracking from horseback, and stopped just outside arm's reach, and studied Cyrus the way Pell studied coastlines. "Do you know what my hounds told me about you? Not the scent-report. The other thing. The thing the leash lets through whether I want it or not."

"No."

"They said you sound like home." Voss's jaw worked. "Twenty-two years I've handled these animals. Twenty-two years, and they have never once said anything to me that sounded like *home*, and then this morning a smuggler sits down on a rock in the middle of nowhere and they tell me he sounds like home. Do you understand what that does to a man?"

Cyrus understood. Understanding rose in him cold and total, like well water. Because Voss's wrist-cuff hadn't been decoration, and the exhaustion in his eyes wasn't the honest fatigue of a hard campaign—it was the specific, corroding tiredness of a leashed thing. The hounds were Gifted and conscripted. So was their handler. Somewhere in the capital, years ago, men in better offices than this one had found Rell Voss's thin place and knotted a leash through it, and everything since—the purges, the burnings, the twenty-two years of obedient horror—had been done by a prisoner guarding a prison, telling himself it was duty.

"You could let us pass," Cyrus said.

"I could lose my family their pensions. I could send the next handler, who will be worse, because the next handler will not be me." Voss's hand drifted, unconsciously, to his cuffed wrist. "You think I haven't done the arithmetic? Every fugitive I don't catch is a village that burns hotter next season, to teach the province we're still serious. You're not buying mercy, rat-speaker. You're buying *time*. Time bought with other people's villages. I've made that trade so long I dream in it."

Behind the boulder, Odile shifted—he heard the leather of her grip creak, heard her heart hammering, heard the eldest hound hear it too and turn her gray muzzle toward the stone. Cyrus spoke to her then, directly, openly, the first fully-open conversation of his entire hidden life, shutters gone, door flung wide:

*Sister. Gray-mother. Look at me. Look at all of me.*

She looked. The crescent of hounds stilled. And what passed between Cyrus and the nine of them in the following minute cannot be written in any alphabet—only that he showed them the rats of Thornfell rerouting a caravan around death; showed them Tovi singing to crows with her face turned up like a plant to sun; showed them the Door in the drowned sea that Pell believed in with his nine remaining fingers; and asked them nothing. Asked for nothing. Just stood in the open, whole, the thing he had spent a lifetime refusing to be, and let them see that such a thing could exist.

The eldest hound lay down. Then the second, and the third, like dominoes falling in slow motion, nine huge bodies settling onto the gorge stones in surrender—or refusal; the distinction, Cyrus understood, was theirs to make, and they had made it in the only language their masters allowed them.

"She refuses the hunt," Voss said. His voice had gone strange. Flat. He was looking at his hounds the way a man looks at water rising around his boots. "Twenty-two years. She has never refused."

"Because nobody ever gave her anything worth choosing," Cyrus said. "Until this morning, Captain, nobody ever *talked* to her. They only listened through her."

Voss was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, the flatness had cracked and something older and rawer showed through. "I have to write a report. I have to write it tonight. If it says *refused*, there are protocols. You don't want to know the protocols."

"So write something else."

"And when the next hunt comes through this gorge, and the one after, with handlers who aren't me?" He shook his head slowly. "You've bought days. Maybe a week. Take your people north through the scree—I'll report the gorge flooded, trail washed out, hounds lost the scent in the water. It's even true; your stream's been singing loud enough to drown a legion." He turned away, then stopped. "Oyelaran. The thing my hounds said. About how you sound." His back was to Cyrus, and his cuffed wrist was visible now, and the metal under the sleeve caught the light. "No one has ever sounded like home to me either. Not once in fifty years. I'd forgotten it was a thing that could happen to a man."

Then he mounted, and whistled his hounds up—all but standing there a heartbeat too long, watching the gray-muzzled eldest look back at him from the trail—and the column turned south and was swallowed by the pines, and the gorge went quiet in the ordinary way at last.

* * *

They didn't speak until dusk, camped high in the scree where fires couldn't be seen. Tovi fell asleep against Odile's shoulder mid-sentence, which was how they learned she'd been awake for thirty-one hours. Pell traced tomorrow's route in the dirt with his hook-hand, north by northwest, toward the lowlands, muttering about salt.

Cyrus sat apart, as always, at the rear of the firelight. But for the first time in twenty-six years the sitting-apart felt less like discipline and more like a wound.

"You bought us a week," Odile said, settling beside him. Not asking. Offering company the way she offered everything—bluntly, and meaning it.

"I bought us a week and left nine prisoners behind bars." He watched the fire. "They chose me, Odile. For one minute on that trail, they were free, and I walked away and let them march back south."

"You couldn't free them. Not with words. Not with anything you have."

"I know what I *couldn't* do." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "That's not the same as it costing nothing."

Odile was quiet awhile. When she spoke, it was the voice she used for confessions. "I left people behind too. Whole garrisons of them. Men who did what I did because the alternative was unthinkable, and I told myself for eleven years that thinking it was impossible." She looked at him sideways. "It isn't impossible. That's the worst discovery of my life. You can always think it. And then you carry everyone you didn't save, and you walk north anyway, because the walking is the only part you can actually do."

Cyrus looked at her—really looked, the way he had looked at the gray-muzzled hound, doors open—and thought: *this is what it costs, standing in the open. This is the price, and it's charged daily.* He thought about Voss's tired gray eyes, and the metal beneath the cuff, and the word *home* passing between species like contraband.

Far to the south, faint on the night wind, a hound began to howl. Not the hunting cry. Something longer, lower, that rose and held and broke and rose again, and the ravens in the dark pines stirred and answered it, and Cyrus sat with his arms around his knees and listened until it faded, unable to shake the feeling that it had not been a farewell at all.

It had sounded, he thought, like a promise. Or like a warning.

North, in the lowlands, Pell said the wells had begun to taste of salt.
