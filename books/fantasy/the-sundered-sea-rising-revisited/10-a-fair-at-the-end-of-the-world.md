## 10. A Fair at the End of the World

Cyrus smelled the town before he saw it: woodsmoke and frying onions, tar and tallow, the sour-sweet reek of a place where too many people had lived too close together for too long without anyone telling them what to do.

He had grown up believing that smell meant danger. In Thornfell, crowds meant eyes, eyes meant questions, questions meant the Gift-hunters' ledgers. Twenty-six years of instinct told him to turn the band off the road, cut wide around whatever settlement squatted at the bottom of this green valley, and lose themselves in the hills until it was safe to come down again.

But Odile stood at the crest of the ridge with her hand shading her eyes, and she was smiling.

It was such an unexpected sight that Cyrus nearly checked her for fever. He had known Dame Odile Vasseur for two months now—had dragged her half-dead out of a ditch, had watched her eat rats' rations without complaint, had listened to her confess by firelight to things that made his stomach turn—and in all that time he had counted her smiles on one hand. This one was small and crooked and tired, but it reached her eyes.

"Greben's Hollow," she said. "Or what's left of it. The crown burned the garrison three years ago when the toll-reeve tried to tax the river traffic twice. The soldiers left. The merchants stayed." She glanced at him. "Nobody governs here, Cyrus. Nobody *can*."

"Then who keeps the peace?"

She tapped the side of her nose. "You'll see."

* * *

The road descended through orchards gone wild, apples fattening unpicked on branches that no lord's men had stripped for tithe. Tovi rode Pell's cart with the map-tubes clutched to her chest like kindling, her crows riding the cart's frame, their heads swiveling. Since the gorge, since the hounds, she had been quieter than usual—not frightened quiet, Cyrus thought, but listening quiet, the way he himself went still when a new animal entered his radius.

"There's so many people," she said as they rounded the last bend.

There were. Greben's Hollow spilled down the valley floor along a brown river, and on this day—the fourth day of the week, someone told them later, though the day mattered to no one—it was holding a market. Not a market the way Thornfell held markets, all licensed stalls and stamped weights and a crier reading the tariff schedule. This was a fair in the old sense of the word: a chaos of canvas and rope, of carts backed up axle to axle, of a thousand voices haggling, singing, arguing, laughing. A man juggled knives for coppers while a woman beside him sold live eels out of a barrel. Children wove between the legs of oxen. Somewhere a fiddler played something fast and slightly out of tune and nobody minded.

Cyrus sat very still on the cart's bench and let the noise wash over him.

The beasts were everywhere. That was what struck him first, before the colors, before the smells. Dogs under every table. Geese in rude pens. Mules and ponies and one ill-tempered camel whose presence raised questions Cyrus decided not to ask. And beneath the ordinary animals, threaded through the crowd like a second current, the Gifted ones—a goose with too-knowing eyes, a terrier who watched the pickpockets work with professional appreciation, a piebald mare near the far gate whose thoughts came to Cyrus clear as a bell across water:

*Free here,* the mare said, to no one, to everyone, to the warm morning. *No bit. No whip. Free.*

His chest ached with something he didn't have a name for.

"You're staring," Odile murmured.

"I'm listening." He didn't correct her further. Among these four—Odile, Pell, Tovi—he had stopped pretending he wasn't what he was. It had happened gradually, the way a fist unclenches after hours of holding something too tightly. "There are Gifted beasts here. More than I've felt anywhere outside Thornfell. They know they're safe."

"Word travels." Odile's voice went carefully neutral. "Among the council, a beast-speaker is worth more than a sword-arm. Keep that in mind today."

"Why?"

"Because we're about to ask them for something." She nodded toward the far end of the fair, where the canvas gave way to stone—an old customs house, its royal seal chiseled off the lintel, the scar of its removal still pale against the weathered rock. "And the council doesn't give gifts. It trades."

* * *

They left the cart at a paddock run by a one-eyed woman who charged them nothing and asked nothing, only looked at Odile for a long moment and then spat, ceremonially, into the dirt—which Cyrus gathered from Odile's answering grin was either a blessing or a challenge, possibly both.

The fair swallowed them.

Cyrus had spent his whole life moving through crowds the way a fish moves through weeds: present, unnoticed, gone. But Greben's Hollow did not allow invisibility. People looked. People greeted. A fishwife pressed a smoked trout into Pell's hands because, she said, he'd drawn her father's shop sign twenty years ago and never taken payment. A boy of maybe ten fell into step beside Tovi, saw the crows, whistled three notes—and Tovi, before Cyrus could stop her, whistled three notes back, and the boy laughed with pure delight and ran off shouting for his sister.

Cyrus waited for the fear to close around his throat. It always did when Tovi was openly herself among strangers.

It didn't come.

Here, her crow-song was not evidence. Here it was a trick, a delight, a thing children learned from each other in alleys the way they learned to skip stones. No one glanced toward a magistrate's door because there was no magistrate's door. No one lowered their voice because there was no decree hanging over the valley requiring lowered voices.

He thought of the tollgate at Thornfell, of the neighbor's goat-dog shrieking in the hunter's iron cage, of the proclamation nailed up in fresh ink over older proclamations. *By order of the Crown, all persons harboring or concealing the Gifted—*

"Stop thinking so loudly," Tovi said, bumping his hip with hers. "Your face goes all folded."

"My face is fine."

"It's folded like Pell's maps." She took his hand, matter-of-fact, the way she'd been doing since the ash road, since the night she'd found him shaking by the fire and simply decided, with an eight-year-old's tyrant certainty, that he required holding. "Look. Sweet stalls."

They ate honey-bread at a stall whose proprietor had lost an arm to a press-gang and gained, in compensation, a booming voice and strong opinions about everything. He told them the news of the Hollow unprompted: the herring run was late; the ferry-wrights were feuding again; a delegation of hill-farmers had come down last month to petition the council about wolves, and the council had sent, instead of hunters, a woman who spoke to wolves, and now there were no wolf problems and also, somehow, a standing trade agreement whereby the wolves got the sick sheep and the farmers got warning before hard winters.

"A woman who speaks to wolves," Cyrus repeated.

The stall-keeper shrugged with his whole body. "Works better than arrows. Arrows just make the wolves careful."

*Arrows just make the wolves careful.* Cyrus turned the sentence over in his mind like a coin of unfamiliar minting. His entire life had been built on the opposite principle—that the gift was a secret to be hoarded, whispered, buried, that discovery meant the cage and the ledger and the pyre. He had built his rules like walls: never speak where men can hear, never help beyond what's needed, never be seen being what you are. And those rules had kept him alive, yes. But they had kept him alone, too, alone in a way he had mistaken for safety, the way a prisoner mistakes his cell for a fortress.

Here a woman spoke to wolves in the open and the result was not a pyre. It was a trade agreement.

"What is this place?" he asked quietly, to Odile, to Pell, to anyone.

Pell spread his nine fingers on the counter. "This," he said, "is what the kingdom looks like when the kingdom looks away. There are dozens of these, Cyrus. More every year. Every village the purges empty, every road the tolls price out of use, every honest man the crown turns criminal—it all drains downhill, and downhill is here." His missing fingers made a shape like a small dark cave. "The crown thinks lawlessness is a wound. It isn't. It's a tide."

"And you want to add a door to the sea to it," Odile said dryly.

"I want to *find* the door to the sea," Pell corrected. "Adding comes later."

* * *

The customs house had become a hall. Its great chamber, once lined with tariff desks, now held long tables, a hearth big enough to roast an ox, and a dais that held not a throne but five mismatched chairs, each occupied by a person who had clearly arrived at their authority by entirely different roads: a heavyset ferry-mistress with river mud permanently in the seams of her knuckles; a lean old man with a scribe's ink stains and a duelist's scars; a woman in hill-wool with a falcon on her shoulder that Cyrus felt immediately and deeply was *not* a falcon; a fat young man eating constantly and missing nothing; and, at the center, a gray-haired giant of a woman with a blacksmith's forearms and a face like a cliff, whom everyone else plainly feared and obeyed.

"Vessek," Odile said.

The giant woman rose. For a moment the hall went silent—not the silence of fear, Cyrus judged, but the silence of a room watching a wave decide which way to break.

"Dame Vasseur." Vessek's voice was gravel poured slow. "Three years, and you walk into my hall smelling of woodsmoke and royal ash, with a knight's sword on your hip and a hunter's cloak—" her eyes flicked to the travel-stained garment, "—on your back. Sit down before I decide whether that's an insult or an apology."

Odile sat. So did the rest of them, pulled down by gravity of the moment.

"We need passage east," Odile said. "Through the lowlands to the salt coast. Quietly. Your roads, your ferrymen, your blind spots."

"The salt coast." Vessek's eyes narrowed. "The drowned country. You're the madman's expedition, then." A jerk of her chin at Pell. "The cartographer who thinks there's a door under the water."

"There is a door under the water," Pell said, with the mild stubbornness of a man who has been laughed at by experts for thirty years and has outlived several of them.

"Mm." Vessek turned back to Odile. "Passage east costs. Everything costs; that's how the Hollow stays standing. But passage isn't your problem today, Dame Vasseur." She planted both forearms on the table, and the wood groaned. "Your problem is that you're asking favors of people whose villages you burned."

The hall's silence changed quality entirely.

Cyrus felt Odile go still beside him—not flinching stillness but the stillness of a soldier hearing her name called at muster. He realized, with a lurch, that he had known she carried guilt; she had confessed it by the fire, and he had shared the weight of it as best he could. But confession by firelight was private. This was public. This was a hall full of people who had lost what she had helped take, and every one of them was looking at her now.

"Some of you served with me," Odile said evenly. "Some of you bled because of me. Both things are true."

"They are," Vessek agreed. "Brannik."

From the shadowed end of the table, the lean ink-stained man spoke for the first time. His voice was soft as a blade leaving cloth. "Kettlewatch. Winter of the purge years. My sister's family. You signed the order, Dame Vasseur. I've seen the signature."

"Yes," Odile said. "I signed it."

"No excuses?"

"None that would interest you."

Brannik smiled without warmth. "Good. Then we understand each other." He looked at Vessek. "I say she pays."

"She'll pay," Vessek said. "Everyone pays. The question is the currency." Her gaze moved across their little band, weighing them: Pell with his maps, Tovi with her crows shifting uneasily on the cart-frame outside, Cyrus—

—and stopped.

Cyrus had seen that look before. On Sera's face, the last time, in the rain, right before she said his name aloud to a hunter. The look of a person doing arithmetic with another person's life.

"You," Vessek said. "The beasts outside have been talking about you since you crossed the south bridge. The mare at the gate says you greeted her. The geese say you apologized for the pens. The dogs—" a ghost of humor crossed the cliff-face, "—the dogs mostly say you smell honest, but dogs will say anything for a scratch."

The hall was staring at him. Dozens of faces. Cyrus's hands wanted to shake, and he forbade them to.

"He speaks," Tovi announced proudly, from her chair, to the entire council, as if presenting a prize hog at a fair. "To everything. Even rats. Especially rats."

"Tovi," Cyrus said weakly.

"A beast-speaker." Vessek leaned back, and the falcon-that-was-not-a-falcon on the hillwoman's shoulder turned its head fully upside down to look at Cyrus, which was deeply unpleasant. "In my hall. Asking for passage." She let the silence stretch until it sang. "The Hollow has use for such a tongue. Wolves, river-things, the deep-flock that raids our nets. We've paid gold for lesser gifts and gotten liars."

"I'm not for hire," Cyrus said. It came out steadier than he felt.

"Everything's for hire. The only question is the price." Vessek spread her enormous hands. "So. Here is the shape of it. Passage east, quiet roads, ferries that don't log names—we grant it. In exchange, Dame Vasseur stands judgment before this council. Full accounting of the purge years. Open session. Whatever verdict we reach, you accept, or the bargain dies and you walk east alone through country that wants you dead."

"That's not passage," Brannik said softly. "That's a trial."

"That," Vessek said, "is what justice costs when there's no king to pay for it. Someone has to hold the scales. Might as well be us." She turned back to Odile. "Well, knight? Three years ago you enforced the crown's law. Here we keep a different one. Will you answer to it?"

Cyrus's heart was a drum. He looked at Odile—at the woman who had confessed her crimes to him in whispers, who had carried them like stones in her pack, who had chosen mercy against her training on the day they met and every day since. He watched her jaw set.

Before she could speak, Cyrus heard his own voice enter the silence of the hall.

"She'll answer," he said. "For everything she did. All of it." He met Vessek's eyes, then Brannik's, and found, to his own astonishment, that he was not afraid—or rather, that he was afraid, and speaking anyway, which he was beginning to suspect was the same thing as courage. "But if this council judges her, it hears all of her. What she did *and* what she's done since. Both ledgers, or none."

Vessek's eyebrows climbed slowly toward her hairline. Beside him, Odile had gone very still.

"You'd speak for her," Brannik said. "Knowing what she is?"

"I know exactly what she is." Cyrus's hand found Odile's under the table. "That's rather the point."

The hall held its breath. Vessek looked at Odile, at their joined hands, at Tovi beaming, at Pell already sketching something on the edge of a napkin. Then she laughed—a short, grinding sound, like a millstone starting to turn.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "Full council. Open session." Her eyes settled on Cyrus with the weight of a falling wall. "And you, beast-speaker, can stand beside her and say your piece. Let's see if the Hollow's law bends for love."

Outside, the fair roared on, oblivious—music, haggling, children, the free mare dreaming her dream of open hills—and Cyrus walked back into it holding the hand of a woman bound for trial, understanding suddenly that he had glimpsed another world and that every world, even this one, demanded its price.
