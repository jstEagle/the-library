## 12. Rell Voss at the Ford

The river announced itself before they saw it—a smell of cold stone and melted snow, a change in the birdsong, the mules lifting their heads with sudden purpose. Cyrus had been listening to the water for two days through the mouths of smaller things: a dipper's report of the current, a kingfisher's grudging account of the fish. The ford was real. The ford was passable. The ford, every feathered informant agreed, was empty.

Cyrus no longer believed in empty.

"Slow down," he said, and Pell reined the cart to a walk, and Odile moved up beside it without being asked, her hand resting easy on the pommel of the sword she'd taken from a dead man whose name she'd made Tovi promise to remember. They had crossed half the lowlands on salt-tasting water and burnt-map rumor, five fugitives and a mule named Barley who had opinions about everything, and Cyrus had learned that the crown's hunters did not announce themselves. They arrived like weather.

Tovi rode in the cart bed among the maps, her legs swinging, a crow perched on each shoulder like a pair of dark epaulets. She had named them. This was the part that still frightened him most—not the singing itself but the naming, the open affection of it, as if loving a thing out loud were not the fastest way in the world to lose it.

"The water says there's gravel under the middle," Tovi reported. "And something dead upstream. A deer. The crows are excited."

"Tell them they can have it after we're across," Cyrus said.

"They already know." She grinned at him, gap-toothed, unbothered. "They just like being told."

He looked at her a moment too long. *That was me once,* he thought. *A child who talked to animals because no one had yet taught her what it would cost.* Except no one had ever taught Tovi anything; she had simply never learned to be afraid, because Sera's betrayal and the burning villages had come after the singing was already rooted in her, and roots that deep did not pull out clean.

Odile caught his eye. She'd gotten good at reading his silences these past weeks—the knight without a banner, learning the language of a man who'd spent his whole life speaking in one. "You feel something," she said. Not a question.

"I feel a ford that's too quiet," Cyrus said. "No frogs. No midges over the shallows. Water that size should be loud with life this time of year."

Pell, hunched over the reins with his eight fingers, went pale in the way he did when geography confirmed dread. "Hunters clear the banks," he said. "It's in the old purge manuals. Nothing flies, nothing sings, nothing moves within a league of where they mean to strike."

"Then we don't cross at the ford," Odile said.

"There is no other crossing for forty miles," Pell said. "And behind us—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Behind them was smoke, and the road they'd burned their names off of, and the slow certainty that Rell Voss did not lose people. He collected them.

Cyrus closed his eyes and let his hearing drop down into the small world, the whisper-world, the place he'd lived since he was four years old and a rat taught him that words could be a hiding place instead of a wound.

The river gave him nothing. That was the wrongness of it—rivers never gave nothing. Even poisoned water spoke through its insects. This silence had edges. It had been *made*.

"He's here," Cyrus said. "Or he will be by morning."

* * *

They came down to the ford at first light, because there was nowhere else to go, and because Odile argued—and Cyrus hated how much sense she made—that a hunter who has chosen his ground waits better than he chases. The mist lay on the water like a shroud laid out neat for viewing. The far bank rose in grey terraces of slate, and on the highest terrace, black against the brightening sky, sat riders. Six of them. Motionless. Patient as stones.

And below them, along the near bank, spaced like pieces set on a board, stood the hounds.

Cyrus's breath went out of him.

There were nine. He knew them instantly, individually, the way you know voices—the scarred bitch with the torn ear, the big brindled male who had once held another dog's scruff to stop a fight rather than start one, the young one who dreamed in colors. He had spoken to them in the gorge outside Harrow Fen. He had heard what was inside them: minds like banked fires, Gifted, awake, leashed with iron-and-word collars that turned their knowing into a hunter's instrument. He had bought their escape with a lie about rabbits and left them standing in the dark, haunted ever since by the sound of their going—obedient, silent, *still leashed*.

Now they stood between him and the water, and their handler was not with them, and Cyrus felt the leash-words humming in the air like a struck string that hadn't stopped ringing.

"Tovi," he said quietly. "Get in the cart bed. Under the maps."

"I'm not—"

"*Under the maps.*"

She went, muttering, and the crows went with her, folding themselves into the cart's shadows like ink poured back into a bottle.

Odile dismounted and drew her sword—not raised, just drawn, the way a man lays a truth on a table. "Captain Voss," she called across the water. Her voice carried the way it always did, parade-ground clear. "Dame Odile Vasseur requests parley."

"You have no banner to request anything," said the man on the high terrace, and came down.

Rell Voss rode like a man poured into his saddle, unhurried, precise. Up close he was younger than his reputation—thirty perhaps, with a long, careful face and eyes the colour of river ice. He stopped his horse at the water's edge and regarded them across the shallow braided channel, and Cyrus saw what he had been too hunted to see in the gorge: the way Voss's gaze kept flicking, tiny involuntary flicks, toward the hounds. Toward the trees. Toward the *dipper* working the shallows twenty feet downstream, which should not have been there, which Cyrus had asked to be there.

Watching a man watch the world talk was like watching someone try not to look at their own hands.

"You're the beast-speaker," Voss said. His voice was flat, official, and underneath the flatness something strained, like ice under weight. "Oyelaran. Thornfell. There's a price on your name that would buy a ship."

"Names are cheap this year," Cyrus said. "Everything's contraband. Goats. Maps. Children who sing."

Something moved at the corner of Voss's mouth. Not quite a smile. "I burned my first village at nineteen," he said. "Do you know why I was given the commission?"

"No."

"Because I find things." He said it simply, terribly. "I have always found things. I walked into the quartermaster's records vault at Vail when I was twelve years old and told the clerk which drawer held the falsified ledgers, because the *paper told me*. Because everything tells me. Every lock, every trail, every lie has a voice, and I have never once in my life been able to make it *stop*." His hand tightened on the reins, a small spasm he immediately stilled. "The crown did not give me my gift, smuggler. The crown gave me a use for it. And a leash."

The word landed on the bank like a dropped stone.

"The hounds," Cyrus said.

"The hounds are what I was, before they promoted me." Voss looked at the nine dogs ranged along the water, and for one unguarded instant his face was naked—grief so old it had fossilized into duty. "Gifted beasts, conscripted at the whelping. Collared with binding-verse so their knowing serves the throne. I trained them. I wrote half the collar-liturgies myself. And in the gorge, when your rabbits scattered them—" His jaw worked. "When they came back to heel, all nine of them, they came back *different*. Quieter. Like men returning from somewhere they'd been told not to speak of."

"They weren't different," Cyrus said. "They were *asked*. For the first time in their lives, someone asked instead of commanded. It changes the shape of a mind. You'd know that, if anyone had ever—"

"Do not." The ice cracked clean through, one flash of it, and then Voss was smooth again, smooth as a frozen pond, and Cyrus understood suddenly that this man's stillness was not discipline. It was a cage built from the inside. "You have until the mist lifts. My orders are to take the child alive and the rest as found. If you surrender her, the others walk."

Behind him in the cart, under the maps, Tovi began—very softly—to hum.

* * *

The song was a crow-song, one of the counting songs, and it carried under the mist like a current under ice. The crows rose from the cart in silence—no cawing, nothing so crude—and split around the ford like smoke around a hand.

Voss's riders reached for their bows. The hounds did not move.

That was the thing Cyrus would remember afterward, telling it later by firesides that didn't exist yet: the hounds did not move. Nine leashed animals, bound by iron-and-word to a throne's command, stood at the water's edge while crows wheeled above them, and their ears turned—not to their handlers, not to their collars, but to the *song*.

*Tovi,* Cyrus thought. *What are you doing?*

But he knew. He had watched her sing to crows in a burning village's shadow; he had watched her give her gift away like sweets because hoarding it had never once occurred to her. She wasn't attacking. She was *introducing herself*. She was saying, in the only language the deep places of the world respected: *you are not alone in here either.*

The young hound broke first. It took three steps toward the water, whimpering, its collar sparking blue-white where the binding-verse fought the wanting. Then the scarred bitch. Then the brindled male, and he did not whimper at all—he walked to the river's edge and sat down, deliberately, in plain view of the riders, and looked up at Rell Voss.

Looked at him the way you look at a mirror.

"You should call them off," Odile said quietly. She hadn't moved. Her sword point rested toward the ground. "Before they break, Captain. It's kinder."

"You think I can?" Voss's composure was gone now, stripped away, and what was underneath was so tired it made Cyrus's chest ache. "I *wrote* the liturgies. Do you know what happens to a handler whose hounds turn? What the tribunal asks? What they *find*, when they look into a Gifted officer too closely?" He laughed, short and awful. "They ask who else the gift talks to. They always ask that."

"Then don't be there when they decide," Cyrus said. He waded forward into the shallows, cold biting his shins, close enough to speak without carrying to the terrace. Close enough to see that Voss's hands were shaking. "You found us. Fine. You're the best finder the crown ever leashed. Now find yourself a door, Captain. Because the crown is erasing everything that talks—beasts, children, drowned kingdoms, *you*—and the day your finding gets inconvenient, there won't be a village far enough or a leash tight enough."

"You presume to know my life."

"I presume to know the *shape* of it," Cyrus said. "Because I lived inside that shape for twenty-six years. Whispering. Hiding. Calling it safety." He glanced back—at Odile standing open-faced in the shallows with her sword down, at Pell gripping the cart rail, at the lump of maps under which an eight-year-old was still humming, fearless, *audible*. "I hid so well I nearly died of it. Don't mistake a cage you built yourself for a country."

For a long moment the only sounds were the river and the hum, and the small blue sparks dying on nine collars as the binding-verse lost, thread by thread, to something older than verse.

Voss looked past Cyrus at the hounds—at the brindled male sitting in the water, soaked to the chest, unmoving, *choosing*—and Cyrus watched a man watch his own reflection refuse to obey.

"The mist is lifting," Voss said at last, mechanically, and indeed it was, drawing back off the water like a curtain. He turned his horse. "Riders! To me. We've lost the scent—the river's cut it." A pause. The lie cost him visibly, like passing a kidney stone. "We'll cast wider downstream."

His officers hesitated—one of them, a hard-faced woman with a sergeant's knots, looked from the hounds to the fugitives to her captain and clearly did not believe a single word—but they wheeled and followed him up the terrace, because that was what the leashed did.

All except the hounds.

Nine dogs remained at the water's edge. Nine collars sparked once more, together, and then—Cyrus felt it happen, felt it the way you feel a knot slip loose—went dark. Not broken. *Released.* Whether the verse failed on its own or whether somewhere inside himself Rell Voss finally spoke the counter-word he must have known all along, Cyrus never learned. But the scarred bitch shook herself, walked to the cart, and lay down beneath it like a soldier taking off her boots at last.

Voss did not look back at them. But at the top of the terrace he stopped his horse, one hand raised against the risen sun, and spoke without turning—his voice carrying flat across the water, stripped of rank, stripped of everything:

"A warning, since I owe the crown one less debt today. They're not hunting you anymore, Oyelaran. You were never important enough. The purges, the burnings—it's all forage. They're gathering gifts. Every beast, every child, every leashed thing they can carry, driven east to the capital." His hand closed slowly into a fist. "They're raising something there. Something that needs feeding. Whatever door your madman thinks he's found—get through it before the crown finishes building whatever's on the other side of *theirs*."

Then he rode, and his riders rode, and the ford belonged to the river again.

* * *

They crossed in the long gold light of morning, the cart axle groaning, the hounds swimming beside the wheels in a loose wedge like an honor guard. Tovi emerged from under the maps flushed and triumphant and was promptly scolded by everyone, which she bore with the serenity of the genuinely heroic.

"He let us go," Pell said, for the fourth time, steering through the braided channels. "Why? He could have taken us. He *should* have."

"Because we showed him something he already knew," Cyrus said. He was walking beside the cart, and the scarred bitch paced at his knee, and every few strides her shoulder brushed his leg—deliberate, warm, asking nothing. "That the leash goes both ways. That whoever holds it is holding it too."

Odile rode down from the point where she'd watched the terrace long after it emptied. Her face was troubled in a way Cyrus had learned meant thinking, not fear. "He said the capital," she said. "He said they're raising something. I've seen the requisition ledgers, Cyrus—cages, chain, choir-rosters. I thought it was purge logistics." She met his eyes. "What kind of thing does a kingdom build out of stolen voices?"

Ahead of them the road ran east through the salt-stained lowlands, toward wells that tasted of the sea, toward a coast no map admitted to. The hounds fanned out around them, free and silent, and somewhere behind, growing fainter with every mile, the capital waited with its mouth open.

Cyrus put his hand on the scarred bitch's head, and thought: *whatever they're building, it eats exactly what we are.*

"Hurry," he said, though the mules needed no telling. The whole shore of the world seemed already to lean that way—eastward, toward the water, toward the Door—like beasts who had finally been called home.
