## 13. The Drowned Bells

Pell had been chasing salt for eleven years, and he had learned to read it the way other men read scripture.

It began in the mortar of old walls—salt blooming white through the joints like frost that refused to melt. It crept into wells a finger's width deeper each year. It lay in the hollows of stones along the high passes where no sea had come in living memory, glittering there on mornings after rain, and if you licked your thumb and touched it and put your thumb to your tongue, it tasted of a depth that had no business being under a mountain range.

The crown's surveyors called it mineral contamination. The priests called it a sign of the world's slow rotting, one more proof that everything outside the King's Peace was already dying. Pell called it what it was: a tide-line, rising. And beneath every salt-line he ever traced, when he pressed his ear to the stone at dawn, when the wind was right and his heart was quiet—he heard bells.

Not ringing. Not yet. A bell that has been drowned does not ring; it *remembers* ringing. It hums its old note into the water around it, low and patient, the way a widow keeps her husband's coat on a hook. Eleven years Pell had listened to that humming through stone and soil and the roots of firs, following it west and north through country that grew stranger with every league—the trees leaning all one direction as if combed by a wind from the sea, the birds flying patterns that made no sense until you laid his maps over them and saw they were tracing coastlines.

He had lost two fingers to the last purge, and he had traded those fingers for this map, in a sense, because everything he had done since—the exile, the cliffside workshop, the years of silence—had been payment toward it. And now here he sat on a ridge of black rock with three fugitives and a child who sang to crows, looking down at a coast that did not exist.

"I've checked," he said, mostly to himself, because he had been checking for an hour and could not stop. "Every chart. Every atlas the Guild would sell me and half the ones they wouldn't." His voice came out strange, thin with awe. "There is nothing here. The cartographers drew ocean past this point and stopped. *Nothing*, as though the ink itself were afraid."

Below them the land simply ended—not in cliffs, but in a long descending shelf of dark stone, terraced, stepped, running down and out into a grey sea that lay flat as hammered pewter. And the sea was wrong. Pell had sailed the southern coasts in his youth; he knew how a sea breathed, how it swelled and sighed against land. This water did not breathe. It lay against the shore like something listening.

Tovi heard it first. Of course she did.

"It's singing," she said. She stood at the edge of the shelf with the wind pulling her hair eastward, and she was smiling in that unguarded way of hers that made Cyrus flinch every time, because it was everything his gift had never been allowed to be. "Down there. It's not the water. It's—" she tilted her head, listening with her whole body, the way she listened to crows. "Bells. Lots of bells. All different sizes, all different songs, but they're all trying to sing the same thing."

"Do you know what they're saying?" Odile asked quietly.

"No." Tovi's smile dimmed a little. "But they know I'm listening. They got louder when we topped the ridge."

Pell's hand found the map tube at his hip without his willing it. He had drawn this coastline forty times over eleven years, from salt-lines and bell-hum and the testimony of gulls—and every drawing had been a hypothesis, a faith, an act of stubbornness against every credible authority in the kingdom. He had expected to feel triumph. He had expected, at the end, to be right in the way scholars are right: satisfied, vindicated, a little smug.

Instead he felt like a man standing at his own grave and finding it full of light.

* * *

They went down onto the shelf as the tide withdrew—which was the first impossible thing, because Pell had watched the tide for two days and it obeyed no moon. It withdrew like a held breath released, sliding back off the land in sheets, uncovering more than any tidal chart could explain. A hundred yards. Two hundred. The grey water peeled away from the dark stone and left it gleaming, and the stone, up close, was not stone.

"Look at the grain," Pell said, kneeling. He ran his eight fingers across it. "This is dressed work. Cut and laid. This whole shelf is a road."

"A road to where?" Cyrus asked. He had hung back at the top of the descent, arms crossed, wearing the look he wore whenever hope presented itself—suspicious of it, watching it for the trap inside. But he had come down anyway. He always came down. That was the thing about Cyrus Oyelaran that Pell had catalogued early, the way he catalogued everything: the man's suspicion was a kind of devotion. He examined hope so carefully because he intended, despite everything, to keep it alive.

"Down," Pell said. "Out. Toward the sound."

They followed the road as it descended in long shallow ramps, and the ramp walls rose on either side of them, and the walls were cut with niches, and the niches held things.

Lamps, first—thousands of them, their glass clouded with silt, their wicks long gone to nothing. Then plaques. Pell stopped before one and wiped the wet grime away with his sleeve, and the letters underneath were in no alphabet he knew, curved and flowing, but beneath them, worn nearly smooth by water, someone had carved a second inscription in Trade-tongue, the old pidgin smugglers still used in Thornfell's deepest warrens:

*WE WERE HERE AND THE WATER DID NOT ASK OUR LEAVE.*

He read it aloud. None of them spoke for a while after that.

Odile broke the silence, and her voice had the flattened carefulness she used when she was standing on her own history. "How long ago?"

"Longer than the kingdom," Pell said. "Longer than the kingdoms before the kingdom. There are records—a merchant's diary from four hundred years back mentions 'the sunken parishes' as already legend. But the salt says otherwise. Salt doesn't lie about age; it only accumulates. Some of these deposits—" he touched the bloom creeping up the wall beside him, white and fine as sugar— "are older than the trade roads. This place didn't drown recently and it wasn't drowned by accident." He looked up at the terraced walls climbing away above them, tier on tier, vanishing into the grey haze where the sea waited at the edge of its long breath. "A city this size doesn't sink. It's *put* underwater. Someone decreed it."

"Like Thornfell," Tovi said.

Nobody answered her, because there was nothing to say that wasn't yes. Pell watched Cyrus turn away and stare down the empty road, and he recognized the set of the man's shoulders—he'd seen it on refugees all his life, the posture of someone looking at a mirror made of their own worst fear. The Gift-hunters burned villages. Whoever built this place had drowned a city. The methods changed; the decree did not. History, Pell thought, was not a wheel or a ladder. It was a tide. It went out and came back, and each time it came back it wore away a little more of whoever stood lowest on the shore.

* * *

By late afternoon they reached the rooftops.

That was how Pell thought of it afterward, and how he wrote it in his field journal that night, though the words looked absurd even to him: *We walked down into the sky of a buried city.* Because that was what it was. The road ended at a vast descending bowl, a harbor basin miles wide, and rising out of its floor—uncovered now by the retreating water, streaming and dripping, gleaming dull silver-grey in the failing light—were spires.

Hundreds of them. Towers and steeples and the masts of ships that had gone down fighting, all of it furred with weed and crusted with salt, all of it pointing up at a surface that had abandoned it. Streets ran between the spires in grids and spirals, visible now like the veins of a leaf, and down the middle of the widest avenue ran a channel—a canal? an aqueduct?—and in the channel, impossibly, water remained, pooled deep and black and utterly still, holding the inverted image of the sky.

And the bells. Now that the water had drawn back from them, they rang—or rather, they resumed a ringing so old and slow it had decayed into something between sound and weather. Each spire that bore a bell gave off its note, and the notes braided together into a chord that Pell felt in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his missing fingers, in the backs of his eyes. It was not loud. It was enormous, which is different. It was the sound of a thing that had been counting the years and had finally decided to stop waiting.

Tovi walked to the edge of the bowl and spread her arms, and she sang—one clear note, high and simple, a child's nonsense melody she'd made up on the road somewhere in the lowlands—and the chord shifted. Bowed toward her. Thousands of drowned bells adjusting themselves by fractions toward a girl of eight, the way flowers find the sun.

"They know the song," she said, wonder-struck. "Or—they want to learn it. They're asking me to sing it again."

"Don't," Cyrus said sharply. Then, softer, catching himself, gentling his voice the way Pell had watched him gentle it around animals: "Not yet, little bird. Let's see where we're standing first."

But Pell had seen the exchange, and he filed it away as he filed everything: the bells knew her. Or knew what she was. The Door below—his Door, his life's obsession—was singing to a Gifted child, and the crown's hunters were three days behind them at most, and none of these facts were separate. He was beginning to understand that they were all one fact.

They made camp that night on a terrace halfway down the bowl's rim, in the shell of what might have been a customs house—it still bore, carved above its doorway, scales whose pans had been filled centuries ago with concrete. Odile took first watch. Cyrus sat apart, as always, at the campfire's margin, and Pell settled next to him deliberately, because some observations required proximity.

"You knew," Cyrus said, before Pell could speak. "Eleven years. You knew this was real and you let us walk into it anyway."

"I hoped it was real. Hoping and knowing are different trades." Pell turned his palms up, the eight-fingered hands, the cartographer's hands, ruined and precise. "I'll tell you what I know now. The salt is rising everywhere in the kingdom—the wells, the cellars, the mountain passes. The borders aren't dissolving because the realm is failing. They're dissolving because something underneath is coming *up*. And that city down there was drowned by decree, and my guess—the guess of a man who's spent his life reading water—is that whatever drowned it is the same thing the crown is raising at the capital. Voss warned us."

"Voss warned us about the crown," Cyrus said. "Not about the sea."

"The sea is older than the crown." Pell poked the fire. "Everything the kingdom has ever done—every purge, every border, every burning—it's all been a dam, Cyrus. Built on a flood plain. And dams don't hold forever. They just decide *where* the water goes when they break."

Cyrus was silent a long time. Down in the bowl, the bells hummed their vast slow chord, and the black pools held their upside-down stars, and somewhere in the drowned streets fish moved through doorways where children had once been sent to bed.

"My mother used to sing," Cyrus said at last, so quietly Pell almost missed it. "Not to me. To the rats in the walls. She thought I didn't remember." He stared into the fire. "I always thought the gift was mine. Something I invented, alone, in the dark, because I was alone. Sitting down there—" he nodded toward the drowned city— "listening to those bells answer a child like it was the most natural thing in the world. I don't think I invented anything. I think I'm an echo. Of something much bigger that they tried to drown."

Pell said nothing. There was nothing to add, and cartographers know better than anyone that the truest lines on a map are often the ones you leave undrawn.

* * *

Near midnight, the tide returned—but wrongly.

Pell woke to Odile's boot nudging his shoulder and the sound of Tovi crying softly, and he came out of the customs house to find the water climbing the bowl's inner walls faster than any tide had any right to move, silent, starlit, filling the streets from below like ink poured into a vessel. It swallowed the lowest rooftops. It climbed the spires. And as it rose, the bells' chord changed—tightened, brightened, resolved—until for one moment, one impossible moment, the whole drowned city rang out clear.

Not a hum. A peal. Eleven years Pell had chased a memory of ringing, and now it rang, overhead and below, from every tower at once, a sound like the world's largest grief and the world's largest joy occupying the same instant—and then the water covered the highest bells and the sound sank beneath the flood, and the sea went flat and silent again, keeping its secret.

Except that now it had shown them where to look. In the exact center of the bowl, where the deepest channel converged, the water had risen around a single structure it had previously left veiled: a dome, vast and ancient, crowned with a spire of pale stone that caught the starlight and threw it back wrong—not reflecting it but *holding* it, glowing faintly, like a lamp lit from within by something patient.

Tovi had stopped crying. She was staring at the dome, and she was humming the crow-song, and Pell could have sworn—even knowing what he knew, even being a man of measurements—that the glow pulsed gently in time with her tune.

"That's it," Pell whispered. His voice shook. Eleven years, two fingers, one ruined life, and here it was at the bottom of a drowned world, lit like a candle in a well. "That's the Door."

Cyrus stood at the terrace edge, firelight behind him, and the glow from the dome touched his face, and Pell watched something cross it that he had seen only once before—on the face of a drowning man he'd once pulled from the surf, at the exact moment the man stopped fighting the water.

"Cyrus?" Odile said.

Cyrus didn't answer her. He was looking at the dome, and the dome, Pell thought, was looking back.

"It's been calling me," Cyrus said. "My whole life. Every whisper I ever gave an animal—I thought I was speaking. I was answering."
