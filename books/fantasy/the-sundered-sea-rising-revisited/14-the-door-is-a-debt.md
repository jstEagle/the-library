## 14. The Door Is a Debt

They came down into the drowned city at dawn, when the tide was at its lowest and the rooftops stood black and dripping above the waterline like the spines of buried animals.

Cyrus went first. He had not decided to go first; his feet had simply made the decision somewhere in the dark hours before, while he lay awake listening to the bells. They rang beneath the water still—Pell swore it was current dragging through hollow towers, Odile said it was nothing but wind over stone—but Cyrus knew better. The bells were speaking a language he did not know and yet understood completely, the way he understood the mules and the rats and the hounds: not with words but with the shape of wanting.

*Come down,* the bells said. *Come down. Come down.*

Behind him, Pell picked his way along a rooftop ridge with his eight fingers spread for balance, map-case strapped to his back like a hump. Tovi rode on Odile's shoulders, her small hands knotted in the knight's collar, her crows wheeling overhead in a loose gray spiral. The girl had been quiet since the coast appeared—a silence Cyrus recognized because he had worn it himself for twenty-six years. It was the silence of someone holding something too large for their chest.

The water between the rooftops was green-gold in the early light, and beneath it he could see streets. Lamps still hung from iron brackets, their glass clouded with a century of silt. Doorways opened onto staircases that descended into forever. A child's wooden horse lay on its side in what had been a gutter, and Cyrus found himself unable to look away from it as they passed.

"They had warning," Pell said softly. His voice carried strangely over the water, flattened by the fog. "That's what the old accounts say. Three generations of rising salt, three generations of the crown calling it lies. Then one spring the wells turned brackish in the capital itself, and the court packed up and moved east and told everyone else to stay put."

"And they stayed," Odile said.

"People always stay." Pell's missing fingers flexed against his thigh. "You have a house. You have your dead in the ground. Where would you even go?"

Cyrus thought of Thornfell, of Sera's children, of the villages burning on the ash road, and did not answer.

They descended by way of a great staircase that broke the surface at low tide, each step furred with weed and barnacled at the edges. By the time they reached the dry streets below—the water withdrawing behind them with a long sighing sound, like something exhaling—the sun was fully up, though down here it came only in shafts, falling through gaps in the collapsed upper stories and standing in the gloom like pillars of honey-colored glass.

It should have been terrifying. It was, a little. But mostly it was beautiful, and that frightened Cyrus more. This was a whole world erased by decree—not burned, not hunted, simply abandoned to the sea and struck from every map until men like Pell spent lifetimes proving it had existed. He thought of the proclamation nailed to the Thornfell tollgate. He thought of how easily a country could decide what was real.

The beasts were already here.

He felt them before he saw them—the pressure of other minds, other hungers, brushing against the edges of his gift like fish against a swimmer's legs. Fish, yes, and crabs, and pale eels that slid through flooded doorways. Gulls nesting in the broken belfries. Rats—of course rats, there would be rats at the end of the world—in colonies so vast he could feel them as warmth, as a single slow heartbeat under the stones.

But there was something else. Something older. Everything here, every creature in the drowned city, was oriented the same way, the way iron filings lie around a lodestone. All of them, without knowing it, faced the same direction.

Deeper in.

* * *

They followed the facing-creatures through streets where seawater pooled in the gutters, past a marketplace whose stalls had rotted into lace, past a fountain where a stone mermaid held out her hands to catch rain that never came. Tovi began to hum. Cyrus felt the song before he heard it—a thin bright thread of Gift running through the melody—and this time he didn't flinch. None of them did. The crows settled onto ledges and listened. Even the eels rose near the surface of the pools, motionless, their faces lifted.

"She's been humming it for two days," Odile murmured to Cyrus as they walked. "Since we saw the first rooftop. Do you know what it is?"

"No." But he did, a little. It was the bells. She was singing the bells' language back to them, an answer across a hundred years of drowning, and whatever conversation the two of them were having, it was about him. He could feel that too, the way you can feel eyes on your back. He had spent his whole life being looked *past*. Now he was being looked *at*—by a child, by a city, by something vast and patient underneath everything.

The street ended at a wall.

Not a wall—a façade, the front of a building so large that Cyrus had taken it for a cliff face, so covered in sculpted figures that at first glance the stone seemed alive. Men and women, carved life-size, ascending spirals toward a peak far above the fog line. And every figure's mouth was open. Every figure was singing. A thousand singers in stone, frozen mid-note, climbing toward—

Toward the Door.

It stood at the summit of the façade where the sculpture converged, though *stood* was the wrong word. It *was*, the way a person is rather than a thing. An archway of black material that was not obsidian and not iron and not wood, tall enough that a cathedral could have passed through it, and it did not open onto darkness or light or the street behind. It opened onto *depth*. Looking at it was like looking down a well that had no bottom and no walls, only a kind of patient attention, and Cyrus understood with absolute certainty that it was looking back.

His knees nearly went. Odile caught his arm without comment.

"It's real," Pell whispered. The cartographer had gone gray. Eighty miles of salt roads, ten years of obsession, and now that it stood in front of him he could barely breathe. He sank to his knees in the wet street and laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound. "Oh, you stubborn old liar, you were right. You were right all along."

"Whose?" Odile asked, hand drifting to her sword out of pure habit. "Whose door? Who built it?"

"The old realm," Pell said. "Before the crown. Before the purges, before the decrees, before anyone decided the Gifted were contraband." He wiped his face with his sleeve, and Cyrus realized the man was crying without noticing it. "This is where they lived. This is what the kingdom drowned and denied. The Sundered Sea isn't a legend, Dame Vasseur—it's a *graveyard*, and this—" he pointed up at the arch, and his hand shook— "this is the headstone. Or the gate. I don't know which."

"I do," Cyrus said.

They all turned. He hadn't meant to speak. The words had come up out of him like water finding a crack, drawn by the Door's attention, which had settled on him now entirely, wholly, the way the sun settles on the one spot of ground where it means to burn.

"It told me," he said. "On the road. In the wells. In the bells. I thought I was following Pell's maps." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, ragged. "I wasn't following anything. I was being *called*."

* * *

They made camp in the shell of what had been a temple, on the steps below the façade, because none of them could bear to leave and none could bear to approach. The tide came in during the afternoon and rose halfway up the carved singers' bodies, and when it receded the Door remained, unchanged, attentive, waiting.

Pell spread his maps on the temple floor and worked feverishly, sketching the city, measuring the façade with knotted string, filling pages with the ecstatic handwriting of a man who has waited his whole life for one hour. Odile cleaned her sword and then, finding nothing else to clean, sat sharpening a knife she had already sharpened twice. Tovi slept curled against the knight's side, and even in sleep she hummed.

Cyrus walked.

He told himself he was scouting. He was good at telling himself things; twenty-six years of practice. But his feet took him back to the base of the façade again and again, and each time the Door's attention gathered on him like warmth, like weight, and each time he stayed a little longer.

On his fourth return, it spoke.

Not in words. Never in words—it used the same channel he used with beasts, the wordless exchange of want and meaning, except that where a rat's mind was a small warm room, this was a hall the size of the sky. And what it showed him was simple. Terribly simple.

*A price.*

Images unrolled through him like tide: himself, standing in the archway. The gift leaving him. Not suppressed, not hidden, not muted the way he had muted it every day of his life—but poured out, emptied, given *wholly*, the way you cannot give half a cup if the cup must be shattered. He saw the waters part. He saw a passage east, dry stone underfoot where the sea had stood for a century. He saw Odile and Pell and Tovi walking through, into lands beyond the kingdom's reach.

And he saw himself after. Standing on the near shore. Deaf.

The silence of it hit him before it was even real—a silence so total it had texture, weight, a silence like snowfall burying a house. No mule-trains grumbling their complaints. No rats negotiating. No hounds. No gulls. No Tovi, her crow-song bright as struck flint. Nothing. Just the ordinary human dark that every unblessed man and woman in Thornfell had lived inside all their lives, the dark he had pitied without ever once envying.

He sat down hard on the wet stones.

"Cyrus." Odile. She had followed him—he'd known she would; she always did, now, and some traitor part of him had begun to count on it. She lowered herself beside him, sword-side habitually away, the way she'd learned sat easier with him. "What did it show you?"

"How did you know it showed me anything?"

"Because you've got the look Voss's hounds had. When they heard him coming." She said it plainly. She had learned, over these weeks, to say plain things. "What's the price?"

He looked up at the Door. The carved singers climbed past it into the fog, mouths open, mid-hymn, a thousand years into a note.

"It opens for a Gifted one who gives the gift up," he said. "All of it. Not locked away—gone. Surrendered into the sea." His voice was steady. He was almost proud of how steady it was. "And then it stays open long enough for others to pass. Anyone. Everyone. It just needs—one payment."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the tide sighed against the stairs.

"There'll be another way," she said finally. "There's always—"

"No." He shook his head. "Dame Odile. Odile." Her name in his mouth still felt new, still felt like a coin he hadn't earned. "I've been running my whole life. Hiding, muting, whispering in cellars where no man could hear. My one rule. And the whole time—" he gestured at the city, the graves, the erased streets, the burning country behind them— "the whole time, the thing I was hiding from was also the thing I was walking toward. Every route I ever chose bent east. Every client, every job, every smuggler's trail. I thought I was clever. I wasn't clever. I was *summoned*. This door has been calling me since before I could talk, and everything I built to hide from it just turned out to be the road."

"That's not destiny," Odile said. "That's exhaustion talking. You're tired and it's offering you a story where your whole life makes sense. That's what those things do." She nodded at the arch, and Cyrus heard something in her voice he had never heard there before: fear. Not of the Door. For him.

"Maybe." He smiled, and it cost him. "But here's what it doesn't do, Odile. It doesn't lie about the price. Beasts don't. Water doesn't. It showed me exactly what I'd lose, down to the last gull. And do you know what I felt? When I saw myself deaf?"

She waited.

"Relief," he said. "God help me. Twenty-six years carrying a secret heavy enough to crush a village, and for one moment I imagined putting it down, and it felt like—" His voice failed. He tried again. "It felt like Sera's kitchen. Before everything. Warm bread. Being nobody special. Being safe."

Odile's hand found his. Her palm was scarred and rough and it did not let go.

"You are nobody special," she said. "You're Cyrus Oyelaran, smuggler, liar, rat-charmer, the worst horse-trader I've ever met, and the only reason any of us is alive. That's worth more than every beast from here to the capital, and if you give that up thinking you're buying our passage with it, then you've miscounted, because what you actually carry isn't the gift. It never was."

He turned to look at her. Fog beaded in her cropped hair. Behind her, high on the façade, the stone singers sang their silent hymn, and the Door waited with the patience of deep water.

"Then why does it feel like the gift is the only true thing I own?"

"Because you were taught that," she said. "By a country that burns children for singing to crows. They taught you your inheritance was contraband. It isn't. And neither is the rest of you."

He wanted to believe her. He was astonished to find how much he wanted it, and how much that wanting terrified him—because wanting things was how you got hurt, wanting things was why Sera's betrayal had cut to the bone, and he had built his whole life on needing nothing.

"Tovi," he said suddenly.

"What?"

"The Door wants *a* Gifted one. It doesn't say who." His stomach turned as he said it. "It called me. But it will take whoever walks through willing. And that child has been humming its song for two days, Odile. She hears it clearer than I do. Children always hear clearer than—"

"No." Odile was on her feet before he finished, and the fear in her voice had become steel. "No one is sacrificing a child. No one is sacrificing anyone. We find another—"

"There is no another." He stood too. Below them, in the shallows of the flooded street, the eels had risen again, and the gulls had gone silent, and every living thing in the drowned city was watching him with the same patient, terrible attention. "There's the debt, and there's the debtor, and there's the door. And I think—" He stopped. Listened. Beneath the bells, beneath the tide, beneath Tovi's distant sleeping hum, something vast turned over in the deep and spoke one wordless sentence straight into the marrow of him:

*Soon.*

"—and I think it's done waiting," Cyrus finished quietly.

From the direction of the temple, faint through the fog, Tovi's humming stopped.

Then the child screamed.
