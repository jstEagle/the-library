## 15. Tovi's Choice

The Door had been singing to her for three days.

Tovi heard it first as a thread of sound beneath the tide, thin as a crow's whisker-bone, rising out of the drowned city every time the water pulled back. It wasn't a song like the ones she sang to her crows—those were quick and bright and full of names. This was older. Slower. It sounded like a bell that had been ringing underwater so long it had forgotten it was a bell and thought it was the sea itself.

*Come,* it said. Not in words. In the way her mother's lullabies used to say *sleep*, all wrapped around in warmth, in the feeling of being wanted somewhere.

She hadn't told anyone at first. She told her crows, though. She always told her crows. She sat on a fallen lintel above the flooded street where they'd made their camp, with Bramble preening on her knee and old Grey-Beak hunched on her shoulder like a feathered grandmother, and she hummed what the Door sang back at them, low, so the others wouldn't hear.

Grey-Beak tilted her head. *Small one sings the deep-song,* she said, in the way crows say things—not words exactly but shapes of meaning, black and sharp-edged and true. *Deep-song is not for small throats.*

*I know,* Tovi said.

But she kept humming anyway. Because there was something else under the Door's singing, once you listened long enough. Something she'd only understood on the second night, when she couldn't sleep and crept down to the waterline alone. The Door didn't just sing *come*. It sang *one*. *One gift, given whole. One voice, poured out.* And every hour since they'd arrived in the drowned capital, Tovi had watched Cyrus look at the water with a face she recognized. She'd seen that face on people in her village, the day before the hunters came. It was the face of someone who had already decided to be spent.

Cyrus was going to give his gift away. Everyone knew it, even if nobody said it. Pell talked about the Door like a man talking about a cure for a sickness he'd carried forty years, and Odile polished her sword at night with her jaw set like stone, and Cyrus—Cyrus sat apart at the fires they lit in roofless houses, speaking softly to the gulls and the eels and the pale crabs that came up from the drowned streets, and Tovi could hear, underneath everything, how much quieter his voice got each day. Like a man putting his house in order before he burned it down.

He was going to walk into the water and give away the only thing that had ever been truly his, the thing that had saved them all—the rats in Thornfell, the hounds in the gorge, every beast on the shore when the crown came riding—and he was going to do it because that was what Cyrus did. He hid. He took the worst of everything and folded it up small and tucked it inside himself where no one could see, and then he called it staying safe.

Tovi had been mute for two years after her village burned. She knew all about folding things up small.

* * *

On the third morning, the tide went out farther than it ever had, farther than Pell said it should, baring streets of green-black stone that no living person had walked in three hundred years. And the Door stood open at the end of them—a doorway of white coral grown through the bones of a tower, tall as three men, its threshold washed by a hand's depth of clear water—and it sang so loudly that Tovi pressed both palms over her ears and it still came through, through her skin, through her teeth, through the hollow places in her chest.

*One gift. Given wholly. The way opens.*

Nobody else could hear it. She'd asked. Pell heard bells sometimes, he said, and Odile heard nothing at all, and Cyrus just looked at her very carefully when she mentioned it, with an expression she hated, an expression like pity, like she was something breakable.

So she didn't mention it again. She made her plan instead, the way she'd planned how to get out of the burning village: quietly, completely, alone.

She would go down at slack tide, when the water over the threshold was shallowest. She would stand in the Door and sing her gift out of herself—all of it, the crow-song, the deep-listening, the way beasts' hearts opened to her like doors—and she would give it before Cyrus could. He was bigger and older and his gift was heavier, but the Door hadn't said *the biggest gift*. It had said *a gift*. Hers counted. She was almost sure hers counted.

And then Cyrus wouldn't have to be empty. Because she'd watched him these last weeks, really watched, the way you watch a fire to see which way it will spread. When he spoke to animals, he wasn't hiding anymore. His shoulders came down. His eyes went soft. On the shore the night he called every Gifted creature within leagues, hounds and gulls and mules and ten thousand rats pouring out of the dark to stand beside him—he had looked like a man hearing music after decades of deafness. He'd wept without knowing he was weeping.

That was not a thing you gave away. That was a thing you built a life around.

Hers was smaller. She could spare it. That was the arithmetic she did in the dark, lying between Odile's bedroll and Pell's maps, and it balanced, it balanced perfectly, if you didn't count the part of her that screamed.

She counted that part too. She just decided it didn't get a vote. Grown-ups made decisions like that all the time. She'd watched Sera do it—Sera, whose name Cyrus still couldn't say without his voice going strange. Sera had traded Cyrus's name for her children's lives, and everyone said it was wrong, and everyone also said they understood, and Tovi had learned from watching that love made people do enormous terrible arithmetic and call it sense.

Her screaming part said: *you're eight, you don't even know all your songs yet.*

Her other part said: *exactly. Better to spend it on something now than lose it to a hunter later.*

And a third part, quietest of all, the part that sounded like Grey-Beak, said: *small one, the deep-song does not want the smallest coin. It wants the truest.*

She told the third part to be quiet too.

* * *

She went at dawn, before anyone woke, or thought anyone woke. But Grey-Beak woke. Grey-Beak always woke. The old crow lifted off her shoulder as Tivi climbed down the broken stair into the bared street, and Tovi whispered, "Stay. Stay hidden. If you follow me I'll never forgive you," which was a lie, because she'd never forgiven anything in her life, but Grey-Beak landed on a drowned chimney-pot anyway and waited, feathers puffed against the cold, one black eye fixed on her the whole way down.

The street under the retreating sea was slick with weed and littered with shells that crunched like teeth. Fish traps hung open in doorways. A stone face leaned out of a wall, worn smooth, its mouth eroded to a soft O, forever singing. The Door grew louder with every step until it filled the world, until Tovi's bones hummed with it, until she felt less like a girl walking toward something than a drop of rain walking home to the ocean.

At the threshold she stopped. The water ran over the sill, clear as glass, warm as bathwater, impossibly warm this far north. Beyond it the Doorway held darkness—not the dark of night but the dark of the inside of a shell, pearlescent, waiting.

"I'm coming," she told it. Her voice came out tiny. She cleared her throat and tried again, louder, the way she sang to crows. "I'm coming, and I've brought my gift, and it's a good one. It found my village when the soldiers came. It called three hundred crows. It—" her voice cracked. "It's mine. I'm giving it on purpose. Nobody's making me."

The Door's song gentled. *Little voice,* it seemed to say. *Little bright voice.*

And then a hand closed on her shoulder, and she was spun around, and Cyrus was kneeling in the wet street in his shirt with his boots in his hand, having run barefoot over shell-streets, and his face was white as bone.

"Tovi." Just her name. He was breathing hard. Behind him, up the stair, Grey-Beak sat on the chimney-pot looking insufferably calm, and Tovi realized with a surge of fury that the crow had gone and fetched him. Traitor. Oldest friend in the world and a traitor.

"Let go," Tovi said. "You don't get to stop me. You don't get to decide."

"No," Cyrus agreed. His voice shook. He didn't let go. "Neither do you."

"It's MY gift!"

"Yes. And it's yours tomorrow, and next year, and when you're twenty, and when you're eighty, if I have anything to say about it." He pulled her back from the threshold, one step, two, until the singing faded enough that she could hear the real sea again behind it, and he knelt down in the shallow water so their faces were level, heedless of his soaked trousers. His hands were trembling. She noticed that. She filed it away like a weapon.

"You think I don't know what you were doing," he said. "You think I haven't done the same sums? *My gift is smaller. Mine can be spared. I'll spend me so he doesn't have to.*" His voice cracked on the last words. "Tovi, that's not sacrifice. That's just another kind of hiding. The same trick I've been doing my whole life, dressed up brave."

"That's not fair."

"No," he said. "It isn't fair. Nothing about any of this is fair. The hunters weren't fair, and the burning villages weren't fair, and the Door asking for the one thing none of us can replace isn't fair." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist; whether it was seawater or the other thing, Tovi couldn't tell. "But listen to me. Listen." He took her by both shoulders. "When you gave your songs to your crows after the village burned—when you sang them back to life—did it feel like losing?"

She blinked. "No."

"When you sang in the market-town, openly, in front of strangers, and the whole square went quiet—did it feel like shame?"

"No."

"And when you taught me that gull-call outside Saltmere, and I laughed for the first time since Thornfell—what did that feel like?"

She looked down at the water swirling around their knees. "Like flying," she admitted, very quietly. "Like I was flying."

"That's your gift," Cyrus said. "Not the sound. The flying. And I will not stand here and watch you cut off your own wings so an old smuggler doesn't have to feel sad." His grip softened. His thumb brushed her hair back from her face, the way her mother used to. "You want to protect me? Then grow up. Grow loud. Grow into every note you've got, and then come find me on the far side of whatever comes, and *sing at me*. Sing at me until I'm sick of it. That's how you save me. Not this."

Tovi's eyes stung. "Then who?" she demanded, hating him, loving him, hating the Door, hating the whole rotten unfair world. "If not me, then who pays for the way out?"

Cyrus was quiet for a moment. The tide stirred around them. Far above, gulls began their morning arguments along the drowned rooftops.

"Me," he said simply. "Because it's mine to give. Because I've been hiding for twenty-six years and I'm tired, Tovi, I am so tired of whispering. Because the Door isn't taking my gift from me—it's letting me put it down." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, and it was the bravest thing she'd ever seen. "And because you have sixty more years of flying ahead of you, and I won't take one minute of it. That's what family is. It's not everyone throwing themselves on the fire. It's everyone agreeing whose turn it is."

Tovi threw her arms around his neck and cried into his shoulder, furious, salt-faced, eight years old, and Cyrus held her while the Door sang its patient, ancient hunger, and neither of them moved.

Behind them, boots splashed. Odile stood at the edge of the bared street in her mail, sword at her hip, Pell beside her with his map-case clutched to his chest, and both of them wore expressions that said they'd heard everything.

"The tide turns within the hour," Pell said quietly. "Whatever's going to happen has to happen now."

Odile drew her sword—not at them, just enough that the steel caught the light. "And we're not letting him go down there alone."

Cyrus set Tovi on her feet. He looked at each of them in turn—Odile, Pell, Tovi, Grey-Beak spiraling down from the chimney-pot to land on the girl's shoulder—and something in his face settled, like a man closing a ledger he'd been keeping all his life.

"No," he said. "You're not."

Far out beyond the drowned rooftops, across the gray morning water, a horn sounded—long, deep, unmistakable. Once. Twice. A third time.

Odile went rigid. "That's not a hunting horn," she breathed. "That's an army."

Pell turned toward the sound, his face draining. "They're not coming for us," he whispered. "They're coming for the Door."
