## 16. The Crown Rides Out

Dame Odile Vasseur had stood at the edge of a great many battles, and she had learned to read them the way Pell read maps—the way the light fell on armor, the way birds left a valley before the drums did. The morning the crown came to the drowned coast, she woke before dawn because the gulls had gone silent.

She stood in the doorway of the salt-ruined customs house where they'd made their camp and looked east along the tide-line, where the water lay low and strange over rooftops that no map admitted existed. Below her, in the drowned streets of the old realm, Tovi was singing to the crows—openly, fearlessly, her voice carrying across the green shallows—and the crows were answering from every broken spire.

And beyond them, far out on the grey water, the light was wrong.

Not dawn-light. Lantern-light. Hundreds of lanterns strung along hundreds of masts, riding in formation like a city that had decided to move.

"Odile." Cyrus came up beside her, quiet as he always was, though she had long since stopped being startled by it. He looked out at the fleet, and his face did the thing it always did when the world confirmed his worst assumptions—that terrible calm, like a man watching weather he'd predicted years ago. "How many?"

"Forty ships, maybe more." She counted by habit. It was the counting that had gotten her knighted once; it seemed fitting that it should damn her now. "Enough."

"Enough for what?" Pell asked from inside, his voice tight. He emerged with his maps clutched against his chest like a breastplate, nine fingers white-knuckled on the oilskin. "We're at the end of the kingdom. There's nothing behind us but sea. What can they possibly—"

"They're not here for us." Odile heard her own voice go flat, the voice she'd used for twenty years when delivering orders she didn't believe in. She hated hearing it now, aimed at these people. "Look at their heading. They're not making for the shore. They're making for the Door."

* * *

They gathered on the customs house's shattered balcony while the fleet grew out of the haze—black-hulled transports, siege barges with cranes folded flat, and at the fore a long galley flying the royal standard, gold sun on black, the banner Odile had once knelt beneath with her sword held up and her heart empty of doubt.

"They mean to seal it," Cyrus said. "Don't they. The crown would rather drown the way out than let anyone use it."

"It's not the way out they fear," Odile said. "It's what's on the other side of it." She had spent three days listening to Pell explain the Sundered Sea, the old realm erased by decree four hundred years past, and she had recognized the shape of it immediately. A country that had been Gifted too richly, too openly. A king who had decided that such a people could never be subjects—only a threat. So the borders closed, the waters rose—or were raised, if you believed Pell's oldest charts—and history was rewritten until even the maps forgot. "The crown burned Thornfell because it was afraid. Do you think it will be less afraid of an entire drowned nation?"

"But the Door—" Pell's voice cracked. "It opens *east*. It's been waiting four centuries—"

"And Voss has orders to make sure it waits forever." Odile turned from the rail. Her hip ached where the purge officer's mace had found her in Thornfell, six weeks and another lifetime ago. "Stone and mortar. Sinking the passage. Perhaps something worse—I've seen the siege barges carry things I couldn't name, back when I signed the manifests myself." She said it plainly, because she had promised them that much: her guilt laid open, itemized, never hidden again. "Whatever they brought, they brought enough of it."

Tovi climbed up onto the balustrade, small and bird-boned, her crow perched on her shoulder like a second shadow. "Then we stop them," she said, with the simple ferocity only children and saints could manage.

Odile crouched to her level. "Sweeting, there are forty ships down there and five of us."

"The hounds," Cyrus said quietly.

Everyone turned. He was looking west, inland, his eyes gone distant in the way Odile had learned meant he was listening to something no one else could hear. "The ones we left in the gorge. They've been following us. All this way." A pause. His jaw worked. "And not just them. Crows. Mice. Something big moving through the flooded streets below—things that lived down there before the drowning and never left." He blinked, and came back to himself, and looked at Odile with an expression she had never seen on him before. Not fear. Not calculation.

Resolve.

"I spent my whole life whispering," he said. "Keeping my voice so small no one could hear it. If they're going to seal the door on every Gifted thing in this kingdom—" he gestured at Tovi, at the crows wheeling above the spires, at the invisible chorus only he could hear, "—then I'm done being quiet."

* * *

The royal host began landing at midmorning.

Odile watched from the high street of the drowned city—a road that ran along a ridge of old masonry just above the tide, flanked by rooftops like the backs of sleeping animals. The soldiers came ashore in disciplined waves, pikes and pavises, engineers with instruments she remembered too well from the purge campaigns: sounding lines, demolition charges, crates stamped with the seal of the Office of Borders.

At their head rode Captain Rell Voss.

Even at distance she knew him—broad, unhelmed, his scent-hounds loping at his stirrups. And she saw, with a clarity that felt like a wound reopening, the thing she had refused to see at the ford: the way the hounds moved around him. Not like beasts leashed to a master. Like soldiers leashed to a commander who held their leash because someone else held his.

*I know exactly what you are,* she thought. *I wore your collar for fifteen years.*

The column split. Most of it ground toward the headland where Pell's charts placed the Door—a black archway rising from the shallows, older than any stone in the living kingdom. But one company peeled off toward the ridge, toward the high street, toward the customs house.

Toward *them*.

"Down," Odile said. "All of you. Now."

* * *

The battle in the flooded streets was unlike anything in Odile's twenty years of war, and she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it.

There was no line, no field, no honorable geometry. There were streets under a foot and a half of green water, and rooftops, and arches, and a fog rolling in off the sea that smelled of salt and old bells. The soldiers came up the drowned lanes in file, and the water itself seemed to fight them—Cyrus standing knee-deep at a junction with his hands half-curled, murmuring, and the eels and the drowned-city fish rising around the soldiers' legs, tangling, tripping, harmless and relentless as rain.

Odile fought the way she had sworn she never would again: against the crown.

The first soldier to reach the ridge was young, maybe nineteen, his pike shaking. She took him apart without killing him—disarmed him, dumped him into the flood, let the current have him—and the shame of how easily her body remembered rose in her throat like bile. *You trained me,* she wanted to shout down the street. *Every stroke of this is yours. You taught my hands and now you'll feel them.*

Pell fought too, badly and bravely, swinging a boat-hook from a rooftop and shrieking warnings in cartographer's jargon—*the arch, mind the arch, don't let them near the arch*—and Tovi—

Tovi sang.

That was the part Odile could not have imagined, could never have invented. The child stood on the highest roof with her arms spread and her voice thrown out over the battlefield like a banner, singing the crow-song, the same three rising notes Cyrus had first heard in a burning village, and the sky *answered*. Crows came out of the fog in hundreds, then thousands, a black weather front of them, diving at visors and standards, tearing banners to ribbons, driving the officers' signal-birds into shrieking confusion until no order could travel faster than a man could run.

And the hounds.

When Voss's own hounds reached the ridge, Odile braced herself for teeth—but they broke around her like water around a stone, ignoring her utterly, and went to stand at Cyrus's side. Six of them, lean and scarred and Gifted, their amber eyes intelligent and grief-stricken, and when Voss bellowed a command in the handler's tongue they flinched but did not obey.

Voss reined in at the mouth of the high street, and for a moment, in all that chaos, everything stilled.

He looked at his hounds standing with the fugitives. He looked at the crows darkening the sky. He looked at Cyrus Oyelaran, smuggler, beast-speaker, standing open and unhidden in the middle of a battlefield for perhaps the first time in twenty-six years.

"You don't understand what you're guarding," Voss called out, and his voice carried strangely—it *carried*, unnaturally, the way sound carries when a man's gift is pushing it. "I've read the sealed dispatches. My lord, I've *seen* what's behind that Door. The crown isn't sealing it to cage its people. It's sealing it because of what's coming *through*."

"Then tell us!" Odile shouted back. She stepped forward into the open, sword bare, her old knight's stance, and saw recognition move across his face—she'd been famous once, before the fall. "Tell us, Captain, and be believed! That's all anyone in this kingdom has ever asked—to be told the truth instead of purged for asking!"

For one heartbeat—one—something moved behind Voss's eyes. Doubt, or memory, or the ghost of the man he'd been before the leash.

Then the horns sounded from the headland, and the moment drowned.

* * *

What happened at the Door, Odile saw only in fragments, because she was fighting for her life in waist-deep water between two warehouse walls, holding the lane against a squad of border-engineers who had finally abandoned subtlety and begun hauling demolition charges toward the arch.

She killed for the first time since Thornfell. She felt each death land in her chest like a debt entered in a ledger she would never balance, and she kept fighting anyway, because Pell was screaming and the arch was *right there*, black stone veined with faint silver light, humming like a struck bell—

—and Cyrus walked into the water toward it.

Later, she would try to reconstruct the moment and fail. What she remembered: the fog lifting as if drawn upward. The bells beneath the sea beginning to ring—actually ring, four hundred years silent, a carillon swelling up through the flooded streets. Every beast within leagues answering, pouring out of the hills and the ruins and the deep water, filling the shore the way she'd once seen pilgrims fill a cathedral. And at the center of it all, Cyrus, small and ordinary and lit from within, speaking—not commanding, never commanding—*speaking* to the sea.

The engineers froze. Even Voss, arriving at a dead gallop, pulled up short.

And the Door opened.

* * *

Odile stood ankle-deep in the shallows with her sword point-down in the sand and watched the waters part eastward—a corridor through the sea itself, walls of green water trembling on either side, a road of wet stone running out toward a horizon that shimmered with a light no storm could account for.

The royal host did not advance. Could not. Soldiers dropped their pikes or knelt or simply stood staring, and their officers made no move to stop them, because the officers were staring too.

Voss dismounted. He walked past Odile close enough to touch, and stopped at the edge of the parted water, and Odile saw his shoulders shake once—saw the leash, whatever it was made of, strain against what he was seeing.

"Seal it," he said to his lieutenant, very quietly. "That was the order."

No one moved.

"Captain," the lieutenant said carefully, "seal it *how*?"

Voss didn't answer. He was looking at Cyrus—at the man swaying in the shallows with his hands empty and his face wet, whether from spray or tears impossible to say—and then at the hounds sitting in the surf behind him, and then, briefly, unbearably, at Odile.

She held his gaze and did not look away. *Come with us,* she almost said. *You know what waits for you if you stay.* But some debts could not be paid with invitations, and she knew it, and she thought perhaps he knew it too.

"Fall back to the ships," Rell Voss said at last, hoarse. "All companies. We hold the shore."

The lieutenant stared. "My lord—the King's order—"

"*We hold the shore.*" Louder now, and beneath it, so faint only a Gifted ear might catch it—or a woman who had spent fifteen years learning to hear the things commanders didn't say—something else entirely: *Let them go. Let them go, and may God forgive us both.*

* * *

They crossed at dusk.

Pell went first with his maps wrapped in oilskin held overhead like a relic. Then Tovi, her crows streaming after her through the corridor of water, their wings brushing the green walls and coming away dry. Then the hounds, one by one, glancing back only once. Odile went last but one, pausing at the threshold of the Door with her hand on black stone that hummed like a living heart, looking back at the kingdom that had made her and broken her and made her again.

Behind them, on the shore, the royal host stood in silence, lanterns burning, swords sheathed, a thousand men witnessing the end of the world they'd been ordered to protect.

Ahead, through the parted sea, something new.

Odile took a breath that tasted of salt and beginning, took Tovi's hand, and stepped through.

Behind her—far behind, at the edge of hearing, at the very limit of the light—Rell Voss stood alone on the drowned coast, lowered his sword, and whispered something she would not learn for a long time:

that the crown was already raising something at the capital, and that he had seen it, and that it was waking.

The water closed behind them like a book.
