## 19. The Far Shore

The first thing Cyrus learned about the far shore was that it was quiet.

Not silent — the world here was full of sound, gulls crying over grey water, wind combing through dune-grass, the far-off complaint of some animal he could not name moving through scrubland he could not name either. The sounds were all there, layered and ordinary, the way sound had always been for every other person in every other place.

What was gone was everything underneath them.

For twenty-six years, Cyrus Oyelaran had lived in a second country that no one else could see. Beneath the creak of a wagon wheel there had been the axle-mice muttering about damp. Beneath a market's roar, the dogs trading gossip like coin. Beneath his own footsteps on Thornfell stone, the slow mineral patience of the mountain itself, half-asleep, dreaming in words that were not quite words. He had never once in his life been alone in a room, a road, or a grave-yard of a town. Even in the worst years — the hunger years, the year Sera stopped speaking to him and the year she started again — even then, the rats had kept him company, chattering their small ambitious politics into the dark beneath the floorboards.

Now there was nothing. Just him, inside his own skull, like everyone else.

He had thought he understood what he was giving up. Standing at the Door with the sea roaring through the drowned streets and Voss's army drawn up on the ridgeline, he had thought: *I know the price. I have counted it.* He had spoken his surrender anyway, had felt the gift pour out of him like heat leaving a body, had watched the waters part and known, in the same breath, both grief and something so close to joy it frightened him more than the loss did.

He had not understood. You cannot understand a silence until you are standing in the middle of it with nothing between you and the rest of your life.

* * *

They came through the passage at dawn, though dawn meant little under the strange doubled light of the Sundered Sea, where the sun seemed to rise twice — once from the east over the far shore and once, pale and ghostly, from the drowned west behind them, reflected up off the parted water as if the old realm were holding a lamp to light its visitors out.

Cyrus walked last. He told himself it was to keep watch on the rear, which was a lie, because there was nothing behind them now but closing sea. He walked last because he could not stop looking back.

The Door stood open still — Pell had sworn it would hold until the tide turned, and Pell's maps had not lied to them yet — but already the passage was narrowing, walls of standing water leaning inward like curtains being drawn. Through it he could see the shore they had left: the flooded rooftops of the drowned capital, black spires rising out of green shallows, and above them, tiny with distance, the banners of the crown's host still ranked along the ridge. Somewhere down in those streets, he knew, were the hounds. His hounds, if a man could be said to own anything that had chosen him. They had held the line against the royal spearmen while the fugitives ran for the Door, and when he had called out to them one last time —

But that was the thing. He couldn't call out anymore. That last call had been the gift spending itself, every beast within leagues answering, and the hounds had turned their scarred faces toward him across the water and *heard* it, and now the channel between them was shut, and he would never know — not certainly, not in the marrow way he had always known things about beasts — whether they had made it clear.

He stopped walking. Behind him the water-curtains sighed. Ahead, on the shingle of the far shore, the others had noticed and stopped too, and he felt their eyes on his back, patient and worried, and hated that he could feel it without any gift at all, just the ordinary human art of knowing people you love.

"Cyrus." Odile's voice. She had come back down the beach for him, boots crunching on shell-sand, her sword still slung across her back where she hadn't taken it off since the battle. There was blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers. "It's closing."

"I know."

"You can't stand in a door forever."

"That," he said, "is the first true thing anyone's said to me since we got here."

She didn't smile. She took his arm instead, firmly, the way she'd once dragged him out of a burning granary in Ash Road country, and walked him forward, and he let himself be walked, because the alternative was standing there until the sea shut on the sight of home forever, and some part of him wanted exactly that, and he knew better than to trust the parts of himself that wanted things like that.

The last thing he saw through the narrowing gap was a crow. One crow, ragged-winged, flying low over the parting water toward him — Tovi's crow, the one she called Sister-of-Cinders, the one whose song had betrayed a village and saved a kingdom. It beat through the gap with a strangled cry as the sea closed behind it, tumbled, righted itself, and landed on Cyrus's shoulder with claws that bit down hard enough to mean something.

Then the passage shut, and the Sundered Sea became simply a sea, whole and unbroken, lying between two shores that would never again be one country.

The crow said something. Cyrus heard only a raw bird-noise, harsh and meaningless.

He put his hand over it anyway, careful around the wings, the way he'd done ten thousand times, and found that his hand still remembered how.

* * *

Pell cried when they reached dry ground.

Nobody remarked on it. The cartographer dropped to his knees in the dune-grass, pulled his ruined map-case against his chest, and wept with his whole body, great ugly sobs, and after a moment he drew out the stubs of his missing fingers and pressed them flat against the sand of the far shore like a man taking an oath, or making an apology, or simply confirming — *this is real, this is real, I am touching it.*

"Nine fingers and forty years," he managed finally, looking up at them with a face wrecked and shining. "Forty years I've been laughed at. Called mad. They took my fingers for less than this." He looked out past them, past the dunes, at the country beyond — and Cyrus followed his gaze and saw it properly for the first time.

Green hills going blue with distance. A river, silver, running free with no tollgate on it. Terraced fields climbing a slope, and smoke rising thin and domestic from chimneys, and — Cyrus's breath caught — beasts. Animals in the fields, oxen and sheep and a dog trotting a fence-line, living visibly, unhidden, unmolested, in the open air of someone's livelihood.

No decree nailed to any post. No hunters. No burned villages smoking on any horizon.

"It's not paradise," Odile said quietly. She said it like a soldier, like a warning, and Cyrus loved her a little for saying it. "No place is. We don't know their laws, their lords, whether they'll want us. Don't go handing them our names before we know what names cost here."

"No," Pell agreed, wiping his face with the back of his hand, leaving a streak of salt and ash. "But it's *somewhere.*" And he laughed, wet and cracked, and began, immediately, absurdly, to unpack his maps right there in the grass, spreading them against the wind, already correcting coastlines that had never before been drawn by a living hand.

Tovi did not look at any of it.

The girl sat apart on a drift-log, knees drawn up, the crow preening on her shoulder, and stared back the way they'd come. She had not spoken since the Door. She had sung — Cyrus had heard her, faintly, in the passage, a thread of crow-song pitched under the roar of the sea, the way she sang when she was keeping herself together stitch by stitch — but she had not spoken, and when Odile offered her bread she took it and held it and did not eat.

Cyrus went and sat beside her. It was what he knew how to do. Before he'd ever had friends, he'd had animals, and animals taught you the first law of company: you don't demand. You arrive. You settle. You make your body quiet and wait for the other one to decide you're safe.

So he sat. The surf worked the shingle. Somewhere inland the unnamed dog barked twice and fell silent. The crow sidled along the log toward Cyrus and stood on his knee with the proprietary weight of a creature who had decided, sometime during a battle in flooded streets, that he belonged to it.

"He didn't cross," Tovi said at last. Her voice was small and scraped hollow. "Brother-of-Salt. He stayed on the ridge. I saw him. When the water closed." Brother-of-Salt — the young hound, the one who had first answered Cyrus's call in the gorge, the one who had come back at the ford with the pack at his heels and turned the king's own trap inside out. "He looked at me. Right at me. And then the sea came down."

"I know," Cyrus said. Because he had seen it too, and because lying to her was the one cruelty he refused, always, even the kind lie.

"Is he dead?"

Cyrus was quiet a long time. "I don't know," he said. "I used to be able to ask the gulls. Gulls see everything. They gossip worse than rats." He tried to make it light and heard it fail. "I can't ask anything now. Not the gulls, not the wind, not the worms in the dirt. It's all—" He gestured vaguely at his own ears, his own chest. "Quiet."

"Does it hurt?"

He considered lying again, and again didn't. "Yes. Like losing a language. Like waking up deaf. Everything's still there, Tovi. I just can't hear it anymore. And the worst part—" his voice caught on it, surprised him — "the worst part is I remember what it sounded like. Every day for the rest of my life I'll remember, and every day I won't hear it."

Tovi absorbed this with the terrible thoroughness of eight-year-olds. Then she slid off the log, took his hand — her palm small and gritty with sand — and said, "Give me your hand. No — *give* it. Properly."

He gave it.

And she hummed.

It was the crow-song, the one she'd sung over the burning roofs of her village, the one the crows had carried league upon league, the one that had summoned a fair-town's worth of wings to a river ford. But she sang it low, almost wordless, a thread of melody passed mouth to mouth between her and the bird now perched above them — and she pressed his hand flat against her own throat so that he felt it as much as heard it, the vibration of it buzzing through skin and bone and into the bones of his hand, up his wrist, into his chest.

The crow picked it up. Harsh-throated, off-key, it croaked the phrase back, and Tovi hummed it again, and Cyrus sat there with his eyes stinging, feeling the song travel her throat into his palm, and understood what she was doing.

She was giving him back the underneath. The second country. She was telling him, in the only tongue either of them trusted completely, *it isn't all gone. Here is a piece of it. I will carry the hearing, and you carry the memory, and between us we'll manage.*

"Tovi," he said, when the song wound down and the surf filled the space it left.

"Mm?"

"When we get wherever we're getting — if they'll have us — there will be children like you. Gifted ones. Born here, maybe, or fled here the way we did. And they'll need someone who knows both sides of it. What it costs to hide, and what it costs not to." His thumb moved absently over her knuckles. "I'm deaf now. But I remember everything. Every grammar of every beast I ever spoke to. Someone should write it down before it dies in me. Someone should teach it."

Tovi looked at him with her crow-bright eyes, and for the first time since the Door, she smiled — small and crooked and slightly wicked, the smile of a child who has just been promoted.

"You'll be the teacher," she said, "and I'll be the singing."

"That's the arrangement," Cyrus agreed. "Don't let me take the singing. I'd be terrible at it."

* * *

They walked inland as the light went long and gold.

Odile ranged ahead the way she always did, checking the terraces, reading fences and footpaths the way Pell read coastlines — for danger, for welcome, for the shape of whoever held this ground. Twice she stopped them behind a rise while riders passed on a distant track; twice the riders proved to be farmers, unarmed, incurious, and moved on. The dog from the fields appeared at the edge of camp at dusk, sat down at a polite distance, and watched them with frank brown eyes, and Cyrus caught himself, three separate times, tilting his head to listen for what it was thinking, and catching nothing, and hurting, and watching it anyway.

Because that was the discovery he was making, hour by hour, in the enormous silence: he could still read beasts. Not their words. Their weight, their angle, their patience. Twenty-six years had built something in him deeper than the gift — the gift had only ever been the door, but the house behind it was his, and the crown could burn doors, not houses. The dog was curious and unhungry and had been sent, probably, by someone curious and unhungry. That much he knew. That much nobody could take.

"They're coming," Odile said, dropping down beside him at the fire. She said it without alarm, which from Odile meant she'd already counted them and liked the odds. "Four. On foot. One's wearing a magistrate's chain — or whatever passes for one here. They've been circling since midday, deciding about us." She fed a stick to the flames. "Word travels fast even in empty country."

"Should I wake Pell? He'll want witnesses to the first contact. He'll want to draw their faces."

"Pell's asleep sitting up with a pen in his hand. Leave him." She glanced across the fire at Tovi, curled against the cartographer's side with the crow tucked under her chin, and lowered her voice. "Cyrus. Whatever they ask — we tell the truth about Thornfell. All of it. What the crown did, and what we did. If they turn us away for it, they turn us away for the truth, and I can live with that. I can't live with another country built on my lies."

He looked at her — the knight without a banner, firelight moving on the old scars, offering herself up to strangers' judgment before they'd even asked — and felt the thing he'd felt at the Door, the thing that had let him pay the price without flinching: that love was not a softness added on top of a hard life. It was the reckoning itself. You stood in the open or you didn't stand at all.

"Whatever they ask," he said, "we answer together."

Down the dark slope, lantern-light bobbed. Voices, a language he didn't know, drawing closer. The dog by the fire lifted its head, ears forward — and Cyrus watched it, read it, the old way, the human way, the way that had survived the sundering of everything else:

and saw that it was wagging its tail.
