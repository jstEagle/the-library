## 1. The Night Without Stars

The trouble with the Valley of Stillness, Odile's mother used to say, was that nothing ever happened in it — and the trouble with that was that when something finally did happen, nobody would know what to do.

She'd said it laughing, usually while burning bread or losing an argument with a stubborn kettle. Odile thought of her now as he stood at the workbench of the Marchetti workshop, sanding the final imperfections from a ward-lamp casing, and tried very hard to think about anything else.

Tomorrow he would be initiated into the Guild of Wardwrights. Nineteen years of his life had funneled toward this one ceremony like water toward a drain: the lessons in sigil-script copied until his fingers cramped, the apprenticeships sweeping workshop floors and fetching flux-sand for masters who never learned his name, the examinations passed with marks good enough to be praised and not so good as to be *noticed*. Tomorrow morning he would kneel on the cold mosaic floor of the Guildhall, and Master Corvane — the one teacher who *had* learned his name, and used it kindly, which Odile suspected was its own kind of trick — would press the guild seal to his palm, and that would be that. A life measured out in lamp casings and border maintenance reports.

He should have been excited. Everyone kept telling him how excited he ought to be.

"You're not excited," said Pip Marchetti, who was eleven, technically his cousin, and functionally a small disaster with legs. She was sitting on the good stool, which she was forbidden to sit on, eating a candied plum she had absolutely stolen from the shop tin.

"I'm focused," Odile said.

"You're sanding the same corner for an hour. I counted."

"It's an important corner."

"It's a *lamp*, Odie." She swung her feet, heels thumping the cabinet. "It sits on a shelf. In a valley where nothing ever happens. Grandmother says the last exciting thing to occur here was when old Fenwick's cow got into the bell tower, and that was before I was born."

"The cow was exciting," Odile allowed. He set down the sandpaper and flexed his aching hand. Outside the workshop window, the evening was settling over the valley the way it always did — soft, gray-violet, utterly reliable. The mountains stood in their ancient ring. The wards hummed along the border the way they had hummed for two hundred years, too low to hear, too constant to notice, like the sound of your own heart.

That was the whole point of the Stillness. The world outside was full of magic, the elders taught — wild, hungry, terrible magic that ate the unwary. The wards kept it out. Inside, a person could live a whole life without ever flinching at a shadow. Wasn't that worth a little gray? Wasn't that worth everything?

He'd believed it the way you believe in the floor beneath your feet. You don't examine it. You just stand on it.

"Odie," Pip said, in the particular tone she reserved for observations designed to ruin someone's evening. "The stars are gone."

"They're clouds," Odile said, not looking up. "Or it's fog. It's always fog by the river."

"No, I mean—" She was at the window now, nose nearly touching the glass. "I mean they're *gone*. All of them. At once. That's not weather, Odie. Weather doesn't do *all*."

He looked up to tell her she was being dramatic — a reasonable assumption; Pip had once claimed to see a ghost that turned out to be a coat on a nail — and the words died somewhere behind his teeth.

The sky was black. Not night-black, not cloud-black, not even storm-black, which still had texture and movement and the promise of lightning underneath. This was black like the inside of a closed box. Black like a hole cut out of the world. Where the constellations should have hung — the Shepherd, the Salt Road, the great slow wheel of the Wanderer that every child in the valley learned before they learned to read — there was nothing. No stars. No moon. No faint gray breath of the Milky Way.

Nothing had ever been so thoroughly nothing.

"Pip," Odile said quietly, "get away from the window."

"Is it the wards?" Her voice had gone small, which frightened him more than the sky did. Pip did not do small. "Did the wards break?"

"The wards can't break." The words came out automatically, catechism-flat, the way they'd been drilled into every apprentice since infancy: *the wards cannot break, the wards are eternal, the Stillness endures.* His grandmother had helped maintain them. His mother before that. The Marchettis were wardwrights back six generations, keepers of the small household charms, and the family creed was stitched into a sampler that hung by the door: *What is warded is safe.*

The sampler seemed suddenly, obscenely cheerful.

Then the humming stopped.

You couldn't hear the wards — that was the point, that was the whole architecture of the place — but you could feel them, the way you feel a floorboard under you even through thick boots. And Odile felt them go. It was like a great held breath releasing all around the valley at once, a pressure lifting off his chest and ears that left everything feeling strangely loud, strangely *thin*, as though the world had been a drum skin someone had finally let slack.

Pip grabbed his sleeve. "Odile. What was that."

"I don't know," he said, and the honesty of it terrified them both.

* * *

Their grandmother was in the kitchen, which was where Nonna Marchetti existed in the same fixed and eternal way the mountains did, shelling peas into a bowl with the brisk rhythm of a woman who had outlasted three floods, two husbands' worth of opinions, and every attempt anyone had made to modernize her stove.

She looked up when they came in, took one look at their faces, then one look out the window, and Odile watched sixty years fall off her posture and something much older settle into its place.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. Odile, light the lamps. All of them."

"Nonna—"

"All. Of. Them."

So he went through the house lighting lamps — the fat beeswax ones, the little oil floats, the ward-glow globes in the hall that were supposed to be decorative but tonight looked like small brave animals huddled together against the dark. Pip trailed after him asking questions with increasing velocity: *is it the end of the world, is it goblins, is it the sea people, why aren't you answering me, Odile, ODILE.*

"I'm answering you by not lying to you," Odile said. "I don't know."

"That's worse!"

"Yes," he agreed. "It is."

By the time he got back to the kitchen, Nonna had put the peas aside — which was how he knew it was serious; Nonna had once finished shelling peas through a minor earthquake — and had taken down the iron box from the top shelf of the pantry. Odile had seen the box exactly twice in his life. Once when he was seven and curious enough to climb, and been punished with a week of latrine duty. Once at his mother's funeral, when Nonna had held it in her lap through the entire service and not opened it.

Now she set it on the table between the lamps and looked at Odile across it, and her eyes in the lamplight were wet and furious and afraid.

"Your initiation is tomorrow," she said.

"Yes."

"Forget it."

"*Nonna.*"

"Listen to me, boy." She gripped his wrist, and her hands, usually so steady, trembled like the flame beside them. "Whatever comes through that door tonight, whatever anyone tells you tomorrow, whatever they ask of you — you remember one thing. Your name is Odile Marchetti. Not 'the heir.' Not 'the vessel.' Not any word the guild puts on you. *Odile.* Say it."

"Nonna, you're frightening me."

"Good. Fear keeps the head clear. Say your name."

"Odile Marchetti," he said, feeling ridiculous and cold.

"Again."

"*Odile Marchetti.*"

Something in her face eased, barely. She was reaching for the iron box's latch when the knock came.

Three blows, uneven, weak — the knock of someone using the doorframe more than the door. Then a sound like a body sliding down wood.

Pip shrieked. Nonna was already moving, faster than Odile had ever seen her move, snatching the fire poker on her way. Odile got there first anyway, youth beating out terror by half a second, and hauled the door open onto the dark.

A man lay crumpled against the threshold.

He was dressed as a courier — the guild's gray-and-white livery, travel-stained past recognition, boots worn through at the soles as if he'd run a very long way. But no courier of the Stillness had ever looked like this. There was blood soaking his side in a spreading black bloom. There were burns on his hands that didn't look like any burn Odile knew — the flesh silvered and cracked like glaze on old pottery, like something had passed *through* him rather than merely touched him. And the air around him moved wrong, rippling faintly, heat-shimmer in a cold night, as though the space he occupied couldn't quite decide he belonged in it.

"Help me carry him," Nonna said, and there was no argument in her voice, so there was none anywhere.

They got him inside, onto the long worktable, lamps crowded close. Up close he was young — younger than Odile had first thought, maybe mid-twenties, hollow-cheeked, lips gray. His eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling with an expression Odile had no name for: not fear exactly. Grief. The specific grief of a man carrying news he knows he will not live to deliver properly.

"He came through the western pass," the courier rasped, when Nonna pressed water to his mouth. "Before the wards... before they fell, I felt them go, I felt it from miles out, like the world exhaling—" A cough wracked him, wet and terrible. "No one else will come tonight. The guild will lock its doors. They'll say containment protocol. They've known. Do you understand me? *They've known for years.*"

"That's sedition," Nonna said, but softly, and not like she disbelieved it.

"It's arithmetic." His gaze crawled across the room and snagged on Odile, and something in it sharpened, focused, became urgent. "Marchetti. You're a Marchetti. Which one — the grandson? The son of Ilse?"

"My mother was Ilse," Odile said. His voice sounded far away to himself.

"Then it's you." Relief and horror crossed the courier's face together, twins holding hands. "Oh, thank the deep dark, and oh, you poor fool, both at once."

"What's me?" Odile asked. "What's me? What are you talking about?"

But the courier was already moving, dragging his ruined hand into his coat, and what he drew out made the nearest lamp flames bend toward it as if leaning in to look.

It was a sigil — a disc of cold iron the size of a plum, worked on one face with script Odile almost recognized and couldn't read at all, characters that seemed to shift when he wasn't directly looking at them, rearranging themselves like insects disturbed under a stone. It was beautiful the way a cliff edge is beautiful. It radiated cold. Not temperature — Odile's fingers, when the courier seized his wrist and pressed it into his palm, told him it was room-warm — but some other kind of cold, a cold of *distance*, of vast dark spaces between things.

The moment his skin touched it, the sigil flared — a silent pulse, a heartbeat of pressure — and Odile felt it *know* him. There was no other word. Something enormous and patient on the other side of the iron had turned its attention, looked through him, found what it was looking for, and settled.

"Blood-bound," the courier whispered. "Not training. Not office. Blood. Six generations the guild has fed it heirs and called it service." His grip tightened, shockingly strong for a dying man. "Take it. Don't give it back. Whatever they promise you, whatever they threaten — the binding dies with the line, and you're the last of the line, do you hear me? *You're the last.*"

"The last of what?" Odile's voice cracked. "The last of what? What binding? What have we *done*?"

The courier smiled. It was the worst thing yet — that smile, tired and pitying and almost kind, aimed at Odile like a lantern aimed into a cellar.

"You'll want to ask your grandmother that," he said. "Ask her about the anchor. Ask her about the king." His eyes were going glassy now, sliding toward the middle distance where dying men keep their appointments. "And ask her why she stayed in the valley all these years, Marchetti. Wardwrights don't retire. Wardwrights keep watch. Ask her what she's been watching."

His whisper dropped lower, lower, until Odile had to bend his ear close, close enough to feel the last warmth of the man's breath:

"Odile. Odile Marchetti. It knows your name already. It's been saying it for nineteen years, waiting. Don't let it be the only one who—"

The sentence never ended. The breath stopped in the middle of it, the way a clock stops, mid-tick, and the silence afterward was total — deeper than the starless sky, deeper than the dead wards, the silence of a world whose floor had finally been examined and found to be a lie all along.

In the doorway behind them, Pip began, very quietly, to cry.

Nonna closed the courier's eyes with two fingers, gently, the way she'd closed jars and books and, once, long ago, a coffin lid. Then she straightened, and looked at the iron sigil in Odile's white-knuckled fist, and at her grandson above it, nineteen years old, one day short of the only future he'd ever been promised.

"Well," she said, in the voice she used for burning bread and stubborn kettles and the end of worlds. "Get your coat, Odile. And take your cousin's hand. We're not staying in this house tonight."

Outside the window, where the Shepherd constellation should have been, the darkness seemed — Odile could not have said how he knew, only that every hair on his body rose to confirm it — to be listening.

And somewhere, far beyond the broken border of the only world he'd ever known, something that wore a crown like a glove whispered his name, and smiled.
