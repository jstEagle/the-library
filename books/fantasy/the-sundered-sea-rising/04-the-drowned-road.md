## 4. The Drowned Road

The journal smelled of salt and old smoke, and it did not want to be read.

That was Odile's first thought on the second morning beyond the valley, crouched under a leaning slab of slate that had once been somebody's barn roof. The pages resisted him the way a cat resists being held — not violently, but with a pointed, personal affront. The ink shifted when he looked away and settled again when he looked back, as though the words were shy of strangers.

"Fine," he muttered to it. "Be difficult. Everyone else is."

He'd slept maybe two hours. The night before, he had lain in the ruin of what he guessed was a shepherd's cottage and watched the darkness where stars should have been and tried very hard not to think about the sound the courier had made at the end — that small, astonished exhale, like a man remembering something he'd forgotten his whole life. *Odile Marchetti.* His name in a dying stranger's mouth, pressed into the air along with an iron sigil still hanging cold against his chest.

The sigil hadn't warmed since. He'd checked. Eleven times. It sat under his shirt like a coin left too long in snow.

The journal, at least, was cooperating now. Sort of. When he held it flat and let his eyes unfocus — the way Master Corvane had taught him to read faded ward-diagrams back in the guildhall, *let the pattern come to you, boy, don't chase it* — the shifting ink steadied into lines:

*Follow the old coast road west. Where the water takes the road, take the shore. The tide-reader will know.*

There was no signature. There was never any signature. Whoever the courier had been, he wrote like a man who expected his letters to be read over his shoulder by people who could kill him for it.

Odile closed the journal, tucked it into his coat beside his ribs where it rode against the sigil's chain, and stood up into the grey morning.

The world outside the valley was loud.

That was the thing nobody warned you about. Not the magic — though there was plenty of that, humming in the grass like a hive of patient bees, prickling along his arms whenever the wind changed — but the noise. In Stillness, everything arrived muffled, wrapped in wool, as if the whole valley spoke through a wall. Out here a bird could be a catastrophe. The sea, when he first heard it two days ago, had genuinely frightened him: a vast slow breathing behind the hills, in and out, in and out, like something enormous asleep in the next room.

He walked toward it anyway. That was the other thing nobody warned you about — how much you'd want it.

The old coast road found him by midmorning, or what was left of it. It had clearly once been grand: wide enough for six carts abreast, edged with carved posts worn faceless by weather, running straight and confident toward the horizon. But the sea had gotten to it. Whole sections lay drowned beneath a hand's depth of green-glass water, the paving stones visible below like teeth behind a parted lip. Farther on, the road climbed a headland only to stop abruptly at a fresh cliff-edge where the ground simply gave up and fell into foam.

"So the sea is eating the world," Odile said aloud, to no one. "Good to know. Very reassuring."

His voice sounded absurd out here. He kept talking anyway. Two days of silence after nineteen years of a valley full of voices — forge-hammers, market-calls, his mother's humming, Corvane's dry lectures — and the quiet had begun pressing on him like deep water. Talking to himself was either madness or survival. He'd decided it was survival and refused to revisit the question.

He picked his way along the drowned stretch, boots splashing, and that was where the magic got strange. Underfoot, in the water-warmed stones, something pulsed. Not wardlight — he knew wardlight, knew its tidy amber order the way he knew his own handwriting. This was older and less polite, a rhythm without a pattern, like a heartbeat belonging to someone who wasn't bothering to keep time for his benefit.

It made his teeth ache. It also, embarrassingly, made him laugh.

Because this was it, wasn't it? This was what the guild had walled off. Nineteen years of diagrams and drills and the litany they made every initiate recite — *without the wards, the wildness; without the wildness, ruin* — and all the while the "wildness" was this: warm stones and a pulse and a light on the water like hammered tin. He stood knee-deep in a drowned road laughing like a madman while the sea breathed its slow breath, and if anyone had seen him they would have locked him up, and he wouldn't have cared.

Then the tide came in early.

He didn't know it was early. He learned that later, from her. What he knew was that one moment the water around his shins lay slack and sun-warmed, and the next it *moved* — gathered itself with intent, swirled up past his knees, his thighs, tugging at him like a large dog deciding you were coming whether you liked it or not. He stumbled, went down hard on a slick stone, and came up soaked to the collar, sputtering, clutching the journal above the waterline with both hands like an idiot holding a loaf of bread from a flood.

"Oh, come *on*," he told the sea.

The sea did not apologize.

He wrung himself out an hour later on a shelf of dry rock above the tideline, feeling sorry for himself with great thoroughness, when he saw the smoke.

Thin, deliberate, one thread of it rising off the point ahead — the kind of fire someone built who knew exactly who else might see it and didn't care. Odile crouched behind a boulder and had a long internal argument. The courier had died pointing him here. The journal said *the tide-reader will know.* But the courier had also died, which struck Odile as a data point worth weighing.

Curiosity won, as it always had. It was the trait his teachers liked least about him. *A wardwright's first duty is to leave things alone,* Corvane used to say, and Odile had spent nineteen years leaving things alone and had just watched the wards fail anyway, so perhaps the doctrine deserved some scrutiny.

He crept forward along the rocks.

She was sitting cross-legged by a small fire, roasting a fish on a stick, and she did not look up when he stepped on the one loose stone in a hundred paces, the crack of it carrying like a gunshot across the water.

"You walk like a collapsing building," she said.

"I—" said Odile.

"There's a gull behind you that's been following you since the headland. It thinks you're going to die and it's waiting for its turn." She turned the fish. She was perhaps his own age, maybe a year older, dark-haired, sharp-faced, wearing a coat that had been good quality roughly a decade ago and had spent the decade since losing arguments with weather. Around her neck hung a loop of small objects — shells, bits of glass, a knuckle of driftwood, a fishhook — that clinked softly when she moved. Tide-tokens, he thought, and then wondered how he knew the word, and realized he didn't; the sigil had gone faintly warm against his chest for the first time since the night the stars vanished, and the word had simply arrived, the way words arrive in dreams.

"You're the tide-reader," he managed.

"And you're the boy who fell in the shallows an hour ago and blamed the ocean for it." She finally looked up. Her eyes were pale grey, unimpressed, and slightly amused, which he would learn was her resting condition. "Sera Voss. You're the one from inside the wards."

"How do you know I'm—"

"You've got valley-ward chalk dust worked so deep in your knuckles you'll need a priest and a chisel to get it out. You flinched when a gull cried. And you're looking at my fire like it might be a trap, which nobody born out here does, because out here a fire is just a fire." She pulled the fish off the stick and held it out. "Sit down. You look like a drowned clerk."

He sat. He took the fish. It was excellent, and he hated how much he enjoyed it, because enjoying it felt like conceding a point.

"The journal," he said, mouth half full. "It says a tide-reader would know. Know what?"

"Where the road goes, once the sea's finished with it." She nodded west, along the coast, where the cliffs marched off into haze. "And where *you* go, which is the same place, apparently, because the sea has opinions about you."

"The sea has—"

"It's climbing, valley-boy." All the amusement went out of her voice at once, like a lamp shuttered. "You noticed the road drowning. You haven't asked why. Everyone asks *how high*, eventually. Nobody asks *why*, because the answer is rude." She fed a twig to the fire. "Tides answer to the moon. Moon's still up there — well, somewhere; can't see it, thanks to whatever happened three nights ago. Tides answer to coasts, winds, the shape of the deep channels. None of those have changed enough to explain what I'm reading. The water isn't being pushed." She looked at him directly. "It's being *called*. Something inland is singing to the sea, and the sea is walking home to it. Toward your valley. Toward whatever used to be behind those pretty wards."

The sigil burned cold against his sternum — cold the way ice is cold, the way iron is cold, the way a held breath is cold. The word *anchor* surfaced in him unbidden and sank again before he could grab it.

"That's ridiculous," he said.

"Almost certainly," Sera agreed cheerfully, and held out the loop of tokens. "Want to see?"

*

What she did, she did with the tokens and a shallow pool left by the retreating tide — a mirror of water cupped in rock, still as glass. She knelt over it and let each token fall from her fingers one at a time, and Odile, who had spent his life studying wards and therefore believed himself immune to spectacle, forgot entirely to breathe.

Because the water *answered*. Each token landed without splash. The shell spun slowly, deliberately, and stopped pointing west — inland, toward the mountains, toward home. The bit of sea-glass sank and hung suspended mid-depth, glowing faintly green. The driftwood floated in a perfect circle that tightened like a noose. And the fishhook — the fishhook trembled, quivering on the surface like a compass needle having second thoughts, swinging between west and north, unable to choose.

"What's the hook mean?" Odile whispered.

"Me." Sera's voice had gone somewhere distant and careful. "It always points where I'm supposed to go. Lately it can't decide if I'm meant to run toward the thing or away from it." She swept the tokens up sharply, breaking the spell, and the pool became ordinary water again. "Three years ago I read these same tokens for the Brackhollow harbor-master. I told them the stars would go out and the sea would rise against the land, and I gave them the date." Her jaw tightened. "They gave me exile. Said I'd cursed the fleet. Said a woman who predicts disaster is a woman planning it."

"They exiled you for being right?"

"They exiled me for making them feel things," she said. "Being right was incidental. It's the worst crime there is." She glanced sideways at him, and something in her expression shifted — the wariness easing by one careful degree, a gate opened a crack rather than unlatched. "You know something about institutions that eat their own truth-keepers, I think. You've got the look."

"The look."

"The look of a man whose whole childhood just turned out to be a load-bearing lie." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "I had that look for about a year. It goes away. Mostly. You get a new face underneath. It's angrier, but it fits better."

Odile looked out at the water, at the drowned road gleaming under the afternoon light, at the far haze where the mountains hid the valley that had raised him on lies like a mother feeds milk. He thought about the courier dying in his father's workshop, whispering a name. About the guildmaster's face when Odile had asked the wrong question. About the sigil, warm now, pulsing gently in time — he was almost sure — with that strange heartbeat in the drowned stones.

"My grandmother," he heard himself say, "was from out here. From the coast. Nobody ever talked about her. I found out two nights ago that's because she did something enormous and terrible and the guild built my entire life on top of it." He laughed, short and bitter. "I don't even know her name."

"Voss," Sera said quietly.

He stared at her.

"Your grandmother. The binder. My family kept the coastal records — tide-readers have kept them for nine generations, we just weren't important enough for the guild to bother confiscating ours until recently." She was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse — not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. "Her name was Voss. Which makes us cousins, probably, twice over and inconveniently. Which makes your inheritance my inheritance. Which is why the hook can't decide which way to point." She sighed and poked the fire. "I really hoped I was wrong about you. I'd made a whole plan for being wrong about you. It involved a fishing village and a lot of sleeping."

Odile sat with that. The gull, faithful to its pessimism, screamed somewhere overhead. The sigil against his chest beat its slow cold beat, and for the first time it felt less like a key and more like a hand — reaching across ninety years to grip his collar.

"So the sea is climbing toward my valley," he said slowly, "because something in my valley is calling it home. And the journal wants me to follow the drowned road west. And you—" he gestured at the fishhook, now stowed among its sister tokens, "—can't decide whether to help me or shove me in the water."

"Those aren't contradictory," Sera said. "Some days they'll be the same gesture." She kicked sand over the fire and stood, shouldering a battered pack. "Come on, cousin. The road drowns fully at dusk, and there's a chapel on the headland ahead where the roof doesn't leak. Mostly. You can tell me everything the guild did, and I'll tell you everything the sea's been saying, and tomorrow we start walking toward the thing that ate the stars."

"Why would I tell you anything?" Odile asked, standing. "We met an hour ago."

"Because I'm the only person in the world who believes you," Sera said, already walking, "and you're the only person in the world who believes me, and lonely people tell each other everything. It's practically physics." She paused at the edge of the rocks and glanced back, and for just a moment the sharp humor in her face gave way to something rawer and younger. "Also — the water rose a foot last night. A foot in one night, valley-boy. Whatever's calling it home is getting louder. We don't have time to be strangers."

Far to the west, past the drowned road, past the haze, thunder rolled — except the sky was clear, and the sound came from the wrong direction, from inland, from the mountains.

From home.

Sera's tokens clinked softly as she started walking, and Odile, heart hammering in time with the cold iron at his chest, followed her.
