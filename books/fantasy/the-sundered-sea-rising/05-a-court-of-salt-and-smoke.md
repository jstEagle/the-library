## 5. A Court of Salt and Smoke

Sera Voss had been thrown out of better harbors than Brackhollow, but never one that smelled so much of nothing.

That was the first thing she noticed on the morning they walked down the drowned road's last mile and through the Salt Gate: the smell. Every port she'd ever known announced itself half a league out — tar and fish guts, rotting kelp, the sweet-sour breath of a thousand lives crammed between sea and wall. Brackhollow smelled of salt, yes, but salt as if it had been strained through linen. Salt with all the life boiled out of it. Clean salt. Dead salt.

"Something wrong?" Odile asked. He was walking beside her with his hood up and his shoulders hunched like a boy trying to make himself smaller than the world, which, given what he was carrying in his pocket, was almost funny.

"You're asking *me* if something's wrong," Sera said. "The valley boy who slept standing up for two days because he didn't trust the ground to stay put."

"It moved."

"The ground is *supposed* to move. That's my entire profession." She pushed back her own hood and let the gray light hit her face. If the tide-readers' guild had spies in Brackhollow — and they did; old Master Halloway had eyes everywhere except, apparently, in the sky the night the stars went out — then hiding was pointless anyway. Better to be seen and be boring. "Keep your mouth shut, keep the sigil under your shirt, and whatever you do, don't tell anyone where you're from."

"Why not?"

"Because 'the Valley of Stillness' sounds like either a lie or an invitation, and both get people killed here." She glanced at him sideways. "Also because you have the look. I had the look too, when I first came out. The look of someone whose whole world just ended and who hasn't decided yet whether to grieve or be furious about it."

"And you chose?"

"Furious. It keeps the blood warm."

The Salt Gate was open — gates always were, in Brackhollow; there hadn't been a raid in forty years, or a storm, or, Sera suspected, much of anything worth gating against — and the two guards flanking it were arguing in low voices over a dice game set up on an overturned bucket. They looked up as Sera and Odile passed, gave them the flat, unhurried once-over of men who had not been truly alarmed by anything since childhood, and went back to their dice.

One of them yawned so widely Sera could count his teeth.

She felt Odile tense beside her. "Shouldn't they—"

"Ask our business? Search us? Wonder who we are?" She shook her head. "Watch. Just watch. That's what we're here for."

* * *

Brackhollow rose from the harbor in tiers, white stone going gray at the waterline, streets climbing the hillside in switchbacks like a snail's shell. From the seawall it must have been beautiful once. Even now, with the morning haze burning off, there was something handsome in its bones — the old lighthouse on the eastern mole, the bell tower at the city's heart, the long arc of the Grand Quay where fishing boats should have been shouldering each other for space.

There were boats. That wasn't the problem. There were perhaps sixty boats moored in neat rows, each one identical distance from its neighbor, each one with furled sails and coiled lines and not one plank out of place.

And not one of them moving.

Sera stopped at the head of the quay and let Odile see it. The water inside the harbor lay like poured glass. No chop. No swell. No glitter of current dragging weed past the pilings. The tide marks on the stone — she made herself look, made herself point — ran in a single unbroken line around the entire basin, exactly the same height, day after day after day, because the sea came in to the same inch and went out to the same inch and had done so for years.

"There's no tide," Odile said slowly.

"There's a schedule." A woman hauling a barrow of ice paused beside them, overhearing, and smiled with genuine warmth. "High water at the sixth bell, low water at the eighteenth. Never fails. Isn't it wonderful?"

"Wonderful," Sera said.

"The king's peace," the woman said, and there it was — the phrase, the little benediction, the words Sera had heard a hundred times since her exile began, always spoken in that same tone of settled gratitude. "You're travelers? You'll want to see the Changing of the Harbor Lights tonight. Free wine at the Fountain Steps. His Majesty provides."

She trundled off with her barrow, humming.

Odile watched her go. "She sounded happy."

"She sounded like someone reciting," Sera said. "Come on. Ice doesn't melt properly here either — did you notice? The fish markets sell fish that's three days dead and still smells fresh. Everything holds. Nothing changes." She started up the hill. "Welcome to the safest city in the world. Try not to scream."

* * *

They took a room above a chandlery on Rope Walk, paid for with the last of the courier's coin, and Sera spent the afternoon doing what she did best: listening to the water.

Officially, tide-reading meant charts and instruments and long tables of predicted heights. Unofficially — the way her grandmother had taught her, kneeling on cold stones with her palms flat against a harbor wall — it meant listening. The sea talked. Constantly. To anyone patient enough to press their hands against something it touched and shut up long enough to hear.

Brackhollow's sea didn't talk. It hummed. One note, low and even, like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Sera sat on her narrow bed with her palms pressed to the plaster of the outer wall and listened to that single sustained note for an hour, until her teeth ached, before she gave up and went down to find Odile.

He was in the chandlery's common room, sitting by the window with a cup of tea he hadn't touched, watching the street.

"Tell me what you've seen," Sera said, dropping into the chair across from him.

"People are kind," he said, and he sounded troubled by it, which she supposed was progress of a kind. "The innkeeper gave me extra bread without being asked. A child fell in the street earlier and three adults helped her up before she'd finished crying. Everyone says good morning to everyone."

"Yes."

"Nobody argues. Nobody hurries. I watched this street for two hours, Sera. A cart lost a wheel outside the ropewalk and four men fixed it and nobody raised their voice, and when it was done they all just... stood there. For a minute. Like they'd forgotten what came next." He turned his cup in its saucer. "In the valley we were quiet too. Still. But it felt like holding your breath together. This feels like—" He searched. "Like breathing that someone else is doing for you."

Sera stared at him. Nineteen years behind wards that ate magic, and the boy had put in one sentence what she'd failed to say in six years of exile and three hundred pages of confiscated journals.

"That's it exactly," she said quietly. "That's the king's peace. Nobody decided to be gentle here. Gentleness is just... the only weather left."

* * *

Evening brought the Changing of the Harbor Lights, and Sera took him to see it, because some lessons you couldn't teach — you had to let a person walk into them barefoot.

The Fountain Steps filled at dusk. Hundreds of people, families mostly, sitting shoulder to shoulder with cups of the promised wine while the lamplighters made their slow procession along the mole. And it would have been lovely, genuinely lovely, except—

"They're all smiling," Odile murmured. "Why does it look like mourning?"

Because the smiles were all the same size, Sera thought. Because the laughter came at the right moments, evenly spaced, like bells. Because an old man near the front was staring at the darkening harbor with tears running freely down his face and a smile fixed on his mouth, and no one around him seemed to find this strange, including, from the placid set of his shoulders, the old man himself.

A herald in silver-and-blue called out the king's titles from the lighthouse gallery, and the crowd murmured its gratitude in unison — not a cheer, a murmur, soft as surf on a beach with no waves.

Then the lights changed, all along the mole, green to gold in one ripple, and the crowd sighed its pleasure, and somewhere in the middle of it all Sera felt Odile go rigid beside her.

"What?" she breathed.

"The sigil." His hand was pressed flat against his chest, over his heart, where the iron key hung on its cord beneath his shirt. "It's — warm. It's been cold since the courier died. It's *warm*, and it's pulling, like—" He swallowed. "Like something on the other side of a door just put its hand on the same handle."

"How warm?"

"Not burning. Not yet."

She scanned the crowd. The murmur of gratitude was still rolling gently through the Fountain Steps, wave after contented wave. But near the back, at the top of the steps where the lamplight didn't reach, three figures in the king's blue stood perfectly still, facing the crowd, not watching the lights at all.

Watching, she would have sworn, the exact spot where Odile stood.

"We're leaving," she said, taking his elbow, casual as anything, steering him down a side lane away from the steps. "Now, and not fast, and not looking back."

"Sera—"

"I know. I know what it means. It means something in that palace felt you arrive." The hum was louder here, or maybe she was just finally letting herself hear it, that endless note under the cobbles, under the harbor glass, under everything. Six years she'd thought the starless night was a catastrophe she'd failed to prevent. Now, walking fast through streets where nothing ever changed, she understood it was worse than catastrophe.

It was a summons.

Behind them, faint and beautiful and utterly soundless, the harbor bells began to ring the hour — and Sera, who had grown up reading water the way other folk read faces, felt every drop of it in Brackhollow turn, very slightly, toward the boy walking beside her.

The king knew he was here.

And the king, whatever wore the king, was inviting him to court.
