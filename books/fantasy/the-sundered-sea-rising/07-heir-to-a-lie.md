## 7. Heir to a Lie

The knock came at midnight, and it was not a knock.

It was a pressure — three slow pulses through the floor of his cell, through the soles of his boots, up through the bones of his legs, as though the palace itself had a heart and Odile had just pressed his ear against it. The sigil in his hand pulsed in answer, once for each beat, like a child echoing its parent.

Odile sat very still on the edge of the narrow bed and did not answer the door.

He had answered the *other* knock. That was enough answering for one night.

The pulses stopped. Somewhere far above, muffled by floors and stone and the particular hush that seemed baked into every brick of this place, the birds of the aviary court were still singing. They hadn't stopped since sundown. He could hear them even here, three levels down, a thin bright thread of sound threading through the silence like water through cloth.

*They sing because something changed,* he thought. *Or they sing because something arrived.*

He looked at the courier's journal, which he had carried against his chest since the drowned road, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his own body. The guards had searched him thoroughly when they took him — patted down his coat, emptied his pockets, confiscated a pencil stub and half an apple with the solemnity of men disarming a siege engine. But the journal they had left. It lay against his ribs now like a sleeping animal, and Odile was beginning to suspect it had let itself be found.

*Journals don't decide things,* he told himself firmly, in the voice Master Corvane used for correcting students who attributed intentions to ward-stones. *Paper is paper.*

But Corvane had also taught him that wards were just geometry until you fed them intent, and Odile was no longer entirely certain which side of the geometry he stood on.

* * *

The cell was not cruel, which was somehow worse.

It was a comfortable room, honestly — a real bed with a feather mattress, a washstand with a copper basin, a window of thick green glass that turned the daylight sea-colored and the night sky into something drowned and distant. There was even a bookshelf, stocked with perhaps forty volumes: histories of the realm, all ending at roughly the same year; a treatise on beekeeping; three copies of the same book of devotional poems, as if someone had ordered them in bulk and never checked.

"They want me comfortable," Odile said aloud, to the bees. "That's what worries me."

Bees, being bees, offered no opinion.

He had tried the door on the first morning. It wasn't locked — that was the joke of it, the little joke the palace played on everyone, he suspected. It simply didn't open. His hands pushed and the door remained a door-shaped fact of the universe, indifferent as weather. Magic, then, or something wearing magic's clothes. Everything here wore everything's clothes. The servants smiled a half-second after their eyes finished smiling. The meals arrived hot without anyone ever seeming to carry them. And King Aldric —

Odile made himself think about it, because he'd been refusing to think about it since the throne room, and refusal was starting to ache like a splinter worked too deep.

The king's eyes had not moved. Not once. Not to follow Odile across the floor, not to blink, not to flick toward the chancellor when the man leaned in to whisper. The mouth spoke, and the voice came a breath behind it, like thunder arriving late to its own lightning, and the whole time those pale eyes had rested on Odile with the fixed attention of a portrait — except portraits don't make your skin crawl, and portraits don't *know* what you're carrying.

*You bear your grandmother's key,* the king had said. Not: how did you come by it. Not: who are you. He had known. The way a trap knows the shape of the foot that finds it.

And then the king had smiled — mouth first, voice trailing after, eyes not at all — and said, "We have waited a long time for the Marchetti heir," and Odile's entire understanding of his life had quietly come apart at the seams, right there between two pillars, while courtiers watched with their hollow grateful faces.

*The Marchetti heir.* Not a wardwright initiate. Not a guild apprentice of nineteen years, graded and measured and promised a white sash if he kept his lines clean. An *heir*. As if he were a title walking around in a boy's coat.

Well. Tonight the boy intended to find out what the title meant.

* * *

The journal fought him for the better part of two hours.

The courier — Wenna Tolliver, according to the faded name inked inside the cover, followed by a string of letters that might have been guild ranks or might have been curses — had written in a cipher, but it was a cipher Odile recognized, because he had learned it at twelve years old as part of his training and been told it was obsolete. Guild shorthand. Wardwright's knot-script. The elders called it a dead language, useful only for reading the old foundational texts.

Which meant either Wenna Tolliver had been fond of dead languages, or the things she wrote needed to be unreadable to anyone the guild hadn't trained.

*Or,* said a small cold voice in Odile's head, *she wrote it this way because the last person who carried this journal was also guild-trained, and she knew exactly whose hands it would fall into.*

His hands. Chosen hands. Hands that a dying woman had pressed a cold iron sigil into, whispering his name — not a warning, not a message, just *his name*, like a delivery being signed for.

He opened the journal to the first page and began to translate, writing the decoded text in the margins in pencil he no longer had, so instead he scratched the letters into the wax of the bedside candle with a pin pulled from his collar. It was slow, painstaking work, and it gave his hands something to do while his stomach slowly filled with ice.

*Day one of travel. The valley holds. The anchor holds. She is fading but she is holding, and I am beginning to hate her for it.*

*She.* Odile frowned at the candle-wax letters. He assumed at first it was a companion, a fellow courier. Then:

*Spoke with her again today. She weeps. I no longer know whether the weeping is hers or the thing's. Sometimes I think there is no difference anymore. Sometimes I think that was the whole design.*

*The guild calls her the Anchor. The old texts call her the Binder. Her name was Elira. Elira Marchetti.*

The pin slipped. Odile stared at the word *Marchetti* scratched crookedly into candle wax, and the room went very quiet, the way rooms do when they understand something before you do.

His grandmother's name had been spoken exactly twice in his childhood. Once by his father, drunk on festival wine, saying *you have her hands* and then refusing to explain whose hands. Once by Grandmother Sable, who was not actually his grandmother but a neighbor granted the honorary title, who had said *Elira would be proud* and then gone pale and left the room, and no one ever mentioned it again.

In the valley, you did not ask about Elira Marchetti. This was not a rule anyone stated. It was simply one of the stillnesses of the Valley of Stillness, one more thing held motionless, like the weather and the tides and the stars.

Odile sat with his hands flat on the journal, breathing.

Then he kept translating, because his hands needed doing and his heart needed somewhere to put itself, and grief and fury, he discovered, both work well as fuel for cipher-work.

* * *

By the third hour he had the shape of it, and the shape of it was monstrous in the plainest, most bureaucratic way imaginable — which made it worse than any monster he could have invented. Monsters at least require courage. This required only paperwork.

Elira Marchetti had been a binder of the first generation. Before the valley was the valley, it had been a mountain pass, and beneath the pass — beneath, Odile now understood, the specific ground his family's workshop had stood on for four generations, the ground he had slept over every night of his nineteen years — something vast had been wounded loose into the world. The journal did not name it. Wenna wrote around the name the way you step around a hole in the dark road. She called it *the Stillness*, which was the cruelest irony Odile had ever read, or else not an irony at all, because that was apparently what it wanted. A world without tide or storm or star. A world held, perfectly, forever, like a fly in amber.

Elira had bound it. Not killed it — killed, the journal implied, was not possible, any more than you could kill the ocean's weight. Bound it under the mountain pass, and anchored the binding not in stones or wards alone but in herself. In her blood. In a bloodline.

*The binding requires a living heir of the binder's line within the wards' radius,* Wenna had written, in the cramped handwriting of someone racing exhaustion. *This is why the guild settles families there. This is why the Marchettis have never been permitted to leave. We tell them it is tradition. We tell them it is honor. We feed each generation to the anchor one spoonful at a time and call it apprenticeship.*

*Elira's granddaughter bore the weight longest. She lasted eleven years before the stillness took her mind. Her daughter —*

The entry stopped. The next line was written later, in shakier script:

*I cannot write her name yet. I keep seeing the workshop. I keep seeing the boy.*

Odile set down the pin.

He thought about his mother, whom he barely remembered — a laugh, mostly, and the smell of rosemary, and a single afternoon spent teaching him to whistle through a blade of grass before she went into the guildhouse one autumn and did not come out, and the guildmaster had appeared at their door with his hat in his hands and said the words *the wards required her service*, and everyone in the valley had nodded as if that explained anything, because in the Valley of Stillness *the wards required* explained everything. It was the answer to every question. Why the same gray skies. Why the same mild seasons. Why the stars went out one constellation at a time over the years, so gradually no one marked it.

Why his mother went into a building and became a sentence.

*Her daughter,* the journal said. His mother. Fed to the anchor. And then the guild had turned to her son — turned to *him*, a child, and begun the long patient work of preparing the next spoonful. The lessons. The initiations. The promise of a white sash. Nineteen years of careful cultivation, and Odile had thought he was learning to protect the valley.

He had been learning to *ripen*.

The anger arrived all at once, total and silent, the way floodwater arrives in a dry gully — nothing, nothing, and then everything, moving faster than thought. He was on his feet without remembering standing. The candlestick was in his hand without remembering reaching. For one long moment he genuinely considered hurling it at the wall, at the green glass window, at the smug beekeeping treatise, at the entire architecture of a world built on his family's graves.

Instead — and he would remember this, later, as the moment he understood he was more his mother's son than the guild's — he set the candlestick down, sat back on the bed, and laughed. A short, cracked, awful laugh, but a laugh.

"Of course," he said to the empty cell. "Of course the initiation was scheduled for this year. Of course the wards collapsed *now*. I'm ready, aren't I? Ripe." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Nineteen years. You couldn't even let me finish growing up before you started harvesting."

Somewhere overhead, the birds sang on, relentless, and he wondered if they sang for his mother too, and whether they had sung the day she died, and whether anyone in that beautiful lying valley had heard them.

* * *

He translated until the candle was a puddle and his eyes burned, and near dawn he found the entries that mattered most, the ones Wenna had written smallest and pressed hardest into the page, as if she could push the truth deep enough into the paper to hide it from herself.

*The king is not possessed. I need to write this plainly because everyone at court insists otherwise, because possession is a horror and horror can be pitied. What sits on that throne volunteered. Aldric of House Merren opened the door himself, twenty years ago, when the binding began to strain. He believed he could hold it. A king, holding a god — he thought it would be like holding reins.*

*It is not reins. It is a glove, and the god wears it, and the king is still inside, awake, watching his own hands sign the quiet laws. I have heard him. At night, through the door, when the thing sleeps shallow. He begs. He has been begging for twenty years.*

*The sigil the heir carries is not a weapon. It is the counterweight. Elira made two keys: one to bind, one to unbind, and the second she hid in her own bloodline because she did not trust the guild — and gods forgive her, she was right not to. The guild wants the unbinding key destroyed. They have always wanted it destroyed. Every heir who carried it was supposed to surrender it to the guildmaster, and every heir who did —*

Another gap. Another shaky resumption:

*— did not survive the surrender. The guildmaster does not destroy the key. He cannot; it is blood-bound. So he keeps it, and the keeping drains the carrier, and the carrier fades, and the guild calls it service, and I am done calling it service. If you are reading this, heir, you are the key now. Not the bearer. The key. I am sorry. I chose you the only way left to choose: by dying near you.*

*Find the tide-readers. Find the grimoire. And when you stand before the anchor, remember that Elira did not bind the Stillness out of fear.*

*She bound it because she loved the world too much to let it stop moving.*

Odile closed the journal.

The green glass of the window had gone from black to the color of deep water — dawn coming, filtered through whatever enchantment kept this palace suspended in its permanent mild afternoon. Below, faintly, the birds were still singing. He realized he had stopped finding it eerie. It sounded, now, like a countdown. Like something counting the days until the world remembered itself.

He thought about the guildmaster's face — old Master Ellery Vane, who had taught him his first ward-lines, who had ruffled his hair at seven and called him *little anchor-man* as a joke, a joke, a *joke*, gods, it had been a joke all along — and the anger came back, but quieter now, colder, which he suspected was the more dangerous form.

They hadn't stolen his inheritance. That was the thing he kept circling and circling. They had done something far more efficient. They had *raised* him into it, the way you raise a calf, with warmth and shelter and kind voices, and the whole time the pasture had a gate and beyond the gate a scale.

"I'm not angry that I'm the key," he said quietly, testing the words, seeing if they were true. They mostly were. "I'm angry that you made me love you first."

The sigil pulsed against his sternum where he'd tucked it, slow and patient, and he got the sudden distinct impression — irrational, unwelcome, probably true — that it had heard him. That it had *always* heard the Marchetti heirs. That his grandmother's iron had opinions about grief.

"All right," he told it. "I know what I am. Debt, not gift. Fine. But debts go *both* directions, and somebody owes my mother, and somebody owes Wenna Tolliver, and I intend to collect."

Which was very fine speech-making for a young man locked in a luxurious cell with a puddled candle and no pencil, and he knew it, and the knowing took some of the wind out of him. He flopped back on the mattress and stared at the ceiling and let the fury settle into something he could carry, the way you let silt settle so the water runs clear.

He was still staring when the door — the door that did not open, the door that was a law of physics — swung inward without a sound.

Sera Voss stood in the frame, dressed in the grey-and-teal livery of a palace laundress that fit her badly and fooled no one, least of all her expression, which said she had climbed three levels of a warded prison before breakfast and found it merely annoying. Behind her, in the corridor, a guard stood facing the wall with the serene blankness of a man who had recently decided walls were fascinating.

"Up," she said. "Get the journal. Walk casually."

Odile sat up slowly. "How—"

"Later. Possibly never. Do you want to philosophize, or do you want to see what's under the vaults?" She glanced back down the corridor, then at him, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for truths. "Because I've been thinking about what you won't explain, heir. And I think it's the same thing my family's charts were taken for. And I think whatever's under this palace, we're the only two people alive who want to look at it for the *right* reasons."

She held out her hand.

Outside, the birds reached some crescendo only they understood — and below them, below the palace, below the vaults, something vast shifted in its sleep, and turned over, and noticed.
