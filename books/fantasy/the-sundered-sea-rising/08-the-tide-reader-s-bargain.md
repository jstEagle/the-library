## 8. The Tide-Reader's Bargain

Sera Voss had been thrown out of better cities than Brackhollow, and she had learned one lesson in all of them: never fall for a man who arrives bleeding and interesting. Interesting men were trouble. Bleeding men were worse. Bleeding *and* interesting men — the kind who turned out to be heirs to ancient bindings and got themselves arrested by the palace within three days of arriving in port — were a category of disaster she had sworn off entirely.

And yet here she was, at dawn, standing on the fish-stinking quay below the palace seawall, counting guards.

"Two on the gate," she murmured to herself. "One asleep. One pretending not to be asleep, which is somehow more useful." She chewed the end of her pencil. "Four on the wall walk, but two of them are watching the harbor, which means they're watching me, which means they're doing their jobs badly, because if I were planning anything I'd hardly stand here counting them."

She was, of course, planning something. She had been planning it since the night before, when the news had come down through Brackhollow's whisper-roads like oil through water: the stranger from the valley had been taken to the palace. The one with the iron sigil. The one whose pocket she'd picked and whose journal she'd read while he slept off a fever in her rented room above the chandlery.

She'd returned the journal. Mostly. She'd kept the pages about the vaults.

That was the thing about Sera that her examiners at the Tide College had never understood, back before they'd stripped her credentials and put her on a boat and told her never to come back: she did not steal because she was dishonest. She stole because paper told the truth, and people so rarely did. Her family had been chart-makers for six generations — Voss charts, drawn by hand, corrected against no official record because there *was* no official record worth correcting. The sea moved; the College's maps did not. Her grandmother had died reading tides the College called impossible. Her mother had drowned following a current that appeared on exactly none of their precious sanctioned surveys, and Sera had spent eleven years proving that current existed, and they had rewarded her by exiling her for predicting the starless night.

*Predicting.* As though noticing that the sea had gone quiet as a held breath were a crime.

Now the whole sky had gone dark, the tides had stopped moving, and every chart in the realm was wrong. She would have found it funny if she hadn't been so angry.

The palace vaults sat below the waterline — old smuggler warrens, back when Brackhollow had been honest about what it was. When the king's peace settled over the city twenty years ago, the Crown had confiscated everything that contradicted it: tide-tables, storm-ledgers, the great libraries of the shipping families. Including, on a gray morning Sera still remembered too well, four trunks of Voss charts, carried out of her childhood home by soldiers who wouldn't meet her father's eyes.

Her father had died the next winter. Quietly, the way everything died under the king's peace.

So. Two birds. One very illegal stone.

* * *

Getting into the palace dungeons was insultingly easy, which should have worried her more than it did. The seawall gate had a grate loose enough to admit a slim woman with a grudge, and the guard pretending not to sleep had achieved such perfect sincerity that Sera could have walked past him playing a trumpet.

The cells below were damp and dim, lit by lamps that burned without flickering — still-flame, the valley folk called it, magic pinned into glass like butterflies. Everything in this city was pinned. The harbor didn't breathe. The bells rang silent. Even the gulls seemed embarrassed to make noise.

She found Odile Marchetti in the fourth cell, sitting on the floor with his back to the wall, looking less like a prisoner than a young man conducting a private argument with the ceiling.

"You look terrible," she said.

He startled upright. "Sera? How did you—"

"The same way everyone gets anywhere in Brackhollow. Nobody's watching, because nobody believes anything can change." She crouched at the bars. "Also your jailer owes me money."

"He doesn't know me."

"No, but he knows my cousin, who knows his wife, who is having an affair with my other cousin, which means the whole arrangement balances out like a tide-table." She smiled. "Everyone in this city is holding someone else's secret. It's the only currency the king's peace left us."

Odile came closer to the bars. Up close he looked worse than terrible — gray around the eyes, the kind of tired that comes from learning too much too fast. Nineteen years old and carrying a dead courier's debt, a guild's lies, and whatever that sigil was doing under his shirt. She'd seen it glow once, in the night, faint as a dying coal.

"They're going to question me tomorrow," he said quietly. "I heard the guards. Someone from the court itself. They want to know how I got past the border wards."

"And you told them?"

"That I walked."

Sera laughed despite herself. "You told the court of the Sundered Sea that you *walked* through wards that have killed everything that touched them for a century?"

"It seemed simpler than the truth."

"Oh, you're going to be fun to rescue." She pulled a bent hairpin from her sleeve and went to work on the lock. "Here's the situation. The cell door opens. We go down through the old smuggler tunnels — I know them, don't ask how — and we surface somewhere the guards won't think to look for a week. Then you and I are even, and you owe me nothing, except—"

"Except," Odile said.

She hated that he wasn't stupid. Stupid marks made everything easier. "Except," she admitted, "there's a stop we make on the way. The palace vaults. Sub-level, below the waterline. There are charts there. My family's charts. Confiscated twenty years ago, and I want them back."

The lock clicked. She pushed the door open and looked up at him, braced for the negotiation, the haggling, the wounded pride of a man being asked to commit burglary as payment for his freedom.

Instead Odile said, "Your family's charts?"

"Tide-charts. Current-maps. Six generations of them." She heard her own voice go tight and stopped, annoyed at herself. "They took everything that disagreed with the official version of the sea. Which was everything my family ever made."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "My grandmother helped build the thing that's wearing the king. The guild fed my mother to a binding I'm apparently supposed to inherit. So." He shrugged, and it was a small, bitter shrug, the shrug of someone discovering that grief comes in families. "Two inheritances, two debts. I'll get your charts."

Sera blinked. In her experience, men of nineteen required either flattery or threats to be talked into anything worth doing. This one simply agreed, as though burglary were a chore like hauling nets.

"Don't sound so noble about it," she said, to cover the strange warmth in her chest. "You'll ruin my opinion of you, and I've worked hard on that opinion."

"What is your opinion of me?"

"Currently: a fool with good posture who sleeps through pickpockets." She handed him a dark coat she'd stolen from a laundry line — his, actually, laundered and stolen back, which she felt was poetic. "Come on. Tide turns in an hour. Well. It would turn, if tides still did that. Consider us turning metaphorically."

* * *

The smuggler tunnels smelled of salt and old rope and, increasingly, of something else — a thin wrongness, like the memory of a smell rather than the smell itself. Sera noticed Odile noticing it.

"The stillness gets into everything," she said. "Walls, wood, water. Twenty years of no weather, and the stone forgets how to be stone. My grandmother used to say the sea has moods, and moods need room to move. Take away the movement—" she tapped the tunnel wall, and it gave a dead, flat knock, "—and you take away the sea."

"And what's left?"

"A body without breath." She said it lightly, and then wished she hadn't, because he looked at her with those serious valley eyes and she remembered that his whole world had ended nine days ago and he hadn't screamed once, which either meant he was very brave or hadn't finished noticing.

They descended. The tunnels narrowed, then opened into a low brick gallery where water lapped at ankle height — actual water, moving, the only moving water in Brackhollow, seeping up through cracks the stillness hadn't reached. Sera loved this place irrationally. It was the last honest thing in the city.

"The vaults are through there." She pointed to an iron door crusted with green. "Old customs house, then evidence storage, now they keep everything that frightens them. Charts. Ledgers. Books." She hesitated. "There may be a complication."

"There's always a complication."

"The door is warded. Not strongly — the king doesn't fear thieves, he fears *change*, and no thief can steal the stillness. But the ward will feel the sigil. It may... announce us."

"Announce us how?"

"I genuinely don't know. That's the exciting part."

To his credit, Odile laughed — a short, surprised laugh, as though it had escaped custody. "You enjoy this."

"I enjoy being right," Sera said. "Being right is the only pleasure they left me. Come on."

The vault beyond the door was colder than the tunnels, dry as a tomb, and stacked to the ceiling with the confiscated truth of a kingdom. Shelves of ledgers. Trunks stenciled with family names. And charts — hundreds of them, rolled and tied, hanging in racks like cured fish.

Sera found the Voss trunks in the third row, and her hands shook, and she let them, because there was no one to see.

Four trunks. Her grandmother's hand in the oldest ones, the letters slanted like wind-blown grass. Her mother's neat capitals in the newest. A whole family's argument with the sea, preserved by the very people who'd tried to silence it. She pressed her palm flat against the lid of the first trunk and stood there longer than she meant to.

When she turned, Odile was holding a roll of parchment, unrolled halfway, frowning at it.

"This is a map of the valley," he said slowly. "Of *inside* the valley. With the wards marked. Dated forty years ago." He looked up at her. "The guild said no one outside had ever mapped them."

"The guild says a lot of things." She took the chart, glanced at it, and felt the cold walk up her spine. Whoever had drawn it had known things about the wards that even the College's forbidden archives hadn't. "Someone was selling secrets both directions, or someone in your guild had friends in high, bad places."

"Or both."

"Both is usually the answer. Both is practically the motto of this coast." She rolled the chart and tucked it into his coat. "Take it. Consider it a bonus. Now help me carry my inheritance, heir-to-a-lie, before the ward decides to sing."

They each took two trunks. The Voss charts weighed almost nothing — paper and ink and stubbornness, lighter than they had any right to be. At the tunnel mouth, pausing in the moving water, Sera looked back once at the vault, at all the other names on all the other trunks. Other families. Other debts.

"We should come back," Odile said, following her gaze. "For the rest of them."

She stared at him. "You realize you just proposed robbing the palace *again*. Voluntarily."

"I realize the charts aren't ours alone to take." He shifted his trunks. "If my grandmother's binding is the reason all this was confiscated — if the stillness is the reason — then everyone down here lost something to the same lie. It seems wrong to only save our own."

And there it was again, that warmth, inconvenient as spring. Sera Voss had survived exile by trusting no one further than she could throw them, and here was this soaked valley boy talking about saving *everyone's* inheritances like it was obvious, like it was Tuesday.

"You're going to get us both killed," she said.

"Probably."

"Good. At least I'll die correctly." She turned and led the way into the dark, and found, to her considerable irritation, that she was smiling. "Tunnels fork ahead. Left goes to the chandlery, right goes to the harbor stairs. We take left, we hide the trunks, and then, Odile Marchetti, you and I are going to have a long conversation about what your journal says happens if we don't reach the anchor before the king does."

"You read my journal."

"I read everyone's journal. It's a flaw." She glanced back at him, wading through the last honest water in Brackhollow, and behind them, far above, the silent bells began to ring — no sound at all, only a shiver in the stones, like the city clearing its throat.

Odile froze. "What was that?"

Sera stood very still, listening to the silence, and for the first time in eleven years of exile she felt not anger but fear, clean and cold as deep water.

"That," she said, "was the palace realizing we're gone. Move. Quickly. And heir or no heir — if anyone asks, you're my idiot cousin from upcoast."

"I thought I was a fool with good posture."

"Cousins can be both. Keep walking."
