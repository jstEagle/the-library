## 9. Beneath the Vault of Charts

The strange thing about hiding in the place everyone was searching for, Odile decided somewhere around the third hour of crouching in salt-smelling dark, was that it worked almost too well.

"They won't come down," Sera had said, prying open a maintenance hatch behind the palace's drowned laundry — because of course the king's peace had drowned the laundry too, and no one had bothered to fix it, because nothing in Brackhollow changed, including its plumbing. "The vaults are warded against the king himself. He can't look at what's down there without hurting, and he doesn't like to hurt. He likes things *smooth*."

So they had gone down instead of out, down slick stairs and along a corridor where the water reached their knees and then their shins and then, mercifully, stopped, as if the sea itself had drawn a line and agreed to wait.

Now Odile sat on the bottom step with the courier's journal open on his thighs, reading by the light of the sigil in his palm. The iron key had begun to glow faintly since they'd entered the vault — a pale, patient blue, like moonlight remembering itself — and he had learned that if he held it near paper, the ink gave up its secrets more willingly. His grandmother's cipher, the one that had defeated him for two days in a prison cell, unraveled here like wet thread.

"Any progress?" Sera stood at the edge of the light, arms crossed, watching the stairwell. She had watched it for three hours without appearing to blink. Odile was starting to suspect she didn't, much. It was unsettling in exactly the same way the king had been unsettling, which was a thought he kept carefully locked in a box.

"Progress," he said. "I've confirmed my grandmother wrote like a woman who expected her grandchildren to be idiots."

"Kind of her."

"No, genuinely. Look." He tilted the page toward her. The letters were large, deliberate, spaced for someone reading by bad light with shaking hands. "*If you are reading this, you are either my blood or you have murdered my blood, and in the second case I have arranged several surprises for you that will make your remaining hours educational.*"

Sera snorted. Then she caught herself, as if humor were something she'd exiled along with herself, and turned back to the stairs. But Odile saw her shoulders loosen a fraction, and counted it a small victory.

He read on.

The journal's earlier pages had been about the valley — the wards, the stillness, the careful arithmetic of a prison disguised as a sanctuary. But the last third was different. Denser. The handwriting shrank as though the truth, written small enough, might be easier to carry.

*I have bound it twice,* one passage read. *Once with stone, once with blood. The stone is the anchor. The blood is the door. The guild believes the door can be welded shut forever. The guild is wrong. Doors do not understand forever. Only the sea understands forever, and the sea forgives everything except being told to stop.*

Odile read that line four times. Around him the vault breathed — a slow, tidal sound, air moving through drowned corridors far below, in and out, in and out, like something enormous sleeping.

"Voss," he said quietly.

"Mm."

"What's below us?"

"Charts," she said. "Ten thousand charts. Every map the crown ever confiscated from every tide-reader, storm-priest, and smuggler who knew too much. My family's among them." Her voice did something complicated on the word *family*. "And below the charts—"

She stopped.

"—below the charts," Odile finished, "is whatever makes the air move like that."

They looked at each other across the blue glow of the sigil. For once, neither of them made a joke. That, more than anything, told Odile how afraid she was. Sera Voss joked the way other people bled — constantly, and to prove she was still alive.

"Well," she said at last. "You're the heir. Heirs go first. That's what inheritance means, I've decided."

"That is absolutely not what inheritance means."

"It is tonight."

* * *

Below the chart room — and the chart room alone was worth the descent, aisles and aisles of rolled vellum rising to a ceiling lost in shadow, the collected memory of a coastline that no longer existed — there was a door.

Not a grand door. A workmanlike door, oak and iron, with no lock at all. Instead there was a recess in the wood, hand-shaped, and beside it words burned into the grain in Odile's grandmother's unmistakable blocky script:

*ONLY THE BINDER'S HAND. EVERYONE ELSE, PLEASE KNOCK AND WAIT.*

"She had a sense of humor," Sera said faintly.

"My mother used to say the whole valley was built as one long joke the guild forgot to laugh at." Odile flexed his fingers. They were shaking. He put his right hand into the recess anyway.

The sigil flared. The door drank the light and sighed open on hinges that hadn't moved in nineteen years and should have screamed and somehow didn't — as if even the hinges had been waiting.

Beyond lay a round chamber, dry as bone, domed, lit by nothing and yet not dark. In the center stood a table of black stone, and on the table lay a book.

It was not a large book. That was Odile's first thought, and it arrived with a kind of offended disappointment. After everything — the courier dying in the workshop doorway with the sigil going cold in his fist, the stars going out, the prison cell, the king's unblinking face — after all that, the answer to the world was a slim volume bound in gray leather no bigger than a bread loaf, sitting there patiently, the way his grandmother had probably sat through a hundred guild meetings, letting important men mistake her quietness for agreement.

"You would have liked her," he said aloud, not entirely to Sera.

"I already do," Sera said. "Go on. Touch it. If it kills you, I want to know quickly so I can start looting."

He touched it.

The moment his skin met the cover, the sigil in his other hand sang — one clear note, felt in the teeth rather than heard — and the book opened itself to the middle, to a page headed in his grandmother's hand:

*THE UNBINDING.*

*Read all of this before you feel anything. You will want to stop halfway. Do not stop halfway. Halfway is how the guild got where it is.*

So he read. And Sera, who had sworn she'd stay by the door, drifted to his shoulder despite herself, the way people drift toward fires and accidents.

*There are two ways to end a binding,* the page said. *The first is destruction. Break the anchor stone, and the binding breaks with it. This is simple. This is also suicide on a scale your imagination has not yet achieved. What is caged beneath your valley has been pressed flat for sixty years, the way a spring is pressed, the way grief is pressed. Release it all at once and it does not return to the world. It detonates into it. I have seen a fraction of it escape for a single night — you call that night the Starfall Famine, if the guild let you call it anything — and a fraction was enough.*

*The second way is assumption.*

Odile's mouth had gone dry two paragraphs ago. He made himself keep reading.

*A binder may take the bound thing into themselves. Not destroy it. Hold it. Carry it, as a vessel carries water, as the king carries it now — but freely, and whole, and with consent on both sides. This is what the guild never understood and never wanted to understand: the entity beneath the valley is not a wound in the world. It is a will in the world. And a will, offered a hand instead of a cage, may choose to be carried rather than to break free.*

*Understand the price. The vessel does not remain unchanged. What you take in, you live with. Every day. Forever. There is no rite to pass it on — I looked for one, believe me, for thirty years I looked — because passing it on is simply making another prisoner, and I refuse to build that rite. Whoever assumes the binding ends the line of binders. There is no heir after the heir. The debt stops with you, or it swallows everyone.*

*That is the bargain. Take it in, and live changed, or break the stone, and the world changes without asking anyone. There is no third door. I am sorry. I searched for a third door for most of my life. Doors do not understand forever, but neither, apparently, do I.*

Odile became aware, distantly, that he was sitting on the floor. He didn't remember sitting down.

Sera crouched beside him. She didn't touch him — she wasn't a touching person, he was learning — but she stayed close, and read over his arm, and when she spoke her voice was stripped of all its salt and armor.

"There it is," she said. "The price."

"The price," Odile agreed. His voice sounded like someone else's. Like his mother's, maybe. He was beginning to suspect he'd inherited his voice from a dead woman, along with everything else.

"Break the stone," Sera said slowly, thinking aloud the way tide-readers think, out loud, in currents, "and the thing under your valley detonates. Takes it in, and you become—" she gestured vaguely at him, at all of him, "—a *house*. Forever. With a tenant."

"With a god, possibly. Depending what it actually is."

"Do you know what it actually is?"

"No." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Isn't that wonderful? I'm the heir to a debt and I don't even know the creditor."

He turned pages with hands that wouldn't quite obey. His grandmother had left diagrams — circles within circles, an anchor stone rendered in cross-section like a heart, lines of force drawn in ink that shifted when he wasn't looking directly at it. Margin notes everywhere. Little asides. Once, in the corner of a page dense with binding-geometry, she had written: *Note to self: Aldric was right about the northern vector. Tell him. He will be insufferable.* 

And beneath it, later, in different ink, faded brown:

*Aldric volunteered. Remember that. Whatever they say of him after, remember that he knelt down into it with his eyes open, because I asked, and because someone had to hold the door while I built the walls.*

Odile stared at that for a long time.

"Sera. The king. Before the thing wore him — he was my grandmother's friend. Her *colleague*. He helped build the binding. And then he—" He couldn't finish it.

"And then the binding needed a keeper inside the cage," Sera said quietly, "and a man who loved the work said yes." She shook her head. "That's not a monster story. That's worse. That's a *volunteer* story."

*Halfway is how the guild got where it is.* Odile looked back at the page. *Do not stop halfway.*

His grandmother had known. Sixty years ago she had known exactly what her heirs would feel standing in this room: the vertigo of it, the horror of a choice with only terrible doors. She had known they'd want to slam the book shut and run home to the valley's soft silence and pretend the stars had merely blinked.

So she had written to them across the decades, in big friendly letters, like a hand on the shoulder:

*You will hate me for this inheritance. Good. Hate is honest. Use it. But hear me too: I did not choose you because you are strong. Strength is common; the guild breeds it like sheep. I chose this line because we are stubborn in the specific way required — we cannot walk past a locked thing without wanting to know what's inside, and we cannot learn what's inside without wanting to mend it. That is not a curse, child, whatever it feels like tonight. That is the whole of what I have to give you. It was given to me the same way, by someone I also resented, and I forgive her now, which took forty years, so allow yourself at least ten.*

Odile realized he was crying only when a drop hit the page and the ink welcomed it, blooming slightly, drinking it the way the door had drunk the sigil's light.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's — I'm fine."

"You're clearly not," Sera said.

"I'm *fine*, I just—" He wiped his face with the heel of his hand and laughed, wet and helpless. "She *forgave* the person who gave it to her. It took forty years and she wrote it down so I'd know forgiveness was allowed to take that long. Who — who *does* that?"

"A grandmother," Sera said, "who loved a grandchild she'd never meet, in the only way available to her: homework." And then, softer, almost against her will: "I'd have liked homework like that."

* * *

They might have stayed until morning. Odile was beginning to understand the shape of the rite — the standing-in-two-worlds of it, the anchor stone, the words that had to be spoken by someone whose blood was both the lock and the key — when the chamber changed.

It was subtle at first. The air stopped moving. The slow tidal breathing of the vaults, the in-and-out he'd stopped noticing hours ago, simply — paused. Held. Like a swimmer between strokes.

Then the gray book grew warm under his hands.

Every page began turning at once, gently, of its own accord, backward and forward, as if the book were shivering. And in the margin of the unbinding rite, beneath his grandmother's final instruction, new ink surfaced — rising up through the vellum like a body rising through water, letter by letter, in handwriting that was almost hers and was not hers at all, smoothed of every human irregularity, perfect as the king's unmoving eyes:

*HELLO, LITTLE HEIR.*

*I HAVE BEEN READING OVER YOUR SHOULDER.*

Sera was on her feet with a knife out that Odile hadn't known she carried. "Tell me you see that."

"I see it."

"Good. Because for a moment I worried I'd finally gone properly mad down here, and frankly the exile's pension doesn't cover it."

More ink rose. The chamber's not-dark dimmed, and somewhere far above them, muffled by ten thousand charts and a hundred feet of stone, Odile felt — more than heard — the first tremor of the silent bells, shivering down through the palace foundations like a swallowed scream.

*YOU FOUND THE BOOK THE OLD WOMAN HID FROM ME IN MY OWN HOUSE,* the writing climbed the page. *CLEVER BLOOD. STUBBORN BLOOD. I REMEMBER HER HAND TOO.*

The ink paused. When it resumed, the letters had grown smaller. Quieter. Almost gentle, which was infinitely worse.

*SHE OFFERED ME A HAND ONCE, YOU KNOW. THAT IS NOT IN HER BOOK. ASK YOURSELF WHY SHE LEFT IT OUT.*

*THEN DECIDE QUICKLY, LITTLE HEIR. THE BELLS ABOVE ARE FOR YOU — AND MY SOLDIERS DO NOT KNOCK AND WAIT.*

The book snapped shut.

In the sudden silence, Sera looked at Odile, and Odile looked at the door, and both of them thought the same thought at the same moment, though only Sera said it aloud:

"So. Idiot cousin. How attached are you to knowing what she left out?"
