## 10. Hollow Bells

The first thing Sera noticed about freedom was that it sounded wrong.

She and Odile had come up through the vault stairs and out into the palace's lower courtyard, past two guards who were either bribed, asleep, or — she suspected — simply no longer certain what they were guarding or why. The night air of Brackhollow should have hit them like a wet wool blanket, thick with brine and tar smoke and the particular sourness of fish left too long on the quay. Instead it felt thin. Scrubbed. Like air that had been told to behave.

Then the bells began.

Every bell in Brackhollow rang at once — the great tide-bell in the harbor tower, the chapel bells on Candlemaker's Row, the little cracked thing in the fish market that had been ringing badly since before Sera was born. She knew this city's bells the way she knew her own heartbeat. She'd grown up falling asleep to their quarter-hour arguments.

They rang now, all of them, mouths open, clappers swinging — and made no sound at all.

"Oh," said Odile beside her. "That's not good."

"You think?" Sera grabbed his sleeve and pulled him toward the alley behind the chandler's shop. "When a city's bells go silent, that's not *not good*, valley boy. That's a scream with the sound cut out of it."

He stumbled along after her, still clutching his grandmother's grimoire to his chest like a man carrying a sleeping infant he wasn't entirely sure was alive. His face was pale in the way that only people who had grown up somewhere safe could be pale. She found it, against all her better instincts, a little endearing.

"Where are we going?"

"Anywhere the bells aren't." She glanced back. The harbor tower stood black against the sky, its bell swinging madly, silently, like a bird beating itself against a window. "And then somewhere we can see the water."

"Why do we need to see the water?"

"Because I want to know how angry it is."

* * *

It turned out the water was very angry indeed.

From the high shelf of rock above the Tanners' Stair — a spot Sera had used for illegal tide-readings since she was fifteen, back when the tides had still been worth reading — they watched the sea misbehave.

Not storm. Storm would have been almost comforting. A storm had rules: wind from such a quarter, swell from another, and if you read your charts right you lived to complain about it. This was something else. The sea lurched. It rose a full fathom in the space between one wave and the next, held there like a held breath, then dropped so far that the harbor mud lay bare and gleaming under the stars — the first stars Sera had seen over Brackhollow in years, and she would have wept at them if she hadn't been busy being terrified.

"The king's peace," Odile said quietly. "It's breaking."

"It's *fraying*." Sera's hands were steady; she was proud of that. Inside, some long-starved part of her was singing. "There's a difference. A rope doesn't snap all at once. It unravels strand by strand, and every strand makes a sound, if you're listening."

"And you're listening."

"I'm always listening. It's why they exiled me."

Below them, the fishing fleet was coming home — or trying to. Sera counted the boats, out of old habit. Fourteen had gone out that morning, because even in the king's strange stillness, people had to eat. Only nine were coming back, and three of those were riding too low in the water, listing, dragging.

By the time the first boat bumped against the quay, the crowd gathering there could smell why. The hold was full of fish, and every one of them was dead. Not rotting-dead, not caught-and-stayed-too-long dead. Dead the way things are dead when something has passed over them. The herring lay silver and perfect, eyes clear, bellies unmarked, as though they'd been paused mid-swim and simply never started again.

Sera heard a woman on the quay begin to cry.

Here was the thing about Brackhollow under the king's peace, the thing Sera had tried to explain to tribunal after tribunal until they'd stopped listening: nobody cried. Nobody laughed, really, either. They went to work, ate their bread, thanked the king for his calm seas, and their eyes had the shine of glass floats. She'd hated it. She'd assumed they were broken.

But watching that woman clutch a basket of dead silver fish and weep like the world had ended — Sera realized the people hadn't been broken. They'd been *held*. And something tonight had let go.

"She remembers," Sera murmured.

"What?"

"Grief. Her brother went out on the *Kindly Merrow* two winters ago. No storm took him — there are no storms — the sea just... kept him. And she couldn't mourn him because mourning needs weather, needs movement, needs something to push against." Sera's throat tightened. "Look at her, Odile. She's grieving two years late, all at once, and she doesn't understand why, and it's going to look like madness by morning."

Odile was quiet a moment. "Is it madness?"

"No." She said it firmly enough that she half believed it. "It's the opposite. It's everyone waking up at the same time in a house that's on fire. That's not madness. That's just very bad timing."

* * *

They came down into the Warrens as the silent bells finally stopped — stopped mid-swing, all together, which was somehow worse than the ringing. In the narrow streets, lamplighters stood at their posts holding unlit torches and staring at their own hands. A baker came out of his shop door and sat down on the step and put his face in his floury hands, and Sera heard him say, over and over, "Mara. Mara. Mara," a name like a key turning in a lock.

An old man danced. There was no other word for it. He shuffled and spun in the middle of Rope Lane with his arms out, laughing — actually laughing, a rusty, wheezing sound like a pump that hadn't run in a decade — and when children gathered to stare at him, he laughed harder, and then two of the children laughed too, startled into it, the way laughter spreads, and Sera had to stop walking because her eyes had gone hot and stupid.

"Gently," she whispered, to herself, to the street, to whatever power had cracked the king's peace open. "Gently, gently. Don't shake them too hard."

"They're happy," Odile said. He sounded bewildered. "Why does it hurt to watch?"

"Because joy after stillness comes out sideways." She wiped her eyes briskly with her wrist. "You've spent nineteen years in a valley where nothing changed. Imagine if someone lifted that off you tomorrow. All the fear you swallowed, all the grief, all the wanting — it doesn't come out politely. It comes out all at once, wearing whatever clothes were nearest." She nodded down the lane, where a quarrel had broken out between two neighbors over — Sera listened — a fence post that had been leaning the wrong way for six years. Both men were shouting. Both men were also, unmistakably, *alive*, faces red, hands flying, and around them a small crowd was gathering not to stop the fight but to watch it with naked delight.

"That's the most violent thing I've ever seen," Odile said.

"That's a fence dispute. Give it an hour and they'll drink together." She pulled him onward. "Come on. We need to reach the salt-house before the king's riders remember they have orders."

* * *

The salt-house was exactly as she'd left it: dark, reeking pleasantly of brine, and stuffed to the rafters with the confiscated charts of three generations of Vosses. Her family had been tide-readers since before Brackhollow had walls, mapping the sea's moods in ink and needlework, and the crown had taken all of it the day she was exiled — except what her mother had sewn into the lining of a coat and smuggled to a sympathetic salt-merchant, who had kept it behind forty barrels and never once asked questions. Sera loved that merchant the way other people loved saints.

She lit no lamp. She didn't need one; her fingers remembered the charts the way a mouth remembers a song. She spread the great coastal chart across a barrel head and weighted its corners with salt blocks, and Odile leaned over it, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"There," she said at last, tapping the drowned road north of the city. "That's where we came in. And here—" her finger traced a line out past the shoals, through water marked with the old Voss symbols for *sings at low moon* and *do not trust the current, it flatters* — "is how we get anywhere the king's soldiers can't follow. Assuming the sea still honors these paths."

"Will it? Now that the peace is breaking?"

"Now that the peace is breaking, the sea might do anything." She looked up at him. In the faint gray light through the salt-slats, his face had lost the last of the valley's smoothness. Good, she thought. You'll need edges. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Your rite, the anchor, all of it. We're not sneaking the world back to what it was. We're letting it move again."

"And moving things can crash."

"Moving things can also *dance*. Ask the old man on Rope Lane."

Odile smiled — a real smile, tired but true — and Sera felt something in her chest loosen that had been tight since the tribunal, since the exile, maybe since childhood. Trust was a foolish investment; she'd told herself that for years, and she'd believed it right up until the moment a star-struck valley boy had handed her his grandmother's grimoire without hesitation, as if she were obviously the person who should carry knowledge. As if she were trustworthy. The nerve of him.

A sound outside made her freeze. Boots — many boots, moving in the disciplined rhythm of men who had not yet woken up, whose hearts the peace still held. Palace riders, sweeping the Warrens street by street.

"Back wall," she breathed. "There's a gap behind the barrels. Move."

They slipped out into the maze of alleys behind the fish market just as lantern-light bloomed in the salt-house windows. Sera led Odile down routes she'd known since she could walk — the passage behind the cooper's yard, the stair that wasn't on any map because she'd personally removed it from every map she could get her hands on, a girl of twelve already learning that information was the only inheritance no tribunal could confiscate.

Above the rooftops, the harbor tower's bell swung once more — a single silent arc — and stopped forever, or so it seemed.

And then, faint and far away, another bell rang. A real one, with sound. Out past the point, where the wrecks lay. Sera knew that bell; every Voss knew that bell. It hung on the drowned shrine of the Tide-Mother, off the island the charts called Saint Ede's and the sea called something older, and it had not rung in living memory, because it only rang when the sea wanted to say something important.

Her blood went cold and singing at the same time.

"Sera." Odile had stopped. He was looking at the sigil in his hand — the cold iron heirloom that had dragged him out of his valley and into all this. Its surface, which had been dull as old coin, was now glowing faintly, pulsing like a slow heart. "Something's coming. Something's coming *here*, fast. I can feel it through this."

"Can you tell what?"

He shook his head, and his voice when it came was flat with dread. "No. But it feels like the wards did. The night they broke. It feels like the guild."

Sera stared north, toward the mountains, toward the valley, and thought: *they sent someone.* Of course they did. Guilds were like tides that way — pull back hard enough and they always sent something crashing ashore after you.

"Right," she said, and heard her own voice settle into the old chart-reader's calm, the one her mother had drilled into her: *the reader is the last thing on the ship that panics.* "Then we don't wait to meet it. We take the sea-paths tonight, at slack before dawn, and we stay ahead of whatever they've loosed." She gripped his shoulder. "But Odile — whatever's coming, and whoever sent it — remember they taught it everything it knows. Which means it knows exactly how frightened they are. And people that frightened will do anything at all to get that sigil back."

Far out on the black water, the drowned shrine's bell rang again, closer now, though that should have been impossible.

Sera ran.
