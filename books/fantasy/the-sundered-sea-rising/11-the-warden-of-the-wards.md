## 11. The Warden of the Wards

The sea found us three days out of Brackhollow, and I hated it immediately.

Not the sea itself — I had made a kind of peace with the sea, in the way one makes peace with a large dog that has knocked you over twice but seems sorry about it. It was the *smell* that got me. Salt and rot and something older underneath, like the whole coast was a cellar where the world kept its forgotten things.

"You're making that face again," Sera said. She walked ahead of me on the drowned road, boots splashing through water no deeper than my ankles but moving with a purpose that suggested it might not stay that way.

"What face?"

"The face of a boy who grew up in a valley where the worst smell was bread."

"The worst smell in the valley was Master Fenwick's dye vats," I said. "But yes. Close."

She snorted and kept walking. Three days of alliance had taught me that Sera Voss communicated primarily in snorts, and that each one contained roughly a paragraph of meaning. This one meant: *you're stalling because you're scared, and I'm allowing it because I'm generous.*

She wasn't wrong. We were following the journal's directions toward a shrine on a drowned island — her idea, born of a chart she'd memorized before her exile, drawn from memory in the dirt with a stick while I watched and tried not to look too impressed — and every mile of coastline we covered felt like walking further into the mouth of something patient.

And behind us, all three days, someone had been walking the same road.

I'd felt it first as a wrongness in the sigil. The iron key rode against my chest on a cord, cold as ever, but lately it had developed a kind of *tension*, the way a plucked string holds its note after the finger leaves it. At night, when Sera slept and I kept watch, the tension sang. Not sound exactly. More like the memory of sound, the shape a bell leaves in the air.

Someone was following us, and they were carrying guild iron.

"They'll catch us at the water," Sera said now, without turning around. She'd stopped pretending she couldn't feel it too. "Whoever it is. They're faster than you think, and you walk like a man reading a very long letter."

"I walk *carefully*. There's a difference."

"There truly is not." She pointed ahead, where the drowned road bent around a headland of black rock. Beyond it, if her memory-charts held true, lay a shingle beach and the shallows where an old ferryman's skiff might still be wedged, half-buried, waiting for someone foolish enough to need it. "We make the bend, we launch, we're on open water by dark. They can follow us onto the sea if they want, but the sea doesn't love the guild the way the land does."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I asked it to drown three guild surveyors when I was nineteen, and it said no." A pause. "It said no *rudely*, mind. But it said no."

I was still deciding whether she was joking when the sigil went tight against my chest — tight enough to hurt — and Sera stopped walking so suddenly I nearly went into her back.

"He's here," she said quietly.

"I know."

"No, Odile. He's *here* here."

I looked up. On the ridge above the road, black against a sky the color of dirty wool, stood a figure in a traveler's cloak that did nothing to hide the cut of guild wool beneath it. He didn't call out. He didn't draw anything. He just stood there, and even at forty paces I knew the set of those shoulders, the particular way he held his hands still at his sides, as though movement were a courtesy he extended only when necessary.

Master Corvane.

My teacher for eleven years. The man who taught me ward-script, who taught me that a warden's first duty was attention and his second was mercy, who once spent an entire winter teaching me to whistle through cupped hands so I could call gulls that would never come to a landlocked valley, purely, he claimed, so I would have one useless beautiful thing in my life.

He started down the slope toward us.

"Run," Sera said.

"He's alone."

"That's not a reason to trust him. That's a reason to be *worried*. Guildmasters send one man when they want something done quietly."

Corvane reached the road twenty feet from us and stopped. Up close he looked terrible — hollowed, unshaved, his cloak stiff with salt spray, as though he'd walked the whole way from the valley without stopping, which knowing Corvane, he had. His eyes found mine and there was something in them I had never seen in eleven years of lessons.

Fear.

"Odile." My name in his voice sounded the way it always had at the end of a lesson — tired, fond, resigned. Then he saw Sera's hand drift toward her knife, and added, "Voss."

"Master Corvane." Her voice could have frozen the shallows. "Come to drag the boy home?"

"I've come to give you both a chance to run properly." He said it flatly, without drama, the way he used to announce pop quizzes. "There are others behind me. Two days, maybe three. Wardens. Real ones, not old teachers past their prime. And behind them—" his jaw worked, "—behind them is a thing wearing the king, and it knows where the boy is going, because it has known since the night the stars went out. You're not running away from it, Odile. You're running along a path it drew."

The wind came off the water and pushed at all three of us, and nobody moved.

"Why should we believe you?" I asked. It came out harder than I intended, and part of me flinched at that, and a larger part of me didn't.

"You shouldn't," Corvane said simply. "That's the first thing I taught you. Trust is earned; belief is given; never confuse the two." He took a slow breath. "So don't believe me. Watch me instead. Watch what I do."

"And what are you going to do?"

He reached into his cloak.

Sera's knife was out and level before his hand cleared the fabric, and I confess my own hand was halfway through a ward-gesture I only half remembered, and then Corvane — Master Corvane, who had disarmed three older students simultaneously during the incident with the stolen exam keys — knelt down in the wet gravel of the drowned road and set his sword on the ground in front of him.

Then he sat back on his heels and looked up at me with the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a very high place.

"I'm going to tell you what happened to your mother," he said. "And then you're going to decide whether I live long enough to be useful. I'd ask you to hear all of it before choosing, but I taught you better than to promise things you can't guarantee."

* * *

He talked for a long time, kneeling in the wet, and I stood there and let him, because sitting felt like forgiveness and I was nowhere near ready to spend any.

He told it plainly, the way he'd taught me to write reports — no ornament, no escape hatches. The binding beneath the valley needed an anchor of living blood, renewed each generation. The guild called it *service*. The heirs were brought to the anchor stone young, before their training finished, before they understood what they were agreeing to, and the binding drank them slowly, over years, and the wards stayed strong and the stars — until recently — stayed put.

"Your grandmother built it that way deliberately," he said. "Cruel, but careful. She bound herself first, as the anchor, and designed the rite so it could only pass to her bloodline. No stranger could be fed to it. Only family. She thought — I believe she genuinely thought — that love would keep the guild honest. That no one would feed their own children to a stone."

"And the guild said," Sera said softly, "wonderful, free labor."

"The guild said," Corvane corrected, "that the alternative was the world ending, and that grief was a price worth paying, and then it arranged matters so that the people deciding were never the people grieving." His voice didn't waver. That was somehow worse. "I was a young warden when your mother came of age. I argued against it. I wrote letters. I stood up in council and quoted her mother's own principles at them." A pause. "Then the vote passed, and I obeyed, because obedience was the first virtue they ever taught me and I was thirty-one years old and had never once questioned it. I stood at the door of the anchor chamber, Odile. My job was to make sure she didn't run. She looked at me — you have her eyes exactly — and she said, 'You're the one who teaches the children to whistle, aren't you?'"

The sea filled the silence. Somewhere behind the headland, water slapped stone with a rhythm like a slow heart.

"She knew," I said. My voice came out strange, far away. "She knew what was happening, and she — she still—"

"She walked in on her own feet. Because the alternative was the wards failing and everyone she loved dying badly. Yes." Corvane finally looked away from me, out at the gray water. "I've had nineteen years to decide whether that makes her brave or whether it makes *us* monstrous for requiring her bravery. I've landed on both, most days."

"And the guildmaster sent you," Sera said, "to bring back the heir."

"The guildmaster sent me to retrieve the sigil." He met my eyes again. "What happens to the heir afterward was left, in the council minutes, extremely vague. I've read those minutes, Odile. I helped draft them. 'Retain the asset by whatever means preserve continuity of the binding.' Your mother was somebody's asset once, too. In writing. In her file. I signed the intake form."

Something hot went through me, and for a moment I honestly didn't know which way my hands would go, and neither, judging by his face, did he.

Here is the thing I learned in that moment, kneeling-adjacent on a drowned road with murder in my throat and my oldest teacher's sword lying in the gravel between us: grief is a tool, and tools don't care whose hand holds them. The guild had used my mother's death to build walls. The entity under the valley would use it to build cages. Corvane was handing it to me openly, saying *here, this is yours, this has always been yours* — and the only question was what I would build with it.

A weapon, or a door.

I thought about him whistling lessons in the winter workshop, gulls that would never come. I thought about my mother's face in the one portrait the guild hadn't confiscated. I thought about the king's eyes that never moved.

"You could have told me," I said finally. "Any time in eleven years. One sentence. *Ask about your mother.*"

"Yes."

"You didn't."

"No." He didn't dress it up. "I told myself you'd have gotten yourself killed trying to tear the guild down, and maybe that was even true, and mostly it was just easier. Cowardice is very good at wearing wisdom's coat." He spread his empty hands. "So. You have the truth, the sword, and the man. The other wardens are two days back. If you kill me, take the road north after — the coast watch rotates at dawn, you'll have a clear window to the shrine."

Sera looked at me. She didn't say anything, which from Sera was practically a speech. It said: *your choice, your inheritance, and for what it's worth I've got a knife and no strong opinions.*

I looked at Corvane, kneeling in the wet with nineteen years of complicity on his back, and I found — to my considerable irritation — that I believed him. Not because he was convincing. Because he was terrible at it. Every lie I'd ever caught him telling involved smooth delivery. This was a man fumbling his own confession like a dropped armful of firewood.

I crouched, picked up his sword, and held it out to him hilt-first.

His face did something complicated.

"Get up, Master Corvane," I said. "You're coming with us. You know the wards, you know the guild's plans, and you know what the anchor looks like from the inside, which none of us do. You're useful. That's the only reason." I paused, because lying to him felt like a bad habit I didn't want to start. "Also, my mother trusted you at the end, apparently, and I'd like to know why before I decide what to feel about it."

"Odile—" His voice cracked on the second syllable, and for a moment he was just an old tired man in wet gravel.

"Don't thank me. Don't apologize yet either. Walk." I turned toward the headland before my face could betray anything else, and found Sera watching me with an expression I'd never seen on her — something soft and startled, quickly buried under professional scowl.

"Well," she said. "That was almost mature."

"Don't ruin it."

"I'd never dream of it." She fell into step beside me, then glanced back at Corvane hauling himself upright, joints cracking like green wood. "Fair warning, Master Warden. I exiled myself rather than serve your guild, I predicted your starless night, and I once stabbed a harbormaster. Lightly. He deserved it. So you'll be walking with two people who owe you nothing."

Corvane settled his cloak, retrieved his sword, and slung it across his back — carried, not worn, a small surrender he probably thought we wouldn't notice. We noticed.

"Understood, Voss," he said. Then, quieter, catching up beside me: "You whistle yet? The gull-call?"

"Every year on midwinter," I said, not looking at him. "No gulls in a valley. Never stopped me."

Beside me, very briefly, Master Corvane made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something breaking, and after that none of us spoke for a while, which was fine, because ahead of us the road bent around the black headland, and beyond it we could see the shingle beach, and wedged in the shallows, half-buried and waiting, an old ferryman's skiff — and standing on the sand beside it, patient as tide, a figure in palace white who had clearly been expecting us.

The sigil against my chest began, very softly, to ring.
