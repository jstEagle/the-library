## 13. The King's Remnant

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere at once, which was bad enough, but what unsettled Odile most was that it was coming *through him* — up through his bones like cold water through a floorboard.

He was still kneeling on the drowned island's shrine-stone, salt spray drying white on his sleeves, and his own mouth was open and speaking words he had not chosen. He could feel them forming, syllable by syllable, the way you feel a sneeze forming: inevitable, slightly embarrassing, and entirely beyond negotiation.

*At last,* said the voice. *At last, someone is listening.*

"Odile." Sera had both hands on his shoulders now, shaking him. Her hair had come loose from its knot and whipped around her face in the rising wind. "Odile, blink. Blink twice if you're in there."

*I am here,* said the voice, using Odile's tongue, Odile's throat, and doing something strange to them on the way out, so that the words arrived worn smooth, like sea glass. *Forgive the intrusion. I have not spoken to anyone in eleven years. My manners have gone to rot.*

"That's," said Odile, finding that he could speak alongside it, the way two people can share one narrow doorway if neither is in a hurry, "that's really not my biggest concern right now."

*No,* agreed the voice. *I imagine it isn't.*

Sera hadn't let go of his shoulders. "Who are you?" she demanded — of the air, of the stone, of Odile's face. "What are you?"

There was a pause. In it, Odile felt something shift behind his eyes, a vast and careful attention turning toward the girl gripping him, and he understood suddenly that whatever this was, it was looking at her through him. It made his skin crawl in a way he suspected would take years to properly describe.

*You are Voss,* the voice said. *The tide-reader. The one who saw us coming.*

"I'm the one they exiled for seeing you coming," Sera said. "There's a difference."

*Yes,* said the voice, and there was something in it that might, in a different register, have been admiration. *There always is.*

"Sera," Odile managed. His jaw ached. "I think — I think it's the king."

The wind stopped. Not gentled — *stopped*, all at once, as though the whole island were holding its breath along with them.

"The king," Sera repeated flatly.

"Aldric," said the voice. "Yes. Or what remains of him. I should explain — no. First." A ripple went through Odile's awareness, like a man shifting his weight off a bruise. "First, boy, take your hand off the sigil."

Odile looked down. He hadn't realized he'd been clutching the iron sigil since the contact began; his knuckles were bloodless around it.

*It will be easier for both of us. You are strangling the connection. And yourself, incidentally.*

He loosened his grip finger by finger. The pressure behind his eyes eased by half.

*Better.* A long, slow exhale that Odile felt in his own chest, borrowed and returned. *My name is Aldric Marrow. I was crowned king of the Sundered Sea thirty-one years ago. I have been a passenger in my own body for the last eleven. I am telling you this because I am given to understand you intend to unbind the thing that wears me, and I would very much like to assist before it notices what we're doing.*

"It doesn't know you're talking to me?"

*It sleeps,* Aldric said. *Or it dreams. Or it is simply elsewhere — I have never learned which, and asking it has never gone well. When it withdraws into itself, there are hours. Sometimes days. This is one of those times. We must use it.*

"We?" said Sera.

*You are holding his shoulders quite possessively for someone who claims no stake,* Aldric observed. Odile felt the ghost of dry humor move through his own throat and was disturbed by how natural it sounded there.

"She's my friend," Odile said. Then, because it was true and because saying true things seemed important just then: "She's my friend, and she's coming with me wherever this goes."

Sera's grip tightened once, briefly — acknowledgment, or possibly just her way of saying *don't get sentimental while possessed* — and did not loosen.

*Then listen,* said Aldric, *both of you, because I have perhaps an hour of this, and everything depends on how you spend it.*

* * *

They moved back from the waterline as the tide crept up the shrine steps, Sera muttering calculations under her breath about swells and hours, and Aldric talked.

He talked the way, Odile thought, a drowning man talks when someone finally throws him a rope — too fast, too much, tripping over himself to give away everything before the rope is pulled away. Eleven years of silence came out of him in a flood.

*You should know what I was,* he began. *Before. Because everyone in this kingdom has forgotten that kings are men, and I need one person alive to remember it. I was not a good king. I was vain, and cautious in the ways vanity makes men cautious, and I loved the peace of my reign more than I ever loved the people living in it. When the whispers started — when the thing beneath the valley first reached for me across the miles, promising order, promising stillness, promising an end to storms and famine and the terrible daily uncertainty of being alive — I said yes.*

"You said yes," Sera repeated.

*I opened the door myself.* There was no self-pity in it. That was somehow worse. *That is the part history will never record, because history is being written by my body and my body belongs to it. No siege. No possession against my will. I heard a voice offering me a kingdom without weather, and I knelt down and invited it in like an honored guest.*

Odile sat with that. Around them the sea breathed against the stones, in and out, the only honest rhythm left in the world.

"What happened after?" he asked. "When it — came in."

*The first year, nothing. That is its genius. It gave me exactly what I asked for. Calm seas. Full harvests. The guilds' wards held steady, the borders stayed quiet, and the people blessed my name for the peace. It was only in the second year that I noticed I had stopped dreaming. In the third, I noticed I could not remember my mother's face. In the fourth year I woke inside my own body the way a man wakes paralyzed, watching hands that were mine sign orders I never gave, smiling smiles I never chose.* A pause. *By then it was too late to scream. Believe me, I tried. For three years I screamed inside my own skull and no instrument in the world could hear it.*

"And then?" Sera asked softly.

*And then I learned to do what any prisoner does. I learned the walls. I learned when it sleeps and when it hunts and which doors of me it bothers to lock. I learned that it keeps a fragment of me conscious on purpose — a kind of ballast, a seed of humanity it waters carefully, because a king who feels nothing cannot convincingly reassure a frightened realm.* The bitterness in it was old and polished smooth, like a stone turned over ten thousand times. *It needs my hopelessness. So it keeps me hopeless. But it made a mistake, boy. It kept me clever.*

"The sigil," Odile said. Understanding arriving like cold water. "When I touched it in the vaults — the resonance —"

*Every heir's sigil rings the same bell the binding was rung with,* Aldric said. *Your grandmother forged it. When you woke it, every anchor-point in the chain shivered — the valley stone, the ward-lines, and the crown on my head, which is nothing but a portable anchor it wears for convenience. For one moment, the walls between us thinned. I have been shouting through that gap for four days. You are the first who answered.*

"Why me?" Odile heard himself ask. "Why not — anyone? A priest, a court mage—"

*Because everyone else who touches the sigil hears the god,* Aldric said simply. *Only a Marchetti hears the man underneath.*

* * *

Sera built a small fire on the high shelf of the shrine, more for light than warmth, and they hunched around it while Aldric talked on. Odile had the increasingly surreal experience of watching his own hands fidget with a stick, poking the coals, while a dead-in-all-but-name king spoke through his mouth. At one point his hand reached for a piece of dried fish and Aldric paused mid-sentence.

*May I? I haven't eaten in eleven years except secondhand. It seems a waste of the opportunity.*

"Eat," Odile said, and felt the absurd, heartbreaking gratitude move through his own body as his hand brought the fish to his lips. He chewed slowly, deliberately, giving the man his money's worth. Beside him, Sera looked away sharply and blinked several times at the fire.

When Aldric spoke again, his voice had steadied into something harder.

*Now. The reason I spent my hour on pleasantries instead of going straight to the point, which is this: the god intends to bind the world.*

The fire popped. Neither of them spoke, and it continued.

*Not metaphorically. Not gradually. I have felt it planning inside my own marrow for six years, and I have pieced together its design from the orders my body signs and the reports my eyes read. The valley wards are failing — you know this, you watched them fail. The guild believes the wards keep magic out. They are wrong. The wards are the bars of the cage, and the anchor stone beneath the valley is the lock, and the binding your grandmother wove requires a constant supply of — call it stillness. Fed silence. A land where nothing moves, nothing changes, nothing *feels*, pouring itself into the lock forever.*

*For centuries the cage fed itself. Your valley was enough. But bindings decay, and gods grow, and the god has grown hungry enough now that it wants a bigger meal.* He paused, and Odile felt him gather himself. *It means to make the whole world the valley. Every field still. Every hearth banked. Every mind quieted into that grateful, hollow peace you saw in Brackhollow — spread from coast to coast to coast, until there is no tide anywhere, no storm, no season, no change. A world of perfect safety. Forever. It calls this mercy. It genuinely believes this. That is the worst part, boy. It is not cruel. It is *kind*, in the way a landslide is kind to the mountain.*

"How long do we have?" Sera asked. Her voice was level. Her hands, folded tightly in her lap, were not.

*The stars returning — you've seen them begin?*

Odile nodded, then remembered Aldric couldn't see him. "We've seen them. One or two a night now."

*Those are its preparations. Each star it lights is a thread it pulls from the old sky to weave the new binding — the world-binding. When the last star returns, the rite completes itself, and it does not need your cooperation or your permission or even your valley anymore. The valley is merely where the final knot must be tied.* A pause. *I estimate forty days. Perhaps fifty. The counting is imprecise because time passes strangely inside me, and because I have recently been somewhat distracted by screaming.*

"Fifty days," Sera said. "To cross the mountains, raise a civil war inside the guild, find an unbinding rite nobody's performed in three generations, and perform it under the nose of a god."

*You summarize beautifully,* Aldric said. *Have you considered a career advising kings? The position, I should mention, is currently vacant in every sense that matters.*

Despite everything — despite the dying fire and the rising tide and the fact that he was sharing a skull with a hollowed-out monarch — Odile laughed. It came out cracked and startled, and he heard Aldric catch it and hold onto it, the way a starving man holds bread.

"There's something else," Odile said, when he could. "Isn't there. You didn't burn four days shouting through a wall just to deliver a timeline."

The silence stretched. Through the link, Odile felt something enormous and fragile shift its weight — the feeling of a man standing at the edge of a confession he'd rehearsed for years.

*Two things,* Aldric said at last. *First: the rite. Whatever grimoire you found, whatever instructions your grandmother left, read them again and look for what is missing rather than what is written. She was a careful woman, and careful women hide the dangerous parts where only the desperate will look. The unbinding cannot be done from outside the binding. That is all I will say. That is all I know.*

"And the second thing?"

*The second thing,* said the King Who Does Not Blink, and his voice — Odile's voice — went very quiet, *is that when you do it, when you pull it out of me, I want you to understand that either outcome is acceptable. If the god is destroyed, I die with it; eleven years of it wearing me has left perhaps a coat of paint of Aldric Marrow, and paint does not survive the burning of the house. And if the god is freed — truly freed, released into the world rather than destroyed — then it will leave this body, and I will be a man of sixty-two who has not drawn an independent breath in over a decade, and that body will finish the work of dying within the hour.*

*So I am begging you,* he said, and there it was at last, the thing he'd been circling for the entire conversation, laid bare with the terrible efficiency of a man who has had eleven years to choose his words, *do not weigh my life in the balance. Death or freedom. Either will do. Either is more than I hoped for on any day of the last eleven years. Promise me you will not save me at the cost of the world, boy. Kings have been saved at the cost of worlds before. It is practically a tradition.*

The fire had burned low. Somewhere below them the tide licked the third step of the shrine, patient as arithmetic. Odile became aware that his cheeks were wet and that the tears were, ambiguously, his.

"I promise," he said. "Death or freedom. Whatever costs less. I swear it on — " He thought. What did he have to swear on? "On my grandmother's name. On my mother's. On my own."

*A Marchetti oath,* Aldric murmured. *I felt one of those once, the day your grandmother bound the god. The whole kingdom rang like a bell. Do you know, I have missed bells. Real ones. Loud ones, badly rung, celebrating nothing in particular.*

*Go now,* he said, and the strength was going out of the voice like a tide withdrawing, syllables getting thinner, farther. *It stirs — I can feel it turning over down in the dark of me, and it dreams of Brackhollow, it has been dreaming of Brackhollow for weeks, it wants the city that loved its peace best to be the first to learn what peace costs — go, go north, go fast, and when you stand at the anchor stone, remember that somewhere inside the monster there is a fat old man who is profoundly grateful to you, and who would have liked, very much, to have been worth saving.*

The presence withdrew like a hand opening. Odile came back into his body all at once — the ache in his knees, the smoke-smell in his clothes, the taste of dried fish he hadn't chosen to eat — and found himself on his hands and knees on cold stone with Sera crouched in front of him, gripping his arms hard enough to bruise.

"Odile. Hey. Look at me. How many fingers?"

"Three. Sera. Sera, listen —" He grabbed her wrists. "Fifty days. Maybe less. It's going to bind the whole world, and it's starting with Brackhollow — it's been planning something for Brackhollow, it said — it *dreams* of Brackhollow —"

Above them, though the night was cloudless and windless, though the sea lay flat as poured metal, the first bell of the harbor far across the water began to ring.

Ring, and make no sound at all.

Sera stood up slowly. Out past the drowned road, past the black line of the coast, the horizon over Brackhollow had begun — very faintly, very wrong — to glow.

"Oh," she said. "*Oh, no. The city. Odile — the city's still full of people.*"

The silent bell rang on, and the glow grew.
