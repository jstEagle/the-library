## 14. Storm Over Brackhollow

The water came for the Saltrow district first, and it came politely.

That was the worst part, Sera thought later — that it didn't roar or crash or behave like any flood she'd ever read in the old charts her grandmother had inked by candlelight. It simply rose, quiet as a debt collector, sliding up between the cobbles of Saltrow Lane until it stood knee-deep in the streets at midnight, reflecting a sky with no stars and no moon and no business being so calm.

She knew before the first scream. She felt it in her teeth, the way she always had — the tide-reader's ache, the one that had gotten her exiled from the guild hall on Tide's End for the crime of being right about the starless night. The sea was not rising anymore. It was *reaching*.

"Odile." She shook him awake in the loft above Maren's chandlery, where they'd been sleeping in shifts since the vaults. "Get up. It's started."

He sat up too fast and cracked his head on a drying net, which under other circumstances would have been worth savoring. "Started? What's—"

"The king knows we took the grimoire. And he's not sending soldiers." She was already pulling on her boots, already doing the arithmetic she hated: how many streets, how many doors, how many people who had looked through her all these years like she was a stain on the harbor wall. "He's sending the sea."

By the time they reached the top of Chandler's Stair, the lower districts were gone — not destroyed, *drowned*, whole neighborhoods standing in black water to their second stories, lamplight wobbling on the surface like drowned stars. And the people were coming up. Hundreds of them, wading, carrying children and chickens and one memorably stubborn pig, all of them moving with the terrible slow calm of folk who had never once in their lives been told to run.

"Nobody runs," Sera said, half to herself. "They've never had to. They don't know how."

"They'll learn," Odile said grimly, "or you'll teach them."

She looked at him. Nineteen years old, valley-born, three weeks into a world he'd been raised to fear, holding a dead woman's grimoire against his chest like a shield — and looking at her with absolute, infuriating confidence.

"I'm exiled," she said.

"You're *useful*," he corrected. "There's a difference. Ask anyone."

* * *

The secret sea-paths had come down to her the way everything did — from her grandmother Vessa Voss, tide-reader, chart-maker, and the only person in Brackhollow's history to be remembered fondly by both the guild and the smugglers. The paths weren't roads, exactly. They were *certainties*: channels cut deep enough to hold air even at high water, vaulted passages beneath the warehouse quarter where the old city's foundations met the sea wall, stairways that surfaced in courtyards and cellar doors, all mapped in faded ink on charts the guild had confiscated forty years ago and locked in the palace vaults — charts Sera had finally seen again three days ago, thanks to a nervous boy with an iron sigil and a talent for being in the wrong place.

Now those charts lived folded inside her coat, and her coat was getting very wet.

"This way!" She stood on the fountain rim in Gullmarket Square, water swirling around her calves, addressing a crowd of maybe two hundred soaked and bewildered citizens. "Everyone from Saltrow and the Netmenders' Row — follow me, single file, hold the rope! There are passages under the warehouses, dry ground all the way to the Heights!"

A fishwife squinted at her. "You're that Voss girl."

"I am."

"The one who said the stars would go out."

"That's also me."

"She said the stars would go out," the fishwife announced to the crowd, in the tone of a woman presenting credentials, "and then the stars went out." A murmur ran through the crowd — not gratitude exactly, but something adjacent to it, something Sera had never once heard directed at herself: *belief*.

"Well," she said, slightly dazed. "Follow me anyway."

They followed. All of them, and more behind them as word spread through the drowning streets — the rope line growing longer, the passages filling with the smell of wet wool and lamp oil and livestock, the pig expressing its opinions at length. Odile brought up the rear with Maren the chandler, an enormous woman of sixty who had appointed herself the expedition's quartermaster on the grounds that someone had to stop people from bringing furniture.

"Maren," Sera heard him say somewhere behind her, breathless, "you cannot save the loom."

"My mother wove that loom."

"And your mother is currently saving your *grandmother*, which seems like a better use of the family."

Sera almost laughed. Almost. Then she turned a corner in the passage and saw, through a grate meant for drainage, the water outside — still rising, still silent, and now moving with purpose, curling around the grate's bars like fingers testing a lock.

It was looking for them. It was *herded* by something that wore a king like a glove, and it knew exactly which passages held the thieves who had stolen its grimoire.

She kept walking, and said nothing, and walked faster.

* * *

By dawn-gray nothing — there being neither sun nor proper night anymore, just a bruised perpetual twilight — they had moved six hundred souls to the Heights, the high terrace where Brackhollow's oldest families had built their homes back when the sea was a thing you admired rather than survived. The Heights folk stood in their doorways watching the procession of dripping commoners arrive in their courtyards, wearing expressions Sera recognized intimately, because she'd worn them herself for years: the expression of people who believed safety was a place rather than a practice.

An old man in a fine gray coat blocked the gate to the upper terraces. "This is private ground."

"So is the bottom of the sea," Sera said, "and look how well that's working out for everyone." She stepped past him. To her lasting astonishment, he let her.

What else could he do? The water below had swallowed the Harbor Quarter entirely now, and the great bells of the Temple of Tides rang without sound — hollow bell after hollow bell, mouths open, voices gone, the king's perfect silence fraying at last into something everyone could see. Fish lay belly-up in fleets across the flooded streets. And here and there among the refugees, people had begun to *feel* things — really feel them, loudly, for perhaps the first time in years. A woman wept over a cat. Two brothers shouted at each other with tremendous joy about whose fault the pig was. An entire family hugged on a doorstep for no reason anyone could name, and couldn't stop.

The stillness was breaking. The entity's peace, stretched thin by the theft of its grimoire and the waking of the anchor far away in the valley, was tearing — and what came through the tear was ordinary human messiness, and it looked, Sera thought, like the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen.

"Voss!"

She turned. Tam Broadstairs, harbormaster's clerk, the closest thing Brackhollow had to a friend of hers before exile — a round, anxious, loyal man who had once smuggled her contraband tea on the grounds that "the ban on eastern blends is clearly a clerical error."

"Tam. You're alive."

"Barely. The water took my office, which I choose to read as a professional blessing." He caught his breath. "There are people trapped in the Undertown cisterns — the old ones, the ones off your gran's charts. Maybe fifty. The water's found two of the entrances already."

Sera closed her eyes and saw the charts, every line of them, inked in her grandmother's careful hand. *The Cistern Walk. The Long Throat. Vessa's Folly.* She had spent ten years hating this city for what it did to her name, and now her name was the only map out of the dark, and there was no version of herself she respected that wouldn't go.

"I need rope, lanterns, and everyone strong enough to carry a child," she said. "And Tam? If anyone asks who's in charge—"

"It'll be a cold day in the Sundered Sea," Tam said solemnly, "before I let anyone think it's me."

* * *

The cistern rescue took everything she had.

The Undertown passages ran beneath the drowned districts like veins beneath skin, and the water was in them now, patient and searching, rising a hand's width every hour. Sera went in with Odile, Maren, twelve volunteers, and the pig — the pig having apparently decided it belonged wherever Maren went, an attachment Sera chose to find touching rather than alarming.

Twice the water nearly had them. Once it came down the Long Throat in a smooth black wave while the last family was still climbing, and Odile — pale, shaking, the sigil blazing cold against his chest — planted himself at the passage's mouth and *held*, some inherited instinct of binding running through him like a struck bell, buying them ninety seconds that cost him a nosebleed and three days of pallor. Once Sera misjudged a turn and led nine people into a dead end, and stood there in the lantern light with her grandmother's charts trembling in her hands and failure rising in her throat like the sea itself—

—and then Maren put a hand on her shoulder and said, mildly, "Well. We're lost. Good thing we've got a lost-and-found person," and pointed at the small carved mark on the wall that Sera's grandmother had left forty years ago for exactly this kind of idiot descendant: an arrow, and beneath it, in the old shorthand, *breathe, girl.*

They got all fifty out. Every one. Plus the cat, plus the pig, plus a stowaway raccoon nobody could account for and nobody wanted to discuss.

When Sera climbed out of the last hatch onto the Heights, into the crowd of hundreds she had pulled from the dark, they did not cheer, because Brackhollow folk were out of practice. But they parted for her. They looked at her. Someone pressed a cup of hot broth into her hands, and an old netmender said, "Your gran's girl," like a title, like a name that meant something good.

Sera Voss, who had spent her whole life believing belonging was something you inherited or were denied, stood in the bruised twilight holding broth, and understood at last that it was neither. It was a thing you *did*. It was rope and lanterns and going back down into the dark one more time.

"All right," she said to Odile, hoarse, soaked, inexplicably smiling. "I take back most of what I've said about this city."

"Only most?"

"The pig can still drown."

The pig snorted indignantly. Somewhere in the crowd, the two brothers started arguing again, happily.

Then the light changed.

Not the twilight — the *water*. Out beyond the harbor mouth, where the Sundered Sea stretched flat and obedient to the horizon, the surface began to move against every law Sera had ever charted: drawing back, drawing *up*, gathering itself into a single vast swell that stood, for one impossible moment, like a wall between Brackhollow and the open deep. And in the heart of that wall of water, lit from within by something cold and ancient, Sera saw a shape — vast, coiled, grieving, furious — and heard, not with her ears but in the tide-ache behind her teeth, a voice older than the guild:

*Return what is mine, heir, or I will take the city stone by stone and teach your valley what drowning means.*

Beside her, Odile went white. His hand found the sigil. "It's not attacking yet," he whispered. "It's *waiting*. Sera — it wants us to come to it."

"Then we'd better decide," Sera said, staring at the wall of water, at the mountain road beyond it, at the long climb home toward the valley and the anchor stone, "whether we walk to it on our own feet — or swim."
