## 15. Corvane's Confession

They carried Master Corvane into the fish-smoker's cottage on a door torn off its hinges, because there was nothing else in the drowned quarter of Brackhollow big enough and flat enough and — this was the detail Odile kept snagging on, the way his mind always went for the absurd when grief came knocking — nothing else *smelling enough like herring*.

The door had been painted green once. The paint was peeling in long curls, and where it had peeled, the wood was stained dark with the water that still seeped out of the man's coat every time someone shifted him. He was soaked through. He had been soaked through since they pulled him from the flood-choked lane below the salt market, and he would stay soaked through until the end, whenever the end decided to arrive. It seemed rude somehow, the way the sea clung to him. Like it was keeping a piece.

"Put me down near the wall," Corvane said. His voice was a ruin — gravel dragged over gravel — but the *manner* in it was intact, that dry classroom authority that could make a boy of twelve feel simultaneously scolded and cared for. "Near the wall. I've spent forty years sleeping against walls. I'm not going to change my habits now."

Odile knelt beside him on the smoker's gritty floorboards and did as he was told, easing the old man's shoulders down against the stone. Sera hovered at the edge of the room with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore when she wanted very much to help and would rather drown again than admit it.

"He needs a physicker," she said.

"The physickers are pulling bodies out of the Tanners' Row," Odile said quietly. "There are people ahead of him."

"I know there are people ahead of him." Sera's jaw tightened. "I said what I said anyway."

Corvane laughed. It turned into coughing almost immediately, wet and terrible, and Odile's hands moved without asking his permission — one bracing the man's shoulder, one finding the thin wrist, feeling for the pulse beneath skin gone the color of old candle wax. The pulse was there. Small. Stubborn. Very much like its owner.

"Don't fuss," Corvane managed. "I have spent four decades teaching wardwrights not to fuss. Fussing is how you crack a seal."

"You're not a seal," Odile said.

"No," Corvane agreed. "But I've been carrying one."

* * *

The flood had come at dusk two days past, when the thing wearing King Aldric's face had finally tired of patience and simply told the sea to rise. Three districts of Brackhollow had gone under in an hour. Sera had led four hundred souls out through her secret paths — the smugglers' tunnels, the drowned culverts, the old eel-fishers' stairways that no chart but hers remembered — and Odile had spent those same hours doing the only useful thing he knew how to do: holding the sigil and pushing back, thread by thread, against the stillness that poured into the streets behind the water. Where he stood, things stayed *moving*. Fish kept gasping instead of lying dead and glassy-eyed. People kept weeping instead of standing in the floodwater with that awful grateful blankness the king's peace wore like a mask.

He hadn't seen Corvane go down. That was the worst of it, or one of the worsts, ranked somewhere alongside the memory of his mother's grave and the sound the bells made when they rang with no sound at all. One moment his old teacher had been at his shoulder in the churning dark, shouting instructions over the roar — *left, boy, LEFT, do you want to die literate or alive* — and the next moment the water had folded between them like a page turning, and Odile had been swept on with the crowd, and Corvane had not surfaced again until the tide-readers pulled him out near dawn, half-drowned, ribs stove in, internal bleeding that no one in a flooded city could do a thing about.

Now it was evening again. Candlelight. Herring smell. A dying man propped against a wall who kept trying to teach.

"You're sitting wrong," Corvale said.

"I'm sitting fine."

"You're sitting like a guild apprentice waiting to be graded. Shoulders up. Hands clasped. You look like you're about to recite the Nine Principles of Containment." A pause; breath rattling in and out of him like water through a broken pipe. "Good. Recite them."

"What? Why?"

"Because if your voice works, then mine doesn't have to carry the whole conversation, and I am extremely tired." Another pause, shorter. "Also because I want to hear whether you say them like a believer or like a man who's read the footnotes."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then, because it was easier than arguing with a dying man, and because some part of him understood that this was a kindness being offered — a rope thrown across a gap — he recited them.

"*The world is wild. Wildness is wound. The wound must be closed.*"

"There," Corvane said softly. "You see? You got to the third principle and your voice did a thing. A little hitch. Like a cart hitting a rut." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gray-lipped and bloodless, and also unmistakably the smile of the man who had taught eleven-year-old Odile to hold a quill, to read a ward-line, to gut a fish on the family workbench without gagging. "When I was young I believed all nine principles the way I believed in breakfast. When your grandmother wrote them—" he stopped himself, corrected, "—when *Mirella* wrote them, they were true. They were true for a hundred years. Truths rot, Odile. Even good ones. Especially good ones, because nobody checks a good truth for rot. We just keep eating it."

"Master—"

"Hold on." He raised one finger, and even now, even like this, the gesture had such institutional weight behind it that Odile actually stopped talking. Forty years of classrooms lived in that finger. "Before anything else. Before confessions, before seals, before whatever speech you've been drafting in that head of yours — I can see it, you draft speeches the way your mother drafted letters, three times and never sent any — before all of that, I owe you a correction. A teacher's last duty. You've had a hard week of learning that everything you were taught was built on a lie. And that's nearly right. But not quite." His eyes found Odile's, and they were clear, painfully clear, clearer than Odile had ever seen them in a classroom. "It wasn't built on a lie. It was built on a *fear*. A fear so old and so large that we mistook it for bedrock. Lies can be torn down, boy. Fear has to be *outgrown*. Do you understand the difference?"

"I think," Odile said slowly, "that it means you're about to ask me for something harder than forgiveness."

Corvane laughed again, and this time the laugh didn't turn into coughing, which somehow made it worse. "Ohh. Oh, Mirella's blood, no mistake there. Yes. All right." He tried to sit up straighter and failed, and Odile helped him, and the helping meant putting his hands on the man's body and feeling how little of it was left that worked. Under the sodden coat, the ribs moved wrong. Under the sodden shirt, something was wrapped around the man's torso — cloth, many layers of it, wound tight from hip to collarbone like a bandage, except bandages don't get worn for decades.

* * *

"It was the night your mother died," Corvane said.

Sera, who had drifted nearer despite herself, sat down on an upturned crate. She didn't pretend she wasn't listening. Sera Voss had never once pretended anything in her life, which was why she'd been exiled, and why Odile trusted her more than he'd ever trusted anyone raised inside the valley's polite silences.

"She came to the guildhall at midnight. Soaked to the bone, no lantern, no escort — she'd swum the border pools, do you understand? Swum them, through the wards' blind spot, the same blind spot your courier used, the same blind spot the entity itself is even now pouring through. She'd been outside for three years by then. Exiled, in everything but paperwork, because she asked questions about the anchor stone that the elders had voted unanimously never to answer." His breath caught; steadied. "She put her hand flat on the council table and she said, *I know what's under the valley. I know what you feed it. I'm going to unbind it.*"

Odile's mouth had gone dry. "Feed it?"

"Every heir, boy. Every inheritor of the binding, generation after generation. Not their lives — the binding takes something smaller and crueler than lives. It takes their *stillness*. Their capacity to rest. Each binder who holds the key carries a fraction of the cage's weight, and each one bends a little further under it, and each one dies a little earlier, and each one passes the whole of it, plus interest, down to the next." He looked at Odile with an expression that was not apology — Odile realized, with a strange lurch, that the man was too honest for apology, that apology would have been a mercy and Corvane had decided Odile deserved truth instead. "Your mother held it for six years. Your grandmother held it for fifty. You've held it for a season and already you dream standing up. Don't bother looking surprised. I watched you sleep in the camp above the coast road. Everyone in the valley dreams standing up eventually. We call it *watchfulness*. We give it medals."

"And my mother came back," Odile said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "To unbind it. And the guild—"

"The guildmaster ordered her sealed into the anchor chamber. Alive. To strengthen the binding — a willing binder strengthens it, unwilling ones just burn out faster. She fought. Four wardwrights died. She was winning, Odile. She was *winning*, and then Corvane—" the old man's voice did something it hadn't done yet, cracked clean through the gravel, "—then I put a stasis-writ on her from behind. Full paralysis. I was her teacher too. Did I mention that? Everyone forgets I taught her first. Eleven years old, couldn't hold a quill straight. Best student I ever had. Worst decision I ever made, and I made it in four seconds, because the elders said the world would end, and I believed them, because believing them was easier than being the man who let the world end."

Silence. The candle guttered. Somewhere outside, in the ruined city, ordinary sounds were happening — a dog barking, water dripping, someone singing very badly three streets over — and every one of them felt obscene and precious at once.

"You killed her," Odile said. There was no heat in it. That frightened him most of all. Heat would have been simple.

"No," Corvane said. "The entity killed her, slowly, from inside its cage, the way a prisoner kills his jailers by making jailers of them. But I handed her to it. Yes. I will not argue semantics with you. Arguing semantics is how cowards pray." He drew a long, shuddering breath. "And then I did the second-worst thing of my life, which was to volunteer to carry the founding seal. Out of the vault. Out of reach of the guildmaster. Because I no longer trusted the men I'd obeyed, and because carrying it meant I'd be the one who decided, someday, whether it reached the next heir — and I thought, in my pride, that I would know better than anyone when the time was right." A pause. "Forty years, boy. I slept with it strapped to my chest for forty years. You want to know why I never married, why I took the cold rooms in the east wing, why I flinched every time a student hugged me at graduation? Iron remembers. Founding iron especially. It sits against your heart and it hums, very quietly, all night, every night, and after ten years you stop hearing it and start *being* it. I have been furniture for a lock since before you were born."

"Why tell me this?" Odile asked. "Why now?"

"Because I'm dying, and dying men get one luxury: they can say the unsayable without having to live in the house afterward." The ghost of the classroom smile. "And because the time is right, and I was wrong about knowing when. It's not when the heir is ready. There is no ready. It's when the heir *chooses*. And you chose, Odile. In the flood. I saw you stand in the water and refuse to let things stop moving. I've waited forty years for someone to choose that. I just expected it to take longer and hurt less, and I expected, frankly, that it wouldn't be the son of the woman I paralyzed." He closed his eyes. "Mercy was never my discipline. Patience was. Patience is easy — it's just cowardice with a calendar. Mercy you have to practice daily, like scales. Teach me some, before the end. I'd like to learn it properly, at last."

* * *

Unwrapping the seal took a long time, because Corvane insisted on doing it himself, refusing all help with small furious gestures, and because his hands shook, and because there was a great deal of cloth. Layer upon layer, decade upon decade, the wrappings stiff with age and sweat and salt, and Odile found himself thinking absurdly of the mummified saints in the chapel alcove at the guildhall, the ones the apprentices dared each other to touch.

Beneath the last layer lay a plate of black iron the size of a spread hand, shaped like a wave frozen mid-curl, etched so densely with script that it looked less engraved than *infested*. The moment the air touched it, the sigil in Odile's pocket woke — he felt it through the fabric like a second heartbeat, urgent, recognizing kin — and the candle flames all leaned toward the seal together, as if bowing.

"That's the founding seal," Sera said. Not a question. She'd gone very still. "That's *Mirella Marchetti's own hand*. Tide-readers have songs about that seal. We thought the guild melted it down generations ago."

"The guild thinks so too," Corvane rasped. "That was rather the point." He lifted it — both hands, trembling, an effort visibly costing him — and held it out. "The rite needs three anchors. The sigil you carry is the key. The grimoire is the word. This is the *weight* — the original covenant, the thing that makes the unbinding an unbinding instead of just an explosion. Without it, you cannot free the king without freeing what's under him. With it..." He swallowed. "With it, you can choose. Which is all anyone ever wanted, isn't it? All any of us ever wanted. We were just too afraid to pay for it."

Odile took the seal. It was heavier than iron had any right to be — heavier than grief, colder than the valley in winter — and the moment his fingers closed around it, the humming Corvane had described transferred itself, faint and ceaseless, into his palms. He understood immediately that he would hear it every night for the rest of his life, and that this was the inheritance, the real one, the one no bloodline could dodge: not power. Weight.

"Say something," Corvane whispered. "Anything. Curse me. I'd take a curse gladly. A curse is at least addressed to someone."

Odile looked at him for a long time — at the wreck of the man, at the teacher, at the jailer, at the boy who had once been someone's best student — and found that the words available to him were all either lies or cruelties, and that he refused to offer either.

"Not yet," he said finally. "I can't forgive you tonight. Maybe not tomorrow. But I'll carry you with us up the mountain, and you'll teach me the rite's foundations while you can still talk, and when it's done — if it's done — I'll come back to this question with better answers than I have now. That's not mercy. That's just... patience done honestly. Cowardice with a calendar. You taught me that."

For a moment Corvane said nothing. Then he began to laugh — really laugh, deep and helpless, wincing around his broken ribs, tears sliding sideways into the gray stubble of his cheeks — and Sera looked at Odile over the dying man's shaking shoulders and mouthed, *you're both impossible,* and Odile discovered that laughing while crying and grieving was apparently something the human body could do all at once, like playing three instruments badly in the same orchestra.

"All right," Corvane wheezed, when he could speak. "All right. Better answers later. I accept the terms. Now — the foundations. Fetch ink. Fetch paper. My handwriting's shot, so you'll write, and I'll dictate, and we'll begin where Mirella began, with the first principle of the *true* covenant, the one the guild cut out of the Nine Principles because it was too honest to teach children—"

Outside, far away across the drowned city, a bell began to ring.

Not soundlessly, this time. It rang low and strange and *urgent*, over and over, and Sera was on her feet with her head cocked, reading the tone the way she read tides, and the color drained out of her face.

"That's the harbor bell," she said. "Odile. That's the *storm bell*. The real one. The sea's coming up the harbor stairs — and it's not the king's doing." Her eyes went wide. "Something's answering the seal. Something on the water. And it's *fast*."
