## 16. The Long Climb

The mountains had a way of making you honest.

By the third day of the climb, Odile Marchetti knew three things about himself with absolute certainty: he was not built for altitude, his boots had been designed by someone who hated feet, and the grimoire did not like being read by firelight.

"It doesn't *dislike* it," Sera Voss said from across the campfire, not looking up from her charts. "It's a book. It has no feelings."

"It has *entirely* feelings. Look at it."

He held the grimoire up so the firelight caught its pages. In daylight, the ink sat obediently where his grandmother had put it, neat columns of script and diagrams like any other book. But by flame, by any flame, the letters began to drift — sliding a half-line up, then down again, as though the page were breathing, or as though someone beneath the paper were trying to read along over your shoulder.

"That's not feelings," Sera said. "That's tide-logic. Water remembers movement. Fire makes everything move. Your grandmother bound her notes in salt-water inks so they'd only hold still for someone patient enough to read them slowly." She finally glanced up, and there was the ghost of her usual dry smile. "You're just impatient."

"I'm being *pursued*, Sera. Pursuit encourages impatience."

"Pursuit also encourages falling off cliffs. Read slower."

Corvane, sitting at the edge of the firelight with his wounded side propped against a boulder, made a sound that might once have been a laugh. It came out of him rusty, like a gate hinge nobody had oiled in forty years, which Odile supposed was an accurate description of Master Corvane himself these days.

"Something funny?" Odile asked.

"Your mother said nearly the same thing. About the book." Corvane's eyes stayed on the dark slope below them, where the switchback trail descended into blackness. "She read it slow. She read it very well." A pause. "They fed her to it anyway."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a long sighing sound, like the mountain itself disapproving of the conversation. Odile looked down at the grimoire in his lap — at his grandmother's handwriting, at the margins where she'd pressed flowers that were now forty years flat — and felt the old anger rise in him, hot and familiar, and then do something new: settle. Cool into something heavier and more useful.

Not rage. Resolve.

"We should sleep in shifts," he said. "Sera first watch. The soldiers won't stop at the treeline."

"They haven't stopped anywhere," Sera agreed, rolling out her bedroll with the economy of someone who'd slept badly in many places. "Still-bound don't tire, don't eat, don't complain about their boots." She shot him a look. "Lucky them."

* * *

The soldiers had found them on the second morning, far below on the drowned road's last surviving stretch, and Odile would remember the sight of them for the rest of his life — however much of it remained.

There had been twelve of them, climbing in perfect unison, and the wrongness of it was what made his stomach turn. Not their armor, though the king's silver-and-grey livery was strange enough this far from Brackhollow. Not their faces, which were ordinary faces, tired faces, faces of men who had probably been farmers or fishermen before the palace guard had taken them for the binding rites.

It was the rhythm. Twelve men walking as one footfall. No one stumbling on the scree. No one adjusting a strap or wiping sweat. They climbed the way water climbs a hill when it has decided to — without effort, without doubt, without a single wasted motion or thought.

And they did not blink. Odile had watched for it. He had stood behind a rock with Sera's hand clamped over his mouth and counted, waiting for the flicker of lids, and it never came. Eyes like polished stones set in living faces.

"The binding takes the pauses," Sera had whispered afterward, as they scrambled up a goat track the soldiers' perfect unison couldn't follow. "Everything a body does between decisions — blinking, breathing deep, hesitating. That's where the person lives, mostly. Take the pauses, and what's left walks and fights and follows orders until it drops."

"And then?"

"Then it gets up and keeps following orders. I told you. I've seen it." Her jaw had tightened. "My brother is one of them. Somewhere in that column, maybe. He stopped writing to me four years ago, and I used to think he was angry about my exile."

Odile hadn't known what to say to that. He still didn't. So instead, that night and every night since, he had opened the grimoire and read slower.

* * *

On the fifth day they crossed the spine of the range, and Odile learned what the rite actually asked of him.

They had stopped at a tarn — a small grey lake cupped in the saddle of the pass, so still it held the sky perfectly, which of course meant it held nothing at all, because the sky above the Sundered Sea had held no stars for months. The water was the exact color of the Valley of Stillness's sky. Seeing it, Odile felt homesick and sick in equal measure, which he was learning were often the same feeling.

"Read me the passage again," Sera said. She had taken to quizzing him the way she'd once quizzed apprentice tide-readers, apparently, with merciless patience. "The part about standing."

Odile found the page. His grandmother's hand grew larger here, more urgent, the salt-ink letters pressing hard enough to emboss the paper:

*The binder must stand in both worlds at once. One foot in the stillness, one in the sea. If he stands only in stillness, the binding holds but the world dies by inches, quiet as frost. If he stands only in the sea, the flood comes and takes everything, loud as grief. Only between — only balanced — can the door be opened without becoming the thing that comes through it.*

"You've read it eleven times," Sera said. "What does it mean?"

"I don't know yet."

"Yes, you do. You're hoping it means something easier."

He laughed despite himself. "All right. It means—" He turned the page, and stopped.

Because there, in the margin beside the passage, in smaller writing squeezed into the space where the flowers had been pressed, was a note he had somehow missed eleven times. His grandmother's hand again, but older, shakier:

*Balance is not half and half. Balance is all of both. You cannot stand in two worlds by cutting yourself in two. You must become large enough to hold them whole.*

"Well?" Sera prompted.

"She wrote a correction," Odile said slowly. "Years later. The first version of the rite — the version she performed on the entity — she thinks it failed because she split herself. Held half her heart back. Kept one foot safely in the stillness." He looked up. "That's why the binding leaked. That's why it needed anchoring at all. She didn't fail because the rite was impossible. She failed because she did it afraid."

Sera was quiet a moment. Wind moved across the tarn, and the reflected dead sky shivered.

"And the corrected rite?"

"Requires you to be unafraid." He heard how it sounded and winced. "Which is—"

"Impossible," Sera finished. Then she shrugged. "Good. I was worried this would be simple."

* * *

He practiced.

There was no other word for it, though *practice* seemed absurd applied to the task of holding two entire worlds in one heart. But the tarn helped. Each evening, while Corvane kept watch with a crossbow and the grim competence of a man making amends at bolt-point, Odile sat at the water's edge and tried.

First he tried the splitting — the obvious approach, the natural one. Stillness in the left hand, sea in the right. The sigil cold in his palm for the valley; the grimoire open before him for the deep. And every time, the moment he gathered both, they fought each other inside his chest like two currents meeting, and he ended up gasping on his knees with his ears ringing and Sera dumping a waterskin over his head.

"Again?" she'd ask.

"Again."

Third time, fifth time, ninth time. On the twelfth attempt, somewhere past midnight on the sixth day, he stopped trying to divide himself — and simply stopped dividing.

It happened almost accidentally. He was tired. Tiredness makes you honest; the mountains had taught him that too. And in his exhaustion he stopped reaching for the sigil and the book as two things, two inheritances, guild and blood, valley and sea, the lies he'd been raised on and the truth he'd fled toward — and instead he thought, with the simple stubbornness of a nineteen-year-old who has run out of clever ideas:

*I am one person. All of it is mine.*

The stillness rose in him — the valley's hush, the wardwrights' silence, his mother's voice teaching him the calibration chants before she died, the smell of cold iron and lamp oil in the workshop. And the sea rose too, at the same time, in the same breath — Sera's charts, the drowned road, the vast patient pressure of the tide against the world's edges, his grandmother's ink breathing under firelight. Neither pushed the other away. There was no seam. There had never been a seam. The seam had been the lie.

For three heartbeats, Odile Marchetti stood in both worlds at once, whole, and the tarn at his feet went utterly, perfectly calm — not stillness-imposed calm, but the true calm of water that has nothing left to argue about — and in its surface he saw stars.

Stars that weren't there. Reflected anyway. Waiting.

Then his knees buckled, Sera caught his shoulders before he went face-first into October-cold water, and the vision shattered.

"What did you see?" she demanded, hauling him back from the edge.

"Stars," he managed. "In the water. They're coming back, Sera. Or—" he swallowed, "—or something's using them to come back. I couldn't tell which."

"Cheerful." But her grip on his shoulders was trembling slightly, and he suspected it wasn't the cold.

* * *

Corvane found him alone on the seventh morning, at the false dawn hour when even Sera slept.

His old teacher lowered himself onto the rock beside Odile with a grunt, wound and all, and for a long while neither of them spoke. Below them the trail dropped away through pine and shadow toward the distant line of the lowlands — and beyond it, invisible at this distance but vivid in Odile's mind, the valley. Home. The place that had raised him on a lie and killed his mother to keep the lie fed.

"I owe you an accounting," Corvane said at last.

"You've given me several. Most of them mid-confession. You're rather dramatic for a man who spent forty years pretending to be boring."

A pause. Then, rustily, the gate-hinge laugh again, longer this time. "Your mother said that too. 'Master Corvane, you are the most tedious man alive, and I refuse to believe it.'" He shook his head. "She saw through everyone. It's why they chose her for the binding anchor work. People who see through everything make the best anchors. The entity can't get a grip on them."

"That's a horrible sentence, Corvane."

"Yes. Most true sentences are." He turned, and in the pre-dawn grey his face looked carved out of the same stone as the mountain. "I didn't come to confess again. I came to teach you the last thing the guild knows that's worth knowing. The thing they taught me to teach the heirs — except the heirs never lived long enough for the lesson."

Odile went still.

"The entity will speak to you," Corvane said. "When you begin the rite, it will speak, and it will not threaten you. Threats strengthen resolve; whoever bound it originally understood that. It will offer. It will show you the thing you want most in the world and hold it out like a lamp in a doorway, and it will ask you to step inside and rest." His voice dropped. "It showed your mother a workshop where you were grown and safe and happy. She stepped in smiling. That's how they took her."

The wind moved through the pines. Far below, impossibly far, Odile thought he could see a smudge of grey-green that might have been the valley's rim.

"How do I refuse it?" he asked.

"You don't." Corvane's eyes, for the first time since Odile had known him, were wet. "You witness it. Whatever it shows you — take it, look at it fully, say *I see you, and it is real, and it is not mine*. Grief isn't refused, boy. It's carried. That's the last lesson. That's the whole lesson. Everything else is technique."

He got up stiffly and limped back toward the campfire, and Odile sat alone in the growing light with his grandmother's grimoire heavy on his knees, turning the words over like river stones: *I see you, and it is real, and it is not mine.*

* * *

They saw the valley at dusk on the eighth day, from the last shoulder of the pass, and none of them cheered.

It lay below them exactly as Odile remembered it — the green bowl of it, the patchwork fields, the town's slate roofs, the guildhall's white tower catching the last light. Beautiful. Preserved. Perfect.

And wrong, in a way that took him a full minute to name: it was glowing. Faintly, from within, the way a lamp glows through parchment — a soft pulse of pale light rising from somewhere beneath the valley floor, swelling and fading, swelling and fading, steady as a heartbeat.

"That's not the wards," Sera said quietly. Beside her, her hands had gone white on the strap of her chart-case. "Wards don't glow. Wards *silence*. That's—"

"The anchor," Odile said. His mouth was dry. "It's drawing power. Grandmother's notes said it would — the final act, the great binding. It's going to try to still everything, everywhere, all at once." He looked at the pulsing light and felt, absurdly, like a man watching a fuse burn toward his childhood home. "How long do we have?"

Behind them, far down the trail they'd climbed, something caught the dying light: a glint of silver and grey. A dozen figures, moving in perfect unison, not tiring, not stopping, not blinking.

Corvane began reloading his crossbow.

"Not long," he said.
