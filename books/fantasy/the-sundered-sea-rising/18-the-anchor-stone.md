## 18. The Anchor Stone

The way down began where the valley's stillness was thickest, which Sera had always privately thought was a very guild thing to do: bury your worst secret under the quietest floor you could find.

They descended through three days of tunnels that had no business existing. The passages were old — older than the guild, older than the wards, older by the smell of them than anything Sera had ever charted. The walls were not cut stone and not grown stone but something between, ribbed like the inside of a shell, and they sweated salt. Salt, a hundred miles from any coast, seeping out of the mountain like an old wound weeping.

"Your grandmother," Sera said, holding her lantern high, "built a prison inside a mountain, anchored it to a valley, and then let everyone forget she'd done it."

"She was thorough," Odile said.

"She was *terrified*," said Master Corvane, from the back, in the voice of a man who had spent forty years carrying a seal against his ribs and had only recently learned what it cost him to say so. He walked badly now. The wounds he'd taken in Brackhollow's flood had gone bad despite everything Odile's grimoire-craft could do, and he refused, with the stubbornness of the truly guilty, to be left behind. "You don't build *this* out of confidence, boy. You build this out of a night when you were sure the world was ending, and you never stop being sure."

Sera didn't answer. She was busy being angry at the walls.

It was a specific anger, the kind she'd gotten good at over five years of exile: the anger of a professional recognizing another professional's work and hating how good it was. She read water for a living — had read it, before the starless night and the tribunal and the word *heretic* spoken in a room full of people who owed their careers to her charts. And these tunnels were full of water. Not flowing water. *Held* water. It hung in the stone the way breath hangs in a held chest, and every step deeper made her skin prickle with the wrongness of it. Water that had been told to wait. Water that had been waiting so long it had forgotten it was water.

The valley above wasn't still because nothing lived there. The valley was still because something below it had been ordered to hold its breath for sixty years, and everything else had been ordered to hold its breath along with it.

"Gentle humor, Voss," she muttered to herself. "Find the gentle humor."

The gentle humor was that Odile Marchetti, heir to the most terrible inheritance on two coasts, savior-in-progress, chosen vessel of an unbinding rite, could not cook. They'd discovered this on the second night, when he'd produced a travel ration that turned out to be mostly ash and optimism, and Corvane — dying, traitor, penitent Corvane — had eaten it anyway and pronounced it "an improvement on guild fare." Sera had laughed until her ribs hurt, and then laughed a little more than the joke deserved, because laughing felt like a finite resource down here and she wanted to spend it while she still could.

On the third day, the tunnels opened.

* * *

The chamber was round, and vast, and wrong in a way her eyes kept trying to file and failing. It looked, at first, like the inside of a well seen from the bottom — stone rising smooth and dark all around them, curving inward overhead until the lantern light couldn't find the top. The floor was black stone veined with white, and the veins moved. Slowly. The way clouds move. The way, Sera thought with a lurch, tide-lines move across a flat when you watch long enough.

And in the center stood the anchor stone.

It was not large. That was the first obscenity of it. After three days of descending through the mountain's gullet, after the ribbed tunnels and the held water and the weight that pressed on the ears like a dive too deep, the thing itself was barely taller than a man: a pillar of sea-glass green, worn smooth, carved over every surface with script Sera didn't recognize and one line of script she did, because Odile had shown it to her in the journal, traced by his grandmother's own hand:

*I bind thee not because thou art evil, but because I was afraid.*

Below it, the floor was wet. Not damp — *wet*, shining, a thin sheet of seawater lying perfectly flat across the whole chamber, motionless as poured glass, reflecting the lantern light back up at them like a second sky.

"No tides," Sera whispered. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "There's no tide in here. There should be a tide in here. Everything has a tide. Even blood has a tide."

"It's been bound," Odile said quietly. He had his hand on the sigil at his throat — the cold iron key he'd carried since the night the stars went out. It had begun, in the last day, to hum. Not sound, exactly. A resonance in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the wet part of the eye. "Everything in here has been bound. Including us, if we stand here too long."

"Then we don't stand here too long," Sera said briskly, because someone had to say things in a brisk voice or this whole expedition would lie down in the pretty black water and go to sleep. "Odile, your rite. Where do you need to be?"

"Here." He stepped toward the stone, and the humming changed pitch, and Sera saw his shadow shiver on the wet floor — shivered *against* the light, which shadows are contractually forbidden to do. "The binder stands at the stone. The rite takes—"

He stopped.

So did everything else.

* * *

Later, Sera would try to describe what happened next, and she would fail, and she would hate herself a little for failing, because she was a reader of tides and patterns and this was the largest pattern she had ever seen and she could not get it onto paper. But she would try, because some things deserve the indignity of a bad map over the dignity of silence.

The entity spoke to each of them. Not aloud. Not in words, exactly — though it used words, their words, words lifted from their own mouths and rearranged into voices that were almost right. It spoke *through longing*. That was the mechanism; she understood that much even as it happened. It found the wanting place in each of them, the sealed room behind the sternum where a person keeps the thing they'd trade everything for, and it opened the door, and it furnished the room, and it invited them in.

For Corvane, it spoke as forgiveness.

Sera heard it because Corvane answered it aloud, which was the cruelest mercy of the whole siege — hearing him talk to it meant she got to eavesdrop on his soul, and no one should have to hear that. He had sunk down against the curved wall, gray-faced, and the air around him had gone soft and warm, and a voice like his mother's — a woman forty years dead, whose name Sera learned only then, *Maren*, *Maren, you did the best you could* — was telling him that the guild had needed what it needed, that feeding heirs to the binding had been triage, that Odile's mother had died knowing her son would be safe, that Maren forgave him, that everyone forgave him, that he could put the seal down now, put it all down, rest.

"No," Corvane said. His voice cracked down the middle. "No, I know what you are. You're the shape of what I want, wearing my dead like a coat."

*Maren is not dead,* the voice said gently. *Nothing here dies. That is the gift. Come and see her hands. She is kneading bread.*

Corvane made a sound Sera had never heard a man make, and dragged himself two steps toward the center of the chamber before Odile caught his arm.

For Odile, it spoke as his mother.

Sera watched him go rigid, watched the color leave his face, watched him take one step and then another toward the water, and the voice that reached her ears — because the entity did not bother hiding from her, why would it, she was already caught — was a young woman's voice, tired and kind, saying: *Odile. You found it. You found the truth. I'm so proud of you. Come here. Let me look at you. Let me hold you once, properly, the way I never got to.*

And Odile — nineteen years old, raised on lies, orphaned by the guild's arithmetic, walking toward a sheet of glass-flat water with tears running freely down his face — said, in a small ruined voice, "I just want to know if you knew. If you knew what they'd do to me. Tell me if you knew."

*Come and ask me yourself.*

Sera would have gone to him. She tried. She got one step and then the water was talking to *her*, and everything else went away.

* * *

What the entity showed Sera Voss was the sea.

Not the king's sea — not the flat, obedient, windless pond that had lain off Brackhollow for years, the sea with no storms and no tides and no honest weather, the sea she had been exiled for mourning. The *real* sea. The sea of her childhood, before the stillness spread: the grey-green violence of it, the swell coming in long and patient off the deep water, the taste of spray, the way a hull groans and lives beneath you, the whole enormous breathing animal of it, indifferent and magnificent and *moving*.

She was standing on the deck of her father's boat. She could feel the deck. She could feel the wind — actual wind, with weather in it, with rain coming somewhere behind it. Her father was at the tiller, and he turned and grinned at her, and he was not drowned, he had never been drowned, the storm that took him had never happened, and he said, "Well? Are you going to stand there all day, girl? The tide's turning. Best tide of the year. Get up here and read it."

And Sera, who had spent five years in exile reading puddles and gutters and the miserable brackish ditches of the inland flats, who had been *right* about the starless night and punished for it, who had watched the thing she loved most in the world be flattened into a millpond by a god in a crown — Sera felt her feet begin to move.

That was the horror of it, the thing she would carry afterward like a scar: it wasn't a trick she saw through. She *knew*, even as she walked, that the deck under her boots was a sheet of black water in a hole under a mountain. She knew her father was bones and driftwood twenty years gone. Knowing changed nothing. The longing didn't live in the knowing part of her. It lived lower, older, in the part that had been a girl on a boat with salt in her hair, and that part was *starving*, and the entity had set a feast in front of it, and her feet were moving, and the water was warm when it touched her ankles, and her father was saying *the tide's turning, Sera, come read it with me*—

"Tides don't turn in here," she said.

Her father smiled. It was a perfect smile. It was a smile built from memory by something that had never had a face.

"That's how I know you're a liar," Sera said, and her voice came out ragged and furious and hers. "My father never asked me to read a tide in his life. He read them himself. Badly. He used to get the turn wrong by half a bell and blame the moon. You gave me a perfect father, and my father was never perfect, he was *better* than perfect, he was real, and you can't have him, and you can't have the sea either, you great grieving *thief*—"

The illusion flickered.

Only for a heartbeat. Only like a lamp in a draft. But in that heartbeat Sera saw the chamber again — the black stone, the green pillar, Odile standing ankle-deep in the water with his arms half-raised toward a mother who wasn't there, Corvane crawling, crawling, whispering *Maren* — and she saw, underneath the water's surface, far down, something vast turning over in the deep like a whale deciding whether to breach. Something that was looking at her. Something that, in the last instant before the illusion closed over her again, did not look triumphant.

It looked *lonely*.

Then her father was calling her name again, warmer now, softer, and the water was at her knees, and the terrible thing — the thing she would tell no one for a long time — was that some part of her kept walking anyway. Because the entity had learned something in that flicker. It had stopped offering her the perfect sea.

It started offering her the true one. Storms and all. Drowned father and all. Grief and all. *Even the grief,* it said, tender as a hand on her hair. *We will give you back even the grief. Is that not worth wading for?*

And God help her, God forgive her, it nearly was.

* * *

What stopped her was not courage. She wanted to be able to say, later, that she had mastered herself, that her will had proven stronger than the siege. It wasn't that.

What stopped her was Odile swearing.

A very specific oath, in fact — the one every wardwright's apprentice learns in their first year, the mild little curse guild children use because real profanity is frowned upon in the Valley of Stillness. *"Stars and stones,"* Odile said, somewhere behind her, in a voice cracked wide open and shaking with effort, *"stars and stones, Mother, I loved you, and you're DEAD, and dead people don't knead BREAD—"*

And there was something so absurd in it — the heir to the unbinding, the chosen vessel, standing in a haunted pool cursing like a homesick apprentice because the entity had made a tactical error and offered him domestic comfort instead of cosmic revelation — that Sera laughed.

Laughed, knee-deep in the false sea, with the false tide pulling at her like a hand.

And the laugh broke it. Not entirely — the water stayed, the warmth stayed, the longing stayed, lodged in her chest like a fishhook — but her feet stopped. She stood there shaking, half sobbing, half giggling, and said aloud, to the thing in the deep, in the flattest Brackhollow dockside accent she owned:

"You'll have to do better than my da. He never once told me he was proud of me. You overshot. Amateur."

The water went cold.

All at once, everywhere, the warmth drained out of it like a lie collapsing, and the illusions tore away all together — Corvane slumped and weeping against the wall, Odile soaked to the waist and glaring at the center of the chamber with the sigil blazing at his throat, and Sera herself, standing in three inches of ordinary, dead, bound seawater, alive, furious, and *seen*.

The pressure in the chamber changed. The moving white veins in the black stone slowed. Somewhere far beneath them, the vast thing turned over again, and this time Sera felt it the way she felt weather coming: a change in the weight of the air, a gathering.

It had stopped pretending. It had also, she realized, stopped *hunting* — and that was worse. A hunting thing can be outrun. This felt like a thing that had decided it no longer needed to run them down, because it had learned everything it needed from the chase. It knew Corvane's guilt. It knew Odile's hunger for his mother. It knew her sea.

It knew exactly where each of them was softest. And the stars, when they finally clawed their way back into the sky outside — faint, few, trembling — were not returning for anyone's sake.

They were fuel. It was drawing power for its final act, and it had let them come all this way because it wanted the binder *here*, at the stone, on its own ground, when it pulled the trigger.

Odile waded out of the water, dripping, hollow-eyed, and looked at the anchor stone, and then at her, and then at Corvane, who was trying and failing to stand.

"It knows the rite," he said quietly. "It's known the rite longer than my grandmother did. It's going to start the binding itself — the whole world, into stillness — and it's going to use *me* to do it, because the rite needs a binder, and I'm the only one left."

"Then we start it first," Sera said.

Her legs were still trembling. The fishhook of longing was still in her chest, and would be for the rest of her life; she suspected she'd feel the pull of that false tide every time she stood at a real one. But she pulled the courier's journal from her coat, and the grimoire pages they'd copied in the vaults, and slapped them down on the black stone like a fishwife laying out the morning catch.

"So," she said. "Teach me the words, heir. We've got until the stars finish coming back — and Odile?"

He looked at her.

"When this is done, if we live, you're learning to cook. I mean it. I've faced a god tonight. I'm not facing your rations too."

But even as she said it, she was watching the water. Watching it begin, very slowly, impossibly, to rise.
