## 20. What Wears the Wearer

The first thing Odile understood about the space inside the binding was that it was not a space at all.

It had no floor, but he stood on it. It had no light, but he could see. It stretched in every direction the way grief stretches — not wide, exactly, but *deep*, the way a single moment can hold forty years if you let it.

And it was full of sea.

Not water. Not the Sundered Sea with its drowned roads and its salt-stiff piers and its fish that had begun, in these last frantic days, to remember how to swim crooked. This was the idea of sea. The sea before shores. The sea as it might have dreamed itself before anyone drew a map and spoiled the mystery.

He was standing in the middle of it, nineteen years old, still wearing his mother's sigil on a cord around his neck, and he was entirely alone except for everything.

"You're the question," Odile said aloud, because his grandmother's grimoire had been very clear on one point: speak. Whatever else you do in there, *speak*. "I'm supposed to answer you."

The sea moved.

It did not surge or crash or rise in a threatening wall the way seas do in the stories the guild told children to keep them indoors. It simply turned its attention toward him, all of it at once, the way a room turns quiet when someone important enters. And out of that attention something assembled itself — not a body, exactly, more like a shape the vastness wore for his convenience, the way you'd put on your good coat to meet a guest.

It looked like a woman made of tide-line and driftwood and the particular gray-gold of dawn over open water. It looked like his grandmother. That was, he suspected, an accident of the material available. He had come carrying her grimoire in one hand and her guilt in the other, and the entity had read both like letters left lying open on a table.

"Small one," it said. Its voice was the sound waves make when they decide, after centuries, to stop. "You said you see me."

"I said I see you," Odile agreed. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was a skill he'd learned from Sera, who could say *the city is drowning* in the same tone she used to complain about bad tea. "That was the whole trick of it, apparently. Nobody ever tried just saying it."

"They tried other things." The god — he was going to have to think of it as a god eventually; the shrine vision had settled that, whatever Corvane's training manuals called it — tilted its borrowed head. "They tried chains. They tried walls. They tried a valley with no weather in it and children raised to believe the sky was only ever so big." Something moved behind its face, deep down, like a shadow under ice. "Your guild was very thorough."

"Yes." Odile didn't flinch from it. That was the second thing the grimoire insisted on: do not apologize on their behalf, and do not pretend it away. "They were. My grandmother most of all. She wrote about it. She knew what she was doing was wrong even while she was doing it, which I think is somehow worse than if she hadn't known."

"You are angry with the dead woman."

"I'm furious with her." He heard his own voice crack and let it crack. In the valley, cracking was failure. Out here, Sera had taught him, cracking was just what happened when you were honest at volume. "She bound you into a stone and built a town on top of the lock and then — this is the part that keeps me up at night — she wrote instructions. A whole rite. A way to undo it. She knew it would need undoing, and she made sure the key would land in her own bloodline, and then she let us all grow up thinking we were wardwrights. Keepers of safety." He laughed, and it came out bitter. "We were jailers' kids. We were the warden's family, living in the warden's house, eating off the warden's table, and nobody ever once told us there was a prisoner."

The god was silent for a long time. The sea-that-was-not-sea breathed around them.

"And you?" it asked finally. "Are you the warden's family? You took me in. You stand in my deep place wearing your little iron star. Why did you come?"

"Because Aldric begged me to," Odile said. "Because the alternative was letting you bind the whole world still, and I've lived in a still world, and it looks like peace but it tastes like nothing." He paused. "But those are the reasons I'd give the guild, if the guild were here, which thank every honest star it isn't. The real reason—"

He stopped. Inside the binding there was nowhere to look away to, no window, no horizon, no convenient distraction of weather. The god waited with the patience of something that had been bound for three generations and had learned what waiting actually meant.

"The real reason is that you were crying," Odile said.

The stillness that followed was enormous.

"When the stars went dark," he went on, quieter now, "when the wards fell — everyone in the valley thought it was an attack. The elders were screaming about invasion. But I was standing right at the border when it broke, and what came through wasn't rage. I know what rage feels like; I've met Master Corvane on a bad day. What came through was—" He searched for it, and found it where it had lodged itself in his chest that night and never quite left. "*Relief.* Like something that had been holding its breath for sixty years finally getting to exhale. Gods don't exhale when they escape, do they? Prisoners do. But you didn't run. You didn't scatter. You went looking for a king, and you put him on like armor, and you've spent every hour since trying to build a world with no storms in it. No tides. No change." He looked up at the vast gray-gold attention of it. "You weren't trying to conquer anything. You were trying to make sure nobody could ever do to the sea what the sea did to you. What *we* did to you. Stillness isn't your weapon. It's your scar tissue."

The shape of his grandmother flickered. For a moment the entity wore no shape at all, and Odile felt the true scale of it press against him like deep water on a diver's ears — old, so old, older than the valley, older than binding, old enough to remember being worshipped by people who at least had the decency to be afraid honestly.

Then the shape returned, and the god spoke, and its voice had frayed at the edges.

"The sea cast me out first."

"I know. Sera's reading showed us."

"Mortals bound me because they feared me, yes, but they feared me because I had already failed them. There was a drowning, small one. A generation of it. I held the storms too long and the coasts too long and I could not tell anymore where their prayers ended and my will began, and fishermen died in fleets, whole fleets, and their widows sang songs about my cruelty, and they were not wrong." The tide-line face did something that was not weeping but was adjacent to it. "I loved them too much to let them go, and it looked exactly like hatred. So they caged me. And the ones who caged me wrote it down as duty. And their grandchildren inherited the cage and called it the world."

Odile sat down.

There was no floor, but he sat anyway, cross-legged, the way he used to sit in the workshop as a boy while his mother soldered ward-anchors and told him the two rules of good craft: *know what you're holding*, and *never pretend the thing you're holding doesn't have weight*. The god watched him sit. Somewhere in the impossible distance of this place, the last stars of the returning sky were filtering through like light through deep water.

"My mother," Odile said, "died feeding the binding. Did you take her?"

"No." The word came swift and absolute. "I have never taken anyone. I could not reach past the anchor to save myself, let alone to harm. Her death was the guild's arithmetic. They fed heirs to the lock the way you feed a fire — and told each one it was an honor." A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "You thought perhaps I had eaten her. That is why you were afraid of me, even as you opened the door. Even as you said *I see you*. You saw me, and you were afraid, and you did it anyway."

"Yeah, well." Odile shrugged, and discovered that shrugging in a place without shoulders was surprisingly difficult and deeply satisfying. "Sera says courage is mostly fear that showed up on time. Or possibly she stole that from a tavern sign. I've stopped checking her sources."

Something happened to the sea around them. Later, struggling to describe it, Odile would call it laughter — not sound, exactly, but the pressure-change of amusement moving through something vast. The god was amused. He had made a god laugh with a joke about Sera's plagiarism, and some distant part of him noted that he would absolutely be insufferable about this later, assuming there was a later.

"Small heir," the god said. "You came to unbind me. You have done it. The stone is sand. The king sleeps human. The sky remembers its stars. And now you sit in my deep place, and I am wearing you the way I wore him, and you have not yet asked the question you crossed the mountains to ask."

Odile looked down at his hands. They were his hands, mostly. There was a new mark on the left one, faint as a watermark: a spiral, turning inward, like a wave seen from above. He flexed his fingers and the spiral flexed with them.

"Am I going to survive you?" he asked. "Is there a me after this? Because I promised Sera I'd help her chart the new coasts, and I promised the valley I'd rebuild the guild into something that isn't a lie, and I can't keep either promise from in here."

"That depends," said the god, "on whether you intend to keep me."

The question landed in the center of him and rang.

"Keep you," Odile repeated.

"I was bound in stone for three generations. Before that I was bound in ritual and fear. Before that I was bound in my own grief, which was the strongest chain of the three. Every vessel before you has either fought me or surrendered to me, and both are prisons, small one, and I am very tired of prisons." The god knelt — the gesture absurd and enormous and somehow humble, a tide kneeling on no floor at all. "You offered me witness. Do you understand what that means? Not worship. Worship is another cage, prettier, gilded, but the door locks the same. Witness. To be seen changing. To be allowed to change. Your guild never imagined I could be anything other than what I was on the worst day of my existence. Can you?"

Odile thought about the valley. About the guildmaster's face when the truth came out — not monstrous, just tired, just a man who had inherited a lie and chosen the lie because the truth required courage he'd spent decades talking himself out of. He thought about Corvane, bleeding on the coast road, asking for a forgiveness Odile couldn't give yet and giving his seal anyway. He thought about Sera, exiled for being right, choosing charts over reinstatement because the wild new coasts mattered more than being forgiven.

People changed. That was the entire discovery of his nineteenth year. People changed, and gods presumably could too, and the difference between a prison and a home was mostly whether anyone believed you could become something else.

"I'm not keeping you," Odile said slowly. "And you're not keeping me. We're—" He groped for it. "Roommates. Terrible, cosmic, extremely temporary roommates. You get the parts of me that understand mourning. I get the parts of you that remember how to move water. And when we get where we're going — wherever that is — we go our own ways, and I will tell your story honestly, including the ugly parts, especially the fleet, especially the widows. That's the deal. Witness goes both ways or it isn't witness."

The god regarded him for a long, long moment.

"You drive a hard bargain," it said, "for a boy who cannot yet grow a proper beard."

"It's a valley thing. The stillness stunted everyone's growth, emotionally and otherwise."

This time there was no mistaking it for anything else. The sea-that-was-not-sea laughed, openly, hugely, and the sound of it was the sound the Sundered Sea must have made before anyone was frightened of it, and Odile laughed too, and the two sounds braided together somewhere in the middle of everything and became, briefly, indistinguishable.

Then the god rose, and began, gently, to set him down.

Not drop — *set*, the way you set down something you mean to find again. Odile felt the deep place receding around him, felt the stars sharpen, felt salt air arrive from somewhere impossibly far away, and at the very last moment the god leaned close and pressed something into his awareness like a seal pressed into wax: a spiral, turning outward now, and beneath it four words that were less words than weather:

*I will remember you.*

"And I'll remember you," Odile said. "Every tide. That's the deal."

* * *

He woke face-down on wet grass with Sera's hand fisted in the back of his collar and her voice doing the thing it did when she was terrified and refusing to admit it, which was to become extremely precise about irrelevant details.

"—breathing, obviously, you're breathing, I checked twice, but if you've died anyway just to inconvenience me I want you to know I'm going to be *furious*, and also the sky is back, Odile, look at the sky—"

He looked at the sky.

Stars. Hundreds of them, thousands, wheeling in their honest ancient courses, half of them in constellations no one in the valley had ever been allowed to name because naming implied the sky was bigger than the guild. Overhead hung a band of pale fire that Sera's confiscated charts had called the River of Heaven and the guild had called Nothing, and it was so beautiful that Odile lay in the wet grass and cried about it, quietly, without shame, while the tide came in below the cliffs at exactly the speed tides were supposed to come in, neither lurching nor frozen, just moving, gloriously, ordinarily, alive.

Sera sat down beside him. She was soaked to the knees, singed on one sleeve, and had a cut over her eyebrow that she was going to insist was nothing. She looked at him for a long moment.

"There's a mark on your hand," she said.

"I know."

"It's moving."

"I know."

"Does it hurt?"

"No," Odile said, and found, examining it, that it was true. "It feels like being remembered."

Sera nodded slowly, in the way of a tide-reader receiving data she'd have bet money against. Then she pulled a battered notebook from her coat, licked the pencil, and said, "Right. Start from the beginning. Leave nothing out. And Odile—" the pencil paused, and for one unguarded second the exiled, disgraced, permanently bracing-for-the-worst Sera Voss looked at him with naked hope, "—does it work? The sea. Is it free?"

Below them, in the dark, the water sighed against the rocks, turned, and went out again — and Odile felt it pass through the spiral on his hand like a handshake, like a pulse, like something vast and wounded and healing saying *still here, still here, still here.*

"Sera," he said, sitting up, wiping his eyes, grinning like an idiot at the impossible stars, "you are not going to believe what I have to tell you."

Far out beyond the cliffs, where the drowned road ran down into the deep, something answered — a long swell rolling shoreward out of season and out of pattern, rising green-black against the starlight, carrying on its crest, if you knew how to look and Sera very much knew how to look, the unmistakable shape of a message.

Or an invitation.

Or a summons.

"Oh no," said Sera Voss, already reaching for her charts.
