## 21. Tides Returning

The tide came back to Brackhollow on a Tuesday, which everyone agreed was typical of it.

Sera Voss stood on the seawall at dawn with a mug of chicory going cold in her hand and watched the water do something it had not done in eleven years: leave. It slid out past the breakwater, past the drowned pilings of the old fish market, past the sandbar that had been the town's most reliable source of gossip and grief since before her grandmother was born, and it kept going, baring mudflats wide enough to walk across if you were fool enough to trust it.

It would come back. That was the miracle. Not that the sea had risen or fallen or swallowed a district — Brackhollow had survived all three — but that it *left and returned*, on a schedule, for no reason anyone could command. The first time it went out, half the town ran down to the flats convinced it was another trick of the king's peace, another stillness wearing the ocean like a glove. Old Marta Tull had planted herself at the tideline with a broom, daring the water to come back, and when it did — six hours and eleven minutes later, exactly as Sera's grandmother's charts said it should — Marta had burst into tears and beaten the surf with her broom handle until the handle snapped.

"Best day of my life," she'd told Sera afterward, dripping, hoarse, holding the broken broom like a war trophy. "I cried at the ocean and it didn't even notice."

That was the thing about real tides, Sera was learning. They didn't care about you. After years under the king's peace — no storms, no seasons worth arguing about, no weather except the weather you were given, like a ration — the sheer indifference of the returning sea felt almost indecent. Almost holy.

She drank her chicory. The gulls, who had spent the starless months silent as furniture, were screaming their heads off over the flats, fighting over worms. Somewhere behind her, the city was waking up to argue about drainage ditches, which after everything — the floods, the drowning of Saltrow district, the bells that rang without sound while the world frayed at its seams — struck Sera as the most hopeful sound in creation.

People argued when they believed tomorrow existed.

* * *

Aldric was buried nine days later, on a gray-gold morning with an actual breeze in it.

They buried him in the old royal plot above the harbor, which had been sealed since his coronation because kings under the peace did not die; they simply stopped being discussed. The gravediggers had to break the seal with crowbars, and when they pried the stone lid off the empty crypt beneath, they found it full of dry salt and nothing else — thirty years of a reign, and no body had ever lain there. So they buried the man they'd had instead: Aldric as he'd been found at the end, in the wrecked throne room, an old man suddenly old, freed from whatever had worn him, dead within hours of Odile's unbinding with an expression on his face that the priests kept describing as "rested."

Sera stood at the back of the crowd, in her good coat, feeling like an impostor in her own city.

She had known the king only as a wrongness — the unblinking eyes, the voice arriving a half-beat late, the sense of standing near a well with something enormous coiled at the bottom. But watching the coffin go by, borne by fishermen and dockhands and two of the palace guards who had wept openly through the whole procession, she understood that Brackhollow had loved him anyway. Or loved what he'd been before, or loved the idea of him, or simply loved having someone to blame for the weather. Grief is not particular about its objects.

"He was my cousin," said a voice beside her, and Sera nearly spilled her dignity onto the cobblestones.

The woman next to her was seventy if she was a day, small and iron-backed, dressed in mourning black so crisp it could have cut bread. She looked familiar the way a name in an old ledger looks familiar.

"I'm sorry," Sera said. "I don't—"

"Dowager Lady Cressa Vane." The woman said it the way other people said *mind the gap*. "Aldric's mother's people. I knew him when he was eight years old and afraid of eels." Her eyes stayed fixed on the coffin. "He used to hide from them in the chapel. Under the altar. I told him God wouldn't protect him from eels, and he said that was why he liked the arrangement — God handled heaven, and he'd handle the eels himself."

"That sounds like a boy who'd grow up to make a terrible bargain," Sera said, before her mouth remembered it wasn't supposed to have opinions in front of nobility.

Lady Cressa turned and looked at her properly, and Sera saw where the iron came from: the woman's eyes were pale gray and entirely without mercy, including toward herself.

"Yes," she said. "It does." A pause. "You're the tide-reader. The one they exiled. The one who told the truth and was punished for it, and then told the truth again during the flood, and saved four hundred people through sea-paths my family helped confiscate the charts for."

"Six hundred and twelve," Sera said. "And the charts weren't confiscated for me specifically. My whole family suffered that indignity."

Something happened at the corner of Lady Cressa's mouth. On a younger face it might have been called a smile.

"The regency council meets Thursday," she said. "There will be seven seats. Six are already promised to men who think the proper response to the last year is to pretend it didn't happen and rebuild the walls higher." She began to move away, then stopped. "I'm told the seventh seat is vacant, and that the woman who predicted the starless night, read the tides against exile, and led six hundred souls out of a drowned city has certain qualifications."

"I'm not—" Sera started.

"Thursday," said Lady Cressa Vane, as though Sera hadn't spoken, and departed into the crowd like a ship of state coming about.

* * *

The reinstatement offer arrived formally three days later, on actual paper, with a wax seal, delivered by a very nervous junior clerk of the Tide-Wrights' Guild who stood in Sera's doorway holding the scroll out at arm's length as if it might go off.

"They voted," he said. "Unanimous. They're — they want to say the exile was an error of judgment. In light of circumstances. There's a ceremony. There's a sash."

"A sash," Sera repeated.

"Ceremonial. Blue and silver. Master Ellery says—" the clerk consulted the scroll, visibly dreading it, "—Master Ellery says 'the guild turns its face toward the future and welcomes home its prodigal daughter.'"

Sera took the scroll and read it twice, slowly, while the clerk sweated. It was a fine document. Generous, even. Full salary restored, back pay calculated to the month of her exile — someone had clearly lost that argument — a house stipend, a title, a seat at the high table, her name struck from the censure rolls and entered instead in the roll of honor, in ink.

Her name. Written by the same hands that had burned her grandmother's charts, that had called the rising sea hysteria, that had stood in their tower counting their own instruments while the stars went out.

"You know what's strange?" she said to the clerk, conversationally. He flinched. "Eleven years ago, when they exiled me, I wanted this. Exactly this. I lay awake nights composing the speech I'd give when they crawled back. I had *phrases*." She handed the scroll back. "I don't want it anymore. Isn't that funny? You can't win an argument you've stopped having."

The clerk stared at her with the expression of a man realizing his afternoon had just become much worse. "Master Ellery will want to know — a reason? For the record?"

"Tell him the tides are moving again," Sera said. "Tell him somebody ought to find out where they're going."

* * *

She told Odile that evening, on the roof of what had been the palace vaults and was now, per the regency council's first official act, "the Archive of Recovered Materials," which is what governments call a room full of things they haven't finished being embarrassed about.

He was sitting cross-legged on the lead sheeting with the grimoire open on his knees, though he wasn't reading it. He was doing the thing he did now, more often than he probably realized: sitting very still, head tilted, listening to something on the far side of his own skin. Since the unbinding, Odile Marchetti carried the sea's old god somewhere inside the architecture of himself, and some evenings it showed — a faint pressure behind his eyes, a way the lamplight bent slightly wrong around him, the sense that if he exhaled too fully, the harbor would notice.

Tonight the harbor seemed calm. He looked up when she dropped onto the roof beside him.

"You gave back the scroll," he said.

"How do you know that already?"

"Marta Tull told the entire fish market, the fish market told the harbor watch, and the harbor watch has apparently decided I'm interesting enough to report on." He shrugged. "Also your face does a thing when you've just refused authority. It goes very satisfied. Like a cat that's knocked something off a table on purpose."

"My face does not—"

"It absolutely does." He grinned, and for a moment he was just a nineteen-year-old from a valley with no sea in it, sunburned along the hairline from a week of helping re-survey the coast, absurdly young to be carrying a god and rebuilding a guild. Then the grin settled into something quieter. "Are you sure? The sash was blue and silver. You'd have looked devastating."

"I look devastating regardless. It's a burden." She pulled her coat closer against the evening wind — a real wind, gusty and badly behaved, smelling of kelp and rain that hadn't decided yet. "Odile. Can I tell you the actual reason?"

"You've been telling me reasons all week. The guild is ossified. The guild is cowardly. Master Ellery signs his letters with a flourish he can't possibly execute with that tremor."

"All true." She looked out at the harbor, where the lights of the fishing fleet were coming up one by one — boats going *out*, at night, into water nobody governed, because the fish had come back changed, running in new patterns, schooling in depths the old charts marked as empty rock. "But the real reason is that everything I ever learned, everything my grandmother bled into those confiscated charts, described a sea that doesn't exist anymore. Eleven years of false calm, then the unbinding, and now—" she gestured at the horizon, where a line of cloud was building with a swagger no cloud had dared under the king's peace, "—now the whole coast is rewriting itself. Currents moved. Sandbars walked. There are channels where there weren't channels and rocks where there were channels. Every chart in that archive is a portrait of a dead world."

"And you'd rather draw the new one."

"I'd rather be useful than honored," Sera said. "Honored means it's over. Useful means it's still happening." She paused. "Also I want to see what's out there when nothing's binding anything anymore. The deep water, Odile. What does the sea remember when it's allowed to?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, there was a carefulness in it that made her glance over.

"When it's quiet," he said, "and I'm paying attention — sometimes I can feel it. Not thoughts. More like... weather. Old weather. The god remembers being prayed to. It remembers being feared. And underneath both of those, it remembers being *alone*, for a very long time, in the dark, wearing a king." He rubbed his arms. "It likes the tides coming back. I can tell. It's the closest thing I'd call happiness in there, and I don't entirely know what to do with that."

"Chart it," Sera said.

"What?"

"You heard me. If the god remembers the old coasts — the coasts from before any chart, before any guild — then you're the only living map of the deep water there is. You and it, together. I'll bring the instruments and the insolence. You bring the interior geography of a deity." She nudged his knee with hers. "We'll need a boat. Preferably one captained by someone who owes us their life, so I was thinking of asking the widow Ferrane, whose son you pulled off the Saltrow rooftops."

"She already offered. She told me the *Kestrel's Mercy* is ours whenever we stop being idiots." He smiled, then it faded into something more serious. "You understand it's not safe. Nothing out there is surveyed. The storms are learning to be storms again — they're clumsy, they overshoot, they come apart halfway and start over. The god says the deep water is..." he searched, "...*awake*. Everything's awake now. Waking up cranky."

"Good," Sera said. "Cranky things are honest things." She stood, brushing grit from her coat, and looked south along the darkening coast, toward the drowned island shrine, toward the open water beyond it that no one had named because no one had needed names for water that never changed. "My grandmother had a saying. When I was small and asked why she kept redrawing the same stretch of coast every spring, she said: *the sea forgives nothing and forgets less, and that's why you measure it twice*."

"Cheerful woman."

"Runs in the family." She stuck out a hand and hauled him to his feet. For a moment they stood together on the roof of the vaults, two exiles in a city learning to breathe, while below them Brackhollow argued about drainage ditches and the fleet put out into the swell and the tide, right on schedule, indifferent and faithful, began its long crawl back up the mudflats.

Then, out past the breakwater, where the last light was failing, Sera saw it — a long, low swell rolling in from the deep water, smooth and green-black and wrong, moving against the wind. Behind it, faint as breath on glass, the surface of the whole harbor shivered once, all at once, like a sleeping animal turning over.

Odile's hand went to his chest. His expression flickered — surprise, recognition, and something that might have been, impossibly, delight.

"Sera," he said. "It wants us to see something. Out past the shrine." He was already moving toward the roof ladder. "It says there's an island on the new charts. One that isn't on any of the old ones."

Sera looked at the darkening water, at the swell still rolling in from nowhere, at the harbor lights trembling in a shiver that hadn't finished.

"Well," she said, reaching for her coat pockets to count her instruments by feel. "I suppose we'd better measure it twice."
