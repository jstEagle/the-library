## 22. The Wardwright's New Seal

The first lesson I ever taught was a disaster.

I want that on the record, carved into the lintel of the new hall if it comes to that: *Here, in the spring after the world unbound, Odile Marchetti taught eleven students and set fire to his own lecture bench.*

It wasn't even interesting magic. A simple warming charm, the kind valley grandmothers had done in secret for generations under their blankets while telling the guild it couldn't be done. I demonstrated it badly, overcorrected, and the bench went up like a festival pyre. My students — six valley children, four Brackhollow fisherfolk, and one bewildered tide-reader who had only come to supervise — stood in a ring around the flames and watched me stamp them out with my coat.

"Note," said Sera from the doorway, "the fire did not spread. That's control, at least."

"Note," I said, singed and smoking, "that you could have helped."

"You were doing so well on your own."

But here is the thing I could not have known on the day of the burning bench: when I looked out at those faces afterward, not one of them had run. The valley children were leaning forward. The fisherfolk were grinning. Little Ansa Corren, whose family had tended ward-lamps for five generations without once asking what the wards were for, raised her hand and asked, "Master Marchetti, can you show us how you got it wrong? So we don't?"

And I understood that the guild had died in the flood of returning magic, and something better had already begun to grow in its ashes — something that learned by burning its benches instead of burying its mistakes.

* * *

We rebuilt in the old guildhall because tearing it down felt like lying, and leaving it untouched felt like kneeling to it. Instead we changed what it held.

The great map of the valley's borders — the one that had hung in the entry chamber for two hundred years, all walls and wards and warnings — came down in the second week. Sera and I carried it together to the courtyard and, after some debate, did not burn it. We cut it into strips and wove the strips into paper lanterns for the Festival of Returning Tides. There is a certain poetry in the old border map floating down the river carrying candlelight into the sea, and if there isn't, I've decided there is, and as head of the guild my decisions are final on matters of poetry.

In its place we hung the new seal.

Designing it took longer than rebuilding the east wing. The old seal had been a closed circle: a wall, a lock, a single eye watching inward. Corvane had worn its founding counterpart against his heart for forty years, hidden under his shirt like a wound he'd agreed to carry. When I sat down with charcoal and paper, I kept drawing circles. Circles are what I was raised on. Walls, wards, rings of protection — the whole grammar of my childhood was enclosure.

It was Sera who broke me of it. She watched me ruin a dozen sketches and finally took the charcoal from my hand and drew one long line across the page — a road, running off the edge of the paper in both directions.

"There," she said. "Start there."

So the new seal is an open line. It runs through a wave and over a mountain and does not stop at either; it simply continues, past the edge of the medallion, as if to say: *this is not the whole world, and we do not pretend it is.* The smiths in Brackhollow struck three hundred of them in the first year. They hang on doorposts now, valley and coast alike, and children trade them like river-stones, and sometimes I find one in my pocket and cannot remember which student put it there, which I consider the highest honor the guild has ever paid me.

* * *

Teaching, it turns out, is nothing like binding.

Binding — true binding, the work my grandmother did beneath the valley — requires that you decide for another thing what it may be. Teaching asks only that you stand beside someone while they decide for themselves, and try to keep them from setting themselves on fire in the process. Some days I miss the certainty of the old work. Most days I don't.

Our first joint class nearly ended in riot. Valley folk and sea folk had spent two centuries believing mutually incompatible things about magic — the valley that it was a disease kept outside, the coast that it was a god grown cruel — and neither belief surrendered gracefully. Old Maren Ostwick, who had pulled nets from the Sundered Sea since before I was born, refused to sit in the same room as Wardmaster Pell's daughter, whose family name meant *keeper of the wall* in the old valley dialect.

"I don't care what her name means," Maren announced. "Her people drowned mine in silence for two hundred years."

"And yours let the sea eat our roads," the girl shot back, "and called it weather."

I let it go on for most of an hour. That was the hardest discipline I learned in those early days — not intervening. Sera stood beside me with her arms folded, watching me watch them, and when I glanced at her she murmured, "You want to bind this room. I can see it in your hands."

I looked down. My fingers had curled into the old shapes, the ones from the grimoire, the ones that hold a thing still so it can be remade.

So I uncurled them, and I waited, and eventually Maren Ostwick asked the Pell girl to show her again how the warming charm worked, because her hands ached in the cold mornings, and the girl showed her, and Maren's face when the warmth bloomed between her palms was the face of someone meeting a stranger they'd been told was a monster and finding only another tired person.

That is the whole curriculum, really. Everything else is footnotes.

* * *

My mother's grave sits on the hillside above the workshop where I grew up, where the courier bled out on our floor and handed me an iron sigil and a debt I didn't know was mine.

For a long time I avoided it. Then, for a different length of time, I visited every day and said nothing, because everything I thought of saying sounded either too small or too enormous. Grief has poor manners. It arrives either whispering or shouting, never conversing.

Corvane is buried two hills over. He survived the wounds he took in the Brackhollow flood by a season, long enough to see the first joint class, long enough to sit in the back with his hood up like a man attending his own trial, long enough to tell me — in the last clear week he had — that he'd taught my mother the same warming charm I'd set the bench alight with, and she'd done it perfectly on the first try, and laughed at him for being nervous.

"She'd have liked your students," he said. "She'd have been terrible about the bench, though. Merciless."

"I know," I said. "You told me she laughed at everything."

"Not everything." His eyes had gone to the window, toward the hills, where the stars came back each night now in their proper numbers, as if checking on us. "She didn't laugh at the guild. She just believed, right up until the end, that it could be made honest. Foolish woman."

"Foolish woman," I agreed, and found I was smiling, and found he was too, and that between us — without ceremony, without absolution asked or formally granted — something had quietly finished settling, like sediment clearing from water.

He asked me to bury the founding seal with him. I did. But I kept the rubbings I'd taken of it, and those hang in the new hall beside the open-line seal, side by side: the circle and the road, the wall and the way through. I am not going to pretend the circle was never part of us. The guild that forgets its wall becomes capable of building one again. The guild that remembers it can explain why the road matters.

* * *

On the third anniversary of the night without stars, I finally climbed the hill to my mother's grave with words in my mouth.

I brought bread and honey — her preferences, according to Corvane, who knew her tastes better than any document the guild kept on her. The guild had kept many documents on her. Files, assessments, a sealed report on her death that I burned unread, because I decided some inheritances should be refused. What I chose to keep of her was the warming charm and the laugh, and I have built a life on less.

"Hello, Mother," I said. "You're probably wondering why I'm standing here talking to a stone."

The stone said nothing, being a stone, but the wind moved through the grass in that particular way the valley wind has now — no longer flattened into stillness by the anchor's absence, but gusting, changing its mind, alive. Magic ran loose through the topsoil these days. The wheat grew in slow spirals. The bees navigated by something other than instinct. It alarmed the older farmers until the harvests came in heavier than any in living memory, at which point they decided magic was traditional after all and had probably always been there, which is how people forgive anything, eventually: they rename it heritage.

"I forgave Corvane before he died," I told her. "I don't know exactly when it happened. Somewhere between him bleeding on the coast and him sitting in the back of my classroom pretending not to be proud of me. Forgiveness turned out to be less like a decision and more like weather — it arrived when it arrived, and I mostly just tried not to fight it."

A bee — one of the spiral-flying kind, fat and gold and slightly wrong in a way I have come to find charming — landed on the headstone and considered me.

"I forgave the guild too. That one took longer, because the guild is harder to hate than a person. A person at least knows what they did. An institution just keeps doing it, generation after generation, feeding heirs into a binding like grain into a mill, and everyone inside it is both innocent and guilty, and the walls get higher, and nobody remembers who ordered the first stone." I turned the iron sigil over in my pocket — I still carry it; I suspect I always will, the way a sailor carries a piece of the wreck that didn't kill him. "They were afraid. That's the whole confession, isn't it? Two centuries of fear wearing the costume of duty. And fear is forgivable, Mother. Fear is almost forgivable by definition. It's the hiding that takes work to forgive, and the feeding, and the silence. But I'm working on it."

The bee departed, unconvinced or satisfied; bee theology is hard to read.

"That leaves me," I said. "Which is the hard one. Because I knew. Not at the start — but by the vaults, by the grimoire, by the moment I stood in front of Aldric and saw what was wearing him — I knew what the inheritance was, and I took it anyway, and part of me took it gladly. Part of me wanted the power of it. Wanted to be the one who mattered, the heir, the chosen fool with the key. If I'm honest, the entity spoke to me in the dark under the valley and offered me my deepest longing, and my deepest longing wasn't peace or safety or even your forgiveness. It was significance." My voice caught, and I let it. "So I have to forgive a boy who wanted to matter more than he wanted to be good. And I think — I think I finally can. Because wanting to matter isn't the sin. It's what you build your mattering out of. Walls or roads. Locks or lanterns."

I laid the bread and honey on the stone. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, came the sound of the evening class letting out — laughter, argument, someone's charm misfiring with a smell of scorched cedar that I would recognize anywhere, having created it personally.

"They're learning," I said. "Slowly. Loudly. With fires. You'd have liked them. You'd have been merciless about the bench."

I stayed until the sun went down and the first stars came out — real stars, in their proper numbers, none of them going anywhere.

* * *

Sera found me on the path down, lantern in hand, which was unnecessary given the stars but very much in character.

"Well?" she asked. "Did the dead woman accept your apology?"

"The dead woman is laughing at me somewhere. I felt it."

"Good. You need more people laughing at you. It keeps the sigils humble." She fell into step beside me, and for a while we walked in the easy silence of people who have drowned a city district together, escaped a palace together, and argued about curriculum design for three years, which by certain measures is the greater ordeal.

At the fork in the path she stopped. "The Brackhollow delegation arrived today. While you were communing. They want to talk about opening a second hall on the coast. A real one. Tide-reading and wardcraft under one roof, gods help us."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them the head of the guild would say yes, because he says yes to everything, it's a sickness."

"He would also say," I said, "that the coast delegation should bring its own benches. Fireproof ones. I learn slowly."

Sera smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one I'd crossed a kingdom and unbound a god to see and would deny under oath that it had been worth it, purely to keep her from getting smug. Then her expression shifted into something more careful, and she reached into her coat and produced a chart. New-drawn, the ink barely dry, full of coastline that had not existed four years ago.

"One more thing," she said. "The deep readings came back from the southern shelf. There's a current down there that shouldn't exist. Warm water, moving against every law we have, steady as a pulse." She tapped the chart. "And at the center of it, Odile — the instruments pick up something old. Something patient. Not the entity. Something else. Something that's been waiting under the sea since before the guild was founded, and now that the stillness is broken, it's starting to wake up."

I looked at the chart a long moment. At the long, impossible current, running off the edge of the paper in both directions like a road.

"Of course it is," I said at last, and pocketed the sigil, and started walking. "Wake the students. All of them. And Sera — bring extra benches."

"*Fireproof* benches," she agreed, falling into step beside me, and together we went down the hill toward the lamplit hall, where the doors stood open, as they always would now, to whoever came.
