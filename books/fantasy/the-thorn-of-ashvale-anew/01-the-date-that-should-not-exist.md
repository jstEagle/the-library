## 1. The Date That Should Not Exist

The vault of the Ashvale Wardens smelled of cold stone and older paper, a scent Isandro Delacroix had come to think of as the smell of safety itself. Nine years she had walked these shelves, first as an apprentice trailing behind Senior Archivist Mott with her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, and now, at twenty-three, as a full Warden of the Second Rank—junior enough that inventory duty fell to her every autumn, senior enough that no one checked her work twice.

It was the kind of task that made a person small in a comfortable way. Count the candlestock. Note the condition of the salted hides. Confirm that the iron-bound chests on Row Three still held what their labels swore they held. The guild had kept meticulous records for a hundred and twelve years, and every autumn the youngest Warden traced those records with her finger and found them true.

Isandro was good at being small. She had learned it early, the way orphans learn things—not through instruction but through the slow accumulation of evidence. The Wardens had taken her in when she was fourteen, after the fever took her parents and left her a name that meant nothing to anyone. Delacroix. A name with no history attached, no cousins, no family plot in the terraced cemetery above the millpond. Just a girl who could copy a ledger hand faster than anyone in the hall and never asked questions that made people uncomfortable.

She had built a life out of not making people uncomfortable. It was a good life. Warm bread on festival mornings. The low murmur of the training yard drifting up through the archive window. Guildmaster Veyra's hand heavy and brief on her shoulder each solstice, saying, *You do honest work, Isandro,* which was the closest thing to love anyone in the valley said aloud.

Tonight the vault lamps burned low and yellow, and the tedium wrapped around her like a blanket. She worked down Row Seven, the row nobody cared about—old ledgers superseded by newer ones, guild histories copied so many times the ink had gone brown, almanacs of tides for a valley that had no sea.

The book was wedged flat behind a false panel in the shelf's back wall, and she only found it because the panel had swollen with damp and stood proud of the wood by the width of a knife blade.

Isandro knelt there for a long moment, telling herself it was nothing. A repair job someone had abandoned. But her fingers were already working at the seam, and the panel came away with a sound like a sigh, and behind it lay a book bound in leather gone dark as dried blood.

It was heavier than it should have been. The pages were thick, soft-edged, written in a dozen different hands spanning what looked like generations. Not a guild ledger. Not a history. The headings, when she turned carefully toward the lamp, read like entries in a diary kept by someone who expected to be believed: *Observed. Confirmed. Witnessed under seal.*

Prophecies.

The word arrived in her mind and sat there, ugly and foreign. The Wardens did not traffic in prophecy. The Wardens kept magic *out*—that was the whole of the oath, recited every morning until it wore smooth as river stone. *Magic cannot follow where it is not invited. We are the valley's uninviting.* Prophecy was a kind of magic, wasn't it? Or close enough to it that the book had no business existing anywhere inside these walls, let alone hidden behind a panel in the back of Row Seven.

Her first instinct—the instinct nine years of good, small living had trained into her—was to put it back. Close the panel. Dust her knees. Log Row Seven complete and go up to supper feeling the pleasant tiredness of a day honestly spent.

Instead she turned to the last entry.

The handwriting here was old but sure, letters tall and narrow, ink faded to the color of weak tea. The entry began mid-page, beneath a date from before the guild's founding, and Isandro read it once without understanding, then again more slowly, and then a third time with her heart going strange and heavy in her chest.

*When the moon completes her thirteenth turn upon the covenant's breaking, the valley's debt comes due. One shall cross who carries the name of the last to leave: the thorn-child, Delacroix's line, keeper of what was surrendered. She shall stand at the stones and choose—and her choosing shall be the reforging, or the ending of both lands together.*

Below it, in the same narrow hand, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*The price is memory. This is why we broke it rather than pay.*

Isandro's hands had gone very still around the book. She noticed this the way one notices one's own breathing—as something suddenly requiring management.

*Delacroix's line.* There were perhaps forty people in Ashvale named Delacroix, most of them related to the cooper's family up Water Street. But none of them were Wardens. None of them had any reason to be written in a century-old book hidden in the vault.

Unless.

Unless the book was not a century old at all. Unless the final entry had been added later, the ink fresher than the rest—

She tilted the page toward the lamp. The ink was faded. Uniformly, honestly faded, the way ink gets when it has been sitting in the dark for longer than she had been alive.

And then her eye fell on the date inscribed at the head of the entry, the date the prophecy was *witnessed under seal*, and the vault went very quiet around her, quiet in the way the valley goes quiet just before snow.

The date was tomorrow's.

Not approximately. Not a coincidence of numerals she could squint at and explain away. Tomorrow's date, exact, in a book whose other entries spanned two hundred years, in ink that had faded for longer than her lifetime, naming a family that was hers and no one else's.

The lamp flame bent in a draft she didn't feel. Somewhere far above, through layers of stone and timber, the evening bell rang for supper, and Isandro did not move. She knelt on the cold floor of Row Seven holding a book that knew tomorrow existed, and felt the shape of her whole life shift slightly, the way furniture shifts when a house settles—a small sound, a small movement, and afterward everything sits in a different place whether you want it to or not.

*Thorn-child.* Her grandmother had called her that exactly once, when Isandro was very small, pulling a thorn from her palm after she'd grabbed at a rose stem too greedy to wait for the gardener. *Careful, little thorn-child. You're the sort that stays stuck.* Her grandmother, whose own name Isandro could never quite recall being spoken—who appeared in no family stories, whose grave, if she had one, Isandro had never seen.

She had thought, all these years, that she simply hadn't listened closely enough. Children don't retain names. It was a small absence, the kind a person fills in with assumption and moves past.

Now, kneeling in the dark with a dead prophet's ink drying on her conscience, the absence opened under her like a trapdoor.

* * *

She did not remember deciding to hide the book. That was the part that unsettled her most, later—how smoothly the decision arrived without passing through anything she'd recognize as a choice. One moment she was reading; the next, the panel was pressed back into place with the book flat against her stomach beneath her coat, and she was climbing the vault stairs with her ledger held open in front of her like a devout woman carrying scripture, and her face was doing what her face always did, which was nothing at all.

Row Seven, complete. Candlestock counted. Hides confirmed. All in order.

All lies, now, written in her own neat copying hand.

The guild hall above was loud with supper—the clatter of tin plates, Warden Brannic arguing amiably about hunting quotas, someone's dog underfoot begging scraps. Ordinary noise. Safe noise. Isandro moved through it carrying the weight of the book against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and accepted a bowl of barley stew from the kitchen girl, and ate three spoonfuls of it, and tasted nothing.

Guildmaster Veyra sat at the high table, as she did every night, gray-haired and straight-backed, mending a strap from her sword baldric while she listened to reports. Veyra had run the Wardens for thirty years. She had signed Isandro's apprenticeship papers personally. She had once, when Isandro was sixteen and homesick for a home that had never existed, sat beside her in the chapel loft for a whole hour saying nothing at all, which had helped more than words would have.

Veyra looked up across the hall. Their eyes met.

Isandro felt the book against her ribs like a coal.

"Delacroix." The guildmaster's voice carried easily; it always had. "Inventory going well?"

"Well enough, Guildmaster." Steady. Her voice was steady. "Row Seven's finished. I'll start the reliquary drawers tomorrow."

Something crossed Veyra's face—too fast to name, gone before Isandro could decide whether it was a smile or its opposite. "Take your time with the reliquary," Veyra said. "Old things deserve patience."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

Isandro finished her stew without tasting it. Around her the hall roared on, warm and golden and utterly unaware—or was it? Was the warmth thinner tonight, or was that only the coal against her ribs burning through her shirt? She watched Brannic laugh at his own joke. She watched the kitchen girl stack bowls. She watched Veyra fold away her mending and rise, and pause, and look down the length of the hall at nothing in particular with the expression of a woman listening for a sound only she could hear.

Then Veyra climbed to her chambers, and the hall exhaled, and Isandro sat alone among the ordinary noise holding a secret that had tomorrow's date on it.

* * *

She hid the book badly, by the standards of people who hide things often, and well, by the standards of a girl who had never hidden anything larger than a stolen honeycake. Beneath the loose floorboard under her cot, wrapped in her spare shirt, weighted down with the flat iron she used to press her uniform collar. If anyone searched her cell they would find it in a minute. But no one searched cells in Ashvale. No one had ever needed to. There was nowhere to go and nothing to hide from, because the valley was safe, the valley had always been safe, the Wardens saw to it.

She lay on her cot in the dark and did not sleep.

Tomorrow. The word kept turning over. *Witnessed under seal, tomorrow's date.* Which meant either that a fraudster had written it long ago and guessed right—which was absurd, dates were not guessed, not to the day, not across centuries—or that whoever wrote it had known. Had known the day, the name, the family. Had known about a grandmother no record mentioned, a lineage cut clean out of the guild rolls so thoroughly that even Isandro herself had never wondered hard enough to notice the wound.

*This is why we broke it rather than pay.*

Broke what? Pay for what? The covenant—the word surfaced from the morning oath, from the recitation she'd performed ten thousand times without ever hearing it. *The covenant is kept; the valley is kept.* She had assumed it meant the warding itself, the boundary stones, the whole apparatus of safety. But you couldn't break a thing and keep it. The sentence contradicted itself, and contradictions, Mott used to say, are where the truth hides when it doesn't want to be found.

Outside her narrow window, the moon rose over the eastern ridge, one night off from full, silvering the roofs of the town she had never once left. Twenty-three years within sight of those same hills. She knew every trail on the near slopes, every stream, every stand of birch. She knew the valley the way you know your own hands.

And hands, she thought suddenly, staring at her own pale fingers in the moonlight, hands can be looked at too closely. Hands have lines you never drew and scars you can't account for.

The book knew tomorrow. The book knew her name. And somewhere below her floorboards, wrapped in a spare shirt under a pressing iron, it waited for morning with more certainty than she had ever felt about anything in her life.

Isandro closed her eyes. When sleep finally took her, near dawn, she dreamed of a door standing open in a stone wall, and beyond it a country she did not recognize, and someone calling a name across the distance that might have been hers, if names could survive being forgotten.

She woke to the bell, dressed, and touched the floorboard once with her fingertips—an oath, though she couldn't have said to whom.

Down in the vault, the reliquary drawers waited. Down in the vault, Row Seven stood innocent behind its swollen panel. Down in the vault, somewhere in the guild's hundred and twelve careful years, there had to be another mention of the covenant—another thread to pull.

She had inventory to finish. She had a date to keep that she had never agreed to.

Isandro Delacroix picked up her ledger, squared her shoulders like a Warden, and went down the stairs into the oldest room in the valley, carrying tomorrow in her chest like a splinter working its way toward the skin.
