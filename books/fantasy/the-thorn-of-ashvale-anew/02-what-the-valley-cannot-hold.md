## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

Isandro did not sleep.

She had told herself she would—told herself that a book of prophecies was only ink and vellum, that dates could be coincidence, that names repeated themselves across generations the way river stones repeated their shapes. She had lain in her narrow bed above the Warden stables with the blanket pulled to her chin, listening to the valley breathe around her: the creak of the drying racks, the far complaint of a gate hinge, old Marrow coughing two doors down the wardens' row. All the small sounds of Ashvale, each one familiar as her own pulse.

And beneath all of it, one thought turning over and over like a coin on a table:

*Isandro Delacroix.*

Her name. In a book older than the guildhall itself. Dated tomorrow.

So instead of sleeping, at the gray hour before the kitchen fires were lit, she dressed in the dark, took her inventory keys from the hook by the door, and let herself back down into the vault where she had found the thing. If it was a trick—a test, a joke played by some senior warden with an idle season and a cruel streak—then she wanted to catch it in the act. Tricks lived in daylight. Tricks fell apart when you looked at them from underneath.

The vault did not fall apart.

The book lay exactly where she had shoved it, behind the ledgers of toll-records and the boxed regalia of wardens long dead, as though it had been waiting there for her all along—which, she realized with a cold slide in her stomach, it had been waiting for *someone* all along, and had waited a very long time.

She carried it up to the archive room instead of staying below. The vault was Veyra's territory, watched by wards and habit; the archive was hers, cluttered and sunless and safe, a room so full of neglected paper that no one had bothered to enter it since the last archivist died. Isandro lit two lamps, closed the door, and set the book on the table like something that might bite.

Up here, with light on it, she could see what she'd missed in the vault's dimness. The binding was not leather but something finer, darker, with a grain almost like skin. Along the spine ran a line of script in no alphabet she knew—or rather, in an alphabet she knew too well and had never once questioned, because it was stamped on every boundary stone from the north ridge to the mill ford: the Old Marks, the letters the founders had used, the letters guild law said were *decorative*, relics of a pious age, meaning nothing anymore.

Meaning nothing. Isandro traced one with her fingertip. It was the mark carved into the stone at Shepherd's Rest, the one apprentices touched for luck before their first solo patrol. *Luck.* She had pressed her thumb to that stone a hundred times and never wondered why luck should live in a letter nobody could read.

"Right," she whispered to the empty room. Her voice sounded smaller than she wanted it to. "Then we do this properly."

Properly meant the Codex. Every Warden learned it before they learned their sword-grip: *the Law of Ashvale, Being the Rules by Which the Valley Keeps Its Peace.* Isandro had copied out whole chapters of it as a girl for penance—she'd been a restless apprentice, always climbing where she shouldn't—and so she knew its shape the way you know a house you grew up in, even the rooms you never entered. She fetched the master copy down from its lectern, blew dust from the clasps, and opened it across from the prophecy book so the two volumes faced each other like duelists.

The Codex's first law, the law everything else hung from, was simple enough to carve on a coin:

*The Valley is kept, that magic may not enter it. The Wardens keep the Valley. What is kept out cannot harm what is within.*

Isandro read it four times. Then she read the final entry of the prophecy book again, and set the two texts side by side, and felt the first hairline crack open across the foundation of her life.

Because the prophecy did not speak of magic being kept *out*. It spoke of a covenant—one word, repeated, worn into the vellum by some scribe who had written it over and over until his hand shook:

*While the covenant holds, both realms live. When the covenant is remade, both realms live. There is no third way. There is no wall that feeds.*

*A wall that feeds.* Isandro sat back. The lamp flame leaned in a draft she couldn't feel.

Guild law said the valley was protected. Protected implied attack; attack implied an enemy pressing against the walls, wanting in. That was the story every child in Ashvale learned with their first wooden practice blade: beyond the stones lies wild magic, and wild magic devours, and the Wardens are the shield-edge of the world. It was a good story. It made the patrols meaningful and the cold watches bearable and the graves by the guildhall—there were only nine, in three centuries, but every child knew all nine names—into martyrs fallen holding the line.

But a covenant wasn't a wall. A covenant was a *promise*, a thing between two parties, a thing that required both sides to hold up their end. And you could not make a promise to a devouring storm. You could not pause a pact with a flood.

Unless—

Isandro turned pages, faster now, hunting through the Codex for the sections on the boundary stones, the founding, the Sealing—that was the word they used, capitalized, solemn, *the Sealing of Ashvale*, done by the Nine Founders in the year the guild counts from. She found the passage. She had read it a dozen times in her life and recited it twice at festivals. It took up less than half a page, which should have struck her even then as strange, because everything else about the Founders filled books: their virtues, their sayings, the exact cut of their cloaks.

*And the Nine went up to the stones and spoke the words of keeping, and laid their hands upon them, and the Valley was sealed against the wild, and there was peace thereafter.*

That was all. No words quoted. No rite described. No enemy named—the "wild" never given a face, a shape, a single documented act of harm in three hundred years of meticulous guild record-keeping. The Wardens recorded everything. Isandro knew how many nails had been bought for the granary roof in her grandmother's time. And the founding event of their entire existence, the reason the guild drew breath, got four sentences and no details.

Her hands had gone cold on the page edges. She noticed distantly, the way you notice weather.

*Paused,* the prophecy had called it—not broken, not ended. *The covenant is paused, not slain, and what is paused may be resumed.* She flipped back to it and read the surrounding lines slowly, forcing herself, the way you force yourself to look at a wound.

*The Nine stood at the stones and did not pay. They turned the promise inward and locked the door, and called the lock a blessing, and taught their children the lock was the world. But a seal has two faces. What they shut away, they also shut within.*

Isandro read that last sentence five times.

*What they shut away, they also shut within.*

She looked up, without meaning to, at the archive's high narrow window. Beyond the glass, past the rooftops, the shoulder of the north ridge was just visible, black against the graying sky, and somewhere along that ridgeline stood the stones—thirty-one of them, she knew the number from patrol maps, spaced along the valley rim like beads on a string. Thirty-one stones carved in letters no one read, marking a border no enemy had ever crossed, guarding against a danger no record could name.

Not a wall.

A knot. A tourniquet, tied by frightened people, on a limb that still needed its blood.

The vertigo came then, sudden and total, the way it does when the floor turns out to be thinner than you believed. Isandro gripped the table edge. The room seemed to tilt gently around her lamps. Because if the prophecy was true—if it was even a tenth true—then her whole life had been spent inside a locked room, and worse, a locked room whose inhabitants had been taught to sing songs of gratitude to the lock. Every festival. Every oath. Every time she'd stood watch on the ridge at night, proud and cold and certain, looking out at the dark and feeling sorry for whatever was out there—

There was no *out there*. Or rather, there was, but the stones weren't facing it like shields. They were facing it like a lid.

And the last line of the entry, the one dated tomorrow, the one bearing her name, rose in her memory with terrible new weight:

*On the thirteenth turn the choice returns. Let the Delacroix hand choose better than the hands before it.*

*Choose better.* As if the Founders had chosen badly. As if the sealing—the holiest act in the guild's history, the thing the whole Codex bent around like iron filings around a lodestone—had been a *choice*, a wrong one, one someone was expected to come along someday and correct.

Someone like her.

"No," she said aloud, to the room, to the book, to the crack spreading through everything. "No, that's—" But she didn't have an end for the sentence. *That's impossible* was not available to her, because the book had her name and her name was not an impossible thing to write. *That's treason* was available, and true by every law she'd sworn, and it settled over her like wet wool. By the Codex, what she was doing right now—reading this, doubting this—was the first step of the oldest crime: *sunder-thought*, the apprentices called it, jokingly, daring each other to wonder what was beyond the stones. Jokingly. Because no one ever actually wondered. Why would anyone wonder? Wondering was like wondering what was outside your own skin.

Isandro sat for a long time while the light strengthened in the window and the lamps burned yellow and useless against the dawn. Then, because she was her father's daughter and a Warden down to the marrow whether she liked it or not, she did the only thing that steadied her: she worked. She checked the claim against the record.

If the covenant had been paused rather than destroyed—if the stones were a seal rather than a wall—then the stones themselves should show it. Walls deaden. Seals hold tension. Even her limited craft-knowledge told her that much: a maintained working hummed faintly to a trained touch, the way a drawn bowstring thrums. Guild doctrine held that the stones were inert, blessed masonry, needing nothing but repainting of the warning sigils every spring.

Doctrine, she was beginning to understand, was the guild's second language—the one it spoke when the truth would be inconvenient.

There were records of the stones. Of course there were; the Wardens recorded everything. Maintenance logs going back two centuries, entries in neat rotating hands: *North ridge, stone 14, surface sound, marks re-cut, no resonance observed.* Over and over, generation after generation: *no resonance observed.* Two hundred years of wardens touching those stones with trained palms and feeling *nothing*.

Except.

Isandro went through the logs like a woman sorting grain, faster and faster, and found them—the outliers, the entries that didn't fit, scattered thin across the decades like seeds on cloth. *Stone 9, south ford: brief warmth under hand, cause unknown.* *Stone 22, ridge path: low humming heard at moonset by Warden Osk, duration one breath.* *Stone 3: frost pattern on east face overnight despite mild air, arranged in concentric rings.* Each one dismissed in the margin in some long-dead inspector's tidy script: *natural causes. no action.* Natural causes. Frost arranging itself in rings. A bowstring thrumming once, twice, a hundred times across two hundred years, and every warden who felt it writing it off because writing it off was easier than asking what a *sealed* thing was supposed to feel like.

They hadn't killed it. Whatever the Founders had done at those stones, whatever promise they'd knotted and locked, it was still alive in there. Straining. Thrumming against the rope like something patient in a cellar.

And the thirteenth turn—the book had said the moon completes its thirteenth turn, and Isandro's stomach clenched remembering that she didn't yet know how long a turn was, whether she had years or seasons or weeks—whatever was coming, the seal was tied to it somehow, and her name was tied to the seal, and tomorrow's date sat at the bottom of the page like a signature waiting to be witnessed.

She looked at the two books lying open together, law and prophecy, lock and key, and understood that she was holding her entire world in her hands and it weighed less than a cat.

*What they shut away, they also shut within.*

Isandro closed the Codex gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead, and wrapped the prophecy book in her spare cloak, and put it in the bottom of the archive chest beneath twenty years of unsorted requisition forms, because even now—even with the floor gone soft under her—she could not quite bring herself to carry it back to the vault, where the guildmaster's wards might be watching, where Veyra might ask questions in that warm level voice of hers, *Find anything interesting, Isandro?*

She banked the lamps. She climbed the stairs into the ordinary morning smell of bread and horses and woodsmoke, into the valley she had loved the way you love a house you've never doubted had walls.

At the top of the steps she stopped. Below in the yard, early-rising wardens moved through their drills, and the mountains stood blue and eternal around everything, and it was all so beautiful, and it was all a room.

Tonight, Isandro decided. Tonight she would walk the perimeter. Not cross—she wasn't mad—but walk the line of stones and put her palm flat against one and wait, honestly wait, for the answer the maintenance logs had spent two centuries pretending wasn't there.

She was halfway across the yard, composing her patrol excuse, when she saw Guildmaster Veyra standing at the hall door, watching her with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and an expression Isandro had never seen her wear before.

It looked, terribly, like pity.
