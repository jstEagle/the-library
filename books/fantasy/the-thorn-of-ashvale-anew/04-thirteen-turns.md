## 4. Thirteen Turns

The lunar records of the Ashvale Wardens were kept in a room no larger than a pantry, and Isandro had spent three nights pretending she had reason to be in it.

The excuse was legitimate enough on its face. Every Warden of the third circle was required to complete an annual audit of the almanacs—the great leather-bound ledgers that recorded, night by night and year upon year, the phase of the moon as observed from the guild's eastern tower. It was tedious work. It was *supposed* to be tedious work. The tedium was the point; the guild believed, with the quiet fervor of people who had never once been surprised, that a thing watched nightly for four hundred years could not go wrong unnoticed.

Isandro sat at the narrow desk beneath the slitted window with a candle burning low and the prophecy book open on her lap, hidden beneath the audit ledger like a sin beneath a tablecloth.

*When the moon completes her thirteenth turn,* the old script read, *the daughter of Delacroix must stand where the covenant was broken, or both realms shall wither as one.*

Thirteenth turn. She had read the phrase forty times now, and each time it slipped through her understanding like water through cupped hands. Moons did not have turns. Moons had phases—waxing, waning, full, dark—and those turned endlessly, meaninglessly, a wheel that spun whether anyone watched or not. But the ledger was not written in the language of casual observation. The Wardens who had kept these records had been precise to the point of obsession. If they had recorded something called a *turn*, then a turn was a real thing, countable, and somewhere in these pages was the arithmetic of it.

She began with the full moons.

It was simple, once she let herself be simple about it. A turn, she reasoned, must be a full turning of the moon's face—from full to full, or from dark to dark. She took the current ledger, found the most recent entry marked with the heavy inked circle that denoted a full moon, and counted backward through the years, tallying in the margin of her audit sheet in a hand she barely recognized as her own.

Twelve full moons made one year. That was nothing. That was the moon everyone knew.

But the elder ledgers used a different reckoning. In the oldest volumes—the ones whose spines had gone soft as bread, whose ink had faded to the brown of dried blood—the scribes had marked certain full moons with a second symbol: a small thorned star drawn beside the circle. Not every month. Not even every year. Isandro paged back through decades, her finger trailing down columns of circles and crescents, and found the starred moons appearing at intervals so irregular they seemed random until she stopped looking at the dates and started looking at the gaps between them.

Thirteen months apart. Sometimes fourteen. Once, twice, thirteen again.

Her breath fogged the cold page. She sat very still.

There were moons—and she could see them now, marked faithfully across four centuries of careful hands—that came full not twelve times a year but thirteen. An extra moon. A thirteenth turn, tucked into the calendar like a knife into a belt, arriving when it arrived and not before, unannounced by any almanac she'd ever been taught to read. The common calendars hanging in the mess hall counted twelve. The guild's true records counted what the sky actually did.

And the last starred moon had come eleven years ago.

Which meant the next one—the next thirteenth turn—was due within the year. The intervals varied; she checked them all, ran the count three times, her candle guttering, her heart keeping its own frantic time against her ribs. Thirteen months. Twelve. Possibly fewer. The oldest interval in the record was ten months.

Under a year. She had under a year to stand where the covenant was broken—a place she could not find, for a covenant she could not name, in a realm the guild insisted did not exist.

Isandro closed the ledger. Closed the prophecy book. Sat in the dark of the little room with her hands flat on the covers and listened to the wind move through the tower above her, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the safe small life she had resented and loved in equal measure had just acquired an expiration date.

* * *

Elder Maren was dying the way old oaks die—slowly, stubbornly, in place.

She occupied the small stone cell at the end of the infirmary corridor, the one with the window facing west, because she had told the healers she intended to watch every sunset she had left and they had not been foolish enough to argue. She had served the Wardens for sixty years. She had trained three generations of boundary-walkers, including Guildmaster Veyra herself, and her memory was famous throughout the valley for being longer than anyone's patience.

Isandro brought her the tea she didn't drink and the company she pretended not to want, which was how things were done between them. Isandro had been visiting since her first year in the guild, when Maren had caught her practicing sigils out of order and, instead of reporting her, had corrected her grip and said, *You'll do, if you learn to be quieter about being clever.*

Tonight Isandro poured the tea, set it steaming untouched as always, and sat on the stool by the window while the sun went down over the valley in bands of copper and violet, and tried for half an hour to ask nothing at all.

Maren saw through her in eleven minutes.

"You've got the look," the old woman said. Her voice was dry leaves and gravel, worn down by years and illness to almost nothing, but her eyes were still sharp as awls. "The look you get when you've found something in the vaults and you're deciding whether telling me is safer than not telling me."

Isandro looked at her hands. "Is there a right answer?"

"There's always a right answer." Maren shifted against her pillows with a sound like kindling settling. "Whether it's the same answer twice is another matter."

Outside, the first stars were coming up over the Ashvale ridge. Isandro watched them instead of the old woman, because some questions were easier asked to the horizon.

"The prophecy book," she said. "The one in the deep vault. The black one with the iron clasps."

She felt rather than saw Maren go still. The change was small—a cessation of the tiny restless movements that even dying bodies make—but Isandro had spent six years learning to read this woman, and she read it plainly.

"You found it," Maren said.

"During inventory. The final entry has tomorrow's date on it."

"And your name."

It wasn't a question. Isandro turned to look at her then, and what she saw frightened her more than anything the book itself had shown her: not surprise. Grief. A grief so old it had gone smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for a lifetime, taken out only to be held.

"How long have you known?" Isandro whispered.

"That the book exists? Since I was younger than you. That it would speak your name?" Maren's mouth twisted. "No one knew that. No one *could* know that. It names whom it names." She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they had gone fixed and distant, aimed at something further away than the sunset. "I hoped it would stay shut another hundred years. Foolish hope. Books like that don't stay shut. They wait until someone opens them, and then they pretend they were never closed at all."

"Maren—" Isandro leaned forward. "The thirteenth turn. I found it in the lunar records. The extra moon. It's coming within the year, isn't it. That's why the date—"

"Yes."

"It says I have to stand where the covenant was broken. What covenant? Broken *where*? Maren, nobody will tell me anything. The boundary stones are supposed to protect us, but the book calls them a seal, and the outer realm—"

"Stop." The word cracked like a whip, and then Maren coughed, long and terrible, and Isandro held the tea to her lips though neither of them pretended anymore that it was for drinking. When the coughing passed, the old woman lay back, gray-faced, and gripped Isandro's wrist with a strength that startled her.

"Listen to me, girl, because I won't say this twice and I may not have the strength to say it well." Her eyes bored upward. "You're going to go. I can see it in you already—you've got the going-look, the same look she had."

Isandro's mouth went dry. "*She?*"

"The last one named in the book." Maren's grip tightened. "Before your time. Before Veyra's time, even. A warden named Sera Ilvane—no, that's not the name she was born to, but it's the one we knew her by. Clever as a fox and twice as restless. The book named her, just as it's named you, and she went out past the stones to do whatever the book demanded of her." The old woman's voice dropped to a rasp. "Four of us walked her to the boundary at moonrise. I was the youngest. She laughed the whole way out—laughed, and told us to keep the kettle warm, she'd be back before the snowmelt."

The fire popped in the little stove. Somewhere down the corridor, a healer's footsteps faded away.

"She never came home," Maren said.

The words fell into the room and lay there.

"No body. No word. We petitioned the guildmaster of that day to send a search party past the stones, and he refused—refused, girl, with tears in his eyes, saying the covenant forbade it, saying the book had *taken* her and the book could keep her. And then the records were amended. Her name struck from the rolls. Her cell reassigned within the week. As if she had never stood among us at all." Maren's breath rattled. "I was young and I obeyed. That is the sin I'll carry into whatever comes next. I obeyed, and I let them erase her, and I have spent fifty years wondering if she's still out there, waiting for someone from home to come looking."

Isandro's throat ached. "Why are you telling me this? If the guild erased her—if knowing is dangerous—"

"Because you are Delacroix." Maren said the name like a key turning in a lock. "And because I am done obeying." She pulled Isandro closer, close enough that Isandro could smell the sickness on her breath and the lavender oil the healers used, close enough to see that her eyes were wet. "They will tell you the valley is safety. They will tell it kindly, because most of them believe it. But hear me, child: the founders broke something, and everything the guild has built for four hundred years—the walls, the law, the *kindness*—is built on top of the breaking. You cannot fix a thing like that from inside the walls. Whatever the book asks of you, it will ask you to leave. And when you go, they will call it treason."

"Then what do I call it?"

Maren smiled. It transformed her ruined face into something luminous and terrible.

"Yours," she said. "Call it yours. It's the only thing they can't take from you." Her grip loosened, slid from Isandro's wrist to the blanket. "Now help me sit up. The sun's nearly gone and I'll not miss it for the likes of prophecy."

Isandro eased her upright, arranged the pillows, and settled back onto the stool. Together they watched the last light drain out of the valley—the orchards going blue, the guild towers catching the final copper, the boundary stones on the far ridge visible even from here as a faint pale stitching across the darkening land, holding everything in.

Or holding everything out. She no longer knew which, and suspected she never truly had.

"One more thing," Maren murmured, her eyes already closing. "The book. Hide it better than you think you need to. And Isandro—" Her voice was nearly gone now, a thread of sound. "When you stand at the stones, if you find anything of Sera Ilvane out there... bring her name home, even if you can't bring the rest of her. Names are the only luggage the dead require."

Isandro walked back through the corridors of the guild she had called home her whole life, past wardens nodding to her in the lamplight, past the mess hall where someone was laughing, past the notice board with tomorrow's patrol assignments posted in Veyra's neat hand—and felt the whole structure of it differently now. Not a fortress. A clock. Ticking toward a thirteenth moon, with her name wound into its works.

In her room, she lit no lamp. She took the prophecy book from beneath her mattress, and by the light of the moon through the shutters—three days off full, and counting—she turned to the final page and read her own name again.

Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, and began, quietly, to plan how to get past the boundary stones.

Outside her window, the moon rose over Ashvale, patient as a debt.
