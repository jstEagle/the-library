## 5. The Boundary Stones

The bell over the Warden-house door had not yet finished its midnight toll when Isandro slipped out into the cold.

She had dressed in the dark, by feel, the way she'd learned to as a child sneaking down to the kitchens—breeches first, then boots, then the quilted coat that smelled of cedar and old smoke. She left the prophecy book behind, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard under her cot. It felt wrong to leave it. It felt more wrong to carry it. A thing that knew your name was not a thing you wanted pressed against your heart while you walked alone at night.

The streets of Ashvale were empty in the way she loved best: lanterns burned low behind shuttered windows, and the cobbles held the day's warmth like banked coals. Somewhere a dog turned over in its sleep. The baker's chimney breathed the first pale ghost of tomorrow's bread. This was her town. She had mapped it a hundred times in her head without ever meaning to—the crooked lane behind the tannery, the willow that leaned too far over the millpond, the step outside the chapel that caught your toe if you weren't watching. She knew it the way you know your own hands.

And tonight, for the first time, she looked at all of it and thought: *a locked room.*

The phrase had come to her three days ago, standing in front of the boundary stone at the western crossroads with her notebook open, pretending to survey drainage channels for the guildmaster's quarterly report. The stones were not protection. She had checked the law texts until her eyes ached, and the law never once said *protection*. It said *containment*. It said *preservation*. It said, in the founding charter's careful, lawyerly hand: *that which is within shall remain within, and that which is without shall remain without.* A covenant *paused*, Ondine would have called it—if Ondine existed, if Sable Hollow existed, if any of the things Isandro had read in a book that should not exist were true. But the phrasing of the charter itself, the one every Warden memorized at twelve and recited without thinking for the rest of their lives—even that phrasing admitted it. You did not pause something that was dead. You paused something that was waiting.

So tonight she would touch a stone, and she would know.

* * *

The perimeter path was older than the road and meaner than either. It wound along the valley's rim where the foothills began their long climb toward the pass, through gorse and scree, past shrines so small they were hardly more than stacked stones with candle stubs melted into the crevices. Isandro had walked stretches of it before—every Warden did patrol duty along the boundary from the age of sixteen—but always in daylight, always in pairs, always with the cheerful, bored diligence of people guarding a fence around nothing. Tonight she walked it alone, under a moon four nights from full, and everything about the landscape seemed to be holding its breath.

The third stone on the eastern rise was the one she'd chosen. It stood hip-high and weather-smooth, furred with lichen the color of tarnished silver, and it bore the same worn sigil as all the others: a circle broken by a single vertical line. Every child in Ashvale could draw it in the dirt before they could write their own name. *The Wardens' Mark*, they called it. *The Keeping Sign.*

Isandro stopped two paces away and made herself look at it properly, the way you look at a stranger's face and try to find your own features there.

The wind came up off the valley below, carrying woodsmoke and wet grass and the faint iron smell of the smithy's evening quench. Home smells. She breathed them in like a woman memorizing a letter she might have to recite later.

Then she pulled off her right glove, knelt, and laid her bare palm against the stone.

Cold—that she expected; stone at midnight holds the sky's temperature better than the earth's. But this was a different order of cold. It went through her skin and into the bones of her hand and kept going, a cold with a current in it, a cold that *pulled*, the way deep lake water pulls at a swimmer who has gone too far from shore. She gasped. She tried to take her hand back and found she couldn't—not frozen, not gripped, simply *unwilling*, some part of her deeper than decision refusing the retreat.

And then the stone showed her the sky.

Not showed—there was no image, no vision, nothing so orderly as that. It was more that the night above her *changed*, the way a face changes when someone you thought you knew turns out to have been wearing an expression you'd never seen. The stars rearranged themselves. Not moved—rearranged, as if the constellations she had known since childhood were merely one arrangement among many possible arrangements, and the stone had let her glimpse another. There was a second moon. Small, green-white, hanging low near a horizon she did not recognize, and beneath it the land rolled away and away and *away*, farther than the valley was wide, farther than anything inside the seal could possibly be, hills and rivers and a forest that went dark to the edge of seeing, and somewhere out in all that distance a light that was not a star and not a fire but something patient and enormous, like a city breathing.

It lasted three heartbeats. Perhaps four.

Then the cold released her and she was on her knees in the gorse with her palm smoking faintly—actually smoking, a thread of white vapor rising off her skin though there was no burn—and the ordinary stars of home wheeled overhead, indifferent, familiar, and the valley lay below her like a cupped hand holding everything she had ever loved.

Isandro sat back on her heels and laughed, once, a thin and frightened sound.

*Another sky.* Not a metaphor. Not guild rhetoric. Another actual sky, with another actual moon, hanging over an actual country that the founders had locked the door on—and the door was not a wall, it was a *promise*, and promises could be unmade, and the book with her name in it had known, had *known*, before she did—

"Isandro."

She spun, hand flying to the short knife at her belt, and her heart slammed once, hard, before her eyes found him and told the rest of her body to stand down.

Tam Voss stood ten paces up the path, breath fogging, his big frame hunched into a shepherd's cloak with the hood down. He had a lantern in one hand, unlit, and he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read—or rather, with an expression made of several expressions fighting for the same face: alarm, and guilt, and something underneath both that looked almost like relief.

"How long have you been standing there?" she asked.

"Since the ford." He came closer, slowly, the way you approach a startled animal or a friend crying. "You cut across the mill field. I saw your shape against the water." He stopped, looked at her hand—at the last wisp of vapor curling off her fingers—and his voice dropped. "What did you do?"

"What did *I* do?" She got to her feet, knees stiff with cold and adrenaline, and heard her own voice come out sharper than she intended. "You followed me out of the walls at midnight, Tam. In this valley that's worth a hearing before the full chapter. What are you going to tell them?"

"That depends," he said, "on what you're going to tell me."

The wind moved through the gorse with a dry whispering sound, like paper turning. Above them the broken-circle sigil caught the moonlight, and Isandro became suddenly, painfully aware of how it must look from where he stood: her kneeling before the stone, her bare hand upon it, smoke rising. He had seen. There was no version of this night now in which he hadn't seen.

She had known Tam since they were eight years old, when he'd arrived at the Warden-house orphan steps half-frozen with a broken arm and a name he wouldn't say for a month. They had grown up sharing the same narrow beds in rotation, the same punishment drills, the same illicit sweets smuggled past Matron Corla. He was the only person alive who knew she talked to herself when she was working through a hard problem, because he was the only person who'd ever been in the next bunk to hear it. When she'd been passed over for field promotion last spring—too young, too slight, *too much her own head*, Elder Brannic said—he had been angrier about it than she was.

And he was, first and last, a Warden. Sworn at fifteen, like her. Loyal, steady, rule-keeping Tam, who had reported his own favorite drill instructor for falsifying patrol logs because *the record matters, Isandro, the record is the only thing that survives us.*

"The truth," she said, because she was so tired, and because the stone's cold was still in her bones, and because lying to Tam Voss felt like lying to the person she'd been at eight years old. "But not here."

They walked up the path together to the shelf of granite below the old watch-cairn, where the wind broke and the whole valley spread out below them—a scatter of lamplight, the river a black ribbon, the guildhouse roof just visible with its weathervane turning slow. Isandro sat with her arms around her knees, and Tam lowered himself beside her, and for a while neither spoke.

"I found a book," she said finally. "In the vault. During inventory."

"You're supposed to report anomalies."

"I'm aware." She told him anyway. All of it, in a low flat voice, watching the valley instead of his face: the ledger that listed no such volume, the prophecy bound in gray leather that drank the lamplight, the final entry bearing tomorrow's date and her name spelled out in full—*Isandro Delacroix, daughter of none, Warden of the Third Rank*—and beneath it the charge: *she shall walk out, and she shall reforge what was broken, before the moon completes its thirteenth turn, or both realms wither together.*

She told him about the boundary stones and what the charter really said. She told him about the flash of the other sky. She did not tell him about Ondine and Sable Hollow, partly because she wasn't sure how much of what she'd read she trusted yet, and partly—she admitted this to herself with a wince—because that part still felt like hers, a coal she could warm her hands on when the rest of the night went cold.

When she finished, Tam was silent for a long time. He picked up a pebble and turned it over and set it down again, precisely where it had been, because he was Tam.

"The thirteenth turn," he said at last. "That's the lunar count in the almanac. Thirteen months, starting from the last new moon." He frowned at the valley. "That's less than a year."

"I know."

"And you're going to walk out there. Past the stones. Alone. Because a book told you to."

"A book that shouldn't exist told me to," she corrected. "There's a difference. I think." She hugged her knees tighter. "I don't know, Tam. That's the honest answer. I don't know what's true. I only know that the stone showed me a second moon, and the charter says *paused*, and nobody in the guild will even let me finish a sentence about it. I requested the founding correspondence yesterday. Official channels, proper forms. Guildmaster Veyra's clerk told me those records are sealed to my rank." She laughed, without much humor. "Third rank. The rank I'll apparently hold forever."

"She gave you archive duty instead."

Isandro turned to look at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because she asked me," Tam said quietly, "to keep an eye on you. And I said yes. And then I watched you climb the wall at midnight instead of sleeping, which is the opposite of keeping an eye on you, so I suppose I've already failed at my first assignment."

The words landed like a dropped lantern—all the light going out of the night at once. Isandro stared at him. The wind filled the silence with its dry whispering.

"So that's what this is," she said. "You followed me because she told you to."

"I followed you because I was already going to follow you," Tam said. His jaw was set in the stubborn way it got, the way it had gotten when he was eleven and insisted on carrying her across the flooded ford after she'd sprained her ankle, refusing all help, nearly drowning twice out of sheer principle. "Veyra asked me yesterday afternoon. Last night I couldn't sleep for thinking about what you'd asked me—about whether I'd ever noticed anything strange about the stones. So I went to the almanac myself, and I did the lunar count, and I got thirteen turns, same as you would have. And then today you started walking the perimeter alone looking like someone walking to their own funeral, and I—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "I don't care anymore whose orders put me on this path, Isandro. I care where the path goes. And I've been sitting here for an hour deciding, and I've decided."

He reached into his cloak and took out a small object and set it on the granite between them. In the moonlight she saw it clearly: a Warden's oath-token, the little lead seal every member carried, stamped with the broken circle. Except Tam's had been scored through, deliberately, deeply, with a knife—one clean line crossing the circle, breaking the broken thing further.

"You can't unsay the oath," he said. "I know that. Breaking the token is a flogging offense. Maybe worse, the way the elders are lately." He shrugged, an odd, jerky motion, like a man shrugging off a weight rather than the cold. "But I'm done being the guild's eyes. If you're walking out there—to whatever's out there, the second moon, the withering, all of it—then I'm coming with you. Not because anyone ordered it. Because you're my oldest friend and because somebody has to make sure you eat properly, because you forget, you've always forgotten—"

"Tam." Her voice cracked on his name.

"—and because," he went on, relentlessly, the way he did when he was afraid and covering it with momentum, "if the prophecy is real and the realm beyond the stones is dying, then the founders of the only family I've ever had built their house on top of a grave, and I would rather learn the truth from you at midnight than from the guild at sword-point. So." He finally looked at her directly, and in the starlight his eyes were very bright. "Ask me properly. Ask me to come, so it's yours to give and mine to accept, and no orders anywhere in it."

For a moment Isandro couldn't speak at all.

This was the thing she had not known she needed: not an ally exactly, not yet, but a witness. Someone who had seen the smoke rise off her hand and had not run to report it. Someone choosing her—not the prophecy's Isandro Delacroix, not the guild's youngest disappointment, just her, the girl who talked to herself over hard problems and forgot to eat. Loyalty given freely, out here in the wind where no one senior could see it and no record would survive it. The first thing in weeks that had not been written in a book before she did it.

"If I ask," she said carefully, "you understand what it costs. Once we cross, there's no coming back the way we left. Whatever's out there, whatever the guild does when they find us gone—"

"I've done the sums," Tam said. "I do them every night lately. The numbers don't come out in the guild's favor." He nudged the scored oath-token toward her with one finger. "Ask me."

Isandro picked up the token. It was lighter than she expected, warm from his pocket. She closed her fist around it, the way you close your hand around a hand.

"Tam Voss," she said, "come with me."

"Yes," he said, immediately, gratefully, as if she'd offered him supper instead of exile. Then, practical as ever, already unfolding a scrap of paper against his knee: "We go at moonrise tomorrow. The eastern stones are patrolled on odd nights; the northern stretch past the old quarry hasn't had a walker assigned since autumn. I can get us salt pork, rope, and the good flint from the equipment room before second bell. And Isandro—" He glanced back down at the valley, at the scatter of lamps, the ribbon of river, the roof with its turning weathervane. His voice went gentle. "Look at it properly. However long it takes. We won't see it like this again."

So she did. She looked until her eyes stung: the millpond and the leaning willow, the crooked lane behind the tannery, the chapel step that caught your toe, every lamp a family asleep inside its circle of light, all of it held in the cupped palm of the valley, all of it inside the locked door. She looked the way you look at a face across a sickbed. She committed it, unwilling to admit why, to memory.

Below them, far off along the rim, one of the boundary stones gave off a faint pulse of pale light—once, softly, like a lamp being trimmed—and went dark again.

Tam saw it too. Neither of them said a word. But they both understood, in the wordless way of old friends, that the valley had just watched them decide—and that tomorrow at moonrise, it would learn whether the stones were a wall, or a door.
