## 6. Crossing

Tam had always believed the boundary stones were warm.

It was a small belief, a child's belief, the kind you never bother to unlearn because nothing in your life ever asks it a question. The Warden children were taught that the stones held the valley's love—twelve of them, one for each month, set in a broken ring around Ashvale like the ribs of something sleeping. Warmth meant protection. Protection meant home. He had pressed his palm against the third stone as a boy, during some long-ago festival when touching them was permitted, and he could have sworn he'd felt a pulse there, faint and steady as a heartbeat under skin.

Tonight, standing in the bracken forty yards from the seventh stone with Isandro's breath quick and shallow beside him, Tam understood that what he had felt all those years ago was not warmth at all. It was heat the way a fever is heat. The stone was not holding the valley's love in. It was holding everything else out, and it had been straining against that work so long it had grown hot with the effort, like a hand clenched for a century.

"It's moonrise," Isandro whispered.

"I can see that."

"You're stalling."

He was. He knew he was. He catalogued the stalls anyway, the way he catalogued everything when his nerves got loud: the last of the bread in his satchel, gone stale but edible; the knife at his hip, guild-forged, valley steel, suddenly feeling less like a tool than an accusation; the shape of his mother's kitchen two miles behind him, the blue crockery, the way she hummed off-key on saints' days. All of it ordinary. All of it suddenly precious in the way that only things you might be about to lose become precious.

"Isandro." His voice came out lower than he intended. "If we cross, we don't just break a rule. Rules get you mopping floors. This is the covenant. This is the thing the whole guild *is*. If we step past those stones, every person we've ever known will call it treason, and they'll be right by their lights."

She didn't look at him. She was looking at the stone, at the pale seam of moonlight sliding down its face like water down glass. "By their lights," she said. "That's doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean." Now she turned, and in the half-dark her eyes had that quality he'd noticed in her since the night she'd shown him the prophecy book—a steadiness that frightened him more than fear would have. Fear he knew how to answer. Steadiness, in Isandro Delacroix, meant she had already decided, and the only question left open to him was whether he was coming. "The realm outside has been starving for a hundred years, Tam. Starving *because* of us. Because our founders couldn't bear the price of keeping the covenant, so they made someone else pay it. Every day we stand here is a day I'm complicit. Are you telling me you want to be complicit too?"

"No," he admitted. "I'm telling you I'm scared."

Something in her face gentled. "Good," she said. "Be scared. Scared means you understand what we're risking." She held out her hand. "Come on. Before I'm not scared anymore, which would be worse."

* * *

They went the last stretch low and fast, through the bracken, along the sheep-track that the guild maps marked as a drainage run because no honest map was supposed to show anything worth walking toward. Tam counted the wardens' patrol intervals in his head without meaning to—fourth bell, quarter-hour gap, lantern route swinging wide around the seventh stone because even the patrols kept a superstitious distance from it—and hated himself a little for how useful his training was tonight.

The stone stood taller than him, taller than both of them together, a column of gray granite furred with lichen on its northern face. Up close, the wrongness of it was unmistakable. Nothing grew near it. Not weeds, not moss at its foot, not even the particular stubborn grass that survived everywhere else in the valley. A ring of bare earth surrounded it, maybe three feet across, soil packed hard and dark as if something had been pressing down on the world there for a very long time.

Isandro stopped at the edge of that ring. "The elder records called this one the Weeping Stone," she said. "Did you know that? Before they standardized the names. The old wardens used to say it wept at midwinter."

"Wept what?"

"That's the part nobody wrote down."

She stepped into the bare circle, and Tam's whole body flinched forward before he could stop it—some deep animal conviction that the ground inside that ring belonged to something else. But nothing struck her down. No alarm rang across the valley. She simply stood there, close enough to touch the stone, and laid her palm flat against the granite.

The effect was immediate and terrible.

The stone went from fever-warm to cold—not cold like winter, cold like *depth*, like the cold of water too deep to see the bottom of—and Tam watched the cold climb Isandro's arm in visible waves, frost blooming in a lacework pattern up her sleeve. Her eyes went wide and fixed. Her lips moved around words he couldn't hear.

And then, above the stone, where the sky should have been sky—

*something else*.

It lasted perhaps three heartbeats. Tam would spend the rest of his life trying to describe it and never succeed. It was a sky, but not their sky; theirs was a bowl, familiar and closed, and this was a *window*, torn open for a moment, showing a darkness that was alive with movement. Stars beyond stars. A green shimmer like an aurora, except auroras were southern things, southern and rare, and this one hung directly overhead and *breathed*. And threaded through it, faint as veins under skin, ran lines of gold—writing, or roads, or rivers, moving, *alive*, in a heaven that was looking back down at them.

Then Isandro gasped and pulled her hand away, and the window closed, and there was only the ordinary valley sky again, and the ordinary silence, which had never felt so much like a lid.

"Tam," she said, and her voice shook. "There are skies like that. There have been skies like that the whole time, right over us, and they built the stones to make sure we'd never—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"The gap in the patrol," he said, because someone had to be practical, because if he wasn't practical he was going to sit down in the dead grass and put his head between his knees. "We've got maybe ten minutes. How do we actually do this?"

"The book says the seal opens at moonrise to any who carry the oath's intent." She recited it like a catechism, like she'd been repeating it to herself for days. "I don't entirely believe the book. But the stone showed me the way through. When it touched me, I felt—it's like a lock that wants to know if you mean it." She looked at him. "Do you mean it?"

The honest answer was *I don't know*. The true answer was that he'd followed her out here knowing exactly what it meant, and that whatever he felt about the guild, about Veyra, about the whole gray architecture of his life, he could not watch her walk through that window alone.

"Yeah," he said. "I mean it."

* * *

Crossing took four steps.

That was the obscene part, he thought later. Four steps. A hundred years of law, a thousand graves of doctrine, an entire civilization built on the premise that the world ended at the stones—and it came down to four steps through a ring of dead grass, taken at moonrise, while a girl who read too many forbidden books walked first and he followed because following her had somehow become the truest thing he did.

The moment his boot crossed the invisible line, magic hit him like blood returning to a limb that had been asleep his entire life.

He went down. That was the first thing—he simply stopped being able to stand. His knees found the foreign earth and his hands followed and he stayed there, doubled over, gasping, while sensation poured back into places he had never known were numb. It wasn't pain, though at first he thought it was. It was the agony of *too much*: color flooding into a world printed in grays, sound flooding into a silence he hadn't known was a silence, weight and warmth and texture pouring into skin that had been living wrapped in wool.

Because that was the truth the stones had hidden. The valley wasn't dim. The valley wasn't quiet. The valley was *anesthetized*, and he had spent twenty-two years calling the numbness peace.

"Tam—" Isandro's voice came from very far away, and also impossibly close, as if distance itself had gotten louder. "Breathe. Just breathe. It passes. Mostly."

"*Mostly?*" he managed, and then the colors arrived properly and he forgot the question entirely.

The moonlight was the worst of it. Or the best. In the valley, moonlight was silver, pale silver, the silver of pewter and fish scales, and everyone agreed on that, the way everyone agrees the sky is blue. But this moonlight—the same moon, the same light, falling now on land that received it honestly—was not silver at all. It was layered. There was blue in it, a blue like deep well-water, and beneath the blue a green-gold thread like light through honey, and around the edges a violet so faint he might have imagined it except that he could feel it, feel it resting cool against his forearms like the touch of a moth. He looked at his own hands in the moonlight and began, helplessly, stupidly, to cry.

Not sobbing. Worse than sobbing. The silent, full-body weeping of a man whose body has understood something his mind cannot yet hold—that he had been poor his whole life and never known it, that he had been standing in a room with the windows painted shut and calling the painted glass a view. He wept at colors he had no names for, and understood dimly that there were no names because the valley had never needed names for things it refused to see.

Isandro crouched beside him. She was crying too, he realized—had probably been crying since her own crossing—but hers had already burned down to something steadier. She put her hand between his shoulder blades, and even through his shirt her touch sang against his spine, every nerve awake and grateful.

"I know," she said. "I know. Take your time."

"What—" His voice cracked. He tried again. "What *was* that? What did they take from us?"

"Nothing was taken." Her voice had a strange flatness, the flatness of someone holding grief very carefully so it doesn't spill. "That's the thing, Tam. Nothing was taken from us. They just never gave it. The founders sealed the flow, and the flow was—this. All of this. And every warden who ever lived grew up thinking the world was just... small." She looked up at the layered moonlight, at the stars that were somehow *thicker* here, denser, as if the sky itself had more substance. "We're the first ones in a hundred years to see it's not."

* * *

They walked for an hour before either of them spoke again.

The land on the far side of the stones was, at first, disorientingly familiar—rolling hills, scattered woodland, a stream running somewhere below with a sound like murmured conversation. Tam kept waiting for the difference to announce itself. Then he started noticing what was missing.

No birds. He stopped twice to listen, certain he'd simply failed to notice them, and both times the silence closed back over the landscape like water over a dropped coin. No insects singing in the grass. The trees stood full-leaved and yet somehow *thin*, their shadows too faint, as if the moonlight passed through them rather than pooling beneath them. And the flowers—wildflowers crowded the hillsides in profusion, white and yellow shapes nodding in the night wind, but their colors came to him muted, secondhand, as if he were remembering color rather than seeing it.

"It's dying," Isandro said quietly. She had stopped at the crest of a hill and stood with her arms crossed tight against the chill. "The prophecy said the outer realm withers without the covenant. I thought that meant—wars, or blight, something dramatic." She gestured at the flowerless silence of the hillside. "This is worse. This is a land slowly forgetting how to be alive."

Ahead of them, down in the valley of the next hill over, a light appeared. Then another. Then a scatter of them, orange and flickering, arranged in no pattern any garrison or village Tam had ever studied would use. Fires, but fires of people with nowhere defensible to put them.

"Refugees," Isandro said. "Ondine's letters mentioned camps along the river road. People who've come from further in, where the withering's worse."

Tam's hand drifted to the knife at his belt, and he caught himself doing it and made himself stop. Valley steel. Guild steel. He thought of the way the refugees would see that hilt catching the firelight and know exactly what it meant, and the guilt arrived then, fully formed, the shadow that had been pacing behind him all night finally closing the distance.

Wonder and guilt, walking side by side. He was beginning to suspect they would be traveling companions for a long time.

"Isandro." He stopped walking. She turned. Behind her the refugee fires glittered like fallen constellations, and above them the strange, rich, wounded sky wheeled with its unnamed colors, and Tam understood that whatever he said next would be the truest thing he'd ever say, so he made himself say it plainly. "Back there—the valley—I keep thinking about my mother's kitchen. The blue crockery. The way she hums on saints' days. And I keep thinking that I'll never—" His throat closed. He swallowed past it. "I chose this. I need you to know I chose it. But I want it written down somewhere that it cost."

"It's written down," she said softly. "In me. In you. That's what a witness is, Tam. Someone who carries the cost so the story stays true." She held out her hand again, the same gesture from the bracken, hours and a lifetime ago. "Come on. Those people down there are going to see valley steel eventually. I'd rather they saw ours attached to someone friendly."

He took her hand, and they started down the hill together toward the fires, two small figures under a vast and grieving sky.

Neither of them saw, on the ridge they'd left behind, a third figure rise from the bracken where it had lain still for an hour—watching, counting their steps, memorizing their bearing toward the river road.

Neither of them saw it turn back, unhurried, toward the boundary stones and the valley beyond.

But the watcher moved like a warden. And it moved like someone who knew exactly whom it was going to report to.
