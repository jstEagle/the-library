## 7. The Withered Realm

The first thing Isandro noticed was the silence.

Not the silence of the valley at night—she knew that one the way she knew her own heartbeat, a hush threaded with crickets and the creak of the guildhouse settling and Tam's breathing in the next room. This was different. This was a silence with something missing from it. She stood on the far side of the boundary stones with dew still on her boots and listened for the sound of birdsong that should have come from the orchards below, and there was nothing. No wrens. No thrushes. Not even the insects she had always assumed were simply part of what summer was.

"It's quiet," Tam said behind her, and his voice cracked on the word.

They had crossed at moonrise, slipping between two of the great standing stones where the wardens' patrol path bent away from the perimeter, and for a few wild minutes everything had been wonder—the air itself tasting different, sweeter, like rain that hadn't fallen yet; the grass beyond the border growing taller in a single stride; colors arriving in Tam's vision that he had no names for, so that he had wept, actually wept, standing in a field of flowers whose blue made him stumble. Isandro had felt it too: magic flooding back into them like blood into a numb limb, pins and needles of the soul, a tingling heat along every scar she'd ever earned in training. She had laughed. She could not remember laughing like that since she was small enough to believe the world was kind.

Then they had walked down out of the hills toward the first settlement marked on Ondine's map—a village called Brackenlow, half a day's walk—and the wonder had begun to curdle.

* * *

The orchards came first.

Isandro had grown up among orchards. The valley's eastern slopes were stitched with apple and pear trees older than the guild itself, and every autumn the whole valley smelled of cider and cut wood, and children stole fruit from the low branches and were scolded and did it anyway. So she knew what an orchard should look like. She knew the deep green of healthy leaves, the silver-green undersides that flashed when wind moved through, the way a laden branch bowed and trembled.

These trees were gray.

Not dead—dead would have been honest, blackened trunks, snapped limbs, the clean violence of a hard frost or a fire. These trees were *dying*, which was worse, because dying took its time. The leaves hung on, but they had gone the color of old ash, of paper left too long in a window. The bark was peeling in long pale strips, like skin after a burn. And here and there, impossibly, a single branch still carried fruit—small, misshapen apples with a bloom of white mold at the stem, clinging to their branches the way a drowning man clings to driftwood.

Tam reached up and touched one. It came away at the slightest pressure, soft as overripe plums, and when it split in his palm the inside was dry. Not rotten. Dry. A fruit that had never finished ripening because something it needed had simply stopped arriving.

"They're starving," Isandro said, and heard her own voice go strange. "The trees are starving."

She crouched and pressed her fingers into the soil. In the valley, soil was a living thing—you could smell it, dark and wet and busy, full of worms and roots and the sweet rot of last year's leaves. This soil was powder. Pale, fine, lifeless powder that ran through her fingers like sand from an hourglass, and left no smell at all. She dug deeper, looking for moisture, and found none. Three inches down, six inches, a forearm's length—the earth was dust all the way.

"Isandro." Tam had gone ahead to the crest of the hill, and his voice came back flat and careful, the way he spoke when he didn't want to frighten her. "You need to see this."

Beyond the hill, the land opened into a wide shallow vale, and in the vale lay Brackenlow—or what had been Brackenlow. From a distance it looked almost whole: rooftops, chimneys, a church spire with a slate roof catching the morning light. But the light itself was wrong. Everything under it seemed drained, washed-out, a watercolor someone had left in the sun. Half the fields around the village stood fallow, and not deliberately fallow—there were no hedgerows, no rotation, no marks of intention. They were fields that had simply been given up on. The other half held crops: wheat, by the shape of it, but the stalks were knee-high and bone-pale, heads empty, bending in a wind that moved them without any sound of rustling.

And the shrines. There were little shrines everywhere—at the crossroads, at the field edges, on a stone plinth beside the well. Valley folk kept roadside markers too, small carved waystones for travelers, but these were different. These were *tended*. Fresh-cut flowers, gray and papery, lay in niches carved with symbols Isandro didn't recognize: interlocking circles, a tree whose branches became a river, a spiral that hurt her eyes slightly if she looked at it too long, the way bright snow does. Every shrine bore the same spiral somewhere. And every shrine was silent—not abandoned, not ruined, but *silent*, the way a mouth is silent when the person behind it has stopped believing anyone is listening.

At the largest shrine, near the village well, someone had left a child's shoe. Just one. Placed neatly, toe pointing outward, toward the road that led away.

* * *

The people of Brackenlow saw them coming from a long way off. There was no hiding two strangers walking down a bare hillside, and Isandro hadn't tried—she'd told herself they had nothing to hide, that they came with questions, not swords. But she had forgotten, in the vault-lit months of her research, the most basic fact of her own body: she wore valley steel. Her short sword was Ashvale-forged, guild-stamped, the pommel bearing the wardens' mark of the mountain-and-thorn. Tam wore the same. To anyone who knew what that mark meant, they might as well have been wearing executioners' hoods.

An old woman watched them from a doorway and did not run, exactly. She simply stopped moving. Stood very still, hands folded, eyes fixed on the hilts of their weapons, and waited to see what they would do. A boy of perhaps ten, driving three bony geese along a fence line, saw them and turned and *fled*—not panicked, just instantly, quietly gone, geese and all, as though he had practiced it. As though valley steel appearing on the road was a thing that happened often enough that everyone knew the drill.

That was the detail that lodged in Isandro like a splinter. Not hatred. Hatred she could have understood, could almost have respected. This was flinching. This was the reflex of animals that have learned what certain shapes mean. The refugees on the road out of the village—there were refugees, families walking east with bundles and hand-carts, though Isandro didn't yet know from what—they gave the two of them a wide berth, crossing to the far ditch, pulling children close, and one man, gray-bearded and hollow-cheeked, actually spat in the dust as they passed, then looked immediately ashamed of having spent the strength.

"Stay close," Isandro murmured to Tam. "Don't draw. Don't even touch the hilts."

"I wasn't going to." His jaw was tight. "I want to take the damn things off."

"So do I." And she found that she meant it with a fierceness that surprised her. The sword had been her eighteenth-birthday gift from Veyra herself, fitted to her hand, blessed in the guild chapel. She had slept with it in reach every night of her adult life. It had never once occurred to her that from the other side of the stones, it was not protection. It was a confession.

* * *

They found bread and information in the same place, as travelers usually do: a roadhouse at the edge of the village, a long low building with a sign depicting the spiral symbol, where a handful of locals sat drinking something bitter and brown that Tam said tasted like steeped bark and grief. The keeper was a broad woman named Maren with forearms like a smith's and a face that had decided, sometime in the distant past, to stop expecting anything. She served them without greeting, took their coin without counting it, and only when Isandro asked directly—*what has happened here?*—did she look up from her counter and speak.

"Happened?" Maren said. "Nothing's happened, girl. That's the trouble. Nothing's happened for sixty years."

She leaned on the counter and told it plainly, the way people tell things they've told so many times the telling has worn smooth. Sixty years ago—her grandmother's time—the land had begun to fade. Not a blight, not a war, not a plague. A fading. The wells dropped. The bees went first, then the songbirds, then the fish in the river Sable, which now ran clear and empty over gray stones. Crops grew smaller each season, thinner, paler, until the harvest fed half of what it had and the other half was hunger. The healers' craft stopped working—Maren used a word for it that Isandro wrote down phonetically in her journal, *the flow*, and understood later, with a cold turning in her stomach, that the woman was describing exactly what the covenant had carried. Magic, the life of the outer realm, the breath in the shrines, the green in the leaves—all of it had slowed to a trickle and then less than a trickle, and no one knew why, and the priests of the spiral said the covenant had been broken, and no one believed them, because who breaks a covenant?

"And your side of the mountains," Maren said, fixing Isandro with a look that was not quite hostile and not quite anything else, "grew greener every year. We could see it. From the high passes, before the passes closed—we could see your valley shining like a lamp in the dark. Fruit the size of fists. Grass you could lie in. Children fat in autumn." She paused. "We thought it was a miracle. For a while, we thought your valley had been *blessed*."

Isandro's throat closed.

Because she knew. She knew now, in a way that no amount of vault research had prepared her to know, sitting in a roadhouse smelling of woodsmoke and despair. The blessing had been real. The valley *had* been blessed. Blessed by theft. The founders of the Ashvale Wardens had not sealed magic out—they had sealed it *in*, dammed the flow at the boundary stones, turned the entire outer realm into a catchment basin, and drunk the runoff. Every orchard she had ever stolen fruit from. Every healthy child. Every safe, tedious, unremarkable evening of her small, safe, unremarkable life—it had all been purchased, installment by installment, season by season, from accounts like Brackenlow's. Sixty years of withdrawals. An entire realm paying interest on a debt it never agreed to.

"You're wardens," Maren said. It wasn't a question; she'd read the pommels. "From the valley."

"Yes," Isandro said, because lying to this woman's face was suddenly the one thing she could not make her body do.

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then she set down two more cups, poured the bitter brown drink into them, and said, "Sixty years my family has watched that valley shine. Sixty years we've buried our dead thin. And in all that time, not one of you ever came over the stones to ask why our side was dying." She handed them the cups. "So either you're the first, or you're the same as all the rest. You'll tell me which before you leave."

* * *

They stayed the night in Brackenlow, because Maren let them, and because leaving felt impossible now that they had arrived. Isandro spent the evening doing what she had apparently been made to do: listening. She sat by the roadhouse fire and the villagers came, one by one, cautiously, drawn by some mixture of curiosity and the ancient human instinct to tell one's story to whoever will hold it. A farmer described the year the river died and the mill closed and his father walked into the hills and did not come back. A young priestess of the spiral showed Isandro the shrine records—generations of careful entries, offerings logged, prayers dated—and pointed to the exact page, sixty years past, where the entries changed tone. Where hope thinned into pleading, and pleading thinned into bookkeeping. "We keep the shrines anyway," the priestess said, with a small defiant lift of her chin. "A covenant broken can be mended. That's what the spiral means. It comes back around. We keep the shrines so that whatever returns finds the door still open."

Isandro thought of the vault beneath the guildhall, locked and orderly and dark, and had to excuse herself and stand outside in the cold for a while, breathing.

It was Tam who found her there, leaning against the roadhouse wall with her arms wrapped around herself, watching the gray orchard move in the soundless wind.

"I keep thinking about the thirteenth moon," she said, when she sensed him beside her. "The prophecy says both realms wither if the covenant isn't reforged. I thought that meant the valley starts to suffer too. Some balance thing. Symmetry." She shook her head. "But it's not symmetry, Tam. The valley isn't beginning to wither. The valley is *full*. We're a cup that's been filled to the brim for sixty years while—" she gestured at the orchard, the pale fields, the whole starved country rolling away eastward under the rising moon— "while this drank the dregs. If the deadline matters, if both realms fail together, it's because the cup finally runs dry. Because there's nothing left out here to take."

Tam was quiet for a moment. In the moonlight his face looked younger and older at once. "Veyra knows," he said. "Doesn't she. Veyra knows all of this, and she sent me to bring you home anyway."

"I don't know what Veyra knows."

"Yes, you do." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "You've known since the boundary stone burned your hand. You just haven't let yourself finish the sentence."

Isandro looked at the sky. It was enormous here—she'd noticed it the moment they crossed, the stars thicker, closer, arranged in constellations the valley's charts had never recorded, as though even the heavens had been rationed on the far side of the stones. Overhead hung a moon one night shy of full, silver and pitiless, already climbing toward the turn that would begin its thirteenth count.

"The sentence," she said slowly, "is that my family—the Wardens, the guildhall, Veyra, the chapel bell I fell asleep to as a child—built their safety on a slow massacre and called it duty." The words came out of her flat and terrible, and once spoken, they could not be unsaid. "And I wore their steel into the country they starved, and the children here ran from me before I'd said a single word."

Tam put his hand on her shoulder. He didn't say *it's not your fault*, because he was Tam, and he knew better than to offer comfort that was also a lie. What he said was: "Then we'd better find out how to fix it. Before the moon turns."

Behind them, through the roadhouse window, lamplight burned warm in the one lit room for miles. Ahead of them, the road went east into the dark, toward a place called Sable Hollow, where a blind archivist kept records no one in the valley had ever wanted found—and where, if Ondine's letter had told true, someone remembered the name Delacroix.

Isandro picked up her pack. She unbuckled her sword belt, coiled the leather in her arms like something dead, and stowed it at the bottom, beneath the spare shirt and the journal and the pressed sprig of valley thorn she had carried over the stones without knowing why.

The road was waiting.
