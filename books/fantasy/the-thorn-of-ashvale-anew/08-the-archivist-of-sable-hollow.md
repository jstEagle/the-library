## 8. The Archivist of Sable Hollow

Sable Hollow earned its name honestly. The road descended for the better part of a morning into a fold of hills so steep and close that the sun touched the bottom only in the hour around noon, and even then it came down thin and gray, like light that had been poured through cloth. The trees here were older than any Isandro had seen in the valley—black-barked, hung with lichen the color of old pewter—and the air smelled of wet stone and something faintly sweet beneath it, like fruit going soft somewhere out of sight.

Tam walked half a pace behind her, the way he always did now, as though the road itself might turn on her. His wound from the ambush at the shrine was three days closed but still pulled at him when the ground tilted; she could hear it in his breathing on the climbs. He didn't complain. He never complained. That was its own kind of worry.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you walk faster the closer we get to something you're afraid of. Like you can outrun your own feet."

"I'm not afraid."

"You've checked the journal four times since the ridge." He nodded at her pack. "You check it when you're afraid. You hold it like Veyra holds her seal ring."

Isandro slowed despite herself. The journal was nothing—a cheap bound book of blank pages she'd bought at the roadhouse—but Ondine's letter lived folded inside it, and the letter was the first thing in the outer realm that had felt less like a mercy and more like an answer. *Come to Sable Hollow,* it had said. *I keep what the valley buried. Ask me about Delacroix.*

She had not told Tam why that name mattered. She wasn't sure she could yet. Saying it aloud would make it a fact, and facts could not be taken back into the dark where they'd been living.

The Hollow opened without warning. One moment the road was walled in trees; the next it stood before a low stone house built into the hillside itself, its roof green with moss, its single door painted a blue so faded it was nearly the color of dusk. A garden grew wild along the front—not neglected, she realized on second look, but deliberately untended-looking: herbs gone to seed on purpose, flowers allowed to sprawl, everything left to do exactly as it pleased. A blind woman's garden, she thought, and then wondered how she knew that, and realized she didn't. It just looked like a place where seeing had stopped mattering long ago.

A bell rope hung beside the door. Before Isandro could reach for it, the door opened.

"You walk loudly for someone trying to be quiet," said the woman in the doorway. "Both of you. Come in before the damp gets into your bones. It gets into mine by midday and I spend the afternoon regretting hospitality."

Ondine was small and straight-backed, silver-haired, wrapped in a shawl the color of moss. Her eyes were open—milk-pale, fixed slightly past Isandro's shoulder—and they did not move when Isandro moved. She stood aside and let them pass into a room that smelled of paper, ink, lamp oil, and underneath it all, the same faint sweetness as the garden.

* * *

The interior of the house was the archive. There was no other way to put it. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling on every wall, and where the walls ended, free-standing cases filled the middle of the room in ranks tight enough that two people could barely pass abreast between them. Books, folios, scrolls in oilcloth, bundles of letters tied with string, tablets of clay and wax, loose leaves weighted under river stones. Some of it was organized with obsessive precision; some of it looked like a landslide that had been politely asked to stay where it fell.

"Mind the third case on your left," Ondine said, closing the door behind them. "It leans. Everything in this house leans except me."

"You knew we were coming," Isandro said.

"I knew someone was coming. Two someones, one of them favoring his side, both of them wearing their boots too carefully, which means valley-born. Valley people step soft even when they think they don't—it's trained into them, all that sneaking past boundary stones." Ondine found her way to a chair by the cold hearth and sat with the economy of someone who wasted no motion she couldn't afford to lose. "And then there's the name. You've been carrying it like a stone in your shoe since you crossed the border. I could hear it in your silence."

Isandro's mouth went dry. "How could you possibly—"

"Because I wrote to you." The blind eyes turned toward her with unsettling accuracy. "I wrote to a Warden who crossed over asking questions about the covenant, and Wardens who cross over asking about the covenant are always, always asking about one other thing besides, whether they know it or not. Sit down, child. Both of you. This is going to take longer than you want and shorter than it should."

They sat. Tam took the bench near the door, within reach of it, and Isandro noted that Ondine noted it too, and filed it away, and said nothing.

"Tell me your name," Ondine said. "Your whole name."

"Isandro."

"The whole name."

A breath. "Isandro Delacroix."

The word *Delacroix* seemed to fall through the room like a coin through deep water. Ondine's hands, which had been folded in her lap, went still entirely. For a long moment the only sound was the tick of the house settling and, far off, a bird calling in the gray orchards beyond the Hollow.

"Well," Ondine said softly. "Well. So it wasn't only a guess."

"You know the name." It wasn't a question. Isandro heard her own voice come out flat and careful, the voice she used in the vault when handling something fragile. "From the records. You said so in the letter."

"I said someone remembered it. Remembering is different from records, child. Records are what people agreed to write down. Memory is what happened anyway." Ondine rose, moving through the shelves without touching them, hands hovering at hip height like a swimmer's, and drew a slim bundle from a drawer Isandro would have sworn held nothing but dust. She brought it back and laid it in Isandro's lap. "Untie it. My fingers are not what they were, and I want to watch your face while you read. Old habit. The one indulgence of a blind archivist is reading faces instead of pages."

The string was waxed and stiff with age. Inside were letters—dozens of them, yellowed, brittle at the folds, written in a hand Isandro recognized before she understood why she recognized it. The high looping capitals. The little flourish that turned every final *e* into a small unfurling leaf.

Her grandmother's handwriting. She had spent her childhood copying it, practicing it, being scolded for her sloppy descenders. Maera Delacroix had insisted on beautiful script the way she'd insisted on everything: absolutely.

* * *

The earliest letters were dated sixty years back. They began as reports—*the crossing was made without incident; the stones held; the outer roads are worse than the maps say*—written in the clipped cadence of guild dispatches Isandro had grown up reading in the archive without ever once wondering who had written the oldest ones, or where they'd gone. But the reports changed, letter by letter. The formality thinned. Small things crept in: a description of a shrine festival with lanterns set on the water. An apology for ink stains. A line—*tell Isandro's mother the bread here is better than she'd believe and the company better than the bread.*

Isandro's mother had been born after the crossings stopped. After the letters stopped.

"She came over seven times," Ondine said quietly, while Isandro read. "Maera Delacroix. Senior archivist of the Ashvale Wardens—the last one trusted to make the crossing at all, in those years. The covenant was failing even then. Not broken—failing. The flow between the realms had begun to thin, and the founders' descendants had discovered something inconvenient: that the covenant required tending. Renewal. A price paid by someone willing. And the guild had decided, somewhere in the generations, that it was owed its safety for free."

Isandro's hands had gone cold around the letters. "What was the price?"

"That lesson comes later, and from someone else. I only keep the paper." Ondine's voice was gentle and immovable at once. "Read the last one."

The last letter was short. The handwriting was hurried—the leaf-flourished *e*s cramped, the loops cut off. Isandro read it twice because the first time her eyes refused to cooperate.

*They have voted. The stones will be sealed from the inside and the memory of the price struck from the archive entire. I will not be party to it. If I go back, they will erase me too—I have seen the first lists already, and my name is on them, and worse, they mean to strike the whole line. I am staying. Someone from Ashvale must remain who remembers what was done, even if I can do nothing else but remember. Tell them—if anyone ever comes—tell them the Delacroix line did not consent.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be.

"They erased her," Isandro said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Not just her. The whole line. Every record. I grew up in that archive. I shelved the histories. I inventoried the vault. There was nothing—there was *nothing*, not a mention, not a marginal note, not—" Her throat closed. She thought of herself at eleven years old, alphabetizing founder biographies, proud of her neatness. Proud of a shelf that had been made empty on purpose, and never once asking why the space had been left.

"They erased her thoroughly," Ondine agreed. "That was the horror of it, if you want my professional opinion. Not the striking of names—names get struck in every age—but the thoroughness. They removed her correspondence, her field reports, the census entries, her marriage record, her grave. Her daughter—you—no." Ondine paused, recalibrating. "Her daughter was raised inside the valley, told her mother died young of fever. The fever story was standard issue. They used it for everyone they erased."

"My mother never knew."

"Your mother never knew. And her daughter was raised to serve the guild that unmade her family, and given the family name anyway—because a name alone, stripped of every record around it, is just a sound. Harmless. Almost a joke." Ondine's blind gaze held steady on her. "Someone in that guild kept the sound alive, child. Someone wanted it findable. Think on who that might have been, someday, when you can bear to."

* * *

Tam spoke for the first time in an hour. "Why keep these? Why risk it?"

"Because I am an archivist," Ondine said, "and because sixty years ago a woman with beautiful handwriting sat in that chair you're sitting in, Warden, and asked me the question you are currently swallowing. She asked: *if they can erase a person entire, does the person stop having existed?* And I told her what I will tell you: yes, in the record. No, in the debt. Erasure doesn't cancel what was done. It compounds it, with interest, accruing across every year nobody tells the truth. These letters are interest payments." She tapped the bundle in Isandro's lap with one crooked finger. "Sixty years of them, waiting for the account holder."

Isandro looked down at the last letter again. *The Delacroix line did not consent.* Sixty years her family had been a hole shaped exactly like themselves, and she had walked through the guild halls her whole life without falling into it because she'd never been shown where it was.

"There were others," she said. "Erased. Weren't there."

"Dozens. Everyone who argued against the sealing. Everyone who crossed after and chose to stay. Everyone who simply asked too well." Ondine shrugged, a small, terrible gesture. "Ashvale is very good at peace, Warden. You have to understand that about your home. It is peaceful the way a house is quiet after the screaming stops and everyone agrees never to speak of it."

The fire had gone out in the hearth at some point. Isandro hadn't noticed. She became aware, distantly, that Tam was watching her with an expression she couldn't bear to catalog right now—something between pity and awe and grief—and that her own face was wet, and that she had no memory of starting to cry.

She gathered the letters slowly, squared their edges, retied the string with the care she'd learned in the vault, handling fragile things that deserved better than the world gave them.

"Keep them," Ondine said.

"No." Isandro shook her head. "No. When the truth is told in Ashvale—and it will be told, I swear it, publicly, whatever it costs—these letters go with it. Her words, in her hand. She waited sixty years to be read." She met the blind eyes. "I'll carry them. That's what I'm for now, I think. Carrying things other people were made to put down."

Ondine smiled then, and for a moment the milk-pale eyes seemed to see a great deal more than most sighted ones did.

"Then you'll want to know where to carry them next," she said. "Because remembering is not enough, child—it never has been. The covenant your grandmother refused to let die can still be reforged. Three shards of the original oath-stone, scattered among three heirs who have every reason to hate each other and the valley both. The first keeper keeps a ferry on the River Liss, two days east. He is stubborn, honest, and angry, and he will demand things of you that will cost more than comfort." She leaned forward. "And Warden—when he asks you to swear something aloud, understand that he will hold you to the sound of your own voice. Out here, a promise is not a courtesy. It is a blade you hand someone hilt-first."

Isandro rose. The letters went into the journal, against her heart, next to the pressed sprig of valley thorn. At the door she paused, one hand on the faded blue wood, and turned back.

"Did she ever say anything else? My grandmother. In all those years here. Anything about us—about the ones she left behind?"

Ondine was quiet for a moment.

"She said the worst part wasn't being forgotten by them," she said. "It was knowing that someday, someone from her line would walk back into that archive and feel the hole where she used to be, and not know its shape." Her head tilted. "Do you know its shape now, child?"

"Yes," Isandro said.

"Then go east," said the Archivist of Sable Hollow. "And give the ferryman my regards, though he won't thank you for them. He never does."

The door closed behind them on the smell of paper and patience, and the road went down through the gray orchards, east, toward the river—and toward the first promise Isandro would have to make out loud, with no idea yet what it would cost her to keep it.
