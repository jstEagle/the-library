## 9. The First Shard

Tam had never seen a river before.

He knew that sounded foolish the moment he thought it—there was water in Ashvale, of course there was, clear and cold and obedient, channeled through mill-races and cisterns and the long stone sluices that fed the terraced farms. But water in the valley did a job. It went where it was told. This thing that unrolled below them from the ridge where they'd stopped to rest was not water doing a job. It was water *being* something—a broad brown-gold animal, muscular and unhurried, shouldering its way through a country of reeds and willows as if it had been walking this path since before anyone thought to build anything at all.

"Say something," Isandro said beside him. She was squinting against the late-morning glare off the current. "You've been quiet since the shrine road."

"I'm deciding whether to be embarrassed."

"About what?"

"That I nearly cried at a river."

She smiled—the small crooked one she never used in front of Veyra—and Tam felt the familiar ache of it, the one he'd been carrying now for weeks like a stone in his boot. *You followed her under orders.* The confession sat in him still, three days after he'd finally given it up on the road, and it hadn't gotten lighter with telling. If anything it had grown heavier, because she hadn't thrown it back at him. She'd just nodded, once, and said *I know*, and walked on, and somehow that forgiveness was worse than anger would have been. Anger could be earned down. This had to be carried.

"Come on," she said. "The ferryman's downstream. Ondine said we'd know him by his bell."

* * *

They knew him by his bell.

The sound reached them before the landing did—a cracked bronze note, patient, ringing out over the reeds every few hundred paces like the heartbeat of the river itself. When they came down through the willows to the water's edge, Tam saw the source: a flat-bottomed barge moored at a leaning jetty, hung fore and aft with bells of different sizes, each green with age, and at the stern a figure in a gray oilskin coat who was methodically polishing the largest of them with a rag gone black from years of the same work.

The ferryman did not look up when their boots hit the boards of the jetty. He waited until the third bell had rung itself out, then set down his rag with great ceremony, folded it, and turned.

Tam had expected old. Ondine's directions had made the heir sound like a relic, the last keeper of a broken thing, and relics were supposed to be dusty. But the man on the barge couldn't have been more than fifty, broad through the shoulders, with a face like driftwood—weathered silver-gray, grain running every direction, one deep crease between the brows where a lifetime of squinting into weather had carved its channel. His eyes were the pale gray-green of the river in fog, and when they settled on Isandro, they did not move away.

"You're wearing valley steel," he said.

His voice matched everything else about him: low, roughened, unhurried.

"We are," Isandro said.

"Ferry's for travelers." He picked up his rag again, deliberately. "Not for wardens. I don't carry wardens. I don't carry their friends either, if it comes to that. You'll want to walk upstream to the ford at Marrow Bridge. Three days' walk. Good luck to you."

"We're not here for a crossing." Isandro stepped forward onto the barge, and Tam watched her do the thing she did—chin level, hands loose at her sides, the stance of someone who had learned young that being small meant you had to be *certain*. "We're here because of what you keep around your neck."

The ferryman's hand rose—slowly, almost lazily—and closed around something beneath his oilskin collar. For a moment he only looked at her, and the river filled the silence, slapping the hull, worrying the jetty pilings.

Then he laughed. It was not a kind laugh, but it wasn't cruel either; it was the laugh of a man hearing a joke he'd been waiting forty years to hear.

"Ondine sent you," he said. "That blind crow. Of course she did." He drew the chain over his head. On it hung a shard of gray-green stone no bigger than a knife-blade, shot through with a vein of gold so fine it might have been a hair dipped in sunlight. Even in the flat morning light, even dull with river-grime, it seemed to pull at the air around it. Tam felt it in his teeth. "You know what this is?"

"The first shard of the oath-stone," Isandro said. "Broken when the covenant was broken. Your family kept it."

"My family *was kept by it*," the ferryman corrected. He turned the shard over in his palm, almost tenderly. "There's a difference, girl, and the difference is the whole story. My grandmother's grandmother held this when your founders came across the stones with their axes and their laws and their clean consciences. She was told it was an honor to be its keeper. She was told her children would be free men of two worlds." He spat over the side, into the brown water. "What she got was a river to tend and a secret to drown in. Every Delacroix heir since has been born chained to a rock nobody remembers exists."

The name landed in Tam's chest like a dropped anchor.

*Delacroix.*

He looked at Isandro. She had gone very still—the particular stillness he'd learned to read across a hundred night watches, the stillness of a person holding something heavy and deciding whether to set it down or throw it.

"You know my name," she said quietly.

"I know *all* the names worth knowing." The ferryman looped the chain back around his neck and tucked the shard away. "Delacroix. The youngest Warden. The least regarded, if the traders who slip past your boundary stones are to be believed—which they usually are, because traders lie about prices and nothing else." He studied her again, longer this time. "So. The prophecy found you. Or you found it. Doesn't matter which; it matters that you came. Sit down, both of you, before you fall down. You look like you've been eating hardtack and regret."

They sat on the barge's bench while he put a kettle over a little clay stove in the stern cabin. The tea he brought them was bitter and smoky and the best thing Tam had ever tasted, though that might have been the weeks of trail rations talking.

"I'm Corren," the ferryman said, settling opposite them with his own cup. "Last of the Delacroix line on this side of the stones. And before you ask—no. I haven't decided yet."

"Decided what?" Tam asked.

"Whether I'm giving this up." Corren tapped his chest, where the shard hung hidden. "Forty-one years I've worn it. My father wore it thirty years before me, and his mother sixty before him. It's the only inheritance my family has left—everything else, the land, the name's honor, the *reason* for any of it, your guild took or burned or buried. So understand me, Warden. I'm not a generous man. Generosity is a luxury of people who were left something."

* * *

Corren talked until the light went long and gold across the water, and Tam listened the way he'd learned to listen on this journey—with half his mind taking notes and the other half quietly grieving.

The ferryman's history came out in pieces, fitted together like planks of a hull. How the oath-stone had once stood at the heart of the boundary, whole, humming, binding both realms into a single bargain: magic would flow outward into the valley in measured grace, and the valley would flow inward in return—grain, craft, song, memory, *witness*. Not conquest. Exchange. A marriage, Corren called it, not a theft.

"And then your founders decided the bride price was too high," he said. "They wanted the magic without the obligation. So they took their axes to the stone, split it three ways, scattered it among three families they figured they could trust or control—and sealed the boundary behind them. Paused the covenant, they called it. Like a debt you stop paying doesn't stop existing."

"It withers," Isandro said. Her voice was flat, careful. "Everything beyond the stones is withering. We've seen it. The orchards. The shrines."

"Aye." Corren looked out at the river, and for a moment his driftwood face showed something underneath—something old and tired and furious in the way that only grief can be furious. "My wife's people were orchard folk. Apple country, east of here. When I met her, the trees still bore, sparse and small, but they bore. Twenty years later the whole valley floor was gray wood. She tended the last living tree for four seasons like a sick child. Then it went too." He drank. "She didn't die of it. But she stopped singing, if you take my meaning, and she never started again."

Tam took a slow breath. Beside him, Isandro had bowed her head, and he saw her hands—those steady, capable hands, the hands that had pulled him bleeding out of the shrine ambush—pressed flat against her knees as if pinning something down.

"Why you?" she asked finally. "Why does the first shard come to a ferryman?"

"Because somebody had to stay close to the water." Corren shrugged. "The stone was born of the river-rite, oldest of all the rites. Water remembers what people forget. My family ferried pilgrims between realms, back when there were pilgrims, back when there were realms plural instead of one realm dying and one realm pretending." He set down his cup. "And now you're going to ask me for it."

"I am."

"And I'm going to say yes."

Isandro's head came up. Tam felt his own breath catch—after the wall of grief and grievance, after forty-one years of a chained inheritance, he'd braced for refusal, for bargaining, for another memorial to stand through, another price to learn about only after it was paid.

"But," Corren said, and raised one finger, "not for nothing. And not for a promise whispered between three people on a boat. I've heard how promises travel, Warden. They cross borders thin. They arrive changed."

"Name your terms."

Corren leaned forward. The kettle had gone cold; the bells at bow and stern stirred without wind, a faint bronze murmur, as if the barge itself had begun to listen.

"When you forge your new covenant—or refit the old one, whichever your prophecy allows—I want it done in the open. In Ashvale itself. Before your guild, your council, whatever passes for power behind those pretty stones. And I want the truth spoken aloud. All of it. The shattering. The seal. The founders' choice to let us starve rather than share. Every name of every family that withered while the valley ate well." His pale eyes fixed on Isandro and did not waver. "Spoken publicly. Spoken by *you*. Sworn on something you cannot take back."

Tam heard Isandro exhale slowly. He knew what Corren was asking—he understood it before she answered—because it was the same thing the knight's memorial had demanded, the same thing every grieving party on this side of the stones had demanded in one form or another: not apology. Apology was cheap, private, forgettable. They wanted *record*. They wanted the crime entered into the ledger of the world where it could never be quietly removed, the way her grandmother had been removed, the way his own family's history had been burned.

They wanted the truth to cost her something permanent.

"You're asking me to burn my life down in front of the only family I have," Isandro said. It wasn't a protest. Tam heard it as confirmation—her testing the weight of it, the way she'd test ice before stepping.

"I'm asking you to say it out loud where it counts," Corren said. "Anything less is just another whisper, and whispers are how your founders got away with it in the first place. They didn't announce the breaking. They buried it. One quiet decision at a time, until a whole realm was a rumor and my grandmother's grandmother was a footnote." He spread his hands. "I'm the last keeper of the first piece of the truth, Warden. I don't hand it to someone who means to keep carrying it the same way."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out, harsh and lonely.

Isandro looked down at her hands for a long moment. Then she looked up, and Tam saw her do the other thing she did—the rare thing, the thing that always frightened him a little because of what it cost her—she let him see her decide.

"I swear it," she said. "On my name, and on the thorn—" she touched her collarbone briefly, some private gesture Tam didn't understand, "—and on every memory I carry of the valley I grew up in. When the covenant stands again, I will stand in Ashvale, before the guild and whoever else will listen, and I will speak the whole of it. The shattering. The seal. The names of the dead. My voice, in the open, sworn and witnessed. And if I fail—if I falter, or soften it, or let them bury it again—then may everything I've sacrificed on this road be counted as nothing, and may I be remembered only as the last liar of Ashvale."

The words hung over the water. Corren didn't move for the space of ten heartbeats. Then he nodded, once, the way a lock nods when the right key turns.

"Well enough," he said. "Better than well enough. My grandmother would have liked you." He drew the chain over his head for the second time that day, and held the shard out on his open palm between them. Up close, the golden thread within it pulsed faintly, like something sleeping that had begun to dream. "It'll know you. It knew the last Delacroix who crossed, too. That's partly why I'm giving it up, if you want the ugly honest truth of it—it's been pulling westward for months. Toward the stones. Toward home." His mouth twisted. "Seems even broken oaths get homesick."

Isandro took the shard.

Tam watched her close her fingers around it and saw her whole body flinch—not in pain, exactly, but in recognition, the way a numb limb flinches when blood comes flooding back. The bells on the barge rang once, all together, soft and unbidden.

"There's a condition of mine as well," Corren added, standing, gathering his rag and his kettle with the brisk air of a man closing a transaction he'd rehearsed for decades. "When you speak your truth in Ashvale—you send word to me first. A letter, a bird, a bell-rung message downriver, I don't care. I want to hear it before your guildmasters spin it. I've spent my whole life having my history told to me by other people. I'll not have the ending told to me that way too."

"You'll have it," Isandro promised.

"Good." He paused at the stern rail, looking out at his river, and his voice dropped into something quieter. "The second keeper's south of here. Old soldier country. Maren Vale holds the second piece—my father's cousin twice over, if the lines matter anymore, which they don't." He glanced back at them, driftwood face creased into something that was almost, almost a smile. "Fair warning: she hates your guild more than I do, and I've hated it my whole life. Whatever you swore to me, double it before you knock on her door."

* * *

They camped that night on the far bank, in a stand of willows with the river talking to itself a few feet away. Tam couldn't sleep. He lay watching firelight move on the water and turning the day over in his mind like a coin he kept finding new faces on.

Across the embers, Isandro sat awake, the shard in her lap. She wasn't looking at it. She was looking at the dark where the valley lay, somewhere beyond the hills and the boundary stones and three days of hard walking—a locked room she'd grown up in, believing it was the whole house.

"It doesn't feel real yet," she said, without preamble, the way she talked when she'd decided he'd earned honesty. "Any of it. The shard, the oath, the—standing up in front of everyone and saying *they lied to us, they let a world die, and I'm one of them*." Her thumb moved over the stone's edge, back and forth, back and forth. "I've imagined coming home my whole life, Tam. Every patrol, every cold night on the perimeter—that was the shape of it. Coming home to people who knew me. Now I've sworn to walk through the front door and break it."

"You didn't swear to break it," Tam said. "You swore to tell the truth. Those aren't the same thing."

"In Ashvale they are." She was quiet a moment. "Veyra taught me that. First lesson she ever gave me. *The truth is a tool, Isandro. Tools are judged by what they build and what they break, and never by what they cost the one holding them.*" A short, bleak laugh. "I used to think that was wisdom."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was teaching me how to lie gently." Isandro lifted the shard, and the ember-light caught the golden thread inside it, and for a heartbeat Tam thought he saw it pulse—westward, toward the stones, toward home, just as Corren said. "But I meant the oath. Every word. That's the strange part. It didn't feel like a cost when I said it. It felt like—" she searched, "—like setting something down that I've been carrying so long I forgot it was heavy."

Tam rolled onto his side, fire-warm on one flank, river-cold on the other. *Tell her,* said the part of him that had confessed on the road and survived it. *Tell her the rest—the parts you left out, the reports you sent before you chose her, the way Veyra's letters found you too easily, the way the ambushers knew your route.* But the words stuck, as they always stuck, because the next report he'd sent was still unsent, sitting folded in his pack like a splinter working its way toward his heart.

Instead he said, "Get some sleep. We've got old soldier country tomorrow, and a woman who hates your guild more than Corren does."

Isandro snorted softly. "That seems impossible."

"So did a river, a week ago."

She smiled at that—really smiled—and banked the fire, and curled up with the shard wrapped safe against her chest, and for a while the only sounds were water and willow and her breathing evening out toward sleep.

Tam stayed awake a little longer. He took out the folded paper, unread, unsent, and held it over the embers until the corner began to brown. Veyra's handwriting, neat and patient: *Report position. Report progress. Report the shard.*

He let it catch. Watched it curl and flare and go to ash, and ground the ash into the riverbank with his heel, and told himself that was the end of it.

It was only later—much later, when the thirteenth moon was already climbing and everything had gone to ruin and reckoning—that he learned the guild had never needed his reports at all. They had known where Isandro was from the night she crossed the stones.

But that night, on the riverbank, burning his last tether and calling it freedom, Tam slept at last—unaware that somewhere downstream, a bell was ringing a warning he could not yet hear, and that the woman who hated the guild most of all was already waiting for them with a sword across her knees and a grief older than either of their lives.
