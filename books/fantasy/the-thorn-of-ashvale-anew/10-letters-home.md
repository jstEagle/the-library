## 10. Letters Home

The letter came wrapped in oilcloth, tied with valley flax, and sealed in green wax stamped with the ash-thorn sigil of the Wardens. It arrived the way forbidden things always arrive—quietly, folded into an ordinary moment, so that afterward Isandro could never separate the memory of receiving it from the memory of the morning it ruined.

She had been mending nets.

That was the work Ondine had found for her at Sable Hollow, or perhaps the work Ondine had invented so that Isandro would have something to do with her hands while her mind chewed itself ragged on prophecy and shards and the geography of a dying world. The nets belonged to the ferryman, Corren—the first heir, the one who had sworn her to truth before he'd sworn her his shard—and they hung from the beams of his boathouse like the ribs of some great gutted fish. Her fingers knew the work. That was the strange mercy of it. Knots did not care about covenants. Twine did not ask her whom she served. For an hour at a time she could be nothing but a pair of hands and the small animal satisfaction of a mended mesh, and the ache of home would recede to something almost bearable, like weather seen through a window.

Then Tam came down the path from the hollow with a look on his face she had learned to read in only two weeks of traveling beside him: the careful blankness he wore when he was deciding whether to tell her something.

He held the parcel out flat on both palms, the way one might offer bread to a bird that could just as easily take a finger.

"It was tucked into the grain sacks," he said. "The carter didn't know anything. Or says he didn't." A pause, and then, because Tam had never once in his life let her carry a bad thing alone if he could stand under half of it: "It's Warden seal, Isandro."

"I can see that."

"You don't have to open it."

"No," she agreed, and took it anyway, because of course she did, because her name was not written on it but might as well have been, because there are letters you know the contents of before you break the seal and you open them regardless, the way you touch your tongue to a cracked tooth.

* * *

She waited until she was alone.

Not by design—at least that's what she told herself later, turning it over in the dark of the loft where Corren let them sleep. She told herself she went up the ladder simply to be out of the wind, out of the gray light off the river, out of Tam's watchful kindness which was, some mornings, harder to bear than hostility would have been. But honesty had become a discipline lately, forced on her by a ferryman who accepted nothing less, and the honest shape of it was this: she wanted to open the letter alone, because whatever Veyra had written, she did not want anyone watching her face while she read it.

The guildmaster's hand was exactly as she remembered it. That was the first knife. She had spent weeks imagining Veyra's fury—imagined being denounced, hunted properly, named traitor in the guild hall—and here instead was that familiar slanted script, patient and precise, the hand that had corrected her training ledgers when she was fourteen, that had written the note pinned to her first Warden cloak. *Isandro.* Just that, at the top. No title, no accusation. The way Veyra wrote to family.

*You will have heard by now that I know where you are. I want you to hear it from me rather than discover it, as you discovered everything else in that vault, alone and badly.*

*I am not going to pretend I don't understand why you left. I understood before you did. That is not a boast; it is the plain description of my job. I watched you for three years the way I watch every young warden who asks too many good questions, and I hoped, selfishly, that you would stay mediocre long enough to grow old.*

*Come home. Say nothing of what you've read—not to me, not to anyone—and all of it is forgiven. There is no punishment waiting for you. There never was. The archive duty stands; the little room over the map room with the good light is still yours. In spring we'll reassess the boundary rotation and I'll see to it you're posted somewhere green.*

*You are the last person in Ashvale who has ever been kind to me without wanting anything, and I find, to my considerable irritation, that I miss you.*

*Burn this.*

*V.*

Isandro read it four times.

The first time, she read it as a young woman reads a letter from the person who raised her, and her eyes stung, and she had to set it down on her knee and breathe at the rafters until the tightness in her throat eased. The second time, she read it as a Warden, and began to count what the letter actually said. The third and fourth times were war.

Because here was the thing, laid bare between the lines like a bone showing through skin: *I know where you are.*

Not *I suspect.* Not *there are reports.* *I know.* The letter had traveled inside a grain shipment into a village so small it appeared on no guild map Ondine possessed, and it had found her within days of her arrival, and its author expected it burned. Which meant Veyra had eyes in Sable Hollow or eyes on every road out of Ashvale or eyes Isandro hadn't even imagined, and had chosen, deliberately, to spend one of them on a letter that said: *I see you. I have always seen you. I will keep seeing you.*

And beneath that, softer and far more dangerous, the other message, the one written in the ink of genuine feeling: *the little room over the map room with the good light is still yours.* Veyra knew about the good light. Knew Isandro liked to work late and hated cold mornings and kept her knives sharp past the point of reason. Every specific in the letter was a small demonstration of love, and every demonstration of love was also a demonstration of surveillance, and Isandro sat on the edge of her narrow cot with the paper trembling very slightly in her hands and could not find, anywhere in herself, the line where the one ended and the other began.

That was how Veyra had always governed, she realized. Not with cruelty—with attention. The whole guild ran on it. You obeyed the Wardens not because you feared them but because they *noticed* you, noticed everything, noticed your mother's cough and your daughter's talent and the exact shade of worry on your face, and their noticing felt so much like being loved that it took years to understand it was also a net. Isandro had lived nineteen years inside that net believing it was a blanket.

"Did you burn it?" Tam asked from below, and she started so hard she nearly dropped the letter.

* * *

She climbed down to find him sitting on an overturned skiff, whittling nothing in particular, which was what Tam did with his hands when his mind was working too loudly. He didn't look up.

"Well?" he said.

"I haven't decided."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know what you asked." She folded the letter along Veyra's creases, precisely, an act with no purpose except to give her hands a task that wasn't shaking. "She offers full pardon. No conditions except silence. My old room. My old life. She even—" Isandro laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle, "—she says she misses me."

Tam set down the knife. He looked at her then, fully, and his face did the thing it did when he was choosing honesty over comfort, the same expression he'd worn the night he followed her to the stones and asked to come along instead of reporting her.

"Do you believe her? About the pardon?"

"Yes," Isandro said. "That's what frightens me. She means it. She'd forgive me tomorrow, publicly, with cake, if I walked back through the gate and shut my mouth. That's not the trap."

"Then what is?"

"The trap is that she's right about me missing it." Isandro turned the folded letter over in her hands. Green wax crumbs clung to her palm; she brushed them away and they left a faint shine, ash-thorn shaped, that wouldn't quite come off. "The room with the good light. The bells at dusk. Marta's terrible coffee. The sound of forty wardens drilling in the yard at dawn—you stop hearing it after a while and then you're somewhere else and you'd give anything to hear it again. She knows all of that. She wrote this letter knowing I'd read it hungry." She looked up. "Love is supposed to be the one thing they didn't weaponize, Tam. And she's done it beautifully. This letter is kind and true and it is also a leash, and I cannot—I genuinely cannot—tell where the kindness stops and the leash starts. Neither can she, maybe. That might be the worst part. I think she believes she's rescuing me."

"She is," Tam said quietly. "From us. From this." He gestured at the river, the gray orchards beyond it, the pale sky that never quite committed to blue. "From a land that's dying. From a quest that's already gotten one of us stabbed at." He flexed his left arm unconsciously; the wound from the shrine ambush had closed but the scar was new and angry and he touched it the way people touch a door they've locked twice. "Isandro, I'm on your side. You know I'm on your side. But if I got that letter, some part of me would want to go home."

"Some part of me wants to go home *now*," Isandro snapped, and immediately regretted it, because his face didn't harden—it just went sad in that patient way of his, and somehow that was worse. "I'm sorry. It's just—she signed it V. Do you understand? Not Guildmaster Veyra. V. Like I'm still hers. Like I'm still—" She stopped.

"Like you're still her kid," Tam said.

"Like I'm still someone she's *protecting*," Isandro finished, which wasn't the same sentence, and they both heard the difference and neither said it aloud.

* * *

Corren found them at dusk, as he seemed to find everyone at dusk, materializing out of the river fog with the unhurried air of a man who owned the water and merely tolerated the land. He was seventy if he was a day, broad as a door, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had watched a thousand crossings and trusted perhaps nine of them.

"The carter's gone," he said without preamble, lowering himself onto the skiff beside Tam. It groaned. "Paid in full, three villages early on his route, and nervous as a wet cat. Someone bought his silence going and coming."

"Going?" Isandro said sharply. "He carried something *out*, too?"

Corren looked at her with those river eyes, and she saw him decide, again, the daily decision, to extend trust to a girl from the valley that starved his world.

"He carried word of where you slept, child. That's how these things run. A letter comes in on a cart; a report goes back on the next one. You're not hiding in Sable Hollow. You're being *kept*. There's a difference, and it matters." He scratched his beard. "My grandmother used to say: when the shepherd writes you letters, you're not lost. You're his best sheep."

Tam snorted despite himself. Isandro didn't laugh at all.

"Then I should move," she said. "Tonight. We should—"

"And go where?" Corren asked, mild as still water. "They know where you are now. Moving changes nothing except whether you sleep well. No—" he shook his head slowly, "—a letter like that isn't meant to catch you. If they wanted you caught, you'd be caught; you've seen their steel work. It's meant to *turn* you. To make the going-home voice louder than every other voice you've got. Whoever wrote it knows that the sharpest blade against someone like you isn't fear." His eyes rested on her, almost gentle. "It's homesickness."

Isandro stood very still. Downriver, the first lantern of the ferry landing came alight, a small warm gold smudge in all that gray, and it hurt to look at, the way everything honest and small and domestic had begun to hurt to look at since she'd left.

"She's not wrong about me," Isandro said finally, to the fog as much as to the men. "That's the thing none of you understand. I would burn every plan we've made to hear those bells again. I think about my room. I think about the smell of the archive, dust and lamp oil. I dream about it. And Veyra knows I dream about it, because Veyra has known everything about me since I was thirteen years old, and the cruelest part—the part I can't get around—is that she's not lying to me. Come home, say nothing, all is forgiven. She'd honor every word of it. I'd be safe, and warm, and forgiven, and I'd live in the little room with the good light until I died, and the orchards out there—" she nodded toward the border, toward the gray country that had once been green, toward the shrines with no voices in them, "—would go on starving quietly for another hundred years, and no one in Ashvale would ever learn why, because I'd have promised not to say."

"So the letter gives you a choice," Tam said slowly.

"The letter gives me a *test*," Isandro said. "Dressed up as a gift. And the price of failing it is my conscience, and the price of passing it is—" she spread her hands, at the loft, the river, the dying realm, the two men looking at her with something like pity, "—everything else."

* * *

She burned the letter at midnight, as instructed.

She built the fire herself in the boathouse stove, fed it kindling until it stood up properly, and held Veyra's pages over the flames one at a time, reading each a final time as it curled. On the last pass she made herself look for the trick—for the hidden cipher, the invisible ink, the second meaning stitched into the margins the way wardens were trained to hide messages. There was nothing. It was exactly what it appeared to be: a woman who loved her, asking her to come home and bury the truth.

Perhaps that was the most damning evidence of all. No trick. No threat. Just the offer, naked and sincere, of everything Isandro had ever wanted, contingent on her becoming small enough to fit back inside her old life and staying there.

The paper caught. Green wax bubbled and went black. The ash-thorn seal burned last of all, stubbornly, holding its shape in the embers for a long moment—a small dark thorn curled in fire—before crumbling to nothing.

Isandro watched it until it was gone, and then she sat by the stove with her arms around her knees and let herself, for exactly one hour by the boathouse clock, grieve. For the room with the good light. For the bells. For Veyra, who had taught her to hold a blade and write a ledger and keep her word, and who had just taught her, with a single loving letter, the final lesson of her education: that the people who raise you can love you completely and still be on the wrong side of history, and that choosing the world over them does not stop them from being your family. It only stops you from being able to go back to them.

When the hour was up, she banked the fire, climbed the ladder, and woke Tam by pressing one finger to his shoulder in the dark.

"We leave at first light," she whispered. "For the knight."

He blinked up at her, sleep-soft and instantly alert, the way he always was, as if some part of him stood guard even dreaming. "The second shard."

"The second shard." She paused at the top of the ladder, looking back down at his outline in the gloom. "Tam. The carter carried word out. Which means by the time we reach the knight's hold, Veyra won't just know where we've been." Her hand drifted, without thinking, to her pocket—to the space where, though she could not yet have said why, she had taken to keeping a single black thorn pressed in a stub of candle wax. "She'll know where we're going."

Below her in the dark, Tam was quiet for a moment. Then, grimly:

"Then we'd better get there first."
