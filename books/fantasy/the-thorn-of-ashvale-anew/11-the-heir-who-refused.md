## 11. The Heir Who Refused

The road to Cindermarch smelled of old fires.

Tam had noticed it first as a sweetness on the wind, like apples left too long in the sun, and only later understood what it actually was: ash that had never been rained clean. A century of burning, layered into the soil itself, rising up with every footfall to remind the land of what it had lost. His boots came away gray at the edges. Isandro's did too, though she said nothing about it. She hadn't said much of anything since the ferryman's daughter had pressed the first shard into her palm three days ago, warm as a living thing and heavy as a sin.

"It's called a march," Tam said, to break the silence, "but there's no army."

"There was," Ondine had told them, before they left Sable Hollow. Her blind eyes had turned toward a window that looked out on nothing but gray orchard rows. "Cindermarch held the border once. When the covenant still flowed, its knights were sworn to both realms—the only ones who could stand with a foot in each. They were proud of that. *Both*, they'd say, like a toast." She had folded her hands atop her cane. "Now there are no knights. There is one knight, and she does not ride for anyone."

The town, when they reached it, was less a town than a memory of one. Streets that had clearly once rung with markets now held perhaps two hundred souls moving slowly between leaning houses. The great gatehouse at the southern end—built for garrisons, built for glory—stood empty, its banners long rotted off their poles. But someone had swept it. Someone swept it every day, by the look of the stone, worn smooth along one path from the gate to a low building near the center whose roof was new timber over very old walls.

"That one," Isandro said quietly. She had stopped walking. Tam followed her gaze and saw what she saw: the shrine. Not ruined like the others they'd passed—*tended*. Candle smoke ghosted from under its door even in daylight. Fresh-cut wildflowers, the only color for miles, lay in a bundle against the threshold.

"She keeps it herself," Tam said.

"Or she pays someone to keep it so she doesn't have to look at it." Isandro's jaw was set in the way he'd learned meant fear wearing armor. "Either way. Come on."

They found her in the training yard behind the shrine, which surprised Tam until it didn't. Of course she trained. What else did a knight do when the war was over and no one had told her body?

She was tall in the way that made everything else in the yard seem provisional—a woman carved down to essentials, gray-streaked hair bound back tight, practicing forms alone with a blunted sword against a wooden pell scarred nearly through in the shape of a man. Her armor hung on a rack nearby, polished to a mirror shine that reflected nothing worth seeing. Every strike landed exactly where the last one had. There was something terrible in the precision of it, Tam thought. It wasn't practice. It was prayer, or penance, or both.

Isandro stepped into the yard. "Ser?"

The sword stopped mid-arc.

For a long moment the woman didn't turn. Then she did, all at once, the way a door slams, and Tam saw her face and understood Ondine's warning completely. It wasn't a cruel face. It was worse than that. It was a face that had done all its grieving years ago and had no room left for anything softer than attention.

"You're wearing valley steel," the knight said.

Isandro glanced down at her short sword, the Warden-issue blade at her hip, and Tam watched her make the same calculation he was making—that drawing attention to it would be fatal, and hiding it would be worse. "I am," she said finally. "My name is Isandro Delacroix. I've come from Ashvale. I'm told you hold a shard of the oath-stone, and that you're the heir entitled to—"

"I know what I'm heir to." The knight crossed the yard without hurry. Up close, the scars announced themselves: one pulling down the corner of her left eye, another along her throat like a second necklace. "Everyone in this march knows what my grandmother kept locked in her crypt because no one else could be trusted with it. That doesn't mean I'll hand it to the first Warden who walks out of the fog asking nicely." She looked Isandro over with an expression Tam might have called clinical if it weren't so hot underneath. "Delacroix. Another Delacroix. Do you know how many of your family have come through here telling us things will be mended?"

"I—"

"One," the knight said. "One other. My grandmother went to your valley to plead for the covenant's renewal and came home with nothing but a story no one believed. And then your guild sealed the stones tighter than ever, and we all got to watch the orchards die a row at a time." She turned away, dismissing them, and took up her stance at the pell again. "Get out of my yard."

"We haven't even—" Tam started.

"*Out.*"

***

They rented a room above the one tavern still operating in Cindermarch, if *operating* was the word for a place with three patrons and a landlord who watered the ale twice and apologized for it honestly. Isandro sat at the window with the shard wrapped in cloth in her lap, staring at the shrine across the way, where candlelight had begun to bloom as dusk came down.

"She wouldn't even hear me," Isandro said.

"No," Tam agreed. He was stretched on the room's single bed, hands behind his head, watching the ceiling beams. He'd learned on this journey that his role was often just to be present while Isandro thought, a wall she could talk toward. He'd also learned—though he'd never say it aloud—that every hour he spent being that wall was another hour the letter in his pack stayed unburned. Veyra's orders, written in her own hand: *stay close, report true.* He had reported nothing since the crossing. Each day he didn't send word felt less like loyalty and more like debt accruing interest.

"The memorial," Isandro said suddenly.

"What?"

"At the shrine. The candles, the flowers, the sweeping—someone tends it daily. If the whole town died in the decline, or enough of it..." She turned to look at him, and in the fading light her eyes were fierce and frightened at once. "That's not grief she can do privately, Tam. That's a public duty. A vigil. If she holds a service—if there's an anniversary coming—"

Tam sat up. Through the window, across the square, townsfolk were beginning to gather at the shrine door. Not many. A dozen, two dozen. Old people mostly, and children too young to know why they were there except that everyone else was. The knight emerged from her yard in formal dress—not armor, but a soldier's blacks with a single white sash—and began lighting the shrine lamps one by one.

"How would we find out?" Tam asked. "Knock on the door of grief and ask for a schedule?"

But Isandro was already moving, and he followed her down the stairs and out into the cold, because that was the arrangement, and because—though he hated admitting it—he wanted to see what she'd do.

The landlord caught them at the door. He was a broad man with flour on his forearms and the particular tiredness of someone who had buried more customers than he'd served. "You'll be wanting to know about the Vigil," he said. Not a question. News traveled fast in a dying town; strangers were news.

"The Vigil?" Isandro asked carefully.

"Ninety-one years tomorrow night. The Night of the Graying, we call it—the night the last orchard in Cindermarch went bare in a single hour, every tree at once, like the valley exhaled." He wiped his hands needlessly on his apron. "Ser Aldiva stands witness every year. Reads the names. All nine hundred and forty of them. Takes from moonrise to moonset." His voice did something complicated on the number. "You're welcome to attend. Everyone's welcome. That's rather the point of it."

"Will she speak to us after?" Isandro asked.

The landlord looked at her for a long moment—at her valley accent, at the steel on her hip—and Tam braced for the door to close. Instead the man sighed, deep and old. "After? No one speaks after. You stand witness for four hours listening to dead people's names, girl, and then you tell me whether you have anything left to say." He turned back toward his kitchen. "Come at moonrise. Wear plain clothes. Leave the sword."

***

They stood at the back of the crowd the next night, and Tam understood within minutes why Ser Aldiva had refused them.

It wasn't the length, though the length was its own kind of ordeal. It was the names themselves. Aldiva read them in a voice like struck iron, unhurried, giving each one its full weight, and the names were not abstract. They came with the town's own music attached. *Maren Ostwick, cooper, who planted the last green row with her own hands the spring before.* *Tomas Ostwick, her son, who carried water to it until there was no well water left to carry.* *Sela Vane, midwife. Her daughters, Ivy and Bramble. The twins born the winter the gray came, who lived six days.*

Nine hundred and forty names. Tam lost count of the times he swallowed hard. Beside him Isandro stood motionless, and when he glanced at her he saw tears tracking freely down her face, unchecked, and her lips moving faintly—repeating the names to herself, committing them, refusing to let a single one pass through her unclaimed.

This was what the knight had wanted. Tam realized it somewhere around the four-hundredth name, watching Aldiva's candlelit profile as she read without pause, without mercy, without softening. She hadn't refused Isandro an audience. She had refused her a shortcut. Anyone could come asking for a relic with pretty words about covenants and renewal. But to stand here, in the cold, for four hours, hearing the true cost of the valley's safety counted out name by name—that was the price of admission, and no Warden had ever paid it.

*And none of them ever will,* Tam thought, *unless this one does.*

He thought of Veyra then, unexpectedly—of her study, her maps, her careful voice explaining the necessity of the seal. He tried to imagine her standing in this square listening to nine hundred and forty names. He found he couldn't. Not because she was heartless—he knew better now, or believed he did—but because she had arranged her heart so carefully around the arithmetic of survival that these names would never reach it. They'd be filed. Weighed. Found acceptable losses. And she'd sleep soundly after.

The horror of that thought was not that Veyra was a monster. It was that she wasn't.

Moonset came. The last name fell into the silence like a stone into a well: *Aldous Marcher, last knight-commander of Cindermarch, who died believing someone would come back for us.*

Ser Aldiva closed the book. The crowd dispersed in silence, drifting homeward through streets that seemed to hold their breath. And then, before Tam could move, before Isandro could decide anything, the knight crossed the square directly to them, torchlight catching the scars on her face, her eyes red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"You stayed," she said. "All of it."

"All of it," Isandro said. Her voice was raw. "Every name."

"Do you know why I read them aloud instead of praying silently? Anyone can pray silently. Praying silently costs nothing and changes nothing." Aldiva stepped closer. She smelled of lamp oil and cold air. "I read them because your guild founders stood in a warm hall ninety-one years ago and decided, in whispers, that this realm was expendable. Decisions made in whispers deserve answers shouted in public. So." She folded her arms. "You've paid the toll, Delacroix. You stood witness. Now I will hear you—one chance, tonight, and if I hear one word of excuse, one word about necessity or impossible choices or how hard it was on *your* side of the stones, you will leave this march and never return."

Tam watched Isandro straighten. Watched her wipe her face with the back of her hand, unashamed, and meet the knight's eyes.

"I won't excuse it," Isandro said. "I came to tell you it was a crime."

Something flickered across Aldiva's face—too fast to name. "Go on."

"The covenant wasn't broken by accident or worn out by time. Your founders chose it. They wrote the choice down and buried it, and they erased the memory of why, and they raised seven generations of Wardens—including me—to guard a lie and call it protection." Isandro's voice cracked and she pushed through it. "There's a prophecy in our vault, older than the lie, that says the covenant can be reforged. Three shards of the original oath-stone. Yours is one. The price is—" she hesitated, and Tam saw her decide, again, to spend the truth, "—the price is terrible. But it can be paid. And I intend to pay it, whatever it costs me. I'm not asking you to trust the valley. I'm asking you to trust *me*, and I know how little that's worth from a stranger in valley steel. So I'm not asking for the shard tonight. I'm asking for the chance to earn it. Tell me what you require. Whatever it is, I'll pay that too."

The square was utterly silent. Somewhere a shutter creaked in the wind. Tam realized half the town hadn't gone home at all—they stood in doorways and shadows, listening, and every one of them was waiting to see what their knight would do with this girl from the country that had killed their orchards.

Ser Aldiva studied Isandro for a long time. Then, slowly, she reached to her belt and drew out not a weapon but a small object on a cord: a shard of pale stone, veined with faint silver light, hanging beside her grandmother's signet ring.

"My requirement is simple," she said. "When this is done—when the covenant stands or falls—you will stand in *their* valley, in *their* guild hall, and read these names aloud. All nine hundred and forty. To the Wardens, in their warm hall, the way I read them here." She held the cord out over her open palm, not quite letting go. "Justice isn't the shard, Delacroix. Justice is the telling. Swear it."

Isandro reached out and closed her hand over the shard, over the knight's hand, and Tam heard her answer ring off the dark houses like a bell:

"I swear it. On my name, and on every name you spoke tonight."

The light in the shard brightened once, like a heartbeat, and went still.

Aldiva exhaled—long, shuddering, the breath of a woman setting down something carried for decades. "Then come inside," she said gruffly, turning toward the shrine. "All of you—I know you're there, the whole town's eavesdropping. There's soup, and you'll eat it, and then you'll tell me everything. Including," and here her eyes cut to Tam with a sharpness that made his stomach drop, "why your friend has been standing like a man with a letter burning a hole in his pack for three days."

Tam's blood went cold.

Isandro turned to look at him.
