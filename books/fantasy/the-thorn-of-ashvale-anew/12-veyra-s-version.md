## 12. Veyra's Version

The letter sat on Guildmaster Veyra's desk for three days before she sealed it.

She had written it eleven times. The first draft had been a summons—*Return immediately; your absence constitutes dereliction*—and she had burned that one in the candle flame with a steadiness that surprised her, watching the ink curl and blacken like a small dying thing. The second had been pleading. The third had been honest, which was the most dangerous of all, and so of course she had burned that one too.

What remained was the eleventh: *Come home. Say nothing. All is forgiven.*

Forgiven. She turned the word over as she pressed her seal into the wax—a thorned rose, the old Delacroix sigil, chosen deliberately because she wanted Isandro to look at it a long time. Forgiveness implied a crime, and Isandro had committed none. What Veyra meant was *I will pretend you did nothing*, which was a different kindness entirely, and one the girl would see through in a heartbeat if Veyra were not careful.

She was always careful. That was the whole tragedy of her, though only two people alive knew it, and one of them was dead.

* * *

Forty-one years ago, Veyra Solenne had been seventeen years old and the fastest climber in Ashvale's eastern cohort, and on the night of the autumn equinox she had done what every Warden sworn in that century swore never to do: she went over the boundary stones.

Not by accident. Not by folly. By invitation.

Her mentor, Aldous Crane—the man whose portrait now hung in the guild hall with his eyes worn nearly blank by decades of dust and admiring glances—had taken her aside three nights before and said, in his dry way, "You ask too many questions to stay useful, child. Come see the answer instead." And he had walked her out under a fat harvest moon to the northernmost stone, the one shaped like a kneeling figure, and laid his palm flat against its face.

Veyra had expected death. Every recruit learned the litany: *the stones burn, the stones blind, the stones unmake*. What she got instead was a door.

Aldous's hand sank into the stone up to the wrist, the granite going soft as river clay around him, and beyond it—through a seam no wider than a knife blade—she saw sky. Not valley sky. A sky the color of a bruise healing into green, shot through with lights that moved like fish through deep water, and below it the silhouette of towers, or trees, or something that could not decide which it wanted to be.

"The covenant is paused," Aldous said quietly. "Not ended. Never ended. Do you understand the difference, Veyra? An ended thing can rest. A paused thing waits."

"Paused by whom?"

"By us. By the founders. By men who thought they were saving the world and only had room in their arms for half of it." He withdrew his hand. The seam closed like water healing over a dropped pebble. "You are the best of this generation, and I am an old man who has carried this alone too long. So I am giving you the choice I was given, and I warn you: there is no version of it that lets you sleep well again."

Then he told her about the outer realm. About the withering. About what the founders' peace had cost, and who was paying the bill.

* * *

They crossed together at moonrise, and Veyra remembered—for forty-one years she remembered, in perfect merciless detail—the first thing the other realm did to her.

It gave her color.

Not new colors. The same colors, but *more*, the way a fire is more than its smoke. The grass of the outer meadows was green the way the valley's grass was green, but the outer grass *sang* its green, hummed with it, each blade carrying light up from the soil like a cupped hand offering water. She had stood in that meadow and wept without understanding why, and Aldous had waited, patient as stone, because he knew. He had wept in the same place thirty years before.

And then they had walked down into Sable Hollow, and the colors died.

That was the cruelty of it—the architecture of the whole wound, laid bare in a single day's walk. Near the boundary, where the covenant's severed flow still pooled like blood in cloth, the land lived. Farther out, it faded. The orchards came first: gray-barked trees bearing fruit the color of ash, sweet still, edible, but *hollow*—you ate them and felt nothing, not even fullness. Then the shrines, their candles burning without warmth. Then the people.

Veyra had imagined suffering as she understood suffering: hunger, war, plague, the sharp emergencies the valley trained its Wardens against. She had not imagined *slowing*. The outer folk moved like people wading upstream. Children played, but their games lost rules year over year, growing simpler, shorter, until the smallest ones simply stood in fields holding hands. Elders forgot their own names gently, without fear, the way snow forgets the shape of the roof it lands on. Nobody screamed. Nobody starved. Everything dimmed, evenly, everywhere, forever.

"They call it the Quiet," Aldous said. "The mages here know exactly what it is. Their realm fed on the covenant's flow, and ours dammed it. They have petition letters older than our guild. They have sent envoys across the stones nine times in a hundred years. Nine times. You have never heard of a single one."

"Why not?" Veyra asked, and then heard her own question and flinched, because she already knew.

"Because the founders erased them," Aldous said. "Every envoy, every letter, every memory. The warding doesn't just keep magic out of the valley. It keeps the truth out too. We are not protected from the outer realm, child. We are protected from our own reflection."

* * *

She stayed three weeks. Aldous let her, because he understood—you could not choose a burden you had not held.

She sat with a shrine-keeper named Maren who tended a chapel to a god whose name had worn off his own altar. She helped bring in the ash-colored apples, learning the rhythm of hands that worked slower each season and did not complain, because complaint required a future to complain toward. She held a woman's hand while the woman tried, with visible effort, to remember her daughter's face, and failed, and smiled anyway, and said, "It was a good face. I remember that much. It was a good face."

On the nineteenth night, Veyra found the petitions. A vault beneath the hollow's last standing library, maintained by a blind archivist who had made himself the keeper of everything the world was forgetting—names written down before the writers forgot them, read aloud every morning to a congregation of chairs. Nine centuries of letters addressed across the border. *To the Wardens of Ashvale. To whoever remembers us. Please. Please. Please.*

The archivist—he was young then, Ondine's predecessor or perhaps Ondine himself in a youth he did not speak of—told her the arithmetic of it. The covenant, reforged, would restore both realms. But the reforging demanded a price the founders had refused to pay, and so instead they had broken the oath-stone into three shards, scattered them, sealed the valley, and rewritten the ledger so that the theft looked like salvation.

"Why did they refuse?" Veyra asked. "Whatever the price was—they built a guild, they raised generations to guard a lie. Whatever it cost, wouldn't it have been cheaper than all that?"

The archivist turned his sightless eyes toward her. "The price is memory," he said. "Whoever joins the shards and speaks the covenant whole must surrender their memories of the life they bind it to. Every face. Every name. The founders were not cowards, Warden. They were parents. They looked at the price and chose their children over the world, and then they built a church around the choosing so they'd never have to look at it again. It is the oldest sin there is. It is love, pointing the wrong direction."

* * *

Veyra went home. That was the sentence she had never been able to forgive herself, in forty-one years of telling it to herself in the dark: *she saw, and she went home.*

But here was the part no one else would ever say aloud, because saying it required having stood where she stood: the choice was not between good and evil. It was between two griefs.

If she tore the covenant open—if she gathered the shards, spoke the rite, paid the price—the outer realm bloomed again, yes. And the valley? The valley had spent a hundred years building its whole life on ground that had been drained to build it. The wards would fall. Magic would flood back into a place that had forgotten how to hold it, and the flood does not ask permission of the houses in its path. People she loved—people who had taught her to whittle and pray and fight—would be remade or ruined by a truth arriving all at once that should have arrived gently over generations.

Or she could do nothing. Keep the seal. Let the outer realm go on dimming, gently, without screaming, until the Quiet finished its work—and live inside the walls of a lie, raising Wardens to guard a theft, teaching children litanies about protection that were actually litanies about guilt.

*Aldous chose the second,* she reminded herself. *He showed me the wound and then he bandaged it back up and called it duty.* He had crossed over dozens of times in his long service—not to help, never to help, just to witness, to carry the weight of knowing so no one else had to. "Someone must remember for both sides," he told her once, "and someone must keep the secret so the remembering changes nothing. It is a terrible office. I would not wish it on you, and yet I have wished it on no one else either."

When he died—when he walked out to the northern stone one winter night, alone, and did not come back—Veyra took the office. She told herself it was penance. Some nights, in the honest dark, she admitted it was inheritance: the sin passed down like a ring, too large for her hand, worn anyway.

* * *

And then a girl with a Delacroix face had gone digging in the vault.

Veyra had known within a day. Of course she had. She had known the moment Isandro's name surfaced in the inventory logs beside the restricted shelves—the prophecy book's location was not a secret to the guildmaster; the book itself was bait in a trap three guildmasters old, placed where ambition and curiosity would find it, because the guild had learned long ago that secrets kept too well rot into schisms. Better the book be found by one soul at a time, and managed, than by ten at once in a fire.

But it was not the book that had shaken Veyra. It was the name. Delacroix. She had read the erasure orders herself, years ago, in the deepest drawer of the archive—her first act as guildmaster, the traditional corruption, the moment every successor learned what the chair truly cost. *Remove all record. The line ends here.* And yet here was the line, walking through the guild hall with sawdust on her boots and her grandmother's stubborn jaw, asking too many questions, climbing too fast, being the best of her generation in exactly the ways that made her dangerous.

Veyra had watched her the way you watch weather turn. And when the reports came—Sable Hollow, the archivist, the ferryman heir, the first shard—she had sat very still at her desk for a long time, feeling something she had not permitted herself to feel since Aldous walked into the winter:

Hope. And terror, wearing hope's coat.

Because Isandro was doing what Veyra had never dared. She was gathering the shards. She was moving toward the rite. And if she succeeded—if she paid the price the founders refused—then everything Veyra had spent forty-one years protecting would be swept away in an afternoon, and everything Veyra had spent forty-one years concealing would come into the light with her name signed at the bottom of the concealment.

She ought to send Wardens. Hard men, clean retrieval, a cell and a quiet correction of records. Her predecessors would have. Her predecessors had—the ambush was already forming in the eastern barracks, loyalists volunteering, and Veyra had signed the order with a hand that did not shake, because a guildmaster's hand never shakes where anyone can see.

But she also wrote the letter. *Come home. Say nothing. All is forgiven.*

Both things were true at once, and that was the whole of her: she would break the girl and save her in the same motion, and call it mercy, and mean it.

* * *

On the fourth evening, she sealed the letter and gave it to a smuggler she trusted—one of the gray-market runners who moved between the realms through cracks in the seal that even the founders hadn't known they'd left—and then she climbed the tower stairs to the archive's top shelf, where the guild's oldest ledger lay chained behind glass.

She did not open it. She only stood there, an old woman in a lamplit room, looking at the spine.

"You would have liked her," she said to Aldous, to the dust, to no one. "That's the worst of it. You would have liked her, and she'd have followed you over the stones the way I did, and you'd have shown her the orchards and the shrines and the woman who couldn't remember her daughter's face. And then you'd have brought her home and told her to keep the secret, and she'd have said yes, because everyone says yes, and she'd have hated herself quietly for fifty years, the way we all agreed to."

Her breath fogged the glass.

"But she's not saying yes," Veyra whispered. "That's what none of us planned for. She's not saying yes."

Below, in the barracks, the loyalists sharpened their blades and spoke of treason. In the east, past the boundary, a girl with a Delacroix face was closing her hand around the first shard of a broken oath. And between them, riding through the night with a smuggler's satchel, a letter traveled home—*come home, say nothing, all is forgiven*—carrying the only weapon Veyra had ever trusted to work on Isandro Delacroix:

The truth of how much she was loved, and how much that love intended to cost her.

Veyra turned from the glass, took up her cloak, and went down to sign the order that would put Wardens on the road by morning. If the letter failed—if the girl refused the leash, however gently offered—there would be an ambush at some lonely shrine, and Veyra already knew, with the terrible clarity of a woman who has spent her whole life choosing the lesser grief, exactly whose blood would buy the second shard's safety.

She signed anyway.

That was her version. It had always been her version: save what can be saved, mourn what cannot, and never—never—let them see you weep for both at once.
