## 13. Ambush at the Shrine

The shrine had no name that Ondine could give them, only a direction: *follow the gray river until it forgets it was ever blue, and there you will find the stones where they used to pray.* Isandro had been walking for two days when the river made good on the archivist's promise, its water the color of pewter, its banks stitched with orchards whose branches had gone to bone. The shrine rose out of the mist like a rib cage—arches of pale stone, half-swallowed by a creeping moss that was not green but the color of old ash. Whatever god had been worshipped here had not been driven out. It had simply starved.

Isandro stood at the threshold and felt the silence press against her ears like deep water.

"They'll have followed us," Tam said quietly behind her. He had not slept well since the ferry. She had watched him pretend otherwise, watched him arrange his face each morning into something easy, and she had let him, because the alternative was a conversation neither of them was ready to have. "Wardens don't lose people. Not people they want back."

"I know."

"And you came anyway."

"I came anyway." She touched the second shard through the leather wrap at her breast. It was warm. It had been warm since the ferryman had pressed it into her hands with the weight of his oath still clinging to it—*the truth will be told in Ashvale, aloud, in the square, or this means nothing.* Promises made aloud had a texture. She was learning to feel them.

The knight—Serah Vail, the second keeper, who had refused to hear Isandro's name until Isandro had stood a full hour in the cold before the memorial wall and spoken the names of the dead of Sable Hollow one by one—moved past them both into the shrine. She walked like a woman carrying a door on her back: heavy, deliberate, uncomplaining. "We rest here," she said. "The moss keeps the cold out. And the stones—" she laid her scarred palm flat against an arch, and for a moment Isandro could have sworn the gray light bent toward her, "—the stones still listen. Even starved, they listen. If anyone comes, we'll know."

Isandro looked up at the arches. Gaps in the roof let the pale sky through, and the light that fell was the color of milk over iron. She thought of the valley's shrine of the Vigilant Flame, where she had knelt a hundred times in the warm gold glow of ward-lanterns, reciting the Litany of the Kept: *What lies beyond is not our burden. What lies within is not our debt.* She had thought it a comfort once. Now every word of it tasted like ash from these very orchards.

She knelt and unwrapped the shard.

It was smaller than her fist, a wedge of stone that seemed at first to be plain granite—until the light touched it, and then it was not. Veins moved beneath its surface, slow as sap, and they were not gray. They were the only color in the shrine: a deep, patient green, like the memory of a leaf.

"Careful," Serah said.

"I'm always careful."

"You're careful the way a river is careful," the knight said, and there was almost, *almost*, humor in it. "You go around obstacles. You do not stop."

Tam laughed—a short, startled sound, the first in days—and then the light in the shrine changed.

Isandro felt it before she understood it. The milk-pale air thickened. The moss on the arches shivered though there was no wind. And in the back of her mind, in the place where a Warden's training lived like a second heartbeat, something *sang*: the low, sweet, terrible resonance of valley craft being worked at a distance. Ward-lanterns. Warden steel. The particular harmonic of a boundary-stone compass held by someone who knew how to listen to it.

"They're here," she said, and was on her feet before the sentence ended.

* * *

They came out of the mist the way the guild taught its hunters to move: no sound, no shine, no warning. One moment the shrine stood empty in its silence, and the next there were four figures at the broken gate, gray-cloaked, ward-masks of pale porcelain hiding their faces, and the leader's lantern burned with the cold white light of a retrieval warrant.

Isandro knew the stance before she knew the woman. Feet planted at shoulder width, lantern held low and left, blade sheathed but thumb resting on the guard—Warden Third Rank, field retrieval posture, drilled into every initiate until it lived in the bones. She had held that stance herself. She had been proud of it.

"Isandro Delacroix." The voice through the mask was flattened, official, and underneath it—faint, like a thread pulled taut—familiar. "By order of the Guildmaster, you are recalled to Ashvale. You will surrender the items in your possession. You will come home. Resistance will be recorded as severance."

Severance. The guild's gentlest word for its harshest sentence: struck from the rolls, your name unpicked from every record, your family notified that you had died of a fever. Isandro had watched it done once, to a warden who had sold a ward-lantern to a smuggler. She had been fourteen. She had thought it fair.

"Corrin," she said. "Take off the mask."

The figure did not move. "Surrender the items."

"You trained me. You taught me to read the boundary stones. You held my hand the night I took my oath." She took one step forward, and Serah's hand closed on her shoulder—not restraining, just *present*, a weight like an anchor. "Look at me and tell me the valley is worth this." She gestured, one sweeping motion, at the bone orchards, the ash moss, the pewter river. "Tell me they deserved it."

"Isandro." Corrin's voice cracked, just barely, just enough. "I'm not here to argue theology. I'm here because if I come back without you, someone else comes who won't ask you to take off your boots first. Veyra is holding the guild together with her hands. There are wardens in that hall who want you *dead*, do you understand? Not retrieved. Dead. Come home. Say nothing. Be forgiven."

*Say nothing. All is forgiven.* The letter's words, wearing a different voice. Love, weaponized, offered a second time.

"No," Isandro said. It came out quiet. She made herself say it louder. "No. I'm sorry, Corrin. I'm so sorry. But no."

Corrin sighed, and the sigh was the signal.

The other three wardens moved. Not at Isandro—at *Tam*.

She understood a half-second too late, the way one understands a fall only after the ground is rising. They weren't here to negotiate with her at all. Corrin's speech had been the lantern held up for the moth. The real plan was the oldest one in the guild's book: *take what the target loves, and the target follows.* Tam had no ward-mask, no steel, no training beyond a poacher's quickness and a ferryman's balance. He was the softest thing in the shrine.

He was also, Isandro realized with a lurch, standing directly between the lead hunter and the altar where the shard lay open in its wrappings.

"TAM—"

The hunter's blade was out and moving. Tam twisted—good, quick, a poacher's twist, the kind that had gotten him out of a hundred snares in the valley woods—but the shrine floor was slick with ash-moss, and his foot slid, and the blade that should have grazed his shoulder went in under his ribs instead.

The sound he made was small. That was the worst of it. Not a cry. A small, surprised sound, like a man who has missed a stair in the dark.

Isandro did not remember crossing the shrine. She was simply *there*, on her knees in the ash-moss, her hands pressed against the wet heat blooming through his shirt, and the world had gone narrow and red at the edges. Somewhere Serah Vail was roaring—actually roaring, a sound from the old wars—and steel met steel in the gray light. Somewhere Corrin was shouting orders that no one was following anymore, because the plan had been *no blood, minimal force*, and plans like that die the moment they draw first red.

"Stay with me." Her voice came out in pieces. "Stay with me, you absolute fool, you were supposed to be the sensible one—"

"Technically," Tam said, and coughed, and the cough was worse than the wound, "you're the one who walked out of the valley. I just... followed."

"Shut up. Shut up, save your breath—"

"Isandro." His hand found her wrist. His grip was already weaker than it should have been, and that fact went through her like the blade had gone through him. "The shard. Don't let them—"

She looked up. One of the hunters had reached the altar. He had the shard in a ward-glove, and he was turning, moving for the gate with the flat efficient stride of a man completing an errand, and the green light of the covenant was guttering inside his fist like a lamp running out of oil.

Something in Isandro went very still and very cold.

* * *

There is a craft the valley teaches and forbids in the same breath. Every Warden learns it; no Warden may use it. They call it the Unbinding, and it is not a spell so much as a *refusal*—the art of reaching into a worked ward and unmaking the intention that holds it together. It is forbidden because it cannot be done halfway, because it scars the hand that does it, and because a guild built on wards cannot survive wardens who know how to unweave them. Isandro had been taught it by Corrin herself, years ago, in a lantern-lit cellar: *so you'll recognize it if it's ever used against you. Never use it. Promise me.* She had promised. She had been fourteen, and promises made at fourteen are made by people who do not yet know what they will one day be asked to survive.

She stood up.

She did not draw her blade. She walked toward the fleeing hunter the way she had once walked the boundary line at night: steady, unarmored, certain. The ash-moss parted around her boots. The gray light of the shrine bent toward her, and she let it.

"Put it down."

The hunter glanced back, saw an unarmed girl, and did the math wrong.

Isandro raised her right hand, palm open, and reached—not with her arm, but with the thing the guild had trained into her and then forbidden her to touch. She found the ward-glove's working the way a blind woman finds a thread's end: by feel, by *listening*. The retrieval ward was a knot of intention, tight and neat, guild-craft, textbook. She had tied a thousand of them. She knew exactly where the first loop lived.

She pulled.

The ward did not shatter. It *unhappened*. The glove went slack, the binding dissolved into the air like breath off a mirror, and the shard dropped—dropped, turned, and fell into Isandro's outstretched hand, and the green light leapt in it, flared, and *sang*, and every arch in the shrine rang like a struck bell.

The hunter stumbled back from her as if she were on fire. Through the porcelain mask, his breathing had gone ragged. "What—*what did you do*, that's forbidden, that's—"

"I know what it is," Isandro said, and her voice did not sound like her voice. It sounded older. It sounded like the stones. "I know what it costs. I'm paying it. Now *go*."

The green light was still climbing. The moss on the arches had begun, very faintly, to blush with color at its edges—the first green those stones had held in a century, and the wardens felt it the way one feels a change in pressure before a storm. Corrin stood in the gate with her lantern guttering, her mask tilted up at the arches, and Isandro saw the exact moment her old teacher understood: the valley's craft worked *better* out here. The covenant wanted to flow. It had always wanted to flow. They had only ever dammed it.

"Corrin," Isandro said. "Go home. Tell them what you saw. Tell them *all* of it."

For a long moment, the only sound was Tam's breathing—shallow, wet, too fast—and the low green hum of the shard in Isandro's hand.

Then Corrin sheathed her blade, unclipped her lantern, and set it on the shrine floor, still burning, a retrieval warrant abandoned like a snuffed candle. "The wound's deep," she said, her voice stripped of all its official flattening, just a woman's voice now, tired and afraid. "There's a healer in the hollow past the ford. Maren. Tell her Corrin owes her a winter's firewood." She turned. At the gate she paused, and did not look back. "I taught you the Unbinding so you'd recognize it," she said. "I should have known you'd recognize *yourself* in it."

Then they were gone, three gray shapes dissolving into the mist, and the shrine was silent again, and Isandro was on her knees in the moss with Tam's head in her lap and blood on her hands that was not going to stop.

* * *

Serah Vail tore the hem from her own cloak and packed the wound with the economy of someone who had done this on battlefields, in rain, by feel. "Ribs missed the blade. Barely. It's the bleeding." She looked up, and her eyes—gray as the river—held no comfort and no blame, which was somehow its own kind of mercy. "He needs Maren tonight, or he needs a grave by morning. Your choice which we aim for."

"Maren," Isandro said. "The ford. I heard."

"Then carry him. I'll carry the shard. And Delacroix—" Serah's hand closed over hers, brief and hard, "—what you did to that ward. I felt it from across the shrine. It felt like the old stories. It felt like *before*."

Isandro didn't answer. She hooked Tam's arm over her shoulders and lifted, and he groaned, and then, horribly, he laughed.

"You know what's funny," he whispered as they stumbled toward the gate, toward the ford, toward the mist. "Veyra sent me to watch you. First day out of the valley. First *real* day. And I'm already..."

"Don't finish that."

"...already the one who got stabbed," he finished, and grinned, gray-lipped, at the milk-white sky.

Isandro held him tighter and walked faster, and behind them the shrine's arches rang on in a note too low for hearing, and the ash-moss at their feet, in a line no wider than a footprint, had begun—very quietly, very stubbornly—to turn green.

She could not go back. She understood that now, wholly, the way one understands a burned bridge: not as a decision but as a fact. The Unbinding lived in her hands now. The wardens had seen her use it. The guild's law had a name for what she was, and it was not *daughter*, and it was not *warden*, and it was not *forgiven*.

But ahead, past the ford, a healer was lighting a lamp, and in her breast the second shard beat against the first like a second heartbeat, and somewhere beyond the gray orchards a third piece of an old promise waited in a vault she would have to walk into wearing her own name like a target.

"Hold on," she told Tam, not knowing if she meant him or herself. "Hold on. We're not done."

The mist took them, and the river forgot it had ever been blue, and somewhere far behind, in a valley that did not yet know its own history, the moon was already climbing toward its thirteenth turn.
