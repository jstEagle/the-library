## 15. What Tam Saw

The fever broke on the third night, and Tam woke to the smell of yarrow and woodsmoke and, beneath both, something sweeter—orchard blossoms, though the orchards here were gray as old bone. He lay still for a long while, cataloguing his body the way a man counts coins after a robbery. Ribs: intact. The wound in his side: a hot, dull argument he could feel through the poultice. His hands: shaking, but his.

He was in a low room with a turf roof, somewhere on the edge of Sable Hollow. Rain worked patiently at the thatch. A single candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and by its light he could see Isandro asleep in a chair beside him, her head tipped against the wall, her mouth open slightly, one hand still curled around the hilt of a knife she'd clearly fallen asleep holding.

She had not left his side in three days. He knew this because he had drifted in and out of the fever enough times to see her there each time, always in the same chair, always with that knife, always watching the door more than she watched him.

*She trusts you,* said the part of him that had been screaming since the shrine.

*She doesn't know you,* answered the other part, quieter and much older.

Tam looked at the ceiling beams for a long time. Then, because there was no way to do this that wouldn't be terrible, and no terrible way that would get easier with waiting, he reached over and touched her wrist.

"Isandro."

She came awake the way wardens were trained to come awake—all at once, silently, knife already angled. For half a heartbeat the point hovered an inch from his throat, and then her eyes cleared and she exhaled and lowered it, and the whole sequence took less time than a sneeze.

"You're feverish," she said. "You were talking."

"I was?"

"To people who weren't there." She poured water from a clay jug into a cup and held it out, and her hand was steady even though her eyes were rimmed red from three nights of not sleeping. "You apologized to most of them."

"Some of them deserved it," Tam said, and drank, and found he couldn't put the cup down afterward because his hands wanted something to hold onto. "Isandro. There are things I have to tell you. While I've got the fever as an excuse to look awful doing it."

She sat back down in the chair. She didn't say *whatever it is, it can wait.* She didn't say *you nearly died for me.* She just waited, which was worse than either, because Isandro Delacroix had learned patience in a vault full of documents nobody read, and she could wait like stone waits.

So he told her.

* * *

"It started before we ever went to the stones," he said. "Before I asked to come along. You remember the night you touched the boundary stone—you told me about it the next morning, or you tried to, walking around it like a man circling a hole in the floor. You thought you'd hidden how shaken you were. You hadn't. I'd known you nine years, Isandro. I know what your face does when the ground moves."

"I remember," she said carefully. "You followed me out. You asked to come."

"That's what it looked like from where you were standing." His mouth was dry again. He took another drink just to have somewhere to put the truth before it came out. "Two days before that, Veyra sent for me. Private. Not the guild hall—her own quarters, with the shutters closed. She'd seen the vault ledger. She knew someone had pulled the prophecy book, and she knew it was you, because there are exactly four people alive who can open that case and three of them were accounted for."

Isandro had gone very still. Not angry yet. Stiller than anger—the stillness of a woman checking every lock on a door she'd thought was already sealed.

"She didn't punish me," Tam went on. "That should have been my first warning. Veyra doesn't forgive; she repurposes. She said—" and here he made himself repeat it, word for word, because anything less felt like cheating, "she said, *the girl is going to do something foolish, and when she does, I need someone she loves standing close enough to bring her home.*"

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere outside, a shutter banged twice against its frame.

"She didn't say *stop her,*" Isandro said at last. Her voice was flat, level, terrible in its mildness. "She said *bring her home.*"

"There's a difference," Tam said. "I asked her about it. Straight out. I said, Guildmaster, if she crosses, do you want me to drag her back by the collar? And she looked at me for a long time, and then she said, *No. Just stay close. People walk farther when they're alone.*" He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I thought—I told myself it was mercy. That she was sending me as a rope, not a leash. Nine years, Isandro, I told myself stories about that woman right up until the wardens put steel in my ribs at the shrine."

"And the second shard," Isandro said quietly. "When they came for us. When Serah's men circled us among the memorial stones. Did you signal them? Was there a sign you were supposed to give?"

"No." He met her eyes and held them, because flinching now would be the same as lying. "I swear it on whatever's left that matters. By then I'd already chosen. I chose at the boundary stones, the moment I stepped past them and the world turned on like a lamp. But I won't insult you by pretending the choice undoes the order. I was sent to watch you. I watched you. Everything I learned on the road, everything you trusted me with—the shards, Ondine, the ferryman's oath—I carried all of it inside the shape of a task Veyra gave me. If she'd recalled me after the shrine instead of trying to kill us, I don't know what I'd have done. I'd like to think I'd have refused. I'd like to think a lot of things."

He set the cup down. His hands were steadier now, which struck him as unfair; the trembling ought to have lasted through the worst of it.

"The wound wasn't in the orders," he said. "That part was mine. All mine."

* * *

Isandro did not speak for a long time.

Tam had braced for many things—for fury, for the knife coming back up, for her to simply stand and walk out into the rain and leave him to heal or not as the hollow saw fit. What he had not braced for was watching her *think*, openly, methodically, the way she read a document: top line to bottom line, then again, slower, hunting for the forgery between the lines.

"Say it," he whispered finally. "Whatever it is. I've had a blade in me for three days; I can take one more."

"Hush." But there was no edge in it. She leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and looked at her hands. "I'm trying to work out whether it changes anything, and if so what, and I keep arriving at answers I don't trust because I want them to be true. So let me ask questions instead, and you answer them the same way you just did. Badly. Honestly. Can you do that?"

"I'm very good at badly."

"When Veyra gave you the order—did she tell you why? Not the reason she gave. The real one."

Tam considered. "She told me the valley would break without you in it. That's what she said. *The valley will break.* Not the covenant. Not the realms. The valley. Like she was talking about a family."

"Did you believe her?"

"I believed she believed it." He rubbed at his face. "Isandro, I've been thinking about this since the shrine, when I had nothing to do but bleed and review my life. Here's what I keep landing on. Veyra isn't lying to us. That's the horror of her. Every order she gives, she believes she's saving someone—maybe the wrong someones, maybe fewer than she thinks, but she believes it down to the marrow. She sent me after you the way you'd send someone after a sleepwalker near a cliff edge. From inside her head, it was kindness. It was probably *actual* kindness. It just happened to be kindness aimed at keeping a lie alive."

"A kind lie with a body count," Isandro said. "The outer realm has been starving for a hundred years so that our kindness could stay comfortable."

"Yes."

"And you sat in her quarters, heard all of it, took the order—and didn't tell me. Not when I showed you the flash from the stone. Not when we crossed. Not once on the road. Why?" Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing. It stayed exactly level, a surveyor's voice measuring the depth of a drop. "Not for safety. I'd have understood safety. You never once said *Tam, why are you really here?* unprompted. So why carry it?"

"Because telling you felt like choosing," he said, "and I hadn't finished choosing." The words came out of him raw, unpolished, the way confessions do when they've been rehearsed too long and then abandoned. "Every day I waited was another day the choice got easier and more dishonest at the same time. At the stones it was easy—magic hit me and I cried like a child and I thought, *there, done, I'm yours.* At Sable Hollow, seeing the gray orchards, easy. But then the ambush, and blood everywhere, and I realized I'd been building this beautiful story where I was a hero who defected for love of the truth, when actually I was a spy who fell in love with the mission and kept postponing the paperwork." He gestured weakly at his bandaged side. "This is the paperwork."

Something moved at the corner of Isandro's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"You're saying you confessed because it was tactically necessary," she said.

"I'm saying I confessed because if I died with it in me, it stops being my failure and becomes your wound, and you've got enough wounds." He swallowed. "And because you're about to do something enormous, Isandro. The reforging. Whatever price it costs—and Ondine told me enough before we left that I can guess the shape of it—you cannot walk into that carrying a doubt about the person guarding your back. Doubt gets people killed at altars. So. Ask me anything. Test me any way you want. I'll pass or fail, but I'll do it clean."

* * *

She stood. She crossed to the narrow window and pushed the shutter open, letting in the gray rain-light and the smell of wet ash and, faintly, absurdly, those ghost-blossoms. She stood there with her back to him for the length of ten heartbeats, and Tam used the time to notice things: that she was thinner than she'd been a month ago; that she wore her mother's ring on a cord around her neck now instead of her finger, the way people wear things they aren't sure they'll be allowed to keep; that her shadow in the window light looked less like a warden's shadow every day and more like something the old books would have had a name for.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," she said, without turning. "Everyone in this story has used love as an instrument. Veyra used yours. The founders used an entire valley's. Even the covenant itself—it's a bargain, isn't it? Love with terms attached." She turned around. "And now you're handing me yours and telling me it started as an instrument too. How am I supposed to sort the instrument from the rest of it?"

"I don't know," Tam said. "Genuinely. I've been trying to find the seam and there isn't one. It's all one piece. The order and the choice grew together, like—" he groped— "like a tree growing through a fence. You can't pull the fence out without killing the tree. Maybe you just... build the rest of the orchard around it."

"That is the worst metaphor I have ever heard."

"I'm feverish."

"You're recovering, which is worse." But she came back to the chair, and she sat on its arm this time rather than in it, closer, and looked down at him with an expression he'd only seen her wear twice before: once at the ferryman's oath, and once reading her grandmother's name in a blind woman's ledger. The expression of a woman deciding what something was going to mean.

"So here's where I land," she said. "I can't verify your heart. Nobody can. I could spend the rest of the journey testing you and all I'd prove is that I don't trust anyone, which is Veyra's disease, and I am not catching it. So I'm not going to test you." She held up a hand before he could speak. "Instead I'm going to give you something better than trust. I'm going to give you *use*. From tonight, you're not my guard and you're not her eyes. You're my witness. Everything you saw—from Veyra's quarters to the shrine to this bed—you write it down, every detail, names and dates and the exact words of her orders, and it goes into the record with the ferryman's oath and the knight's memorial. When the truth is told publicly in Ashvale—if we live to tell it—your testimony stands next to mine. Including the parts that shame you." Her eyes were bright and hard and dry. "*Especially* the parts that shame you. Because a witness who hides his own guilt poisons the whole testimony, and I need this clean, Tam. Cleaner than the guild ever was. Do you understand what I'm asking?"

He understood. She was not forgiving him. She was conscripting him—into honesty, into consequence, into a loyalty that had terms but honest ones, stated aloud, the way the first heir had demanded the truth be sworn. It was, he realized dimly, the only form of forgiveness Isandro Delacroix currently had room for: not absolution, but *purpose*.

"Yes," he said.

"Then start now. Before the fever comes back and blurs it. There's ink in my pack and a board for a desk." She fetched them, propped the board across his lap, and set the ink pot in his careful hands, and only then did her voice soften by a single degree. "For what it's worth—when you stepped past the stones with me, knowing nothing except that I was frightened and the sky on the other side might kill us—that part was real. I believe that part completely. The rest we'll build on top of it. That's how covenants work, apparently. You don't start clean. You start *chosen*."

Tam bent his head over the page. His hand shook—not from the fever now—and he wrote the date, and then *On the matter of Guildmaster Veyra's orders to Tam Wicker, given in confidence two days before the crossing of the boundary stones*, and kept writing, line after line, while the rain kept its patient time on the roof and Isandro watched the door.

He was halfway down the second page when she stiffened.

It was small—the barest change in her breathing—but nine years is nine years, and he looked up. She was staring at the window, at the gray curtain of rain, where a light had appeared. One lantern, far off across the hollow, bobbing. Then a second behind it. Then a third, moving in the spaced, unhurried cadence of trained men searching a perimeter.

Wardens' lanterns. Coming down out of the hills toward Sable Hollow—and between the hollow and the boundary stones, there was nothing else they could be looking for.

"Isandro," Tam said quietly, setting down the pen. "How much time do we have?"
