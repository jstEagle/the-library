## 16. The Third Keeper

The map of Ashvale had not changed in her absence. That was the first cruelty of it.

Isandro stood on the ridge above the valley with the wind pulling at her cloak and the whole of home spread out below her like a thing she might have dreamed: the terraced orchards going amber with early frost, the slate roofs of Warden's Row, the guild hall's bell tower catching the last of the afternoon light so that it seemed lit from within, a lantern someone had forgotten to snuff. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, orderly columns. Somewhere down there bread was being pulled from ovens; somewhere the evening bell would ring soon, and children would be called in from the lanes, and wardens would light the boundary lamps one by one along the perimeter she had once walked as a matter of sacred routine.

She had crossed half a realm to stand here, and now that she had arrived, her body refused the last mile. Her feet simply would not carry her forward. She stood at the lip of the valley the way a swimmer stands at the edge of winter water, knowing exactly how cold it will be and diving anyway, eventually, but not yet.

*You're being a coward,* said the part of her that sounded like Ondine, patient and blind and unfooled. *You knew where it was all along.*

She had known. That was the worst of it. When Ondine had laid out the three shards on her table in Sable Hollow—the river-keeper's shard wrapped in oilcloth, the knight's shard still smelling faintly of funeral smoke—and told them the third had never left Ashvale, never left the vault itself, some cold part of Isandro had understood before the archivist finished speaking. Of course it was there. The founders who broke the covenant had kept its broken heart under their own floor. They had built their guild hall on top of their crime and called the vault a place of records. It was a reliquary. It had always been a reliquary.

"They hid the third shard in plain sight," Tam had said, when they'd made the plan by firelight. "Under guard, sure, but also under *belief*. No warden would steal from the vault because no warden believes there's anything in it worth stealing. It's the safest place in the valley precisely because everyone trusts it."

"And the only way in is the front door," Isandro had said, and heard her own voice go flat.

Now she heard it again in memory, and shivered, though the wind off the ridge was mild. There were other ways in—she'd spent three nights mapping them in her head, the drainage culvert below the east wing, the gap in the armory wall she'd discovered at fourteen and never reported—but every secret entrance ended the same way: with her sneaking into her own home like a thief. With her confirming, in the only language the guild understood, that everything Veyra believed about traitors was true.

No. If she was going to do this, she would do it in daylight. She would walk through the front gate with her head up and let them see her coming. Let them decide what she was.

Tam crouched beside her in the bracken, chewing a stem of something he'd picked and immediately regretted. He looked terrible. The wound at his shoulder had closed but the healing was slow, magic-fed and stubborn, and he favored his left side in a way he probably thought disguised itself. He followed her gaze down into the valley.

"There it is," he said. "Smaller than I remembered."

"You've been gone two months."

"Feels like ten years." He spat the stem out. "It looks the same. That's the strange part. Everything we learned out there—all those gray orchards, those shrines with nobody tending them—and this place just kept looking like this. Like nothing outside it could touch it." He paused. "Because we made sure of it. Because *they* made sure of it."

"We didn't know," Isandro said. She had said it so many times now that it had worn smooth as river glass, and it comforted her less each time, because Ondine had answered it once, gently and forever: *Not knowing was the luxury your founders purchased for you. Someone paid for it. You are only now meeting the bill.*

"I know," Tam said. "I'm not arguing. I'm just saying it's strange, looking down at it. I used to think the valley was the world and everything past the stones was weather." He glanced at her. "Your hands are shaking."

She looked down. They were. She folded them into her sleeves. "I keep thinking about the kitchen. Marta will be making the evening loaves. She'll have flour on her forearms to the elbow. And I keep thinking—I'll walk past that kitchen window and she'll see me, and for half a second she'll smile, because for half a second she won't remember that I'm the one who ran. And then she will remember. And that half-second is going to be worse than any cell they put me in."

"Marta used to slip you the burnt ends of the loaf," Tam said quietly. "When we were nine. You'd eat them behind the well house so the others wouldn't want any."

"She'd burn them on purpose sometimes. So there'd be ends to give me." Isandro pressed her knuckles against her mouth. "That's what I can't get around, Tam. Not Veyra, not the trial I'm probably walking into. Marta's burnt bread. Everyone in that valley fed me something. And I'm coming back to take the floor out from under all of them."

"No," Tam said. "You're coming back to tell them what the floor was made of. There's a difference. It doesn't feel like a difference. But it is."

They came down off the ridge as the light went long and gold, and Isandro made herself look at everything, because some animal instinct told her she was seeing it for the last time as the person she had been. The millpond with its skin of late leaves. The shrine at the crossroads where travelers touched the stone for luck—not knowing, none of them knowing, that the luck had been bought. The schoolyard fence, mended crooked since the year of the flood. Each familiar thing struck her now like a small bell, ringing with a note of grief she hadn't expected: *homesickness*, yes, but homesickness for a country that had turned out to be a locked room. She missed it and she mourned it simultaneously, and both feelings lived in her chest like birds in the same cage, pecking at each other.

At the guild gate the sentry was a boy named Perrin, fifteen at most, whom she had helped train with the shortstaff two winters ago. He saw her and his face did exactly what Marta's would have done—the brightening, the half-second—and then his hand went to his sword belt and stopped there, uncertain, and his face went carefully blank in a way that was somehow more painful than hostility would have been.

"Warden Delacroix," he said. The rank was an offering and an accusation at once.

"Perrin." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "I need to speak with the Guildmaster."

"She said—" He stopped, swallowed. "She said you'd come. She said if you came through the gate, I was to let you pass and send word ahead. She said—" His young face struggled. "She said you'd come through the front door or not at all, and either way to treat you with respect."

Of course she had. Veyra had always been three moves ahead; it was what made her a good guildmaster and what had made her so hard to leave. Isandro nodded and walked through the gate into the courtyard she had crossed ten thousand times, and her boots rang on the flagstones, and the sound of it went through her like grief through a house.

* *

They gave her a room instead of a cell. She understood the message in that too: you are not a prisoner until you make yourself one. The room was the guest quarters on the third floor, overlooking the training yard, four doors down from the room she'd slept in since childhood. Someone had lit the lamp and left tea steeping on the sill, and a plate of food—bread with the burnt end attached, deliberately, unmistakably, Marta's handwriting in crust—and Isandro sat on the edge of the narrow bed and ate it with tears running silently down her face, tasting home and betrayal in the same mouthful.

The summons came at full dark. A junior warden she didn't recognize knocked, wouldn't meet her eyes, and led her down through corridors hung with the portraits of founders whose names she had memorized at eight years old and whose crime she now carried in her satchel in two pieces of oath-stone wrapped in oilcloth. She passed the archive where she'd spent her girlhood among the ledgers. She passed the door of the vault itself, and felt the shards in her bag grow warm against her hip, answering something below the flagstones, a low harmonic hum like a struck bell still ringing under the earth.

Veyra was waiting in her study, standing at the window with her back turned, and for a moment Isandro simply looked at her—the straight spine, the silver-streaked braid, the guildmaster's chain resting on her shoulders like a yoke she had long ago stopped noticing—and felt the old reflexive love rise in her throat, fierce and stupid, before she strangled it with the truth.

"You look thinner," Veyra said, without turning. "They don't feed you properly out there."

"They feed us fine. The ferryman keeps excellent bees."

"The ferryman." Veyra turned then. Her face was composed, kind, and terribly tired. "Alder. Yes. I wondered which way you'd go first." She gestured to the chair across from her desk, and when Isandro didn't move, she sighed and sat herself, folding her hands. "Sit, Isandro. Please. I'm not going to have you seized. I could have had you seized at the gate, at the ridge, at the border stones two months ago—I chose not to, and you've never once asked yourself why."

"I've asked myself little else."

"Then ask it now." Veyra leaned forward. In the lamplight her eyes were steady and sad. "Why am I letting you sit in my study? Why haven't the loyalists dragged you to the cells? Think, child. Use that mind the archivists always praised."

Isandro sat slowly. "Because you want something."

"I want everything," Veyra said. "I want the valley safe. I want the realm beyond the stones to stop dying—I have never wanted otherwise, whatever you've been told about me out there, whatever version of me Alder or Ser Brandt has painted. I want my wardens to stop splitting into factions and whispering in stairwells like children at a funeral. And I want you home." Her voice caught, very slightly, on the last word, and Isandro hated how much it cost her not to believe in it. "So. Tell me what you've found. All of it. I promise you I already know most of it, but I want to hear you say it, because I want to know what you believe."

So Isandro told her. She told it plainly, without performance: the covenant paused rather than broken, the seal masquerading as protection, the withered orchards and the flinching refugees, the memorial wall with its thousands of names, the grandmother erased from every record, the price the founders had refused to pay. She watched Veyra's face the whole time and saw nothing she could use—no flinch, no guilt, only a deepening stillness, like a lake going quiet before ice.

When she finished, Veyra was silent a long moment. Then she opened the drawer of her desk.

Isandro's hand went to her knife before she could stop it, and Veyra saw, and something flickered across her face—not hurt, exactly. Recognition. Grief taking inventory of itself.

She drew out a cloth-wrapped shape and set it on the desk between them, and unwrapped it, and Isandro's breath stopped.

The third shard was smaller than the other two, and older-looking, worn smooth as a worry-stone, as if it had been handled by generations of hands that needed holding onto something. Even across the desk Isandro could feel it humming against the shards in her satchel, a chord missing its final note.

"You're giving it to me," Isandro said flatly. "Just like that."

"I'm *offering* it," Veyra said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Sit and listen to me, Isandro, because I am going to say things I have never said aloud to anyone, and then I am going to let you choose. That's more than the founders ever gave anyone. More than the covenant gives anyone. Choice is the one currency this guild has never honored, so consider this my first payment."

And she told her. About crossing the stones at nineteen, full of the same certainty, the same righteous fire. About the gray villages and the empty shrines and a woman in a dying orchard who had begged her not to bring back magic but only news—that even news was more than they got. About coming home and reporting everything to the guildmaster of that day, and being told that the council had weighed it all generations ago, and that mercy for the many required abandonment of the few, and that the ledger balanced, and that she should be proud of the valley's peace because she had seen its price. About the decades of telling herself the ledger balanced. About the nights she took out the third shard—it had passed to each guildmaster in turn, an inheritance dressed up as a duty—and held it and wondered what it would cost to set things right, and calculated the cost, and always, always found it too high.

"Because I know what reforging asks," Veyra said quietly. "Ondine told you. Memory. The life you bind it to. Whoever speaks the covenant whole pays with everything they love about the world they're saving." She looked at Isandro with an expression stripped of office, of rank, of everything but a woman's naked fear. "I have done the arithmetic every year for thirty years, child. Every time, the answer comes out the same: I cannot pay it. I am not strong enough. I love this valley too much—its kitchens and its bells and its crooked fences, the smell of Marta's bread, the sound of trainees laughing in the yard. Take all that away and what remains? Nothing I recognize. Nothing I'd fight for. So I chose the valley over justice, every single year, and I told myself it was virtue." She pushed the shard an inch closer. "I am offering you the third piece not because I've changed my mind. I haven't. I still believe the realms must stay divided, that your quest will drown us all in a flood we can't control. I'm offering it because I looked at you tonight and saw myself at nineteen, and I thought: perhaps she'll do the arithmetic honestly, and stop, the way I stopped. Perhaps she'll take the shard, feel its weight, and understand why every guildmaster for a hundred years chose the valley."

"And if I don't stop?"

"Then I will stand in your way," Veyra said simply. "With every resource this guild commands. And I will grieve you, Isandro, for the rest of my life, and I will not apologize for it. Both of those things are true at once. That is what it is to be old."

Isandro reached out and picked up the shard.

Its warmth climbed her arm like sunlight through cold water, and in her satchel the other two pieces sang, and for one vertiginous heartbeat she felt the whole covenant trembling toward wholeness, a bridge with all its stones cut and waiting. She closed her hand around it and looked at Veyra—at the woman who had trained her, promoted her, defended her, lied to her, and loved her in ways too tangled ever to sort into honest and false—and she understood suddenly that this offer was the truest test Veyra could devise, and also the truest gift: the choice, freely given, that no one in this valley had been granted in a hundred years.

"Thank you," Isandro said, and meant it, and hated meaning it. "I'll take the arithmetic from here."

Veyra closed her eyes briefly, as if a blow had landed exactly where she'd predicted. "Then you'll want to know one more thing before you go," she said. "The loyalist faction moved against me today while you sat eating Marta's bread upstairs. Senior Warden Corvane has taken command of the night watch. They believe the shard should never have been offered, and they're right that I've gone soft, and they are coming for it—to secure it, they'd say, though Corvane's ambition has always smelled of more than security." She rose, and her hand rested for a moment on the guildmaster's chain, and then, deliberately, she lifted it over her head and set it down on the desk beside the empty cloth, like a woman setting down a tool she would not need again. "You have perhaps an hour before they find the courage to come to this floor. I suggest you spend it well."

Down in the vault beneath their feet, faint as a heartbeat, the broken covenant sang—and somewhere above, boots were already moving on the stairs.
