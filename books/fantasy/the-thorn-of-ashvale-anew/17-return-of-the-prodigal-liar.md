## 17. Return of the Prodigal Liar

The gates of Ashvale had not grown smaller in her absence. That was the first cruelty of homecoming: everything exactly as she had left it, as if the valley itself refused to change even one stone in her memory, so that when she lost it she would lose all of it at once.

Isandro stood before the Warden hall with the dusk coming down gold behind her and Tam a half-step to her left, his bandaged arm cradled against his chest like something breakable. The third shard waited inside those walls—she was certain of it now, as certain as she had been of the date in the prophecy book. Veyra would have moved it somewhere obvious. Somewhere Isandro could find it. The guildmaster did nothing without intention, and inviting your prodigal liar home through the front door was the most intentional thing of all.

"They'll take it from us," Tam said quietly. "The moment we're inside."

"I know."

"Then why are we walking through the door?"

"Because she asked me to." Isandro touched the folded letter in her coat pocket—the second letter, the one that had come by smuggler's hand two nights past, written in Veyra's careful, upright script. *Come home. Bring your friend. You will be received with honor.* And beneath that, in smaller strokes, almost an afterthought and therefore certainly not one: *Bring me nothing you cannot afford to lose.*

"She's offering us safe passage," Tam said. "That word *honor* is doing a lot of work."

"It always does in Ashvale." She started up the steps. "Come on. If I'm going to ruin my life, I'd rather do it where the light is good."

* * *

The guild hall smelled the way it always had: lamp oil, beeswax on old oak, the faint mineral tang of the ward-stones set into the floor every nine feet in a pattern Isandro had walked blindfolded as a child during training drills. She knew this room the way she knew her own hands. The long trestle tables. The hearth big enough to roast an ox. The gallery above where the senior wardens sat in judgment, their faces shadowed by design so that no petitioner could read them.

Tonight the gallery was full. Every warden still loyal enough to answer a summons—and some, she noticed, who should not have come at all. Old Maren Corvane, who had taught her the first forms of the blade. The twins Aldous and Petra, barely out of their novitiate. And along one wall, standing rather than seated, perhaps a dozen wardens whose faces she could not read but whose presence felt less like judgment than like witness.

Veyra stood alone at the head of the hall, before the great carved seal of the Ashvale Wardens: a thorned branch encircling a closed eye.

"You came through the gate," Veyra said. Her voice carried the way it always carried, level and warm at once, a voice built for telling frightened people they were safe. "I confess I gave odds against it."

"You wrote asking me to."

"I did." The guildmaster descended the three steps of the dais slowly, hands clasped behind her back, and stopped ten feet away—the distance of respect between armed opponents, or of courtesy between family. Up close she looked older than Isandro remembered. Not weaker. Older the way river stone is old: worn smooth by something with enormous patience. "You look well, child. The outer air agrees with you."

"The outer air is dying, Guildmaster. It doesn't have the strength to disagree with anyone."

A ripple through the gallery. Someone shifted; a chair creaked. Veyra didn't glance at them. "And yet here you stand, whole, unmarked, wearing valley steel you've kept polished every single day since you left. I know the habit. I wore the same habit once, on the same side of the stones."

"Then you remember what's out there."

"I remember everything that's out there." For a moment—half a heartbeat, no more—something moved behind Veyra's eyes that was not the guildmaster at all but a girl, young, crossing a border into gray orchards and silent shrines. Then it was gone, filed away, and the guildmaster looked at Isandro again with terrible kindness. "Which is why I'm going to make you an offer, Isandro Delacroix, and I want every soul in this room to witness that I made it fairly."

She extended her hand. In her palm lay a shard of pale stone no bigger than a knucklebone, veined with faint silver light, humming so softly Isandro felt it in her teeth before she saw it. The third shard. Carried out into the open hall like a gift.

Tam's breath caught beside her.

"This is what you crossed the valley to find," Veyra said. "What you bled for in Sable Hollow. What your friend took a blade for at the shrine—I know about the shrine, Isandro; I sent the men who caused it, and I have already disciplined them for exceeding their orders, which is not the same as apologizing, because I am not sorry, and we will come back to that." She held the shard steady. "It is yours. Freely given. No conditions written down, no oaths demanded. Walk out of this hall with it tonight and no hand in Ashvale will bar your way."

The silence was absolute. Even the fire seemed to hold its crackling.

"And the price?" Isandro asked.

"There is no price. There is only a truth, which I will speak plainly so that everyone hears it plainly." Veyra's voice did not rise; it settled, like snowfall. "Take the third shard. Take it to the boundary stones. Join your three pieces and speak your covenant whole if you can. But understand what you will be doing. The founders of this guild faced the same choice you face now, with fuller knowledge than you have, and they chose division. Not from cowardice. From arithmetic. They stood where you will stand, and they learned what the covenant demands, and they decided that one realm's slow grief was a price worth paying for another realm's life. They were wrong about many things. They may have been wrong about that. But they were not liars, Isandro, and neither am I." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice—not to hide it, but to make the hall lean in to hear. "So here is my offer, freely made and freely witnessed. Give me the two shards you carry. Renounce the quest. Let the realms stay divided, let the valley stay alive, and I will give you the third piece anyway—to keep, to bury, to throw in the river—so that no one else can ever do what you're about to do. You keep your memories. You keep your name. You keep your home. You keep Tam."

There it was. Not the threat Isandro had braced for. Worse. A door held open, with everything she loved visible through it, warm and lit.

She thought of the hall as it had been before she left: the drills at dawn, Maren laughing at her footwork, the vault's dry dust, the small certain shape of a life that fit her like a glove fits a hand. She thought of Veyra teaching her letters. Of being eleven years old and feverish and waking to find the guildmaster asleep in a chair beside her bed because no one else had thought to sit there.

And she thought of Ondine's blind eyes turning toward her in Sable Hollow. *Your grandmother crossed over last, girl. They cut her out of every record like a bad tooth. They cut her out of you.*

"If I refuse," Isandro said carefully, "will you take them from me?"

"No." Veyra said it simply. "Because I don't need to. Because half the wardens in this gallery watched you grow up, and when they see what your covenant costs—who it costs—they will stop you themselves. Or they won't. Either way, I will have told the truth, and you will have chosen with your eyes open. That is all I have ever asked of anyone." She turned the shard in her fingers so its light caught the rafters. "But you must choose now, and choose aloud."

Isandro looked at Tam. He was white around the mouth, his good hand gripping the edge of his sling, and she knew he was thinking the same thing she was: that Veyra had built this trap out of love, which was the only material strong enough to hold either of them.

She stepped forward—and did not reach for the shard.

* * *

"Guildmaster." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Before I answer, I want to use my right as a daughter of this house. Floor rights. Testimony."

Veyra's brows drew together—a flicker, quickly mastered. "Floor rights are for members in good standing."

"Then discipline me after. Suspend my rights retroactively. But you taught me the law yourself, Veyra: any warden facing a binding choice may speak testimony before the choice binds. You're offering me a binding choice." She turned to face the gallery, to the shadowed faces above. "You all heard her offer. You're all witnesses that I invoked before I answered."

Someone in the gallery—an old voice, Maren's, dry as autumn leaves—said, "She has it right. By the book, Guildmaster. Your own book."

Something passed across Veyra's face that might, in a less controlled woman, have been admiration.

"Speak, then," the guildmaster said quietly.

Isandro walked to the center of the hall, beneath the carved seal, where generations of petitioners had stood. She made herself breathe. She had rehearsed nothing. She had only a list, and she had carried it across a dead country, and every name on it weighed more than the shards in her coat.

"When I was a novitiate," she began, "we memorized the First Lesson. *Beyond the stones lies nothing. Beyond the stones lies waste.* We said it until it stopped sounding like words. I said it for twenty-two years. I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you—without ever thinking to look underneath." She let the silence sit. "I have looked underneath now. I have walked the waste. So before I tell you whether I accept the guildmaster's offer, I'm going to tell you what the waste looks like. Because the founders chose it for you while you slept, and Veyra is choosing it for you now while you listen, and none of you has ever been permitted to see the bill."

"Isandro—" Veyra started.

"You invoked fair dealing, Guildmaster. This is my dealing. Hear me or strike me down, but hear me."

The gallery had gone very still.

"In the outer realm," Isandro said, "the orchards are gray. Not bare—gray. The fruit comes in colorless and falls colorless and rots colorless, and the people eat it anyway because there is nothing else. The shrines have been silent so long that the children born under them think silence is what holiness sounds like. There is a village called Lowmarsh where they burned their own shrine timbers last winter for warmth, and the oldest man in the village wept—not for the wood. For the fact that no one younger than him remembered why it had mattered." Her voice cracked; she let it. "There are refugees on the roads who flinch when they see valley steel. Do you understand? They flinch. They know what our blades are for. Their grandparents knew. Somewhere in their songs, there is a verse about the day the wardens sealed the stones, and the verse is not kind to us, and they are right not to be kind."

She reached into her coat and drew out a folded page, soft at the creases from handling, and unfolded it with both hands.

"At Sable Hollow, Ondine the archivist keeps a ledger of the dying. Not the dead—the dying. It takes too long to die there to keep count of the finished. I copied the names she would give me. There are more. There are so many more that she stopped writing them down years ago because ink was needed for other things." She lifted the page. "The guildmaster asks me to choose with my eyes open. Very well. Then everyone in this room chooses with their eyes open too. These are the names. I'm going to read them, and no one is going to stop me, because stopping me would prove everything I've said."

"Isandro Delacroix." Veyra's voice had gone quiet and cold. "You are reading a list of strangers to people who owe you nothing—"

"I'm reading a list of people who owed us nothing," Isandro said, "and paid anyway. That's what a covenant broken looks like from the outside, Guildmaster. It looks like strangers paying your debts." And she began to read.

"Bren of Lowmarsh. Ferrymaster on the Sable River, forty-one years at the rope. Died holding the ferry line so others could cross ahead of him."

"Sela of Lowmarsh. His daughter. Kept the shrine lantern lit for nineteen years after the flame went gray, because she said a promise is a promise even when the god can't see it."

"Ottrey. Nine years old. Born gray-lunged, as one child in four is born now. He asked his mother whether the sky had ever been blue. She said yes. He said he wished she hadn't told him."

Her voice held. It surprised her. Around her the hall had become a held breath; she could feel the wardens leaning forward despite themselves, feel the names landing in them like stones dropped into wells they had believed empty.

"Maren Haleth, Warden of the outer watch, last of the line that stayed behind when the stones were sealed. Starved at her post rather than abandon it. She left a message for the guild. Ondine kept it. It says: *Tell them we kept faith. Tell them someone should have.*"

"Yeva of the Thornfields. Midwife. Forty years bringing children into a world that forgot how to feed them."

"Aldric Vane." She paused, because this one cost her. "Knight of the Broken Order. Keeper of the second shard. He buried two hundred of his people in the Year of Gray Frost, and dug every grave himself, and stands watch over them still, and hates us, and has earned the right to hate us down to the marrow. When I stood witness at his memorial, he asked me one question. He asked: *Does the valley even know we exist?*"

She lowered the page.

"That was the question I couldn't answer. That is the question I have brought home." She turned to face Veyra fully. "So here is my answer to your offer, Guildmaster. No. Not with anger—you've seen no anger in me tonight, and there is none. With testimony. You offered to let the realms stay divided so long as I never say their names aloud. But I have said them. In front of your witnesses, in your hall, under your seal. Bren. Sela. Ottrey. Maren Haleth. Yeva. Aldric Vane. Say them back to yourself tonight, Veyra, and tell me honestly that division is still arithmetic. Tell me these were numbers."

Veyra stood motionless at the head of the hall. Her face was the face of a woman watching a dam develop its first hairline crack, knowing the water behind it was fifty years deep.

"The covenant demands a price no one in this room has agreed to pay," she said at last, softly. "You know that. Ondine told you. Whoever reforges it pays with their own memories—their whole life bound to the rite. The founders wouldn't pay. I won't ask anyone else to pay. That is not cowardice, Isandro. That is the only mercy I have left."

"Then ask," Isandro said. "Ask openly, in the light, and let people decide for themselves. That is all I have ever wanted. Not forgiveness. Not even victory. Just the question, spoken aloud, where everyone can hear it: *Who will pay? Who believes it's worth paying?* The founders never asked. You never asked. You decided for us, generation after generation, and called the deciding protection." She held Veyra's gaze. "I am going to the boundary stones, Guildmaster. I am going to pay whatever it costs. And when it's done—if anything of me is left to know it—the valley will finally get to choose its own future. Even if I won't be in it."

For a long moment, nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere above, a chair creaked as a warden rose halfway and sat back down.

Then Veyra closed her hand around the third shard.

"Guards," she said, and her voice was steady, and her eyes were bright with something that was absolutely not triumph. "Seal the doors. No one leaves this hall."

Tam's hand found Isandro's sleeve. Above them, along the gallery, a dozen wardens were rising to their feet—not the ones Veyra had summoned, but the others, the witnesses, Maren Corvane among them, and old Maren's voice rang off the rafters like a struck bell:

"Guildmaster, by the book you wrote—no member may be detained after lawful testimony. She spoke true things. Every soul here knows they were true things."

"I know they were true things," Veyra said, and the rawness in it silenced the whole room. "That is precisely why the doors are staying shut."

Her hand opened. The shard lay gleaming in her palm—and then, deliberately, deliberately, she turned her fist and let it drop. It struck the floorboards at Isandro's feet and rolled, humming, to rest against the toe of her boot.

"Pick it up," Veyra whispered. "And run, child. Run faster than you have ever run in your life. Because I have just broken my own law in front of my own guild, and God help us both, I no longer know which side of the schism I'll be standing on when the moon rises."
