## 18. The Vault of Broken Things

Tam Vess had stolen things before. He wanted to be honest about that with himself, at least, since honesty seemed to be the currency everyone was suddenly demanding of him. He had stolen apples off Warden carts as a boy, because Warden carts were fat and slow and the orchard boys who grew up outside the guildhall's shadow learned early that the guild took and never gave. He had stolen a kiss from Isandro Delacroix when they were fourteen, behind the tannery, and she had stolen one back, and for a season they had been rich in a way neither of them ever named aloud. He had stolen—God forgive him—a year of her trust, sitting across from her at every campfire on the road to Sable Hollow knowing that his presence there had been purchased by the guildmaster's coin and his own cowardice.

But he had never stolen anything like this.

The shard sat three floors below the guild hall, in the Vault of Broken Things, behind a door that had not been opened in living memory without the guildmaster's key. Tam knew this because he had spent two days listening to old Warden Halloway drink himself through the history of it at the Drowned Bell, and drunk men guarding secrets were just secrets waiting to fall out of their pockets.

*The founders broke the oath-stone into three,* Halloway had said, tracing the shape of it on the tavern table with a finger wet with ale. *Kept one here where no one would think to look for it, under all our broken vows. Sent one over the border to the river folk, and one to the knight order in the east, so that no single hand could ever make it whole again. That was the whole genius of it, boy. They didn't hide the covenant. They scattered it among enemies.*

*And if someone gathered the pieces?*

Halloway had looked at him then with eyes gone red-rimmed and terribly clear, the way drunk men sometimes achieved clarity precisely at the moment it was least welcome.

*Then somebody would have to pay the price the founders wouldn't. And nobody in this valley has paid for anything in a hundred years.*

* * *

The schism began, as far as Tam could tell, with a chair.

He was standing in the gallery above the guild hall's great chamber, wedged between a pillar and a suit of ceremonial armor that smelled of dust and old oil, watching two hundred Wardens try to decide what they were. The hall had been built for ceremony—high rafters black with a century of torch smoke, banners of the thirteen valleys hanging motionless in air that never moved, the founders' portraits glaring down from their gilded frames like disapproving ancestors at a funeral. It was built for unanimity. It had never once, Tam suspected, been built for what was happening now.

Warden-Captain Brannoc had demanded the floor, and when Guildmaster Veyra granted it, he had walked to the center of the chamber and set his sword on the flagstones—not sheathed, not offered, just *set down*, the way a man puts down a tool he no longer trusts his hands to hold.

"I stood watch at the boundary stones for nineteen years," Brannoc said, and his voice carried the way voices do in rooms built for oaths. "Nineteen years I told myself we were keeping something out." He looked up at the founders' portraits. "We were keeping something *in*. Us. Our sin. I have read the testimony Delacroix brought back from Sable Hollow. I have read the archivist's records. I have stood in this hall my whole life and recited the Litany of the Shielded Valley, and every word of it was written by people who starved a realm to fill their bellies."

Murmuring started low, like water finding cracks in a wall.

"They are lies," said Warden Serl, from the front rank, an old woman with a voice like a whetstone. "Fabrications of a traitor girl and a blind foreigner. The founders sealed the valley to save it—"

"The founders sealed the valley to save *themselves*," Brannoc cut across her. "The record says so in Ondine's own hand, cross-checked against vault ledgers three centuries old. Read it yourself. Or don't, and keep your comfort. But stop calling it truth."

And that was when the chair happened. Tam would never learn afterward who threw it—some young Warden in the third rank whose name would be lost inside the legend—but a chair went spinning across the flagstones, and then the room came apart along its oldest fault line with a sound like ice going out on a spring river.

Tam pressed himself deeper into the shadow of the pillar and watched the only family Isandro Delacroix had ever known tear itself in half.

* * *

He should describe it more carefully, he thought later. He owed it that much. Because what happened in the guild hall that night was not a brawl—it was a *reckoning*, and reckoning has a grammar all its own.

It moved outward from the center in rings. First the words stopped meaning anything, because both sides were shouting scripture at each other—the Litany against the Prophecy, founder against founder, and Tam realized with a lurch that they had always been quoting from books that contradicted each other, and no one had ever read them side by side until a girl from the inventory detail had pulled a prophecy off a forgotten shelf. Then the ranks broke. Wardens who had trained together for twenty years turned on each other with open hands first, then closed ones. Someone drew steel near the eastern doors and half a dozen voices roared *no blades, no blades*, and mercifully, mostly, they listened. The guild had rules even in its breaking. That was the thing about Ashvale, Tam thought, watching a veteran sergeant weep openly while shoving a younger Warden he'd helped raise back from a fight. Even its fractures followed form.

Veyra stood at the head of the chamber and did not move.

That was the part Tam kept returning to afterward, the image he carried like a stone in his boot: the guildmaster on her dais, silver-haired and still, watching her life's work come apart beneath her, and her face—not rage, not grief exactly, but the terrible patience of a woman who had known this was coming and had chosen, once again, decades ago, to let it come rather than tell the truth that might have softened it. She caught sight of him once—he was sure of it, her gaze climbing the gallery wall and finding him in the pillar's shadow—and she held it for three heartbeats. Not warning. Not accusation. Something worse.

*Get it done,* her eyes said. *Or get her out. Either way, end this.*

Or perhaps he imagined that. Perhaps it was only a tired old woman looking at a boy she had once used as a leash, wondering how the leash had learned to run.

The doors of the vault stair were unguarded. Everyone who should have been guarding them was busy deciding whether they still had a guild to guard them *for*.

Tam went down the stairs two at a time, into the dark.

* * *

The Vault of Broken Things did not announce itself. That was the horror of it. After the roar of the schism overhead, the descent was silence, and the silence had texture—dust, stone, the particular hush of a room where nothing has been disturbed on purpose for a hundred years. His lantern found shelves first: confiscated charms, hedge-witch talismans, children's toys carved with sigils that had been illegal in the valley for longer than anyone could remember. Every object was tagged in fading ink. *Surrendered. Burned pending review.* Pending review for sixty years. A doll with button eyes stared at him from a crate labeled *RECOVERED FROM DELACROIX HOUSEHOLD*, and Tam's stomach turned over, because somewhere in these shelves was the paper trail of a family erased, and Isandro didn't even know her grandmother's name yet, and maybe—God—maybe some piece of her grandmother was sitting right here in the dark, tagged and catalogued and *pending review*.

Not tonight. Tonight there was only one thing in this room that mattered, and it was behind the inner door.

The inner door was iron, and it was not locked.

That undid him more than any lock could have. He stood before it with his heart hammering and his palms slick on the lantern handle, braced for wards, for traps, for some final cunning of the founders—and the door swung at his touch like the door to a pantry. Because the founders had never feared thieves, he understood suddenly. Thieves want treasure, and treasure can be replaced. What the founders had feared was *witnesses*. No one had guarded this door because no one was ever supposed to come looking. The lock was the guild's collective refusal to know. He had simply become a man who knew, and knowledge, it turned out, was the key.

Beyond it: a small round chamber, and in its center, on a plain stone plinth beneath a shaft of moonlight falling through a vent in the ceiling, the third shard of the oath-stone.

It was smaller than he expected. Fist-sized, jagged, dark as river slate—and wrong, somehow, the way a snapped bone is wrong. When Tam's lantern light crossed it, the shadows around the plinth bent toward it slightly, like grass leaning into wind. And underneath the silence of the chamber he heard—or felt, or remembered hearing, in some part of himself that predated crossing the boundary stones—a hum. Low. Patient. The sound of a promise waiting a hundred years to be finished.

His hands shook so badly he had to press them flat against his thighs.

Because here was the truth he'd been circling for days: Isandro had planned to take this shard herself. She'd told him so on the road, in her flat, stubborn way, staring into the fire. *I'll walk in the front door. I'll face them. I owe them that much—I owe *myself* that much. I won't sneak into the place that raised me.* She had meant it. She had meant to stand in that hall and testify, and she had meant to ask—ask, with everything on the line—for the last piece of a covenant her own family had been murdered out of. And Veyra had offered her a bargain instead, and Brannoc had thrown a chair, and the whole careful architecture of her courage had collapsed into chaos she never chose.

So it fell to him. Again. The leash who learned to run, holding the bag for the girl he'd betrayed, doing the dirty work so her hands could stay clean enough to do the holy work.

Terror is a strange companion for exhilaration, but they walked out of that vault arm in arm. His mouth tasted of copper. His ears rang. Somewhere overhead a beam cracked or a banner-pole came down, and dust sifted through the ceiling vent into the moonlight, glittering like the whole room was full of falling stars. And through all of it, that hum from the shard rose to meet him, and it sounded—this was the mad part, the part he would never tell anyone—it sounded *glad*. Like a dog hearing its name after years of being called nothing.

He wrapped it in his scarf. It was cold, then warm, then cold again, cycling like something breathing. He tucked it against his chest, took up the lantern, and ran.

* * *

He made it as far as the armory landing.

The stairwell was full of smoke—not fire, just torches knocked loose and guttering on the flagstones below—and through it came running feet, and Tam flattened into an alcove as a knot of Wardens pounded past upward: Serl's loyalists, faces hard, headed for the vault stairs. Headed, he realized with a wash of ice water, to secure the shard. Veyra's offer had failed; the guildmaster's faction would not leave the last piece within reach of a schism. If they reached the inner chamber before—

They wouldn't. He had locked it. Had he? He couldn't remember locking it. His hands had been shaking too badly to remember whether he'd—

Footsteps again, slower. Descending.

Tam looked around the alcove like a trapped animal and found, beside him, the guild's ancient arms rack—ceremonial pikes, training staves, and one wall of boundary-ward sigils etched on slate, the forbidden craft, the stuff Isandro had used to drive off the ambushers at the shrine. She had taught him the theory on the road, late nights, her finger tracing lines in the dirt: *the wards don't create power, Tam, they remember it. Everything in this valley is soaked in a century of held-back magic. You're just asking the walls to give some of it back.*

He grabbed a ward-slate in each hand and stepped out onto the landing.

Warden Serl stood six steps below him, alone, breathing hard from the stairs, an old woman with a lamp raised and her face already rearranging itself from alarm into contempt.

"Vess," she said. "The guildmaster's little errand boy. Where is it?"

"You know what she saw," Tam said. His voice cracked and he hated it. "Over the border. You've read the ledgers same as the rest of us. You *know*."

"I know my oaths."

"Your oaths were written by cowards." He raised the slates. "I'm sorry about this."

He struck them together the way Isandro had shown him, edge to edge, and the valley's long-imprisoned magic woke in the walls like blood into a numb limb. Light burst white along the stairwell, silent, total, and Serl cried out and covered her eyes, and Tam shoved past her downward through the dazzle, feeling the ward-slates crumble to powder in his fists, feeling—God help him—the grin tearing across his face before he could stop it.

*That's what it feels like,* he thought, taking the last flights three at a time, the shard pulsing warm against his chest like a second heartbeat. *That's what she felt at the stones. That's what we were all starving and never knew.*

The postern gate was ahead, and beyond it the dark slope of the valley, and beyond that the boundary stones where Isandro waited with two shards and a deadline written in the sky. Behind him the guild hall burned its torches into the night, splitting and splitting, old law and new truth tearing along a fault line a century deep.

Tam Vess, thief, liar, leash-turned-runner, put his head down and ran for the light.

He did not see the figure detach from the gatehouse shadow and follow—silver-haired, unhurried, blade sheathed, tears drying on her face—as though she had always intended, all along, to arrive at the stones herself.
