## 19. Thirteenth Turn Rising

The moon rose wrong.

Isandro had watched it climb over the Ashvale hills her whole life—round and patient and ordinary, a lantern hung in a sky that never asked anything of anyone. But tonight it came up the color of old bone, veined faintly with silver light that did not belong to the sun's reflection, and every warding stone along the valley's rim began, very softly, to sing.

She heard it from two miles out: a thin harmonic threading through the dark like wire drawn through silk. Tam heard it too. He stumbled on the switchback path and caught himself against the cliff face, breath ragged, the satchel of shards strapped tight across his chest.

"They know we're coming," he said.

"They've known since Sable Hollow." Isandro didn't slow. The wound in her shoulder where the loyalist warden's blade had kissed her three nights ago pulled with every stride, a hot stitch of pain she'd learned to fold into the rhythm of running. "What they don't know is which pass."

"The Hollow Pass is flooded this time of year."

"It was," Ondine said behind them.

The blind archivist moved through darkness better than any of them, her white staff tapping stone with the certainty of a heartbeat. She had insisted on coming, and none of them had been foolish enough to argue. Somewhere in the folds of her coat she carried the first shard, warm as a sleeping bird; the second rode against Isandro's ribs, still cold from the knight's memorial crypt; and the third—

The third was a weight Tam carried in more ways than one. He had taken it from the vault during the schism, in a hall full of screaming and broken oaths, and Isandro had seen what it cost him. He hadn't slept properly since. Every time he closed his eyes, she suspected he saw Veyra's face turning toward him across the guild hall—not angry, worse than angry—*disappointed*, the way a mother looks at a child who has just taught her something unbearable about herself.

"Was?" Tam said. "Past tense?"

"Floods are water," Ondine said mildly. "Water answers to the covenant. And tonight the covenant is waking up." Her blind eyes turned toward the rising moon, and though there was nothing in them to see, Isandro felt the look land somewhere behind her own sternum. "Everything old is remembering itself tonight, child. Including the passes."

Behind them, down the valley of gray orchards they had crossed to reach this ridge, torchlight bloomed. Not one torch or ten—a river of them, pouring up the road from the shrine country, moving with the terrible discipline of people who believed themselves righteous.

Isandro counted by instinct, the way she'd been trained to count before she'd known why counting mattered. Sixty. Perhaps eighty. Loyalist wardens, the ones who had stayed with Veyra when the guild tore itself apart, carrying valley steel and the certainty that they were saving their home from a madwoman who wanted to unmake it.

*Let them think so,* she thought, and found she could not even hate them for it. Three months ago she would have been marching beside them. Three months ago she would have been *leading* them.

"Hollow Pass," she said aloud, making it real by saying it. "We cross at Hollow Pass. If the floodwater's fallen back, we reach the boundary stones before the moon reaches its zenith." She looked up. The thirteenth moon climbed with what felt like deliberate speed, silver veins brightening. "If it hasn't, we're going to learn very quickly whether I can swim in armor."

"You can't swim at all," Tam said.

"I'm aware. It's on my list of things to remedy after I save two realms."

Despite everything—despite the torches and the singing stones and the memory of his blood on the shrine steps—Tam laughed. It was a small, cracked sound, gone almost before it arrived, but Isandro held onto it the way you hold a coal in cupped hands on a winter march. She would need warmth where they were going. Everyone knew what waited at the boundary stones.

Everyone except, perhaps, the person walking beside her who refused to say it aloud.

* * *

They ran.

The Hollow Pass was a scar in the world, a place where some ancient violence had folded the mountains together and left a corridor of black glass and shattered shale. In spring the meltwater thundered through it in a brown flood that no ferryman would dare; in autumn it lay quiet under drifts of pale sand that whispered when the wind crossed them. Tonight the sand lay damp and gleaming in the moonlight, pocked with standing pools, and above it the walls rose sheer on either side, streaked with mineral light.

And the covenant was indeed waking. Isandro felt it in her teeth, in the shard against her ribs, in the way the pools reflected not the moon overhead but a different sky entirely—one with too many stars, arranged in constellations she half-remembered from Ondine's books. The boundary between realms had always been thinner here. Tonight it was less a wall than a membrane, shivering with everything pressing against it from both sides.

Halfway through the pass, the torches entered behind them.

Isandro turned once, at the mouth, and looked back down the long slope they'd descended. The torch-river had reached the pass entrance and split around it like water around a boulder, and from its center rose a single banner she knew better than her own name: the ash-grey thorn of the Wardens, stitched in silver thread by hands that had probably stitched it a hundred years ago.

"They'll catch us in the narrows," Tam said. He'd stopped trying to hide how badly his hands were shaking. "Sixty of them. Three of us and an archivist."

"Four," said a voice from the shadows of the pass wall.

Isandro spun, hand to her blade—and then stopped, because the figure limping out of the dark wore no warden grey at all, but the mud-brown of a river-ferryman, and the first thing he did was press a fist to his heart in the old gesture of the outer realm.

"Bram," Ondine said, unsurprised. Of course she wasn't surprised. Blind archivists were never surprised; it was one of the many things about her that made Isandro feel perpetually a step behind.

"You swore truth would be told publicly in Ashvale," Bram said to Isandro. His face was gaunt, river-worn, and he walked like a man whose hip had been broken and set badly. "You're about to have sixty witnesses. I mean to see my demand honored." He hefted a ferryman's pole, iron-shod at both ends. "Besides. Someone has to hold the line while you run."

"There won't be a line," Isandro said. "There can't be fighting. If any of them die—"

"Some of them already intend for *you* to die," Bram said flatly. "I watched the shrine burn from the far bank, Warden. Don't lecture me about intentions."

She wanted to argue. The argument died in her throat, because the moon chose that moment to clear the eastern peaks, and the whole pass lit up silver-white, and every pool of water along the floor began to glow with the other sky, and there was simply no more time for anything except forward.

* * *

The narrows nearly killed them anyway.

Not the wardens—the wardens never got the chance. It was the covenant itself, waking faster than any of them had calculated. As they ran the final stretch of pass, the standing pools overflowed upward, sheets of starlit water climbing the glass walls like ivy, and the air filled with voices. Not words. Something older than words—the sound the valley's founders must have heard on the day they broke the world in half. Grief, mostly. Grief and patience, braided together.

Tam went down hard when a sheet of water took his feet from under him, and Isandro hauled him up by the strap of the satchel, and they ran bent-double against a wind that smelled impossibly of orchards in blossom—of the *outer* realm's orchards, the gray ones, remembering what color meant.

Then the pass opened, and the boundary stones stood before them.

There were nine, arranged in a crescent on the high moor, each taller than two men, each carved with script worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather and deliberate neglect. Isandro had touched one of these stones as a girl on a dare and felt nothing but cold granite. Now they blazed like bonfires of silence, and beyond them, past the invisible line they marked, she could see it: the valley. Home. The roofs of Ashvale town, the smoke of evening fires, the guild hall's slate roof catching moonlight. Everything she had ever loved, laid out below her like a map of the life she was about to spend.

And between her and the stones, alone, stood Guildmaster Veyra.

No army behind her. No loyalists—she had left them at the pass mouth, or ordered them back, or perhaps simply outrun them, because Veyra had always moved faster than anyone gave her credit for. Just the guildmaster, in her formal greys with the thorn sigil at her throat, her blade drawn and pointed not at Isandro but at the ground, and tears running openly down a face that had spent forty years learning not to show anything.

Isandro stopped. The others stopped behind her, and for a moment the only sounds were wind, and water, and the covenant singing in the stones.

"You brought no one," Isandro said.

"I brought everyone," Veyra answered. Her voice was steady. Her blade-hand was not. "Every Warden who ever kept watch on this line. Every founder who made the choice you're about to unmake. They're all standing right here, Isandro. They always have been." She lifted the blade at last, and the moonlight ran down it like water. "That's what you've never understood. You think this is law standing in your way. It isn't. It's grief. Forty years of it. Mine, and everyone's who came before me."

"I know why you're crying," Isandro said quietly. "Ondine told us what you saw, crossing over when you were young. What the realm looked like. What you couldn't fix."

Veyra flinched. It was small, and she buried it fast, but Isandro had spent her whole life reading this woman the way other people read scripture, and she saw it land.

"So you know," Veyra said, "that when I tell you to stop, I am not speaking from cruelty."

"I know exactly what you're speaking from." Isandro stepped forward, one step, and the shard at her ribs pulsed hot enough that she gasped. "You're speaking from the arithmetic. You added up two realms' worth of suffering and decided one should carry it, because carrying it was possible and fixing it wasn't. And you've lived inside that decision so long you've forgotten it was ever a decision. You think it's just *how things are*. Like the tide. Like gravity." She drew the second shard from her coat and held it up, and it sang against the stones, and the moon above them brightened another degree. "But it wasn't gravity, Guildmaster. It was a choice. And choices can be remade."

"With your memories." Veyra's voice finally cracked, all the way through. "You know the price. The archivist told you. You'll stand here and save the world and forget you ever had one. Your friends. Your teachers. The taste of the bread in our kitchens. The sound of your own name in your mother's—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were terrible and clear. "You will walk into that valley afterward a stranger, and no one will be allowed to grieve you, because you'll be standing right in front of them."

"Yes," Isandro said.

"And you'd still do it."

"That's rather the point of me, I think."

For one suspended moment, Isandro truly believed Veyra would step aside. She saw the possibility move through the older woman's body like weather—saw the blade dip, saw the shoulders begin to drop—

Then the torches crested the pass behind them, and Veyra straightened, because eighty witnesses were watching now, and whatever the guildmaster's private grief permitted, her office did not.

"I can't let you pass," Veyra said, and this time her voice was iron all the way down. "In front of the guild, I cannot let you pass. Make me move, Isandro. Or go home. Those are the only doors left, and I am so sorry, child, but I am standing in front of one of them."

Isandro looked at her friends. Tam, bleeding through his bandage, jaw set, already reaching for the third shard. Bram, planting his ferryman's pole like a standard. Ondine, serene, her blind face lifted to the moon as if listening for the exact moment history would change key.

The thirteenth moon reached the top of the sky.

"No," Isandro said. "You're standing in front of both of them."

She started forward, and Veyra raised her blade, and the tears on the guildmaster's face froze silver in the moonlight as steel met the beginning of everything.
