## 20. The Reforging

The thirteenth moon rose over the boundary stones like a coin held up to the eye of a god, and Isandro Delacroix ran toward it with the shards of a broken oath burning through her shirt against her heart.

They had come the last mile at a dead sprint. Ondine could not run—Tam had carried her on his back for two miles of dark road, the blind archivist's arms locked around his neck, her white eyes wide and streaming in the wind—and still she had directed them, unerring, by some sense older than sight: *left at the split oak, down past the shrine where the wardens fell, the stones are singing tonight, girl, can't you hear them?* Isandro could hear them. She had been hearing them since Sable Hollow, a thin harmonic beneath the world's ordinary noise, and now it swelled to fill the valley's throat.

Behind them: torchlight. Loyalist wardens, six of them, then eight, their boots striking the frost-hard ground in rhythm, Veyra somewhere among them or ahead of them or both. Ahead: the standing stones, seven of them, taller than any man, ringed around a circle of earth that had lain fallow for a hundred years because nothing would grow there—not from poison, Isandro understood now, but from waiting.

She broke into the circle and stopped so suddenly that Tam nearly bowled her over.

The stones were awake.

Frost crawled up their flanks in patterns like script. The air inside the ring was warm as a held breath. And in the center, where the grass had never grown, the packed earth glowed faintly gold, as though a coal the size of a hearth burned just beneath the skin of the world.

"Put me down here," Ondine said, and Tam set her on her feet at the circle's edge, gently, the way you set down something you are afraid of breaking. "Inside, Isandro. All three shards. In your hands, not a pouch, not cloth. Skin."

Isandro pulled them out. Three pieces of pale stone, each no larger than a fist, each scarred along one edge where they had been sundered from the whole. The first, won from the ferryman across a sworn promise of public truth. The second, taken weeping from the knight after the memorial, after Isandro had stood in the cold and spoken the names of the dead until her voice gave out. The third—

The third Tam had stolen from the vault of Ashvale itself, three nights ago, with half the guild tearing itself apart behind him. He had a bandage wrapped around his forearm where a warden's blade had kissed him on the way out, and he was watching her now with an expression she had learned to read across a thousand miles of exile: fear and ferocity braided into one.

"Whatever happens," he said, "don't look back at us. Look at what you're doing. Only that."

"Tam—"

"No." His voice cracked. "You don't get to say goodbye to me. That's the whole point. You don't get to carry me with you." He wiped his face roughly with his good sleeve. "So don't."

Torchlight bloomed over the eastern rise.

* * *

Veyra came down the hill alone.

That was the worst of it, somehow—that the guildmaster dismissed her wardens with a raised hand and walked the rest of the way by herself, sword sheathed, gray braid loose in the wind, moving with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had already fought this battle in her head a hundred times and lost it every time but one.

Isandro stepped between Veyra and the stones.

"Stand aside, child." Veyra's voice was quiet. It carried anyway; the stones took it up and repeated it in whispers, *child, child, child*, until the word sounded ancient. "I have drawn this line for forty years. I have buried friends who drew it before me. Whatever you believe about the founders, whatever Ondine has shown you—the covenant cannot be reforged by a girl with three rocks and a grievance. The price is real. I know what it costs. I have known since I was younger than you."

"Then you know it can be paid," Isandro said. Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. "You crossed over once. You saw what we did to them. You came home and you chose the valley anyway, and you've spent your whole life calling that choice mercy. I'm not here to take it from you, Guildmaster. I'm here to make a different one."

"The realm will survive divided. It has survived."

"It is *dying.*" The words tore out of her, and the stones sang them back, *dying, dying,* and far away, beyond the border, Isandro imagined gray orchards shivering at the sound. "You told me once that a Warden's first duty is to see clearly. So look. Look at what our safety is made of. Not stone. Starvation."

For a long moment the only sound was wind, and torches, and the low gold hum of the earth.

Then Veyra drew her blade.

It was not a threat. Isandro knew that even as her body forgot it—knew it because the guildmaster's stance was wrong for an attack, open, almost ceremonial, the way wardens stood when administering an oath or an execution. The steel caught the thirteenth moon and threw its light up onto Veyra's face, and Isandro saw, finally and completely, that the woman was crying without any change in her expression at all, tears running down into the collar of her coat as though they were simply another kind of weather.

"I swore," Veyra said, "to keep magic out of the valley where it cannot follow. Do you understand what that means, Isandro? Not *would not follow.* *Cannot.* Because if it follows—if it comes home—it will come looking for payment, and the ones who broke the covenant are dust, and the debt will fall on whoever stands closest. On the guild. On the children in the dormitories. On *you.*" Her voice frayed at last. "I have spent forty years making sure the bill never comes due. And you—you walk into my vault, you find one book, and you decide to pay it yourself."

"Somebody has to."

"Yes," Veyra whispered. "*Yes.* That is exactly what terrifies me."

She raised the blade.

Tam moved—she heard him move, heard Ondine cry out—but Isandro did not step back. She stood in the center of the ring with three shards in her bare hands and watched the steel descend, and in the last instant before it fell she thought, with strange clarity: *so this is how much I love it. This is the weight of the thing I am about to lose.*

The blade struck the earth beside her foot, point-down, and stood there quivering.

"My hand," Veyra said hoarsely, "goes on the stone too. If this is done, it is done rightly—with a Warden's oath and not against one. And when it is done—" her breath caught, steadied, "—when it is done, I will stand in the guild hall and tell them everything. The truth you promised the ferryman. Every name. Mine first."

* * *

Ondine talked her through it, and the stones helped.

The rite was not complicated. That was the cruelest discovery of the night—that a hundred years of dread rested on something as simple as kneeling, and joining, and speaking. Isandro knelt at the center of the ring where the gold light breathed. Veyra knelt opposite her, sword abandoned, and laid her weathered hand on the joined fragments alongside Isandro's own. Tam and Ondine knelt at the circle's edge, witnesses, and Ondine spoke the old framing words in a language Isandro did not know but understood anyway, the way you understand weeping.

The shards came together in her palms with a sound like a key turning.

Not a click—a turning, deep and vast, somewhere below the floor of the world, and light ran up the standing stones one by one like dawn deciding to happen early. Isandro felt the covenant wake under her hands, felt it as a presence, patient and enormous and terribly sad, the way a parent is sad watching a child do a necessary thing. It asked its question without words. It asked, and she understood:

*What will you give?*

And here was the thing she would never be able to tell anyone afterward, because afterward there would be no *afterward* in which she knew it: the memories did not wait to be taken. They offered themselves. All of them, in order, like a book opening to be copied. The dormitory loft and the smell of lamp oil. Veyra teaching her to hold a blade, correcting her grip with two fingers, saying *again, again, again* with terrible patience. The vault, dusty and dim, the prophecy book falling open under her hands. Tam laughing in the kitchen at midnight. The boundary stones under her palm, cold fire. Her grandmother's name, recovered letter by letter from erased records. The taste of the bread they baked on festival mornings. The feeling—she had never told anyone this—of walking the guild halls at night and knowing, with her whole body, that she belonged there.

*What will you give?*

"Everything," Isandro said aloud. "I give everything I was, so that the valley can become something new. Not erased. Spent." She looked at Veyra through the rising light. "There's a difference. Write it down somewhere."

Veyra bowed her head. "Witnessed," she said, in the formal cadence of the guild oath, and her voice broke clean in half on the second syllable.

The covenant took her at her word.

It began at the edges, the way numbness leaves a limb—or arrives in one. The outermost memories went soft first, blurred, unhinged from their names. Isandro felt herself reach for them and felt them dissolve into warmth, into gold, into the general brightness flooding outward from the stones across the frozen fields, over the hills, through the sleeping town where lights were coming on in windows one by one because people were waking and didn't know why. Green, she sensed rather than saw, green pouring westward over the border into the withered realm, orchards remembering how to be orchards, shrines filling with birdsong. Magic moving through the valley like blood into a limb that had been bound for a century, and oh, the *ache* of it, the sweet terrible ache of returning feeling.

She held on to the center as long as she could. To Tam's laugh. To the vault. To the date that should not exist.

But the covenant was gentle, and it was thorough, and it kept its side of the bargain perfectly, which is the nature of covenants. The memories did not scream as they left. They thanked her. Each one, going out, seemed to press itself briefly against her heart like a traveler embracing a host before a long road, and then the gold took it, and the green took it, and it became part of the flowering instead of part of her.

The last thing to go—because she made it the last thing, because she was still, for three more seconds, the author of herself—was a small image she assembled deliberately from all the pieces: Tam in the kitchen at midnight, flour on his nose, telling her some foolish story, and her thinking *this one. This one is mine.*

*I choose to lose you,* she told it. *That's different from losing you.*

And then it went, and she went, and the light closed over the place where Isandro Delacroix had been like water closing over a thrown flower.

* * *

When she opened her eyes, she was lying on the frost inside a ring of standing stones, and the grass was growing up around her hands—actually growing, blades unfurling in fast motion, silver-green in the moonlight. The thirteenth moon hung overhead, whole and high and ordinary. A sword stood planted in the earth nearby. Two strangers were running toward her, a young man and an old blind woman, and their faces were wrecked with grief and joy in equal measure, and Isandro did not know them at all.

"Isandro?" the young man said. His voice shook. "Isandro, can you hear me?"

"That's not my name," she said, and heard how lost she sounded, and started, very quietly, to cry. "I don't—I don't know where I am. I don't know *who* I am."

The old woman knelt and took her hands, and her grip was strong, and her white eyes were full of tears she could not see out of.

"No," the archivist said softly. "But you will. That's the whole point of a beginning, child. Come—we have so much to tell you. We have *years* of things to tell you."

Far away in the town below, bells were ringing—every bell, all at once, for no reason anyone could name yet. And in Isandro's coat pocket, pressed flat in a seal of wax she could not remember receiving, a black thorn waited in the dark, keeping faith.

It would be months before it bloomed. But it had already begun.
