## 22. The Thorn in Bloom

The market at Ashvale opened with the smell of bread and river-water, as it always had—though Isandro did not know that it had always been so. She knew only that the smell settled into her like something remembered by a body that had outlived its own memories.

She came every morning now, not to buy much. A heel of dark rye, sometimes a twist of honey-cake when the coin allowed, and always a sprig of whatever the flower-seller had cut that day, because flowers were the one thing in this valley that made her chest ache without explanation. She would stand there holding marigolds or late roses and feel grief arrive like weather from a country she'd never visited.

"Morning, Isandro," the baker called. He was a broad man with flour in his beard, and he said her name gently, the way everyone did, as if it were a thing that might bruise.

"Morning."

That was the whole of most conversations, and she had learned to be grateful for them. The valley had changed since the night of the thirteenth moon—or so she was told. She had no before to measure it against. What she knew was this: magic ran openly now through Ashvale like water finding old channels. Lamps lit themselves along the market row. A woman two stalls down coaxed green shoots from bare winter branches with a pass of her palm, and no one gasped, because they had all agreed to stop being surprised. The Wardens still existed, but they had become archivists and healers and, more than anything, confessors—standing in the square on certain evenings reading aloud from ledgers no one had wanted opened, naming the dead of the outer realm until the names stopped sounding foreign.

Isandro lived above the cobbler's shop in a room that strangers had furnished for her. They were kind. That was the strangest part—the kindness had no floor to it. People looked at her the way you look at someone who has paid your debt without being able to say thank you properly, because she didn't know what she'd paid, and they couldn't bring themselves to tell her all of it.

She had asked, once, early on. A gray-haired woman tending her—a stranger with kind hands—had wept and said, "You gave everything. Let yourself rest." And Isandro had understood that the story was too heavy to hand to someone who had already carried it once, and had stopped asking.

Mostly.

* * *

It happened on a morning in late autumn, the air sharp enough to bite, the sky the color of unpolished pewter. She reached into the pocket of her coat for a coin and her fingers closed around something small and hard and smooth.

She drew it out into the pale light.

A thorn. Long as her smallest finger, dark as ironwood, pressed flat inside a seal of amber-colored wax that had cracked with age—or with handling. Someone had carried this a long time. Someone had held it often enough to wear the wax soft at the edges, the way a coin goes smooth, the way a prayer bead wears down to silk.

Her heart began doing something strange. Not racing. *Reaching.*

She turned it over. There were no markings. It was just a thorn in wax, an ordinary relic of some rosebush somewhere, except that her hands had begun to shake and her eyes had begun to sting and none of it made any sense, because it was only a thorn, it was nothing, it was—

It was the most important object she had ever touched. She knew that the way you know your own name. Not as thought. As gravity.

"Where did you come from?" she whispered to it.

The flower-seller, an old woman with a shawl the color of moss, glanced over. "Found something, dear?"

"This." Isandro held it up, half-embarrassed, half-desperate. "It was in my pocket. I don't—" Her voice caught. "I don't remember where I got it."

Something moved across the old woman's face—not pity, exactly. Something older and kinder. "Some things aren't for remembering," she said. "They're for keeping."

Isandro closed her fist around the thorn and walked home without buying bread.

* * *

She sat on the narrow bed in her rooms above the cobbler's shop and held the thorn up to the window light for what must have been an hour. The wax had gone translucent at its thinnest points, and behind it the thorn showed like a splinter of night.

If she closed her eyes and let her thumb travel its length, something flickered at the very bottom of her mind. Not images. Not voices. More like the shapes left behind when furniture is moved—a rectangle of unfaded wallpaper where a picture hung, a pale patch on a floor where a chair stood for years. Absences with edges.

There had been a garden. She was almost sure of that. Roses gone feral over a wall, untended, wicked with thorns, and someone laughing—

No. Not laughing. Saying something. Her name? No, she had her own name; she knew that much. Someone else's name, then. A name that started soft.

She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until sparks swam. It was like standing outside a house where music played—all she could catch was rhythm, never melody.

Below, the cobbler's hammer kept its patient time: tap, tap, tap. Somewhere a cart rattled over stones. The valley went about its business, greener than it had any right to be in autumn, and Isandro sat alone with an absence shaped exactly like a life.

"I had someone," she said aloud, to the empty room. "Didn't I. I had people."

The thorn in her palm seemed, impossibly, warmer.

* * *

She went back to the market the next morning because staying home felt like drowning slowly, and markets at least were loud. She bought her bread. She bought her flower. She was turning toward the river path when she saw him.

He stood at the edge of the square near the old well, and something in her body recognized him before her mind had finished looking—a jolt, low and certain, like a struck bell. Tall, dark-haired, a scar she could see even from here running along his jaw. He was holding a paper-wrapped bundle of honey-cakes and looking at her with an expression she had no word for: hope so worn down by months of waiting that it had become almost indistinguishable from fear.

Her feet moved before she decided anything.

"You're staring," she said, when she was close enough. It came out gentler than she meant it to. "Or I'm staring. One of us."

His breath left him all at once, like he'd been holding it since summer. "I'm staring," he admitted. "Sorry. I've—I've walked past this market forty times telling myself today isn't the day. And then it's the day, and I can't remember a single thing I planned to say."

"Well, that makes two of us who are lost," Isandro said. "I'm Isandro."

Something crossed his face—grief and gladness tangled together so tightly she couldn't have pulled them apart with both hands. "I know," he said softly. Then, visibly gathering himself: "Tam. My name is Tam."

"Hello, Tam." The name landed somewhere beneath her ribs and rang. *A name that starts soft.* Her fingers curled around the thorn in her pocket. "Do I know you?"

"No." He shook his head, then corrected himself. "Yes. Both. You used to. You don't anymore—that's not a secret, everyone knows, it's just—" He laughed, helpless. "I'm making a mess of this. Can I start again? Properly?"

"Start again how?"

"From the beginning." He nodded toward the bench beside the well, old stone worn smooth by generations of sitting. "It's a long story. Longer than one conversation. But I promised myself that if I ever found the courage, I wouldn't give you a summary. Summaries are how you lose things." He paused. "I'd tell it all. However many mornings it takes. If you want to hear it. You're allowed to say no. You're allowed to say no every day for a year, and I'd still be here on day three hundred and sixty-six."

Isandro looked at him—at the scar on his jaw, at the bundle of honey-cakes clutched like an offering, at the terrible patience of his face—and the absences in her mind stirred, all their pale edges pointing toward him like compass needles finding north.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "Start from the beginning."

* * *

They sat on the well-bench while the market traded itself busy around them, and Tam began the way she supposed all true stories begin: badly, honestly, with the parts that made the teller look small.

"I followed you," he said. "The first time. Out past the boundary stones. I wasn't brave—I was sent. Guildmaster Veyra told me to keep an eye on you, report back if you did anything foolish. And I told myself that's all it was, right up until the moment the stones let us through and the world turned *on*, and you were standing in another sky looking like you'd finally found the room your whole life had been built outside of." His voice roughened. "I chose you that night. Before I understood anything. Maybe especially before I understood anything."

"Chose me how?" Isandro asked quietly.

"Over the law. Over the guild. Over the version of safe that costs other people everything." He turned the honey-cake bundle over in his hands, not opening it. "You probably want me to start with prophecies. With covenant shards, and the withered realm, and the night at the boundary stones with Veyra's blade drawn. I'll get there. But the beginning—the real beginning—is smaller than that. The real beginning is a girl I grew up with who found tomorrow's date written in a book that should have stayed shut, and instead of burning it or burying it, she *read*. That's who you were, Isandro. That's who you are. The kind of person who sees a locked door and asks what's being kept in, not just kept out."

The thorn pressed against her thigh through the fabric of her pocket, warm as a held hand.

"There was a garden," she said suddenly. "Roses gone wild. Over a wall."

Tam went very still. "Your grandmother's house. Or—no. Ondine's archive, the first night. The roses along Sable Hollow." His eyes were shining. "You remember that?"

"Not remember." She shook her head, frustrated, tears closer than she wanted them. "It's like—like hearing footsteps in another room. I know someone walked there. I can't see them."

"That's more than yesterday," Tam said, fierce and gentle at once. "Ondine says the memory doesn't come back. But she also says—" He hesitated, choosing words like a man crossing ice. "She says what was true about you doesn't need remembering to still be true. You paid everything so the valley could live. Everyone here knows it. We're not going to let you be a stranger in the place you saved. So we'll do the work. All of us. Me first."

"Why you first?"

He smiled then, and the smile cracked something open in her chest that she hadn't known was sealed. "Because I loved you before," he said simply. "And loving someone twice—once with memory and once without—that's not a lesser thing. Ondine says it might be the truest kind. The first time, love has history to lean on. The second time, it's just a choice, made fresh, every single day." He held out the honey-cakes. "I brought these because you always—"

He stopped. Started over. "Because I think you liked these. You used to buy them on market days and complain they cost too much and eat them anyway, walking, getting sticky to the elbow, laughing at yourself."

Isandro took the bundle. Unwrapped it. The smell of honey and warm dough rose up, and behind it, faint as a footstep in another room—market light, autumn air, a laugh she couldn't quite hear but could almost feel in her own throat.

"I think," she said carefully, "I'd like to hear it from the beginning. The real beginning. The girl and the book."

"Tomorrow, then? And the day after. However long."

"However long," she agreed.

* * *

He came the next morning, and the morning after that, and the morning after that. He told her about the vault and the prophecy; about boundary stones that burned cold and showed her another sky; about a blind archivist named Ondine who kept the truth alive in a hollow full of dying orchards; about three shards and three heirs and a ferryman who demanded truth be spoken aloud. He told her about the ambush, and the wound he'd taken that still ached in cold weather, and the confession that had nearly ended their friendship before it was tested by fire. He told her about the guild hall, the schism, the theft, the race against the rising moon.

And every morning, Isandro listened, and the pale rectangles on the wallpaper of her mind grew edges. Some mornings she cried without knowing why and Tam waited, patient, until she could breathe again. Some mornings she laughed at things she shouldn't have found funny—a stolen shard hidden in a bread basket, Tam's face when colors came flooding back into a world that had been gray—and the laughter startled her with how familiar it sounded, like a song hummed by someone else in her exact voice.

On the twelfth morning, frost silvered the well-bench, and Tam arrived with his coat collar up and something wrapped in cloth under his arm.

"What's that?" Isandro asked.

He unwrapped it with ceremony. A small pot. Bare branches. Dead-looking, winter-stripped.

"A cutting," he said. "From the roses at Sable Hollow. Ondine's roses—the ones your grandmother planted, the ones that survived everything. I asked her permission. She said—" he cleared his throat— "she said roses are stubborn that way. They bloom back from anything. From anything at all."

Isandro looked at the bare branches, and her hand went, without her deciding, to her pocket. To the thorn in its cracked seal of wax.

"Tam," she said. "I have something I can't explain."

She drew it out and set it in his open palm, and watched his face change—watched recognition move through him like sunrise moving across a field.

"The thorn," he whispered. "You kept it. Through the reforging—you kept—" His voice broke entirely. He pressed the backs of his hands briefly to his eyes, mastered himself, looked at her again. "You pressed this the night we crossed. From the roses at the boundary. You said—if I lost everything, I'd want one thing that was mine, one thing that proved the road happened." He turned the wax seal over, gentle as handling a bird. "You paid with every memory you had. Every face, every name, every morning of your whole life in this valley. Everything except this. This one thorn. Like part of you refused to sign the whole contract."

Isando watched him hold it, and the absences in her mind did not fill—but they leaned. All of them, all at once, leaning toward the small dark shape in his hands like plants toward a window.

"It bloomed," she said. It wasn't a question; she didn't know why she was sure.

"It bloomed," Tam said. "Last week. In Ondine's keeping, in the middle of winter, one white flower on a black branch. She sent me straight here after. She said—" and here his voice steadied into something like reverence— "she said some vows keep themselves. Even when the one who made them can't recall making them. Especially then."

The wind moved through the market square. Lamps guttered and relit themselves along the row. Somewhere a child laughed, bright and unafraid, in a valley where magic ran free in the open light because a woman had once chosen to forget her own name rather than let two worlds starve.

Isandro held out her hand, and Tam placed the thorn back into it, and their fingers stayed touching around it a moment longer than the exchange required.

"Tell me about the ferryman," she said. "Start there. And don't leave anything out."

He smiled—the worn, patient, twice-given smile—and settled onto the frost-silvered bench beside her, and began again.

And in her pocket, against her heart, the thorn lay warm as a promise: *the road happened. The road is still happening. Come walk it with me once more.*
