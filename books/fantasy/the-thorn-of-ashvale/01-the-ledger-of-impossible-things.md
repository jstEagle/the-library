## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Ashvale Guild of Impossible Things kept its books the way other institutions kept their secrets: badly, and with great ceremony.

Kestrel Galloway had been the guild's inventory clerk for six years now, which was long enough to know that the ledgers lied more often than the exhibits did. The exhibits at least had the decency to be exactly as impossible as advertised. The ledgers, on the other hand, insisted that Item 114 — a jar containing the last honest laugh of a dead king — weighed "approximately nothing," when in fact it weighed approximately four pounds, because honesty, whatever else might be said of it, had heft.

"Approximately nothing," Kestrel muttered, and made a small correcting mark in the margin. He was allowed small correcting marks. He was not allowed opinions. This arrangement suited him fine, or so he told himself nightly, with the particular firmness of a man who has stopped examining his own statements for structural integrity.

Midnight inventory happened once a season, after the vaults had gone quiet and before the dawn shift arrived with its kettles and its gossip. It was Kestrel's favorite hour and his loneliest one, which were, he suspected, the same hour wearing two coats.

He worked by lamplight down the long aisle of Shelf Row Three, ticking items against entries.

Item 201: *Caged song, lark's dawn chorus, captured Vellis, spring of the Old King's last year.* The cage was brass and no bigger than a fist, and inside it something moved like weather made of melody. When Kestrel paused beside it — as he always did, though he pretended not to — the song leaned toward him through the bars the way a dog leans toward a hand it remembers. He did not open it. Opening things was above his station. But he hummed three low notes against the wire, an old shepherd's greeting, and felt the song settle, satisfied, like a cat rearranging itself on a warm windowsill.

*You're up late, little bird,* said a voice from the floor.

Kestrel did not jump. Six years of practice had taught him not to jump. He glanced down instead, with the mild expression of a man checking for dust.

A rat sat on the flagstones by his boot. She was grey, one ear notched from some ancient disagreement with a trap, and she regarded him with the patient insolence of a landlady.

"I'm always up late, Bramblewhisker," Kestrel said. "That's what they pay me for."

"They don't pay you enough," said the rat. "I've seen your supper."

"My supper is none of your business."

"It is when I'm eating it."

This was true. There was a long-standing arrangement between Kestrel Galloway and the guild's rats, unwritten, unacknowledged, and — this was the important part, the load-bearing part — entirely deniable. He left crumbs. They left information. Neither party, if asked, would admit the conversation ever went beyond the ordinary squeaking of vermin.

Because of course it did. Of course it didn't. Rats squeaked. Clerks worked. Nobody in the history of Ashvale had ever heard a man talk to a rat and lived comfortably afterward. The Gifted were burned on Founder's Green, politely, with pamphlets printed beforehand explaining why it was necessary. Kestrel had read one of the pamphlets once, standing in a crowd, holding his breath, while a woman who could speak with oxen wept quietly at the stake. Her oxen had cried too. He'd understood every word of both.

So: Kestrel Galloway was not a beast-speaker. Kestrel Galloway was a clerk with an overactive imagination and a habit of narrating his own day aloud when no one was watching, and if the rats sometimes appeared to answer, well, he was tired, and lonely people imagined all sorts of things.

It was a lie he maintained with the discipline of a monk and the craftsmanship of a forger, and he had maintained it so long that it fit him better than his own name.

"You've got that look again," Bramblewhisker said, washing a paw. "The look where you're deciding not to think something."

"I'm doing inventory."

"You're talking to a rat about your supper."

"That's inventory-adjacent." He crouched, checked the seal on Item 201, and noted in the ledger: *Contents agitated but stable.* Then, softer, almost under the breath: "Is it true about the east archive?"

The rat's whiskers stilled. "What have you heard?"

"Nothing. That's the problem. Footsteps where there shouldn't be footsteps. Lamps lit at hours when even Sorrel's asleep. And the third-floor door was re-waxed yesterday — someone oiled the hinges so it wouldn't announce them."

"Hm," said Bramblewhisker, in the tone of a rat who knew considerably more and was deciding how much of it to sell.

"Bramble."

"You'll owe me. Cheese. The yellow kind, not that white chalk you tried to pass off last month. I still have feelings about that."

"The yellow kind. Fine."

She sat back on her haunches, pleased with herself, and delivered the news like a merchant naming a price. "Strangers. Two nights past. Big one, quiet one. Quiet one wore gloves indoors and touched nothing, which is worse than touching everything, if you follow me. They went through the old stacks. The deep ones. The ones even Sorrel only opens when the moon's being difficult."

"What were they looking for?"

"If I knew that, I'd have charged you double." She sniffed. "They didn't find it, whatever it was. Or they found where it wasn't. The quiet one stood in front of the prophecy shelf for a long time. Long enough that I got bored, and I can be bored at things for hours."

Kestrel wrote *no anomalies* in the ledger, which was either the truth or the most efficient lie of his evening, and it was a crowded field.

* * *

Shelf Row Five held the bottled storms.

There were nine of them, each a squat green bottle stoppered with lead and wax, each humming faintly with the memory of a sky that had done something unforgivable. The guild collected storms the way misers collected coins — one per decade, the worst of the lot, bought or bargained out of the heavens by archivists whose names had been struck from the rolls for reasons the rolls declined to specify.

Kestrel liked the storms. They never pretended anything. A storm in a bottle wanted out, and it wanted out loudly, and it made no secret of it, and there was a kind of honesty in that which he found restful.

"All right, all right," he told the nearest bottle, whose humming had climbed half a tone in welcome. "No, you can't come out. No, not for you either," he added, as the second bottle joined in, and then the third, until the whole shelf was thrumming a chord like distant thunder learning to sing rounds. "You are the worst choir in Ashvale and I mean that affectionately."

*He talks to everything,* observed a voice near his ankle. It was a young rat, one of Bramblewhisker's grandkits, still glossy with the arrogance of youth. *Does he know he does it?*

"He knows," said Bramblewhisker, materializing from behind a crate of bottled fog. "He just pretends harder than anyone I've ever met. And I've met a cat that pretended to be furniture for eleven years."

*I thought humans couldn't hear us.*

*This one hears everything,* said Bramblewhisker. *Birds, rats, the moths in the coat room — gods, the moths. He spent an entire afternoon listening to a moth complain about a lampshade. That's how I knew. Nobody listens that well by accident.*

Kestrel, who was technically hearing none of this, finished his count of the storms, logged it, and let his gaze drift down the row without quite settling anywhere. That was the trick of it, the whole architecture of his life: he heard everything and acknowledged nothing. Fluent in bird, fluent in beast, fluent in the grammar of storms and the dialects of rats — and in the one language that mattered, the language of saying a true thing to another person's face, he could barely order soup.

He thought of Sorrel Vane, his mentor, and rehearsed — as he did most nights, and would never perform — the sentence he'd been carrying around for a week now:

*Archivist Vane, strangers have been in the archives. Should I be worried?*

Fourteen words. He'd polished them like stones. And every night he put them away again, because asking meant admitting he'd noticed, noticing meant admitting he'd listened, and listening — really listening, the way he did — was the first domino in a very short line that ended on Founder's Green with pamphlets and polite fire.

Better to be a clerk. Clerks were invisible. Clerks were safe. Clerks ticked boxes next to impossible things and went home to bread and cheese and a silence they told themselves they preferred.

"You're doing the look again," said Bramblewhisker.

"I'm counting storms."

"You're counting sorrows. Different arithmetic." She scampered up onto the shelf, bold as a duchess, and looked him levelly in the eye — which rats should not do, and which he should find alarming, and which instead felt like the most honest conversation he'd have all week. "One day, little bird, someone's going to ask you a question you can't file away. What then?"

"Then I'll invent a new column," Kestrel said, "and enter it twice."

* * *

Row Seven was the debt room, and the debt room smelled of vellum and grievance.

Here the guild kept its financial impossibilities: promissory notes signed by the dead, interest accrued in dreams, and — in a locked case of black oak, because it deserved the drama — the Duke of Marrowgate's marker. A hundred years ago the duke's grandfather had borrowed something from the guild. Not money. The ledgers were coy on the point, in the way of documents that have been read by too many lawyers, but the item was listed as *"one favor, callable at any time, transferable to the holder of this page,"* and the ink used to write it was still wet.

A century old, and still wet. Kestrel had tested it once, years ago, with a fingertip and a fool's curiosity, and the ink had come away on his skin and stayed there for three days, faintly silver, no matter how he scrubbed. During those three days he'd dreamed of a battlefield he'd never visited, and woken each morning with the taste of iron and old smoke in his mouth, and decided — firmly, clerkishly — that he'd eaten something disagreeable.

He checked the case lock now, noted it sound, and did not touch the page. Some habits were self-preserving.

From the shadows along the wall came a rustle, then another, and then the night's delegation assembled itself at his feet: Bramblewhisker, two of her grandkits, a fat old male everyone called the Alderman, and a newcomer Kestrel didn't recognize — a lean brown rat with river-mud still on her flanks, who looked at the shelves the way pilgrims look at shrines.

"This is him?" the newcomer said. "The soft-foot? The one who leaves the good cheese?"

"The very same," said Bramblewhisker. "Mind your manners. He's shy."

"I'm not shy," Kestrel said. "I'm discreet. There's a difference, and the difference is that discretion keeps me alive."

*See?* said Bramblewhisker to the newcomer. *Deniability. He's very proud of it.*

"And you," Kestrel said, tapping his pencil against the ledger, "are very lucky I'm proud of it, because otherwise you'd all be paying rent like respectable vermin."

The delegation laughed — a dry, whispering sound, like paper turning pages. Even now, after six years, that sound undid him a little. Not the magic of it; he'd stopped thinking of it as magic somewhere in the second year, the way a fish stops thinking of water. It was the company of it. Somewhere between the cages and the bottles and the cases, in the margins where the guild kept nothing official, Kestrel Galloway was known. Known thoroughly, known without ceremony, known by an audience that had never once required him to be less than what he was.

And he could not keep a single word of it.

The thought arrived on schedule, the way the dawn shift arrived, unwelcome and punctual. Here was the shape of his life, laid out in the ledger's neat columns: a man fluent in everything except honesty. Who could hear the grief in a lark's caged song and the grievances of a duke's century-old ink, and who could not say to his own mentor, *someone was here, I am afraid, I am more than I pretend.* Who talked to rats because rats couldn't betray him, and told himself stories about overactive imaginations because stories were safer than selves.

"One day," Bramblewhisker said again, quieter now, watching him with her notched ear cocked, "you'll tell me why you hide. Not tonight. But one day."

"Inventory's nearly done," Kestrel said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

He finished Row Seven, closed the ledger, and carried it back toward the front desk, the delegation trailing him like a small grey shadow with opinions. The guild breathed around him in the dark — the songs shifting in their sleep, the storms murmuring their chord, the wet ink dreaming its iron-and-smoke dreams in its locked case. Everything in the building was impossible except the clerk, and the clerk was the biggest impossibility of all, and nobody had written *him* down anywhere.

Which was, he would remember later, the exact kind of thing a person ought not to think in a house full of prophecies.

At the desk, the return stack waited: volumes needing repair, spines cracked, boards sprung. Routine work. Tomorrow's work. His eye passed over them the way it did every night —

— and stopped.

Near the bottom of the stack lay a book he did not know, though he knew the collection the way he knew his own hands. It was old beyond old, rebound at least twice, its leather gone the color of dried blood. And wedged against its spine was a repair ticket in Archivist Sorrel Vane's own careful hand, dated today:

*Binding failed during cataloguing. Rebind at once. Do NOT read contents. — S.V.*

Sorrel had never in six years told him not to read a book. That was rather the opposite of what mentors were for.

Kestrel stood in the lamplight, the ledger under his arm, the rats gone silent at his feet, and looked at the little ticket with its tidy instruction, and felt the first cold thread of something that was absolutely not curiosity slide down the back of his neck.

"Well," he said, to no one in particular, and to everyone listening. "Isn't that interesting."

He picked up the book anyway.
