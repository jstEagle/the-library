## 2. Ink Older Than the Author

The binding of the damaged volume had been bothering Kestrel Galloway for three days, which was, by his standards, a long time to be bothered by anything.

He was a patient man. Patience was the whole architecture of his life. He rose at the same hour, ate the same breakfast, walked the same route from his lodgings on Cooper's Lane to the Ashvale Guild of Impossible Things, and spent his days among objects that would have driven a less disciplined man mad with wonder — and he catalogued them. Neatly. In triplicate.

But this book. This *book*.

It sat on his workbench now under the good lamp, the one with the green glass shade that made everything look slightly drowned and mysterious, which Kestrel found helped him think. The volume was a wreck. Its spine had split like overripe fruit, boards hanging by threads of rotted cord, and someone — some archivist of an earlier, lazier century — had tried to mend it with wax and prayer. The leather was the color of old blood gone brown. It smelled of dust and, faintly, of something else. Something like a struck match in a closed room.

"Catalogue number eleven-forty," Kestrel said aloud, because saying things aloud kept him honest, or at least kept him company. "Damaged folio, provenance unknown, condition: tragic."

From the wall behind the shelving came a small scratching, then a voice like dry leaves being folded. *"Tragic is right. That thing's been moaning about its spine since winter."*

Kestrel did not startle. He had learned, years ago, not to startle, because startling led to questions, and questions led to conversations he could not afford to have. Instead he reached into his coat pocket, produced the heel of yesterday's bread, and set it on the shelf's edge without looking up.

"You're gossiping again, Pibbet."

*"I'm reporting,"* said the rat, emerging into the lamplight — a large brown fellow with one ear notched from some ancient disagreement with a mousetrap. *"There's a difference. A rat reports. A magpie gossips. You want gossip, go talk to the crows on the roof, though fair warning, they'll lie to you twice before you finish asking."*

"And what has your reporting turned up tonight?"

Pibbet took the bread with the delicacy of a bishop accepting alms. *"That book's been complaining for months and nobody's listened. Old Sorrel said leave it till after the inventory. But it's getting worse. Books are like teeth, clerk. Ignore them long enough and they'll make sure you regret it."*

Kestrel smiled despite himself. This was the small treason of his life, the one indulgence he permitted: the talking. Not talking-to, exactly — he'd never once, in all his years at the guild, let himself think too hard about what it was he did when he answered the rats, or soothed the caged songbird whose melody had outlived its composer, or apologized to the bottled storm on the high shelf when it grumbled against its glass. He simply did it, the way other men whistled, and he kept a careful, locked door between that part of himself and every other part, and behind that door he did not look.

A man fluent in everything except honesty. He'd have laughed at the description if anyone had ever described him, which no one had, because describing people required noticing them, and Kestrel had built a career out of going unnoticed.

"Well," he said, "then we'd better listen."

* * *

Rebinding was the only task at the guild that Kestrel truly loved, though he'd have denied loving anything, love being a word with too many witnesses. There was a meditation in it: the paring of leather, the sewing of cords, the way a broken thing became whole again through nothing but attention. Most of the guild's treasures were impossible — songs in cages, storms in bottles, a duke's debt sealed in amber — but books were merely miraculous, which was better. Books did what they promised.

He worked for an hour in the comfortable rhythm of it. Fold new endpapers. Sew on fresh cords. And then, as he eased the ruined spine away from the text block, the book did something no honest book should do.

It exhaled.

Not wind. Not dust. An actual breath, warm and papery, against his knuckles, and beneath it a smell like the moment before lightning. Kestrel sat very still. On the shelf, Pibbet went quiet mid-chew.

"...Did you feel that?" Kestrel asked.

*"Felt it,"* the rat said, and there was something new in his leaf-dry voice. Something Kestrel had never heard there before. *"Clerk. I don't like it. Rats know when a floor's wrong. That book is a wrong floor."*

"It's a book."

*"So am I just a rat."*

Kestrel looked at him. Pibbet looked back, whiskers trembling, and neither of them pursued the thought, because pursuing thoughts was how men like Kestrel ended up noticed.

Instead, he did what an archivist does. He opened the book.

The first pages were ordinary enough — a ledger, actually, bound inside what he'd assumed was a treatise. Entries in a fine antique hand. *Acquired: one grief, medium-sized, from the widow Aldery. Acquired: the last honest mirror in Ashvale, cracked but functional.* The Guild of Impossible Things had been collecting far longer than Kestrel had worked for it; he'd known that abstractly, the way you know a building has foundations. Here were the foundations, itemized.

He turned pages. The hand changed. Decades passed between entries. Somewhere past the middle of the book, the ledger stopped being a ledger and became something else — entries that read less like acquisitions and more like instructions. *Note: the vessel must be unremarkable. Note: ensure the archives are understaffed on the relevant night. Note: he must find it himself. It cannot be given.*

Kestrel frowned. *He.*

And then he reached the page.

Later — much later, in rooms with worse lighting and higher stakes — Kestrel would try to remember the exact order of what happened next, and he never quite could. What he remembered was the date first, because dates were his native language. Tonight's date. Not approximately. Exactly. Written in ink that had dried a hundred years before Kestrel Galloway was born.

And beside the date, in that same fine antique hand:

*The Thorn is revealed. The clerk finds the book.*

His name followed. His full name. Kestrel Ambrose Galloway — including the middle name his mother had taken to her grave and told no living soul, a name he had never written down anywhere, not once, not even in the privacy of his own ledgers.

The lamp hummed. The storm in its bottle murmured against the glass, restless. Pibbet had stopped breathing, or rats' version of breathing, which Kestrel supposed was possible.

"There's been a mistake," Kestrel said. His voice came out level. He was rather proud of how level it came out. "Obviously. Someone entered this recently. A forgery. A joke. Fenwick has a sense of humor, of a sort—"

*"Fenwick can't spell 'quill,'"* said Pibbet.

"A very committed joke, then." Kestrel tilted the page toward the lamp. Ink doesn't lie about its age — any binder knows this. Iron-gall ink darkens over decades, browns over centuries, blooms and cracks in patterns no forger can fake without faking everything else around it. This ink was deep, settled, a century old at minimum. And it sat *under* the page's water stain, which meant it predated the stain, which meant—

Which meant a dead man had written Kestrel's secret name a hundred years ago and left it here, in a damaged book, in a guild archive, waiting.

Waiting for a clerk to rebind it.

*He must find it himself. It cannot be given.*

Kestrel closed the book. Then he opened it again, because closing it felt like the sort of thing a frightened man does, and Kestrel was not frightened. He was curious. He was professionally curious. Curiosity was practically in his job description, filed somewhere between *dusts shelves* and *inventories caged songs*.

"Cold," he said aloud, testing the word. "The room's cold. That's all this is. Drafty old building, late hour, a strange book breathing on me. Any reasonable man would feel a chill."

*"I'm a reasonable rat,"* Pibbet said quietly, *"and I'm under the shelf now."*

"Pibbet."

*"Don't 'Pibbet' me. Read the rest of the page, clerk. Wrong floors get worse if you stand on them wondering."*

Kestrel read the rest of the page.

Below his name, the entry continued in that patient antique hand, each line laid down like bricks:

*On this night the Thorn shall be revealed, and the city shall learn what it has forgotten. Let the factions come. Let them bid. The vessel will not know what he carries until the hour turns, and by then the choice will be upon him, and the choice is the whole of the design.*

*Let no one tell him. He must read it in his own time, in his own hand's reach, believing it chance.*

*It is not chance. Nothing here is chance. I have arranged every stone on his path, and I am sorry, and it changes nothing.*

Kestrel set the book down with great care, the way you set down something that might be asleep.

His hands were steady. He noted this with detached interest, the way he noted catalogue numbers. His heart, however, was doing something unscheduled, hammering against the locked door in his chest where he kept all the things he didn't look at, and something on the other side of that door had begun, very softly, to laugh.

Coincidence. All right. Let us speak of coincidence like professionals. Names repeat. Galloway was not a rare name — half of Ashvale's lower wards could claim it. Ambrose was commoner still. Some ancestor, perhaps. Some namesake. A century-old prophecy naming *a* Kestrel Ambrose Galloway, clerk, on *this* specific night, in a guild he happened to work in, in a book he happened to be rebinding, with his mother's secret buried in the middle of it like a pin in a cake.

Any reasonable man would call it chance.

Kestrel looked at the bread crumbs on the shelf. At the storm muttering in its bottle. At the green lamp making everything look drowned.

"I'm going to finish the binding," he announced to the empty room, "because the binding needs finishing, and because I am a professional, and because a book — even a wrong book — deserves better than to be abandoned mid-repair out of superstition."

*"That's the most frightened sentence I've ever heard,"* said Pibbet from under the shelf.

"Yes," Kestrel agreed. "It is, isn't it."

He picked up his needle. He did not pick up the book. Between the two lay the rest of the night, and somewhere down in the vaults below, in the dark where the guild kept the things it couldn't sell, something shifted its weight — and the rats of Ashvale, in their hundreds, in their thousands, all at once and everywhere, lifted their heads toward the surface and began, very softly, to say his name.

* * *

He finished the binding. Of course he finished the binding. A man's character is measured by what he does with his hands while his mind is screaming, and Kestrel's hands sewed four perfect bands and pasted down the endpapers and pressed the fresh spine until it gleamed, and all the while the book lay open beside him to that page, watching him work with the patience of a thing that had already won.

By the third hour, curiosity had won the argument he'd been losing. He washed the paste from his fingers, dried them thoroughly — one did not touch old ink with damp hands, his mentor Sorrel Vane had taught him, *ink remembers more than paper does*, she'd said, laughing her warm laugh — and turned back to the page.

Sorrel. The thought arrived sideways, the way important thoughts do. Sorrel Vane, who had hired him eight years ago off the strength of nothing but a neat hand and a nervous manner. Sorrel, who had assigned him to the archives, to the night inventories, to the rebinding queue. Sorrel, who always seemed to know which damaged volume needed him next.

*Ensure the archives are understaffed on the relevant night.*

*He must find it himself.*

"No," Kestrel said to the lamp.

*"No what?"* asked Pibbet, who had crept back up but kept the shelf between them, the way you keep a table between yourself and a dog that's started listening to voices.

"No, she wouldn't. She's—" Kind. Warm. The only person in Ashvale who greeted him by name each morning and remembered he took honey in his tea. The only person who had ever, in eight years, asked him a question about himself and waited for the answer. "She's my friend."

*"Rats don't have friends either, clerk. We have arrangements. Yours ever felt like more than an arrangement?"*

Kestrel didn't answer, which was itself an answer, and they both let it lie there between them, uncomfortable as a wet boot.

He read the page again instead. Then the pages after it. The handwriting stayed constant — the same fine antique script, unhurried, terribly certain — but the content shifted, over decades, from instruction to observation to something almost like confession. *The vessel grows well. He keeps his gift hidden even from himself, which is more than I managed at his age. Tomorrow I will praise his filing.* And further on, in ink a mere thirty years old: *He trusts me. I have made sure of it. May whatever gods remain forgive the length of the leash.*

Kestrel put the book down again. Walked to the window. Looked out at Ashvale at midnight — the crooked rooftops, the palace hill with its lamps like a fallen constellation, the harbor breathing fog. A city that had no idea it was a stage.

Behind him, the book rustled. Pages turning themselves, gently, like a sleeper shifting. When he turned back, it had opened to a fresh page, and the ink there was not a century old.

It was not yet dry.

One line, in that same patient hand, adding itself letter by letter as he watched:

*Hello, Kestrel. Do close the cover. You'll catch cold.*

He closed the cover.

Then he stood in the drowned-green light of his lamp, in the archive where he had spent eight safe invisible years, holding a book that knew his mother's secrets and his mentor's handwriting and the exact weight of his loneliness — and above him, through the floorboards, came the sound of the guild's front doors opening, though the guild had closed hours ago, and footsteps crossing the entrance hall. Two sets. Three. Measured. Unhurried. The footsteps of people who belonged here after hours, or believed they did.

Pibbet appeared at the edge of the shelf, ears flat, whiskers shaking.

*"Clerk,"* the rat whispered. *"Strangers searched the archives last night too. I was going to tell you tomorrow. I'm telling you now. Whatever that book is — it isn't the only thing in this building that's awake."*

The footsteps stopped outside the archive door.

Kestrel blew out the lamp.
