## 3. What the Rats Have Seen

The rats came to Kestrel at half past nine, in the manner of colleagues who had waited all morning to lodge a complaint.

He was at his desk in the inventory office, third floor of the Ashvale Guild of Impossible Things, cataloguing a crate of bottled storms that had arrived from the coast and were sulking about it. The bottles kept rattling on their straw like teeth in a jar whenever he turned his back, which made accurate record-keeping a challenge. He had learned to write fast and look away often — a skill that had, over six years, made him the finest clerk the guild had ever employed, though no one knew precisely why.

*The tall one,* said a voice from under the filing cabinet. *The grey one with the torn ear. He wants to talk.*

"Send him in," Kestrel murmured, without moving his lips much. It was an old habit. Six years of practice will make anything smooth.

A rat emerged from beneath the cabinet with the unhurried dignity of a magistrate. She was large for her kind, dust-grey, with a scarred muzzle and whiskers that had clearly seen arguments. She climbed the leg of his desk, sat on the corner of his ledger, and left four small footprints in the wet ink, because she could.

*You're doing it again,* she said.

"Doing what, Bramblewhisker?"

*Naming me. You know we name ourselves. You gave me that name two years ago and I've been stuck with it ever since, and my daughters think I'm pretentious.*

"My apologies, Madam Whose-Name-Is-Your-Own-Business." He dipped his pen. "You wanted something."

The rat groomed one paw, which among guild rats was the equivalent of clearing one's throat at considerable length.

*Strangers,* she said at last. *In the archives. Last night.*

Kestrel's pen stopped moving. A storm bottle chose that exact moment to rattle, and he pressed his palm flat against the glass until it settled, muttering inwardly. *Later. Behave.* The bottle subsided, sulking.

"Strangers," he repeated aloud, quietly. "How many?"

*Four. Maybe five. They wore soft boots.* She said this the way another creature might say *they carried knives*, with the same tone of professional disapproval. Soft boots were, in the moral vocabulary of Ashvale's rodents, very nearly an obscenity. *They went through the deep stacks. The old shelves. The ones nobody visits except you and the Vane woman.*

"They didn't come up here?"

*No. They didn't touch your office. But they touched everything else.* Her whiskers trembled with remembered indignation. *They opened the cabinet where we keep the winter crumbs. They read the labels on the preserved cheese. One of them sat down on the third shelf of the western stacks and ate a biscuit and left the crumbs, Kestrel. Left them. Like animals.*

"That does sound like animals," Kestrel agreed gravely.

*I'm serious.*

"I know you are." He set down his pen. Through the office window, Ashvale was doing its morning business below — carts in the lane, gulls arguing over the fish market, the bell of the Chantry tolling the hour with its usual air of being put upon. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly where it should be.

Except that someone had been in the archives by night, wearing soft boots, reading labels in the deep stacks.

"And they were looking for something?" he asked. "Or just rude?"

*Looking. They had a lantern with a green shade, the kind that doesn't show through cracks. They pulled books and held them up to the light. Checked the bindings.* The rat paused, and when she spoke again there was less indignation in her voice and something more careful underneath it. *They spent a long time at the prophecy shelf, Kestrel. The damaged one. The one you've been rebinding.*

The cold that went through him then was small and precise, like a needle finding the gap between two ribs.

"The Thornwood Codex," he said.

*If that's what you call it. It's the brown one that smells like vinegar and secrets.*

Everything smells like secrets to a rat; their whole world was conducted by nose. But he knew the volume she meant. Everyone in the guild did, distantly, the way one knows about a relative nobody invites to dinner: a century-old book of prophecies, water-damaged, half its pages fused together, catalogued under a number so dull no scholar had requested it in living memory. Kestrel had been rebinding it for three weeks now, a slow labor of hide glue and linen thread, because Sorrel had asked him to and because he liked work that let his hands be busy while his mind wandered.

His mind had wandered, three nights ago, to a page near the middle of that book. A page with a date on it. Tonight's date.

And a name beside it. His own.

He had told himself it was coincidence. There were only so many names in the world, after all, and Kestrel Galloway was not such an extraordinary collection of syllables that no century-old scribe could have stumbled into it. He had told himself this while his hands shook slightly, and then he had told himself the shaking was the glue fumes, and he had believed himself, mostly, in the way one believes a roof will hold — not because one has inspected the beams lately, but because the alternative is unbearable.

Now strangers with green-shaded lanterns had stood at that same shelf in the night.

"Did they find what they wanted?" he asked.

*They took nothing,* said the rat. *But they measured things. With string. And one of them drew the shelf in a little book. And before they left, the tall one — soft boots, smell of iron and lamp oil — he stood in front of the codex for a long time and said one word.*

She stopped, enjoying the pause. Rats have a strong theatrical instinct; it is why cats underestimate them.

"What word?"

*"Soon."*

* * *

Sorrel Vane received the news with a smile.

That was the first wrong thing, although Kestrel didn't fully register it as wrong until later, turning it over in the dark like a coin he suspected of being clipped. At the time, it simply looked like Sorrel: Archivist Sorrel Vane, silver-haired, quick-eyed, wrapped in a shawl the color of dried roses, standing amid her beloved chaos of the ground-floor sorting room with ink on her fingers and warmth on her face like weather.

"Strangers in the archives!" she cried, when he found her between a crate of bottled lullabies and a cage whose label read *ONE (1) SONG, UNCAGED, HANDLE WITH LONGING*. "Well. That's not so very alarming, is it? Scholars break in everywhere. Half the universities would rob us blind if we didn't lock the doors, and the other half if we did."

"They weren't scholars, I don't think." Kestrel kept his voice easy, clerk's voice, bored even. "They searched the prophecy stacks specifically. They measured the shelving. One of them said—" he hesitated, and decided, and said it— "*soon*."

Something crossed Sorrel's face. He would spend a long time afterward trying to decide what it had been, and the honest answer was that it had been nothing — a perfect, polished nothing, a shutter drawn across a window so smoothly that only the absence of light marked its passing. At the time, tired and rattled, he read it as concern.

"Well," she said softly. "Well, well." She patted his arm. Her hand was small and dry and strong, a hand that had handled ten thousand fragile things and broken none of them. "You did right to tell me, Kestrel. You always do right. That's why I keep you close."

The words landed warmly, and yet some small animal part of him — the part that understood speech in every language except the human ones — noticed that she had answered none of his questions. Not who. Not how they'd entered. Not what *soon* might mean. She had simply taken his alarm out of his hands the way one takes a heavy parcel from a guest, smiling, saying *let me*, and carried it off into another room.

"You'll look into it?" he asked.

"Of course I will." She squeezed his arm. "I'll have the locks changed. I'll speak to Wrenn about the night watch. And you—" her eyes crinkled, "—you will finish that binding, won't you? The codex has waited a hundred years; it can bear a few more weeks of your careful hands. There's no hurry. There's never any hurry with books. They're patient things."

*Patient things.* He thought of the page with his name on it, waiting a century in the dark like a trap with the spring slowly compressing, and smiled back at her, and hated himself a little for the smile, and did it anyway.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The rats say the intruders checked the bindings of the books on the prophecy shelf. Held them up to light. As though they were looking for—" he groped for it— "for a maker's mark. For who'd bound them. Or written them."

For a moment — only a moment — Sorrel Vane looked very old. Not elderly, which she was not, but *old* in the way that furniture is old, in the way that grudges are old, in the way that certain trees are older than the kingdoms planted around them. Then the warmth came back like sun from behind a cloud and she laughed, a bright unbothered laugh.

"Bindings! Kestrel, my dear boy, if I worried every time somebody took an interest in our dusty prophets, I'd have been in my grave decades ago from pure fretting." She steered him gently toward the stairs. "Go on. Back to your storms. And eat something at lunch — you go thin when you're worried, like a candle in a draft."

He went. He was halfway up the stairs before he realized she had never once asked him how the rats had spoken to him, or why a clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things reported intelligence gathered from vermin as casually as other men reported the weather.

She hadn't asked because, he supposed, this was the Guild of Impossible Things, and a clerk who chattered to rats was merely one more item in the catalogue.

It was a comforting explanation. He held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank, and tried not to notice how many nail-holes riddled the wood.

* * *

The rest of the day refused to behave.

The storm bottles had to be moved to the climate vault, and they complained the entire way, thundering mutinously inside their glass whenever the trolley jolted. The duke's debt came due — the Guild held paper on Duke Aldery's second son, who had wished for talent and received it, and the interest was payable in memories, and the courier sent to collect had come back weeping and rich. Kestrel filed the transaction, stamped it, and thought: *everyone pays. Everyone owes. The question is only what currency.*

At dusk he went down to the deep stacks, ostensibly to fetch a ream of archival tissue, actually because Bramblewhisker had sent a message via the chimney-rats — the guild's fastest couriers, being neither seen nor suspected — that said simply: *come. bring bread. bring questions.*

The deep stacks smelled of dust and vellum and the particular cold patience of paper. Kestrel sat down on the floor at the end of the prophecy aisle, broke a loaf, and crumbled it along the flagstones, and within minutes the shadows developed whiskers.

There were eleven of them, which meant the matter was serious. When guild rats assembled in numbers, it was either a wedding or a war council, and nobody had announced a wedding.

*Show him,* said Bramblewhisker.

A young rat — one of the chimney-rats, quick and glossy-eyed — trotted forward and laid something at Kestrel's boot. A button. Brass, military stamp, the kind used on palace livery coats.

Then a twist of black thread, waxed, of the sort used to bind official dispatches.

Then — and Kestrel's mouth went dry around the bread he was chewing — a scrap of paper, torn, bearing four words in a cramped clerk's hand:

*...the vessel must be identified...*

"They were looking for a person," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the stacks, too loud, flattened by all that patient paper. "Not a book. A person. Someone the prophecy points to."

*We can't read,* said Bramblewhisker, with dignity, *but we know what men look like when they find what they're looking for. The tall one smiled when he read that page. Smiled and folded it away and said to the others: "Then it's true, and tomorrow night we'll know the name."*

Tomorrow night. The date on the page. His name beside it.

Kestrel sat very still among the crumbling bread, in the aisle of a hundred-year-old prophecies, and felt the first hairline crack run through something inside him that he had always assumed was load-bearing. Six years he had worked in this building. Six years Sorrel Vane had taught him binding and cataloguing and the gentle art of handling impossible things without letting them handle you. She had found him half-starved at fifteen, sleeping in the stable of the menagerie district because the horses there talked kindly and he'd nowhere else that felt safe, and she had looked at him once, sharply, knowingly, and hired him without asking a single question he couldn't answer safely.

He had always assumed she hadn't noticed his gift.

He had assumed, in fact, that nobody had ever noticed it, and that the noticing was his alone to fear.

*You're doing the face,* said Bramblewhisker.

"What face?"

*The one where you pretend you're fine. We see it a lot. Humans do it constantly. It's exhausting to watch.*

"I'm fine," Kestrel said.

All eleven rats looked at him with the flat, unblinking candor available only to creatures with no stake in politeness.

"Very well," he admitted. "Perhaps I'm not entirely fine. Perhaps a man whose name appears in a century-old prophecy, on tonight's date, while strangers with palace buttons search the shelves by night, might be permitted a small measure of *not fine*."

*What will you do?*

What would he do? He turned the question over. Go to Sorrel — but he had already gone to Sorrel, and had come away warmed like a guest and emptied like a mark. Go to the Watch — the Watch, in Ashvale, was a punchline even the gulls had heard. Do nothing — do nothing, finish the binding, deliver the book with his own careful hands into whatever hands were waiting for it, and discover tomorrow night whether his name on that page had been a coincidence or a delivery address.

The crack widened. Loyalty, he saw suddenly, could look very much like a leash when you finally turned your head and noticed the collar. He had been a good clerk. He had been a quiet, grateful, obedient keeper of other people's impossible things. And somewhere above him in this building, someone he loved had perhaps been writing him into a story for years, the way one writes a ledger entry — neat, patient, in a practiced hand.

"Tomorrow night," he said slowly, "I'm going to finish the codex."

Bramblewhisker's ears pricked.

"Every page. Even the fused ones. I'm going to read all of it before anyone else touches it." He brushed the crumbs from his knees, and the rats fell upon the remains with sudden fervor, the council dissolving into supper, because rats are wise enough not to waste a crisis that comes with bread. "And then," he added, almost to himself, "I'm going to ask Sorrel Vane a question I should have asked six years ago."

*Which is?*

"Why she hired a stable-boy who talks to horses."

The chimney-rat paused mid-crumb. Somewhere far off in the stacks, a caged song shifted in its sleep, and the archive listened.

Above, faint through floors of stone and paper, Kestrel heard the guild doors being locked for the night — and beneath the familiar sound, softer, deliberate, the tread of soft boots crossing the entrance hall, going down, toward the vaults, long after visiting hours had ended.
