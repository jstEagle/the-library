## 4. A Visitor from the Old War

Maren Oduya had been a liar for eleven years, and she had learned one thing above all others: the best disguise was not a false face but a true occupation, performed badly enough to be forgettable.

So she was, tonight, a laundress.

The Guild of Impossible Things employed forty-one people whose names appeared in its ledgers, and another dozen or so who existed only in the margins — night-soil men, lamp trimmers, and twice a week, a woman from the Brightwater Laundry House who came to collect the archivists' collars and cuffs and returned them white as first snow. The real laundress was named Tansy. Maren had watched her for three weeks. She had learned that Tansy hummed when she climbed stairs, counted her coins twice before pocketing them, and complained about the guild's ink stains with the weary devotion of a woman who had made peace with her lot. Maren could do all three. She had practiced the humming until it bored even her.

The collar she carried in her basket now belonged to Archivist Sorrel Vane, and it had cost Maren a full afternoon of charm to acquire legitimately, because Sorrel Vane did not strike Maren as a woman one charmed so much as endured. But the collar was the key. A person carrying a specific collar for a specific archivist walked through the guild's front hall without a second glance. A person carrying *any* collar got stopped at the door and asked questions with teeth in them.

"Evening, Tansy," said the doorwarden, a broad man with a nose like a dropped dumpling. He didn't look up from his dice.

"Evening." She hummed two bars. Complaint about ink: ready if needed.

He waved her through.

*Inside*, she thought, and her heart did the small traitorous leap it always did, the one that said *you are about to do something dangerous and you love it*. Eleven years of courier work for the Warrens, and the leap never dulled. That was either a gift or an illness. Her handler, old Brannoc, said it was both.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a building that had once been a temple, then a granary, then a temple again, and had finally settled into its true calling by simply accumulating. From outside it looked like any other pile of old Ashvale stone. From inside it went on forever. Maren had memorized the public floor plan — reading rooms, appraisal halls, the auction gallery that opened perhaps six times a year — but the public floor plan was a courtesy. The real guild was beneath and behind and inside itself, floors folded into floors, and somewhere in that nested dark was the book.

*The Ledger of the Coming War.* That was what the rebels called it; nobody knew its true title. A century old, sealed by the guild's own charter from all eyes but its keepers, and — according to the fragmentary account of a dying informant — containing a prophecy concerning the Thorn: the last vessel of the war-god Ashkarath's power, due to be revealed on a date that was, Maren checked her memory against the calendar sewn into her sleeve, four days from tonight.

Four days to find a book that a hundred-year-old institution had hidden from everyone including, possibly, itself.

She climbed toward the archive level, humming, counting imaginary coins, and told herself — as she always did — that this was simple reconnaissance. Get in, confirm the book's location, get out. Report to Brannoc. Let wiser heads decide.

Wiser heads. In eleven years, the wiser heads had decided on exactly nothing, because deciding required consensus, and consensus among the Warrens' council moved at the speed of continental drift. Meanwhile palace spymaster Corvus Hale was buying up grain contracts along the border like a man setting a table for guests he intended to starve. Meanwhile the guild was quietly auctioning things that should not exist. Meanwhile war came, patient as winter.

No. If the book could be found, she would find it herself, and read it, and bring back not a report but an answer.

* * *

The archive corridor smelled of dust, vellum, and something faintly metallic — old iron, maybe, or old blood, hard to say in a building like this. Lamps burned low behind green glass. Doors stood in ranked silence, each banded with brass labels in a script Maren couldn't read, which struck her as a design flaw or a boast, depending on your perspective.

She had planned for clerks. Archives kept clerks the way rivers kept water.

What she had not planned for was the young man kneeling in the open doorway of the bindery, talking to a rat.

Not *to* a rat in the way madmen and children talk to rats — no coaxing, no performing. He was crouched with a stack of leather offcuts beside him, sleeves rolled, ink to the elbows, and he was speaking in a low, easy murmur, the way one speaks to a colleague. And the rat — a big brown fellow perched on the offcut pile — was *answering*. Not chittering randomly. There was rhythm to it, exchange, the give-and-take of conversation. Maren had grown up in the dock districts where rats were neighbors rather than pests, and she had never once heard one sound like it was saying something worth hearing.

She pressed herself flat behind a shelf of ledgers, pulse tapping at her throat.

The young man laughed — softly, as though laughter were something he rationed — and said, "Well, I can't help it if he's superstitious. You'd hoard buttons too, if everyone you'd ever trusted was a cat."

The rat made a sound like a rusty hinge with opinions.

"That's slander," the young man said gravely. "I have never once lost a button."

Maren felt the strangest sensation, watching him: recognition. Not of the face — she didn't know the face — but of the *craft*. This was what she looked like, she thought, when she thought no one could see her. This was the private fluency, the thing kept under the floorboards of the self. Whatever this clerk was doing, whatever impossible grammar passed between him and the rat, he did it the way she lied: easily, constantly, and with the careful loneliness of someone certain it must stay secret forever.

A beast-speaker. In the Guild of Impossible Things, hiding in plain sight as a clerk. She almost laughed herself. The city was full of disguised people; it was practically the municipal religion.

Then practicality reasserted itself. A witness was a witness. If he saw her here — a laundress four corridors past any laundry — questions would follow, and questions were how infiltrations died.

She began to ease backward along the shelf.

And her elbow caught a ledger.

It slid. She caught it — mostly — but the corner knocked the shelf, and the shelf answered with a wooden groan that in the archive's hush sounded like a cathedral bell.

The talking stopped.

Silence, thick and total. Maren held the ledger to her chest like a shield and calculated her options. Run: no. Bluff: possibly. There was a legitimate reason a laundress might wander, if one squinted — lost, looking for the collection point, too embarrassed to ask the doorwarden directions. It was thin. It would have to be spun fast and spun well.

Footsteps. Light ones. Stopping just short of the aisle.

"You can come out," said the young man's voice, mild and unhurried. "Unless you're a thief, in which case I should mention the ledger you're holding is *Catalogue of Sentient Debts, Volume Nine*, and it bites."

* * *

Maren stepped into the lamplight wearing Tansy's apologetic smile.

"I'm sorry — I've gone and lost my way, haven't I. The collection point's supposed to be off the east stair, and the fellow at the door said—" she sighed, shook her head, became briefly and convincingly flustered. "I'll confess it plainly: I took a wrong turn and got nosy. It's a wicked habit. My mum said I'd end up in trouble for peeking, and here we are."

The clerk regarded her. He was younger than she'd guessed — mid-twenties, perhaps — with ink-stained fingers, tired eyes, and the kind of face built for going unnoticed, which meant he noticed everything. He glanced at her basket. At the collar lying on top.

"Sorrel Vane's linen," he said.

"You know your archivists' cuffs."

"I rebind what they ruin." Something flickered across his face at the name Vane — quick, unreadable, gone. "East stair's back the way you came, then left at the case of bottled weather. You'll hear it. Don't tap the glass; the hailstorm holds grudges."

"Thank you kindly." She turned, then paused, letting curiosity sit naturally on her features — which was easy, because some of it was real. "Begging pardon, but... were you talking to that rat? Before?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat too long.

"Talking *at* it," he said. "It's a bad habit. Quiet rooms make chatterboxes of us all." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm and modest, and Maren thought: *there it is. The other lie. Beautifully done.*

Because she had heard him before the silence fell. She had heard the rat answer, in cadence, in turn. Whatever story he was telling now — shy clerk, talks to animals the way lonely men talk to themselves — it was a cover, and it was good, and he was deploying it on her right now with the same easy craft she'd used on the doorwarden downstairs.

Two liars, standing in an archive, each politely pretending not to recognize the other's needlework.

"Well," she said warmly. "Between us, I think it's lovely. Talking to things that don't answer back. My grandmother swore her hens understood every word, and honestly, they ran a tidier coop for it."

"Your grandmother sounds wise."

"She was ruthless. Different thing entirely." She hitched up her basket. "Thank you for the directions. Sorry again for startling you."

"You didn't startle me," he said, and there was something odd in it, something that almost sounded honest. "I don't startle easily."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "I don't suppose you do."

* * *

She found the east stair, and the case of bottled weather — she heard it before she saw it, a muffled muttering of rain behind glass, and one jar of fog that seemed to sigh when she passed, like a dog left home too long — and she did not go down the stair.

Instead she went up.

Because here was the arithmetic that had changed while she knelt behind that shelf: a beast-speaker clerk in the bindery, hiding his gift, working alone at night in a guild full of secrets. People hid gifts when the world punished them. People worked alone at night when they were trusted with quiet work. And quiet, trusted, secret-keeping people were precisely the sort who got assigned to damaged volumes — to rebinding, to repair, to handling books too fragile or too forbidden for ordinary hands.

If anyone in this building had touched the Ledger of the Coming War lately, it was him.

She climbed two flights, moving soft, and found a records annex: rows of catalogues, a dead lamp, and — jackpot, sweet and improbable — a loan register left out on the reading table, because even institutions of impossible things grew careless at the edges of their attention. She flipped pages by the light of the corridor. Restoration assignments, current quarter. Bindery, bindery, conservation — and there:

*Vol. unlisted. Provisional title: "Of the Thorn and the Turning Year." Found loose in Legacy Collection, Bay 14. Author unknown. Ink analysis pending — results anomalous. Assigned: K. Galloway.*

K. Galloway. The rat-charmer himself. And the entry was dated nine days ago, which meant the book had been sitting in his hands for nine days, and the revelation it contained was due in four, and nobody — not the palace, not the Warrens, not the hundred-year-old guild itself — had yet connected those numbers aloud, or if they had, they were keeping very quiet about it.

Maren closed the register with hands that were not quite steady.

She should leave. Reconnaissance complete, mission parameters exceeded, report to Brannoc, let the council drift toward a decision at glacial speed. That was the discipline. That was the job.

But the book was *here*, in the hands of a man who spoke to rats and lied beautifully, four days before the prophecy came due, and every hour she spent climbing back down to the council was an hour Corvus Hale spent buying grain and sharpening knives. Old Brannoc liked to say that wars weren't started by evil men but by slow ones.

Maren Oduya had never once been accused of being slow.

* * *

Bay 14 was in the deep stacks, where the lamps gave out entirely and she worked by shuttered lantern, and where the air tasted of time itself — dust and leather and the cold mineral smell of things kept past their natural lives. She found the legacy shelves and worked through them methodically, and it was nearly an hour before she understood that the book was not merely shelved somewhere here.

The book was *missing*.

There was a gap. A clean, deliberate gap in Bay 14, sized for a folio, with a brass tag still screwed to the shelf edge: the tag bore no title, only a date — tonight's date, four days hence, inscribed a century ago — and beneath it, in smaller characters, a single word in that same unreadable guild script. Someone had removed the volume recently. Recently enough that the dust shadows on the shelf were still sharp-edged.

Which meant K. Galloway hadn't finished with it. Or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome — which meant K. Galloway had *hidden* it. Which raised the interesting question of why a shy clerk with a rat would hide a book from his own guild.

Unless the book frightened him.

Maren stood in the dark between the shelves, lantern hooded, and let herself feel the shape of it. A prophecy naming a revelation date. An ink that tested anomalous. A keeper who'd gone quiet and strange. Somewhere above her, the bottled weather muttered and the hailstorm nursed its grudges, and the whole vast guild breathed around her like a sleeping animal that dreamed of auctions.

She had come for a location. She was leaving with a mystery, and mysteries, in Maren's experience, were just locations that hadn't confessed yet.

She was halfway back along the deep stacks when she heard it — faint, filtered up through the floors, barely more than a vibration in the soles of her feet. Voices. Many voices. Not human ones.

Rats. Dozens of them, all through the walls around her, and every one of them making the same sound, over and over, urgent as a fire bell: a rising, rhythmic cry that no ordinary ear would mark as anything but vermin noise, but that Maren — who had grown up among docks and gutters, who had listened to rats her whole life without ever hearing them *say* anything — suddenly, absurdly, understood was a name.

They were calling a name.

And the name, as far as she could make out, was *Galloway*.
