## 5. The Date Arrives

The Guild of Impossible Things had three rules posted by its front door, and all of them were lies.

*No item shall be sold twice.* (The guild sold the same bottled storm to the same merchant four times in one decade; he never noticed, because he kept it in a cellar and was afraid of it.) *No member shall profit from another's misfortune.* (Half the inventory *was* other people's misfortune, catalogued and dusted.) And third: *All business conducted at the guild shall be honest.*

Kestrel Galloway had copied those rules onto fresh parchment every spring for six years, and every spring his pen had hesitated a fraction longer on that last line, like a horse refusing a fence.

Tonight the doors were locked behind him, and the rules were nowhere in sight.

He stood at the top of the grand staircase, holding a tray of programs he had printed himself — heavy cream stock, gilt edges, the guild's sigil embossed in silver foil that Sorrel Vane had ordered special from the coast. The programs listed tonight's lots in elegant italics. *Lot One: a caged song, provenance unknown. Lot Two: the last breath of a drowned saint. Lot Three: knowledge.*

"Knowledge," Kestrel murmured to the tray. "That's not an item, that's a category."

"You'd be surprised what fits in a category if you press hard enough," said the tray.

Kestrel did not jump. Six years in this building had cured him of jumping. He glanced down at the mahogany beneath the programs and felt the faint, papery attention of the wood itself — old furniture in the guild was often awake, and usually bitter about it. This particular table had been a wardrobe in a previous life and still resented the demotion.

"Not now," Kestrel told it under his breath.

"You never want to talk when it's interesting," said the table, and sulked in the way that only furniture can, which is to say completely silently, so that no one watching would ever know.

Below him, the entrance hall filled with Ashvale's finest liars.

They came in ones and twos, wrapped against the autumn drizzle, each one handing over an invitation bearing a name that belonged to nobody. Kestrel had spent the afternoon checking invitations against the guild registry, and the registry against the city census, and the census against common sense, and he had concluded that roughly half the people arriving tonight did not exist. There was a shipping magnate called Master Ferrow whose ships appeared in no harbor ledger. A widow named Anselm whose mourning veil was cut from cloth woven in the palace workshops. A broad-shouldered gentleman who signed himself "Mr. Tealeaf" and had the posture of a man who had broken things professionally.

And one woman in a gray traveling cloak who gave her name as "Mistress Quill" and whose eyes went, before anything else, to the staircase leading down to the archives.

Kestrel wrote her name on the guest list and put a small star beside it. He had been putting stars beside things all week. It was the closest a clerk could come to screaming.

* * *

Sorrel found him at the wine table, which was where Kestrel stationed himself whenever he needed to look busy and think clearly. Archivist Sorrel Vane was seventy if she was a day, small and neat as a pressed flower, with white hair pinned so precisely it looked engineered rather than grown. She wore her guild keys on a chain at her belt, forty-one of them, and Kestrel had long ago stopped wondering why an archivist needed keys to her own memory.

"There you are." She took his arm with the easy familiarity of six years' mentorship. "You've gone quiet on me, Kestrel. Quieter than usual, I mean. You've achieved a new register of silence. I'm half tempted to catalogue it."

"Just tired," he said. "Big night."

"Mm." Her eyes were sharp and brown and kind, and they stayed on him a moment too long, the way a hand lingers on a door it means to close. "You know, when I found you — that miserable winter, copying deeds in the Chancery basement, sneezing on vellum — do you know what I saw?"

"A clerk with good penmanship."

"I saw someone the world hadn't gotten around to using yet." She smiled and patted his arm. "I have always been right about people, Kestrel. It's my single most tiresome quality. Go and see to the vaults, there's a dear. Everything below must be perfect before the bidding starts."

Everything below. The vaults. Where the prophecy book sat in its cradle of velvet, dated a hundred years back, inscribed with his name beside tonight's date in ink that predated his birth.

He went. His legs carried him because legs are loyal even when their owners are not.

* * *

The lower vaults smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and, underneath, something older — the smell of a place where many secrets had been kept so long they'd begun to compost. Kestrel walked the rows with his lamp, checking locks he had checked twice already, and tried very hard to be a clerk.

Clerks counted things. Clerks did not stand in front of a velvet cradle at midnight with their heart going like a trapped bird.

The prophecy book lay open to the marked page, because Sorrel had left it open, because Sorrel wanted it open, and Kestrel made himself read it again for the hundredth time.

*On the night of the sealed door, the Thorn shall stand among them, unmarked, unnamed, and know itself at last.*

His name. Tonight's date. A century old.

He had spent nine days constructing explanations, and he presented them to himself now like a barrister defending a hopeless case. Coincidence: names repeat; somewhere in Ashvale there were certainly eleven other Kestrel Galloways, probably all clerks, probably all having worse weeks than his. Forgery: but the ink tests were conclusive, and Sorrel herself had run them, looking grave, looking *satisfied*. Error: perhaps the date was in the old reckoning, off by a year or a lifetime—

The rats had stopped believing him days ago.

They gathered along the skirting now as he passed, a dozen of them, sitting up on their haunches with the patient air of an audience that has already read the reviews. The largest, a scarred female he privately called Duchess, twitched her whiskers at him.

"You're doing the thing," Duchess said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you explain it away until the explaining runs out and then you stand very still." She groomed one paw. "We've seen strangers in the walls, we've told you about the strangers, we've told you about the book, we've told you about the way the Archivist looks at you like a woman counting coins. What's left to tell you?"

"That I'm wrong," Kestrel said. "Somebody say I'm wrong."

The rats looked at one another. Rats are capable of great tenderness, but not of lying, and they all knew it, and for a moment the silence between them was the kindest cruelty he had ever received.

"We could bite someone," offered a young rat hopefully. "It might help."

"It wouldn't."

"It would make us feel better."

"Fine," Kestrel said. "Bite Mr. Tealeaf. Just once. On my authority as assistant archivist."

The young rat practically glowed. It is astonishing how little it takes to make a rat happy, Kestrel thought, and then thought: *and how much it takes to make me lie to myself, and how good I've apparently gotten at it.*

Because here was the thing he had not let himself think, the thought crouched at the bottom of the well of the week, and it rose now as he stood over the open book with the auction beginning overhead:

If the prophecy was real — if the ink was a century old and the name was his — then this night had been arranged before he existed. His job, his mentorship, his little room above the chandler's shop with the view of the gutter he'd come to love — none of it was a life. It was a *setting*. Props arranged around a stage. Someone had built a box around his whole existence and tonight was the night the lid came off, and everyone downstairs had paid handsomely to watch.

He wasn't a witness to the prophecy.

He was the lot being sold.

The vertigo arrived the way it always did, gently at first, like the floor tilting two degrees, and then all at once, like the floor resigning. He gripped the edge of the cradle. The wood — awake, of course, everything in this building was awake — murmured up through his palms.

"Breathe, dear," said the cradle. "I've held worse than you."

"Have you?" Kestrel managed.

"I held the heart of a storm for sixty years. You're smaller, but louder inside."

* * *

Upstairs, the auction had begun.

Kestrel took his place at the side of the hall with the ledger on his arm, recording bids, and watched Ashvale's factions sit shoulder to shoulder pretending to be strangers. The guild enforcer — a mountain of a man called Brannoc who signed the guest list as "a friend of the house" — stood by the wall with his arms folded, radiating the specific patience of someone paid to wait. The spymaster Corvus Hale was either the elegant man calling himself "Master Ferrow" or the widow Anselm, and Kestrel genuinely could not tell, which he suspected was the point. Mistress Quill sat near the back with her hood down just far enough to watch the stairs.

Sorrel presided from the podium like a small bright bird on a branch of gilded oak.

"Lot One," she announced. "A caged song. Composed by a nightingale the morning after the Battle of Redford, in a cage that heard everything. Bidding opens at forty crowns."

The song went to the widow Anselm for two hundred and ten, and Kestrel recorded it, and hated how steady his handwriting was. Hands, at least, had not been informed of the situation.

"Lot Two," said Sorrel. "The last breath of a drowned saint, preserved in glass. It is said to grant one true answer to one true question."

This drew murmurs. Master Ferrow bid first, smooth and high. Mr. Tealeaf countered. The price climbed past three hundred crowns while Kestrel's pen scratched on, and he found himself thinking, absurdly, of the questions he would ask. *Who wrote my name? Why me? What am I?* And then thinking that the breath of a saint was probably like everything else in this building — genuine, and not nearly enough.

Then Sorrel set down her gavel and smiled at the room, and the room went quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes.

"And now," she said, "Lot Three."

She did not consult her program. Kestrel realized, with a small cold click, that the program in every guest's hand listed Lot Three simply as *knowledge*, and that Sorrel had known exactly what knowledge, and for how long, and that the program had been *his* printing, his typesetting, his hands arranging the announcement of his own sale without knowing it. Even his labor had been conscripted into the design. Even his careful, honest, clerkish work had been a prop.

"Lot Three," Sorrel repeated, "is a name."

The hall leaned in. Forty faces, false-named, hungry.

"For a century, this guild has kept a certain volume in its deepest vault. A book of prophecy, verified beyond doubt, which foretells — among other matters of interest to several parties present — the coming of a figure the old texts call the Thorn. Tonight is the night the texts name. And tonight, I can confirm—" she paused, savoring it, a woman unwrapping a gift she had wrapped herself— "the Thorn walks among us. Has walked among us for years. Is, in fact, employed by this guild."

Somewhere in Kestrel's chest, something that had been holding itself carefully together for nine days quietly put down its tools.

"The particulars of the identity," Sorrel continued, "will be revealed to the winning bidder alone. Sealed bids, as agreed. You may write your offers now."

Pens moved. The widow Anselm wrote without hesitation. Master Ferrow wrote slowly, smiling. Mr. Tealeaf wrote nothing at all, merely folded his arms, and Kestrel understood that some bidders intended to acquire the information by other means before dawn.

And Kestrel stood at the side of the hall with the ledger on his arm, recording nothing, because there was nothing left to record, and understood the shape of his whole life at last. Not a life. A ledger entry. *One Thorn, condition good, raised in-house, ready for market.* Six years of kindnesses — the apprenticeship, the room, the trust, the warmth — all of it purchase and preparation. Sorrel's hand on his arm at the wine table. *Someone the world hadn't gotten around to using yet.*

She had gotten around to using him. She had simply waited for the date in the book.

The vertigo came back and this time he let it. He let the floor tilt and the hall tilt and the gilt-edged programs tilt, because fighting it had gotten him nowhere, and in the tilt he saw it plainly: every faction in Ashvale was about to own a piece of paper with his name on it, and every one of them would decide what he was for. Weapon. Asset. Threat. Kindling. Everyone except him.

A clerk's instinct surfaced through the drowning — the small, stubborn, professional reflex that had kept him sane through six years of impossible inventory: *when the ledger is wrong, you find the error. When the ledger is a lie, you find the liar. And when you cannot speak—*

Well. He had never been able to speak. That was rather the point of him. But he could count, and he could move quietly, and he knew this building better than anyone alive, including, he suspected, the woman who had built her century around owning it.

Sorrel rang a small bell. "Sealed bids to the table, please. The house will deliberate at midnight."

The guests rose, murmuring, glasses refilled, alliances forming and dissolving like weather. Mistress Quill's eyes lifted from the crowd and found Kestrel across the hall — found him and stayed, sharp and unreadable, the way she had looked at the archive stairs.

And by the wall, Brannoc the enforcer unfolded his arms, and began, unhurriedly, to walk toward the side of the hall where the clerks stood.

Kestrel closed the ledger.

"Excuse me," he said to no one, in the voice of a man going to fetch more wine. "I'm needed below."

The vaults were dark, and the rats were waiting, and above him the sealed doors of the Guild of Impossible Things held shut against the night — with him on the wrong side of every lock he knew.
