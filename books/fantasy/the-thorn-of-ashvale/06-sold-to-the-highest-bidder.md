## 6. Sold to the Highest Bidder

Sorrel Vane had sold many impossible things in her long tenure at the Guild of Impossible Things, and she had learned one lesson above all others: never sell a secret cheaply enough that people stop fearing it.

Tonight she intended to sell it dearly indeed.

She stood behind the auctioneer's lectern in the Vaulted Hall — not the public hall with its gilded cages and bottled weather, but the deep room beneath, where the ceiling was carved with the guild's oldest motto: *ALL THINGS MAY BE KNOWN, AT A PRICE*. The motto had been Sorrel's suggestion, ninety years ago. She had always felt it was honest in a way the guild rarely managed.

The bidders sat in a crescent of low chairs, each separated from their neighbors by exactly enough distance to make eavesdropping difficult and paranoia easy. Sorrel had arranged the seating herself. Paranoia loosened purses.

Brannoc occupied the seat nearest the door, because enforcers always did. He was a slab of a man with a jaw like a dock cleat and the patient stillness of someone paid by the hour rather than the outcome. The Guild's Enforcer Corps had bid on his behalf before he'd arrived: three hundred crowns and first refusal on any "security implications." Brannoc himself would simply sit there and radiate the quiet menace of a man who could carry a grand piano up four flights without complaint.

In the third chair sat a slender gentleman calling himself Master Ferrow, wearing a grey coat cut in the palace style and a signet ring turned inward so the crest wouldn't show. Corvus Hale was many things — spymaster, gardener of informants, a man who watered his secrets daily — but subtle was not among his talents when he believed himself unobserved. He kept touching the ring. He had been touching the ring for an hour. Sorrel found it almost endearing, the way a cat finds a mouse's trembling almost endearing.

And in the last chair, hood drawn back just enough to suggest honesty while concealing everything useful, sat a courier called Merrin Stave, whose papers were excellent forgeries and whose hands were too scarred for a courier and too steady for anything else. Maren Oduya. Sorrel had recognized her within four seconds of her entering the building, mostly by her boots. Rebels bought their boots in batches, and rebel boots had a particular way of being worn — heels struck flat, as though the wearer had spent years walking on ground that might be trapped.

Sorrel did not mind. A sale needed tension, and nothing produced tension quite like an assassin pretending to be a customer.

* * *

"Friends," Sorrel began, and let the word hang there, warm and slightly ironic, "colleagues, rivals, representatives of institutions I am legally obliged to describe as 'respected' — welcome."

A dry chuckle from Master Ferrow. Nothing from Brannoc. The hooded figure shifted, weight forward, the posture of a person counting exits.

"You come tonight under false names," Sorrel continued, "which the guild regards not as deception but as courtesy. We are all of us more honest in disguise. It is one of the small mercies of this profession." She rested her hands on the lectern. "You have received the catalog. You know that lot nineteen concerns the Thorn of Ashvale — the identity of the vessel, the location, the manner of claiming. You have each brought sums that would embarrass a bishop. And you have each, I suspect, brought contingency plans for what happens if you do not win."

She smiled at them, the smile of a grandmother offering biscuits that might be poisoned.

"I will be candid, because candor is rarer than gold and worth more. There will be no bidding war tonight. There will be a single question, asked three ways, and whoever answers it best takes the prize."

Brannoc's head tilted, one degree. Even the furniture listened now.

"The Thorn," Sorrel said, "is not a sword. It is not a crown. It is not hidden in a vault or buried under a hill, and if any of you came tonight expecting to purchase a map, you may leave now and save your money."

Nobody left. Of course nobody left. Hope is the least rational creature in any menagerie, and Sorrel fed it gently.

* * *

She began, as she had planned to begin for a century, with history.

"A hundred and six years ago," she said, "the last war ended not with a treaty but with a surrender. The god's power — call it what the old books call it, the Ember — passed out of the world, or seemed to. The victors wrote histories saying it was destroyed. The losers wrote histories saying it was stolen. Both were wrong. Power like that does not die. It waits."

Master Ferrow leaned forward. "Waits where?"

"Ah." Sorrel held up one finger. "That is lot nineteen, Master Ferrow. Patience. You've come this far on patience; the palace trained you well." She enjoyed, briefly and privately, the flicker of alarm that crossed his face — *how much does she know?* — before he mastered it. Everything, dear boy. I knew your predecessor, and his predecessor, and I attended the wedding of the man who founded your office, and I gave the bride away because nobody else would.

"The Ember requires a vessel," she went on. "Living flesh. Willing flesh — that part matters enormously, and we'll return to it. For a hundred years it has slept in its vessel, dormant, unrecognized even by itself. And tonight—" she let the pause stretch until the candles seemed to lean in— "tonight, by the terms of an instrument older than everyone in this room except me, it wakes. Or rather: it becomes claimable. The prophecy is specific. Tonight the Thorn is revealed."

"The prophecy," the hooded figure said. The voice was pitched low, roughened — Maren had done her homework, disguising even her voice, which Sorrel thought showed real professional pride. "We'd like to hear it. The actual text."

"All in good time. First, the fragments. Free of charge, as a gesture of trust." Sorrel lifted the first card. "Fragment one: *the Thorn walks freely through the halls of power and none bar his way.*"

Silence while they chewed on it. Ferrow's eyes narrowed — a clerk? a minister? some minor functionary in the palace itself? Brannoc's brow furrowed with the effort of a man doing arithmetic in his head.

"Fragment two." Second card. "*He speaks with every tongue but his own.*"

Something changed in the room then. Sorrel watched it happen with the satisfaction of a chef watching a soufflé rise. Ferrow went very still. The hooded figure's breath caught — audibly, professionally fatal, and Sorrel made a note to be kind about it later, assuming there was a later. Even Brannoc straightened, and Brannoc only straightened for two reasons: orders, and instinct. This was instinct.

Because everyone in Ashvale knew the rumor, the half-joke told over tavern tables: that somewhere in the Guild of Impossible Things worked a clerk who could talk to animals. Nobody believed it. That was the beauty of it. A beast-speaker hiding in plain sight was protected by the very absurdity of the idea.

"He talks to rats," Brannoc said slowly. "The tall clerk. Galloway. I've seen him crouched in the storeroom, muttering. Thought he was mad."

"People often think that," Sorrel agreed pleasantly. "Madness is such a convenient explanation for talent."

"You're selling us your own employee," Ferrow said. There was something between disgust and admiration in his voice, and Sorrel decided to be flattered by the latter.

"I am selling you the truth," she said, "which has never once required my approval to exist. I merely decline to apologize for it. Fragment three." Third card. She read it aloud, and her voice did not waver, because she had rehearsed it for a hundred years and grief, like wine, improves with discipline. "*When the Thorn is known, the knowing shall cost him everything, and the city shall choose what it costs.*"

She set the cards down.

"Now. Questions."

* * *

Brannoc went first, as direct as ever. "Can it be taken by force? The vessel, the power — can we just take the man?"

"No." Sorrel let the word fall like a stone down a well. They heard it land. "The Ember passes only to willing acceptance. Torture him, cage him, threaten everything he loves — the power will sit in him like a stone in a boot and pass to no one. It must be *offered*, and he must accept with open eyes. Which is why, gentlemen and lady—" she inclined her head toward the hood, enjoying the tiny start it provoked— "the winning bidder will not receive a prisoner. The winning bidder will receive a persuasion problem."

"Persuasion," Ferrow repeated.

"Kestrel Galloway is twenty-six years old, an orphan of the river districts, employed by this guild for nine years, competent, punctual, and — this is the important part — desperately lonely. He is fluent in the speech of every animal in this city and cannot say a true sentence to another human being. He trusts precisely one person in the world." Sorrel paused. "Me."

She let that settle. She watched Corvus Hale understand it — watched the palace arithmetic run behind his eyes: *control the mentor, control the vessel.* Watched Maren Oduya understand it differently, the rebel arithmetic: *the mentor is the lever; remove the lever.* Watched Brannoc understand nothing at all except that the guild wanted the boy alive, which for the Enforcer Corps was the same as an order.

"What does the guild want in exchange?" Ferrow asked. "Money seems beside the point."

"Three conditions." Sorrel raised fingers. "One: the Thorn remains in Ashvale. The power leaves this city, the prophecy reasserts itself and begins again — you do not want to meet the next version of this evening; I assure you it is less polite. Two: no harm comes to the vessel before acceptance. Dead Thorns make ash, and ash makes nothing. Three—" and here she allowed herself, at long last, after a century of rehearsal, one visible crack in the porcelain, quickly mended— "three: when it is done, when he has accepted and the Ember has passed out of him and into its new keeper, the guild's records concerning Kestrel Galloway are burned, unread. He deserves to vanish clean. Whatever else he becomes afterward, let him not be remembered as a thing we sold."

It was, Sorrel reflected, a remarkable performance, because every word of it was true, and yet it was also the most elaborate lie she had ever told. She was not selling Kestrel to these people. She was choosing among them. She was handing her boy — her furious, gentle, rat-whispering boy — to whichever wolf would keep him safest while the Ember drained out of her own failing body and into his, and the transfer completed, and she could finally, finally die.

But wolves do not respond to grief. Wolves respond to scarcity. So she had made him scarce.

"Bidding opens at one thousand crowns," she said, "payable in influence, favors, silence, or coin, in ascending order of usefulness."

* * *

They bid the way predators circle: politely, and with total commitment to the kill.

Brannoc opened for the Enforcer Corps — protection, employment, a place inside the guild's walls where the boy already belonged. Safe, Sorrel thought, and small. The corps would use Kestrel as a bloodhound and bury the Ember under procedure until it choked.

Ferrow bid the palace: amnesty for whatever crimes his gift technically constituted (there were three statutes; Sorrel had written two of them), a salary, a title, purpose. *Become the Thorn deliberately,* the offer amounted to, *and the crown will love you.* Sorrel nearly laughed. The crown loved no one. The crown kept people, the way a collector keeps beetles — pinned, labeled, dusted.

Then the hooded figure spoke.

"I bid the truth," said Merrin Stave, in that roughened voice, and stood, and pushed back the hood, and let them see her face — because Maren Oduya understood, as Sorrel did, that sometimes the boldest disguise is none at all. "I bid him the truth. That every person in this room intends to use him. That the prophecy was written by someone who needed him to exist. That the only way out of a book is to burn it — and I know where the book is, and I know how to burn it properly."

The room went taut as wire. Brannoc's hand moved toward his belt. Ferrow rose halfway out of his chair.

And Sorrel Vane, Archivist of the Guild of Impossible Things, felt something cold walk down her spine, because Maren Oduya should not know that. No one living should know that. The book's true nature — that it manufactured its own heir, that it was a trap laid in ink a century ago by a woman who had once been young — that knowledge lived in exactly one skull in the world, and the skull was hers.

Unless.

Unless the boy had read further than she'd allowed. Unless he had gone past the page with his name on it, past the pretty dread of coincidence, down into the machinery underneath.

*He knows,* Sorrel thought, and the thought arrived with an emotion she had not budgeted for tonight, something adjacent to pride and adjacent to horror, and she could not have said which side of it she stood on. *My clever, quiet boy has been reading in the dark.*

"Guards," said Ferrow.

"Don't," said Maren, hand vanishing into her coat.

Brannoc moved, unhurried as ever, and Sorrel opened her mouth to restore order — she was, whatever else, still the auctioneer, and this was still her hall —

And below them, faint through the floor, the vault bells began to ring.

Every cage in the Guild of Impossible Things opening at once has a sound. Sorrel had heard it described by survivors of the incident of '41 and had always assumed they exaggerated. They had not. It sounded like a thousand small doors, and behind the doors, a thousand voices — whistles, screams, songs, the low groan of a bottled storm finding its lid loose — rising together into something that was unmistakably, improbably, joy.

And threaded through it, from everywhere and nowhere, the freed beasts of the guild were speaking a name.

Not barking it. Not mimicking it, the way birds mimic. *Speaking* it — in a hundred throats, rat and raven and the old blind badger from cellar three — one name, rising up through the floors of the Guild of Impossible Things like water through a drowned ship:

*"Kestrel. Kestrel. Kestrel."*

Sorrel closed her eyes.

"Well," she said, to no one in particular, in the tone of a woman whose soufflé has just walked out of the oven, "it appears the lot has sold itself."

She was already moving toward the stairs, and she was, despite everything — despite the century of planning, despite the dying fire in her own chest, despite the betrayal now unfolding one flight below — smiling.

Her boy was running. Good. Running meant he hadn't accepted yet. Running meant there was still time.

"Hold the hall," she told Brannoc, taking the steps two at a time for the first time in forty years. "No one follows me down. This part," she said, and the lie came smooth as silk off a loom, "is family business."
