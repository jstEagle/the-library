## 7. Flight Through the Caged Songs

The gavel had not yet finished falling when Kestrel moved.

He did not remember deciding to move. That was the strange part — later he would search his memory for the moment of choice and find only a gap, a blank space where a sensible man would have stayed seated behind his inventory desk with his ledger open and his heart beating politely out of sight. Instead he was already through the side door marked *Authorized Personnel Only*, and the door was already locked behind him, and the auction hall was already roaring with voices that all wanted one thing.

*The Thorn. The Thorn is here. Find the clerk.*

His name. His actual, unremarkable, ink-stained name, spoken in a room full of dukes and spymasters wearing borrowed faces.

Kestrel ran.

The vaults beneath the Ashvale Guild of Impossible Things were older than the guild itself, older than the city's cobbled streets, older — Sorrel had once said over tea, with that particular smile she used when she wanted to seem casual about something enormous — than most of the wars Ashvale pretended not to be preparing for. They were cut from grey stone that sweated in summer and wept in winter, and they were arranged, as far as anyone knew, in no order whatsoever. Corridors branched like roots. Staircases went down to arrive at floors above where you'd started. One stairwell, catalogued as Item 4-117 (*Staircase, Recursive, Do Not Count the Steps Twice*), had eaten two apprentices before the guild gave up trying to explain it and simply posted a sign.

Kestrel had spent eleven years learning these vaults the way other men learned scripture: quietly, at night, alone.

He took the left fork past the bottled storms.

* * *

The storm room was his favorite, though he'd have denied it under oath. Three hundred jars on iron shelves, each one holding weather that someone, somewhere, had decided was too much for the world outside. A hurricane off the southern coast, caught mid-scream by a collector who'd died before he could decide what to do with it. A hailstorm the size of a chapel. A single thunderclap, bottled by a grieving widow who had paid the guild an obscene sum to keep it forever, because it was the last sound her husband ever made.

Kestrel had spoken to the storms sometimes. Not beast-speech exactly — storms weren't beasts — but something adjacent, something the rats had taught him without meaning to, the way you learn a language from people who don't know they're teaching. *Hello,* he'd say, walking the rows. *Still angry? Still loud?*

The storms never answered. But they leaned toward him, all three hundred jars, the way flowers lean toward a window.

Tonight he didn't stop to say hello. Tonight he stopped to commit arson.

Well. Not arson. He preferred to think of it as *inventory redistribution*.

He pulled the first jar from its shelf — the thunderclap, because he owed the widow's grief a kindness and this was the only kindness he could imagine — and hurled it against the corridor wall.

The world came apart.

Sound has a body. Everyone knows this who has stood too close to a bell. The thunderclap hit the stone like a battering ram made of noise, and the wall did not break so much as *reconsider its position*, and somewhere above them a hundred chandeliers in a hundred rooms rang in sympathy, and every guard in the vaults heard it and thought, with the beautiful predictable logic of trained men, *that came from the east corridors.*

It had come from the east corridors. But so, now, did Kestrel — running west.

Behind him, freed from its shelf, the southern hurricane began, very slowly and with tremendous enthusiasm, to unscrew its own lid.

*Sorry,* Kestrel told it, not slowing down. *Sorry. It's not you. It's me. It's — everyone, actually.*

The storm did not answer. Storms never answered. But as he rounded the corner he heard the lid come off, and the first wet breath of a sea-hurricane tasting vault air for the first time in forty years, and he thought: *well. If I'm burning my life down, I may as well do it thoroughly.*

* * *

They found him within minutes. Of course they did. He'd known they would; that was the point of the storms, not escape but misdirection, buying him corridors instead of freedom. Two enforcers in guild grey came around the corner of the aviary wing moving with that terrible unhurried competence that meant they'd done this many times to many people.

Kestrel did what any reasonable man would do.

He opened every cage.

The aviary wing held the guild's collection of caged songs — not birds, precisely, though they looked like birds the way a letter looks like paper. Each one held a song that had been captured alive: a lullaby a mother had sung for sixty years until the guild bought it; a war-chant so old no living army remembered the words but the song itself remembered everything; a drinking song, God help him, that had escaped three previous owners and was widely considered uncatchable, which is why the guild had caught it, because the Guild of Impossible Things collected impossibilities the way magpies collect shine.

Kestrel threw the latches wide and thirty-one songs burst into the air.

Here is what a caged song does when freed after years: it sings. Here is what thirty-one caged songs do when freed simultaneously in an enclosed stone corridor: they sing *over each other*, in competition, at volumes that had been accumulating interest for decades. The lullaby went for the enforcers first — it was a kind thing at its core, and it saw two men rigid with fear and did what lullabies do. The war-chant went for Kestrel, wrapped itself around his shoulders like a mantle of drums, and suddenly his legs were not tired anymore, his legs were *glorious*, his legs had been waiting all his life for someone to give them permission, and he ran the length of the aviary wing laughing like a maniac while a drinking song followed him singing a chorus about a woman named Brigid and a barrel with no bottom.

The enforcers sat down in the corridor. Later, Kestrel would learn, they sat there for nearly an hour, being sung to sleep by a sixty-year lullaby, and one of them wept for his own mother, and neither of them ever quite explained it in their report.

Kestrel did not know this yet. He only knew the songs were with him, and that the vaults had never sounded like this, and that some traitorous chamber of his heart was singing along.

*This is exhilarating,* he thought. *I am going to die.*

* * *

He went down. Down was the only direction the guild forgot, because down was where the guild kept things it preferred to forget. Past the recursive staircase (he counted the steps once, defiantly). Past the room of mirrors that showed you your face at whatever age you'd been happiest (he kept his eyes shut; there wasn't time, and he suspected he already knew). Past the cold storage where the guild kept —

The menagerie.

Every impossible creature the guild had acquired in a century of acquiring. The last wyvern of the eastern peaks, old and arthritic and deeply sarcastic. A fox made of autumn. Something in the far corner that had no name in any language Kestrel spoke, which communicated exclusively in the smell of rain, and which had once told him — in rain-smell, patiently, the way you'd explain a joke to a child — that it had been alive before stone learned to hold still.

"Little clerk," rumbled the wyvern as Kestrel skidded into the dark and started throwing latches. "You smell of panic and bad decisions."

"I smell of *freedom*," Kestrel gasped.

"You smell of both," the wyvern conceded. "They're often the same thing. Open my cage, then, if you're going to stand there philosophizing."

He opened everything. The fox made of autumn poured out of its enclosure like spilled leaves and reassembled itself at his heel, looking up at him with eyes the color of October. The rain-smell thing simply *wasn't there* anymore, one moment contained and the next everywhere, a presence like weather filling the vault. And the small creatures — the ones nobody catalogued carefully, the mice and rats and moles and one extremely indignant hedgehog — came pouring out of the walls themselves, because the walls had always been theirs, because the guild had never understood that a cage of stone means nothing to something that can hear the stone's dreams.

And they all said his name.

That was the moment it stopped being exhilarating. Or rather, that was the moment the exhilaration curdled, turned over in his chest like milk in sun, and showed him what it had been hiding underneath.

"*Kestrel.*" The rats, a hundred small voices braided together. "*Kestrel Galloway.*" The bats from the ceiling, high and papery. "*Kestrel.*" The wyvern, deep as a landslide, almost gentle. "*Kestrel, Kestrel, Kestrel*" — the fox made of autumn, the hedgehog, the moles, the mice in their hundreds, the songs still circling overhead weaving his name into a chorus about Brigid and the bottomless barrel, until the whole underground roared with it, his name in a hundred voices, his name in voices that had never spoken a human word in front of a human before, his name given freely, gladly, *publicly* —

His gift. Eleven years hidden. Hidden even, mostly, from himself — hidden in the way you hide a thing by never letting yourself look directly at it, keeping it in your peripheral vision where it couldn't hurt you. Fluent in everything except honesty, he'd joked to himself once, alone, at night, and the joke had been a lock and tonight every door in the vault was open and the lock had come undone too.

"They know," whispered the fox made of autumn. Its voice was wind through dry grass. "The two-legs upstairs. We can smell them wanting. They want *you*, Kestrel Galloway, and they will dig for you like winter digs for the last green thing."

"Then we shouldn't keep them waiting," Kestrel said, and hated how steady his voice sounded when nothing inside him was.

He ran again. And the impossible things ran with him.

* * *

Later — much later, in a hiding place he hadn't found yet — he would try to reconstruct the route and fail completely. Memory refused to file it as a journey; it filed it as a *storm*. There was the corridor where he released the memory-moths and the pursuing guards stopped mid-stride to relive their best days, standing in a row like statues of themselves. There was the flooded gallery where the bottled rivers lived, and the moment he broke the seals and the Ash itself — the river the whole city was named for, or a stolen century of it, the guild had never been precise — came home through the vaults, roaring, and carried him half a mile on a wave that smelled of every spring since the founding. There was the hedgehog, who at some point simply attached himself to Kestrel's boot and declined all further discussion of the matter.

There were the guards. There were always more guards. And each time, the creatures got there first — not fighting, never fighting, just *witnessing*: the fox shaking out its autumn coat and filling a corridor with the smell of bonfires and endings; the rain-smell thing pressing close around a squad of enforcers until each of them, unaccountably, remembered being seven years old and happy, and forgot, briefly, why they'd been chasing anyone at all.

The creatures were protecting him. That was the terrible part. They were spending themselves for him, revealing themselves, burning their own cover the way he was burning his, and they did it *gladly*, and their gladness was the loudest sound in the world.

*They love you,* said a small voice that might have been a mouse or might have been the dark itself. *We all love you. You are the only two-legs who ever asked how our day went.*

"That can't be true," Kestrel panted, splashing through ankle-deep river.

"It is entirely true," said the voice. "Two-legs talk *at* us. You talked *with* us. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what it is worth down here?"

"Apparently it is worth a hurricane," Kestrel said.

"Apparently," the voice agreed, and if a voice could shrug smugly, that one did.

* * *

He surfaced through the coal chute behind the tannery, which stank like a decision everyone regretted, and came up into the night air of Ashvale gasping, soaked, covered in ash and autumn leaves and one hedgehog, with a war-chant still drumming faintly at his shoulders and the drinking song trailing off toward the tavern district singing about Brigid at last finding her barrel.

Above the guild, the sky glowed orange. The southern hurricane had reached the upper galleries. Somewhere inside, three hundred bottles of weather were introducing themselves to the architecture.

Kestrel stood in the alley between the tannery and a shuttered dye-house and laughed — a wild, cracked, disbelieving sound — because the alternative was sitting down in the filth and never getting up.

Then he heard the footsteps. Soft ones. Many of them. Not guards — guards marched; these *drifted*, the way people walk who know the alleys better than the people who built the alleys.

The fox's ears went up. The hedgehog bristled against his ankle. From the drain grates and the gutter-tops and the eaves of the dye-house, a hundred pairs of eyes opened in the dark, all fixed on the same point behind him, and the rats spoke in their braided hundred-voice whisper:

"She has found you, Kestrel Galloway. The one who moves like a shadow and smells of secrets. She has been hunting the vaults all night, and now she has found *you*."

Kestrel turned.

At the mouth of the alley stood a figure in courier's leathers, hood back, one hand very near a knife, watching him with the expression of someone who has finally cornered the answer to a question she's been asking for years.

"Well," she said. "The prophecy's little clerk. You have *no idea* how much trouble you're in."

"You'd be surprised," Kestrel said. Behind her, at the alley's mouth, the first light of dawn caught the smoke rising from the Guild of Impossible Things — his life, his eleven quiet years, going up in weather and birdsong. "I'm starting to get a sense of it."
