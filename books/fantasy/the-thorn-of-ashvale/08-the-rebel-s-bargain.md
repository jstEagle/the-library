## 8. The Rebel's Bargain

The trouble with smuggling a fugitive through Ashvale, Maren Oduya had learned, was that fugitives came in two varieties: the kind who ran quietly, and the kind who apologized to every cat they passed.

Kestrel Galloway was the second kind.

"Sorry," he murmured for the fourth time since they'd left the Guild district, ducking his head at a tabby perched on a rain barrel. The cat stared at him with the flat insolence of its kind. "Sorry — we're just passing through."

"Mm," said Maren.

She kept her hood up and her hand on the knife at her hip and counted doorways the way other people counted sheep. Behind them, somewhere in the maze of lamplit streets, the Guild's enforcers were tearing the night apart looking for their runaway clerk. She'd heard the whistles. She'd seen the lantern-glow bloom against the low clouds like a bruise. What she hadn't yet heard — and this bothered her more than she let it — was what the man beside her had *done* to earn that kind of pursuit. Clerks didn't get chased. Clerks got *filed*.

They cut down Tanner's Row, where the smell of lye covered everything including their nerves, and slipped through a gate that wasn't locked so much as *convinced* to be elsewhere. The Warrens swallowed them.

To an outsider, the rebel quarter beneath the old tannery looked like nothing: damp stone, dripping pipes, the skeletal remains of some failed brewery. To Maren it was home, which meant it was also a performance. Every shadow here had a job. Every pipe had ears that weren't pipes.

"Oduya." A voice from the dark, young and skeptical. It belonged to Pip, who was nineteen and had opinions about everything, most of them loud. "You brought a *guest*."

"I brought a package," Maren said. "Handle him gently. He rattles."

Kestrel made a small wounded sound. In the torchlight he looked exactly like what he claimed to be — an archivist's clerk, ink-stained, hollow-eyed, his coat scorched along one sleeve where something in the vaults had gotten enthusiastic about freedom. But there was ash on his cuffs and a look in his eyes Maren recognized from mirrors: the expression of someone whose life had been picked up and shaken until all the furniture rearranged itself.

"Pip, fetch broth. The good kind, not the kind you make."

"That was *one time*—"

"One memorable time. Go."

Pip went, grumbling. Maren steered Kestrel down a side passage to a cell that had once held casks of vinegar and now held a cot, a stool, a candle stub, and her entire collection of contraband maps, which she kicked under the cot with the ease of long practice.

"Sit," she said. "Breathe. In this room, nobody is hunting you for the next four minutes. I can't promise five."

He sat. He breathed. Then he looked up at her with those tired, careful eyes and said, "Why are you helping me?"

*Because I've been watching you for three weeks,* she did not say. *Because I followed you into the Guild archives on the night everything went wrong, because I saw you talk to a rat like it was an old friend, and because I need something you have.* 

"Because you're worth more alive," she said instead, which had the advantage of being true.

* * *

The broth arrived — Pip's, unfortunately, which tasted like salted regret — and Kestrel drank it anyway, holding the bowl in both hands as though it might bolt. Around them the Warrens settled into its nighttime rhythms: boots on stone, the far-off clatter of the printing press that produced the rebels' pamphlets, someone singing badly two corridors away.

"You know," Kestrel said, after a silence, "the last place I sheltered was a records vault. There was a storm in a bottle on shelf nine. And a cage of songbirds that sang in a language nobody spoke anymore. And a debt owed by Duke Ellery-March, written on vellum, for a favor I genuinely cannot describe without laughing." He paused. "This is nicer."

"This is a hole under a tannery."

"Yes. Nicer." He smiled, and it transformed his whole face, and Maren thought: *careful*. Smiles were how people got under your guard. She'd used enough of them herself to know the inventory.

"You're taking this remarkably well," she said. "Most men who watch half a city mobilize to hunt them spend the first hour vomiting or bargaining."

"I spent the first hour running." He turned the bowl in his hands. "And before that I spent several hours learning that my entire life has been... arranged. That tends to put a person in a philosophical mood about furniture." He glanced around the cell. "Whose cot is this?"

"Mine, when I'm here. Which is rarely. I'm a courier. I'm never anywhere longer than it takes to lie and leave." She said it lightly, and watched it land, and watched him hear the shape of it — a life built out of departures — and recognize it the way one craftsman recognizes another's joinery.

"You're very good at that," he said quietly.

"At what?"

"Lying like it's breathing. You did it twice just now. The tone goes flat, right at the end. Like a page being smoothed shut."

Maren stared at him. Of all the things she'd expected from the trembling clerk — gratitude, fear, questions about the menu — professional critique of her tradecraft had not made the list. Something in her chest did an uncomfortable flip that she chose to interpret as indigestion.

"And you'd know," she said, "because?"

"Because I do it too." He set the bowl down. "Have done for years. There are things about me I keep folded up small, where nobody looks. Tonight they unfolded." His jaw tightened. "All of them. In front of everyone."

"The beasts." She kept her voice casual. Kept her eyes on his face. "In the vaults. They followed you. They called you by name."

Something flickered there — fear, yes, but underneath it something older and lonelier. "You saw that."

"I see most things. It's the family curse." She leaned back against the damp wall. "Here's what I know, clerk. The Guild sealed its doors tonight for an auction. By morning, every power in Ashvale will know what the Guild knows. Whatever's in that prophecy book — whatever makes a mild-mannered inventory clerk worth a citywide manhunt — it's coming for you. And when it comes, you'll want friends. Friends with knives. Friends with tunnels. Friends who can make you disappear so thoroughly that even the Guild's ledgers forget you existed."

"You're offering."

"I'm offering."

"In exchange for?"

There it was. Not stupid, then. Maren had prepared three versions of this conversation, ranked by likelihood, and he'd skipped straight past the two dishonest ones. She respected it. She resented it. She decided to split the difference.

"The book," she said. "The prophecy volume. Sorrel Vane wrote things in it a century ago that are happening *now*, word for word. Do you understand what that means? It means the war coming for this city has already been written down. It means someone read the ending and decided to sell tickets." She leaned forward. "My people have fought the Guild's shadow war for forty years, clerk. Forty years of dying blind. If I can put that book in our hands — if we can read what's coming before it arrives — we might actually stop it. Not survive it. *Stop* it."

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, the tannery's machinery groaned in its sleep.

"And if I tell you where it is," he said slowly, "your people protect me."

"House, food, papers, a face no one in Ashvale knows. The works."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you're a stranger in a hole full of strangers, and strangers talk, and eventually the wrong person hears that the Guild's lost clerk is sleeping thirty feet below a chandlery on Rope Street." She shrugged. "I'd rather not have that conversation with you. I liked you better when I thought you were boring."

That earned a ghost of a smile. But behind it, she could see the ledger turning — she could practically *hear* the pages — a mind built for inventories weighing assets against debts. And she realized, with the particular clarity that comes to liars watching other liars, that he was going to give her something. Just not the truth.

"The archive," he said. "Sub-level three. Restricted stacks, aisle fourteen. The prophecy volume was scheduled for rebinding — damaged spine, water damage, the whole sad catalogue. I rebind the restricted volumes myself; Sorrel doesn't trust anyone else with them." He met her eyes, steady as a filed report. "It'll still be there. Or it won't — the enforcers tore through the archives tonight, they may have taken it. But that's where it lives. Aisle fourteen."

Every instinct Maren owned stood up and applauded. The detail was perfect. Too perfect? No — *well*-perfect. Rehearsed-perfect. The way a forged signature is perfect right up until an expert holds it to the light.

And the thing was, she thought, watching his tired, honest face, that she couldn't actually prove it was a lie. Maybe it was true. Maybe the exhausted clerk really was handing over the location out of simple, desperate gratitude.

But she'd smoothed pages shut with that same flat tone, hadn't she?

"Aisle fourteen," she repeated.

"Sub-level three. Ask for the rebinding queue."

"Mm." She stood, brushed off her coat, and smiled her best smile — the warm one, the one that opened doors. "Then welcome to the Warrens, Kestrel Galloway. You're one of us now."

The words came out easily. That was the seduction of belonging, she knew: it asked so little. Just a name given, a hand shaken, a lie accepted on both sides of the table. Already she could see it working on him — the way his shoulders dropped half an inch, the way he looked at the shabby cell and the bad broth and the badly sung songs down the corridor as though they were a fire in winter. People would forgive a lot, she thought, for the feeling of being *kept*.

She knew, because she'd forgiven a lot for it herself. She'd been lying to these people for six years about who sent her and why, and every day the lie felt more like home than the truth ever had.

"Get some sleep," she told him. "Tomorrow I go after your book."

* * *

He slept, eventually. She heard his breathing slow through the thin wall while she sat sharpening a knife that didn't need it, which was what she did instead of thinking.

Aisle fourteen. She'd been in sub-level three. Twice. She'd memorized the restricted stacks during the infiltration — had, in fact, gone looking for the prophecy volume herself, following the notes of a dead archivist who'd called it *the Thorn Ledger* and died inconveniently before explaining anything. Aisle fourteen held guild charters. Tax exemptions. A truly alarming collection of apology letters from dukes.

The prophecy book had been in the conservation room. She'd seen it through glass, chained to a reading desk, and she'd run out of time before she could crack the case.

So the clerk had lied. Beautifully. Generously, even — pointing her at a real place, a plausible place, a place where she'd waste a day and maybe lose a person getting in. Why? Fear. Self-preservation. Or something else — something in the way he'd said *it lives*, present tense, possessive, like a man talking about a thing he'd already read.

Maren set down the knife. Outside the cell, Pip was arguing with the cook about onions. Down the corridor the bad singer had moved on to a ballad about a drowned king. The Warrens hummed around her, warm and shabby and full of people who trusted her, and she sat in the middle of it feeling the fresh lie settle between her and the sleeping clerk like a third tenant — his lie and hers, sharing the walls, keeping each other company.

*Two liars in a cellar,* she thought. *The Guild should auction us together.*

But there was this, too, and it surprised her: she believed him about the important part. Whatever was in that book, whatever the Guild had tried to sell tonight, it had *him* at the center of it — she'd seen the fragments Sorrel Vane read aloud before the lights went out and the screaming started. *The Thorn shall stand among the shelves. The Thorn shall speak with borrowed voices.* Borrowed voices. Beasts' voices. She'd thought it was poetry.

It wasn't poetry. It was a description.

Which meant the book wasn't just a record of the future. It was a *recipe*. And somewhere in that recipe, in words older than either of them, was a line that ended with Kestrel Galloway's name — and another line, further down, that she very much needed to read before anyone else did.

Maren blew out her candle, lay down on the floor beside the maps she'd kick under any bed again come morning, and began composing tomorrow's lies: for Pip, for the quartermaster, for the council, for the tired clerk with the honest eyes and the dishonest tongue.

Through the wall, faint as breath, she heard him murmur something in his sleep. It sounded almost like words. It sounded almost like a hundred small voices answering.

She pulled her coat over her shoulders and did not sleep at all.
