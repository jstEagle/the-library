## 9. Reading the Thorn

The Warrens had a library.

Kestrel learned this the way he learned most things in the three days since his life had caught fire — by accident, while looking for something else. In this case, something else was a privy, which in a settlement built from smugglers' tunnels and abandoned cisterns beneath Ashvale's eastern hill turned out to be a genuinely urgent question.

He'd asked Maren where it was. She'd said "down and left." Down and left, after two turns and a staircase carved by hands that had clearly been in a hurry, was not a privy. It was a door of riveted iron set into rock, with no lock that Kestrel could see, and when he pushed it, the hinges made a sound like a contented cat and swung inward onto lamplight and books.

Hundreds of books. Thousands. Shelves cut into the living stone, ladders on iron rails, reading desks scarred by decades of elbows. The air smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of ink that had been old before Kestrel's grandfather was a rumor.

"Well," said Kestrel, to nobody, because there was nobody to lie to. "This is inconvenient."

Because here was the trouble: he had told Maren he didn't know where Sorrel kept the prophecy book. He had told her this with the particular calm, slightly bored tone he used for all his lies, the tone of a man discussing weather or the price of tallow. And she had believed him, or seemed to, and now he stood in her library holding the memory of exactly where that book lived — third shelf from the floor, behind the tax ledgers, because Sorrel believed no one alive would voluntarily touch a tax ledger — and every hour he didn't tell her, the lie grew another ring around him, like a tree adding wood.

He should leave. He should find the privy, which was presumably down and *right*, and go back to the cot they'd given him in the old guardroom, and keep lying, because lying was what had kept him alive for thirty-one years.

Instead, Kestrel Underhill walked into the library and closed the door behind him.

*Just for a moment,* he told himself. *Just to think.*

The lamplight came from oil lamps set in niches, each one trimmed and full. Someone maintained this place. Someone loved it. He ran his fingers along a shelf spine and felt the small familiar pleasure of paper under his fingertips, the only intimacy he'd ever been good at. Books didn't care if you were fluent in everything except honesty. Books just wanted to be read.

There was a desk near the center with work in progress: a half-transcribed ledger, a pot of ink gone thick, a magnifying lens. Beside it, a plate with the fossilized remains of a bun. Kestrel smiled despite himself. Somewhere in the world there was always another clerk. Even underground. Even at war. Especially at war; wars generated paperwork the way rivers generated silt.

He sat down — sat down! in a rebel stronghold, uninvited, like a man testing whether a bear trap was sprung — and looked at the transcription.

It was a list of names. Dates beside them. Some marked with small symbols he didn't recognize: a circle, a broken line, a thorn.

His own handwriting habits were neater, but the abbreviations were ones he knew. Guild shorthand. Archive shorthand. *Sorrel's* shorthand.

Kestrel sat very still.

Then he did the thing he was actually best at in this world, better than beast-speech, better than binding, better than lying: he started working. Because when the ground opened under a man, the only sane response was to catalog what fell out.

* * *

It took him four hours. He knew it took four hours because a rat came in partway through — Warrens rats, leaner and warier than the guild's fat archivists, but still rats, still speaking the same liquid language of whisker and squeak — and told him, unprompted, that the tall one who smelled of knives had passed the door twice and both times stopped, listened, and moved on.

"Does she know I'm in here?" Kestrel asked, low, automatic.

"The knife-smelling one thinks you are sleeping," the rat said. "She is wrong. You smell of lamp oil and fear."

"Thank you," said Kestrel, who had long ago stopped finding it strange to be thanked-by or thanked-for nothing. "Is there any chance of a second opinion on the fear?"

"No," said the rat, and left, and Kestrel thought: *this is why I have no friends among humans.* Though the thought arrived without its usual sting. Perhaps because everything else had already burned; a little loneliness was hardly worth mourning on top of an arson.

By then he had the shape of it.

Maren's library was a counter-archive. Every document Sorrel's guild had classified, suppressed, misfiled, or simply lost, someone down here had copied, traded for, stolen, or reconstructed from fragments. There were marginalia in six hands, some arguing with each other across decades. One annotator signed only as *Oduya*, in a fierce slanted script, and Kestrel realized with a small cold jolt that Maren hadn't come to the guild on a whim. She'd been raised inside this argument. She was the third generation, at least, of people trying to read the same book correctly.

And the book — the book was here. Not Sorrel's original, but a fair copy, complete, rebound in grey leather by someone with more devotion than skill. Kestrel pulled it out with hands that were almost steady and laid it open on the desk under the best lamp.

*The Ledger of the Thorn,* said the title page. Below, in smaller letters: *Being a true account of the power of Ashvale's god of war, and of the manner of its keeping, set down so that none who come after may claim ignorance.*

*So that none may claim ignorance,* Kestrel read again.

He read that line four times before the horror of it finished arriving, the way a storm arrives over a plain — you see it coming for a long time and then suddenly it is on you.

Because he had spent three days assuming the prophecy was a prediction. A terrible, uncanny, coincidental prediction, but a prediction: words describing a future. That was bad enough. That had been bad enough to make him flee his own life.

But this wasn't a description.

He began at the beginning and read like a man defusing something, turning pages with two fingers, and the gentle humor drained out of the night entirely, and what was left was this:

* * *

A hundred years ago, Ashvale had a god of war the way a house has a hearth — everyone used it, no one owned it. His name, as far as the book preserved it, was Vashkarr, though the text notes dryly that this was probably not his real name, gods being famously cagey about such things. His power did not live in temples. It lived in a person. Always one person at a time. The book called that person the Thorn, and explained why with a metaphor the original author clearly found very clever: a thorn holds the rose's fury but not its beauty; it draws blood from anything that grasps too hard; and it cannot remove itself.

The last Thorn of the old war — the one whose refusal had ended it, the one whose story even Kestrel knew in nursery-rhyme form — had died refusing to pass the power on. Had died *deliberately* refusing, the book was clear about that, had gone to the pyre clutching it rather than hand it to any of the factions circling her deathbed like gulls.

Everyone assumed the power died with her.

The book disagreed. The book, in a section written in a different ink, a steadier hand, said: the power does not die. The power waits. It has waited before. It will pass to the next vessel who meets three conditions, and here the text enumerated them, and Kestrel read them aloud to the empty library in a voice that did not sound like his:

"*First: that the vessel be named in the telling, before the appointed hour.*"

Named. Written down. His name had been in that book for a century.

"*Second: that the vessel learn of the naming, and of the hour.*"

Learned. Tuesday night. A damaged volume, a rebinding job, a page turned out of idle habit.

"*Third:*" — and here Kestrel's voice failed him, so he read it silently, and then read it aloud anyway because some things you have to hear in the air to believe — "*that the vessel accept the mantle knowingly, by spoken word, before witnesses.*"

Knowingly. By spoken word. Before witnesses.

It was not a prophecy. It was a recipe.

Kestrel pushed back from the desk so fast his chair skidded, and stood in the middle of a rebel library with his heart going like a trapped bird, and understood, in one long nauseating roll, what Sorrel had done.

She hadn't predicted him. She'd *built* him. A hundred years ago — or whenever she'd actually written it, and some treacherous part of his mind was already doing that arithmetic, was already noticing how old her hands were, how old her eyes — she had written down the conditions of transfer disguised as foretelling, and then she had arranged, patiently, generation by generation, archive by archive, for the conditions to be met. Named him before the hour. Ensured he would learn. And now all that remained was the third condition, and the whole apparatus of the city — guild, palace, rebels, auction, hunters — was converging on him not because fate willed it but because *a woman with a pen had decided it should*, and every faction chasing him thought they were seizing destiny when they were only following instructions.

Words as weapons. Not metaphors. Not poetry. Words as actual weapons, load-bearing words, words that reached forward a century and picked a man out of a crowd and said *you*.

"That's not possible," Kestrel said to the library.

The library did not answer. But the book lay open on the desk, and the lamplight lay on the page, and the page said otherwise, and the worst part — the truly unbearable part — was that Kestrel *believed* it. Because he was a clerk. He knew what records did. He had watched dukes squirm over a single line in a ledger; he had seen debts enforced by nothing but ink and consensus. The guild's whole trade was built on the principle that what is written becomes real if enough people agree to act as though it were. Sorrel had merely applied the principle at scale. She had written a fiction so authoritative that reality had agreed to perform it.

And the annotations — he went back to them, because grief makes scholars of us all — the annotations got worse. Oduya's fierce hand, decades back: *"If the third condition requires consent, then the book is a trap with a polite door. Refuse, and it waits for the next name. There is no refusing forever. There is only choosing who."*

Someone else, later, angrier: *"Burn the original. Burn the copy. Burn the idea."*

Another, weary: *"We burned a copy in '61. The conditions persist. The words have already done their work. You cannot unsay a century."*

You cannot unsay a century. Kestrel put his palm flat against the page, as if he could hold the words down, pin them like insects. They were warm. That had to be the lamp.

* * *

He didn't hear the door. He heard the rat, though — the same rat, or its cousin, clearing its throat at ankle height with the delicacy of a butler announcing a guest — and Kestrel had perhaps four seconds to decide whether to hide the book, and chose, in those four seconds, not to. Partly because hiding it was impossible. Mostly because he was so tired of hiding things.

Maren came around the shelves with a lamp of her own and a loaf of bread under her arm, of all things, and stopped dead when she saw him standing over the open Ledger like a man at a graveside.

"You found it," she said.

"I found your library," Kestrel said. "I was looking for the privy."

Something crossed her face — suspicion, amusement, recognition; with Maren it was often all three wearing different hats — and then she saw which page was open and the bread dropped onto the desk and she forgot it existed, which Kestrel suspected she never did otherwise.

"How much did you read?"

"All of it." He turned to face her. "Maren. Do you understand what this is? It's not a prophecy. It never was. It's an instruction manual wearing a fortune-teller's coat. The first condition is a name written down. The second is the victim being told. The third—" His voice cracked and he hated it and pressed on. "The third is a yes. That's all it needs. A yes, spoken, witnessed. Everything else — the auction, Corvus Hale, the enforcers, you, *me* — we're all just delivery mechanisms for a sentence a woman wrote a hundred years ago."

"I know," Maren said quietly.

Kestrel blinked. "You know."

"We've known the shape of it for sixty years. My grandmother annotated that exact passage. Why do you think the Warrens has a counter-archive? Why do you think I risked the guild?" She stepped closer, and her voice had the flat intensity he was learning meant she cared enormously and would die before showing it normally. "We've spent three generations trying to break it, Kestrel. Burning copies doesn't work. Denying it doesn't work. The words are already in the world. The only thing anyone's ever agreed on is that the third condition can't be forced — it needs a willing yes. So we need to reach whoever's named before anyone can arrange a yes."

"And instead," Kestrel said, "you found me. Lying to you about where the book was. While sitting in your library reading it."

"Yes," said Maren. "That has been noted."

The silence stretched. Above them, muffled through stone, Ashvale went on being Ashvale — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the ordinary music of a city that didn't know it was a stage play with one speaking role left unfilled.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Kestrel asked. "About the mechanism. About the conditions. Three days, Maren. Three days of you asking me questions I thought were kindness."

"Because I needed to know if you'd read it and run to claim it," she said. "Power like that doesn't advertise itself as evil. It advertises itself as *useful*. As necessary. As the only way to save everyone you love." Her mouth twisted. "I've read enough history to know the yes usually comes dressed as duty. I wanted to watch you find it yourself and see what your face did."

"What did my face do?"

"Honestly?" Maren glanced at the open page, then back at him. "Your face did arithmetic. All night, I'd wager. You're the first person to read that book who immediately started calculating who wrote it and why, instead of dreaming about what the power could do." She picked up the bread, tore it, handed him half — a gesture so ordinary it nearly undid him. "That's either very hopeful or very alarming. I haven't decided."

Kestrel took the bread. He held it and didn't eat it, and heard himself say, before the clerk in him could file the impulse away: "Sorrel wrote it."

Maren went still. "You don't know that."

"The transcription on your desk uses her shorthand. Her private abbreviations — she thinks she invented them; she invented them forty years ago and told the apprentices they were guild standard. And the ink changes halfway through the original sections, older hand to newer, same penmanship, same habits, same — Maren, she corrects her own margins. Nobody corrects their own margins across a century unless—" He stopped. Breathed. "Unless the century is the point. Unless she's been keeping the trap oiled the whole time. She raised me. She taught me binding. She gave me the volume with my name in it, Maren. Damaged. Needing rebinding. *Needing* me to open it. Condition two, delivered with tea and encouragement."

The bread sat between them like a treaty neither side would sign.

"If you're right," Maren said at last, very carefully, "then the woman who taught you has spent your entire life growing you like a crop. And if you're wrong—"

"If I'm wrong, then I've just accused the only person who ever showed me kindness of manufacturing me, and I'll deserve whatever she does to me." Kestrel laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "Do you see the elegance of it? Even my doubt serves her. Every road leads to the third condition. Accept it willingly — her heir. Refuse it and name a successor — still her design, still her machinery. Die refusing, like the last Thorn, and the power waits, and the book waits, and somewhere she waits, patient as ink." He looked down at the page, at the neat, steady, monstrous hand. "There's only one move on the board she hasn't accounted for."

"Which is?"

"I don't know yet." And that was even true, which was becoming a novelty he intended to explore further. "But she wrote a book that runs on witnesses. On people agreeing. Everyone in that hall the other night agreed. The palace agrees. The rebels agree. The *beasts* agree — I heard them repeating it, Maren, the prophecy has gotten into the animals' mouths, that's how deep the words have rooted." He closed the Ledger, gently, the way you close a thing you respect and fear equally. "So the move has to be one that breaks agreement. Something that makes the words untrue by making them *seen*. Something—"

Boots in the tunnel. More than one pair. Moving fast, and not trying to be quiet, which in the Warrens meant either catastrophe or ceremony.

Maren snuffed her lamp with two fingers. "Corvus Hale's people found a way in," she breathed. "Or the guild did. Or both." She grabbed his wrist. "Kestrel. Whatever your one unaccounted move is — I suggest you find it quickly."

Above them, faint through the stone, a voice that Kestrel recognized with a lurch — smooth, reasonable, terribly kind — called down into the dark:

"Master Underhill? Might I have a word? I've brought an offer the crown assures me you can't refuse — and I do so hate violence before breakfast."

Kestrel looked at Maren. Maren looked at the bread.

Down and left had definitely been the wrong way.
