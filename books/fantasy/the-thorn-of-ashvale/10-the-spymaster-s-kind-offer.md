## 10. The Spymaster's Kind Offer

There were three ways to enter the Warrens, and Corvus Hale had memorized all of them before his twenty-fifth birthday, which was fortunate, because tonight he intended to use the one that smelled least like fish.

The tunnel beneath the tannery was dry, low-ceilinged, and lit at intervals by lamps that some rebel engineer had rigged to burn without oil — a small impossibility, the kind that made Corvus's professional heart ache with envy. The crown paid him very well to know things, and here were people giving away light for free just to spite the tax collectors.

He walked with his hands visible and his coat unbuttoned, because a spymaster who arrived looking armed was a spymaster who had already said too much. His boots made no sound on the packed earth. They never did. He had paid a cobbler an indecent sum for that, and considered it the best money he spent annually, including what he paid his actual spies.

At the third turning, a voice came out of the dark.

"That's far enough."

"Good evening," Corvus said pleasantly, to no one in particular. "Lovely tunnel. Excellent drainage. I want you to know I mean that as a compliment; most rebel infrastructure I've inspected has been tragically damp."

A pause. Then, grudgingly: "You're expected. Third door on the left. Don't touch anything."

"I never touch anything," Corvus said. "Touching is for amateurs."

* * *

The room they gave him had once been a counting-house, judging by the ghost-marks of ledgers on the plaster walls. Someone had hung a map of Ashvale over them, which struck Corvus as either optimism or irony. A single candle burned on the table between two chairs.

Kestrel Galloway sat in the far chair with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he clearly wasn't drinking, and looked, Corvus thought, like a man who had recently learned that the floor of his life had been painted on.

He'd read the file, of course. He'd written half of it himself over the years — *Galloway, Kestrel. Clerk, Guild of Impossible Things. Unremarkable. Punctual. Pays his rent early.* Files were portraits painted with the sitter's permission, and Galloway had spent a decade sitting very still for his.

But files didn't capture the eyes. The file said *unremarkable*. The man across the table had the particular stillness of someone listening to something no one else could hear. Corvus had seen it once before, decades ago, in a horse-trader who turned out to be able to calm any animal with a whisper — and who had died badly when the Guild found out what he was. Corvus had been young enough then to feel sick about it. He'd gotten older. He'd kept the feeling, locked away where it couldn't interfere with his work.

"You're the palace spymaster," Kestrel said.

"I'm Corvus Hale. The title is optional. Most people prefer the name; it sounds less like a summons."

"Why are you here?"

Corvus sat down without being invited, arranged his coat, and helped himself to the teapot. It was terrible tea — rebels always bought the cheap leaf, another thing they had in common with the crown's accounting office — but he drank it anyway, because a man who drank your tea was a man who trusted your poison-testing, and trust was the whole game.

"I'm going to tell you a story," he said. "It will take four minutes. You may interrupt twice."

Kestrel blinked. "Twice?"

"Three times and I'll conclude we're not suited and leave. Two is respectful. Zero is suspicious. Go on."

"...All right."

"A hundred years ago," Corvus began, "there was a war. Not the kind they put in ballads — those are about the winners. This was the other kind, the kind they bury, where everyone loses slowly and the ground itself learns to be afraid. At the center of it was a power. Old, enormous, and hungry. It lived in a person — the Thorn, they called the vessel — and the Thorn ended the war by refusing to give the power up, or so the story goes, until the power passed on and the vessel was left empty. The factions signed their peace, each one privately swearing to find the Thorn's next heir first and own them."

Kestrel's cup had stopped halfway to his mouth. He set it down carefully, the way men handle things when their hands aren't steady enough to admit it.

"And now," Corvus continued, "the Guild of Impossible Things is auctioning knowledge of the new Thorn's identity. Tonight. Which means the power has found its next home, and every knife in this city is being sharpened toward one person." He smiled, gently. "That's the story. You've used both interruptions already, incidentally."

"I haven't interrupted at all."

"No," Corvus agreed. "That was the interesting part."

* * *

He watched Kestrel work through it. He was good at watching people work through things; it was, professionally speaking, most of what he did. Galloway went through denial — a flicker, quickly banked — then arithmetic, lips barely moving, tallying everything he knew against what the spymaster had just handed him. The ledger-clerk habit ran deep. Corvus found it oddly endearing. Most people in this city lied badly and often; Galloway calculated.

"The auction," Kestrel said at last. "You know who's selling."

"Sorrel Vane. Your mentor. Yes."

Something moved behind the clerk's face — grief, mostly, arriving before anger could get organized. Corvus noted it and filed it. A man grieving his betrayer was a man who could still be reached.

"Then you know," Kestrel said quietly, "what she's selling."

"I know what she thinks she's selling. An identity. A name to hand to whichever bidder pays." Corvus set down his cup. "I'm here to offer you something different. Something better than a name."

"And that would be?"

"Amnesty," said Corvus Hale. "Full, sealed, royal pardon — for the beast-speech, for tonight, for whatever you did getting out of that vault, though I confess my sources disagree about the details in a way I find delightful. In exchange: you come to the palace. Voluntarily. Openly. And you become what the prophecy says you are — deliberately, on your own terms, under the crown's authority instead of the Guild's or the rebels' or whoever else comes knocking."

Silence. Somewhere above them, water dripped through old brick with the patience of things that outlast cities.

"You're asking me to become the Thorn," Kestrel said.

"I'm asking you to *choose* it before it chooses you. There's a difference, Master Galloway, and it's the only difference that has ever mattered." Corvus leaned forward. "Let me paint you the honest picture, since everyone else in your week has apparently been painting fraudulent ones. If you run, the Guild hunts you. If you hide with the rebels, Maren Oduya shelters you right up until her superiors decide a live Thorn is more dangerous than a dead one — and they will decide that, probably within the month; I've read their correspondence, it's very tidy. And if you do nothing, the power finds you anyway. That's what vessels are *for*. You don't get to opt out of being found. You only get to choose who's standing beside you when it happens."

"And if I stand beside the crown?"

"Then the war ends before it starts." Corvus spread his hands — the open gesture, the honest one, the one he'd practiced in mirrors until it stopped feeling false. "Think about it. Every faction wants you because they're afraid of everyone else having you. Take yourself off the board — place yourself in plain sight, bound by law and oath and, yes, supervision — and there's nothing left to fight over. No auction. No assassins. No war. One man walks into a palace and saves a hundred thousand lives by lunchtime."

"You make it sound simple."

"It is simple. It isn't easy. Those rhyme but they are not synonyms." He let a little warmth into his voice, real warmth, because the trick of temptation done properly was that none of it needed to be a lie. "I've read the old accounts, Galloway. The last Thorn was alone. Hunted by day, haunted by night, surrounded by people who wanted pieces of him until there was nothing left. I am offering you the opposite of that. Advisors. Protection. Purpose. And when it's over — if it can ever be over — a pension and a cottage somewhere green, and my personal word that no file with your name in it survives me."

"That last part," Kestrel said, "is strangely moving."

"I've been told I have a gift for endings."

* * *

The clerk was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Corvus refilled his own cup, drank, and refrained from filling the silence, which was the hardest discipline of his trade. Let the man think. Men thought best into silence; words were what they used to avoid thinking.

When Kestrel finally spoke, it wasn't refusal and it wasn't surrender.

"You said the last Thorn refused to give up the power."

"So the histories say."

"And the histories were written by whom?"

Ah. Corvus felt the question land somewhere near his ribs and take hold. "The victors. The Guild. The usual suspects."

"Then maybe the histories are wrong. Maybe refusing was the right call." Those grey eyes came up, and for a moment the unremarkable clerk was nowhere in the room. "You're offering me amnesty and a cottage, Spymaster. But you're also offering me a leash, and you've dressed it so carefully in duty that I think even you might believe it isn't there. I've spent my whole life around creatures in cages. The kind ones always say it's for the animal's protection."

Corvus held the look and did not flinch, because flinching was for amateurs.

"You may be right," he said. "About the leash. About the dressing. I won't insult us both by denying it." He rose, buttoned his coat, and laid a card on the table — heavy cream stock, the palace seal, nothing written on it but an address. "Here is what I can promise that no one else in this city can: I will tell you exactly what the leash is before you take it. Every clause. Every ugly practicality. The rebels need you to be a symbol, the Guild needs you to be an asset, and I need you to be a solution — but a solution gets told the truth. That's my entire pitch. Truth, from the one profession famous for lacking it."

Kestrel looked at the card but didn't touch it. "And if I burn it?"

"Then I'll write in my report that I found a clerk drinking bad tea and got lost on the way out." A pause. "Though I'd ask you to consider one thing first."

"What?"

"Tonight, while you've been sitting here, the rats in this district have been talking." Corvus smiled at whatever crossed the clerk's face. "Yes, I know about the rats. I know rather more than anyone finds comfortable — it's the job. And what they're saying, all of them, cats and pigeons and one extremely opinionated goose by the tannery, is fragments of your prophecy. Word for word. The text is spreading, Galloway. Into the streets. Into the animals. Into the world itself." He tipped his head toward the dark mouth of the tunnel. "Whatever you decide — decide soon. Because I don't think books are supposed to do that. And neither, I suspect, do you."

He left the candle burning behind him and walked into the dark, whistling softly, a man satisfied with an evening's work — and entirely failing to notice the small grey shape that detached itself from the shadows and followed him up toward the world, carrying word of the meeting back to ears he had never once thought to count among his spies.
