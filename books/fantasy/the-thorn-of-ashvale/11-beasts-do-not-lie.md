## 11. Beasts Do Not Lie

The menagerie district smelled the way it always had — straw and dung and roasting chestnuts from the vendor carts that lined Grackle Row — but to Kestrel's ears it had never sounded like this.

He had come there because it was the one place in Ashvale where a man could be strange in public without anyone looking twice. A clerk with hay on his coat and no destination was invisible among the beast-tenders and cage-menders and the pilgrims who came to press their palms against the bars of impossible things. Here he could think. Here he could breathe air that didn't taste of guild wax and old paper.

He had been breathing it for perhaps an hour when the mynah spoke.

*"...and the Thorn shall be revealed,"* said a voice from the aviary awning above him — a voice with the clipped, rounded vowels of Archivist Sorrel Vane, coming unmistakably out of the beak of a bird worth maybe six coppers at market.

Kestrel stopped walking so abruptly that a chestnut vendor swore at him.

He looked up. The mynah — gray, scruffy, missing half its left tail-feather — looked back down at him with the flat, incurious eye of a bird that had merely repeated what it had heard, which was what mynahs did, which was fine, which was *normal*.

Except that Sorrel Vane had never, in all his years at the guild, spoken those words aloud. He would have staked his modest salary on it. She kept the prophecy's language locked behind her teeth the way she kept everything else — filed, catalogued, and under key.

*"…revealed,"* the mynah said again, and then, in a different register entirely, a man's voice Kestrel didn't recognize: *"—on the night of the sealed auction—"*

"Who taught you that?" Kestrel asked, before he could stop himself.

A woman passing by gave him a look reserved for people who talked to birds, which was fair, since he was a person talking to birds. The mynah ruffled its feathers and answered with something far more useful than an answer:

*"Who taught you?"*

* * *

Kestrel stood very still for a long moment while the market flowed around him like water around a stone.

Then he did what he had done his whole life when the world grew too loud: he went to find someone smaller who might tell him the truth about it.

The rats were easy. They were always easy. Three of them lived beneath the chestnut cart — he'd known their grandmother, a magnificent scarred matriarch named — well, her name didn't translate, but it meant something like *Warm-Under-Cobblestones*, and she had once saved him from a beating by arranging for a constable to step in something memorable. Her grandchildren were less clever but more numerous, and they came to him now when he crouched by the cart wheel and made the small sound in the back of his throat that wasn't quite speech and wasn't quite squeaking.

*You smell of fear,* said the boldest one, a young male with a torn ear. *And of vault dust. You've been running.*

"I've been hiding," Kestrel corrected. "There's a difference."

*Not to us,* said the rat, reasonably.

Kestrel glanced around. The market crowd paid him no attention; a man crouching by a cart was furniture. He lowered his voice anyway, into the register that only the small ones caught properly.

"Something has happened," he said. "Something is — moving through the city. Words. Prophecy words. Have you heard them? From the other animals?"

The three rats looked at each other. It was a genuinely unnerving thing, watching rats exchange a glance — the little economy of it, the shared understanding.

*Everyone has heard them,* said the torn-eared one. *That is why we came to the surface. That is why we are telling you, Warm-Voiced One, though you have not brought bread.*

"You usually charge for information."

*This we give free,* said the eldest rat, an old female gone gray at the muzzle, *because it frightens us. Listen. Two nights past, the pigeons on Temple Spire began saying it. All together. At dawn. Like a bell that rings itself.* She paused, whiskers twitching. *We do not say things all together. That is not how speech works. Speech is mine or yours. This was everyone's.*

"What did they say?"

The old rat closed her eyes, and when she recited it, her voice changed — flattened, formalized, worn smooth like a stone in a river, as if the words had passed through ten thousand mouths and each mouth had sanded them a little more alike:

*"When the last door is sealed, the Thorn shall stand among the bidders, wearing a borrowed name."*

Kestrel felt the words go through him like cold water through cloth.

That was a line from the book. He knew it the way you know a scar — he had read it four nights ago in Sorrel's century-old volume, by candlelight, with his own heart hammering in his ears. *When the last door is sealed.* The sealed doors of the auction hall. *Wearing a borrowed name.* His name, borrowed, false, written on a bidder's chit he'd never held.

"Pigeons," he managed. "Temple pigeons said this."

*And the gulls. And the horses in the palace stable, so the stable boys say, though boys lie. And the dogs.* The old rat opened her eyes again. *And now us. We do not want to say it, Warm-Voiced One. We say it anyway. It rises in the throat like water rising. That is not what words should do. Words should be carried. These words carry you.*

Kestrel sat back on his heels.

Above him, the mynah said, conversationally, to no one: *"—and whoever accepts it knowingly shall wear the god—"*

"Stop," Kestrel whispered.

The mynah, having no reason to obey him, did not.

* * *

He spent the rest of the day walking the district, and everywhere he walked, the prophecy was waiting.

In the tinker's yard, a parrot named Captain — famous across Ashvale for knowing exactly forty words, thirty-nine of which were curses — sat on his perch and recited, in a voice like a hinge: *"The power passes only to the willing hand."* Then, brightly: *"Bollocks! Bollocks! The power passes—"*

In the fishmonger's alley, cats sat along the walls in a silent row, and when Kestrel passed, they turned their heads in unison, and one of them — a huge tom with a dockside swagger — said, in a voice that was somehow both cat and not-cat: *"—the heir shall be chosen before the choosing, and know himself only at the end—"*

At the oxen pens near the stockyard, a plow-team standing hip-shot in the afternoon light murmured it between themselves in the low, slow grammar of cattle, stretching each phrase out like taffy, until the prophecy sounded almost gentle, almost like a lullaby, which was somehow worse.

By evening Kestrel had heard fragments in eleven species' mouths, and the fragments fit together. They were pieces of the same text — Sorrel's text, the guild's text, *his* text — being repeated, garbled, reassembled, spread. Not taught. No animal had learned these words from another animal in any way he understood. They rose in throats unbidden, the old rat had said, like water rising.

Which meant the words were not traveling.

They were *growing*.

* * *

He bought a meat pie he couldn't eat and sat on the low wall outside the exotic wing of the city menagerie, watching the keepers chain up for the night. Gentle humor was going to be difficult to locate today, but the menagerie's oldest resident helped in spite of herself: a tortoise named Duchess, reputed to be older than the guild itself, who had spent two hundred years achieving absolutely nothing and was regarded by the entire district with something close to religious respect.

Duchess was making her nightly journey — eight feet from her water dish to her patch of clover — when Kestrel spoke to her, quietly, in the deep slow way one speaks to tortoises, which is mostly patience shaped like sound.

*Duchess. Old mother. Have they come to you too? The words?*

Duchess considered. Tortoises consider everything; it is their whole philosophy. When she finally answered, her voice was dry leaves and bedrock, and Kestrel loved her for taking so long, because every second of delay was a second the answer hadn't arrived yet.

*They came,* she said. *Three days ago. I was sleeping. I woke, and I knew things I had not learned.*

"What kind of things?"

*I knew your name,* said Duchess placidly, and continued her journey.

Kestrel's pie slid out of his hand and into the dirt.

*I know it still,* Duchess went on, unbothered. *Kestrel Galloway. Clerk. Beast-speaker. Thorn.* She reached the clover and began, with tremendous dignity, to eat it. *I did not want to know it. I have spent two hundred years carefully knowing nothing. It is the secret of my happiness. But the words came into me the way rain comes into a crack in stone — without asking, without malice, and now the crack knows the shape of the water.*

"Duchess." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "How do the words *do* that?"

Another consideration. A clover leaf, demolished.

*Words are older than you think, child. Before a word means, it wants. Most words want small things — to be said, to be remembered, to be useful at market. But some words are built wanting. Whoever made your words wanted very much, and wanted them to spread, and to make real whatever they touched.* She chewed. *Your archivist did not write a prophecy. Your archivist planted one. And plants, Kestrel Galloway, do not care whether you consent to grow.*

"But if the words can just — enter minds, rewrite what animals know — then what happens when they reach people? Everyone in Ashvale will know my name by winter. Everyone will *know* I'm the Thorn whether it's true or not, and if enough of them know it—"

*It becomes true,* said Duchess. *That is what the words are for. Truth is just a story with enough mouths behind it. The book is recruiting.*

Kestrel stared at her.

"The book is *what*?"

*Recruiting,* said Duchess. *A prophecy that no one believes is literature. A prophecy that everyone believes is a plan. Your archivist understood this a hundred years ago. Why do you think she wrote it to rhyme?*

"It doesn't rhyme."

*Everything rhymes,* said Duchess, *if you wait long enough.*

* * *

He stayed on the wall until full dark, because leaving required deciding where to go, and every direction led somewhere with ears.

The menagerie quieted around him, keeper by keeper, lamp by lamp. And in the gathering dark, the district began its new evening ritual — the one it had apparently been performing for three days without him, the one the whole city was performing now, from the temple spires to the sewer grates to the hay-lofts over the stable yards.

The animals spoke the prophecy.

Not all at once. In fragments, overlapping, passing phrases from throat to throat like buckets at a fire — mynah to magpie to barn owl, rat to cat to the great patient oxen, each fragment slightly worn, slightly wrong, growing more wrong and more widespread with every repetition. Kestrel lay back on the wall and listened to the whole district whispering his fate in a hundred voices that had never agreed on anything in their lives, and felt something break in him that he suspected would not mend cleanly.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing underneath everything.

Kestrel Galloway had built his entire life on the belief that the world came in two honest halves. There were humans, who lied — beautifully, constantly, inventively, sometimes kindly, usually not — and there were beasts, who could not. A dog's growl meant what it meant. A horse's fear was true fear. The rats told you when they were frightened and when they were hungry and never once, in all his years of listening, had one of them told him a story that wasn't real. When your whole life was a performance — a clerk's dull mask over a forbidden gift over a loneliness so old it had become furniture — you needed one place where the truth simply lived, without effort, without agenda.

The beasts had been his church. And tonight he sat in the ruins of it listening to his church recite someone else's scripture.

*"...wearing a borrowed name..."* said the owls, high and soft over the rooftops.

*"...the willing hand..."* said the cats in the fishmonger's alley.

*"...and the god shall wake in the vessel..."* said a dog, somewhere, in a perfectly ordinary dog's bark-and-word voice, and then, heartbreakingly, added the tail end of a completely different sentence, something about dinner, as if even prophecy got tired.

Kestrel laughed — one dry, awful note — and then pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

If the words could rewrite what beasts knew, they could rewrite what people knew. Were already rewriting it. Somewhere out there, right now, a baker was waking with a certainty she hadn't earned; a guard captain was remembering a rumor that had never been told to him; the whole city was slowly, gently, being *persuaded* — not argued with, not threatened, just seeded, season after season, until believing in the Thorn would feel like remembering rather than deciding.

Sorrel hadn't predicted the future. She'd written instructions and dressed them as memory.

And the worst part — the part that made Kestrel's chest ache in a place deeper than fear — was that he couldn't even hate the animals for carrying it. They were victims the same as he was. The mynah hadn't chosen those words. The pigeons hadn't. Even Duchess, ancient and serene, had had her two hundred years of beautiful ignorance cracked open like an egg.

*It rises in the throat like water rising,* the old rat had said.

Language itself had been conscripted. Every honest throat in Ashvale was now, potentially, a liar's instrument. There was nowhere a creature could go to speak freely, because free speech — true speech, unwritten speech — was exactly what the book was eating.

He thought, absurdly, of the ledger. Of inventory. Of the guild's shelves of bottled storms and caged songs and all the other impossible things that had once seemed like the strangest objects in the world. None of them, he realized, were half as impossible as a sentence that could walk into your mind and rearrange the furniture.

* * *

Near midnight, the district's whispers began to change.

Kestrel noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know — not immediately, but with a lurch. The fragments weren't random anymore. They were sequencing. Across the rooftops, alley to alley, the animals were assembling the prophecy in order, each voice picking up precisely where the last had dropped it, a relay of throats passing the text down the dark like a message moving through a besieged city.

And the passage they were building — deliberately, unmistakably — was the passage about the *heir*.

*"...the Thorn shall know himself..."* — the magpies.

*"...only at the moment of claiming..."* — a horse, somewhere near the stockyard, whickering the words into the night.

*"...and the one who wrote the words shall choose the one the words describe..."* — the rats beneath him, all of them, in unison, in the dark under the wall where he sat.

Kestrel went cold from the scalp down.

They weren't repeating the prophecy randomly. They were being *directed*. Something was using the city's animals as a messenger system, feeding the text out in sequence, and there were only two creatures alive who knew the full text: the archivist who'd written it, and the fugitive clerk currently sitting in the dirt with meat pie on his coat.

Which meant Sorrel was looking for him.

Or — and this thought arrived with its own private horror — which meant the *book* was looking for him. Reciting itself through every willing and unwilling throat in Ashvale, narrowing, converging, calling its designated heir home the way a bell calls a flock.

Either way, the message ended the same way. The final fragment came from directly above him — a nightjar perched on the menagerie's iron gate, its wide frog-mouthed face tilted down at Kestrel with what he could have sworn, in the moonlight, was sympathy.

*"Come and be revealed,"* the nightjar said, in a voice like Sorrel Vane's, in a voice like a hundred voices braided together, *"little thorn. We are all waiting to believe in you."*

Then it flew, and the district fell silent all at once, every throat closing like a hand.

Kestrel stood alone in the dark menagerie, in a city that now knew his name in its bones, and understood that hiding was finished. The words would find him anywhere. The only question left — the question crawling up through his terror like a shoot through rubble — was whether he would go to the book on his own feet,

or whether Maren Oduya, wherever she was hiding in the Warrens below, had already heard the nightjars too — and whether she had been sent to save the man named in the prophecy,

or to kill the Thorn it described.

He started walking toward the river.
