## 12. Maren's Truth

Maren Oduya had killed men for saying less than what she was about to say, and she had liked some of them better than she liked herself.

She stood in the doorway of the Warrens' guest cell — *guest*, the rebels insisted, as though a room with no window and one door were hospitality rather than strategy — and watched Kestrel Galloway try to fold a blanket. He folded it the way clerks do everything: precisely, apologetically, as if the blanket might file a complaint.

He'd been in the Warrens three days. Three days of soup that tasted like boiled boots, of rebel children staring at him because he was the first person they'd ever seen with ink stains on his fingers instead of soot, of Maren lying to his face at every meal.

"You're hovering," Kestrel said without looking up. "You've been hovering since breakfast."

"I'm standing near a door. There's a difference."

"In my experience," he said, smoothing the blanket's final crease, "the difference between standing near a door and hovering is about ten seconds."

Maren leaned against the doorframe and crossed her arms. Below them, through the floor, she could hear the Warrens going about its evening: someone tuning a fiddle badly, someone arguing about flour rations, someone laughing in the way people only laugh underground, where laughter has to work twice as hard to be believed. She had grown up in these tunnels. She knew every voice down there by its echo.

Kestrel didn't belong here. That was the whole problem. He sat in this cell like a man waiting for a train he hadn't bought a ticket for, polite and patient and utterly wrong, and every hour she let him sit there believing she'd rescued him out of kindness was an hour she spent becoming exactly the kind of person she'd joined the rebellion to stop being.

"Kestrel." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "I have to tell you something, and I need you to not interrupt, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve and we'll both keep pretending, and pretending is how Ashvale got into this mess."

He set the blanket down. He turned around slowly, and she watched him arrive at the truth before she said it — watched it land on him the way rain lands, gradually and then all at once. He had kind eyes. Nobody had ever warned her that kind eyes would make this harder.

"You weren't sent to save me," he said quietly.

"No."

"You were sent to kill me."

The word hung between them. *Kill.* Such a small, sharp thing. Four letters, like *book*, like *thorn*.

"If claiming the Thorn proved necessary," Maren said. "Those were my orders. The Council's words, not mine. If the prophecy's heir accepted the mantle — willingly, knowingly — then the Thorn would have to die before it could become the god's vessel. Before it could become another Sorrel Vane." She made herself hold his gaze. "I was sent to the guild to find the prophecy book and to find you. In whichever order required killing less."

"And which order did you decide?"

"The second one. Obviously." She heard her own voice crack on *obviously*, and hated it. "I've been trying to figure out why ever since. Best theory: you fold blankets like a man who's never once been thanked for it, and I'm a sucker for that sort of thing."

Kestrel didn't smile. That was fair. She hadn't earned a smile; she'd earned a thrown boot, maybe, or at minimum the blanket, hurled with intent.

"How long," he said. "How long have you known what I am? What I might be?"

"Since the auction night. When you ran through the vaults and every caged creature in the building started screaming your name like a hymn." She pushed off the doorframe and took one step closer, then stopped, aware that even her steps needed permission now. "I've been your guard ever since. Not your friend. Your jailer, if you want the honest word. A jailer who brings you soup."

"The soup is terrible."

"I know. It's punishment soup. The cooks think you're a spy."

"Aren't I?"

"Everyone down here is a spy. That's why the soup is terrible for everyone." She dragged a hand through her hair. This was going worse than rehearsed, and she had rehearsed it against a wall while a rat — an actual rat, one of the ones that followed him around like a small furry conscience — watched her from a pipe with what she'd swear was judgment. "Kestrel, I need you to understand the shape of it. The Council voted nine to four. Nine said kill the Thorn on sight. Four said wait and see. I argued for wait and see. I lost. And then I came here anyway and simply... didn't report back accurately."

"Lied to your own people."

"Every dispatch for six days. I told them the trail went cold. I told them the archive fire destroyed the relevant ledgers — which, incidentally, thank you for setting that fire, it made an excellent alibi even though I know you set it to cover your escape and not for my benefit."

"It was a very good fire," Kestrel admitted. Then, before she could be relieved that humor had survived contact with honesty: "Why didn't you kill me?"

And there it was. The question under all the other questions. Maren looked at him — this narrow-shouldered clerk with his ink-stained cuffs and his terrible blanket-folding and his gift for talking to rats that he still pretended was nothing — and she decided that if honesty was going to cost her everything, it might as well buy something first.

"Because I watched you free those creatures," she said. "Every impossible thing the guild owned — songs in cages, storms in bottles, things I don't have names for — and you opened them all. You didn't use them. You didn't sell them. You didn't even hesitate. And I thought—" Her throat tightened. She talked over it, fast, before the feeling could win. "I thought, whoever this man is, whatever's sleeping inside him, he is the opposite of everything the old war was fought over. The last Thorn hoarded power until it hollowed him out. You gave away a bottled thunderstorm to a moth. I saw that. I will see that until I die. A *moth*, Kestrel."

"It looked cold."

"It was a moth."

"Moths are cold," Kestrel said, with the absolute conviction of a man who had, presumably, discussed the matter with the moth. "Nobody believes that. Moths are always shivering. They fly at flames because they're freezing, not because they're stupid, and everyone calls them stupid for it, and—"

He stopped. He heard himself. Maren watched him hear himself — watched the realization move across his face that he was defending moths in a cellar while a woman who'd been sent to murder him waited on his verdict.

"You're not angry," she said.

"Oh, I'm angry." He picked up the folded blanket, considered it, and set it down again, as though anger required props. "I've been angry since chapter — since the auction. Since I found out my entire life was a ledger entry. But you—" He exhaled. "Maren, you were given an order to kill a stranger, and instead you lied to a council of people you've loved your whole life, in their own house, eating their terrible soup, to protect that stranger. Do you know what that costs? I do. I know exactly what loyalty costs when you break it, because I broke mine tonight—"

"What?"

He went pale. Actually pale, the blood leaving his face in real time, and Maren understood with a lurch that he hadn't meant to say that either. Honesty, apparently, was contagious.

"Kestrel. What did you do?"

He sat down on the cot. The cot complained. Everything in the Warrens complained; it was how the tunnels expressed solidarity.

"I lied to you," he said. "At dinner yesterday. When you asked if I knew where the prophecy book was."

Maren kept her face very still. Inside, something dropped through several floors of her chest. "And?"

"And I do know." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet, and furious, and relieved, all three fighting for space. "Or I know where it *was*. The night of the auction, before I ran — I took it. I hid it. I've known where it is every single day you've sheltered me, fed me, lied to your Council for me. And I let you believe I didn't, because—" He laughed, a short, awful sound. "Because I didn't trust you. Because everyone who's come near that book has wanted something from it, and I thought you'd be the same. Because I'm a coward who trusts vermin more than people, and honestly the vermin have a better record."

The rat in the pipe made a sound that Maren chose to interpret as wounded dignity.

"The book is the only weapon against this war," she said slowly. "The Council needs it. The palace wants it. Sorrel Vane would burn cities for it. And you've had it hidden this whole time."

"Yes."

"You let me confess my death sentence to you while sitting on the location of the most dangerous object in Ashvale."

"When you put it that way it sounds much worse than when I put it that way."

"It sounds worse because it *is* worse. Or—" Maren stopped. Breathed. Sat down next to him on the complaining cot, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, far enough that forgiveness still had room to maneuver. "No. It isn't. It's exactly as bad as mine, and mine was worse, actually, because yours is just fear and mine was a knife. We're a matched pair, aren't we? Two liars in a cellar."

"The founding tradition of Ashvale," Kestrel said. "They should put it on the city seal."

Silence settled over them — not comfortable, but honest, which was rarer and worth more. Down below, the bad fiddle found its key at last and began, tentatively, a song. Somewhere a pot boiled over and someone swore with impressive creativity.

"Why did you tell me?" Maren asked finally. "About the book. You could have taken it to your grave. You could have taken it anywhere."

"Because you went first." He said it simply, as if it were obvious, as if it explained everything. "Because I've spent my whole life fluent in everything except honesty — beasts, birds, rats, the language of ledgers — and the one tongue I never learned is the one where you say a true thing out loud and let it cost what it costs. And you just did that. You walked in here and handed me the worst sentence anyone's ever said to me, and you didn't dress it up, and you didn't run." He shook his head. "I've been running from one sentence my whole life. *I can hear the animals.* One sentence. That's all it would have taken to be myself, and I couldn't pay it. So when you paid yours..." He spread his hands, ink-stained and trembling slightly. "It seemed rude not to match you."

Maren stared at him. Something in her chest that had been clenched since the auction night — some muscle she hadn't known she was flexing — let go all at once, painfully, like a fist uncurling.

"We're going to be terrible at this," she said.

"At what?"

"Honesty. We're going to be *awful*. We're both professionals. It's like two master forgers agreeing to sign their real names. There'll be relapses. I'll lie to you about whether I slept. You'll lie to me about what the pigeons are saying."

"They're mostly saying nothing. Pigeons are terribly dull conversationalists. All real estate and crumbs."

"See? Relapse already."

Kestrel laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, brief and rusty, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years. Maren found she wanted to hear it again, which was a dangerous want, and she filed it away in the growing drawer of dangerous wants she was keeping these days.

"So," she said, and stood, and offered him her hand — the same hand, she noted, that had been authorized to end him. "The book. Will you show me? Not the Council — not yet. Me. Us. Whatever this fragile, doomed arrangement is."

Kestrel looked at her hand. Then he reached beneath the loose board under the cot — she should have searched the cell properly, she was losing her touch — and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, roughly the size of a large bread loaf and considerably more apocalyptic.

"I hid it in the one place no faction would ever look," he said solemnly.

"Where?"

"Under my bed. Nobody in the history of Ashvale has ever looked under a clerk's bed. It's the single sacred ground left in this city."

Maren took the bundle. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, the way guilt is heavier than paper, the way promises are. Through the oilcloth she could feel the edges of the boards, the shape of a century of waiting.

"The Council meets tomorrow night," she said. "If I bring them the book, they'll call off the kill order. Probably. Nine votes to four is close enough that a century-old prophecy might tip it. But once I hand it over, it stops being ours and starts being theirs, and theirs has a track record."

"And if you don't bring it," Kestrel said, "and they find out you had it and didn't—"

"Then I'm a traitor twice over, and the Warrens stops being home." She weighed the bundle in her hands. "Funny. Six days ago I'd have handed it over without blinking. Now it feels like handing over something alive."

"That," said Kestrel quietly, "might be because it is."

Above them — impossibly above them, floors and streets and the whole weight of the city above them — something scratched. Once. Twice. A rhythm Maren almost recognized.

Kestrel tilted his head, listening to a frequency the rest of the world couldn't reach, and his expression changed from warm to worried so fast it made her stomach drop.

"What is it?" she asked. "What are they saying?"

His answer, when it came, arrived in a voice gone cold and careful:

"The rats say the guild's enforcers just bought every map of the lower tunnels in the city." He met her eyes. "Sorrel knows where we are. And Maren — she's not sending soldiers."

* * *

Down in the dark beneath the Warrens, past the flour stores and the fiddler and the children, the oldest rats gathered in a circle around a scrap of parchment gnawed from a ledger's spine, and read, in the manner of rats — which is to say, completely, instantly, and without mercy — the contract-magic beginning to seep downward through the stone like water finding a crack.

One of them, the small scarred one Kestrel called Brother, said a single word into the dark.

It traveled upward, tooth by tooth, toward the vaults where a hundred-year-old woman was preparing her ink.
