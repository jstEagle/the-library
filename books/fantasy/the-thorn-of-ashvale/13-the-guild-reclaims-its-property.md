## 13. The Guild Reclaims Its Property

Sorrel Vane had stormed exactly four buildings in a hundred and six years, and she had hated every one of them.

The trouble was not the violence. Violence was honest work, like masonry — it required planning, patience, and a tolerance for dust. The trouble was the *undignified* part: the boots on stairs, the shouting of her own name by men who mispronounced it, the inevitable moment when someone threw something at her head. In the Warrens beneath Ashvale's tannery district, she reflected as her enforcers assembled in the dripping dark, that moment was statistically likely to arrive early.

"Archivist," said Enforcer Brack, who was built rather like a door that had learned to walk, "the rebel lookouts are dealt with. Two of them. They were asleep."

"Asleep," Sorrel repeated. "Guarding the most dangerous secret in the city. Asleep."

"One of them was snoring, ma'am."

"Well. At least the rebels have kept their sense of humor." She pulled her hood up. Underneath it, her hands were doing what they always did now when she let them be still: trembling. She pressed them flat against her coat. *Not yet,* she told the thing coiled behind her ribs. *You will have your heir tonight, and then you may do whatever you like to me.*

The Thorn had been killing her for ninety years, slowly and with great courtesy, the way a guest eats through your pantry. It did not hurt, precisely. It simply took. Her hair had gone from black to ash to nothing at all; her bones had gone quiet; her reflection had begun, some decades ago, to lag half a heartbeat behind her movements, as though even mirrors found her tedious. A hundred years ago she had written a prophecy to solve this problem. Tonight she intended to collect on it.

"Positions," she said softly. "And Brack — no fire. No blades unless they insist. I want the boy *unbruised*."

"The boy, ma'am? You said the property—"

"He is the property." She adjusted her glove. "Mind how you handle him."

* * *

The Warrens were everything the Guild of Impossible Things was not, which is to say warm.

Sorrel felt the wrongness of it immediately — candlelight where there should have been ledgers, laughter where there should have been silence, soup. Someone, somewhere above, was singing badly and with tremendous conviction. It offended her professionally. Archives should smell of paper and restraint. This place smelled of onions and *hope*, and hope, in her long experience, was the most flammable material in Ashvale.

They moved through the smugglers' corridors two by two. Brack went first, absorbing obstacles. Sorrel followed, cataloguing. Rebel graffiti on the walls — *THE GUILD LIES*, painted in red, and underneath, in smaller, more literate hand, *so does the palace, ask us for sources*. She allowed herself one dry breath that might charitably have been called a laugh.

The hidden library was at the heart of it, because rebels were sentimental creatures and always put their books somewhere central, like hearts. And if Kestrel Galloway had done what she knew he would have done — clever boy, dutiful boy, boy she had spent twenty years shaping with the particular tenderness one gives to a lock being cut — he was reading the prophecy right now. Finding himself in it. Finding the date. Finding the trap laid out in ink older than his grandfather.

She had written him beautifully. That was the shame of it. Twenty years of small kindnesses: the extra coal in his grate each winter, the promotion he never asked for, the way she had taught him to bind books with his own hands so that when he finally held hers, his fingers would know the shape of the truth before his mind did. Every kindness had been a sentence. Every sentence had been a hinge.

She did not regret it. Regret was for people with time.

"Ma'am." Brack stopped at an iron-banded door, hand raised. From beyond it came a sound that made Sorrel's ancient heart perform a small, unwelcome maneuver: a voice reading aloud. Low, careful, growing less careful with every line.

Kestrel had reached the middle passage. She knew it by the rhythm. *And the Thorn shall know its own name, and the choosing shall be made knowingly, or not at all.* He would be frowning. He frowned exactly the way she had taught him to frown over corrupt ledger entries, which was to say, entirely too honestly.

"On my mark," she whispered. "Gently."

She marked. The door came down.

* * *

Later — much later, when the trembling in her hands had spread to everything else — Sorrel would remember the scene in the library as a kind of tableau: Kestrel rising from a crate-turned-table, the prophecy book open under his palm, a stub of candle guttering, and around him perhaps forty rebels reaching for weapons they mostly didn't have. But in the moment itself, all she saw was his face when he turned and found her standing in the wreckage of his hiding place.

He looked, first and foremost, *glad*. For about one full second. That was the worst of it — that second, before the arithmetic caught up. One second of pure relief, *she's here, she'll fix this*, written across features she knew better than her own. Then his eyes moved to Brack. To the enforcers. To the iron manacles Brack carried with the delicacy of a man holding someone else's baby. And the gladness went out of him like heat out of an opened oven.

"Sorrel." His voice stayed level. He'd learned that from her too. "You found me."

"I wrote you," she said. "Finding was incidental."

One of the rebels — a big woman with a tanner's forearms — stepped forward. "You're not taking anyone out of—"

"You're Maren Oduya's people," Sorrel interrupted pleasantly. "Maren Oduya is currently negotiating with the palace spymaster, who has promised her a pardon in exchange for the boy. Ask her when she next lies to you. Which will be soon." This was, strictly speaking, only seventy percent true, and Sorrel had survived a century on a strict diet of seventy percent truths. The rebels wavered, which was all she needed. People wavered beautifully when you handed them a doubt with a bow on it.

Brack and two others crossed the room. Kestrel could have run — there was a window, a drain, a dozen rat-roads she herself had shown him over the years — but he looked at the enforcers, then at the rebels, then at the book under his hand, and she watched him do the sums. If he ran, they'd take the library apart looking for him and burn the Warrens' goodwill doing it. He was, infuriatingly, still protecting other people. She had installed that too. She was beginning to understand that she had never once in a hundred years done anything without installing something.

The manacles closed around his wrists. Contract-iron. She saw him feel it — the little cold tug at the base of the throat, where the words lived — and saw him try, experimentally, to speak.

His mouth moved. Nothing came out.

"That's the binding," she said gently. "Clause nine. Your voice is guild property until the transfer. I'm sorry for the indignity of it. Truly." And she was, in the way one is sorry about weather.

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment. He couldn't speak, so he did the thing that had always unnerved her slightly: he listened past her. His gaze drifted down — to the floor, to the shadows along the wall, to the small grey shapes that had frozen there when the door came down. Warren rats, dozens of them, whiskers trembling.

He couldn't say a word. But his face said plenty, and what it said was: *they can hear you. Everything you say now, they hear. And they tell him things.*

Sorrel Vane had planned for swords, for fire, for treachery, for Corvus Hale's velvet knives. It had genuinely not occurred to her that her own architecture might include a listening apparatus with whiskers.

"Kill the rats," she said wearily, and then, as Brack drew breath to obey: "No — don't, we haven't time and it's cruel besides. Just—" She waved a hand. "Everyone stop saying anything useful. Move."

* * *

They brought him up through the tannery yard into the grey end of night, bound and silent, flanked by enforcers, while above them Ashvale's bells counted a hour that no longer belonged to him. Sorrel walked beside him because she wanted to talk to him and talking required proximity, and also — she admitted this in the sealed vault of herself where she kept such admissions — because she wanted, one last time, to walk beside him.

The wagon waited at the yard gate. Beyond it, the city lay under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and somewhere in that city every faction was awake and sharpening, because tonight the auction concluded and the lot was a person.

"You're wondering," she said, "when I decided."

He couldn't answer. She answered anyway; that was rather the point of her.

"It wasn't tonight. It wasn't last month. It was a hundred and six years ago, on a battlefield you've read about in sanitized editions." The wagon swayed. Brack drove. The rats, damn them, flowed alongside in the gutters like a grey tide keeping pace, and she made herself ignore them. "I was there at the end of the old war, Kestrel. Not as a soldier. As a clerk." She smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "Yes. Even then. The last Thorn was a woman named Isolde Ferrane, and she was dying, and the power in her needed somewhere to go, and everyone — kings, priests, generals — everyone wanted to catch it like a bride-catch at a wedding. She refused them. All of them. Do you know what refusing does, when the thing inside you needs a vessel?"

Kestrel shook his head slowly. His eyes hadn't left her face.

"It tears," Sorrel said. "It goes out of her sideways, into the ground, into the water, into the *words*, and it waits. Power like that doesn't die. It just gets patient. It soaked into the language of this city like wine into bread. Every prophecy since, every omen, every fortune-teller's fever dream — all of it, downstream of Isolde Ferrane saying no. And I stood in the mud with my ledger and my pen, twenty-six years old and already too fond of ink, and I thought: someone should write this down properly. Someone should write it *forward*."

The wagon turned onto the processional road. The Guild's towers rose ahead of them, lit for the occasion, windows glowing gold like a cake set with candles.

"So I did. I wrote a prophecy with a name left blank and a date left blank and a shape left deliberately, carefully, precisely shaped — shaped like a lonely clerk with a gift he hides even from himself. It took decades to settle. Prophecies are stubborn things; you can't force them, you can only garden them. I planted circumstances instead of seeds. I arranged for a certain orphan to be hired at a certain archive. I taught him to bind books. I taught him to trust me." Her voice caught on the last word — a genuine hitch, unplanned, hateful — and she pressed on. "And the blank filled itself in the way blanks do, given enough pressure and enough years. With your name. With tonight."

They passed under the gate. Enforcers saluted. Somewhere above, the auction hall was filling with liars in good coats.

"Why?" The word didn't come out of Kestrel's mouth — it came out of his eyes, his shoulders, his whole straining silent self, and Sorrel heard it perfectly.

"Because it's killing me," she said, simply, and watched the sentence land on him like a dropped book. "The Thorn doesn't need a vessel to exist. It needs one to *stop eating*. I've carried the seed of it for a century — dormant, waiting for the ritual, waiting for a willing heir, because unwilling heirs make ruins and I have seen the ruins. Ninety years of it drinking me by inches. My hair. My warmth. My—" she flexed her fingers, and they trembled, and this time she let him see. "I am not a monster, Kestrel. Monsters don't get tired. I want to put it down. That's all. That is *all*. One willing soul takes the mantle, the hunger leaves me, and I can finally — finally — rest. Die, probably. Gladly. But rest."

For a long moment neither spoke. The wagon rolled through the guild courtyard, past the caged songs — and Kestrel turned his head toward them, toward the faint impossible chorus of bottled nightingales and imprisoned thunder, and something in his expression made Sorrel's chest ache in a way she had not authorized.

Then he looked back at her, and his eyes were wet, and furious, and — this was the unbearable part — *compassionate*.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Don't you dare pity me. I built you. I built the trap you were born into. I let you love me so the prophecy would hold, and it worked, and in three hours you'll kneel in that hall and choose, willingly, because the alternative is handing a war-god to Corvus Hale and letting the crown wear it like a crown. That isn't cruelty, boy. It's triage. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. And I would do every single moment of it again."

He held her gaze. Silent, bound, property — and somehow, in that moment, the only person in Ashvale telling the truth.

The rats poured up over the courtyard wall behind them, dozens strong, and sat in the shadows with their whiskers forward, missing nothing.

Sorrel stepped down from the wagon and straightened her coat, and the doors of the Guild of Impossible Things opened onto light and murmuring and the smell of money.

"Bring my property inside," she said. "The ritual begins at midnight."

Behind her, unheard by anyone but the rats, Kestrel's silent mouth formed a single word against the binding — and the smallest of the rats cocked its head, and remembered.
