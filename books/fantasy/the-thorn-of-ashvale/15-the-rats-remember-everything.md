## 15. The Rats Remember Everything

The rat's name was Button, and Button had a grievance.

It was not the first grievance in his long and storied life — he had once spent an entire winter nursing a vendetta against a specific boot — but it was, by his own accounting, the gravest. The tall one, the quiet one who spoke to them in the dark hours when no two-legs listened, who had fed them cheese rinds from his own supper and asked nothing but gossip in return — *that* one was locked behind iron now, and the iron was wrong.

Not iron-and-stone wrong. Rats understood iron. Iron was honest. It sat where you put it and did what iron did. No, this wrongness smelled of *paper*. Of ink dried over promises. The whole lower vault hummed with it, a low sour note that made Button's whiskers ache and sent three of his cousins to bed early with headaches.

The other two-legs called it contract-magic. The rats called it *the bad smell of words that bite*.

Button stood on a barrel of pickled harpy feathers — do not ask; guild inventory was a strange country — and addressed the assembled clan of the Ashvale undercrofts: forty-one adults, nine adolescents of questionable judgment, and one elderly matriarch named Duchess who had survived four purges, two floods, and a brief fashionable period when the guild had tried to train cats.

"You all remember the Tall One," Button said. "The one with soft hands and sad eyes."

"He gave us the good cheese," said a young rat named Splinter. "Not the wax kind. The *aged* kind."

"He spoke to us," said Duchess, from her place of honor atop a fallen ledger, "as though we were worth speaking to. And now the words have bitten him shut." She let that settle over the clan like dust. "I have lived a long time, longer than any rat has a right to, and I will tell you what I know about words that bite. They are written somewhere. Everything is written somewhere. The guild writes down its sins and files them alphabetically."

A ripple of appreciative squeaking ran through the clan. The guild's filing system was, all rats agreed, its single greatest gift to their species. A building that kept everything, labeled everything, and stored everything in chewable proximity — it was practically a temple.

"So," Button said, "we go to the ledgers."

"The ones that bite?" asked Splinter.

"Especially the ones that bite." Button bared his teeth, which for a rat is less a threat and more a way of life. "We have been gnawing at the edges of impossible things since before this guild had walls. Tonight we gnaw at the worst one. Tonight we set a voice free."

Duchess raised herself up on her haunches, old bones creaking like a ship in harbor, and spoke the words every rat of Ashvale knew by heart — the oldest law, older than the guild, older than the city that had grown up around their tunnels without ever once asking their permission:

"*What is written can be eaten.*"

And forty-nine rats, plus one elderly matriarch, went to war.

* * *

Three floors above and one world away, Maren Oduya pressed herself into the shadow of a buttress and counted guards for the fourth time, because the first three counts had made her want to lie down in a gutter and reconsider her life.

Twelve on the guild's outer wall. Eight in the courtyard. Two at each door, and the doors themselves were sealed with paper seals that glowed faintly in the dark like the guild was wrapping itself in very expensive bandages. Somewhere below all of it, in the vaults, they were holding Kestrel Galloway — the clerk, the liar, the man who had looked at her in the Warrens with those careful gray eyes and told her exactly as much truth as a man tells when he's deciding whether you're worth deceiving.

She should have hated him for the lies. Instead she kept thinking about the moment in the hidden library when he'd read the prophecy's true mechanism aloud — his voice going quiet and horrified, like a man finding his own fingerprints on a murder weapon — and how he'd looked up at her and said, "You knew the book could do this. You just didn't know it would be me."

He wasn't wrong. That was the humiliating part.

"Maren." Wick's whisper came from the drainage grate at her feet, along with a smell that suggested he'd enjoyed the journey more than anyone should enjoy a sewer. "Tunnel's clear to the undercroft. Well. Clear-ish. There's something down there."

"Something?"

"A lot of somethings. Small ones. They're... organized." He paused. "Maren, I've seen rebel cells with worse discipline."

She slid down beside the grate. Below, in the lamplight bleeding up from the tunnel floor, she saw them: rats. Dozens of rats, sitting in rows along the sewer wall like a congregation waiting for a sermon, and at their head an enormous gray matriarch regarding Maren with the flat patience of a creditor.

"They've been following me since the guild," Wick said. "I thought it was the bread in my pockets."

"It was never the bread," Maren murmured, because some part of her — the part that had grown up on her grandmother's stories about the old wars, when the beasts of Ashvale still spoke and the city listened — understood exactly whose side the rats were on.

She knelt at the grate's edge, feeling absurd, and addressed the matriarch directly. "You know the way in."

The matriarch blinked slowly. Then she turned, marched six precise steps to a rusted grate-cover Maren would have sworn was bolted, and nudged it with her nose. It swung open like a well-oiled door.

Behind her, in ranks, forty-odd rats turned to look at Maren in perfect unison, which was the single most unnerving thing she had experienced since joining the rebellion, including the time she'd been shot.

"Right," Maren said faintly. "After you, then."

* * *

In the deepest vault of the Ashvale Guild of Impossible Things, bound in chains of contract-paper and silence, Kestrel Galloway was having a conversation with himself, which was difficult, because he couldn't speak.

Not couldn't-speak the way a shy man can't speak. Couldn't-speak the way a bird in a cage of clauses couldn't fly. When he opened his mouth, the words came out as breath, as shape, as nothing. The binding sat across his throat like a hand. Every attempt felt like swallowing a closed book.

So he talked inside his own skull instead, which after three days had developed a certain rhythm. *All right. Inventory of the situation.* Old clerk habits died hard. *One: I am imprisoned beneath the guild I served faithfully for eleven years. Two: my mentor — my only friend, if you can call a leash friendship, and apparently I could, which says something damning about me — wrote a hundred-year-old prophecy to turn me into a vessel for a dead god's temper. Three: tomorrow night, she performs the transfer ritual, and if I refuse, the power goes to Corvus Hale, who smiles like a knife being polite.*

*Four:* — and here his internal voice wobbled — *four: nobody knows where I am except people who want me to be here.*

He tested the binding again anyway, the way you press a bruise. His lips shaped *help*, and the word dissolved on his tongue like sugar, leaving only the taste of ink. The contract-ledger chained to his wrist rustled its pages contentedly. He was fairly sure it was smug. Books could be smug. He'd catalogued enough of them to know.

That was when he heard the scratching.

At first he took it for the building settling, or his own despair inventing sounds. But it came again — small, rhythmic, patient — from the ventilation shaft near the ceiling, and it was joined by a second scratch, and a third, until the walls of the vault seemed to murmur with tiny industrious teeth.

Kestrel went very still. Three days of silence had frayed him, but some instincts ran deeper than despair, and one of them was this: *when the small things start working together, pay attention.*

A gray head poked out of the vent. Then another. Then, with the unhurried confidence of a duchess entering her own ballroom, an enormous old rat descended the stone wall headfirst, crossed the floor, and sat down directly in front of Kestrel's chained wrist, regarding the contract-ledger with an expression of profound professional interest.

"Duchess?" Kestrel breathed — uselessly, soundlessly, but old habits. "Is that you? How did you—"

The rat ignored him. She was examining the ledger's binding the way a jeweler examines a setting, whiskers twitching, testing the leather with one delicate nip. She recoiled slightly — the bad-smell-of-biting-words, Kestrel realized, even he could feel it humming against his skin — shook her head hard, twice, like a horse refusing a fence.

Then she looked back over her shoulder at the vent and made a sound Kestrel had never heard a rat make: a short, commanding chirp.

The vent erupted.

They poured out of it like spilled ink given purpose — dozens of them, fifty, more, flowing down the walls in gray rivers, and every last one of them converged on the ledger at his wrist. They didn't scatter or squabble. They formed lines. They took positions along the binding's edge like sappers along a wall, and when Duchess chirped again, they began, in unison, to *chew*.

"Oh," Kestrel said, or meant to say, or thought so loudly it barely mattered. "Oh, you brilliant, terrible little—"

He wanted to tell them to stop, that the binding might snap back, that Sorrel would punish them, that they were rats and this was magic and the gap between those things had always been supposed to keep them safe. But he had no voice to forbid them with, which struck him, even mid-crisis, as the funniest joke the universe had played on him yet: silenced by ink, defended by teeth.

The ledger shuddered. Its pages flipped wildly, showing him glimpses of clauses as the rats chewed toward their roots — *the Bearer shall not disclose* — *disclosure constitutes breach* — *breach forfeits the tongue's consent* — and around each clause, gnawed clean through, little crescent bites in the vellum, and where the bites went, the words went with them, fading like footprints in tide-wash.

Because that was the thing about contract-magic, Kestrel thought, watching a rat named — he was almost sure — Splinter worry at a crucial subordinate clause like it owed him cheese. It was still *paper*. It was still *words*. And words, however mighty, however prophetic, however ancient and cruel, had one universal weakness, one enemy older than any guild:

Everything written can be eaten.

The ledger screamed. There was no other word for it — a high papery shriek as its spine split, as clauses unraveled into loose threads of meaning, as a century of binding-magic came apart between the teeth of creatures no one had ever thought to bind because no one had ever thought them worth the ink.

The chain fell off his wrist like a shed skin.

And the hand around Kestrel's throat — the ink hand, the silent hand — let go.

He sucked in air. He tasted dust and freedom and, distantly, rat. And then, because eleven years of clerical restraint shattered all at once, Kestrel Galloway opened his mouth and *laughed* — a ragged, rusty, ridiculous sound, loud in the vault, loud in three days of enforced quiet — and the sound of it echoed up the ventilation shaft and out into the dark of the guild, where every beast that had escaped during his flight through the caged songs lifted its head and heard it, and began to move.

"*There* he is," said a voice from the vent — a fox, sleek and urban, who had spent a week in the vault of bottled storms and come out with opinions. "Took you long enough. We've been chewing since midnight. Do you know what contract-vellum does to the digestion?"

"You can talk," Kestrel croaked.

"We could always talk. You could always hear. The difference," said the fox, hopping down and shaking himself, "is that nobody was listening before, least of all you. Now come on. Your rat friends say there are two-legs in the sewers with your scent on a scrap of your shirt, and the big one with the scarred knuckles is crying, which is frankly embarrassing for everyone."

* * *

Maren came up through the floor of the archive anteroom behind a wave of rats, a crow, and one deeply indignant tortoise, expecting a fight.

She got Kestrel standing in a doorway covered in paper dust, rubbing his wrists, looking at her like a man seeing weather after a long drought.

"You're late," he said.

His voice. Rough, cracked, real. Maren felt something in her chest unknot so suddenly she nearly staggered. She'd told herself she'd come back for the book, for the rebellion, for strategy. Standing here, she admitted what Wick had known for weeks: she'd come back for the clerk with the careful eyes who lied beautifully and forgave easily and carried everyone's secrets except his own.

"The sewers were busy," she managed.

"With what?"

"Democracy, apparently." She jerked a thumb at the rats streaming past them into the dark, moving with terrifying purpose toward — she would learn later — the guild's main record hall, where a certain archivist's private ledgers awaited the attention of forty-nine sets of teeth. Duchess brought up the rear and paused, just for a moment, to fix Kestrel with a look that needed no translation anywhere in any kingdom: *you owe us the aged cheese. All of it.*

"I heard you laughing," Maren said. "Half the guild heard you laughing. It was the best sound I've heard in months."

"It'll be the last thing Sorrel hears too, before she realizes—" He stopped. His face changed. "The ritual. Tomorrow night. And Corvus—"

"Corvus Hale arrived an hour ago," Maren said grimly. "Under seal of the crown. He's upstairs drinking Sorrel's wine like it's already won."

"Then we don't have until tomorrow night." Kestrel flexed his newly freed hands, and Maren saw it happen — the clerk falling away, something harder and kinder taking its place, something no ledger had written and no prophecy had planned. "We have until he finishes the wine. Maren — the rebels, your people, everyone you trust, get them here. Because I'm done being the answer to someone else's question."

Above them, through floors of stone and shelving, they both heard it begin: the small, ceaseless, joyful sound of the guild's records being devoured, alphabetically, by the smallest citizens of Ashvale.

Somewhere on the upper floors, Sorrel Vane went quiet mid-sentence, listening to the sound of a century of secrets turning into nesting material — and reached for her oldest name, and her oldest power, because the heir she had manufactured was coming up the stairs, and he was bringing the whole chewing, flying, unbindable world with him.
