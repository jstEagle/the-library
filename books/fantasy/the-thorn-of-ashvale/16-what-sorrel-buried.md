## 16. What Sorrel Buried

The vault beneath the Guild of Impossible Things did not have windows, which was a mercy, because Sorrel Vane had spent a hundred years avoiding the sky. The sky kept track of things. It counted days the way she counted debts — patiently, and without forgiveness.

She stood over Kestrel Galloway where he lay on the reading table, wrists bound in contract-ribbon that shimmered like spider silk dipped in candlelight. His voice had been taken from him an hour ago; his eyes were doing all his talking now, and they were saying quite a lot.

*Fury*, mostly. And under it, worse: *hurt*. She had known the boy for nine years. She had taught him to rebind spines with linen thread and beeswax, to catalogue bottled storms by the pitch of their murmuring, to never, ever trust a ledger with clean margins. She had liked him. That was the part she had not planned for, back when he was still a name in her own handwriting.

"You're wondering," she said, "when I became this."

He didn't answer. He couldn't. But his jaw set in a way she recognized from mirrors she no longer owned.

"Let me tell you about the war," Sorrel said, and pulled a stool close, the way she used to when he was new and nervous and dropped everything made of glass.

* * *

"The histories say the War of the Sundered Crown began over territory. They say two dukes quarreled over a river valley, and the quarrel grew teeth." She smiled, thin as paper. "Histories are written by people who need the past to be boring.

"The war began because of a man named Aldous Ferren. Does the name mean anything to you? No, of course not. We buried him very thoroughly. He was the Thorn."

Kestrel's breath caught. She watched him work it through — the word from the prophecy, the word everyone in Ashvale had been killing toward all week — and she watched him arrive at the obvious question he could not ask.

"What *was* the Thorn? I'll tell you. He was a vessel. A man who, through no virtue and no fault of his own, carried the last living power of the war-god Ashkarath. Do you know what that power does?" She leaned forward. "It doesn't make you strong. That's the nursery version. What it actually does is make you *certain*. Perfectly certain. About everything. You look at a battlefield and you know, bone-deep, unshakably, which side is right. You look at a negotiation and you know exactly what must be done. And everyone around you feels your certainty and mistakes it for wisdom, because certainty is the most contagious disease there is.

"Aldous Ferren was a farmer. A good one, by all accounts. Kind to his oxen. Then the god's power found him — it always finds someone; it's drawn to people who doubt themselves, which is the cruelest joke in its design — and overnight, Aldous became the most trusted man in three kingdoms. Kings asked his blessing before battles. Armies marched on his word alone.

"And here is the tragedy, Kestrel. He was *right*. Every time. His certainty was accurate. He ended sieges in afternoons. He settled blood-feuds that predated the kingdoms. For six years, the world worked better than it had ever worked, because one modest farmer knew exactly what to do and everyone believed him."

She let that settle. In the silence, somewhere above them, a caged song shifted in its sleep — a lullaby from a drowned village, murmuring half-forgotten verses.

"Then came the day he was wrong."

Kestrel's eyes narrowed slightly. *Wrong how?*

"He ordered a retreat at the Battle of Marrow Ford. It was the correct call — any general since would have made it. Eleven thousand men lived because of it. But the retreat passed through plague country, and the soldiers carried the sickness home, and forty thousand civilians died in the winter that followed. Not his fault. Not anyone's fault, really, except the plague's. But Aldous had spent six years being perfectly certain, and the world had spent six years believing him, and when his certainty finally broke, it broke like a dam." Her voice went quiet. "He did not surrender the power, Kestrel. That's what the old books won't say plainly. The power *can* be given up — poured into consecrated ground, released, ended. There is a way. There has always been a way. But giving it up means admitting you might be wrong again someday, means going back to being an ordinary man whose opinions are only opinions. And Aldous — kind, decent, broken Aldous — could not do it. So instead he tried to *fix* what he'd broken. More orders. More certainty. Each remedy worse than the wound. Two dukes raised armies against him within the year, and the Sundered Crown burned for eleven years, and it all traces back to one man who could not bear to be merely human again.

"When he finally died — and it took an army to manage even that — the power slipped loose and went looking. It drifted for a generation, then another, settling nowhere, waiting. And I—" She stopped. Started again. "I found it first. Or it found me. I was younger then. Younger than you'd believe."

* * *

She rose and crossed to the far wall, where the oldest ledgers slept behind glass, and pressed her palm to a panel so old the mechanism answered to blood rather than keys. The case swung open on a single book — plain, brown, unremarkable, the sort of thing you'd skip past a thousand times.

"I was born in the year the war ended," she said, her back to him. "I am one hundred and four years old. The guild's founding charter lists me as 'Sorrel Vane, junior archivist,' and I have been junior archivist, senior archivist, head archivist, and finally a myth called 'the Founder' whom nobody believes is real, all without aging a day past fifty. Do you know why?"

She turned. Held out her hands — steady, unspotted, wrong.

"Because the Thorn's power doesn't just leave when its vessel dies. It *waits*. And something of it soaked into me the night Aldous died, when I stood too close to the body. A splinter. A seed. Enough to stop my clock. Not enough to give me certainty — thank every merciful power for small favors — but enough to keep me alive while it waited inside me for a proper vessel to come along."

Kestrel had gone very still. Stillness was a language too, and she read him fluently: *You've been carrying it. All this time.*

"A century," she said. "Do you understand what that means, practically speaking? It means I have watched every person I ever loved grow old and die, twice over. It means I founded this guild to house impossible things and became the most impossible thing in it. It means I have spent one hundred years with a splinter of a war-god lodged under my ribs, whispering — not words, nothing so kind as words, but *certainty*, leaking into me like damp through stone. Some nights I know things I should not know. Some mornings I wake up absolutely convinced of some terrible necessity, and I have learned to check those convictions against written records, because half of them aren't mine."

Her composure cracked — just a hairline, just enough. She sat down heavily on the stool.

"And it's killing me, Kestrel. Slowly, these last twenty years, faster lately. The splinter wants a true vessel, and I keep refusing to be one, and the refusal is eating me from the inside the way a fire eats a house that keeps its doors shut. I have perhaps a year left. Less, if I keep using guild magic, which I have been doing shamelessly all week."

She looked at him then, directly, and let him see all of it — the exhaustion under the archivist's crisp manner, the age under the unlined face, the hundred years of funerals.

"So I wrote the prophecy."

His whole body flinched against the ribbons.

"Not a prophecy. A *trap with elegant typography*. I took everything I knew — the power's habits, its preferences, its timetable — and I wrote it down as though it were foretold. Because here is the secret at the bottom of everything, Kestrel, the thing I have learned across a century of cataloguing miracles: *prophecy is just history wearing tomorrow's clothes.* The power reads what is written about it the way water reads a channel. Tell it, in ink old enough, that a clerk named Kestrel Galloway will be revealed on a particular night, and the power begins arranging itself to be true. I chose you the way you'd choose a lock for a door. Carefully. With measurements."

*Why me?* his eyes demanded, and she flinched at the honesty of it.

"Because you doubt yourself," she said simply. "That's the criterion — I told you, it's drawn to self-doubt. But more than that: because you're *good*. Because in nine years I never once saw you cheat a debtor or mock a donor or crush a beetle that wandered onto your desk. Because if someone had to inherit a war-god's certainty, I wanted it to be someone who would hate having it. Someone who would feel the weight. Aldous was good too, and it destroyed him — but Aldous had no warning. I gave you warning. Pages of it. Time to think. Time to decide *knowingly*, which matters, because the transfer requires knowing acceptance. That was my one act of conscience in the whole design, if you want the truth. I built you an exit into the trap."

She laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle.

"Do you see the shape of it now? The auction. The factions. I leaked the prophecy's existence deliberately, let every hungry faction in Ashvale come sniffing, because the power needed witnesses — belief is fuel; the more people who believe tonight matters, the truer tonight becomes. And when the ritual completes and you accept — and you will accept, Kestrel, because the alternative is watching Corvus Hale take it with the crown behind him, and I've read your face long enough to know you'd rather burn than hand that man a war-god — when you accept, the splinter leaves me. I die. Finally. Ordinarily. On schedule, like a person, instead of hanging on like a debt unpaid."

She reached out and, gently, touched his bound wrist. He didn't pull away. That was somehow the worst part.

"I'm not asking your forgiveness. I'm telling you what I buried, because you deserve the whole ledger, even if I'm the one keeping it. The war didn't begin with ambition or greed or any of the grand stupid reasons wars claim. It began with a good man who couldn't put down something heavy. And I have spent one hundred years making sure that when it happened again, there would be someone standing nearby holding out their hands to take the weight — so it wouldn't have to be me anymore."

The confession hung in the vault air, and for a moment she felt lighter than she had in decades — the specific lightness of a grave finally opened and aired.

Then Kestrel moved. Slowly, deliberately, despite the ribbons, he turned his head toward the shadowed corner of the vault — toward the dark seam of the ventilation shaft where, Sorrel realized with a cold spreading horror, a pair of small bright eyes had been watching from the shadows for some time.

A rat. One of *his* rats. Grey, ragged-eared, patient.

And beside it, gnawing steadily through the spine of a contract-ledger as though it had been at the task for hours, a second.

Sorrel came to her feet. "No," she said. "No — those are bonded instruments—"

The rat in the shaft chittered once, high and sharp, and from everywhere in the walls came an answering sound — dozens of voices, dozens of small teeth beginning to work in earnest, and above the vault, faint but unmistakable, the caged songs waking one by one into song.

* * *

Sorrel lunged for the alarm bell, and the first ledger fell open, bitten through, its binding-spells unraveling into ordinary thread.
