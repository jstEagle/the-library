## 17. The Ritual Begins

They had dressed me for it.

That was the detail that undid me more than the chains had — more than the contract-magic coiled around my throat like a collar of cold ink, more than the vault converted overnight into something that looked less like a library and more like a church that had been argued with. They had *dressed* me. Someone — Sorrel, I suspected, because Sorrel believed ceremony was a kind of honesty, which was the single funniest thing about her — had laid out robes of deep ash-grey on the cot in my cell, with a thorn-work embroidery at the hem so fine it must have taken someone months.

I put them on. Not out of compliance. Out of curiosity. Also, the cell was cold, and vanity is the last fortress of a doomed man: if I was going to die or worse in this room, I intended to look like the villain of someone else's story while doing it.

The robes fit perfectly.

*Of course they do,* said the rat in the wall, who had not left my side since the night she gnawed her way through three ledgers and half an enforcer's boot to reach me. She sat now in the shadow of the washbasin, whiskers twitching, and her voice came into my head small and dry as paper turning. *She's been measuring you for years, Kestrel. Every time you bent over a ledger. Every time you reached for a high shelf.*

"That's not funny," I murmured. My voice came out rough. The contract-magic didn't stop speech — only one particular sentence, one particular truth, wound so tight around my windpipe that even thinking about saying it made my throat feel like a drawer being closed. "None of this is funny."

*I'm a rat,* she said. *Everything is funny if you survive it long enough. That's how you know you haven't yet.*

* * *

The vault doors opened at midnight exactly, because Sorrel Vane had spent a hundred years learning that timing was the difference between ritual and theater, and she refused to be accused of theater.

Four enforcers walked me down the central aisle of the guild's deepest archive, where the shelves usually held bottled storms and caged songs and the debts of dukes. Tonight those shelves stood empty. Cleared. In their place: candles, hundreds of them, arranged in concentric rings around a circle of white chalk on the floor, and the circle itself inscribed with words in a language I recognized only because I'd spent the last weeks drowning in it — the old tongue of the Thorn, the war-god's grammar, the language in which every verb meant *to endure* and every noun meant *the cost*.

Sorrel waited at the circle's edge. She wore no robes. She wore the same grey archivist's coat she'd worn every day of the eleven years I'd known her, ink-stained at the cuffs, and somehow that hurt worst of all. Betrayal in ceremonial dress would have been a stranger. This was just Sorrel, my mentor, the woman who taught me to mend spines without breaking them, standing beside an altar made of my life.

"You look well in ash-grey," she said.

"Someone measured me for years without telling me," I said. "I should hope it fits."

Something moved behind her eyes — regret, maybe, or its expensive imitation. "Kestrel—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of me harder than I meant. An enforcer shifted; the rat in my sleeve went still. "You don't get to say my name like it's a kindness tonight. You wrote me, Sorrel. Before I could walk. Before I could talk. You wrote me into a book and then you hired me and taught me to love the shelves where you kept the trap, and I want you to know—" My voice caught. Not on the magic. On everything else. "I want you to know I loved working here."

"I know," she said softly. "That's why it worked."

* * *

The bidders arrived after midnight, because shame keeps late hours too.

Corvus Hale came first, of course. The palace spymaster entered like a man entering his own drawing room, which is the most dangerous way anyone enters anything. He wore court black trimmed in silver, and he smiled at me across the candle-rings with the warm, reasonable smile of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice because other people's voices did all the raising for him.

"Master Galloway." He inclined his head. "Or may I call you the Thorn? It saves time, and time is the only thing His Majesty and I are short of."

"He's not the Thorn yet," Sorrel said. "That's rather the point of tonight."

"And yet here we all are, dressed for it." Corvus drifted along the circle's rim, reading the chalk inscriptions upside down with the ease of a man who read everything upside down professionally. "Tell me, Archivist — when he refuses, and he will refuse, he strikes me as a refuser — the power passes to the next willing vessel. And we both know who that is."

"The crown's candidate," Sorrel said carefully, "would require the Thorn's consent to be named as heir. The ritual allows the current vessel to designate—"

"Yes, yes." Corvus waved this away like smoke. He looked at me again, and the warmth in his face did not diminish, which was the frightening thing about him. The warmth never diminished. "Let me speak plainly, Galloway, since your mentor prefers riddles and my schedule doesn't. Refuse her, and the god's power goes hunting. It will find me. I have prepared for it, I am willing, and the crown stands behind me. Ashvale gets a Thorn on a leash instead of a Thorn at large. Everyone sleeps soundly. Or—" he spread his hands, generous, terrible— "accept it yourself, keep your own conscience as your master, and we all take our chances with your famously soft heart."

"You're offering me a choice between your leash and your leash," I said.

"I'm offering you a choice between two leashes," Corvus agreed pleasantly. "One of them holds a man who enjoys the work. Choose accordingly."

Maren wasn't there.

I looked for her anyway — scanned the shadows past the candlelight, the gallery above the stacks, every place a rebel courier might hide — and found nothing but dark and dust and the small furious heartbeat of a rat pressed against my wrist. But I thought of her. I thought of the Warrens, and her confession by lamplight — *I was sent to kill the Thorn if it came to that* — and her hand shaking slightly when she said it, and how forgiveness had felt like stepping onto ice and finding it held. If Maren was in this building tonight, she was outnumbered ten to one. If she was outside it, there was nothing she could do but what rebels always do: wait, and grieve in advance, and plan.

*Stop looking for her,* the rat said. *Look at the circle. Look at the chalk. Rats can't read, but we remember everything we've chewed through, and I've chewed through ledgers older than her, and that inscription—* her whiskers trembled— *that inscription is wrong, little clerk. One line of it. Deliberately wrong. I don't know what it does. But she drew it wrong on purpose, and she's standing very close to it.*

I filed that away in the small locked room inside me where I kept everything I couldn't yet use. It was getting crowded in there.

* * *

"Before we begin," Sorrel said, and her voice changed — dropped the archivist's crispness and took on something older, something that had waited a century for this sentence — "you deserve to understand what you're choosing."

She stepped into the outermost ring of candles. The flames bowed toward her, all together, like a congregation.

"The Thorn is not a person. You learned that already — I'm proud of you for it, actually, you learned faster than any of my previous candidates." She said *previous candidates* the way other people say *deceased relatives*. "It is a mantle. A weight. The last vessel of a war-god's strength, passed from bearer to bearer for nine hundred years, and each bearer has grown drunk on it, or mad, or merely tired enough to believe the world would be simpler under their thumb. The last war began when a Thorn refused to set it down. I have spent one hundred years making certain the next transfer happens cleanly, willingly, and to someone whose character I have personally inspected." She smiled at me, and it was almost tender, and it made me want to be sick. "Eleven years of inspection, Kestrel. You were the best of them. Kind. Careful. Fluent in every voice but your own — which means you listen, and listeners make poor tyrants."

"And if I say no?"

"Then Corvus says yes, and we discover whether a leash holds a god's remnant or merely decorates it." She lifted the prophecy book — my book, the book with my name in century-old ink — from the altar. "There is no door out of this room that leads somewhere clean. I won't pretend otherwise. I've never lied to you, Kestrel. I've only ever let you lie to yourself, and there's a difference, though I grant it's a fine one."

"There is one truth you're keeping me from saying," I managed. The contract-magic tightened at my throat, a drawer closing on my windpipe. "You bound it. Why? What truth is so dangerous to your ritual that you had to stitch my mouth shut?"

Sorrel's expression flickered — the first honest thing on her face all night, and it looked like fear.

"Because truths spoken before witnesses lose their power," she said quietly. "And there is one truth in that book that must keep its power until the transfer is done."

* * *

The ritual began with her reading.

She read in the old tongue, and I felt every word land on me physically, like snow accumulating on shoulders — each syllable a small cold weight settling into place. The chalk lines brightened. The candles leaned inward. Somewhere far above us, muffled by stone, I heard the guild's freed creatures stirring — the things I'd released during my flight, the caged songs and restless beasts that had followed me through the vaults calling my name in a hundred voices — and their noise grew angrier, closer, like weather deciding.

Corvus watched from the gallery steps with his hands folded, patient as arithmetic.

And I stood in the center of the circle with a rat in my sleeve and a dead god queuing up behind my ribs, and I did the only thing left that was mine: I thought.

Every door was terrible. Accept, and become the thing the old war fought against — a soft-hearted tyrant, which history assures us is the most efficient kind. Refuse, and hand the mantle to Corvus Hale, who would wrap it in law and patriotism and feed it the city one reasonable decree at a time. Run, and the power simply took whoever it found next — some baker, some child, some stranger with less warning than I'd had. There was no clean exit. Sorrel had built the room that way. She'd had a century of practice building rooms.

But she had also drawn one line of chalk wrong on purpose.

And she had stitched shut my mouth against one specific truth — which meant that somewhere in the burning heart of that awful book, there was a sentence she feared more than she feared me.

*Little clerk,* said the rat, very softly, as the chalk-light climbed my legs like rising water. *Whatever you're going to do, do it before the water reaches your head.*

The old words closed around me. Sorrel's voice rose toward its final cadence, a hundred years arriving at its destination. Corvus leaned forward on the gallery step.

And I opened my mouth — not to accept, not to refuse — and reached for the one truth in the world that Sorrel Vane had forbidden me to speak.

Her eyes went wide.

"No," she breathed. "*Not yet—*"

The contract-magic strained against my throat like a chain tested to its limit — and somewhere above us, faint but unmistakable, I heard the scrabble of many small teeth beginning to work through old paper.
