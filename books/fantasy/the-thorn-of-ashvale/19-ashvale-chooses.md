## 19. Ashvale Chooses

### Corvus Hale

Corvus Hale had attended forty-one auctions in his career as spymaster, and he had a professional rule about all of them: the moment the room stops watching the seller and starts watching each other, someone is about to die.

The prophecy book was burning. That alone would have unsettled him — books in the Ashvale Guild did not burn; they were bound against it, warded against it, occasionally assassinated against it — but what truly troubled Corvus was that Kestrel Galloway was still talking.

The clerk stood on the auction dais in his rumpled inventory coat, reading aloud from a text that was consuming itself even as he spoke, and every word that left his mouth landed in the room like a dropped lantern.

"—and the Thorn shall be revealed by the hand of she who wrote him," Kestrel read, voice cracking but carrying, "for the mantle requires an heir who *chooses* it, and choice cannot be manufactured except through the manufacture of the chooser—"

"Stop him," said the palace delegation's second, a nervous man named Pell whose entire function in life was to say stop things.

"No," Corvus murmured. "Watch."

Because here was the thing Corvus understood that Pell did not: the book had been the crown's best asset for exactly one hour. In that hour, Corvus had learned the identity of the Thorn, drafted three contingency plans, and mentally composed a letter to the Lord Protector beginning *Dear sir, I am delighted to report*. Now the asset was ash, the clerk was broadcasting its contents to two hundred armed people, and the only question that mattered — the only question that had ever mattered in Ashvale — was who else knew what, and how badly they wanted to keep it secret.

The answer, judging by the faces around the hall, was: everyone, and very.

Across the chamber, Guild Enforcer Dagny Marsh had gone the color of old tallow. Corvus knew why. Fragment seven of the prophecy, the one about *the enforcer who sold her own sister's name to buy her rank*, had just been read aloud, and Dagny's sister happened to be standing in the merchant delegation, and her sister happened to be holding a knife she had apparently brought for other purposes and was now reconsidering.

*There it is,* Corvus thought, with something almost like affection. *There's the war.*

It was beautiful, really. Every faction had come tonight believing itself the cleverest thing in the room, and now they all stood revealed to one another — the rebels' tunnels mapped in fragment twelve, the palace's bribes itemized in fragment nine, the guild's debts of blood tallied throughout. The prophecy hadn't predicted a war. It had *inventoried* one, complete and cross-referenced, and Kestrel Galloway was reading the ledger out loud.

"Someone kill him," whispered Pell.

"He's not the problem anymore," Corvus said. "He finished the problem when he opened his mouth. The problem is that we all heard it."

And then the doors opened, and the animals came in.

### Maren Oduya

Maren had fought in three skirmishes, survived two betrayals, and once talked her way past a checkpoint using nothing but a borrowed hat and total commitment to being someone named Bess Tulliver. None of it prepared her for the fox.

It trotted down the central aisle of the great hall with the unhurried confidence of a landlord inspecting property, and behind it came everything else — rats in rivers, hawks off their perches, the freed menagerie from the vaults pouring through every door and window like water finding its level. A bear took up a position near the wine table and ate something that might have been the ceremonial centerpiece. Nobody stopped it.

"They followed him," Maren breathed. She was pressed against the wall near the dais, close enough to see Kestrel's face — pale, singed at the sleeve, but standing straighter than she'd ever seen him. "All of them. They followed *him*."

The fox reached the front of the room, sat, and looked at the assembly with an expression Maren could only describe as judicial.

Then it spoke.

"*You want the power so no one else can have it,*" it said, in a voice like dry leaves, and it was looking directly at Corvus Hale.

The spymaster did not flinch, which Maren noted professionally. But he also did not deny it, which she noted personally.

The fox turned its head. "*You want it because you think it will make you matter.*" This to the merchant delegation's matriarch, Dame Ilse Ferran, whose mouth opened and closed twice without producing sound. "*You want it because you are afraid of dying, and you have confused power with living.*" To a hawk-faced man from the temple contingent. "*You want it because your father wanted it, and you never learned there was any other thing to want.*"

On and on. The fox moved through the crowd like a lamp through a dark house, and everywhere its gaze fell, Maren watched people flinch from their own reflections. It wasn't cruel, exactly. That was the terrible part. It simply *stated*, the way Kestrel stated things, plainly, without decoration, and the plainness was the cruelty.

A rat scrambled up a pillar beside her and addressed the guild enforcers collectively: "*You keep secrets because knowing things is the only way you know you exist.*" A raven landed on the auction block: "*You bid on people because you were sold once yourself and swore you'd be on this side of the scale.*" A slow old tortoise, deposited apparently by some cosmic joke at the feet of the palace delegation, said in a voice like a door creaking: "*You would start the war tomorrow if you were certain of winning, and you call that peacekeeping.*"

"Stop them," someone shouted, and half a dozen blades came out, and not one person moved toward the animals, because everyone in the hall had realized simultaneously what Maren realized a heartbeat later:

If you drew steel on a creature that could see into you, everyone would know *why* you drew it.

Dagny Marsh lowered her knife. Her sister lowered hers. Somewhere in the middle of the room, two rival merchants who had spent the evening pretending not to know each other were caught, mid-glance, in the act of recognizing each other, and both looked away too late.

"It's not attacking us," Maren whispered. She found she was gripping Kestrel's sleeve without remembering reaching for it. He had come down off the dais at some point, drawn by the same gravity she had. "Kestrel. It isn't attacking anyone."

"No," he said softly. "It doesn't need to." He was watching the fox with an expression Maren couldn't read — grief and relief fighting over the same ground. "It's just telling the truth. Turns out nobody in this city has ever been attacked by that before."

The fox's ears swiveled. Its eyes found Kestrel across the crowded floor.

"*And you?*" it asked. "*What do you want?*"

The whole hall went quiet enough that Maren could hear the book burning — a soft, papery whisper, like snow falling on coals.

Kestrel opened his mouth. Closed it. And then, wonder of wonders, he laughed — a short, startled, honest laugh, the first Maren had ever heard from him.

"I don't know," he told the fox. "That's the truth. I genuinely don't know. Isn't that marvelous?"

The fox considered this for a long moment, tail curled neatly over its paws.

"*Yes,*" it said. "*That is why we follow you.*"

### Sorrel Vane

Sorrel had lived one hundred and six years, and she could count on one hand the nights that had gone worse than this one, mostly because most of those nights had involved actual fire rather than merely metaphorical fire, and metaphorical fire, she was discovering, burned longer.

She stood at the edge of her own auction hall, in her own guild, and watched two hundred of Ashvale's most powerful people stand meekly still while a fox audited their souls.

She should have felt triumphant, perhaps, or ruined. She felt neither. What she felt — and this was the humiliating truth of it, the kind of truth the beasts were handing out freely tonight, so she supposed she might as well have hers — was *tired*. Bone-tired. Century-tired.

Look at them, she thought. Look at them seeing themselves. The spymaster, caught wanting a leash instead of a weapon. The dame, caught wanting significance. The priest, caught wanting his father's approval across a hundred years of grave dirt. All the secret engines of the city, running in the open air, sputtering, stalling —

— starving. That was the word. The war was starving. Secrets were its grain and the beasts had scattered the granary, and already she could feel it in the room, the peculiar deflation of people who had been braced for violence and been offered honesty instead. Fury needs somewhere to go. Truth, spoken plainly, gives it nowhere.

"You planned this," said a voice at her shoulder.

Sorrel didn't turn. She knew Dagny Marsh's tread, and she knew the particular flatness in her enforcer's voice that meant Dagny had decided not to draw her blade only after extensive internal negotiation.

"I planned a transfer," Sorrel said. "Not a reckoning. The boy exceeded my design. They usually do, in the end. It's the flaw in all prophecies — you write a person, and they insist on being a different one."

"The fragments. About me. About my sister." Dagny's jaw worked. "Did you write those true?"

Sorrel finally looked at her. "I wrote them *effective*. Whether they were true was never my department. That was always the guild's whole business model, Dagny. We sell the mirror. What people do when they look into it has never once been our responsibility."

"That's a coward's philosophy."

"Yes," Sorrel agreed pleasantly. "I'm a century old; cowardice is load-bearing at my age."

But she watched, as she said it, as the hall continued to rearrange itself around the truth. The factions weren't reconciling — she was under no illusions about that; hatreds a generation deep do not dissolve in an evening, and Dame Ferran was already whispering furiously with her attorneys — but they were *recalculating*. Every plan in the room had been built on the assumption that one's own intentions were hidden. With that assumption dead, the plans were dead with it. You cannot ambush a man who can see you coming. You cannot blackmail a city that has heard itself described.

The war-god's mantle, Sorrel thought, feeding on ambition, on secrecy, on the gap between what people showed and what they were — well. Tonight that gap had closed like a market closing. The god must be feeling peckish.

Her eyes went to Kestrel Galloway, standing in the wreckage of her life's work with ash on his sleeves and a fox at his heel, and she felt the mantle stir inside her own failing body like a hungry animal smelling an empty dish.

*Soon,* it seemed to say. *One way or another. Soon.*

She had one card left. It was a terrible card. It was, in fact, the worst card she had held in a hundred years, and it was going to hurt everyone in the room including herself, and the mantle was rising whether she played it or not.

Sorrel Vane straightened her spine, arranged her face into the expression that had governed this guild since before most of these families had names, and began to walk toward the dais.

### Kestrel

Later, Kestrel would try to explain the sensation of the hall choosing, and fail. There was no vote, no declaration. It happened the way weather happens.

It began with Dame Ferran, of all people. The matriarch rose from her seat, smoothed her skirts, and said, in a voice pitched to carry, "For the record, I withdraw my interest in whatever remains of tonight's proceedings. I find I've been outbid." And she sat back down to the stunned silence of a woman who had, apparently, surprised herself.

Then the hawk-priest stood and said, "The temple also withdraws," and added, quieter, "and I would like it known that my father was wrong about many things," which was not strategically necessary but visibly lifted something off him.

The enforcer Dagny Marsh sheathed her knife with an audible click and announced that the guild's contracts, effective immediately, were "under review," which in enforcer language, Kestrel was learning, meant *open revolt*.

Piece by piece. Faction by faction. Not peace — Kestrel harbored no illusions; Ashvale would spend a decade digesting tonight — but disarmament. Everyone had come to the auction holding their intentions close as coins, and the beasts had made fists of all their hands, and now the coins lay on the floor where everyone could count them, and it turned out that when a city's secrets are spent, its wars go unpaid.

The animals were leaving, too, now that the work was done. Rats streaming out through the wall-cracks, hawks spiraling up through the broken skylight, the bear ambling out with the ceremonial centerpiece (nobody pursued it). At the door, the fox paused and looked back at Kestrel one last time.

"*Keep speaking,*" it said. "*Even when it costs. Especially then.*"

And then it was gone, and the hall was full of humans again, blinking in the sudden ordinariness of one another.

Which was when Kestrel saw Sorrel crossing the floor toward him, and saw — the way the beasts had taught him to see, in one strange borrowed hour — what walked beside her.

It was not visible, exactly. It was more like heat-shimmer, or the shape you glimpse in smoke. Something vast and patient and *hungry*, wearing his mentor like a coat, and rising in her now with unmistakable intent, because the mantle needed a vessel and its current one was dying and its chosen heir had just done the unthinkable thing: he had made himself worthless to it. No ambition. No secret wants. Nothing for a war-god to grip.

Except — Kestrel felt his stomach drop — it did not need him willing anymore. Not if it could take him mid-ritual. Not if Sorrel forced the transfer while his guard was down, here, now, with the wards of her own guild hall still half-alive around them.

"Sorrel," he said carefully. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"What I'm about to do," she said, arriving at the foot of the dais, drawing something small and folded from her sleeve — a page, torn long ago from a book, yellowed and covered in ink older than either of them, "is finish what I started a hundred years ago, dear boy, before this room finds its courage again."

Behind her, Maren was already moving. Across the hall, Corvus Hale had gone very still, doing spymaster arithmetic. And the scrap of paper in Sorrel's ancient hand began, gently, to glow.

"Kestrel," Sorrel said, and for one unguarded instant her voice was not a mentor's or an archivist's or a villain's, but simply an exhausted old woman's. "Hold still. This will only take the rest of your life."

The page unfolded. The light went up.
