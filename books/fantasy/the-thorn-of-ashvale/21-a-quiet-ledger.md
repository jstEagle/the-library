## 21. A Quiet Ledger

The morning light came through the guild's tall windows the way it never had before — unfiltered by caged songs, unbent by bottled storms, falling in plain rectangles on a floor that had finally been swept of its secrets.

Kestrel Galloway stood where the auction block used to be and tried to remember what it had felt like to be sold.

It had been six weeks since the night the book burned. Six weeks since he had spoken the whole ugly prophecy aloud to a hall full of liars and watched the words die in the air like moths hitting a lamp. Six weeks since the Thorn-power had poured into him and he had carried it out to the old battlefield at Ashvale's edge and let it soak into the ground like rain into thirsty earth. Six weeks since the beasts had gone quiet in his ears — all of them, all at once, like a city holding its breath.

He still caught himself listening for them. That was the strange part. He would stand at the archive door in the early morning, before the clerks arrived, and tilt his head toward the gutters out of pure habit, waiting for the rats to tell him the weather, or the pigeons to complain about the baker on Cordwainer Street who had started putting sawdust in his loaves again.

Nothing came. Just wind, and cart wheels, and the ordinary noise of a city that did not know it had been saved.

"Talking to yourself again?" Maren Oduya said from behind him, balancing a crate of ledgers on one hip like it weighed nothing, which for her it nearly didn't.

"I was listening," Kestrel said.

"To what?"

"That's the problem. Nothing." He turned and took the other end of the crate. "I keep expecting the walls to have opinions."

"The walls do have opinions. Sorrel wrote half of hers down." Maren set her end on the long table that had replaced the auction block, and together they began unpacking. "Which reminds me. The north reading room is done. You can open Sorrel Vane's records to the public whenever you stop finding reasons not to."

"You found those reasons faster than I did last week."

"Yes, well, I'm a professional procrastinator. I had training." She brushed dust off her sleeves. "The rebels trained me to delay anything that might get us killed. Opening a dead woman's private papers to an entire city qualifies."

"She's not dead."

Maren looked at him over the crate. "She's in a cell under the palace, Kestrel. Waiting for a trial that will take a year and end however the judges' breakfasts went. For practical purposes—"

"For practical purposes she's the most dangerous person in Ashvale, and her records are the most dangerous objects in this building, and I want to read every page twice before some alderman's nephew reads them once and decides to build a statue to her." He said it plainly, without hedging, without the old clerk's trick of burying his real opinion under three layers of polite agreement. It still surprised him how easy it was getting. Saying what he meant. Like a muscle he'd never known he had, gone soft from disuse, waking up sore and then, gradually, strong.

Maren noticed too. He could tell because she had stopped doing the thing she used to do — watching his mouth when he spoke, as if the true sentence were hiding behind the spoken one. These days she just listened.

"All right," she said. "Twice through the records first. But we open the doors by month's end. The city needs to see inside this place. They need to see that the Guild of Impossible Things is now just... things."

"Just things," Kestrel agreed. "Honest, catalogued, boring things."

"Boring," Maren said, with the reverence other people reserved for miracles.

* * *

The inventory took most of the morning, and it was the strangest inventory of Kestrel's life, because everything in it was possible.

Crate forty-one: forty-three volumes of guild correspondence, 1121 to 1189, water-damaged, partially legible, value historical. Crate forty-two: the bottled storm — except when they'd opened the vault after the burning, the storm had already gone. Just a bottle now, and a smell of rain, and a scrap of label in Sorrel's handwriting: *acquired from a farmer who could no longer afford to be angry.* Kestrel kept the bottle on his desk. He liked having it there. An empty vessel that had once held something enormous. There was a lesson in it he wasn't sure he wanted to examine too closely.

Crate forty-three was harder. The caged songs.

There had been eleven of them once, little brass cages with nothing visible inside, each humming faintly with a song someone had caught and kept — a lullaby, a marching tune, a grief so heavy it had to be carried in wire. During the flight through the vaults, on the night everything burned, Kestrel had opened nine. The songs had poured out into the dark and followed him through the guild like a choir with nowhere to go, and then they'd scattered out the broken doors into the city, and gods knew where they'd lodged themselves. In chimneys. In wedding bands. In one unlucky fiddler on Tallow Row who had suddenly become, by all reports, magnificent.

Two cages remained. One held the lullaby — they'd decided to keep that one, unopened, as a memorial to the guild's strange history. The other held the marching tune, and nobody knew what to do with it.

"We should let it go," Maren said, standing over the cage with her arms crossed. "It's a war song, Kestrel. We're trying to end a war."

"It's also somebody's grandfather's favorite. There's a card. His granddaughter donated it after he died. She visits it sometimes."

Maren was quiet a moment. "Does she?"

"Third Thursday of every month. She sits in the reading room and asks if she can hear it, and we tell her the same thing we tell everyone: the collection isn't on display yet, but soon." He picked up the cage. It hummed against his palms, faint and stubborn, drums and fifes and men's voices pitched to carry across a field no one alive remembered. "I spent a hundred years' worth of other people's secrets learning that you don't get to decide what a thing means to someone else. Even a war song. Especially a war song."

"So we keep it."

"We keep it. Catalogued honestly. *One (1) marching tune, provenance: donated in grief, contents: war.* Let the future make up its own mind about it."

Maren grinned at him. "You're getting frighteningly good at this honesty business."

"I had a very dramatic teacher."

"You had a very dramatic year."

"That too."

* * *

The afternoon brought the aldermen.

They arrived in a delegation of five, robed and solemn, with the particular gravity of men who had come to inspect a miracle and were disappointed to find only shelves. Alderman Prue led them — a broad, red-faced woman with ink stains on three fingers and the eyes of someone who had read every budget the city had ever produced and trusted none of them.

"Master Galloway." She said his name carefully, the way people said the names of men who had been in the broadsheets. THE CLERK WHO SPOKE, the headline had run, which Kestrel had found both accurate and mortifying. "The Council wishes to confirm the transfer of the guild's holdings to civic administration."

"The holdings are transferred. The building is transferred. The debts—" he pulled the ledger toward himself, "—are being sorted. Which reminds me. The Duke of Marrowgate owes the guild four hundred crowns from a wager placed in 1147 on the sex of a calf. The calf, for the record, was neither, which the duke has always maintained was cheating."

Alderman Prue blinked. One of the younger aldermen made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"The debt stands," Kestrel continued. "We'll pursue it through proper channels. But I want the Council to understand what they're taking on. This archive isn't just paper. For a century, this guild ran on the principle that knowledge was leverage. Everything here was collected so it could be *used* — against rivals, against kings, against the truth itself. Sorrel Vane built a machine for manufacturing inevitabilities. What we're handing you is the machine, dismantled. Some of the parts are still sharp."

"And your recommendation?" Prue asked.

"Open access. All of it. Every citizen of Ashvale gets to read what was read about them. Yes, it will cause scandals. Yes, three noble families will discover things about their grandmothers that will ruin at least two dinner parties apiece. Do it anyway." He heard his own voice come out steady and clear and thought, not for the first time, *so this is what it's like.* "Secrets rot. Shared, they compost."

Prue stared at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, barking laugh, entirely unlike the polished chuckles aldermen kept for ceremonies. "Compost. Fine. Motion carries. Gods help us all." She paused at the door on the way out. "For what it's worth, Master Galloway — my grandmother appears in these records. Page two hundred and six. I've read the entry. I'd rather know than not."

"That's the whole argument," Kestrel said, "in one sentence."

After they'd gone, Maren appeared in the doorway with two cups of chicory and the expression of a woman who had watched the whole meeting from the hall while pretending to sort pamphlets.

"'Three noble families,'" she quoted. "'Two dinner parties apiece.' You've been saving that."

"I've been *composing* it. For weeks. In my head. Like a normal person, with a normal head, instead of consulting rats about rhetorical strategy." He took the cup. "Do you know what I miss? Honestly. Actually miss?"

"The rats."

"How did you—"

"Because I miss them too, and I never even had them." Maren sat on the corner of the table. "Six weeks ago I could walk through the Warrens and the mice would scatter before me like I was famous. Now they just... look at me. With their little faces. Judging me silently, the way mice do, but without the commentary."

"They were never commenting on you specifically. Mostly they commented on bread quality and which guards took bribes."

"See, that's exactly the kind of intelligence network this city is poorer for losing."

Kestrel snorted into his chicory. Then, because the moment seemed to invite it, he said the true thing underneath the joke: "I don't miss the information. I miss being *known*. Somewhere in this city, on any given day, something small and alive understood me completely. Now I'm fluent in exactly one language, with one species, and half of them are aldermen."

Maren wrapped both hands around her cup. "And the other half?"

"Is you. And the stray dog situation, which we will discuss when there's more to discuss."

"There's *more to discuss?*"

"There might be." He set the cup down. "But not today. Today we finish the east wing cataloguing, because if I leave it to tomorrow, tomorrow will become Thursday, and Thursday is when the granddaughter visits, and I refuse to have boxes stacked in front of the marching tune on the third Thursday of any month ever again."

* * *

Evening settled over the archive like dust settling over a clean floor — slowly, gently, and then all at once.

Kestrel worked late, as he always did now, though the work had changed shape entirely. Once, inventory had been espionage: noting which items moved, which patrons lied, which shelf had been disturbed by hands too fine for their gloves. Now inventory was just inventory. A thing was where it was, or it wasn't, and if it wasn't, you wrote down that it wasn't, and someone eventually brought it back, because the city had decided — tentatively, suspiciously, the way a stray decides about a hand — that this place told the truth.

He was rebinding a volume of harvest records from 1103 when he found it: a pressed flower between pages 88 and 89, flat as a coin, brown as old blood. Someone had put it there a century ago and no one had ever found it, because no one had ever had reason to read harvest records cover to cover.

He sat with it a while. A flower, kept. By whom? A clerk like him, probably. Someone who spent their days among numbers and hid one soft thing in the middle of them, trusting that someday, somehow, another pair of hands would find it and understand.

He pressed it back between the pages, closed the covers, and wrote in the catalogue, in his plainest hand: *Contains one (1) flower, pressed, keeper unknown, meaning unknown, presumed love.*

Maren found him there an hour later, asleep at the table with his cheek on the finished binding, the lamp burned low. She didn't wake him. She'd learned that much about him in the Warrens — that Kestrel Galloway slept like a man paying off a debt, deeply and without negotiation — and besides, the east wing could wait until morning.

But as she draped her coat over his shoulders, she paused. Because from somewhere outside — the gutter, maybe, or the eaves, or the dark space under the steps where strays sheltered from the river wind — came a single sound. Small. Distinct. Almost word-shaped.

Almost like a voice, saying almost like a name.

Maren stood very still in the empty archive, listening to nothing, and decided — wisely, professionally, in the manner of a woman who had survived three governments — not to mention it to anyone. Yet.
