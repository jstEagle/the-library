## 2. Proclamation at the Crossroads

The frost had come early to Hollowmere that year, silvering the pump handles and stiffening the mud in the market square into ridges hard as old scar tissue. Marguerite Hartwell noticed such things the way other men noticed weather—professionally. Cold made bones brittle. Cold made them sing differently under the knife, a higher, thinner note, like a woman's voice through a shut door.

He was carrying a crate of finished carvings to the crossroads stall when he heard the horses.

Not one horse. Many, and riding in formation, which no one did in Hollowmere unless they were tax collectors or worse. He set the crate down on Odile Prunier's doorstep without knocking—the old woman would grumble about it later, which was her way of saying thank you—and turned toward the sound with his heart already climbing into his throat.

Six riders came up the salt road from the lowlands, cloaked in crown grey, their livery stitched with the sunburst-and-scales of the Royal Inquisition. Their breath steamed in columns. Their boots were wrong for the mountains—heavy cavalry leather, built for parade grounds, not switchbacks—and Marguerite found himself cataloguing this the way he catalogued everything: automatically, uselessly, the habit of a man who survived by noticing what others overlooked.

They stopped at the crossroads where four ways met: down to the salt flats, up to the Thornfell passes, east to the shrine ruins, west to nothing much at all. The lead rider dismounted. He was young, younger than Marguerite, with a face like a well-scrubbed kitchen and eyes that never moved past anything—they moved *through* things, scanning, weighing, dismissing.

He carried a hammer, a fistful of iron nails, and a rolled proclamation sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

Marguerite did not run. Running was the first mistake. Instead he drifted to the edge of the gathering crowd the way smoke drifts along a ceiling, unremarkable, one more craftsman come to gawk. Around him the square filled: millers with flour still on their sleeves, the cooper's boys, Odile Prunier wrapped in three shawls despite the hour, her sharp old face tilted up like a bird's.

The rider unrolled the decree against the crossroads post and nailed it up with four blows of the hammer, each one ringing out over the square like a small bell.

*Like a bell,* Marguerite thought, and something cold walked down his spine.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third," the rider read, in the flat carrying voice of a man who had said these words in forty towns and would say them in forty more, "all practitioners of the forbidden art of ossomancy—all those who hear the voices of the dead through bone, who traffic with spirits, who carry the taint of the old craft—are hereby commanded to present themselves for sanctification within thirty days' turning."

The word *sanctification* landed on the crowd like a dropped stone lands on water. Ripples everywhere; nobody spoke.

"Sanctification is an act of mercy," the rider continued. "Those who surrender willingly will be cleansed of their affliction under the Church's care. Those who conceal themselves—" he let the pause stretch, letting every mother in the square imagine her own son's face "—will be found. The Crown has been given instruments that do not err. Harborers of hearers will be judged as hearers. Rewards are offered for information leading to discovery."

He stepped back. The wind took the corner of the parchment and snapped it, once, twice, like a thing trying to get free.

Marguerite stood very still among the millers and coopers and did not look at anyone, because looking was the second mistake. But he heard everything. He heard Odile's breath go shallow. He heard the cooper's youngest boy ask his father what ossomancy meant and get a hand clamped over his mouth before the second syllable. He heard, beneath all of it, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the dead of Hollowmere murmuring in the stones of the square itself—the old foundation bones, the femurs and ribs ground into the mortar two centuries back when the town was raised, whispering the way they always whispered:

*They came before. They will come again. Hide, hide, hide.*

The dead were not subtle. They were just easy to ignore, if you tried.

---

He warned her that same evening, because she was the only one in Hollowmere besides himself who needed warning.

Maren Collier lived at the end of Tannery Row, in a house that smelled perpetually of oak bark and lye, and everyone knew her for a widow who talked to herself while she worked the hides. What they didn't know—or pretended not to know, in the forgiving way of small towns that preferred their mysteries domestic—was that Maren wasn't talking to herself. She was talking to her husband, Colm, dead eight years now, whose knucklebones she kept in a pouch at her throat and touched the way other widows touched wedding rings.

She was a hearer. A small one, a quiet one, the kind who never carved, never listened for strangers, never did anything but keep her dead husband's voice company through the long winters. Marguerite knew because he'd sold her a carved keepsake three years ago—a little bone wren, wing folded, beak open mid-song—and when her fingers closed around it she had gone white as tallow and looked at him with the naked relief of a drowning woman shown a rope.

Neither of them had ever spoken of it since. That was the arrangement. That was how it worked in Hollowmere: silence, and small kindnesses, and everyone pretending the water they all swam in wasn't there.

He knocked twice, the pattern they'd agreed on years ago—two quick, one slow, never used except when it mattered—and she opened the door with lye-stained hands and knew immediately, the way hearers always know.

"No," she said.

"You need to hear me, Maren."

"They're not coming here. This is Hollowmere. We're nobody. We're a day's ride from anywhere worth taxing." But she was already pulling him inside, already glancing up and down the row, and her hands were shaking, which meant she believed him even as she argued.

"The bell," he said quietly, once the door was shut. "There's talk of a relic. Something the Inquisition carries that rings when one of us is near. I don't know if it's true. I don't know how far it reaches. But Maren—if it's true, then hiding isn't enough anymore. Being quiet isn't enough." He made himself say the rest. "You should go down to your sister's in Vell. Tonight. Leave the hides, leave the house. Take Colm's bones and go."

She stared at him. In the lamplight her face was all planes and stubbornness, a woman shaped by decades of work that ruined soft things. "And say what? That I'm leaving my home because voices told me to? Because a carver knocked on my door with a feeling?"

"Not a feeling. The riders came today. You saw the decree."

"I saw a piece of paper." Her voice rose, cracked, dropped. "I've seen pieces of paper before, Marguerite. Twenty years ago there was a purge in the lowlands and we all sat tight and it passed over us like weather. Silence keeps us safe. Quiet and small and useful—that's what keeps us safe. You taught me that."

"I never taught you that."

"You lived it. All of us live it." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, against the pouch that hung beneath her smock, and for a moment her anger faltered into something rawer. "Colm is here. Everything of him that's left is here. And you want me to run into the winter with a pouch of knucklebones and pray the roads are kind?" She shook her head. "Thirty days. The paper says thirty days. I'll think on it. That's fair. That's time enough."

It wasn't time enough. Every instinct he owned, every voice in every bone he'd ever touched, screamed that it wasn't nearly time enough. But he also knew Maren Collier, and he knew that dragging a stubborn woman out of her own house was a way to make certain she dug in like a tick.

So he did the thing he was best at, the thing that had kept him alive and invisible for thirty-one years.

He left it alone.

"I'll send word through the stall," he said. "If anything changes. If you hear anything, anything at all—"

"I know the knock," she said, softer now, and touched his sleeve at the wrist, brief and dry and warm. "You're a good boy, Marguerite Hartwell. Your mother would be proud, wherever she's listening from."

His mother. Dead before he could walk, before he could carve, before he knew why the bones sang to him and to no one else. He managed a smile that cost him something, and went home through the freezing dark, and lay awake listening to the house timbers creak, and told himself the lie he had built his whole life upon, plank by careful plank:

*Small kindnesses. Quiet warnings. No names given, no patterns made. If you never draw the lightning, it cannot find you.*

Somewhere below the floorboards, in the earth, in the packed bones of the mountain itself, the dead murmured their one-word chorus, and he pulled the blanket over his head like a child.

*Hide, hide, hide.*

---

They came back in nine days.

Marguerite was at his workbench, hollowing the femur of a ferryman named Ansel Droc into a keepsake for the man's daughter—a tiny boat, he'd decided, with a sail no bigger than a thumbnail—when the screaming started.

He knew the voice before he knew the words. That was the worst of it. Grief comes in many registers but terror has only one, and he had heard it before, in other towns, in other years, always from a distance, always muffled by walls and prudence. This time it came up Tannery Row clear as a bell across a lake, and it was Maren.

He was in the street before he remembered deciding to move.

They had her in the square. Four of them this time, plus the young officer with the scrubbed face, and Maren between them with her arms pinned and her gray hair torn loose from its braid, and she was not fighting them so much as she was *reaching*—reaching back toward her house, toward the pouch they'd already torn from her neck, toward the life she was being hauled out of by the roots.

The Chirurgeon's Bell hung from the officer's saddle in a cage of black iron, and it was swinging gently, and it was silent, its work done.

"You don't understand," Maren was saying, over and over, the words shredding. "I'm not—I never hurt anyone, I only listen, I only ever listened to *him*, he's been dead eight years and I only wanted—"

"Madam, you will have opportunity to explain everything during sanctification." The officer's voice was gentle. That was somehow the most horrifying part. He sounded like a man comforting a child after a fall. "The Church is merciful."

"You're lying." Her eyes swept the square, wild, hunting for one face, one friend, one hand that might reach out—and Marguerite watched, frozen at the mouth of Tannery Row, doing the arithmetic of cowardice at terrible speed. Forty people in that square. Not one of them moving. The cooper staring at his boots. Odile Prunier's jaw set like a clenched fist, her hands white-knuckled on her shawl, old and frail and smart enough to know that moving would only add a second name to the ledger.

Then Maren's eyes found him.

Across the square, across forty motionless bodies, she found him the way a drowning woman finds a rope, and this time there was no relief in it at all.

"*Marguerite!*"

His name cracked across the square like the officer's hammer on the nails.

"*Marguerite, help me! You told me—you KNEW, you came to my door, you TOLD me—*" The soldiers tightened their grip and she twisted, shrieking now, no dignity left, none asked for. "*Help me! Please! Somebody—Marguerite!*"

He didn't move.

That was the fact of it, the fact he would carry in his chest for the rest of his life alongside everything else he carried: he stood at the edge of the square with his hands at his sides and he did not move, because moving was death, because one name given freely was worth less than a name taken later under the bell, because every survival instinct he had honed over three decades locked his knees and glued his feet and kept his face arranged in the proper mask of a shocked bystander, a good craftsman, a stranger.

A stranger. To a woman he had known for fifteen years. To a woman whose husband's knucklebones he had polished smooth with his own thumb because she couldn't bear the rough edges against her skin.

They dragged her past the crossroads post, past the proclamation with its blood-colored seal snapping in the wind, toward the horses. And because the world is cruel in the specific ways it is cruel, the soldiers had to pause there, adjusting their grip, and Maren got one last look back—not at the officer, not at the neighbors, but at the post itself, at the decree, and she laughed. It was the worst sound Marguerite had ever heard, worse than any voice the dead had ever put in his ears.

"Sanctification," she said to the paper. "Mercy." Then they lifted her onto the horse sideways, like cargo, and the officer mounted, and the column turned down the salt road toward the lowlands, and the hoofbeats faded, and the square stayed silent for exactly as long as it takes a held breath to become unbearable.

Then everyone began talking at once, loudly, about the weather, about the price of salt, about anything, about nothing, forty voices building a wall of noise to hide behind, and Marguerite stood in the middle of it with Maren's scream lodged in his skull like a splinter driven under the nail.

Odile Prunier appeared at his elbow. She didn't look at him. She looked at the empty road.

"She said your name," the old woman murmured, barely a breath.

"She said a lot of things."

"Mm." Odile's eyes flicked to him, one flash of them, sharp as a blade's edge and gone again. "Best hope the paper doesn't learn to read, boy."

She shuffled off toward her house. Marguerite remained in the square until his legs obeyed him again, then walked home the long way, past the tannery, where the door stood open and the lye-smell spilled out into the cold like a wound bleeding.

Inside, on the table, someone had left the little bone wren. The soldiers hadn't wanted it—it was only a trinket, only a carving—but they had emptied the house looking for Colm's knucklebones, and the pouch was gone, and the wren sat alone on the bare wood with its beak open mid-song, singing to a room with no one left to hear it.

Marguerite picked it up. And because he was what he was, because he had never learned how to touch bone without listening, the wren spoke to him in Maren's voice, in the memory her hands had pressed into it over three years of anxious holding:

*He said I'd be safe. He said quiet and small and useful. He promised me.*

He stood in the ruined tannery holding a songbird made of a stranger's bone, and the belief he had built his entire life on—the plank-by-plank cathedral of *silence keeps us safe*—did not collapse. Cathedrals don't collapse all at once. But somewhere deep in the structure, a keystone shifted, and he felt it go, felt the first hairline crack run through the architecture of his caution, and understood with perfect clarity that it would never hold weight the same way again.

Because he had warned her. He had done everything right. He had knocked the secret knock, spoken the careful words, offered the escape, respected her choice, protected his own name—and she had been taken anyway, screaming his name into a square full of neighbors who suddenly could not hear.

Silence hadn't saved her. Silence had only guaranteed that when the end came, she would die knowing exactly who had failed to speak.

That night he did not sleep. Near dawn, he took the bone wren to his workbench, and instead of putting it in the drawer with the finished pieces, he picked up his finest knife and began, very slowly, to carve the wings open.

Let it fly, he thought. Let it carry what it knows.

Outside, far down the salt road, beyond the hills, beyond the lowlands, the crown was mustering something larger than six riders. He couldn't have said how he knew. He only knew that the dead of Hollowmere had changed their tune overnight—the old murmur of *hide, hide, hide* giving way to something new, something older, rising up out of the mountain's deep bones like water rising in a mine.

They weren't saying *hide* anymore.

They were saying a name. One word, repeated in ten thousand buried voices, growing louder with every hour, rolling down from the high passes where the snow had begun to fall early and thick:

*Caerlum. Caerlum. Caerlum.*
