## 3. The Relic That Listens Back

The bell did not look like an instrument of death.

That was the first thing Seraphine Voss had learned about it, seven years ago, when the Chirurgeon's Order had pressed it into her gloved hands in a cellar beneath the capital and told her, *you will be its voice now*. It was small—no larger than a fist—and cast from a dull greenish bronze that seemed to drink lamplight rather than return it. Its surface was worked all over with anatomical reliefs: ribs like cage bars, a spine for a handle, tiny skulls where clappers should have been. When she carried it at her hip in its lead-lined case, it was silent. It was always silent.

Except near them.

She stood now in the frozen muster-yard of Fort Andrel, holding the bell aloft before forty soldiers who had been marched out of their barracks before dawn and did not trouble to hide their resentment. Breath steamed. Frost furred the flagstones. Somewhere behind the fort's eastern wall, a cook was shouting about porridge, and the ordinariness of it made Voss's task feel both absurd and necessary.

"Look at it," she said, turning so the grey morning light caught the bronze. "Memorize it. If you see one like it on another officer's belt, you will know that officer carries the crown's authority over life and death in matters of the gift. There is no appeal. There is no trial. The bell is the trial."

A sergeant near the front—a broad man with a beard full of ice, named Harkaway according to the roster—shifted his weight and muttered something to the soldier beside him. Not quietly enough.

"Say it aloud, Sergeant."

Harkaway's jaw worked. He was one of those men whose honesty lived in his face whether he wanted it there or not. "Begging pardon, Inquisitor. Only—it's a lump of metal, isn't it? We've been told lumps of metal do a lot of things. Half of it's theater, if you ask me. Scare the peasants."

Voss had heard this argument before, in a dozen garrisons, from a dozen honest men. She did not blame them. She had been one of them once—a border captain's daughter who had watched a bone-hearer talk a dying village into burning its own grain stores because *the dead were hungry*, who had seen what faith in whispers could do to people too frightened to think. Theater, yes. But theater killed.

"The bell is not theater," she said. "And I will prove it this morning, though I would rather not have to."

She let the silence sit. Forty men looked anywhere but at her.

"Three days ago," she continued, "a woman in the town of Millbeck was reported by her neighbors. A tanner's widow, name of Odile Ferran. She kept to herself. She sold remedies—not poisons, remedies, and good ones, which was the first sign. And twice last winter, she was heard speaking to her late husband at his graveside. Not mourning him." Voss met their eyes, one by one. "*Answering* him. The report says she paused after each question as if listening. The report says she smiled at what she heard."

Someone in the ranks crossed himself with the wrong hand—the old mountain sign, warding against the under-world. Voss pretended not to notice.

"This morning, Widow Ferran is in the guardhouse of this fort. Brought in overnight. And this morning, Sergeant, you will hear the bell work, or you will not. Either way, you'll stop calling it a lump of metal."

She lowered the bell into its case without letting it touch air, snapped the lead shut, and felt—as she always felt—the faint relief of the silence returning. The men were dismissed. They scattered gratefully toward the mess hall, and Harkaway lingered just long enough to give her a look she had catalogued many times: not hatred, exactly. The wariness of a man watching someone walk a ledge he'd have to walk someday too.

*Good,* Voss thought. *Be wary. Wary men live longer than certain ones.*

It was only later, walking the cold corridor toward the guardhouse, that she admitted the thing she admitted to no one: she had not slept well in three nights, and it was not the widow keeping her awake.

It was the tone.

* *

Odile Ferran did not weep, and that unsettled Voss more than weeping would have.

They put her in the interrogation room at the end of the cell block—a stone chamber with one high window, a table bolted to the floor, two chairs. The widow sat in one of them as if she had chosen it herself. She was perhaps fifty-five, thick-bodied, with a tanner's scarred hands folded on the table like two sleeping animals. Her hair was going grey at the temples and she wore it plainly pinned. Her eyes were dry and dark and very steady.

"You may sit," she said, before Voss spoke. "I know why you're here. The dead told me you'd come."

Voss did not sit. She set the bell case on the table between them and kept her hand flat upon it.

"They told you."

"Not in words." The widow's mouth twitched, almost a smile. "You've never spoken with them, so I don't expect you to understand how it works. It's like—remembering something you never knew. You wake up knowing the frost will come early. You know the shape of a thing coming up the road. Last night I woke knowing there was a woman with a bell, and that her boots hurt her feet, and that she was sorry about something." The dark eyes flicked down, briefly, to Voss's left boot—the one with the mended seam. Then back up. "Are you sorry about something, Inquisitor?"

"The neighbors say you speak with your dead husband."

"I speak. He doesn't answer. That's not how it works either—he's not sitting behind some curtain waiting to chat. What comes through is older than him. Older than anybody's husband." She said it patiently, the way one explains rain to a child. "But they knew him. That's enough. When he died I couldn't bear the quiet, and something in me reached down, and something answered. I didn't ask for it. I'd thank you to write that down."

"It's in the file." Voss opened the case. The bell sat in its velvet cradle, drinking the light from the high window. "Do you know what this is?"

"A Chirurgeon's Bell." No fear in the widow's voice. Only a tired recognition. "My grandmother saw one used. In the Harrowing, before the amnesty. She said the gifted could hear it before it rang—like a toothache getting worse." The widow looked at the bell the way one looks at a snake one has decided not to run from. "Is that true?"

"I don't know," Voss said honestly. "I'm not gifted. I only carry it."

"That must be lonely."

Voss's hand tightened on the case lid. Of all the questions thrown at her across seven years—curses, pleas, spittle, silence—this plain sympathy was the one that landed. She closed the lid without ringing the bell. Not yet.

"Widow Ferran. If you renounce the practice—publicly, before the garrison—if you take the Rite of Severance and swear the oaths, you will be sent to a cloister in the lowlands. You will live. I am authorized to offer that, and I am offering it."

Something moved behind the widow's eyes—not hope. Calculation, maybe. Or grief.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the bell rings, and the law takes its course."

The widow nodded slowly. She unfolded her scarred hands and laid them flat, palm-down, as if steadying the table. Or the world.

"My husband has been dead four years," she said. "My sons are grown and hate me for reasons I no longer remember precisely. The cloister is a pretty word for a cell with prayers in it. And the thing that answers when I reach down—" she paused, and for the first time her composure cracked, a hairline fracture across her voice, "—the thing that answers has been *screaming* for three nights, Inquisitor. All of them. Every voice I've ever touched. They're saying a word over and over, and it isn't my name, and it isn't God's."

Voss went very still. "What word?"

The widow laughed—one short, terrible bark. "You wouldn't believe me. And if I said it aloud and you wrote it in your file, they'd come asking me how I knew it, and we both know how that ends." She straightened her shoulders. "No. I won't take your rite. I've spent four years being the town's ear, and every soul I listened for is screaming right now, and you want me to sew my ears shut in a lowland cell while the mountain burns. I'd rather die listening. Ring your bell."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Through the high window, snow was beginning again—fine, dry, wrong-for-the-season snow, falling early even for Thornfell's autumns. Voss thought of her orders, sealed with the privy seal, precise as surgery. She thought of the amnesty her grandmother's generation had won and her own generation had quietly revoked. She thought: *she knows the word. The same word. How does she know the word.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, the doubt she kept in the locked room at the back of her mind turned over in its sleep.

"Very well," Voss said.

* *

She rang the bell in the yard, because the demonstration required witnesses, and witnesses required a yard.

Forty soldiers formed their square again. Harkaway stood front rank, arms crossed against the cold. Two guards brought the widow out; she walked between them upright, unbound, having refused the rope with a look that made even the guards embarrassed to insist. She stopped where Voss pointed, on a flat flagstone worn smooth by decades of drilled feet, and folded her hands, and waited.

Voss lifted the bell from its case.

This part she had done eleven times. Eleven rings, eleven deaths, in seven years, and each time the ritual was the same because ritual was armor: she raised the bell, she spoke the charge, she gave the condemned the final chance to speak, and then she swung the little hammer—the one shaped like a stylized femur—that hung from the bell's yoke.

"The accused, Odile Ferran of Millbeck, stands charged with the practice of bone-hearing under the Statute of Quiet Bones. The relic will judge."

She struck the bell.

It did not chime. Everyone expected a chime—the first time she'd demonstrated it, years ago, a young lieutenant had flinched at nothing, waiting for a sound that never came. What came instead was a resonance, a pressure, a note felt in the fillings of teeth and the wet of the eyes and the hollows of the chest. The soldiers shifted as it passed through them. Horses in the far stable whinnied and kicked their stalls.

And the bell's surface changed. Along the ribbed reliefs, along the cast spine and the tiny skulls, a light woke—pale gold at first, deepening to amber, crawling like sap through the bronze. When the glow reached the skulls, the note sharpened, climbing toward a frequency that made the nearest men clap their hands over their ears.

Widow Ferran stood in the center of the light and did not flinch.

"It works," she said clearly, over the rising note. "Tell them it works, Inquisitor. Tell them I heard it coming for three days, like a toothache."

"Hold still," Voss said, and hated how gentle it came out.

"No." The widow smiled at her—truly smiled, with something like kindness in it, which was the worst thing yet. "You hold still. Listen to it. Really listen. You're the only one here who can."

Voss frowned. The note climbed. And then—

There.

The flaw in the tone. The thing that had been keeping her awake.

Every ring had a voice, or so she'd privately come to think of it: the bell's fundamental note, steady as doctrine, rising when the gift was near. She knew its pitch the way she knew her own heartbeat. But this time—only this time, only since Hollowmere, only since they'd crossed into the shadow of the Thornfell massif—there was something underneath the note. A second tone, lower, almost subsonic, a groan beneath the song. As if the bell were ringing over a much larger bell buried somewhere below the earth. As if the bronze itself were answering something.

Or being answered.

The amber light stuttered. For half a heartbeat—one heartbeat—the glow guttered, dimmed, and flared a different color. Not gold. Not the doctrinal amber of detection.

Green. Deep, forge-green, the color of the bronze before it was cast.

Then it was gone, and the bell sang true, and Odile Ferran was looking at Voss with an expression of terrible confirmation, as if the widow had heard the second tone too, and knew what it meant, and pitied her for it.

"Do you hear it?" the widow asked softly. "Underneath. Do you hear what it's saying?"

"Inquisitor." Harkaway's voice, strained, from the front rank. His face had gone the color of old tallow; his hand hovered near his sword without quite touching it. "Inquisitor, make it stop."

Voss struck the silencing bar against the rim. The note collapsed. The light died. The yard rang with ordinary silence, ordinary wind, the ordinary snort of frightened horses—and forty soldiers staring at the spot where the widow stood as if expecting the ground to open.

"The relic confirms the gift," Voss announced, and her voice held. Seven years of practice; her voice always held. "Sentence is executed. Sergeant, detail of six. See it done."

They brought the block out—it traveled with the column, iron-banded oak—and the widow knelt before it without assistance. The guards were rougher than they needed to be, and she bore it the way she had borne everything, with that awful patience, and at the last she turned her head and found Voss's eyes across the yard.

"He's coming up the mountain road," she said. Conversational. Almost fond. "The carver. The one the dead have been singing to. You'll meet him soon, Inquisitor. Be kinder to him than you were to me. He's carrying something heavier than bones."

The axe fell.

The yard was very quiet afterward. Snow ticked against the flagstones. Voss dismissed the men, and they went, too quickly, and she stood alone over the body while the guards' boys fetched the bier, and she made herself look at the widow's face until the lesson was learned, whatever the lesson was.

*He's coming up the mountain road.* Prophecy, or trick, or a dying woman's spite. It belonged in the file. Everything belonged in the file.

Instead Voss stood in the falling snow and did what she had never done after ten previous executions.

She took off her glove, knelt, and pressed her bare hand to the widow's brow, already cooling.

"For what it's worth," she said, to no one, to the dead woman, to whatever listened underneath the world, "I'm sorry about something."

The bell, in its case at her hip, gave no answer. But for a moment—just a moment, just once, and never again however many times she checked—Voss would have sworn she felt it leaning toward the north.

Toward the mountains.

Toward Caerlum.

That evening, a courier rode in through the gate with sealed orders from the capital: the expedition was to move within the week. Their guide would be arranged locally. Their destination was not to be discussed outside the command tent.

Their destination was Caerlum.

Voss broke the wax, read the orders twice, and found herself thinking of a second tone beneath a first, a green light beneath an amber one, and a widow who had died smiling because she knew something Voss did not.

She packed the bell herself, wrapping it in wool, and caught herself whispering to it—just once, absurdly, before she caught the habit and killed it:

"What are you listening to?"
