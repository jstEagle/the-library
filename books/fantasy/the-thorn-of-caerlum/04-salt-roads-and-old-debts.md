## 4. Salt Roads and Old Debts

The summons came the way all of Brannoc's summons came: a child at the door, breathless, with a strip of knotted cord in his fist.

Three knots for *come now*. A fourth, red-dyed, for *bring your knives*. Marguerite turned the cord over twice in his fingers while the boy fidgeted on his step, snow gathering in the hollows of his boots, and felt the familiar cold weight settle behind his ribs that had nothing to do with the weather.

"Where's she sitting?" he asked.

"The Drowned Eel. Back room."

Of course. The back room of the Drowned Eel was where Brannoc conducted every conversation she didn't want the walls to hear, and in Hollowmere the walls heard everything. Half the town's houses were mortared with smuggled salt, their beams bought with smuggled tin, their cellars stacked with smuggled brandy wrapped in sheepskins marked as wool. Thornfell lived off what the crown taxed and the clans moved, and everyone from the miller to the midwife knew it and said nothing, because saying nothing was the only law Hollowmere had ever kept well.

Marguerite pulled on his coat, checked the small blade at his hip out of habit more than fear, and followed the boy down into the blue dusk.

* * *

The walk took him past his own shopfront, dark now, the shutters latched against the wind coming down off Caerlum. Through the gap he could see the day's work still laid out on his bench: a length of femur, honey-colored and fine-grained, half-carved into a keepsake comb for a woman who had died last month with no one to mourn her but him. He'd promised her he'd finish it before the deep snows. He'd promised her other things too, things she'd whispered into the bone while his chisel sang through it — where her real will was hidden, which of her nephews had poisoned her husband twenty years ago, why she'd never once slept with a lamp lit. The dead trusted him because he listened without judgment, and they told him everything, and everything sat in him like stones in a riverbed, worn smooth but never gone.

Tonight the bones in his satchel were quiet. They usually went quiet when he walked toward Brannoc, as if even the dead respected the old woman's privacy. Or feared her. With Brannoc it had always been hard to tell the difference.

The Drowned Eel smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of ale that had been watered twice. He nodded to Sera Tallow at the bar — she nodded back without asking anything, which was its own kind of greeting — and pushed through the curtain into the back room.

Brannoc sat exactly where she always sat: in the chair nearest the fire, so that anyone entering had to come to her with the light behind her and their own face lit up like an offering. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a doorframe left standing after the house burned around it, and her hands — scarred, knuckle-swollen, steady as bedrock — lay folded on the table beside a single sheet of paper.

Two others shared the room. Old Wren, the clan's quartermaster, grey-bearded and gray-eyed, occupying the corner with the sightline to both door and window because Old Wren had never in forty years occupied any space he couldn't defend. And a stranger.

The stranger was young, well-fed, and dressed too finely for Hollowmere in a way calculated not to look too fine — good cloth, muted colors, boots that had never carried anything heavier than a ledger. His hair was cropped short in the style favored by clerks and soldiers alike, and when he looked up at Marguerite, there was something in his face that wanted very badly to be unremarkable and wasn't managing it.

"Sit," Brannoc said.

Marguerite sat. He did not look at the paper. Looking at things you weren't given was how you got people killed, and he'd spent thirty years learning what not to look at.

"You've heard the proclamation," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question. Everyone in Hollowmere had heard the proclamation; the crossroads post wore it like a wound, three days nailed up now, the ink bleeding in the wet. *All practitioners of the bone-hearing art shall surrender themselves to crown sanctification.* Sanctification. Such a clean word for whatever happened to people after the witch-hunters' wagons rolled them away.

"I've heard it," Marguerite said.

"And yet here you sit. Not sanctified."

"I'm a carver. Keepsakes and reliquaries. The priest at the chapel buys incense from me." He kept his voice flat, bored, the voice of a craftsman discussing grain directions. "I don't hear anything. I just make pretty things for grieving people."

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes were the color of thaw-water and missed nothing, and Marguerite had the sudden, vertiginous certainty that she had known what he was since the night she'd pulled six-year-old him out of a refugee cart on the Thornfell road, shivering and silent, clutching a dead woman's fingerbone like a talisman. She had never once said it aloud. Neither had he. That was the covenant between them, older than any paper on this table.

"Wren," she said.

Old Wren leaned forward. "Paying work," he rasped. "Big paying work. A party wants over the passes to Caerlum before winter seals the high road. Nine clients, plus their gear. Guides, mules, safe conduct through clan territory, no questions asked about cargo or purpose. They're offering—" he named a figure, and Marguerite's stomach dropped, because the figure was enough to buy the whole clan out of the salt trade for two years. Enough to buy every family in Hollowmere through a famine winter with meat on the table.

"That much money," Marguerite said carefully, "buys a great many silences."

"It does," Brannoc agreed.

"And silence is the one thing we can't afford to sell wrong this season." He let himself look at the stranger now, briefly, deliberately. "Not with hunters in the valley."

The stranger smiled the way a man smiles when he's been told he'll be discussed and has decided to enjoy it. "Inquisitor Voss is in Lowmark," he offered. "That's a week's ride south. Whatever she's doing there, it isn't climbing Thornfell in November."

"No," Marguerite agreed pleasantly. "She'd send someone cheaper to climb it for her."

Something flickered across the stranger's face — quick, quickly mastered. Wren grunted, and it might have been a laugh.

"Enough fencing," Brannoc said. She slid the paper across the table with one finger, turning it as she did, and Marguerite understood the gesture a heartbeat before the wax seal came into view.

He should have looked away. Every instinct bred into him by thirty years of survival said *look away*. Instead he looked.

The crest pressed into the broken seal was a tower beneath a rising sun. Gilt-edged, official, unmistakable.

The crown.

The room went very quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the curtain, Sera Tallow laughed at something a customer said, bright and oblivious, and the sound seemed to belong to another world.

"You knew," Marguerite said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "You knew when you sent for me."

"I suspected when the inquiry came through the Lowmark brokers. I knew when I saw the seal." Brannoc folded her hands again. "And I sent for you anyway, because I need you to hear the rest of it with your own ears before you decide what kind of fool you're going to be."

"Crown money moving through smuggler channels," Marguerite said. "Crown clients wanting passage to Caerlum, of all the frozen hells in this country. Crown business that doesn't want to be crown business." He shook his head slowly. "Mother. This isn't a job. This is a hook with our whole clan for bait."

"It's rent," Brannoc said flatly. "It's grain and coal and medicine for every soul from Hollowmere to Graywater, paid in advance, half again on delivery. You think I don't know what it smells like? I've been smelling crown traps since before your father was whelped." Her jaw tightened. "But we don't get to choose what winter costs. We only choose whether we pay it. Refuse this, and the clan limps through on thin salt and thinner luck, and when the hard months come — and they're coming, boy, the birds have already gone quiet on the peaks — we watch children go hungry knowing we turned down the one purse that could have fed them."

"And take it, and guide nine strangers full of crown secrets into the mountains—"

"—and take the money, yes. And do the job clean, the way we do every job, because the clans survive on reputation the way fish survive on water." She leaned forward, and the firelight carved deep shadows under her cheekbones, making her look suddenly ancient, a monument to every hard winter Thornfell had ever survived. "You want to know the worst part? Ask me. Go on. Ask me who they want as lead guide."

Marguerite already knew. He'd known since the fourth red knot on the cord. He made himself say it anyway. "Me."

"You. Because the passes are closing early this year and there are perhaps three people alive who know the high route past the Weeping Stair, and two of them are dead, and I'm too old to spend a month above the snowline." Her voice didn't soften, but something behind it did — some old tenderness, buried deep, surfacing like a body coming up through dark water. "I raised you on these roads. I taught you the stars and the scree and which cornices lie. There's no one else I trust with my people's lives walking point on this one. That's the truth of it, ugly as it sits."

"And if the crown's purpose up there is what I think it might be?"

"Then it's still not ours to know. We carry cargo. We don't open cargo. That rule has kept us alive through three kings and one regency." She tapped the table once, a sharp knock, final as a coffin lid. "Guide them over. Keep your head down and your mouth shut and your gift locked up tighter than the vault at Caerlum itself. Come home. Spend the coin. Feed the town."

The stranger cleared his throat. "There is one condition from the client side," he said, apologetic in the way of men delivering terms they've been coached to deliver gently. "The lead guide must present himself under a working name. Nothing exotic — the client simply prefers discretion regarding local personnel. And he must submit to a brief interview tomorrow morning at the pass gate. Routine vetting."

"A brief interview," Marguerite repeated.

"With the client's security officer." The stranger produced a folded letter, sealed with the tower-and-sun. "A woman. Very thorough, I'm told."

Very thorough. Marguerite thought of the proclamation at the crossroads. Thought of Tam's sister, dragged screaming through the square three years ago in the last purge, and how the word *sanctification* had sounded then too, clean as new snow over a shallow grave.

He thought of Brannoc's hands, folded on the table. Of the grain ledgers and coal tallies, the medicine caches, the hungry children waiting inside every one of those numbers. Of the covenant neither of them ever spoke aloud, and how it cut both ways: she had kept his secret for thirty years, and now she was asking him to keep hers.

If he refused, the clan would find another guide — a worse one, someone who didn't know the high route, and the party would die in a whiteout or a crevasse or an avalanche, and the crown would come asking questions about the deaths, and questions from the crown in a purge year ended in fire. If he refused and ran, same ending, slower. If he accepted —

If he accepted, he would walk into the teeth of the witch-hunt wearing a false name, with a relic-reading security officer ten feet behind him for a hundred miles of the most haunted ground in the kingdom. Ground where the dead had lately begun to murmur. He hadn't told anyone that part. For weeks now, every bone he touched had been whispering the same word, over and over, from keepsake to keepsake, from the old battle-relics in his stock drawer to the fresh-combed hair of the newly dead:

*Caerlum. Caerlum. Caerlum.*

And underneath it, softer, stranger, a tone he'd never heard from the dead before — not grief, not warning, but something closer to dread.

They were afraid. The dead, who had passed beyond everything that could touch them, were afraid of something at Caerlum.

"Mother," he said quietly, when the stranger had finished his terms, "give us the room."

Wren raised an eyebrow. Brannoc held the stranger's gaze until the man shrugged, gathered his letter, and withdrew through the curtain. Wren followed without being told; forty years of reading rooms.

When the curtain settled, Marguerite reached across the table and took Brannoc's hand. She let him. That alone told him how frightened she was; Brannoc let no one touch her hands in front of witnesses, because they were the map of everything she'd ever done to keep her people fed.

"If I go up that mountain," he said, "and the crown's business there touches the gift — mine, or anyone's — I won't be able to stand aside. You know that. You've known it since you found me with that fingerbone. I can carry cargo with my eyes shut. I can't carry cruelty with them open."

"I know," Brannoc said.

"And if I don't come back—"

"You'll come back." Her grip closed on his, iron over paper-thin skin. "Because I am telling you to, and because I have outlived two husbands, one king, and every man who ever said the Thornfell clans were finished, and I will not outlive you. Do you understand me, Marguerite? Whatever's up there — crown secrets, old ghosts, whatever the dead have been muttering into your ear that you think I don't notice — you come home. That's the debt you owe me. That's the price of every meal I put in you."

He almost laughed. Almost wept. Did both, a little, somewhere behind his sternum where no one could see.

"One condition of mine," he said. "If I signal — anything, any knot-code from the road — you pull every soul of ours back from the pass towns and you burn the ledgers. No arguments, no heroics. The money won't matter if they turn the hunt north."

Brannoc studied him for a long moment. Outside, the wind rose against the eaves of the Drowned Eel, carrying the first dry hiss of snow off the high peaks, early this year, earlier than any winter in living memory.

"You've seen something," she said at last. Not a question either.

"I've heard something," Marguerite said. "And I don't know yet whether it means I should guide these people to Caerlum or stand in front of the vault door and dare them to move me."

For the first time in thirty years, Brannoc Delacroix looked genuinely afraid. She covered it fast — smoothed it away like a wrinkle in cloth — but he had seen it, and she knew he'd seen it, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"Then guide them," she said quietly, "and listen harder than you speak. Winter's sealing the passes in twelve days. Be at the gate by dawn tomorrow, and whatever name you give them—" she squeezed his hand one last time, hard enough to grind the old scars together, "—make sure it's one you can wear all the way home."

* * *

He worked late that night, though he couldn't have said later what drove him. The shop was cold; he didn't light the lamp. By feel, by memory, he finished the dead woman's comb — fine teeth, a thorn-and-rose motif along the spine, the little secret she'd asked for carved into the inner curve where only a thumbnail would find it — and wrapped it in linen for delivery.

Then, because his hands wouldn't stop, he took out his stock drawer and lifted the oldest piece he owned: the fingerbone. Thirty years of handling had polished it to the color of candlelight. The woman it belonged to was dust before Brannoc found him, nameless, one of the war-dead scattered down the centuries along the Thornfell roads.

He held it to his ear the way a child holds a shell to hear the sea.

*Caerlum,* whispered the bone. And then, for the first time in thirty years of listening, it said something new.

*They're coming back. Don't let them open the cage.*

Marguerite set the bone down very carefully, the way one sets down a live coal, and stood a long time in the dark shop while outside the first snow of winter began to fall on Hollowmere — early, silent, and thick as a shroud.

Dawn was four hours off. The pass gate lay six miles north.

He packed his kit, chose his false name, and went out into the falling white.
