## 5. The Expedition Assembles

The pass gate at Hollowmere had been built by people who expected to be besieged, and in the three hundred years since, no one had ever bothered to attack it. It was a squat arch of grey granite straddling the salt road, its battlements crumbled to a jagged grin, its iron gate long since rusted into open permanence. Children played beneath it. Goats grazed on the ramparts. It was the kind of ruin that made travelers feel safe precisely because it looked like nothing bad could happen there.

On the morning the expedition assembled, Marguerite stood beneath it and felt his teeth ache.

Not from cold—though the wind coming off Thornfell's shoulders carried enough of it to strip paint. The ache came from the bones. He had learned early that bones remembered places as well as people, and the gate's foundation stones were mortared with them: femurs and ribs tumbled into the builders' lime like river pebbles, each one humming faintly with the residue of whoever it had been. Usually they murmured the way old men murmur over pipes—shapeless, contented, half-dreaming.

Today they were whispering.

He had heard it first at dawn, when he came down from Brannoc's house with his pack already strapped, and it had grown louder as the column assembled in the trampled snow below the gate: mules stamping, soldiers checking straps, hired porters arguing about wages in the flat accents of the river towns. And under all of it, threading through the granite like water through a sponge, the dead saying one word over and over in voices that did not belong to any single skull.

*Caerlum.*

*Caerlum.*

*Caerlum.*

Marguerite shifted his weight and kept his head down. He wore the carver's apron he always traveled in—the one with wax-stiff pockets and bone dust ground so deep into the leather it would never come out—and he had introduced himself yesterday to the expedition's paymaster as *Marek Ostler, journeyman carver, hired for camp work.* The lie had gone down smooth as melted butter. He had been lying about who he was since he was nine years old, when Brannoc first taught him that a name is a door you can bar from the inside.

But the dead were not lying, and they were not calm, and for the first time in his life Marguerite Hartwell heard fear in voices that had nothing left to lose.

The column was larger than Brannoc had let on. Twelve soldiers in crown livery, their blue-and-silver tabards new enough that the dye still smelled faintly of urine and oak galls; six porters; a cook; a man whose job appeared to be nothing but carrying a lacquered case the size of a coffin lid; and two figures near the front whom Marguerite watched the way one watches weather.

One was a woman in black, tall, still, her hair bound back so severely it seemed to pull the world straight around her face. She carried nothing but a satchel and a sword she clearly knew how to use, and when she turned to survey the gate her gaze moved across the crowd with the unhurried precision of a woman counting coins. Inquisitor Seraphine Voss. Marguerite had never seen her before in his life, and he knew her instantly, the way you know a wolf track without ever having seen the wolf.

The other was a soft-bodied man in a cartographer's coat gone green at the elbows, fussing with a map case and apologizing to a mule.

And between them, wrapped in wool against the cold, hung the reason the dead were afraid.

Marguerite could not see the relic. It stayed inside the lacquered case or beneath the woman's cloak—he wasn't sure which—but he could *feel* it, the way you can feel a struck tuning fork through a tabletop. A thin, wrong note, persistent as a headache, sitting just below hearing. Every bone in the gate hummed away from it. Every bone in *him* wanted to.

*They brought something that listens back,* whispered the femur nearest his boot, its voice dry as moth wings. *Hide, little hearer. Hide.*

"I am hiding," Marguerite said under his breath. "That's rather the point."

"Talking to yourself, carver?" One of the porters grinned at him, a gap-toothed boy of maybe sixteen. "Bad sign, up here. Mountains don't like folk what talk to themselves."

"They love it," Marguerite said mildly. "Gives them someone else to argue with."

The boy laughed and went back to his ropes, and Marguerite smiled, and underneath the smile his heart was beating like a fist on a locked door.

* * *

Seraphine Voss had commanded witch-hunts in four provinces, and she had learned that the first hour of any expedition told her everything the last month would confirm.

She stood apart from the assembling column with her hands folded behind her back and read her instruments. Not the Chirurgeon's Bell—that stayed wrapped and sleeping in its case, and would stay there until there was cause—but the human ones. Soldiers. Porters. Weather. The way a crowd arranges itself around its own fear.

Her soldiers feared the mountains. That was good. Fear of the mountains kept men close to the column and honest with their own limits. What she needed to worry about was the other thing, the thing she saw flickering in the corner of every eye that touched her satchel: fear of *her*. They had heard what happened at Duncarrow. Of course they had. She had made certain the demonstration was public enough to travel faster than she did.

*Let them be afraid,* her instructors had written, in the handbooks she had once copied out by candlelight, believing every word. *Fear of sanctification is the first sacrament.*

She no longer believed every word. She believed the words that mattered: the gift was real, and untrained, and it killed. She had seen the corpses. She had buried the children the hearers' guild called *vessels* and she called by their names, because someone had to.

Still. Her thumb found the edge of the bell's case through her coat, an unconscious gesture, and she caught herself doing it and stopped.

There had been a tone at Duncarrow. When the bell rang for the hearer—a weaver, a grandmother, guilty beyond doubt—there had been a second note underneath the first, low and resonant, like a bell ringing inside a bell. The texts said the Chirurgeon's Bell sounded one pure tone in the presence of bone-hearing. One tone. Hers had sounded two, and no text explained why, and she had lain awake three nights turning it over like a stone in the palm before deciding it was a flaw in the relic, age-wear, nothing.

She almost believed that.

"Ma'am." Sergeant Corliss, her best, a broad-faced veteran with frost in his beard and a farmer's patience. He nodded toward the far end of the line. "Paymaster says the guide's confirmed. Local clan sent him. Fellow named Ostler, does carving work. Says he knows every goat path over the Thornfell shoulder."

"The clan vouches for him?"

"The clan *insists* on him." Corliss lowered his voice. "Brannoc's people. You know what they are."

Smugglers. Everyone in the high country knew what they were, and everyone pretended not to, because the alternative was paying crown tariffs on salt. Voss considered this. A smuggler's foster-son guiding a crown expedition through passes the crown did not officially need guided through—it was absurd, and it was also exactly how the crown preferred to do its absurd business. Deniability layered like winter clothes.

"Watch him," she said.

"He's watching us," Corliss said, with the flat honesty she paid him for. "Been watching since dawn. Quiet fellow. Stands like a man trying to be furniture."

Voss looked down the line. The carver stood beneath the gate arch, adjusting a pack strap, a plain brown man among brown men. Ordinary. Carefully ordinary, the way a forged document is carefully ordinary.

Everything was carefully ordinary until the bell rang.

"Mount when the paymaster gives the word," she said. "And Sergeant—if anyone leaves the column in the night, I want to know before they're out of lantern light."

"Aye, ma'am."

Above them, on the ruined battlements, a raven shifted its feet on cold stone, and Voss felt—for half a heartbeat, absurdly—as if something were counting heads along with her.

* * *

Ellery Finch had been disgraced in the spring, exiled from the Royal Cartographic Office in autumn, and rehired in secret by winter, which Finch considered proof that disgrace, properly managed, was simply employment with better scenery.

His crime had been accuracy. He had charted the coast of the Weeping Sea and put a bay where the Crown's charts showed none, because the bay was there, and the Admiralty's favorite patron owned land that would flood if the bay existed. He had refused to erase it. There had been a hearing, a stripped commission, and a year of drinking cheap wine in cheap rooms and redrawing old maps for bread money, until the sealed letter arrived bearing the privy seal and a sum that made his hands shake.

*You will chart the route to Caerlum. You will speak of it to no one. Your disgrace will be reversed upon completion.*

Finch had spent two days deciding whether the reversal was worth whatever waited under Caerlum's mountain, and then spent ten minutes realizing the decision had never been his. Men who refuse the privy seal do not get hearings. They get accidents.

So here he stood at the Hollowmere pass gate, mapping a road that officially did not exist, for an expedition that officially did not exist, alongside an inquisitor who officially was not present, and the only honest thing within forty miles was the weather.

He had his maps spread on a fold-table beside the paymaster's wagon, weighted with stones, and he was making his final corrections when the carver wandered over.

"Might I look?" The man had a carver's forearms and a traveler's boots and a voice like warm water. "I've walked these passes my whole life. Never seen them drawn."

"Few have," Finch said, pleased despite himself. Accuracy was lonely work. "Mind the ink."

The carver leaned over the sheets—long fingers, ink stains of his own, the calluses of a man who worked fine material—and Finch watched his eyes move across the contours with something that was not quite reading. It was more like listening.

"You've got the defile wrong," the carver said.

"The—?" Finch pulled the sheet closer. "Which defile?"

"There." A finger, blunt-nailed, precise, tapped a valley north of the marked route. "The Grey Throat. Your contour lines show it narrowing to a gorge. It doesn't. It widens, halfway down, into a bowl—old battle-ground, the smugglers call it. Then narrows again. If your route crosses the upper Throat in a thaw, you'll want to know about the bowl."

Finch stared at the finger, then at the man, then at the map, and the small hairs on his arms rose for reasons he couldn't immediately name.

Because the finger was right. He *knew* it was right, the way he knew a coastline was right—not from evidence but from the deep, unreasonable certainty of thirty years of practice. And more than that: the way the man had described it, the cadence, *widens into a bowl, then narrows again*—

"That's odd," Finch said slowly.

"What is?"

"Nothing." He shook his head. "Thank you, Master...?"

"Ostler. Marek Ostler."

"Master Ostler." Finch made the correction on the spot, freehand, widening the contour lines of the Grey Throat, and as he drew he became aware that the carver had gone very still beside him, watching the pencil move with an expression Finch could not read at all.

It was not the expression of a man looking at a map.

It was the expression of a man looking at handwriting.

* * *

They marched at noon, twelve soldiers, six porters, one inquisitor, one cartographer, one cook, one case-carrier, one guide, and twenty-two mules, out beneath the rusted-open gate and up the first white shoulder of Thornfell, and the mountain took them the way the sea takes swimmers—one degree at a time.

By the third hour the trees had given up. By the fifth, the wind had found every gap in every hood and collar and begun its patient work. The soldiers stopped talking somewhere around the fourth hour, conserving breath, and after that the column moved in a silence broken only by creaking harness and the crunch-crunch-crunch of boots, and Marguerite, walking at the front beside the paymaster's lead mule, listened to the dead.

They were louder up here. That was normal—high places held bones well, the cold preserving them like cellar fruit—but the *quality* of it was not normal. The usual chorus of the pass was a diffuse thing, hundreds of small voices, shepherds and travelers and the occasional frozen fool, each muttering its private fragment. Today the fragments were arranging themselves. Today they were converging, the way streams converge, and what they converged on was a single current running beneath the snow like a vein of dark ore:

*Caerlum. Caerlum. Do not go to Caerlum.*

*We were there when it was sealed. We were there. We helped. Gods forgive us, we helped.*

*The fire is not going out. The fire is being held down. Do you understand the difference, little hearer? Do you?*

Marguerite kept walking. His legs knew the path; his mind was elsewhere, sinking through the voices like a man sinking through ice-water, deeper and deeper, past the recent dead, past the old dead, into strata of whisper so ancient they had worn smooth as sea glass—

And then the bell rang.

Not fully. Not the toll he'd been dreading since Hollowmere. Just a hum, a single sustained note bleeding faintly from the inquisitor's satchel as she walked thirty paces behind him, a sound like a wet finger circling a goblet's rim, thin and sweet and absolutely wrong.

Every head in earshot turned toward the sound. Voss's hand closed over the satchel flap, pressing it quiet, and for a moment her eyes swept the column—quick, professional, hungry—and passed over Marguerite the way a lantern beam passes over a wall.

Then the hum faded. The column breathed again. The march resumed.

Marguerite discovered that his hands were shaking, and pressed them flat against his thighs, and made himself breathe, and did not stop walking.

*Little hearer,* whispered a rib somewhere under the snowfield to his left, a shepherd dead four hundred years, its voice cracked with pity. *Run while your legs are yours.*

"Can't," he mouthed, silent, to the snow. "They'd follow. And I need to see it."

*See what?*

And that was the question, wasn't it. The dead were afraid of Caerlum, but afraid the way you are afraid of a sickroom, not a battlefield. Underneath the warnings, underneath the *do not go*, ran another thread, thinner, older, almost unbearable in its longing:

*Someone is coming. Someone who hears. At last, at last, someone who hears is coming.*

*Tell him. Tell him what waits in the cage. Tell him what our grandmothers built. Tell him before the last light goes.*

Marguerite looked back down the column—once, briefly, over his shoulder. The soldiers plodding, the porters hunched, Finch scribbling notes against his knee, Voss walking like a drawn blade, the lacquered case swaying on its porter's back.

Somewhere ahead, past the Grey Throat, past the shoulder of the world, the last dragon-fire guttered in a cage his family had built, and the dead were lining up along his path like witnesses outside a courtroom, waiting for him to arrive.

Behind him, soft and brief, the Chirurgeon's Bell hummed again.

This time, Marguerite was almost certain it was pointed at him.

* * *

They made cold camp in the lee of the gate ruins' outer wall, and Seraphine Voss sat apart with her supper and her thoughts and unwrapped the bell just far enough to look at it.

The Chirurgeon's Bell was small—smaller than people imagined, when they imagined it at all. A hand's height of pale bronze, cast in some forgotten century by a craftsman whose name the Church had eaten along with everything else, its surface worked into interlocking anatomical figures: hearts, lungs, spines, skulls, all flowing into one another like water. It had rung at Duncarrow with two tones. It had hummed twice today on the march, unprompted, both times when the wind blew from the front of the column.

Both times, she noted now, writing it small in her cipher-book, *both times when the wind blew from the direction of the guide.*

She watched the carver across the firelight. He was mending a porter's torn strap with neat, economical stitches, and laughing softly at something the gap-toothed boy said, and being, in every visible particular, exactly what he claimed to be.

*Marek Ostler,* she thought. *Journeyman carver.*

She wrote the name in her book, underlined it once, and did not yet write anything beneath it. Suspicion without evidence was the disease her office bred in lesser officers; she had seen men hang whole villages on it. She would watch. She would wait. And if the bell rang true—

If the bell rang true, she reminded herself, she would do her duty, as she had always done her duty.

Under the wind, very faint, the bell gave the smallest shiver of resonance against her hip, like a dog stirring in its sleep.

Voss closed the case, and latched it, and did not sleep well.
