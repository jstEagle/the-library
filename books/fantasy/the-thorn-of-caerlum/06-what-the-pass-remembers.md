## 6. What the Pass Remembers

The defile began where the pines gave up.

One moment the column moved beneath green shadow and the smell of resin; the next, the trees simply stopped, as if they had reached some old agreement with the mountain and would not go further. Beyond that invisible line, the pass narrowed into a throat of grey stone, walled on either side by cliffs so sheer they seemed less carved than broken, and the wind came down through it with a sound Marguerite had never heard before—low, sustained, almost like breathing. Like something enormous asleep and drawing slow breath through its teeth.

"Single file," he called back, and his voice sounded small against the stone. "Keep your hands on the guide-rope. The scree gives way without warning."

Sergeant Odo Hask repeated the order down the line in his flat parade voice, and forty soldiers became a thread of men winding into the mountain's gullet. Behind them came the mules, then the supply cart with its protesting axle, then Inquisitor Seraphine Voss riding at the rear like a black needle sewn through the fabric of the march. She had not spoken to him since Hollowmere. She did not need to. He felt her attention on his back the way he felt frost forming—not painful yet, but certain.

Ellery Finch walked beside him instead of riding, map case slung across his chest like a breastplate. The disgraced cartographer had taken to studying the terrain with an intensity that bordered on devotion, muttering elevations under his breath as though they were prayers.

"This is the Sorrowway," Marguerite said. "Older name for it, anyway. The maps call it the Caerlum Defile now, but my mother—" He caught himself. Brannoc was not his mother by blood, though she had been everything else. "The smugglers call it the Sorrowway. There's a reason."

"What reason?"

"You'll see."

They saw within the hour.

The floor of the defile was not stone. Not entirely. As the light of midmorning slanted down between the cliffs, the ground beneath their boots resolved itself into something else—a pavement of bone, fused and weathered and packed hard by centuries of snowmelt and wind, stretching ahead of them into the blue distance of the pass. Ribs broad as oars. A skull the size of a rain barrel, its sutures gone silver with age. Femurs laid across one another like fallen timbers, worn smooth along their upper curves where ten thousand years of travelers had stepped.

The soldiers had stopped talking. Even the mules went quiet, ears flat, refusing at first to put their hooves down until the drovers coaxed them forward with trembling hands.

"Saints' blood," Hask breathed. "Whose dead are these?"

"Nobody's," Marguerite said quietly. "Or everybody's. It depends which story you were raised on." He crouched, ostensibly to check a guide-knot, actually to press his bare palm flat against a rib that arched up from the bone-pavement like the rib of a drowned ship.

And the Sorrowway woke up.

It always started as cold. That was how hearing began—the cold of the grave passing up through his skin, past the wrist, settling into the hollow behind his sternum where his gift lived. Then the murmur, the layered whisper of voices long settled into their own substance, speaking not in words at first but in impressions, in the residue of what they had been. He had walked this defile a dozen times in his smuggling years, and every time the chorus had been the same: grief, exhaustion, fear, the fading echoes of a battle so vast the individual dead had dissolved into it, become less like souls and more like weather.

But today the chorus was different.

Today it was *loud*.

*They come again,* whispered a voice like wind through arrow-slits. *They come again, and we must rise.*

Marguerite's fingers curled against the rib. He kept his face blank—he had a lifetime's practice at a blank face—but inside his skull the voices were unspooling, dozens of them, hundreds, braided together into a single rope of memory, and the memory was moving. He could feel it move, the way you can feel a dream shift when someone speaks your name.

He closed his eyes. Let the cold take him deeper.

---

He stood on the bone-pavement and it was not a pavement. It was a field, and the field was alive with screaming.

Dragons filled the sky.

Not the dragons of the chapbooks, the black-winged devils that mothers invoked to frighten children into obedience, the ancient enemy that had come out of the north in the Age of Ashes to burn the works of man. These were—*wrong*. Wrong in color, wrong in bearing, wrong in the very architecture of their flight. They flew in formation. They wheeled in answer to horn-calls blown from the ground below, great serpentine bodies catching the light in scales of copper and slate-green and one that shone like hammered gold, and beneath them, impossibly, *beneath* them, men fought.

Men fought alongside them.

Marguerite watched a shield-wall hold against a tide of things that wore the shapes of wolves but were too large, too jointed, their hides stitched-looking, their eyes lit from within like coals seen through a cracked door. And above the shield-wall a dragon dove—sleek and green, no bigger than a hay barn—and its fire fell in a precise arc, a drawn curtain of flame that cut the wolf-things off from the men entirely, herding them, *herding*, not burning indiscriminately but fighting with the care of a soldier who knows exactly where his allies stand. Men cheered. He heard them cheer, ten thousand throats, and the sound rose into the dragon's wake and the dragon banked toward the sound as if the cheering were a hand pulling it home.

A woman's voice spoke beside him—or rather, through him, because she was dead and he was only the shape her memory poured itself into. She had been an officer. He could taste her rank the way you taste salt: *banner-sergeant of the Third Wall, sworn to the Ember Accord.* The words arrived with weight, formal as an oath.

*We swore together,* she said, and her pride was a physical thing, warm as a hearthstone against his ribs. *Man and wyrm, wyrm and man. The Accord held for nine hundred years. My grandmother carried water to the wounded drakes at Redford Field. My daughter will nurse hatchlings in the vaults of Caerlum.*

*Caerlum,* Marguerite thought, and the word detonated through the chorus like a dropped lantern through dry grass.

Every voice turned toward it at once.

*Caerlum, where the fire sleeps. Caerlum, where the cage was built.* The banner-sergeant's warmth curdled. Around her the other voices surged—confusion, outrage, a grief so old it had fossilized into something harder than bone. *Who told them the dragons were the enemy? Who burned the Accord? Who taught the children to fear the sky?*

*I don't know,* Marguerite tried to say. *I don't know, I was taught the same—*

*You carry their hands,* said another voice, deeper, older, a voice with bedrock in it. *We know those hands. We built with those hands. And now those hands walk our bones toward the cage, wearing the livery of the ones who locked it.*

The cold in his chest turned to ice.

*They're coming to finish it,* the voices whispered, rising, pressing against the inside of his skull like floodwater against a door. *The hunters. The bell. The crown that fears any fire it cannot own. If they reach Caerlum—*

He tore his hand off the rib.

---

The world snapped back: grey stone, white sky, the groan of the wind, Sergeant Hask asking him a second time if he was well. Marguerite realized he was on one knee in the snow-melt, that his palm had left a print in the rime coating the bone, and that the entire front half of the column had stopped to watch him.

"Slipped," he said. His voice came out sanded smooth, the craftsman's calm he'd worn since boyhood. "These old bones are slicker than they look."

Hask grunted and waved the column onward. But Finch lingered, offering a gloved hand, and when Marguerite took it the cartographer's sharp grey eyes searched his face with uncomfortable precision.

"You looked like a man listening," Finch said.

"To the wind."

"The wind doesn't make people kneel." Finch said it lightly, but his eyes didn't lighten. He adjusted the map case at his chest—a habitual gesture, Marguerite had noticed, like a man touching a wound—and trudged on.

Marguerite followed on legs that did not feel entirely his own. Everything he had ever been taught lay shattered around him like the bones underfoot. The Age of Ashes. The Dragon War. The great enemy from the north whose defeat had founded three kingdoms and justified a thousand years of vigilance, of witch-hunts, of sanctification pyres. Every hearer knew the catechism: *the dragons burned first; the heroes bled; the fire sleeps still, hating, waiting to devour us.* He had recited it as a child with Brannoc's arm around his shoulders, safe in the smugglers' hall while blizzards screamed outside.

And it was a lie. Or half a lie, which was worse, because half a lie is a blade with the maker's name filed off. The dragons had flown. The war was real. But the dragons had been *allies*—sworn under something called the Ember Accord, fighting beside human shields against enemies he couldn't even name—and somewhere in the deep time since, someone had turned that alliance inside out, dressed it as invasion, and buried the truth under ten thousand years of bone.

Someone had built a cage at Caerlum and called it a tomb.

His ancestors. The voices had known his hands. *We built with those hands.* The thought made his stomach turn over slowly, like a boat shipping water. Every keepsake he had ever carved, every dying whisper he had faithfully carried—had it all been service to a dynasty of liars? Was even his gift part of the mechanism?

Ahead, the defile bent around a shoulder of black rock, and the wind changed note.

It should have been nothing. Mountain winds change all the time. But this new note had a harmonic in it, a thin silver overtone sitting above the breath of the pass, and Marguerite's skin prickled before his mind caught up, before he understood where he had heard that overtone before—

Hollowmere. The crossroads. The day the Chirurgeon's Bell rang over the market crowd and a neighbor stopped screaming forever.

He turned in his tracks. Halfway down the line, Voss had reined in her horse. She sat motionless in the saddle, hood back despite the wind, her pale face tilted upward, listening. Her hand rested on the case strapped before her saddle, the one that never left her side, and from within that case—faintly, unmistakably, threading through the wind like a wire through wool—came a hum.

Not ringing. Humming. The bell was not announcing a detection. It was *answering* something, resonating with something, and Marguerite realized with a lurch that the something was not him. It was the pass itself, the bone-pavement, ten thousand years of the war-dead singing their fury into the stone, and the relic that hunted his kind was vibrating to their chorus like a tuning fork struck against a cathedral wall.

Voss looked down the column. Her gaze traveled soldier by soldier, mule by mule, unhurried, thorough, and Marguerite watched it come toward him with the fatalism of a man watching a wave approach a sandcastle. He arranged his face into mild, tired competence—the carver's face, the false-named porter's face, *Tam Weller, guide, at your service*—and gripped the guide-rope, and waited.

Her eyes passed over him. Paused.

Moved on.

She spurred forward toward the head of the column without a word, and the hum faded behind her, sinking back beneath the wind, and Marguerite discovered he had been holding his breath long enough to see sparks.

Finch appeared at his elbow again. "She does that," the cartographer murmured. "Stops and listens. Started two days ago, right after we entered the high passes. I asked her once what she hears." He glanced back at the black needle of her retreating figure. "She said, *'The mountains are ringing.'*"

The column moved on. The bone-pavement rose gently beneath their boots, mile upon mile of the fused honored dead, and Marguerite walked among them with his gift straining against its leash. The chorus had not gone silent after his vision—it had merely lowered its voice, the way a crowd lowers its voice when the magistrate enters the room. Beneath each footfall he could feel them watching. *You carry their hands,* the bedrock voice had said, and now, softer, with each step:

*Remember what you heard. Remember what they built. When you reach the gate—and you will reach the gate—remember.*

Near dusk the defile opened onto the high shelf where they would camp, a bowl of sheltered stone with a frozen spring and room enough for fires. The soldiers set about their routines gratefully, and the mundane sounds of an army making camp—clinking mess tins, Hask's voice setting watches, a mule complaining—settled over Marguerite like a familiar blanket. He helped pitch the command tent, sawed kindling, kept his hands busy and his gift folded small.

It was full dark when Voss finally approached his fire.

She came alone, which frightened him more than an escort would have. She stood at the edge of the firelight with the bell-case in both hands, and for a long moment she said nothing, only studied him across the flames with those winter-pale eyes.

"Tam Weller," she said at last. "That's the name on the clan ledger."

"That's me, Inquisitor."

"Mm." She turned the case slowly in her hands. From within it, faint as a sleeper shifting, the bell hummed—one soft pulse, like a heart deciding whether to beat. Voss looked down at it. Then up. "Do you know what I heard today, Weller? On the Sorrowway?"

"The wind, I'd guess, Inquisitor. Wind's strange in the passes."

"Yes," she said. "Strange in the passes." She held his gaze a moment longer—long enough for the fire to crackle twice, long enough for Marguerite's pulse to hammer against the inside of his calm—and then she turned away. At the edge of the light she paused, her back to him, and said, without turning:

"In the morning, you'll lead us up the Stair. The last ascent to Caerlum's gate is carved, not climbed. My orders say the vault opens to no key and no force." A beat. "Only to bone-hearers."

The wind came down off the peaks, cold and clean and carrying, very faintly, the far-off sound of the war-dead singing.

"So sleep well, Tam Weller," said Inquisitor Voss. "Tomorrow, we learn what you really are."
