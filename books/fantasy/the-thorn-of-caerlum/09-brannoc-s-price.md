## 9. Brannoc's Price

The storm found them two days past the Widow's Teeth, and Brannoc found them one day after that.

Marguerite knew she was coming before he saw her—knew it in his wrists, in the old scar along his knuckle where a fishhook had torn him at nine years old and Brannoc had sewn it shut with sail-thread, swearing at him gently the whole time. The dead did not speak to warn him this time. It was simpler than that, and worse: some part of him had always known she would come.

He was walking point when the first arrow buried itself in the snow three paces ahead of the lead soldier, fletched in goose-grey, smuggler-fletched, the shaft cut with the spiral notch that meant *we see you, we choose not to kill you yet*. The column stopped. Twenty soldiers dropped behind their shields with the practiced grace of men who had done this before, and Voss's hand went to the Chirurgeon's Bell at her belt, and Finch nearly fell off his mule trying to get behind the mule.

"Bandits," said Sergeant Corren, spitting into the wind.

"No," Marguerite said quietly. "Worse."

They came down out of the whiteout in ones and twos, figures wrapped in wolf-pelts and ash-smeared leathers, faces blackened so only their eyes showed—and even those eyes were wrong for bandits, too calm, too disciplined. He counted eleven. He recognized six of them. The big one on the left with the crossbow held low was Dagny Hale, who had taught him to swim in the freezing quarry ponds by the simple method of throwing him in. The slight figure beside her was young Piet, who still owed him two silver for a carved mourning ring he'd never collected.

And at their center, taller than all of them because she refused to be anything else, stood Brannoc Ashvane in a borrowed coat of bear fur, her grey-streaked braid whipping in the wind, her face bare and unblackened because she had never once in her life hidden it from anyone.

"Well met, travelers!" she called, and her voice carried the easy menace of a woman who could be friendly or fatal and hadn't decided which. "This pass is closed. Toll's your cargo, your coin, and whatever letters you're carrying south. Pay it and walk away warm. Refuse—" she spread her hands, "—well. The mountain does what mountains do."

Corren started forward with his sword half-drawn. Marguerite moved without deciding to move, stepping between the line of shields and the line of wolves, arms out, palms empty.

"Sergeant. Wait." He turned to face the bandits, and let his voice break its disguise at last, letting the Hollowmere accent through, letting the cadence of the salt-roads through. "Auntie. You know this is foolishness. There are twenty soldiers here with royal steel, and a woman at the rear who burns people for a living."

Something flickered across Brannoc's eyes—the briefest crack in the performance—and then she laughed, loud and rough, playing her part to the last row of the theater.

"Do I know you, bone-man?" She stalked closer, boots crunching, until she stood an arm's length from him, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and tar and the rosemary oil she'd rubbed into her hair since before he could remember. Close enough that when she spoke again, quieter, under the wind, it was for him alone. "Hello, lamb."

"Auntie." His throat ached around the word. "You shouldn't have come."

"Oh, I shouldn't have done a great many things." Her eyes went past him, cataloguing the column with a raider's efficiency—the supply sledges, the water barrels, the sealed strongbox lashed to the second sledge that had never once been opened in his presence, the bell at Voss's hip. "Twenty soldiers, a burned-out cartographer, and the Inquisitor herself. All walking into my mountains in the dead season, following a route nobody's walked in four hundred years, carrying a box heavy enough to need two mules." She looked back at him. "I paid you to carry bones, lamb, not to carry crowns."

"I didn't know," he said. "When I signed on—"

"You suspected." No anger in it. That was the worst thing. Brannoc angry was a summer storm, all noise and lightning and then sun. Brannoc quiet was winter, and winter did not end until it was finished with you. "You suspected from the moment you saw the crest on the commission letter, and you signed anyway, and you told me nothing. I had to hear it from Piet, who heard it from a drover at the pass gate, who saw a carver with your hands take crown coin under a false name." Her jaw tightened. "*Marrow.* You couldn't even pick a name that wasn't yours."

"It needed to be close enough that I'd answer to it."

"It needed to be honest, is what it needed to be."

Wind screamed through the defile. Behind him, Marguerite heard Corren murmur orders, the soft slide of blades being readied, Voss's measured footsteps as she came forward through the column. He did not turn to look at her. Looking at her would make everything worse, though he could not yet have said how.

"Brannoc Ashvane," Voss called out, and of course she knew the name—of course she did; the crown kept files on every clan chief from Thornfell to the coast, and Brannoc's file was probably thick enough to stop an arrow. "You are harboring a confessed hearer of the dead. Step aside, surrender him and your people, and the crown will weigh mercy."

Brannoc smiled, wide and white and terrible. "Inquisitor. They told me you were clever. They lied."

"I have a relic that rings when the gifted stand near. Shall I ring it now and count how many gifted stand in your little war-band?"

"Ring whatever you like, crow." Brannoc's smile didn't waver, but Marguerite saw her left hand flex once at her side—the old signal, *hold, hold, hold*—and saw Dagny's crossbow dip a careful inch. "But we both know you're not here for bone-whisperers. Bone-whisperers are the excuse. That box on your sledge is the errand. So here's my toll, revised and final: you turn this parade around, you go back down to the lowlands, and you forget Caerlum exists. In exchange, my people melt back into the snow, and everyone keeps their blood on the inside where it belongs."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then the mountain eats you." Brannoc shrugged, vast and casual beneath the bear fur. "Avalanches happen. Cold happens. Starving men make mistakes on ice ledges. Nobody in Hollowmere will say a word against it, and neither will any grave." Her eyes cut to Marguerite, and there it was at last—not anger, something older and more frightened. "Nobody will say a word against it. Isn't that right, lamb?"

Marguerite understood then, fully, what she had climbed into the high passes to do. Not rob them. *Erase* them. Let the expedition die nameless in the white, and bury the truth of Caerlum under twenty corpses and a winter, the way the salt-roads had always buried things. And she had come to offer him a door out of it first, because she loved him, and because she believed—had taught him to believe—that silence was shelter and small kindnesses were armor and if you just kept your head down and your hands busy the world would pass over you like weather.

The neighbor they'd taken at the crossroads had believed that too. She'd been dragged off screaming his name.

"Auntie," Marguerite said. "I can't."

Brannoc's face went very still.

"The fire under Caerlum is failing," he said. "You've seen it. We've all seen it—the frost coming down early, the springs running thin, the sky over the northern peaks going the color of old pewter. It isn't weather, Auntie. It's the flame guttering in its cage. And when it goes out, it doesn't just take the mountain. It takes the gift. It takes every hearer alive, everywhere, all at once. It takes me. And the dead—" his voice caught, steadied, "—the dead are afraid, Brannoc. Do you understand? Ten thousand years of dead, and they are *afraid*, and they've been whispering one thing into every bone I've touched since Hollowmere. *The cage must open or the light goes out.*"

"You don't know that," she said. "That's what the crow wants you to think, so you'll open whatever door she's paid you to open—"

"The dead aren't the crown's creatures. The dead don't lie about themselves." He took a breath. "And there's more. The cage was built by hearers. By my ancestors—by *ours*, Auntie, by the first bone-carvers of Thornfell. They caged the dragon-fire to keep its power for themselves, and they built the hunts to burn anyone who might set it free. Everything we've been running from, our whole lives—all those pyres, all those names—we weren't fleeing the truth. Our own family wrote it."

For one heartbeat, one long heartbeat, Brannoc Ashvane looked at him and he saw her believe it—or at least see it, the way a woman sees a wave rising higher than any wave she has ever seen, and understands that the sea has changed.

Then the heartbeat passed, and she shut it out.

"No," she said. "No. Whatever voices you've been hearing, lamb, they've lied to you. Voices do that. Dead things want what living things want—warmth, company, someone to talk to—and they'll tell you the moon is made of bread if it keeps you carving." Her voice hardened. "Or maybe you've heard true. Maybe it's all exactly as you say. And you'd still be standing there asking me to help you open the cage that started a war. A war, Marguerite. Dragons and men, burning the world down to the roots, and you want to hand the match back because the dead sound *sad*?"

"The alternative is the dark."

"The dark is familiar! The dark is survivable! We've lived small and cold for four hundred years and we LIVED—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again, each word was laid down like a stone in a wall. "Come home. Right now. Walk away from them, walk with us, and we seal the passes behind us and let the crown freeze. I will burn every record, every map, every scrap of paper that points to Caerlum, mine and yours both. You carve keepsakes and salt-caches and mourning rings until your hands give out, and you die old, in a bed, in a house full of people who love you. That is what I'm offering. That is the last thing I will ever offer."

Behind Marguerite, the column had gone silent. Twenty soldiers listening. An Inquisitor listening. Finch, somewhere near the sledges, listening and probably writing it all down in that battered notebook of his.

Publicly, then. She meant it to be public. Some deep, stubborn, loving corner of him understood that too—if he humiliated her in front of her people, her people might survive her mercy; but if she humiliated him in front of the crown, the crown might leave the clan alone. She was building him a scaffold and calling it a road home, and either way, one of them was going to bleed in front of witnesses.

"I can't," Marguerite said again. Gently. Which was the cruelest way to say it. "You raised me on the salt-roads, Auntie. You taught me that a debt unpaid is a rot in the bone. Well—I owe the dead. Every secret they ever trusted me with, every cache I dug up and kept quiet about, every grief I carved into comfort instead of carrying it to the light. I owe them all of it, and the interest has come due, and the payment is this walk. I'm sorry. I love you. I'm going."

He watched her heart break. It was not dramatic. It was terribly quiet. Something behind her eyes simply closed, like a shutter drawn against a storm, and what remained was the clan-chief, the survivor, the woman who had buried two husbands and a daughter and kept walking because the roads needed walking.

"So be it," Brannoc said, and her voice carried flat across the snow, pitched now for every ear, his and theirs and the mountain's. "Hear me, all of you. This man was given to me half-dead in a fish-crate thirty years ago, and I fed him, and I named him, and I taught him the roads and the signs and the silence that keeps small folk breathing. And he has repaid it by taking crown coin to guide the crown's knives to the last door in the world that keeps us safe." She drew a knife—his own work, he realized distantly; he had carved that handle for her twelfth winter ago, juniper and whalebone, the best thing his hands had ever made—and scored a long line through the snow between them. "The clan is crossed to you, Marguerite Hartwell. Your name is struck. Your debts are struck. If you starve on the mountain, no hand of ours digs you out. If you crawl back to Hollowmere, no door opens. You are no son of mine, and no kin of ours, and may whatever gods listen to bone-hearers have mercy on you, because the Ashvanes will not."

Dagny's crossbow dipped. Piet made a small wounded sound and smothered it. Six pairs of eyes that had known him all his life looked at him across the scored line in the snow, and none of them—not one—met his gaze for longer than a blink.

"Auntie—" Piet started.

"Quiet." But it was gentle, that word, gentler than anything else she'd said, and Marguerite understood that she was protecting the boy, not herself.

Voss stepped forward into the space between them, and Marguerite braced for the arrest—for the bell, the irons, the end of the walk right here on the mountainside. Instead the Inquisitor looked at Brannoc with an expression he could not read, and said only:

"You threatened to murder a royal expedition, Ashvane. I should hang you where you stand."

"Try," said Brannoc.

"I won't." Voss's voice was dry as old paper. "Not because I can't. Because you've just done my work for me—you've shown every soldier on this line that the man leading them chose us over his own family, knowing the price, and paid it in front of God and the snow. There is no better proof of loyalty than that." She turned, coat snapping in the wind. "Column! Move. We've wasted enough daylight on theater."

The column moved. Shields up, sledges groaning, boots crunching past the scored line in the snow, past eleven motionless figures in wolf-pelts, past a woman who did not watch them go—watched, rather, the middle distance, the peaks, anything but the boy walking away at the head of the enemy.

Marguerite made himself walk. One boot, then the other. The wind took his breath and gave it back colder. At the edge of hearing, almost lost in it, he thought he heard Piet call his name once—just the name, *Marguerite*, stripped of everything it used to mean—and then the whiteout swallowed the sound, and the bandits, and the line in the snow, and the woman who had pulled him half-drowned from a fish-crate and loved him for thirty years and now would not look at him.

He did not cry until the camp that night, and then only briefly, privately, with his back against a sledge wheel and his hood up, while the soldiers pretended not to notice and Finch sat nearby saying nothing, keeping him company the way cartographers keep company: silently, precisely, at a respectful distance.

It was Finch, later, who shook him awake.

The camp was stirring wrong—not the slow dawn-stir of routine but the sharp motion of alarm. Torches. Shouted numbers. Corren pointing north with an arm rigid as a signpost.

Marguerite rolled to his feet and looked, and his stomach turned to ice that had nothing to do with the altitude.

Above the next ridge, silhouetted against the stars, the snowfields were moving. Whole slopes of them, sliding loose, gathering mass, silent as a held breath—a white ocean leaning over the pass they would have to cross at dawn.

And beneath the rumble, beneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, the dead in the mountain began to scream.
