## 10. Avalanche of Voices

The mountain spoke first.

I had been listening to it for three days without admitting what I heard—not the dead this time, but the mountain itself, groaning in its sleep the way old men groan before a fever breaks. The pass above the Sorrowmere defile was loaded like a smuggler's scale with too much on one side: six feet of wind-packed snow sitting on a slope of blue ice that had not frozen properly since the autumn rains. Any carter in Thornfell would have read the signs. But soldiers do not read mountains. Soldiers read orders, and Captain Aldery's order was to make Caerlum before the winter gates closed.

"Carver." His voice carried down the column to where I trudged at the rear, my pack straps biting into shoulders that had carried quieter burdens for thirty years. "You've been staring up at that cornice since noon. Superstitious?"

"Practical," I said. "That snow wants to come down. I'd rather we were through the narrows before it makes up its mind."

He grunted—the particular grunt of a man who has decided to take advice from someone he despises because the alternative is dying stupidly—and bellowed for double pace. The column lurched forward, forty men and two mules and one inquisitor in her grey traveling cloak, moving through the throat of the defile where the walls rose sheer on either side and the sky narrowed to a ribbon of hard white light.

Behind me, the Chirurgeon's Bell hung silent in Seraphine Voss's saddle-sling. It had been silent for days now, which I told myself was mercy and knew was only patience. A bell that listens does not need to ring often. It needs to ring once.

I put my head down and walked, and beneath my boots, under forty feet of stone and older snow than any of us would ever see melt, the war-dead murmured their endless litany. *They flew beside us. They flew beside us. Why did they tell you otherwise?* I had stopped answering them. Answering voices in your own skull is the first step toward the scaffold.

Then the mountain made up its mind.

There was no roar at first—that came later, when the sound had already killed you. There was a *crack*, high and sharp as a bone snapping in a fire, somewhere up in the white blindness of the cornice, and every man in the column froze at once, forty heads tilting back like iron filings finding a lodestone.

"*Move!*" Aldery's voice cracked across the defile. "*MOVE—*"

They moved. Men who had marched all day at a trudge suddenly ran like boys, boots punching through crust, mules screaming, Voss hauling her horse around by the bit as the ribbon of sky above us turned grey and then black and then simply *gone*, swallowed whole by a wall of snow taller than the chapel at Hollowmere, taller than anything, coming down the slope with the unhurried terrible patience of something that has never once been argued with.

I did not run. That is the truth I would carry afterward, when the men looked at me differently—I did not run because there was nowhere to run. The defile was too narrow, the wave too wide. Instead I did the only thing my body knew how to do when death came calling in Thornfell: I dropped my pack, threw myself flat against the rock wall, folded my arms over my head, and listened.

The dead beneath me were screaming too. Not in fear. In warning. *Air pocket,* they howled, ten thousand throats speaking as one through the bedrock, *curl small, keep the hollow before your chest, breathe slow—* And I understood, in the half-second the world took to end, that some of these dead had died exactly this way, in exactly this pass, three hundred years ago, marching under a banner I could not see, and they had learned the lesson with their lungs.

The snow hit like the fist of a god who had been waiting all day for permission.

* * *

Silence. Pressure. Cold so complete it stopped being cold and became simply fact, like stone, like gravity.

I was buried. I could not tell which way was up except that my body remembered the dead's instruction—curled small against the rock face, arms crossed, a hollow of air crushed shallow before my chest. Snow had packed into my collar, my sleeves, my mouth. When I exhaled, the warmth came back to me tasting of wool and terror.

Above me—or below me, or perhaps beside me; the mountain had scrambled its own arithmetic—something enormous settled with a long grinding sigh, and then nothing. No wind. No shouts. No bell.

Just the dead, everywhere now, closer than they had ever been, because I was lying among them. The avalanche had torn bones out of the defile's ancient scree and mixed them into the snow like currants in a loaf. I could feel them against my ribs through my coat—fragments, shards, the odd intact femur—and each one touched me the way a struck tuning fork touches its brother. *Here. Here. Here.* The dead of the pass, shaken loose from their three-century sleep, all talking at once.

*Hush,* I told them. *Please. Hush.*

And—this is the part I still cannot explain, the part I will spend the rest of whatever life remains to me trying to earn—they hushed. They gathered close the way Brannoc's smugglers used to gather around a candle in a storm-cave, pressing in not to smother but to shield. Ten thousand voices narrowing to a single question:

*Who lives?*

I opened my eyes in the dark and understood what they were asking me to do.

Through the bones. Not through the snow—snow is water, and water tells me nothing—but through the bones scattered through the drift, the dead packed dense around the living like gravel around gold. Every soldier buried out there was wearing or carrying bone. Belt toggles carved from ox knuckle. Prayer beads. Dice. A fingerbone sewn into a glove against frostbite, an old mountain custom nobody remembered starting. The dead could feel them, the way I felt a woman's femur in my hands in Hollowmere and heard her salt-cache whispering under the floorboards.

And if I let myself listen—truly listen, with everything, holding nothing back for safety or shame—I could find them. Heartbeats. Small stubborn drums in the vast white silence.

I had spent thirty years keeping this gift folded up small as a prayer cloth, hidden even from myself. Lying in the crushing dark with the dead pressed close as family, I unfolded it all the way for the first time.

The world lit up.

Not with light—with *presence*. Dozens of points of warmth scattered through the cold mass above and around me, each one ringing in the chorus of the dead like a struck bell in a cathedral: *here, here, here, here*. Some strong. Some fluttering. One, near the surface on my left, already slowing, and the dead nearest to it keening softly, *too late, too late, oh, too late*—

No. Not today.

I dug.

It is strange what the body can do when the mind has surrendered its last pretense. I clawed upward through packed snow with bare hands until my fingers found open air—a seam where the drift had arched over the rock wall—and hauled myself out into a world gone utterly white and utterly still. The sun sat low and indifferent over the western peaks. Where the column had been, where forty men and a road and three hundred years of history had been, there was only a smooth pale scar, half a mile long, smoking faintly with snow-dust.

Somewhere under it: twenty-six heartbeats. I counted them the way you count coins you're about to bet your life on.

Fourteen were close to the surface. I went after those first, running along the scar, plunging my arms elbow-deep, following the pull in my own bones like a dowser following water. The first man I pulled out was young, red-haired, someone's clerk—I'd seen him copying Voss's manifests at the pass gate. He vomited snowmelt onto my knees and stared at me with eyes like a startled owl and said nothing at all, because there was nothing yet to say.

The second and third were together, a sergeant and a mule driver wedged in an air pocket the dead had guided me to as precisely as a map. By the time I'd dug them free my hands had stopped bleeding—they'd gone past bleeding into a kind of numb burning—and other survivors were stumbling out of the drift on their own, coughing, shouting names.

Aldery emerged fifty yards down the scar, helmetless, one arm hanging wrong, roaring for a muster count. Voss stood apart on a shelf of rock with her grey cloak torn and her dark hair loose for the first time since Hollowmere, and she was not looking at the buried column.

She was looking at me.

Because I had stopped pretending. That was the thing. I had spent four days in this company being the quiet carver, the hired guide, the man who spoke little and noticed less, and in the space of twenty minutes I had walked straight to seven buried men in a featureless field of snow and dug each one out as though the mountain itself had whispered me directions. Because it had.

"Carver!" Aldery limped toward me, face grey with pain and something else. "How? How are you finding them?"

I had a lie ready. I always had a lie ready; lies were the trade I'd plied longer than carving. *Lucky guesses. The drift settles unevenly—air pockets form along the rock. Any mountain man knows it.*

Twenty-six heartbeats. Twelve still buried. Two fading.

"The bones," I said.

My voice came out rough, ruined from snow and smoke of breath, but steady. Steadier than it had any right to be.

"There are bones all through this drift. Old ones. Battle-dead, from the Dragon War. I—" I looked at my own bleeding hands, and made the choice I had been making by inches since the crossroads, since the neighbor they dragged away screaming my name, since the first crack in the dam. "I hear them."

The words fell into the silence like stones into a well.

"I'm a bone-hearer," I said. "That's how I'm finding your men."

* * *

What happened next did not happen the way the law said it should happen, and I think that is why I am alive to write of it—for surely someone will ask me to write of it someday, if any of us live to see paper again.

The law said the men should seize me. The law said Aldery should bind my wrists and Voss should produce her bell and the column should hang me from the nearest dead tree as soon as one could be found above the treeline, and every soldier present would go home to his village and sleep soundly, having done his duty.

Instead, twelve men were still under the snow, and the law had no shovel deep enough.

"Twelve left," I said. "Two are fading fast. We can argue about sanctification when they're breathing, or we can dig now. Choose quickly."

Nobody moved. The red-haired clerk was staring at me with his owl-eyes. The sergeant I'd pulled out had his hand on his sword-hilt, but he wasn't drawing it—he was gripping it the way a drowning man grips a rail, and his face was doing something complicated, faith and gratitude tearing at each other like dogs over a bone.

It was Voss who broke it. She descended from her rock shelf with slow, deliberate steps, and the men parted for her, and she stopped an arm's length away, and her eyes—grey as the bell-metal—went over my face like a reader going over a page she means to memorize.

"You lied to me," she said quietly. "At the ford. I asked whether you'd ever handled remains of the gifted, and you said the gift skips families like yours entirely, and you almost believed it yourself. You lie beautifully, Marguerite Hartwell." She knew my name. Of course she knew my name; she had probably known it since Hollowmere. "Or you did."

"And now?" My heart was hammering, and I knew—God help me, I knew she could hear it, or sense it, or feel it through whatever the bell had taught her about the bodies of the gifted.

"Now you're digging my soldiers out of a mountain," she said. "So the bell can wait." She turned to the column, and her voice changed, became the voice that had executed a man at the crossroads without blinking: "Shovels and scabbard-blades. You follow the carver's marks and you dig where he digs, and you don't stop until he says stop. Move!"

They moved. And here is the thing I will never be able to untangle, the knot at the center of everything that followed: they moved *because of me*. Not because of her authority alone. Because Sergeant Bray—whose name I learned only later, gasping, from his own lips as I pulled him free—looked at me with his ruined face and said, "Where next, carver?" and meant it. Meant it the way you mean things when the law is very far away and the snow is very close and the man standing in front of you is the only person alive who knows where your friends are breathing.

I led them. God forgive me, I led them like a general.

The dead showed me everything. They had no reason to love the living—these were soldiers' bones, men who had died fighting men while dragons burned the sky above them both—but they loved *rescue*, the way sailors love harbors, and they crowded around each buried heartbeat and rang it out for me like a beacon: *here, here, deeper, left, careful of the ice lens, dig there*. I called positions until my voice gave out and then I marked them by driving my knife into the snow and letting the men read the angle of the blade. Digging parties fanned out along the scar. The mule driver's brother wept openly as he shoveled. Nobody sang the hymns against witchcraft. Nobody spat.

Two we reached too late. A pikeman named Corvel, and a boy of maybe seventeen whose name I refused to learn because learning it would have meant carrying it. The dead wrapped themselves around those two fading sparks and eased them out, gently, the way you cup a candle flame against the wind, and I felt them go, and I stood in the snow with blood freezing on my hands and let myself feel it, because thirty years of feeling nothing had bought the world nothing at all.

Ten saved, besides myself. Ten men alive who should have been dead.

When the last one was out—an older soldier, grey-bearded, who shook my hand with both of his and could not speak—the light was failing and the camp was being raised on the rock shelf above the scar, and I sat down at the edge of the drift, exhausted past thought, and found Voss standing over me with the Chirurgeon's Bell cradled in her arms like a sleeping child.

"It didn't ring," she said. "All day. Through all of that. It hasn't rung since the ford."

"Perhaps it's broken."

"Perhaps," she said, in a tone that made clear she considered that possibility roughly as likely as the mountain being broken, "it's listening to something else in you. Something older than the gift." She crouched, so that our eyes were level, and the bell between us caught the last red light of the sun and held it, and I swear on Brannoc's name that the thing *trembled*, once, faintly, like a dog hearing a whistle no human ear can catch. "Tomorrow we reach the high shoulder of Caerlum. And tomorrow, carver, you and I are going to have a conversation about what your ancestors built up there—and why my relic is more afraid of it than I am."

She rose and walked away toward the fires, and behind her, faint as frost forming, the bell hummed a single low note that none of the soldiers seemed to hear.

But the dead heard it. All ten thousand of them, in the bones beneath the snow, went quiet as held breath.

*She's right,* they whispered. *You must know before the gate. You must know what your family did.*
