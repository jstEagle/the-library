## 11. Sanctification

The tent smelled of tallow and wet wool, and Seraphine Voss had been awake for thirty hours when she finally gave the order she had been avoiding since sundown.

"Bring him."

The soldier—Corporal Ashe, a broad-faced man from the river provinces who had not met her eyes since the avalanche—hesitated at the tent flap. "Inquisitor. The men are asking. About what they saw."

"What they saw," Voss said, "was a man digging in the snow."

"With his hands on a dead man's ribs, Inquisitor. Singing to them." Ashe's jaw worked. "He pulled Hallow out of a drift that had already killed him twice over. Pulled him out by *listening*."

"He will be dealt with according to protocol." She heard her own voice do what it always did: flatten, cool, become an instrument rather than a throat. It was a skill she had spent fifteen years perfecting, and lately it felt less like mastery than like suffocation performed slowly enough to pass for breathing.

Ashe left. A minute later the flap opened again, and Marguerite Hartwell—the carver, the false-named man, the hearer—stepped into her firelight with his wrists already offered.

That was the first thing that unsettled her. He did not wait to be seized. He came as if he had read the order before she signed it, and he stood there in his snow-crusted coat with the patience of a man who had rehearsed this moment his whole life and found it, in the event, almost boring.

"You could have run," she said. "Half my column was asleep. The other half owes you their lives."

"I know." His accent was lowland smuggler's cant smoothed over with something finer underneath, the way river stones smooth over iron. "Running would waste the night. And we haven't got many of those left, have we, Inquisitor?"

*We.* She let that pass without answering, because answering meant agreeing that there was a *we*, and agreement was the first crack in any wall.

"Sit."

He sat on the camp stool across from her, folding himself down with a craftsman's economy of motion. Up close, in decent light, he looked younger than the mountain had made him seem, and older than his hands. There were fine white scars across his fingers, dozens of them, the signature of a blade held daily for years. Carver's hands. Bone-carver's hands.

She set the Chirurgeon's Bell on the table between them.

It did not ring. It never rang for him anymore—or rather, it had stopped ringing the moment he stopped hiding, which was either a flaw in the relic or an indictment of everything she believed about how the gift worked. The bell sat there in its lead-lined cradle like a sleeping bird, and Voss hated it a little for its silence, because she had built her whole life inside its sound.

"Do you know what sanctification is?" she asked.

"I've heard the word." He turned his wrists over, examining the manacles the corporal had locked on. Old iron, salt-pitted. "I've heard it done, too. From across the square, mostly. They burn the herbs first so the screaming smells sweeter."

"That is what happens to hearers who resist."

"And to ones who don't?"

"They are purified," she said. "Their gift is burned out of them. Some survive it."

"Some." He smiled, and it was not a cruel smile, which made it worse. "You've seen one. One survivor. I can tell, because you said *some survive* instead of *most*, and you're too precise a woman to waste a word." He leaned back. "Ask your questions, Inquisitor. The men are watching the tent. If you're gentle with me, they'll wonder why. If you're cruel, they'll hate you for it. Either way, you've already lost something tonight. So let's not pretend this is about protocol."

Voss had broken harder men than this with less effort than it took to sharpen a pen. She had once spent four days dismantling a hedge-priest who could blister skin with a psalm, peeling his certainty away layer by layer until he wept into her ledger and confessed every name he'd ever sheltered. She knew the craft the way Marguerite knew bone: where to cut, where to leave the grain whole, how much pressure the material could bear before it told you everything or nothing.

And she knew, sitting here in the tallow-smell and the wind-flap of the canvas, that none of it would work on this man, and that the reason it wouldn't work was the most dangerous discovery she had made in fifteen years of hunting:

Because everything he might confess, she already half-believed.

"Tell me about the bones in the pass," she said instead.

His eyebrows rose—a flicker of genuine surprise, quickly mastered. "That wasn't the first question you planned."

"No."

"But it's the right one." He was quiet a moment. Outside, the wind worried the guy-lines, and somewhere down the column a horse complained in its sleep. "You want to know why the dead in the defile didn't speak of dragons as enemies."

"The bell hummed," Voss said. "For three hundred yards of that pass, the Chirurgeon's Bell hummed like a struck wineglass, and then it stopped, and you nearly dropped your pack, and you want me to believe that's coincidence."

"I don't want you to believe anything." He said it simply, without defiance. "Belief is your trade, Inquisitor, not mine. But I'll tell you what I heard, and you can do with it what your office requires."

"Then tell me."

"There were ten thousand of them in those stones." His voice changed—not deeper, but *wider*, as though he were no longer speaking from his own chest but from some larger chamber behind it. "Not soldiers only. Farriers and cooks and boys who carried water, and women who followed the baggage trains, and a king's herald with a voice like a cracked bell. They died at Caerlum Pass eleven hundred years ago, in the war your histories call the Scourging of Wyrms." He paused. "They call it something else."

"What."

"*The Breaking of the Friendship.*" He let the words sit between them like a coal on the table. "They fought beside the dragons, Inquisitor. Wing to shield-wall. There's a sergeant in that stone—Bren of the Ashford line—who spent his last hour under the shadow of a dragon's wing while it burned a flanking company off the ridge above him. He died thinking the dragon had saved him. He died *grateful*." Marguerite's eyes came up to hers, grey and steady. "Your church says the dragons warred against mankind until the blessed ancestors drove them down. The dead say the war was started by the same hands that ended it. And they say it very quietly, because they've been saying it for eleven hundred years and no one living could hear them until someone like me walked through."

The fire popped. Voss became aware that her hands were flat on the table, braced, as if the tent had begun to tilt.

"That is heresy," she said. "That is the oldest and most settled heresy there is. The Wyrm-heresy. It was burned out of the world before the Church had bells."

"Yes," Marguerite agreed pleasantly. "Before the Church had bells. Isn't that interesting? You'd think the victors would write the history *after* the war. Unless the writing was the war."

"Words," she said. "A hearer's words. Do you know how many gifted prisoners have fed me beautiful stories? I had a woman in Corvane swear the dead told her the crown prince was a changeling. I had a man recite a genealogy proving he was descended from the last dragonkings, right up until the bell proved he was simply mad." She reached out and touched the Bell's cold casing, needing the contact the way another woman might need a knife within reach. "This does not lie. This has never lied. It rang for you in the pass, and it rang for you in Hollowmere, and whatever story you tell me, the metal remembers the truth of what you are."

"It does ring for me," he agreed. "That part's true."

"And yet it's silent now."

"Is it?" He tilted his head. "Put your hand flat on it. Not the cradle. The casing itself."

She almost refused. Then, because refusing would mean admitting the request frightened her, she lifted the Bell from its lead cradle—and felt it. Not heard; *felt*. A vibration so faint it lived below hearing, a tremor in the bronze like the memory of a note, steady as a second pulse.

"It's humming," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"It hasn't stopped since the pass," Marguerite said. "It rings for me, yes. But listen to the shape of it. Your relic doesn't detect the gift, Inquisitor. It detects resonance—it was built to find people whose bones carry echoes, isn't that what the old texts say? Bone-hearers ring because we're tuned to the dead." He raised his chained hands slightly, apologetic. "But I'm not ringing. The *pass* is ringing. Through me. Ten thousand voices, still singing the same song they died singing, and your bell can't tell the difference between the singer and the song."

Voss set the Bell down with great care, as if it had suddenly become an argument she was losing.

"This is exactly the kind of thing a condemned man says," she pointed out.

"I know. That's the elegant horror of your office, isn't it? Anything I say is evidence, and silence is evidence, and the truth is only admissible if it agrees with you." He shrugged. "So don't take my word. Take theirs. You can't—but I can. Ask me anything the dead in that pass would know. Anything. A name, a wound, a debt, a dying word. Test me the way you test a coin."

"Why would I bother? Even if you answer truly, it proves nothing but that you're gifted, which the bell has already sworn."

"Ah." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "No. It proves something much worse. It proves that what I hear is *real*. Because if I can give you verifiable facts out of eleven-hundred-year-old bones—facts no living person knows, facts we can dig for, weigh, match against spearheads in your own reliquaries—then the next thing I tell you isn't a hearer's fancy. It's testimony. And testimony, Inquisitor, even heretical testimony, has to be answered."

* * *

She tested him until the third candle drowned in its own wax.

It went the way drowning goes: gradually, then all at once.

He gave her Bren of the Ashford line's full muster-roll, forty names in marching order, and described a broken molar in the sergeant's jaw that Voss later verified herself by lamplight at the field surgeon's table of recovered remains—a skull fragment, ancient, brown as tea, with a molar split longways by some catastrophic blow. He gave her the herald's dying words, which were a name: *Ilvaine*, repeated nine times, and when she checked the expedition's copy of the royal annals, Ilvaine was listed as the queen-consort of the era, unmentioned in any account of the pass. He gave her the position of a cache of dragon-scale shields buried in a side scree—"the sergeant ordered them hidden rather than melted, he thought the war would turn and they'd come back for them"—and marked it on Finch's map with a carver's neat hand, and did not ask her to promise she'd dig.

"You're not doing this to save yourself," she said at some black hour, realizing it aloud. "You know I can verify all of it and hang you anyway. Verification isn't acquittal. It's just better paperwork."

"No," he agreed. "I'm doing it because you're going to Caerlum, and when you get there, you're going to need to believe something impossible. I'm building the foundation now, while you still have the luxury of doubting me."

"And what is the impossible thing?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the sky was going the colour of dishwater, and frost ferns had grown up the inside of the tent canvas overnight, in patterns too regular to be natural, reaching down from the peaks like fingers.

"When we reach the vault," he said, "you'll see it. And you'll understand why the dead sent me ahead of them."

"I don't take orders from the dead."

"No," he said gently. "But you take orders from the crown, and the crown takes orders from fear, and fear takes orders from the thing in the mountain. We're all somebody's instrument, Inquisitor. The only question worth asking is whether we ever get to choose our song."

She should have ended it there. Fifteen years of discipline said *end it there*: bind the testimony, seal the tent, sleep two hours, ride on. Protocol was not a cage, she had told novices at the Sanctum; protocol was armour, and armour is what lets you walk into fire and come out with your soul intact.

But her soul had been walking into fire for fifteen years and she was beginning to suspect it came out less intact each time, and that the armour had been slowly, patiently, eating the woman inside it.

* * *

Near dawn she asked the question she had promised herself she wouldn't.

"The neighbor," Voss said. "In Hollowmere. The woman they took on the crossroads day. She screamed your name."

Something crossed his face then—the first true expression she'd seen from him all night, and it was grief, worn smooth as driftwood, the kind that has been handled so long it no longer cuts.

"Tamsin Reeve," he said. "She cured hams and sang off-key and hid a deaf nephew from the census for six years. I warned her. I carved her husband's memorial plaque and I put a warning into the grain where only she'd look—in the tooling of the border, a pattern our kind used to use. *Go north. Go quiet.*" He flexed his scarred fingers. "She stayed. Her sister was ill. She stayed, and they took her, and she screamed my name because she thought I could stop it." He looked up. "Could I have, Inquisitor? Honestly. With your riders in the square and your decree nailed to the crossroads. Could I have stopped it?"

"No," Voss said, and the word cost her more than she showed.

"No," he echoed. "And I know that, and I live with it, the way you live with yours." He tipped his head toward the Bell. "How many, Seraphine Voss?"

Her given name, spoken plainly, in the dead hours, landed like a slap. "That is not—"

"How many have you sanctified? Burned out. Or burned up." His voice held no accusation. That was the unforgivable part. It held the tone of a man asking another man the depth of a shared wound. "Fifteen years at the work. I make it sixty, seventy, if you were diligent. Were you diligent?"

"Diligence is my vow."

"That's not what I asked."

The candles were dead. The fire was coals. Somewhere beyond the canvas, the eastern sky was turning the grey-white of old scars, and the peaks around them had gone strange—she had noticed it yesterday and refused to notice it properly—furred with frost at altitudes that should have been bare rock, the glacier lines creeping downslope like rising water in reverse.

"Ninety-one," Voss heard herself say. The number left her like a tooth. "Sixty-one survived the purification. Thirty died under it. Eleven of those I later determined were innocent of the gift entirely—the Bell was misread, or misused, or—" She stopped.

"Or it was ringing for something else," Marguerite finished softly. "Something under the gift. Something older. You've wondered. I've watched you wonder since the pass."

"I have executed people on that bell's authority."

"Yes."

"And you sit there in irons and comfort me."

"No," he said. "I sit here in irons because the alternative was worse, and I think you know it. I'm not comforting you, Inquisitor. I'm recruiting you. Slowly. Over a whole night, the way you'd carve something delicate—you don't force the grain, you persuade it." He almost smiled. "The dead told me you'd come. Did I never mention that? Before the pass, before Hollowmere—they've been murmuring about you for months. *The hunter with the bell in her hand and the crack in her heart. She'll hear us eventually. They all do, the honest ones. It's only the liars who die deaf.*"

The tent flap moved. Corporal Ashe stood there with dawn behind him and an expression Voss recognized because she had trained it into him: duty, holding terror at arm's length.

"Inquisitor. Dispatch rider from the south road. Royal seal." He swallowed. "And the men are asking about the frost, ma'am. It's coming down the valley. The villages—Finch says the map shows three villages on our route and there's no smoke from any of them."

Voss rose. Her body felt like a borrowed thing, stiff and distant.

"Protocol," she said to Marguerite Hartwell, because she needed to say it to someone, "requires that I arrest you. Requires that I transport you to the nearest Sanctum for formal sanctification. Requires that I file your testimony—which I have taken down in my own hand, all of it—as the delusions of a condemned heretic." She picked up the manacle chain and, with a gesture that would have scandalized the entire history of her office, wrapped it around her own wrist as well, linking them together, the slack pooled on the table between them like a small grey snake.

"So this is what we'll do instead," she said. "You stay chained—to me, so that no one else touches you, and so that if you lie to me from this moment forward, we fall together. You guide this column to Caerlum, and you show me what's in that vault. And if the dead have lied to you—if there is an enemy behind that door instead of a truth—then I will sanctify you myself, on this mountain, with my own hands, and I will sleep afterward."

"And if they haven't lied?"

She looked at the window of grey light, and the frost coming down the valley like a tide, and the empty villages with no smoke.

"Then God help us both," said the Inquisitor, "because I'll have to decide whose traitor to be. Move out in one hour. Both of us."
