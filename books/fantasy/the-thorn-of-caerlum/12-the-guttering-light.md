## 12. The Guttering Light

Ellery Finch had spent thirty years learning to read the world as a series of lines, and for thirty years the world had rewarded him for it. Coastlines, contours, the patient mathematics of rivers — all of it could be drawn, measured, made to hold still on vellum. He had believed, with the quiet arrogance of his profession, that anything which could be mapped could be mastered.

Then he had come north of the Thornfell passes, and the sky itself began to lie to him.

It happened first at a place called Wrenlow's Rest, a huddle of stone cottages in the shadow of the high peaks where the expedition stopped to water the horses. Finch stood at the edge of the village square with his field kit under one arm, sketching the ridgeline out of habit more than necessity, when he noticed it: the light was wrong. Not dimmed the way an overcast day dims — there wasn't a cloud worth the name from horizon to horizon. Dimmed the way a candle dims when someone's hand is cupped around it. The northern sky, that clean hard blue he'd charted on a dozen expeditions, had gone the color of pewter, then the color of old lead, and the mountains beneath it stood black against it like teeth.

He checked his instruments twice. The sun sat exactly where it should sit for the hour and the season. The light simply refused to arrive.

"Cartographer." Voss's voice behind him, clipped as ever. "You're staring."

"I'm measuring, Inquisitor." He turned, keeping his tone dry, because dryness was the last tool he had left. "And what I'm measuring is that we've lost a full shade of daylight since yesterday, and no weather I know of accounts for it."

Voss looked north. She said nothing for a long moment, and Finch — who had learned to read silences the way other men read maps — saw something pass across her face that was not doubt exactly. It was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she did not want to finish.

"The fire," she said at last. "Caerlum. It's failing faster than the crown's projections."

*The crown's projections.* Finch filed the phrase away the way he filed everything now, every oddity, every contradiction, into the growing atlas of things wrong with this expedition. A royal survey to assess a dying flame did not require projections. Projections required prior data. Prior data required that someone, somewhere, had been watching Caerlum fail for a very long time — and had told no one.

"Curious," he said mildly, "that the throne can project the death of a fire most scholars insist has burned since before the kingdom had a name."

Voss's eyes cut to him, sharp as a compass needle. "Choose your next words carefully, Master Finch."

"I always do." He smiled, thin and professional. "It's why they took my commission and not my tongue."

She left him standing in the failing light, and Finch went back to his sketch, and drew the ridge three times before he got the shading right, because his hands would not quite steady.

* * *

The villages emptied the way water leaves a lock — gradually, then all at once.

Wrenlow's Rest still had people: a smith, a handful of grandmothers, children who stared at the column of soldiers with the frank hunger of the underfed. But two days further north, at a crossroads hamlet called Ashen Brack, they found doors standing open and bread gone green on the tables. A loom with thread still knotted mid-pattern. A cradle by a cold hearth, empty, its blanket thrown back as if the leaving had been sudden — or as if the family had taken only what they could carry and left the rest for God or the king, whichever came first.

Finch walked through the abandoned houses with his notebook open, cataloguing, because cataloguing was how he kept the fear at arm's length. Dates scratched into doorframes. A tally of some kind on a barn wall — notches, hundreds of them, counting down toward nothing he could identify. In the chapel, someone had torn the royal proclamation off the notice board and nailed up instead a child's drawing: a mountain with a red heart inside it, and the heart crossed out.

Beneath it, in a laboring hand: *THE LIGHT IS LEAVING AND THE KING DID NOTHING.*

"You're collecting evidence of sedition?" Lieutenant Hask, the young officer whose nerves had been fraying since the pass, stood in the chapel doorway. His voice tried for contempt and landed somewhere nearer pleading.

"I'm collecting evidence of observation," Finch said. "These people watched the sky longer than we have. They noticed what we're only now admitting." He tapped the child's drawing. "A heart crossed out. They believe the mountain is dying. Not the fire in it — the mountain itself. That's a distinction worth noting."

Hask frowned. "Fire dies. Mountains don't."

"No," Finch agreed quietly. "They don't. Unless what keeps them warm is being taken away."

He did not say the rest of it — the thought that had been coiling in him since the pass, since the defile full of battle-bones, since the carver Marguerite had gone white-faced listening to voices no one else could hear. He did not say that a land without dragon-fire was a land without spring, that the frost creeping down the peaks each morning reached lower than the day before, that he had personally recorded lichen dying at altitudes where lichen had survived every winter in cartographic memory. He did not say that the map he carried in his breast pocket — the old map, the impossible map, the one drawn in a hand that matched the bone-carver's strokes stroke for stroke — showed Caerlum not as a peak but as a *hearth*, labeled in a dead language he'd finally begun to translate:

*Here burns the warmth of the world. Guard it, or the long cold comes for your grandchildren.*

Finch closed his notebook. Outside, the northern sky had darkened another shade, and the soldiers were lighting torches at what should have been midday.

* * *

That evening, in the lee of a ruined waystation, Marguerite found him.

The carver moved quietly, as he always did, settling onto the stone beside Finch with the ease of a man who had spent his life making himself unremarkable. Up close, in the torchlight, Finch could see the change in him — the hollows under his eyes, the way his fingers kept moving against his knee, carving nothing, tracing patterns that matched, stroke for stroke, the lines on Finch's oldest map.

"They're talking about turning back," Marguerite said. No preamble. He never used them. "Hask and half the men. They say the cold ahead will kill the horses."

"And you?"

"I hear the dead saying worse." He said it plainly now; there was no point in pretense between the two of them, not after the avalanche, not after the night Voss had chained him and questioned him until dawn. "Not just the war-dead anymore. Farmers. Grandmothers. People who died in their beds generations ago, all across the north. They're all saying the same thing." His fingers stilled. "*Faster than expected. Faster than expected.* Like a refrain. Whatever's happening at Caerlum, it's accelerating."

Finch unrolled the old map between them, weighting the corners with stones. In the torchlight the second route seemed almost to glow, that ancient hand winding up toward the sealed vault with a confidence no living surveyor could match.

"When I was disgraced," Finch said, "it was for insisting the old charts contradicted the crown's surveys. Everyone assumed I'd forged them — a failed man manufacturing evidence of his own importance." He laughed, short and bitter. "I nearly believed it myself. Then I met you, and your knife, and I understood that a hand can be taught by its ancestors across centuries without either party knowing it. Which means these lines aren't forgery. They're inheritance."

Marguerite studied the map. "What are you telling me, Finch?"

"I'm telling you what I told no one, because saying it aloud makes it real." Finch's voice dropped. "The vault isn't a vault. Look at the annotations — I've translated perhaps a tenth of them, but the same phrase recurs along every approach. *The keeping-place. The keeping-place. The keeping-place.* You keep things you intend to use again. You seal a tomb and walk away." He looked up at the darkening sky, at the stars winking out one by one above the peaks like lamps snuffed in a corridor. "Someone built that place to hold the fire the way a miser holds coin. And whatever mechanism holds it — it's failing. Not gently. Not on a schedule anyone planned. The frost on the peaks this morning reached the treeline. I measured it against my father's surveys from forty years ago. Forty years of records, and the ice has never once come down past the scree."

Marguerite was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had a strange doubled quality, as though something spoke through him and with him at once. "The dead have a word for it. They use it constantly now, near the high passes. *Cage.* They say *the cage is cracking* and *the cage is cold* and sometimes just — *the cage, the cage*, over and over, like a warning shouted down a well." He turned to Finch. "Your map says keeping-place. Their bones say cage. Both true, maybe. A place built to keep something — that becomes a cage the moment the keeper forgets why."

"And the keeper," Finch said, "is us. Is the crown. Is every generation that inherited the seal and called it safety." He rolled the map away, and his hands were shaking now, openly, and he found he no longer cared who saw. "I spent three years hating the men who stripped my commission. I wanted my name back. And now I stand here realizing the name doesn't matter, the commission doesn't matter, none of it matters, because in a month — perhaps less — the question won't be who governs this kingdom. It'll be whether anything in this kingdom grows again."

A shout went up from the picket line. Both of them turned.

Above the northern peaks, where Caerlum's summit should have been visible as a pale silhouette against the stars, the sky had changed. There was a darkness there now with texture to it, a bruised, swirling absence, and around its edges — Finch squinted, raised his spyglass with hands that would not obey — around its edges the air itself seemed to shimmer and thin, like heat-haze in reverse, like warmth being visibly siphoned away.

Through the glass, he caught it: a flicker. Deep in the mountain's throat, faint as a coal breathed upon, the fire pulsed — once, twice — and each pulse was weaker than the last, and between pulses the darkness pressed closer, like a fist closing.

"It's guttering," Finch whispered. "God help us. It's actually guttering."

Marguerite stood very still, head tilted, listening to a silence only he could inhabit. When he finally spoke, his voice was almost gentle, and it frightened Finch more than any shout could have.

"The dead have stopped whispering warnings. Do you understand? All the way up the pass, it was warnings — turn back, hurry, beware. Tonight they've gone quiet. All except one voice, one old voice, repeating a single sentence over and over." He looked at Finch with eyes that held ten thousand years of other people's grief. "*Come quickly. Come quickly. We cannot hold it alone.*"

* * *

Voss convened the officers' council by torchlight, and for the first time since Hollowmere, Finch watched her speak without certainty.

"The fire fails faster than projected," she told them, pacing before the ruined waystation's broken hearth. "The villages are empty. The frost descends daily. Every hour we deliberate, the margin narrows." She stopped. Turned. "We reach Caerlum's gate in four days if we push. Six if we spare the horses. I want honest counsel — will the column make six?"

Silence. Then Hask, of all people: "Inquisitor — with respect — make it for what? If the fire's dying, we're walking six days to watch a candle go out."

"We walk," Voss said, "because the alternative is to report to the throne that we turned back while the north froze. Because whatever else is true, gentlemen, the answer is in that mountain, and we are the ones sent to find it." Her gaze swept them, and landed last on Finch, and lingered. "Master Finch. Your charts show approaches the crown's surveyors never recorded. Old routes. Sealed routes. If the main path is snowed under, can you find us another?"

Finch felt every eye on him, and understood that this was the hinge — the moment his disgrace became either his damnation or his purpose. He thought of the second route, the ancient hand, the annotations he had barely begun to translate. He thought of the child's drawing in the Ashen Brack chapel, the crossed-out heart.

"I can find you the road," he said slowly, "but I'll warn you what the road says. Those annotations aren't instructions for visitors. They're instructions for *keepers*. Whoever drew that map expected the people following it to open something, tend something, perhaps—" he hesitated on the edge of it, then stepped over, because the hour was too late for caution— "*free* something. The crown sent us to assess a resource. This map suggests we were sent to open a door. Someone in the palace knows more about Caerlum than they've told any of us."

The torches crackled. Somewhere in the dark, a horse screamed at the cold.

Voss regarded him for a long, unreadable moment. "If that's true, Master Finch, then the man who proves it will be either a hero or a traitor. Possibly both." She turned back to the fire. "We move at first light. The fast route. Four days."

As the council broke, Marguerite drifted to Finch's side, and for a moment they stood together looking north at the bruised and darkening sky, at the place where the last fire in the world flickered like a drowning star.

"Four days," Marguerite murmured. "The dead say we should have come four years ago."

Below them, in the valley, the frost crept down through the empty fields, silvering the abandoned harvest, reaching patiently toward the roots of everything alive. And high above, behind the sealing dark of Caerlum, something ancient pulsed again — weaker, Finch was almost sure, than it had pulsed an hour before.

He opened his notebook and wrote the only entry that mattered, in a hand that trembled and did not care:

*Day 31. The light is leaving. We are the last ones coming.*
