## 13. Caerlum's Gate

The mountain did not want them there.

Marguerite felt it in his knees before he felt it in his bones — the wrongness of the ground itself, a deep reluctance in the stone, as if the whole mass of Caerlum had drawn its shoulders up around its summit and was waiting, patient as only mountains are patient, for them to give up and go home. The path narrowed. The frost thickened. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable now, he could hear them: the voices layered in the rock like rings in old timber, each generation pressed against the last.

*They come again,* said a voice near the surface, thin and papery. *The ones with the iron.*

*Let them climb,* said another, older, deeper. *We built it well. We built it to outlast their wanting.*

And beneath that one, so far down that Marguerite had to stop walking and press his bare palm flat against a boulder to be sure he wasn't imagining it, something else. Not a voice at all. A warmth. A slow, vast attention turning over in its sleep, the way a hearth-fire turns when someone opens a door in a distant room.

"Carver." Voss's voice came sharp from behind him. "Why have we stopped?"

He straightened. His hand left the stone reluctantly, and the cold where his palm had been felt like a wound. "Resting point," he lied. "The pass above is exposed. Better to cross it at dawn."

It was not better to cross it at dawn. It was better to never cross it at all, and every dead thing in the mountain agreed with him, and none of them could tell him why.

Behind him the column waited — what remained of it. Eleven soldiers, hollow-eyed from the avalanche and the burial detail, from carrying Finch's body wrapped in canvas because Marguerite had refused to leave him on the mountain and Voss had refused to let Marguerite carry him alone. Two mules. One chained prisoner who was not treated like a prisoner anymore, because the men took turns walking beside him and would not meet Voss's eye about it. And Seraphine Voss herself, grey-lipped with cold, the Chirurgeon's Bell hanging silent at her belt, watching him the way she had watched him every hour since the avalanche — as though he were a door she had opened and could not close.

"The vault mouth is half a day further," Finch had told them all, weeks ago, in the lowlands, tracing the second route with fingers that shook. "There will be a gate. Black stone. No hinge, no handle, no seam you can find by touch. My map says it answers to bone-hearers. I don't know what that means. I don't think anyone living knows what that means."

Someone living knew. Marguerite had known since the defile, since the war-dead sang their impossible chorus of dragons fighting shoulder to shoulder with men. He had known more certainly since Finch died with the map spread across his chest and the ancestral confession copied out in eleven fair hands. He had spent the whole climb since listening to the sediment of voices in the stone, and they were getting louder, and they were saying things he was not ready to carry into daylight.

*The carver comes back,* whispered a woman's voice, old and amused and terribly sad. *After everything, a carver comes back.*

*

They made camp in the lee of a ridge that some ancient hand had cut into steps, though no road ran along it anymore. Marguerite sat apart, as he always did now, working a piece of bone between his fingers without carving anything — just holding it, letting the dead speak through it if they chose. It was sheep bone. Nothing in it. Sometimes nothing was a mercy.

Voss came and sat across the fire from him without asking. She did this now too. The soldiers pretended not to notice; pretending had become the column's chief occupation.

"You stopped on the trail today," she said. "You touched the rock."

"I rest my hands."

"You're a bad liar for a smuggler."

"I'm a good liar," Marguerite said. "I've just stopped bothering with you."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and died there. She looked up at Caerlum's summit, black against the bruised violet sky. "Tell me what you hear."

It was not an order. That was the strangest thing about these night conversations — she asked, and he answered or didn't, and neither of them named what was happening between them, which was that an inquisitor was taking instruction from a bone-hearer and calling it interrogation.

"They're layered," Marguerite said finally. "Like silt. Like flood-mud. Each generation of them pressed down on the last, and the oldest ones are deepest, and the oldest ones—" He turned the sheep bone over. "The oldest ones aren't sorry."

Voss went very still. "Not sorry for what?"

"That's the part they won't say yet." He looked at her across the fire. "They keep saying *wait until the gate*. Whatever's in this mountain, Inquisitor, your Church taught you to kill people like me over a lie, and the lie is older than the Church, and I don't think either of us is prepared for how old."

Above them, behind the sealing dark, something pulsed. Weaker than yesterday. All of them heard it now — not through any gift, but through the soles of their feet, through the water in their skins, through some animal sense that predated gifts and laws alike. A heartbeat in the world's chest, slowing.

* * *

The gate found them before they found it.

The path climbed through a field of scree and then simply ended at a wall of black stone that rose out of the mountainside like a tooth — smooth, seamless, taller than three men standing on each other's shoulders, and utterly unremarked by any of the maps Finch had ever drawn except the second one, the buried one, drawn in a hand that matched Marguerite's own. There was no door in it. There was no mark on it. There was nothing at all, except that when Marguerite came within ten paces of it, the voices inside the stone woke up all at once.

He went to his knees. He didn't decide to; his legs simply went, and the pack slid off his shoulders, and he knelt on the frozen ground with both palms pressed to the black stone while ten thousand years poured through him like water through a broken dam.

*—carved the first seal with our own fingerbones, that the lock might know us—*
*—my daughter asks why the fire must sleep and I tell her winter and she believes me, gods forgive me, she believes me—*
*—the third hearer tried to open it. Our own blood. We put his bones in the foundation. Do not speak of him. Never speak of him—*
*—it dreams of flight. Every year it dreams of flight and every year the cage holds and every year I hate myself more efficiently—*
*—we were not heroes. Write it somewhere. Carve it somewhere. We were thieves, we were hoarders, we took a living sun and called it an enemy so our children would not ask what we had done—*
*—the carver comes back. After everything, a carver comes back—*

"Pull him back!" Someone was shouting. Hands on his shoulders, dragging. Marguerite fought them without knowing he was fighting, because the voices were still coming, deeper now, older, and the deepest layer of all was not words at all but a single sustained note of grief so vast it had gone past grief into geology—

—and underneath the grief, warm and slow and turning over, the other presence. The sleeper. It had noticed him.

It noticed him the way a fire notices an open window.

"No." He wrenched free of the hands, staggered upright, stood swaying with blood running from his nose across his lips. "No — don't pull me away. You'll want me closer in a minute. Trust me."

Voss stood in front of him, bell half-drawn, face white. "What happened to you?"

"It talked back." He wiped his nose with the back of his wrist and laughed, and the laugh came out cracked and strange even to his own ears. "All of them. Every ancestor I ever carved a keepsake from, every name in every grave I ever quieted — they're all in there, Inquisitor. The whole family. Pressed into the door like flowers in a book." He looked at the black stone with something that was not quite hatred and not quite love. "My grandmother's grandmother is in there. She's been waiting four hundred years for someone to knock."

The soldiers had formed a rough ring, spears out, facing not the wall but him. He couldn't blame them. He could smell the blood on his own face and feel the tremor in his own hands and know exactly what he looked like: the witch from the proclamations, kneeling at a demon door, bleeding from the eyes of his craft.

"The dispatch said the vault requires a bone-hearer," Voss said carefully. "Protocol is that you attempt entry under guard. If anything emerges—" she didn't finish it, and didn't need to. If anything emerged, the protocol was fire.

"Nothing will emerge," Marguerite said. "That's rather the whole tragedy of the place."

He walked toward the wall.

Up close, the black stone was not stone. He saw it now with his fingers before his eyes confirmed it — the fine grain, the dense polished weight, the way it drank the cold and gave nothing back. Bone. Not the bone of any creature that had ever lived in the world above, but bone nonetheless, fused and worked and grown together into a seal the size of a cliff-face. Someone had made this. Someone with his gift, generations ago, had taken the material of his own craft and built a cage door out of it, and then pressed their souls into it like mortar.

His ancestors had built the lock. His gift was the key. He had known it since the defile and it still landed in his chest like a dropped anvil.

He laid both hands flat against the surface and closed his eyes.

*I'm here,* he said, into the sediment, into the layers, down through papery voices and amused voices and grieving ones, down to the deepest stratum where the oldest dead kept their silence. *I'm the carver. I'm the one you've been waiting for. Open.*

For a long moment, nothing. The wind moved across the scree. Somewhere below, the pulse stuttered — once, twice, like a heart deciding.

Then the voices began to move.

He felt them shift the way silt shifts in a river current, layer sliding over layer, each generation of the dead passing the request downward like a bucket passed hand to hand down a well. *He's here. Wake. He's here. Wake.* Down and down and down, four hundred years of complicity handing his plea along, until it reached whatever lay at the bottom —

— and the bottom answered with a sound like a sigh four centuries long.

The wall opened.

There was no seam until there was one. A line of dull red light drew itself down the black stone, slow as a knife drawn through cloth, and then the two halves swung inward without sound, without wind, without any concession to physics at all, and the light of the last dragon-fire spilled out over the snow and fell across the faces of eleven exhausted soldiers, one chained inquisitor, and the carver who had opened it.

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. The light was gold and deep and moving like water, and it was dying — anyone could see that now, anyone could see the way it guttered at the edges, the way shadows pooled in places that should have blazed. A hearth going out. A sun being smothered.

"Saints' blood," whispered the sergeant, and crossed himself with the hand not holding his spear, and then, after a moment, crossed himself again with the other hand too.

* * *

Inside, the mountain was hollow.

They descended a stair cut into living rock, wide enough for six abreast, spiraling down into the gold light, and the stair was lined with niches, and the niches were full of bones. Not heaped — arranged. Each skeleton seated, facing inward, hands folded, skulls bowed toward the center of the mountain in an attitude of eternal vigil. Thousands of them. Generations of them. The cage-makers, keeping their own prison, their own sentence, their own shame, down through the long dark.

Marguerite counted the generations as he passed and stopped counting at forty.

At the bottom, the stair opened into a cavern so vast that the torchlight failed to find its ceiling, and there, filling the cavern floor like a fallen cathedral, stood the cage.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it — it was beautiful, wrought of the same black bone as the gate, rising in arches and ribs and interlocking spars into a dome half a league across, every joint carved, every surface worked with scenes: dragons and men plowing fields together, dragons carrying children on their backs, dragons breathing life onto frozen harvests. The artists had carved the truth into their own prison walls. They had lived with the truth every day of their lives, and built the lie anyway, and given the lie to their children, and their children's children, down forty generations to a proclamation nailed to a crossroads post in Hollowmere.

And in the center of the cage, coiled on a bed of ash, sleeping, small — impossibly, heartbreakingly small — lay the ancient enemy.

No bigger than a horse. Serpent-bodied, ember-scaled, its hide glowing with the same deep gold as the failing light, dimming and brightening with each slow breath like coals under ash. Its wings were folded tight against its flanks. Its head rested on the ground, eyes closed, and around it the forge-cage hummed a note so low it was felt rather than heard, and Marguerite understood with sudden terrible clarity that the hum was the cage drinking. Drinking the fire. Sipping it, century by century, storing it, hoarding it, feeding it into the black bone of the walls — the same black bone the crown wanted drained into a reliquary and carried away.

The last dragon-fire was not dying of age.

It was dying of theft.

"Oh," said the sergeant, and sat down abruptly on the stairs, spear clattering. No one reproached him. Half the others looked like they wanted to do the same.

Voss stood at the edge of the light with her chains rattling and her face lit gold, and Marguerite watched the doctrine die in her eyes — watched the ancient enemy, the devil of the proclamations, the justification for every sanctification she had ever performed, lying curled and small and starving on a bed of its own ash, caged by thieves, sung to by the dead, dreaming of flight.

Its eye opened.

One eye, lid sliding back like a shutter, revealing a depth of amber light in which Marguerite saw reflected — tiny, golden, unmistakable — himself, standing at the cage bars, and around him the ghosts of every carver who had ever touched this stone, forty generations of them, all looking out through the dragon's gaze at what they had done.

The dragon looked at Marguerite for a long time. It did not seem angry. That was somehow worse. It seemed — tired. It seemed like a thing that had been waiting so long to be understood that it had nearly stopped expecting it.

Then, without lifting its head, without opening its mouth, it spoke — not in air, but directly into the bones of everyone present, a voice felt in teeth and marrow, in the secret hollows of the ear:

*"Little key. You are late."*

Behind them, far above, the great gate began, slowly, grindingly, to swing shut.
