## 14. The Cage-Makers' Confession

The vault swallowed sound the way the mountain swallowed light.

Marguerite stood at the edge of the forge-cage with his palm still burning from the door, and for a long moment he could not move. Behind him, soldiers held lanterns high and said nothing—said nothing because there was nothing to say that would not have been blasphemy against everything they had been taught. Ahead of him, coiled in the heart of a lattice built from bones longer than church beams, slept an ember-dragon no larger than a mare.

It was dying. He could hear it without touching anything at all: a thin, labored pulse beneath the stone, like a heartbeat heard through three walls of a drowning house.

"Saints above," whispered one of the soldiers, a broad-shouldered man named Corvel who had not spoken since the gate opened. "That's it? That's the ancient enemy?"

"That's what they showed you," Marguerite said quietly.

Voss stood to his left, her chains on him slack between them, her face lit orange by the cage's failing glow. The Chirurgeon's Bell hung silent at her belt. She had not looked away from the dragon since they entered. "The dispatch said a beast of ruin," she said. "Scales like siege-shields. Breath that melted armies."

"The dispatch also said I was a plague." Marguerite flexed his fingers. His hands ached to carve; they always ached near old bone, but here the ache was a shout, a hunger, a thousand hands pressing his palms toward the lattice. "I need to touch it."

"You'll be guarded while you do."

"I'd expect nothing else, Inquisitor."

He crossed the vault floor. The stones were warm underfoot—not dragon-warm, not fire-warm, but the warmth of a thing that had been bled slowly for centuries and never allowed to cool entirely. As he walked, the voices began.

They always began as murmurs when he approached old bones, a tide-sound of the dead going about whatever the dead went about. But here the murmur rose fast, climbing through octaves of whisper until it was less a murmur than a congregation, hundreds of voices braided into one pressure behind his eyes—and beneath them, deeper, older, something vast turning over in its sleep and noticing him the way a sleeper notices rain.

*Bone-hearer,* the deep thing seemed to say, though it made no words. *At last. At last, at last.*

Marguerite stopped before the cage.

Up close, he could see what the lantern-light from behind had hidden: the lattice was not merely built of bone. It was *worked*. Every rib-length beam bore carving—dense, interlocking script that wound around the bone like ivy around a corpse. He knew the style the way he knew his own reflection, because it was his own reflection. It was the hand he used. It was the hand every hearer in Thornfell used, taught mother to child down forty generations: the flowing strokes, the hooked terminals, the little marks that were not letters but instructions.

His ancestors had carved this cage the way he carved keepsakes.

"Oh," he said aloud, very softly, and the word fell into the vault and did not echo, because the vault was already full.

He reached out and laid both palms flat against the nearest bone-beam.

The world inverted.

* * *

He was standing in Caerlum, but a Caerlum with its throat open—a valley of green terraces and rivers running silver, and above it all a sky that *burned*, gently, endlessly, with wings. Dragons. Dozens of them, riding thermals above the peaks, their scales catching sun like hammered bronze. And among the terraces, people worked beside them: hauling stone with dragon-assisted cranes, planting fields warmed by deliberate breath, children sliding down the flank of a sleeping elder who tolerated it with the weary patience of a grandmother.

There was fire everywhere and none of it was war.

*Marguerite.* A woman stood beside him in the vision, solid as memory, her hands stained with the same bone-dust that lived permanently in the creases of his knuckles. She wore the clothes of four hundred years ago and spoke with the accent of the old salt-roads, and he knew her instantly, the way you know your own jawline in a stranger's face. *You came further than we hoped. Listen, then. We were not permitted to write this down where any eye could find it, so we wrote it where only a hearer could reach.*

"I'm listening," he said, and his voice was his own voice, small in the vastness.

*We called it the Accord,* she said. *Man and wyrm, bound not by chain but by exchange. Their fire kept our winters short. Our craft kept their sleep long. For nine hundred years there was no richer peace under any sky.* Her face tightened. *And then my grandmother's generation asked themselves a question that has killed better things than dragons: why share?*

The valley blurred. Reformed. Now the terraces were scaffolding, and the scaffolding was rising around the largest of the dragons—an elder, bronze-black, its eyes half-lidded with trust—as smiths fitted great bands of carved bone across its wings.

*They told it the bands were medicine,* the woman said, and her voice cracked on it. *Rites of longevity. The elder was old, and vain about it, as elders are. It lay down willingly. It let us build the first ring.* Her hands curled into fists. *And when it understood—when it finally understood—it did not burn us. That is the worst of it, carver. It had nine hundred years of proof that we were worth sparing, and it spent its last free hour begging us instead. Asking what it had done. Asking who had turned. And my grandmother stood at the foot of the cage and said, "Nothing. You did nothing. We simply want more."*

Marguerite felt his stomach turn over. Around him the vision shifted again: the cage complete, the dragon diminished inside it, its fire drawn off through channels cut into the mountain's bones—channels that fed forge-towns, that heated palaces, that made the founders of certain families rich beyond the dreams of kings.

*The fire was wealth,* the woman said. *But wealth leaks. Wealth must be guarded. And the ones who might notice the theft, who might hear the dragon's grief through its own caged bones—we had created such people ourselves, in the centuries of partnership. Hearers. Craftsmen of the dead. So we did what cowards do. We invented sinners to hide our crime behind.*

The witch-hunts. He saw them bloom in the vision like rot spreading through bread: decrees drafted in candlelit rooms by men whose family crests he recognized—the same crests that now rode royal livery. Edicts declaring the gift an abomination. Rewards for denunciation. Sanctification pyres, generation after generation, each one pruning away another branch of the people most likely to walk up this mountain, lay a hand on these bones, and understand.

*Every hearer burned was a witness erased,* the woman said. *Every hearer driven underground was a witness scattered so thin they forgot what they were witnesses to. We reduced a covenant to a fairy tale, and the fairy tale to a bedtime warning, and within ten generations even we believed our own lie. Only the bones remember truly. Only the bones were ever honest.* She turned to him fully then, and her eyes were wet. *We are sorry. We are four hundred years of sorry, and we cannot die properly until someone hears all of it. You are the hearing, carver. Finish us.*

* * *

He came back to himself on his knees.

He didn't know how long. The lanterns had burned lower; Voss was crouched beside him, one hand hovering near his shoulder without landing, and half the soldiers had weapons drawn, and none of that mattered at all, because the voices were still with him—they had not released him, they were speaking *through* the contact of his palms on the bone, layer after layer, sediment upon sediment, each generation adding its confession to the last.

He heard a smith from the third century of the cage describe how the fire-channels were rationed, how whole villages froze in hard winters so that the capital's fountains could run warm.

He heard a hearer from the second century—the last one to reach the vault before the hunts closed—describe how she had been given a choice: join the cage-makers' order and keep the secret, or burn. She had chosen to keep the secret. She described, in a voice gone thin with shame, how she had personally written the test questions used to identify gifted children, and how she had told herself every night that keeping the secret kept the peace, and how the dragon's pulse under her feet had sounded, to her dying day, exactly like a person knocking politely on a door that would never be answered.

He heard the cage-makers themselves, the inner circle, arguing across decades. Some wanted to release it. Some argued the dragon would take vengeance. And one voice—dry, old, terribly reasonable—kept repeating the arithmetic: *the fire feeds forty thousand hearths; the dragon's grief feeds none; grief has never warmed anyone; open the ledger and close your heart.*

And beneath all of it, patient as bedrock, the ember-dragon's own voice. Not words. Just presence. Just the slow, immense attention of something that had been listening to its jailers' descendants confess for four hundred years, waiting for one of them to finally say it out loud where it could hear.

Marguerite lifted his head. His face was wet. He hadn't noticed crying.

"It wasn't a war," he said. His voice came out wrecked, sanded down. He said it louder, for the soldiers, for Voss, for the lanterns and the dark. "There was never a dragon war. There was a theft. Nine hundred years of peace, and our ancestors stole the other partner's fire to keep for themselves, and then they hunted their own children for four hundred years so no one would ever climb this mountain and tell the dragon we're sorry."

Silence. Corvel lowered his sword slowly, looking from the cage to Marguerite as if the two were suddenly the same problem.

Voss had gone very still. "Your ancestors," she repeated. "You're saying the crown's founding houses—"

"Were the cage-makers. Yes." He got to his feet, knees protesting, palms still pressed to the bone because some part of him refused to break the connection now that he finally understood what it was. "The witch-hunts weren't piety. They were housekeeping. They've been cleaning up evidence since before your order existed. Your bell, Inquisitor—it doesn't detect corruption." He looked at her, and saw the truth land in her the way it had landed in him, a shaft finding the gap in armor. "It detects proximity to the crime. Everyone with the gift descends from the people who knew. The gift isn't a curse. It's an inheritance of testimony. The crown hasn't been hunting witches for four hundred years. It's been hunting *witnesses*."

One of the younger soldiers made a small involuntary sound, like a man discovering the floor is thinner than he thought.

Marguerite turned back to the lattice. Up close, with the memory still bright in him, he could read the carvings now—not as instructions but as plea. Every hook and stroke was a sentence in the hearers' script, and the sentences were all variations of one message, repeated by generation after generation of guilty hands:

*Let whoever comes next be brave enough to undo this.*

And below the script, worn nearly smooth by centuries of the dragon's breathing, older marks. Dragon-marks. The elder's answer, carved from inside the cage with a claw-tip, over and over, in the years when it still had strength to scratch:

*I forgive. I forgive. I forgive.*

*If only you would ask.*

Marguerite's resolve arrived not like lightning but like dawn—gradual, total, undeniable. The fear he had carried since the crossroads, since the proclamation, since Brannoc's face on the mountainside—all of it reorganized itself around a single clean point. The secret could reignite the war only if it stayed a secret. Kept, it was a fuse. Spoken, it was an apology, and apologies do not start wars; they end the long postponement of them. His ancestors had chosen the hoard and called it peace. He could choose otherwise.

"Finch," he said.

"He's topside with the rear watch," Voss said.

"Get him down here. Get him down here with every map he owns." Marguerite finally pulled his hands from the bone, and the loss of contact rang through him like a snapped string. He turned to face them all, bone-dust glowing faintly on his palms. "Because this confession can't live in my head. My head can burn, or hang, or be sanctified. It needs to go somewhere the crown can't carve out. It needs to go onto paper, onto copies, onto maps distributed to every town from here to the coast. If forty thousand people know the truth, then the truth stops being treason and becomes history—and no throne alive can arrest history."

"And if the crown moves first?" Voss asked quietly.

"Then we'd best not give it time." He looked back at the cage, at the small dark coil of it, at the ember pulsing like a lamp about to fail. The frost creeping down the northern peaks, the emptying villages, the guttering sky—all of it made sense now. The fire had been rationed for four hundred years, and the ration had run out, and the men who built the ledger had never once considered what happened when the account ran dry. "The dragon is dying, Inquisitor. Not the fire being stolen—the *dragon*. And when it goes, everything it warmed goes with it."

Voss's hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the Chirurgeon's Bell at her belt. Her thumb moved over its casing in a slow, unconscious arc, the way a believer touches a relic—or a woman touches a wound.

"The dispatch," she said. "The one waiting at the relay station. I haven't opened it yet." Something moved behind her eyes—dread, or foresight, or the beginning of a decision she could already feel herself making. "I think I'm afraid to."

"Open it," Marguerite said.

She held his gaze for three full seconds. Then she drew the sealed tube from her coat, broke the crown's wax with her thumbnail, and unrolled the paper in the failing amber light of the cage.

Her face, as she read, went through recognition, denial, and a cold final clarity, in the space of a breath.

"Marguerite," she said, and it was the first time she had ever used his true name, "we need to talk about what the throne wants done with the fire."
