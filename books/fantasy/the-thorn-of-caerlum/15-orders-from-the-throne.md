## 15. Orders from the Throne

The hawk came at dawn, screaming down through the frost-fog like something escaped from a nightmare, and Seraphine Voss knew before she cut the message tube from its leg that it carried her death in one form or another.

She had slept badly. She always slept badly now. The vault at Caerlum was two days' march below them still, and every night since the avalanche she had lain awake listening to the wind move through the pass, and hearing—not imagining, *hearing*—a low note underneath it. The Chirurgeon's Bell hung silent on her belt where it had hung for eleven years, and yet some part of her had begun to ring in sympathy with whatever sang beneath the mountain. She had told herself it was exhaustion. She had told herself a great many things lately, and each of them had worn thinner than the last.

The dispatch was sealed with the triple crown of the Royal Sanctified Office, wax the color of old blood. She broke it standing in the lee of the supply tent, her gloved fingers steady, her breath smoking in the grey morning air. Around her the camp was waking—soldiers stamping feeling back into their boots, the cook-fire hissing where snow fell into it, somewhere a mule complaining in its flat, eternal voice. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of an expedition that still believed it knew what it was doing.

*To Inquisitor First Rank Seraphine Voss, by order of His Majesty's Privy Council and the Office of Sanctification:*

*You are instructed that upon reaching the site designated CAERLUM DEEP VAULT, the following actions shall be executed without deviation, delay, or discussion:*

*First. The thermal phenomenon herein referred to as the Last Fire shall be drained by means of the reliquary apparatus accompanying your column, such drainage to be completed before the fire's natural expiration.*

*Second. The creature sustaining said phenomenon shall be put to death by any means necessary, and its remains secured for transport to the capital.*

*Third. The prisoner Hartwell is to be compelled, under sanction, to open and operate any mechanism requiring the gifted touch, after which he is to be sanctified with all haste.*

*Fourth. Upon completion, no witness outside the column is to remain alive who has seen the fire or the creature. The mountain passes will be sealed by avalanche charges already positioned. This order carries the full authority of the throne and admits no appeal.*

*By hand of Chancellor Aldous Renwick, in the name of the King.*

Voss read it twice.

Then she stood very still, the way she had been trained to stand, the way she had taught novices to stand—a body made into a wall against which nothing could knock her over—and she read it a third time, because the first two readings could not possibly be correct.

*Avalanche charges already positioned.*

She looked up at the pass behind them. The great white shoulders of Thornfell rose on either side, beautiful and indifferent, and she thought about the snowfields hanging above the defile they had crossed ten days ago—the same defile where an avalanche had buried six of her soldiers, where the bone-hearer had knelt in the open and used his abomination like a lantern to find the living among the dead, and the men he pulled from the snow had not looked at him with horror afterward but with something dangerously close to reverence.

Charges already positioned meant men already positioned. Men who had climbed these slopes weeks ago, before the expedition ever left Hollowmere. Men who had known exactly where the column would march, because someone had drawn them a map.

Her mind went to Ellery Finch's maps, and stopped, and went back, carefully, the way you probe a wound to learn how deep it goes.

The royal route ended at Caerlum's sealed vault. A vault that answered only to bone-hearers. An expedition that required, by its very design, a gifted guide—which meant the crown had needed a hearer to be found, caught, and chained, or there would have been no expedition at all. The witch-hunts had emptied the valleys of hearers for three generations. Three generations of sanctifications, of pyres, of neighbors denouncing neighbors. And now, when the throne finally needed one alive, one had conveniently surfaced in a smuggling town small enough to control.

Or had he been allowed to surface? Had Marguerite Hartwell spent his whole quiet life inside a pen whose walls he had never seen?

Voss folded the dispatch along its original creases with great precision, as if neatness could restore the world to the shape it had held yesterday.

*Drain the fire.* The reliquary apparatus had traveled with them from the capital in a lead-lined wagon, guarded day and night, and Voss had assumed it was a censer, a vessel for carrying the fire's blessing home to warm the palace chapels. Now she understood it differently. It was a bottle. The crown intended to decant the last dragon-fire in existence into a portable container and carry it away, and what remained behind—a dead dragon, a darkened mountain, a north country sliding into permanent winter—that was simply what the word *aftermath* meant.

*Kill the dragon.* She turned the phrase over. In eleven years of service she had sanctified forty-one people. She remembered every face; that was the price of doing it properly, and she had insisted on paying it. But those had been hearers. Carvers, midwives, a boy of fourteen who heard his drowned mother in the river stones. People whose crime had been an ear turned toward the dead. Never once had the Office ordered her to kill anything that was alive in the ordinary, breathing sense.

Unless the dragon was not alive in that sense. Unless the crown had already ruled it a thing, a phenomenon, a *thermal anomaly*, language so bloodless you could sign its death warrant between breakfast and matins.

And then the thought arrived that she had been refusing for days, the thought that the Bell had been trying to give her since the night she first tested the carver in his tent, when its tone had come out wrong—too deep, too old, ringing like a bell struck in a cathedral rather than a chapel.

*No witness outside the column is to remain alive.*

The villages along the southern approach had been emptying for weeks. She had recorded it in her log as flight from the failing fires, superstition, rumor. But emptying villages along the only road to Caerlum also meant no witnesses along the road to Caerlum. And the frost creeping down from the peaks, the dimming northern sky—if the fire failed while the crown dragged its reliquary home at the pace of oxen, half the kingdom's harvest would fail with it, and the suffering would be laid at the feet of whoever the crown chose to blame. Bone-hearers. Traitors. Smugglers. Anyone.

The crisis was manufactured. Not the failing of the fire—she believed the fire was truly guttering, she had seen the frost with her own eyes—but the *crisis*. The panic. The hunt that had stripped the land of the very people who might have opened the vault decades ago, while the cage built around the fire rusted and the flame starved inside it. The crown had spent three generations burning its own locksmiths, and now it wanted to steal what was left before anyone could ask why the lock existed in the first place.

She had hunted the gifted because she believed they hoarded power. Because she had watched, at nine years old, in the ash-market at Verenne, a bone-hearer tell a grieving father that his son's soul was trapped in a carved rib and could be freed for coin—and she had watched the man pay until his family starved. That memory had been the foundation stone of her life. *Power hoarded corrupts; therefore the gifted must be found and broken before they gather.*

And the whole time, the greatest hoarder in the kingdom had been the throne itself.

Voss became aware that Corporal Danev was standing a respectful distance away, waiting, his breath steaming. Behind him, two soldiers held the hawk's jesses. The bird stared at her with one furious amber eye.

"Inquisitor? Orders?"

The question landed on her like a blow dressed up as routine. Eleven years of orders. Eleven years of never once hesitating, because hesitation was the crack through which doubt entered, and doubt was the crack through which corruption entered, and she had built herself smooth and sealed against both.

"Confirm the escort rotation," she heard herself say. "Double the watch on the reliquary wagon."

"Yes, Inquisitor." He hesitated. "And the prisoner, ma'am? He's asking—" Danev's jaw worked. "Some of the men want him unchained for the descent. Say it'll be safer on the ice if he can use his hands. After the avalanche, they—" He stopped himself, but too late.

*After the avalanche, they trust him.* She saw it plainly in Danev's face: the soldier did not want to say the sentence, because saying it felt like treason, and treason had a specific penalty that everyone in this camp could recite.

"The prisoner remains chained," Voss said. "Protocol stands until I countermand it."

Danev saluted and went. Voss remained alone by the tent flap, the dispatch a small dead weight in her glove.

Here was the arithmetic, then, laid out with the cold clarity she had once prized above all things:

If she obeyed, she drained the fire, killed the dragon, sanctified the carver, and sealed the mountain over the corpses of any witnesses the charges missed. She would return to the capital honored, promoted, perhaps given a seat on the Council itself. And she would spend the rest of her life knowing that the winter killing the north country had been signed for in advance, in triplicate, by the office she served—that the famine coming would be called an act of God while she sat warm in a hall built from the proceeds.

If she disobeyed, she was a traitor. There was no third door marked *reasonable objection*. The Office did not have one; she had helped make certain of that herself, drafting the procedural reforms that closed the appeals loophole eight years ago. She had closed her own exits with the same hands that now held this order.

There was a version of her—the girl at the ash-market, the novice, the young inquisitor who had wept after her first sanctification and sworn to be strong enough never to weep again—who would have obeyed. That girl had believed the machinery was sound and merely needed firm hands. That girl had thought corruption was something you hunted in others, out there, in the valleys, in the bones.

But the Bell. She touched it through her coat, cold even through leather. It had rung for Marguerite Hartwell, yes—but wrongly, deeply, with a resonance the relic-makers had never documented. And it had gone utterly, perfectly silent in the vault-approach tunnels when Finch's second-layer map had shown them the ancestral workings, silent as a held breath, as if the instrument itself were afraid—or listening to something older than the gift it was built to find. What if the Bell had never detected the gift at all? What if it detected what lay beneath gifts—what the gift was made *from*—and the crown had always known?

What if everything she was had been built as a tool for this exact theft, sharpened across three generations of hunts for this single final purpose?

The hawk screamed again on its perch. Voss walked to it, took the reply-tube from her belt, and stood with a blank slip of paper in her hand for a long moment while the camp noise went on around her, oblivious, trusting.

She thought of Marguerite Hartwell in his chains, and the strange gentleness with which he spoke of the dead—as though they were patients, not evidence. She thought of the war-dead chorus he had described in the long interrogation nights, humans and dragons standing together in the old bones, and how she had written *delusion, consistent under pressure* in her notes because the alternative entry had no procedure attached to it. She thought of Finch, who had died—no. Who had *not* died; the reports were confused, and she had not yet let herself examine the confusion. She thought of the soldiers digging side by side with a chained hearer in the avalanche snow, and no one denouncing anyone, and what that silence had sounded like.

It had sounded like a bell that rang true.

Voss wrote four words on the slip, rolled it, sealed it, and threaded it into the tube. The hawk took her wrist hard enough to bruise and beat away south into the pale sky, carrying her answer to the capital.

She did not watch it go. She turned instead toward the prisoners' tent, where a sentry straightened at her approach, and beyond it toward the black notch of Caerlum's gate, two days distant, where a fire older than kingdoms was starving in a cage her own ancestors' hands might have helped forge.

The words she had sent south were: *Orders received and acknowledged.*

Not *will comply*. Not *shall execute*. Acknowledged. A word chosen the way a fencer chooses a line of retreat—technically obedient, substantively empty, and bought at the cost of everything she had been.

Let the Council parse it however they wished. Let them send their next hawk, and their next. She had perhaps three days before the ambiguity ran out, three days in which she was either the most obedient inquisitor in the history of the Office or the most dangerous traitor the crown had ever marched into its own mountains, and the deciding weight would not be a word on paper.

It would be what she found inside the vault. What the cage was. What the fire begged for, if fire could beg.

Voss buckled her coat, checked the Bell at her belt—an old habit, and suddenly a question rather than a comfort—and gave the order to break camp.

Behind them, far to the south, the first of the avalanche charges waited in the snow, and somewhere in the capital a chancellor was already drafting the account of the tragedy that had not yet happened.

Ahead, the mountain listened.

* * *

They made the high shoulder of Caerlum by late afternoon, and Voss understood, looking at the gate, why the dead had been afraid.

The vault door was not a door. It was a ribcage the size of a cathedral, grown from black bone veined with gold, and set into its center—where a heart should be—hung a socket shaped precisely like the Chirurgeon's Bell. Not similar. Identical. Down to the hairline seam her own relic bore from a fall at Verenne twenty years ago.

Marguerite Hartwell stood chained between his guards, and when he saw what she saw, he did not gloat. He only looked at her with terrible, weary kindness, and said the words she would carry into whatever came next:

"It doesn't detect the gift, Inquisitor. It's a key. They gave you the key and told you it was a weapon."

Voss put her hand on the Bell at her belt.

It was already ringing—softly, steadily, sweetly—though she had not touched it, though nothing gifted stood near enough, though every law of the Office said it should be silent.

It was ringing the way a lock rings, when the right hand finally comes home.
