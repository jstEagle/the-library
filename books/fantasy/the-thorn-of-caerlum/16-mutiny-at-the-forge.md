## 16. Mutiny at the Forge

**Corporal Tam Wexler had killed three men in his life, and all of them had been on the other side of a wall or a field. He had never once imagined drawing steel inside a room that breathed.**

That was the only word for it. The vault breathed. The whole cavern of Caerlum drew slow, shuddering breaths around them—the vast forge-cage of black bone and iron rising in arches to a ceiling lost in gloom, and at its heart, coiled like a sleeping hound, the ember-dragon no bigger than a horse, its scales banked to a dull orange glow that pulsed with each breath of the mountain.

The fire was guttering. Everyone could see it now. Where they had first entered, the light had been steady as a hearth; an hour later it flickered, dimmed, flared, dimmed again—each flare weaker than the last. Frost had begun creeping down the cage-bones nearest the door, feathering across iron that should have been warm.

And the dispatch had come an hour before that. Wexler had read it over Sergeant Hale's shoulder by lantern-light: *Drain the fire into the reliquary. Destroy the beast. By order of the Throne.*

"Drain it," Private Osk had said, half a laugh. "Into that thing?" He nodded at the reliquary case the engineers were unstrapping—a coffin-sized box of glass and lead and gold filigree, humming faintly, like a wasp trapped in amber.

"You heard the order," Hale said.

"I heard it." Osk looked at the dying dragon. "I just don't see how you drain a sun into a box."

Nobody answered him. That was the trouble. Nobody answered, because nobody knew, and because the not-knowing sat in the room with them like a fourth soldier, armed.

* * *

**Inquisitor Seraphine Voss stood apart from the soldiers, and she was counting.**

She had spent eleven years counting heretics—counting their lies, their tremors, their tells. Now she counted something else: which way men's eyes moved when they thought no one watched. She counted six soldiers who kept glancing at the chained carver, Marguerite Hartwell, kneeling by the vault door with his manacles still on and his face turned toward the dragon as though listening to music. She counted four who kept glancing at the reliquary, and at her, and back at the reliquary, doing arithmetic about survival. And she counted Ellery Finch, the disgraced cartographer, sitting against the wall with his maps spread out around him like a gambit laid on a table, waiting for his moment with the patience of a man who had already lost everything else.

Voss's own hands were not steady. She had not slept. The night interrogation still rang in her ears—not the carver's answers, but what he had made her hear through them: the war-dead's testimony, layered voice upon voice, dragons fighting beside human shield-walls, humans bleeding for dragons, the whole architecture of her Church's history turned inside out like a glove. She had chained him anyway at dawn because protocol was the only skeleton holding her upright. Protocol said arrest. Protocol did not say what to do when the prisoner was right.

The bell hung at her hip. The Chirurgeon's Bell, silent for days now except when she touched it—and even then it gave off a tone she no longer trusted, low and wrong, like a tuning fork struck near a larger, older instrument.

"Hale," she said quietly.

Sergeant Hale came to her. A solid man, thirty years in crown service, a face like a closed ledger. "Inquisitor."

"The reliquary work begins within the hour. I want the doorway held. No one leaves; no one enters."

"And if someone tries?"

She looked at him. He looked back. Neither of them said what they were both thinking, which was: *on whose side.*

"Then hold it," she said.

* * *

**Ellery Finch had been a coward for most of his life, and he had made peace with it the way a man makes peace with a limp—he built his life so the limp didn't show.**

Cartography was a coward's art. You never stood on the ground you drew; you stood above it, everywhere at once, safe. Even his disgrace had been a coward's disgrace: he had falsified a border to spare a village a tax levy, and when it unraveled he had apologized beautifully and meant none of it.

But last night, in the freezing dark while Voss interrogated the carver, Finch had done the bravest thing of his life, and it had been easy. He had taken out his map of Caerlum—the one with two layers, the royal route inked over the older route drawn in that strange, carved hand—and he had shown it to Marguerite Hartwell. And the carver had put his chained hands flat on the parchment and gone white as the frost on the walls, and told Finch everything the bones had shown him. The cage. The hoarding. The witch-hunts bred like rats in the cellar of the kingdom to keep anyone from ever opening this door. Ten thousand years of war manufactured by his own ancestors to guard a theft.

Finch had copied it all onto fresh parchment by candlelight. Not prose—maps. Maps of testimony, the confession rendered as cartography: trade routes that became supply lines for the cage-builders, village names that became purge-sites, dates in the margins. A document no scholar could dismiss, because it wore the shape of the thing scholars trusted most.

He had made nine copies. They were sewn into the lining of every pack in the column but three.

Now he waited for the moment. The moment would come when the reliquary crews began their work, because that was the moment the split would stop being a crack and become a chasm, and a chasm is the only place where a coward can stand without being seen crossing it.

The engineers began.

They drove anchor-spikes into the cage-bone at the dragon's flank, and the sound was obscene—a wet, ringing crunch, like biting into a living tree. The dragon's eye opened. It did not roar. That was the worst part, Wexler would think afterward; it did not roar. It opened one ancient golden eye, looked at the spikes in its own body, and made a sound like a kettle taken off the heat, like something too tired even to protest.

Osk threw down his pike. It clattered on stone, and in the silence that followed, everyone heard themselves breathing.

"No," Osk said. "No, I'm not doing this."

"Pick it up," Hale said.

"It's dying, Sarge. Look at it. We're grave-robbers. We're graverobbers for a—" he stopped himself from saying *king*, which was interesting, Voss noted; he'd been going to say *king*— "for a box. For gold in a box."

"Pick it up, Osk. That's an order under combat conditions, and you know what that means."

"Yeah," Osk said. "I know what it means." He didn't pick it up. Two others—Bray and the twins' cousin Aldric—stepped to stand beside him, facing the rest of the column, and the geometry of the room changed forever.

* * *

**It started, as these things do, with a shove.**

Hale moved to arrest Osk; Bray shoved Hale; a spear-butt came down on Bray's shoulder; and then the vault was full of screaming steel and the dragon-fire chose that exact moment to gutter hard, plunging half the cavern into blue shadow lit only by the sparks off clashing blades.

Wexler found himself fighting—actually fighting—Aldric, who had taught him dice on the road up and cheated badly enough that everyone let him win. "Stop," Wexler kept saying. "Stop, stop," and Aldric kept swinging, crying as he did it, both of them crying, the whole melee weeping and hacking at itself in the flickering dark.

Through it all, three figures moved with terrible calm.

Marguerite Hartwell rose from beside the door, chains dragging, and walked into the middle of the fight—not to fight, but to shout, and his voice carried the strangest resonance, as though the vault itself lent him volume: "The dead are watching! Every soul your grandfathers burned is watching you decide!" And men faltered. Blades wavered mid-stroke. It is difficult to kill your brother-in-arms when a dead man's chorus is standing behind the words telling you what you already suspect.

Seraphine Voss did not draw her blade. She walked to the engineers' anchor-lines, drew her knife, and cut the first reliquary cable in two.

"Inquisitor—" gasped the senior engineer.

"I am Inquisitor," Voss said, cutting the second line, "and I am telling you the relic order is void. The throne has lied to us from the first seal on the first decree. If any man here wants my badge when this is done, he can have it. Cut nothing else, or answer to me."

Hale stared at her across the chaos. Thirty years in crown service. His face, for the first time, was not a closed ledger but an open wound. "You'll hang," he said.

"Probably," said Voss.

He looked at the dragon. At the guttering fire. At Osk, bleeding, and Bray, cradling his shoulder, and the chained carver standing in the smoke like a saint in a window. Then Sergeant Hale did the hardest thing in his disciplined life: he stepped backward, away from the engineers, away from the throne's order, and planted his spear point-down in the stone between the factions, a boundary marker.

"On this side," he said hoarsely, "we hold the door."

* * *

**Which was when the vault door began to close.**

Someone outside. Someone loyal to the dispatch, some runner or engineer who had fled the melee and decided, in the cold logic of the frightened, that sealing the traitors in with a dying god was the safest outcome for everyone. The great bone-and-iron gate, balanced on counterweights older than kingdoms, groaned and swung inward toward its frame.

If it shut, they would all die here in the dark with the fire—or worse, the fire would die here with them, and everything north of Thornfell would follow it into winter.

Ellery Finch was closest. Finch, who had spent his whole life standing above the ground he drew, saw the closing gap and understood with perfect clarity that there was exactly one body available to put in it.

He went. He went the way he had never gone anywhere in his life: directly, stupidly, without a plan above the terrain.

The door caught him at the shoulders. Stone shrieked against bone; the counterweights howled. Finch got his boot into the jamb, then his knee, then his whole side wedged in the gap, and the pressure was enormous and getting worse, and he was laughing—actually laughing, a wheezing, delighted sound—as he fumbled the ninth copy of the confession out of his own coat, the one he'd saved for himself, and thrust it through the gap into Wexler's grabbing hands.

"Publish it," Finch said. Blood ran from his mouth; something inside had gone. "Every town. Nail it to every crossroads in the kingdom. Make them read it at— at executions. Make it the only thing anyone reads ever agai—"

"Finch—" Wexler had him by the wrist. Others grabbed on. The door pressed. The counterweights sang a note like the Chirurgeon's Bell, low and wrong.

"I falsified a map once," Finch said, smiling with red teeth, looking past them all at the small coiled ember of the dragon. "Best correction I ever made. Hold it open. Somebody hold the bloody door—"

Marguerite Hartwell reached them then, chains and all, and pressed his manacled hands flat against the gate, and the gate—impossibly—hesitated. Shuddered. The dead in the door-bone remembered being door-makers, and for ten heartbeats they refused their own duty, and in those ten heartbeats the soldiers dragged Ellery Finch's body free of the gap and Brannoc-smuggler reflexes slammed a spar of cage-iron into the mechanism, jamming the gate wide.

But the door had already done its work. Finch lay on the stone with his eyes open and his chest not moving, and around him the maps were scattered like fallen leaves, nine copies of a truth that would outlive its author by centuries if the living had any courage left at all.

* * *

**The silence after was total.**

Eleven soldiers and one Inquisitor stood among scattered weapons, and nobody picked anything up, because there was suddenly no one left to fight. The masks were ash. Hale had chosen. Voss had chosen, publicly, irreversibly. Osk and Bray and Aldric had chosen. The throne's order lay cut in two on the floor with the reliquary cables.

Only the fire kept moving, and it was failing faster now—the dragon's glow sinking from ember to coal to something like the last light in a lamp, and the frost racing down the cage-bones to meet it.

Marguerite knelt by Finch and closed the cartographer's eyes with two chained fingers. Then he stood, and looked at the dragon, and everyone saw him understand something at once, the way a man understands a fall after the ledge is already behind him.

"It was never a question of whether," he said quietly, to no one, to the dead, to the small dying star in the cage. "It was only ever a question of who."

The Chirurgeon's Bell, silent at Voss's hip since the melee began, began very softly—to ring.
