## 19. Dragonfire Rekindled

The first thing Marguerite understood about being half a ghost was that the cold stopped mattering.

He had expected pain—the carving of his own life into the lock had promised pain the way an avalanche promises silence—but what came instead was *thinness*. He could see the snow of Caerlum's broken roof through his own left hand, pale flakes drifting down past knuckles that were no longer entirely there. The vault air moved through him like wind through a doorway left open in winter.

And yet he had never felt more solid in his life.

Because he was not alone in this body. He had not been alone since the moment Voss shattered the Chirurgeon's Bell and its stored resonance poured into the lock like a river finding its old bed. Ten thousand years of the dead were threaded through him now—not pressing, not clamoring, but *singing*, low and vast, a chord so deep it was less a sound than a fact, like the weight of stone or the pull of water downhill.

*Hold,* they sang. *Hold and let go. Hold and let go.*

The cage broke around them like an egg.

Not shattered—hatched. That was the truth none of them, living or dead, had fully grasped until this moment: the forge-cage his ancestors had built was never only a prison. It was a chrysalis, warped and poisoned by three centuries of hoarded power, but at its heart it had been shaped from the same bones that sang in Marguerite's blood. The cage-makers had meant to keep the fire small. The bones themselves had meant to keep it *safe*. And both intentions had lived in the iron all this time, tangled together like roots in a wall, waiting for someone with ears to hear the difference.

Now the great ribs of the cage folded outward, one by one, each releasing a note as it went—a scale descending through registers no throat could make—and the thing inside unfolded with them.

Marguerite had seen the ember-dragon small: a coiled horse-sized creature, dull as banked coals, breathing once an hour. He had wept over it without knowing why.

It was not small anymore.

Fire did not grow the way flesh grew. Fire *remembered*. And the last dragon of Caerlum remembered being vast—remembered wings that had shadowed valleys, remember a spine like a mountain range walking. As the final rib fell away, the memory poured back into it the way light pours back into a room when shutters open, and the dragon blazed up through the ruined vault, through the split roof, through the wounded grey sky, until it hung above the peak of Caerlum larger than the storm clouds, every scale a window onto something burning and ancient and unbearably alive.

The soldiers below had dropped to their knees. Some were praying. Some were simply staring with their mouths open, too far past fear for anything so complicated.

Marguerite stood—or floated; standing seemed generous—in the center of it all, half-translucent, and the dragon looked down at him.

Its eye was the size of a chapel door. In it he saw himself reflected: a thin man made of equal parts flesh and chorus, glowing faintly at the edges where the dead showed through, like a lantern with a crack in its shell.

*Little door,* the dragon said, and its voice did not come through the air. It came through the bones. Of course it did. Everything true came through the bones. *You are the one who opened.*

"I'm the one who listened," Marguerite said. His voice came out doubled, harmonized, ten thousand whispers riding under one tired man's rasp. "They did the opening."

*They.* The dragon's attention shifted, and Marguerite felt it turn inward, into him, into the vast sediment of voices he now carried. It was like being read by sunlight. *I know these songs. I have not heard them since the sky was young. They buried my brothers under the northern ice and sang over the graves for a thousand years, and I thought the singing was mourning.*

"It was," Marguerite said. "Mourning and keeping. They're the same word, in some of the old tongues. My hands knew it before I did." He raised the hand that still had some substance to it, the carver's hand, the scarred and clever one. "I've spent my whole life making keepsakes out of your people's bones. Little comforts. Little secrets. I thought that was all the gift was for."

*And now?*

"Now I think the gift is a hinge." He looked up at the immense face, at the fire behind the fire of its eyes. "A door is useless if there's nothing on either side of it. You're on one side. We're on the other. Somebody has to be the wood."

The dragon was silent a long moment. Below them, on the broken slope of the vault's mouth, Seraphine Voss stood amid her kneeling soldiers with the shattered halves of the bell still in her fists, watching the two of them converse across a gulf she could see but not cross. Her lips were moving. Marguerite, who could now hear prayers the way he heard heartbeats, caught the shape of them: not penance, not pleading. *Names.* She was reciting the names of everyone she had sent to sanctification, one by one, like a woman laying stones along a road she intended to walk back down.

*The humans below,* the dragon said. *They carry iron. Iron remembers fire badly—it remembers only the burning. Three hundred years ago their grandmothers' grandmothers looked at my kind and saw only teeth.*

"I know. My family built your cage."

*I know that too, little door. Your family's bones told me while you slept.* Something moved through the enormous gaze that Marguerite, in all his years of reading the dead, would have called grief if grief could be patient enough to wait three centuries. *So. This is the question that decides everything, and you are the only one positioned to answer it. When I spread my wings and the whole world sees what I am—do they reach for swords? Or do they reach for something wiser?*

"That depends," Marguerite said, "on whether they can hear me."

*Then sing loud, carver.*

---

The dragon rose.

There was no describing it honestly, so Marguerite did not try to describe it honestly even to himself. Later, survivors would say the sky turned gold from Hollowmere to the coast. Later, children born that spring would be marked with faint warm patches on their skin, and the midwives would call them *ember-kissed* and refuse to be afraid. Later, cartographers would have to redraw maps, because the frost lines that had crept down from the peaks for three hundred years began, that morning, to retreat.

But in the moment, there was only this: the last dragon-fire of Caerlum climbed out of its broken chrysalis and turned once, slowly, above the mountain, and its wingbeat was a sound like the first day of the world, and everywhere its shadow passed, the snow steamed and the frozen rivers cracked and groaned and began, against all sense, to flow.

And Marguerite opened himself completely, and the dead sang through him to the living.

He did not choose the song. The dead chose it; he was only the instrument, the hinge, the door swung wide. But he felt every voice as it passed through him, and he would feel them forever after, which was the price and the privilege both.

The war-dead went first—the ones from the pass, the ones whose bones littered the defiles of Thornfell, humans and dragons side by side in the old mass graves because they had fallen side by side in the old wars that were not the wars anyone remembered. They sang of standing together. They sang of a compact older than any crown: *wing and hand, fire and craft, neither master nor servant.* They sang the names of dragon-riders whose statues had been melted down for cannon, of hearth-guardians whose stories had been rewritten as warnings.

The cage-makers went next—his own ancestors, the ones whose confession Finch had published on maps that were already circulating through three kingdoms faster than any decree could burn them. They did not sing excuses. That was the thing that undid him, that he would carry even past the end of carrying: they sang *confession*, clear and unflinching. *We were afraid, and we were greedy, and we dressed the greed as safety, and we built the hunts to guard the lie, and every bone-hearer we silenced was one more witness to the truth we could not bear.* They sang it the way Marguerite carved keepsakes: plainly, carefully, leaving nothing ornamental, because the truth needed no ornament.

And beneath them all, underneath everything, the oldest voices—the first hearers, the women and men who had first pressed ear to femur and heard a grandmother's last kindness—sang a single line, over and over, a lullaby so old it predated language and survived it anyway:

*Power shared corrupts nothing. Power hoarded never survives the winter.*

Marguerite sang it with them. His own voice, thin and human and cracking, rode the top of the chord like a kestrel over a flock, and the sound rolled down the mountainside and out across the waking valleys, and every person within a hundred miles heard it in the place where they kept their griefs—heard their own dead saying their own names, heard forgiveness they hadn't asked for arriving unasked.

That was how he knew it would not become a war. Not because the dragon was gentle—dragons were not gentle; dragons were weather with opinions—but because the song carried proof. No army marches against a voice that knows its soldiers' mothers' maiden names. No crown can call a thing monstrous when the thing is singing your own grandfather's lullaby back to you through the mouth of a man made half of light.

Voss understood it first among the living. Marguerite saw it happen: watched the Inquisitor's face, upturned and tear-streaked, move through horror and grief and land somewhere that had no name in any doctrine she'd ever sworn to. She raised the broken halves of the Chirurgeon's Bell above her head—both fragments, held high, the way you'd raise surrender flags or evidence—and her voice, trained to command, cracked magnificently:

**"IT WAS A LIE!"**

The words rang off the peaks. Somewhere far to the south, in a capital built on three hundred years of careful silence, more bells cracked.

"THE HUNT WAS THE CRIME! THE FIRE WAS NEVER OURS TO TAKE!" She lowered her arms, and her voice dropped to something rawer. "I am Seraphine Voss, and I sanctified forty-one innocent people, and I will kneel before whatever tribunal the world builds and name every order I obeyed! Hear me! *Hear me!*"

And Marguerite, floating at the edge of dissolution, thought: *the bell always rang true. It just wasn't ringing for what they thought.* It had never detected the gift. It had detected the dead reaching toward the living—the truth straining at its cage, the same as the dragon, the same as everything. Every person Voss had ever executed had been executed for the crime of being listened to.

He would tell her that later, gently, when she was ready. Or perhaps one of his ten thousand passengers would. There was time. For the first time in three hundred years, there was time.

---

The dragon landed.

It chose the spot deliberately—Marguerite felt the deliberation through the bones of the mountain—settling into the ruins of the vault's mouth, folding its new-old vastness down into something almost approachable, the way a giant lowers itself to speak with a child. Its fire had banked to a glow rather than a blaze. Even dragons, it seemed, understood that the first meeting between species in three centuries should not begin with anyone catching alight.

Soldiers crept closer. Marguerite watched it happen and marveled: men who had marched up the mountain expecting to kill a monster now approached it the way villagers approach a strange, enormous dog—with terror and longing fighting openly on their faces. One of them, a corporal named Aldsey who had been dug out of the avalanche by Marguerite's naked use of the gift, extended a hand toward the dragon's foreleg as if asking permission.

The dragon exhaled, slow and warm, and the snow around them melted back a full arm-span, and the grass underneath—grass that had been frozen for generations—was suddenly, impossibly green.

Aldsey laughed. It was not a brave laugh or a holy laugh. It was just a laugh, startled out of him, and it broke something open in the column, and then they were all laughing or crying or both, and the dragon tolerated their wonder with the patience of a mountain tolerating weather.

*You see?* it said to Marguerite, privately, through the bones. *They reach for something wiser.*

"They needed to hear it from us," Marguerite said. "From the dead. From you. The living lie too easily to each other."

*And you? Will you lie to yourself, little door? Tell yourself the hard part is finished?*

Marguerite looked down at his hands. More translucent than before. The chorus in him was quieter now—not gone, never gone, but settling, the way a cathedral settles after the bells stop, into a permanent resonance. He could feel the shape of what remained of his life: months, maybe a year, of being a bridge before the bridge became simply part of the landscape. He found, examining the feeling honestly, that it did not frighten him. He had been alone his entire life in the exact way that mattered. Now he would never be alone again, and the trade was theft in his favor.

"No," he said. "The hard part is starting. Now everyone has to live with the truth instead of just dying for the lie. That's harder. That's always harder."

*Good,* said the dragon, and there was something in the vast voice that a poet would have called approval and a carver would have called warmth and Marguerite's mother—if he had ever had one—would simply have called love. *Then stay. Stay as long as you can stand it. Teach them the old compact. Wing and hand.*

"Fire and craft."

*Neither master nor servant.* The dragon lowered its head until one enormous eye was level with him, close enough that he could see himself reflected twice over: the thin fading man, and behind him, rank upon rank upon rank, the shining multitudes. *And when you finally come all the way through the door, carver—there is a choir on this side too. It has been waiting a very long time for a voice that can hold the harmony.*

Marguerite smiled. Half his face was light by then, but the smile was real on both sides of it.

"I'll bring the tune," he said.

Far to the south, past the thawing passes, past the villages emptying of fear instead of people, a rider in crown colors spurred her horse northward carrying dispatches that would arrive too late to matter—and behind her, in the throne room of a kingdom that had just lost its oldest secret, a king stood before a cracked bell and began, very quietly, to be afraid.

Below the mountain, Brannoc Hartwell tightened her pack straps, spat into the green snow, and started climbing.
