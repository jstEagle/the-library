## 21. The Carver Who Became a Door

The mountain did not want her climbing it.

Brannoc had smuggled salt through the Thornfell passes for thirty years, had crossed scree fields in whiteout, had walked ridgelines so narrow the wind seemed to carry her by the collar. She knew every goat track from Hollowmere to the high snows. But Caerlum was different. Caerlum pushed back.

By the second day she understood why. The frost came down to meet her—not the ordinary frost of altitude, but a creeping, deliberate cold that slicked the rocks and made her boots skid where they had never skidded before. Then, as she climbed higher, it began to retreat. By dusk of the third day she was walking through mud in January, through green shoots pushing up between stones like the world had forgotten what season it was supposed to be.

She had heard the stories coming up the salt road. Everyone in Hollowmere had heard them, told in the low voices people used for things they were afraid to hope in. The fire at Caerlum was burning again. The sky over the northern peaks glowed at night like a banked hearth. The witch-hunters had been recalled, their proclamations torn down from the crossroads and burned in the market square by the same soldiers who had nailed them up.

And Marguerite Hartwell—her boy, her foundling, the child the river had given her one frozen spring and whom she had never once let herself call son aloud because names could be taken away—that boy had opened the vault of the ancestors and set a dragon loose in the world.

Or the dragon had set him loose. The stories disagreed on the order of things.

So she climbed. Fifty-three years old, knees complaining, pack full of the only currency she trusted: smoked meat, hard cheese, a flask of plum brandy, and the bone-handled knife he had carved for her when he was eleven years old, the handle worn smooth as sea glass from twenty years of her grip.

On the fourth day, the dead began to talk to her.

Not to her, exactly. Around her. In the high defiles where the old battle-bones lay scattered like dropped kindling, she heard something—a murmur under the wind, a texture in the silence. She had spent her whole life around bone-hearers. She knew what their gift cost them; she had watched Marguerite come home gray-faced as a boy because some dead soldier's femur would not stop telling him how it broke. But she had never heard it herself. The dead spoke to hearers, not to smugglers.

Except now, walking the thawing slopes of Caerlum, Brannoc heard them the way you hear a conversation through a wall: not words, but cadence. Grief and gladness braided together, rising and falling like breathing. And underneath it all, threaded through everything like a silver wire through dark cloth, one voice she knew better than her own heartbeat.

He wasn't speaking to her. He was speaking with them—all of them, ten thousand years of them—and his voice had become part of the chorus the way a river becomes part of the sea.

Her hands shook on her walking staff. She kept climbing.

* * *

She smelled the camp before she saw it: woodsmoke and roasting meat, absurdly domestic at the roof of the world. Then she crested the last shoulder of rock below the summit plateau and stopped so suddenly she nearly went down on her knees.

Caerlum's gate stood open.

She had imagined it her whole life—the sealed vault her grandmother's grandmother had whispered about, the door no key could turn, the mountain's locked heart. Smuggler lore said the crown had tried to breach it twice and lost entire companies to the cold. Now it stood wide as a barn door, its ancient stone glowing faintly from within, warm light spilling down the slope like honey.

And above it, against a sky gone violet with early evening, a dragon turned on wings that caught the last light and burned.

It was not enormous. That was the thing none of the stories had gotten right. It was the size of a horse, maybe a little more—an ember-dragon, coiled and bright, its scales the deep orange of a forge at rest. It flew in slow circles over the peak, and everywhere its shadow passed, the snow steamed and the green crept further uphill.

Brannoc stood in the mud with her mouth open like a girl, and felt the strangest grief of her life, because the world she had spent fifty-three years surviving had ended while her back was turned, and nobody had thought to tell her.

"You'll want to close your mouth," said a voice behind her. "The midges are terrible this time of year."

She spun. A soldier sat on a rock ten feet away, whittling, a sword across his knees. Young. Round-faced. One of Voss's column, by the cut of his coat, though the coat had been stripped of its royal badges.

"Easy," he said, palms out. "You're the one they said might come. The smuggler matriarch." He pronounced it carefully, like a title. "He talks about you."

Brannoc's throat closed. "Does he."

"All the time." The soldier went back to his whittling. "Mostly he says you'd come up the east ridge, because you never take the road when there's a ridge. He says you taught him that." He nodded up the slope, toward a path she had chosen precisely because no one who didn't know her would guess it. "Go on up. He's waiting at the gate. He's been waiting three days."

"Three days," Brannoc repeated flatly. "He can see me coming, then."

"He can see everything now." The soldier shrugged, unbothered, or performing unbothered. "Took some getting used to. You will too."

She climbed the last stretch with her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird, and every step of it she rehearsed what she would say. She had disowned him on a mountainside in a snowstorm. She had called him traitor in front of forty men, had told him that if he walked on toward Caerlum he walked alone, that the clan's door was shut to him, that she had raised a stranger and a fool. She had meant it as mercy—a way to give him an exit, a story the others would believe, a rope to climb down that wouldn't drag the whole family into the crown's noose with him. She had watched him understand that, watched him accept it without a word, and she had told herself ever since that he had known. That he had seen the mercy inside the cruelty, because he was the one person alive who could always hear what people couldn't say.

But she had also meant it. Some part of her, in that storm, had meant every word, and that part had been screaming ever since.

Now here was the gate, and here was the light, and standing in the spill of it—

Marguerite.

He looked the same and utterly changed, which should have been impossible and wasn't. Same narrow shoulders, same ink-dark hair she had cut herself every month for twenty years with the same dull scissors. But his skin had a translucence to it, faint as breath on glass, and when he moved, she saw—she was sure she saw, though she would spend years deciding whether she'd imagined it—shadows moving beneath the shadows of him. Other figures. Old ones. Walking when he walked, stopping when he stopped, like reflections in deep water.

His eyes found hers across forty feet of thawing stone, and they were his eyes, and they were also everyone's eyes, ten thousand years of the dead looking out through her boy's face, and every speech she had rehearsed died in her throat at once.

"You took the east ridge," he said.

"You look terrible," she said.

He laughed, and the laugh was his, wholly his, and something in her chest unlocked that had been locked since the snowstorm.

* * *

They sat inside the gate, in the antechamber of the vault, because he said the view made her nervous and he was right. The great forge-cage still dominated the cavern beyond—vast ribs of carved bone arching over an empty center where the dragon had slept for a thousand years, now open like a ribcage cracked by a surgeon's hand. Soldiers moved through it quietly, tending cookfires, hauling water, treating the place with the careful reverence of men who had seen too much to scoff at anything.

Brannoc ate the food he gave her—broth, bread baked in a field oven, cheese that someone had smuggled up from Hollowmere, which made her snort despite herself—and studied her son across the fire.

"So," she said. "You're a door now."

"That's one word for it."

"I've heard others. Saint. Traitor. Bridge. Abomination." She counted them off on her fingers. "The widow Tarnach lit a candle for you in the shrine. Old Cobb spat at your name in the taproom and then bought a round in your honor, which is the most Cobb thing I've ever heard."

"What do you call me?"

Brannoc looked into the fire for a long moment. "I don't know yet," she said finally. "That's why I climbed the mountain."

He nodded slowly. The firelight moved through him strangely, she noticed—warmer on one side, as if something behind his skin leaned toward it. "Ask me what you actually came to ask," he said gently. "You've been circling it since the ridge."

"The dead." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You're threaded through with them. I can see it. Half of Hollowmere can see it in the way you stand, they say—you move like a man listening. So I'm asking, and I want a true answer, and I'll know if it isn't one because you've never once managed to lie to me in twenty-two years: how much of my boy is left in there?"

The question hung between them. Beyond the fire, a soldier laughed at something, and the sound echoed off the cage-bones and came back softened, gentled, like the mountain itself had decided to keep the laugh.

"Some days," Marguerite said, "I'm not sure I'm the right person to answer that. Because the honest answer is that I don't end where I used to end." He held up a hand before she could speak. "But listen. You taught me something once, whether you meant to or not. You taught me that family isn't blood and it isn't even choice, not really—it's practice. You chose me every morning for twenty-two years. Fed me, cut my hair, hid me when the riders came, lied to inquisitors with a straight face. Every single day you chose again. That's what made us family. Not one decision. Ten thousand small ones."

"And now?"

"And now I choose again. Every day. I wake up with ten thousand voices in my head and I choose, every morning, which one I am today. Most mornings—" he smiled, tired and real— "most mornings I choose the one you raised. The carver. Your boy. Because somebody has to keep being that person, and honestly, Brannoc, nobody else can do it. The dead are magnificent and terrible and they have no idea how bread works."

Despite everything—the fear, the grief, the long climb—Brannoc laughed. A real laugh, ugly and loud, the kind that made the nearest soldier glance over and grin.

"They don't know how bread works," she repeated.

"Not one of them. Do you know what it's like trying to explain leavening to a woman who died four thousand years ago? She thinks flour is a form of magic. Which, fair."

"But you're still—" She stopped. Started again. "When I saw you at the storm, when I cut you loose—I need you to know why. It wasn't because I believed the crown's lies. It was because I believed ours. The clan's oldest rule: never let them take two of us when they can take one. I gave them one. I gave them you. And I have regretted it every hour since, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and I don't know how to live with both of those things at once, and I climbed a mountain to tell you that, because you're the only person alive who might understand holding two true things that hate each other."

Marguerite was quiet for a long time. When he reached across the fire, his hand—warm, solid, unmistakably flesh—closed over hers.

"I heard you," he said. "That night. Through the bones in my own pack, through the dead in the pass, I heard you say traitor and I heard what was underneath it, the way I've always heard what's underneath everything. You were giving me a door out. You've been making doors for me my whole life." His grip tightened. "I walked through one of them, Mother. This one. And I'm not sorry, and I think you're not sorry either, and I think we can finally stop pretending that makes us strangers."

Brannoc did not cry. She had not cried since the spring the river gave her a child, and she did not intend to start on a mountaintop in front of soldiers. But her eyes burned, and she let them, and she turned her hand over and gripped his back, bone to bone, the way you hold on when you mean it.

"The clan will want to see you," she said at last, voice steadied by sheer professional discipline. "The young ones especially. There's a girl, Petra, fourteen, started hearing things last autumn. Her mother brought her to me in a panic, and I didn't know what to tell her anymore—everything I knew was built on hiding. Everything I taught you was built on hiding." She met his eyes. "I find I've run out of hiding. So. Teach me what comes next."

"There's a school," he said simply. "In Hollowmere. Finch's maps are being redrawn—openly, this time, every route the ancestors buried. Voss is testifying before the crown in spring, and whatever happens to her, the truth's out of the cage-bones now, and truth doesn't go back in. Come down with me. Bring Petra. Bring all of them." He paused, and the faint shadow-shapes beneath his skin shifted, and for a moment Brannoc could have sworn the whole cavern leaned in to listen. "Bring the clan, Mother. All of it. The dead and I have been talking, and we have work, and we'd rather do it with family."

Outside, wings beat the evening air, slow and vast and unhurried, and the light through the gate flared gold.

Brannoc looked at her son—at the door he had become, with ten thousand souls passing through him and still, impossibly, room left over for one stubborn old smuggler—and she took out the plum brandy, poured two cups, and pushed one across the fire.

"To doors," she said. "May they all swing both ways."

They drank. And far below, in the valley, the first true spring in a thousand years was coming up the road to meet them.
