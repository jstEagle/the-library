## 22. First Fire, Last Chapter

The snow came late that year, and when it came it was thin as a widow's veil, melting off the rooftops of Hollowmere by midmorning. Marguerite Hartwell woke in the house on Tallow Lane—his house now, though he still sometimes reached for the door latch expecting Brannoc's hand to be there before his—and lay listening to the sound of the town breathing.

That was the strange gift the mountain had left him. Not silence anymore. He would never have silence again. But where once the dead had whispered their secrets through every bone he touched, urgent and hoarded and afraid, now they simply *were*, a low chorus at the edge of his hearing like wind through a winter field. Ten thousand years of voices, threaded through him like gold wire through soft lead, and they had nothing left to hide.

He rose, banked the stove, and set water for tea. Through the window he could see the workshop's new sign catching the early light: **HARTWELL — CARVER & HEARER**, the second word painted over only three months ago, after the council had voted, after Voss's testimony had gone south to the capital in the mouth of a disgraced royal courier, after the witch-decrees had come down off the crossroads post one nail-hole at a time.

A child was already waiting outside the shop.

Marguerite saw her from the kitchen window: small, seven or eight, wrapped in a coat two sizes too big, standing very straight in the manner of children who have been told to be brave and are managing it badly. He knew her. Tam's girl from the mill—the one whose grandmother had died at midwinter and whose family could not afford a proper keepsake.

He opened the door. "You're early, little Wren."

"I couldn't sleep." She held out a bundle wrapped in linen, clutching it the way he had once clutched things himself, long ago, in another life. "Mama said you'd want it fresh. Is that true? Does it go bad? Like milk?"

"Like bread," Marguerite said gravely, taking it. "Best the first day." The bundle was warm from her chest. Inside, wrapped with a mother's care, lay a finger-bone, small and clean. He closed his hands around it and let himself listen.

The grandmother came through clear as spring water—not words exactly, not yet, but the shape of her: flour on her knuckles, a song half-remembered in a language the valley had stopped speaking, and love, love worn smooth as river stone, aimed unerringly at the small cold-nosed girl shivering on his step.

"She used to sing to you," Marguerite said. "In the old tongue. Something about a boat."

Wren's eyes went enormous. "Nobody knows that song. Papa says Grandma made it up."

"Grandmas make up the best songs. They keep all the real ones underneath." He crouched to her height. "I'll carve it into the bone. Every note. And when you hold it at night, she'll sing it to you again. But listen—" he tapped her nose, and she giggled, "—you have to promise to teach it to somebody someday. That's how the dead stay alive. Not by hiding. By handing down."

"I promise."

"Good. Come back at dusk." He watched her run off down Tallow Lane, coat flapping, and stood in the doorway a moment longer than he needed to, letting the morning happen to him.

Once, keeping secrets had kept him alive. Now the whole town knew what he was, and he was safer than he had ever been behind silence. There was a lesson in that, and he suspected he would spend the rest of his life learning it properly.

* * *

The morning class arrived at nine.

There were eleven of them now. When he had first nailed the notice to the crossroads post—**THOSE WHO HEAR THE BONES: INSTRUCTION OFFERED, NO FEE, NO FEAR**—only three had dared come, and two of those had stood across the street for an hour before working up the courage to knock. Now the workshop's back room was full: a shepherd who had spent forty years pretending the stones spoke to him; twin sisters from the salt roads who heard through driftwood; an old soldier whose dead comrades had been giving him directions since the war, none of them helpful; a boy of fifteen named Pell who heard through anything that had ever lived, which made markets unbearable and graveyards unbearable in an entirely different way.

"Again," Marguerite said, moving among them. "From the top. What do you hear?"

They sat in a circle around a single rib bone laid on white cloth—a deer's, offered freely by the hunter who had taken it, because consent mattered, because the first thing he taught was that a bone was not a *thing* you took from the dead but a hand you were being trusted to hold.

The shepherd went first, as always, steady as his own flocks. "Grazing. A hillside above the tree line. It's... content." He frowned. "It's content the way I'm content. That's odd. It shouldn't know the word."

"It doesn't," Marguerite said. "It knows the feeling, and your gift translates. That's the whole secret, Osric. We're not eavesdroppers. We're translators. The dead don't speak our language. They speak *being*."

Pell squirmed. "Mine doesn't translate. Mine just talks."

"What does it say?"

"That the cloth is itchy. That it wants to be a flute." Laughter around the circle. "No—it says—" Pell went still, and the laughter died, because everyone had learned to recognize that stillness. "It says there's something coming up the pass road. Something big. It felt the ground shake."

The room went quiet. Eleven faces turned toward the window, toward the north, where the peaks of Caerlum stood white against a blue so clean it looked newly made.

Marguerite felt it too—or rather, felt the chorus shift, ten thousand voices turning like flowers toward sun. He walked to the window.

Far above, a shape circled the highest crag, wings wide as sails, catching the light and throwing it back in sheets of gold and ember-red. It did not come close. It never came close. But every clear day it flew its slow circuit of the peaks, patient as weather, and the whole valley had learned to check the sky the way farmers once checked for storms—and to smile when they saw it.

"The dragon," someone whispered, though there was no need. Everyone knew. Children drew it on slate. The dragon, alive and vast and free, wearing its freedom like a young thing wears a new coat.

"Not coming down," Marguerite said softly. "Just saying good morning." He turned back to the circle. "Which is more than most of us manage. Sit. Again, from the top. Osric, this time tell me not what the bone feels, but what it *wants*. That's where comfort lives—in wanting. Everything else is just weather."

They bent over the rib bone together, eleven heads bowed like a congregation, and Marguerite moved among them correcting, encouraging, translating, and thought: *Brannoc, look at this. Look what we were so afraid of.*

* * *

Brannoc came at noon, as she did twice a week, ostensibly to inspect his larder and actually to drink his plum brandy and complain about the state of the world, which had improved dramatically and was therefore far worse than before.

"They've opened a school in Thornfell," she announced, dropping onto his bench and helping herself to the bottle without asking, which was how he knew she was in a good mood. "For hearers. With *charters*. Charters, Marguerite. Stamped. Signed by some duke. My smugglers are going legitimate faster than I can corrupt them."

"You could always retire."

She looked at him the way she had looked at him since he was nine years old and had suggested, sensibly, that perhaps theft was wrong. "And do what? Garden?"

"How is the road, then?" he asked, because she wanted him to ask. "The passes."

"Clear until first frost, longer than anyone remembers. Your dragon's breath does more for the trade routes than twenty years of my bribes." She poured, drank, studied him over the cup rim. "Voss writes to you."

It was not a question. He had never hidden the letters; they sat in plain view on the mantel, tied in ribbon, because hiding things was a habit he was done with.

"She does."

"And?"

"And she's testifying still. The tribunals will run through winter. She says the crown is tearing itself apart nicely—half the court wants her head, and the other half wants her head *after* she's finished naming names." He smiled faintly. "She asked me again if I'd speak at the final session. In person. In the capital."

"Will you?"

He turned the question over. Once, the capital had meant witch-hunters and bells and death by decree. Now it meant a hall where a woman who had chained him to save her own life was spending that same life buying back the truth, word by word, name by name.

"I think I must," he said. "The dead marched with me this far. It seems rude to stop carrying them at the door."

Brannoc snorted, but her eyes were wet, and she covered it with the bottle. "To doors," she muttered. "Swinging both ways."

"To doors," he agreed.

She stayed till the light went long and amber, arguing cheerfully about everything, and when she left she paused at the gate and looked north at the peaks, where the evening flight had begun, gold against the darkening blue.

"He never lands," she said. "All these months. Why do you suppose that is?"

Marguerite joined her at the gate. The chorus inside him stirred, and he listened, really listened, and heard what he had half-known for weeks and not let himself say aloud.

"Because a cage is a cage even when you build it yourself," he said quietly. "Even a sky can feel too small, if you've been shut in long enough. He flies the boundary of the valley because the boundary is all he trusts yet." He watched the vast shape wheel and climb. "Freedom isn't given, Brannoc. It's practiced. He's practicing."

"And us?"

"We practice too." He squeezed her shoulder. "Every day. Go home, you old thief. Wren comes at dusk, and I have a song to carve."

* * *

He carved through the golden hour, alone in the workshop with the lamp lit and the finger-bone pinned gentle in the vise, and the grandmother sang through him into the grain of the bone, note by note, the old-tongue melody rising out of his hands like water finding its level. This was the work now, all of it: not secrets, not warnings, not the desperate hoarded confidences of the frightened dead. Just this. Comfort, passed hand to hand. Memory, handed down. The boat-song, the flour-knuckled love, the feel of a granddaughter's cold nose under a warm palm—all of it sinking into the bone where a child could find it in the dark and be less afraid.

When it was done he held it up to the lamp and listened to it hum, small and complete, a whole life folded into something the size of a reed.

Outside, the light changed.

He stepped into the yard and looked up, and the whole town seemed to have stepped out with him—doors opening along Tallow Lane, faces tilting back, the miller and the twins and old Osric and Wren's whole family spilling into the street, because the dragon had broken its circuit. For the first time since the fire had rekindled, it was descending.

Slowly. Enormously. Wings spread wide to ride its own falling, gold light sheeting off it, the snow on the peaks flashing answering signals of pink and amber. It came down the valley like a season arriving, and it did not land in the square or on the church or anywhere that would mean conquest. It landed on the bare granite shelf above the town—the old watching-place, the place where sentries had once stood—and folded its wings, and settled, and looked down at Hollowmere with eyes like banked coals.

Not hunting. Not demanding.

Waiting.

The chorus in Marguerite's bones went quiet—not silent, never silent, but hushed, the way a hall goes hushed before someone speaks. And beneath the hush he heard the ember-dragon's voice for the first time since the vault, since the breaking of the cage, and it was not a roar and not a threat. It was almost shy.

*You,* it said. *Door. You are the one who opens.*

Marguerite walked forward through the parting crowd, Wren's bone still in his hand, his heart going hard and simple as a hammer on wood. Behind him, the whole town held its breath. Above him, the last dragon-fire waited, no bigger against the sky than a horse, no smaller than the dawn.

*Yes,* Marguerite answered, with everything he was—carver, hearer, son, door. *What do you need?*

And the dragon said: *Practice.*

The great head dipped, the coal-gold eye closing slowly, deliberately—an offering, an invitation, a bow. Around Marguerite the crowd began, hesitantly, to murmur, and the murmur became a cheer, and high overhead the first stars came out over Caerlum like sparks off an anvil, and Marguerite Hartwell stood in the gathering dark with the warmth of ten thousand dead at his back and one living fire before him, and understood at last the whole of what the bones had been trying to say from the beginning:

Power shared corrupts nothing. Power hoarded never survives the winter.
