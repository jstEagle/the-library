## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with talking to goats was that they never had anything interesting to say.

Cyrus Nystrom had learned this by the age of seven, crouched in the wet grass behind Maren's croft, listening to a billy named Turnip complain—endlessly, bitterly, at length—about the quality of the hay. Not in words, of course. The valley of Highmoor did not permit words that weren't spoken, and it certainly didn't permit whatever it was Cyrus did: that wordless pouring of meaning from one skull into another, like water finding its level. But meaning carried all the same. Meaning always carried.

*Hay is bad,* Turnip grumbled now, chewing with the air of a philosopher who has suffered greatly and intends everyone to know it.

"The hay is fine," Cyrus said aloud, because there was no one for three miles to hear them talking to a goat, and because answering out loud kept the habit of speaking alive in their own mouth—a strange thing to have to practice, but there it was. "You ate the good hay yesterday. I watched you eat it."

*Yesterday's hay is gone. Yesterday's hay does not exist. Only today's hay exists, and today's hay is an insult.*

"You are the most dramatic creature I have ever met, and last spring I watched a ewe faint over a thunderclap."

Turnip flicked one ear—the goat equivalent of a shrug—and went back to his grievances. Cyrus leaned against the fence post and let the morning wash over them: mist burning off the lower slopes, the smell of woodsmoke from the croft chimney, the small honest sounds of Highmoor waking up. A wren scolded something from the hedgerow. Somewhere up the hill, the Hendersons' old dog dreamed loudly of rabbits, his paws twitching against the ground in a way that made Cyrus's chest ache with secondhand joy.

*Rabbits,* the dog murmured across half a mile of morning air. *Rabbits rabbits rabbits—*

It was, when Cyrus let themselves feel it, a beautiful way to live. The whole world talking, if you knew how to listen.

And it was, every single day, a death sentence waiting to be discovered.

That was the arithmetic Cyrus had been doing since they were small enough to still believe that grown-ups could explain things. Beast-speaking wasn't frowned upon in the wider world. It wasn't taxed or licensed or looked down on by priests. It was *burned*. That was what the peddlers said when they came through twice a year, trading salt and gossip at the valley's edge—that beyond the mountains, speakers were hunted down like wolves, dragged before tribunals, and put to the flame while crowds sang hymns about purity. The peddlers said it casually, the way people talk about weather in far-off places, and then they'd look straight at Cyrus a beat too long, and Cyrus would smile and say nothing, and their heart would hammer like a fist on a door.

So: twenty-two years of saying nothing. Twenty-two years of keeping the gift folded up small and quiet inside them like a moth in a closed fist. The valley helped, in its way. Highmoor was magic-dead—had been for as long as anyone remembered—and whatever great ward lay over it, humming along its boundaries like a struck bell nobody could hear, it muffled everything. Cyrus's gift worked here only in whispers. They could speak to the animals of Highmoor because they'd been doing it since infancy, because the knack had rooted itself deep enough to survive the suppression, but it was like trying to sing through a wall of felt. Outside the valley—

Well. They'd never been outside the valley. Nobody had explained to Cyrus exactly what would happen if they crossed the boundary stone, but they'd stood near it once as a child, close enough to feel the hum in their teeth, and the sensation had been so much like standing at the lip of a cliff in the dark that they'd never gone back.

"CYRUS!"

Maren's voice, carrying up the slope from the croft with the force of a woman who had raised a child alone and had long since stopped apologizing for her volume. "Are you talking to those goats again?"

"No!" Cyrus called back, automatically.

A pause, rich with maternal skepticism, drifted up the hill.

"...The goats are talking to *me*," Cyrus amended, which was technically true and, in the privacy of Cyrus's own conscience, not even a lie worth feeling bad about.

Maren's laugh rolled up the slope. "Come get breakfast before it's cold, then, and tell your conversationalists breakfast is for people."

*She means food,* Turnip observed. *All her words mean food eventually.*

"That is," Cyrus admitted, pushing off the fence post, "the single truest thing anyone has said to me today."

* * *

Breakfast was barley porridge with honey, eaten at the scarred oak table where every scratch had a story Maren would tell if you gave her half a chance. This one—"that one's from the night you were teething and I nearly took my own hand off chopping firewood"—this one from a winter storm, this one from a pig that had gotten into the kitchen in the year before Cyrus arrived and had apparently been a legend in its own time.

Maren talked the way she did everything: fully, warmly, with her elbows on the table. She was sixty-odd and built like a root vegetable, all stubborn lumps and hidden strength, with grey hair she hacked off herself each spring using sheep-shears and a hand mirror, with results she described as "rustic" and Cyrus described, when feeling brave, as "criminal." She smelled of tallow and thyme. She had found Cyrus, so the story went, abandoned at the valley's edge one bitter autumn night, and had taken them in without hesitation, and had raised them with a ferocity of love that Cyrus trusted more completely than the sunrise.

There was just the one thing. There was always the one thing.

"So the Hendersons' dog—" Cyrus began, around a mouthful of porridge.

"Mm."

"He's getting old. His hips hurt him in the cold mornings. I thought maybe we could ask Tam to make up one of his willow-bark poultices next time he's through."

Maren's spoon paused, just for a heartbeat, above her bowl. It was such a small pause. Cyrus might have missed it if they hadn't spent twenty-two years learning to read pauses the way other people read books.

"And how," Maren said, lightly, so lightly, "did you come to know the state of the Hendersons' dog's hips?"

"I saw him favoring his leg. When we brought the flock past their field. Last week." Cyrus kept their eyes on the porridge. "Anyone could see it."

"Anyone could," Maren agreed, and resumed eating, and the moment passed over the table like a bird passing overhead—gone, but its shadow had crossed both of them, and both of them pretended it hadn't.

This was the ritual. This had always been the ritual. Maren asked a question shaped like a trap without being a trap; Cyrus stepped around it with the grace of long practice; love continued undisturbed, built on a foundation of mutual, unspoken pretending. Cyrus loved her for it and resented her for it and could not have untangled the two feelings with a team of oxen. Because surely—surely—she knew. Mothers knew things. And surely she had chosen, all these years, not to know, because knowing would oblige her to do something, and the only somethings available were terrible ones.

Or else she genuinely didn't know, and the loneliness of that was a different animal entirely, quieter and worse.

"You'll take the flock to the high pasture today?" Maren said, back on solid ground. "And mind the ford. Rain in the fells last night; the river will be up."

"I always mind the ford."

"You fell in the ford when you were nine."

"I was *pushed.*"

"You were showing off for Petra Henderson and slipped on a rock like a newborn calf, and she laughed at you for a month." Maren's eyes crinkled. "Take the oilcloth for the cheese. And Cyrus—" the pause again, smaller this time, softer— "keep close to home, hm? Stay under the stone line."

Under the stone line. Under the ward. Inside the hum. It was the shape of a hundred conversations they'd had, the shape of Maren's whole philosophy of child-rearing compressed into four words: *stay where it's safe, stay where I can see you, stay where the world can't reach you.*

"I will," said Cyrus, who was twenty-two years old and had never once disobeyed, and whose obedience sat in their chest these days like a stone of their own.

* * *

The high pasture took two hours to reach, the path climbing through birch and bracken until the trees gave out and the world opened into green. Cyrus walked it with the flock strung out behind them—not herded, exactly. Accompanied. There was a difference, and the difference was the gift: a low constant murmur of animal chatter in the back of Cyrus's mind, the flock's simple contentments and complaints, the lead ewe's unshakeable conviction that she personally was in charge of the expedition.

*I am leading us,* she informed Cyrus, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"You are walking behind me on a path I chose," Cyrus said.

*A leader delegates.*

Highmoor spread below and around them as they climbed, and Cyrus let themselves look at it the way they rarely permitted themselves to: really look, with the ache switched on. It was beautiful the way a locked room can be beautiful. The valley ran eight miles end to end, cupped between fells that wore their forests like dark cloaks, the river stitching down the middle in bright silver thread. Two dozen crofts scattered along its floor, smoke rising from chimneys, the tiny figures of neighbors moving through fields in rhythms older than memory. Everyone knew everyone. Everyone's grandmother had known everyone's grandmother. It was the kind of place where a person could be loved thoroughly and known completely, except for the one part of themselves that mattered most, which had to stay shut away forever, in the dark, breathing quietly.

Belonging somewhere built to erase what you are. Cyrus had never managed to phrase it that precisely before, but the thought arrived now, unbidden, on the open slope with the wind in the grass, and it landed hard enough that they stopped walking.

The flock stopped too, sensing the change, twenty pairs of rectangular pupils swiveling toward them with mild concern.

*I'm hungry,* said Turnip, who had followed them up despite having no official role in the flock, because Turnip believed suffering built character and he wished to supervise Cyrus's.

"We're almost there."

*The hay at the top better not be an insult.*

"It won't be an insult."

*It will be an insult.*

Cyrus huffed something that was almost a laugh and almost something else, and walked on.

* * *

They reached the boundary stone at midday, though they hadn't meant to.

The pasture's upper edge marched right up to it—the old grey monolith standing alone where the green gave way to scree and then to the bare shoulders of the fells. It was taller than a man, older than anything, its surface worn featureless except for a spiral carving near its base that no one in the valley could explain and no one much wondered about. Sheep grazed to within a few feet of it and no closer, some instinct turning them aside, and the shepherds of Highmoor had built their fences to respect the same invisible line.

Stay under the stone line.

Cyrus hadn't planned to come here. Their feet had simply carried them, the way feet do when the heart is restless, and now they stood ten paces off with the wind pulling at their sleeves, looking up at the thing that had defined their entire life without ever once explaining itself.

Up close, you could hear it. Not with the ears—with the teeth, the bones, the hollow places of the skull. A hum, vast and low, like the note of an enormous bell rung once centuries ago and still fading. The ward. The valley's great silence, poured out in a circle around eight miles of ordinary human life, keeping the magic of the wide world out—or in. No one knew which. The peddlers said the founders of Highmoor had been refugees fleeing the purges, folk who'd bought their safety with a bargain no one remembered the terms of. A valley where magic couldn't follow. A valley that forgot.

Cyrus pressed their palm flat against the air an inch from the stone's surface, not quite touching. The hum thickened, resonated, climbed into their wrist.

And beneath it—faint, so faint they might have imagined it—they felt the other thing. The thing they never told anyone, never even told themselves in daylight. The sense that the silence of the valley was pressing down on something in Cyrus's chest, holding it underwater, and that the something was awake in there, patient, enormous, waiting.

One step, said the something, in no words at all. One step past the stone and you'd hear the whole world singing.

Cyrus pulled their hand back as if the air had burned them.

"Not today," they told the stone, and hated how small their voice sounded against all that ancient quiet.

*Who are you talking to?* Turnip had wandered up, because Turnip had never met a private moment he didn't want to attend.

"A rock."

*Is it a nice rock?*

"It's the most important rock in the world," Cyrus said, "and it doesn't care about either of us."

Turnip considered this, chewed on it, found it insufficiently related to hay, and wandered off.

Cyrus stayed a moment longer. The sun was warm. The valley below glittered, safe and small and lovely, every soul in it tucked under the hum like children under a blanket. Somewhere down there Maren was probably salting meat or arguing with the loom, and the thought loosened something in Cyrus's chest even as another part of them tightened around the old, unspeakable question:

*How long?*

How many more years of whispering? Of practicing the shape of an ordinary life over the shape of their actual one, the way you'd practice a signature you'd stolen? The purges beyond the mountains weren't ending—the peddler gossip said they were spreading, reaching further every year, tribunals in towns that had never held them before. Even here, even under the ward, the world was closing like a fist.

Cyrus turned back to the flock, and that was when they noticed the weather.

It had come up fast out of the west while they'd been standing at the stone—clouds stacked black and bruised over the fells, dragging a curtain of rain across the high passes. Wind shoved suddenly at the grass in long flat waves. The light went strange and coppery. The lead ewe bleated uneasily, and beneath the bleat Cyrus felt the flock's mood turn, a ripple of animal dread moving through thirty small minds at once.

Storms came to Highmoor often enough, but this one felt wrong—too fast, too low, the clouds moving with a purpose that weather shouldn't have. And there was something else. Something at the very bottom of Cyrus's awareness, thin as a thread pulled through cloth: a wrongness coming down the valley with the rain. Faint. Distant. Moving.

Toward them.

"Home," Cyrus told the flock, and for once none of the animals argued.

They were halfway down the slope when the first drops hit, fat and cold, and the thread of wrongness in Cyrus's mind pulled a little tighter—coming from the river, from the direction of the ford. Coming, Cyrus realized with a lurch they'd remember afterward as the exact moment their old life ended, from the boundary stone itself.

Something had just crossed the line.

Something that should not have been able to.
