## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The storm had been building all afternoon the way storms did in Highmoor—slowly, grudgingly, as if even the weather had to be coaxed over the boundary stone. By the time Cyrus finished mucking the goat shed, the sky had gone the color of wet slate and the wind had picked up that low, fretful moan it made through the pines, like a dog whining to be let in.

Maren had told them twice already to come inside. Twice Cyrus had said *one more chore*, which was a lie both times, because there was always one more chore when you didn't want to sit in a kitchen and be looked at. The goats didn't look at you. The goats looked *through* you, with the mild indifference of creatures who had never once wondered what you were.

That was why Cyrus liked them.

They were on their way back from the far pasture—boots sucking in mud, hair plastered flat, the ewe-lamb Petunia trotting at their heel like an unpaid bodyguard—when they felt it. Not heard. Felt. A wrongness in the air above the river, the same sensation as a word spoken in a room where no one should be able to speak.

Cyrus stopped. Petunia stopped. Both of them stared down the slope toward the ford.

The river ran high and brown with rain, churning white around the stepping stones. And crumpled against the largest stone, half in the water, was a woman.

She wasn't from any village Cyrus knew. Her traveling cloak was fine wool gone gray with riverwater, cut in a style Maren would have called *southern* and then said nothing else about for a week. Her hair had escaped its braid in dark ropes across her face. One arm was flung out on the stones; the other clutched something to her chest with the desperate strength of the nearly dead—a leather case, long and narrow, strapped shut with buckles that gleamed even through the rain.

And her skin—

Cyrus's breath caught.

Sigils. Inked or scarred or somehow *both*, curling up her forearms and throat in lines of deep blue-black, intricate as frost ferns. They should not have been possible. Nothing marked like that could exist inside the ward. The ward ate such things. It ate charms and hexes and whispered prayers; it had eaten every scrap of magic out of this valley so thoroughly that even the old stories called Highmoor *the quiet place*. Whatever those marks were, they were still burning faintly, guttering like candles in wind, refusing to die.

Which meant either the ward was failing—or she had brought something into the valley that the ward couldn't digest.

Both possibilities made Cyrus's stomach drop through the mud.

*Don't go down there.* That was the sensible thought, the safe thought, the thought twenty-two years of careful living had trained into them like a groove in wood. Strangers in Highmoor were rare enough to be events. Dying strangers marked with impossible sigils clutching locked cases were not rare; they were *unprecedented*, and unprecedented things in Cyrus's experience always ended with someone asking questions Cyrus could not answer.

But the woman's hand slipped off the stone. Her head went under.

Petunia bleated in alarm as Cyrus went down the slope at a slide, boots first, arms pinwheeling, dignity abandoned somewhere near the top. The river grabbed them to the waist, cold enough to make their ribs ache, and they hauled the woman up by the collar before the current could take her back. She weighed almost nothing. That was wrong too. People weren't supposed to weigh almost nothing.

"Hey," Cyrus gasped, dragging her onto the bank. "Hey. Stay with me."

Her eyes opened. They were gray, startlingly light against her drowned-dark lashes, and they fixed on Cyrus with a focus that had no business belonging to someone half-drowned.

Then she said it. Four words, slurred by cold and water, but clear enough:

"You're the one I paid for."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Rain hammered the shallows. The woman lay on the bank with her cheek in the mud, watching Cyrus the way one watches a door you're not sure is locked.

"I think—" Cyrus's voice came out strange, high, younger than they felt. "I think you've mistaken me for someone else. I don't know you. Nobody pays me for anything. I pay for things. Sometimes. When Maren gives me coin, which is rarely, because—"

"You're Nystrom's foundling." The words came slow, each one costing her visibly, her lips blue-gray. "The child from the stone. Twenty-two years ago." A shudder went through her, the deep rhythmic shudder of a body losing its argument with the cold. "Ilse. My name is Ilse Vane. I was a courier once. Before."

*Before what?* Cyrus wanted to scream. But you didn't scream questions at dying people. You learned that young, on farms, with animals—that panic was contagious, and the last thing a frightened creature needed was your fear added to its own. So instead Cyrus did the thing they did with frightened lambs: got low, got quiet, got close without crowding.

"We need to get you out of the rain," they said. "Can you walk? Lean on me. Don't be proud about it, I'm built like a fence post but I'm a sturdy fence post."

A flicker of something crossed the woman's face—not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "Fence posts," she murmured, "are underrated."

Getting her up the slope was ugly work. She could put weight on one leg only; the other dragged, and when Cyrus's hand brushed her boot they felt why—the ankle was swollen to twice its size inside the leather, and hot. Broken, or near enough. She bore it without complaint, which frightened Cyrus more than screaming would have. Screaming people wanted to live loudly. Silent ones had made some kind of arrangement.

The goat shed was closer than the house, and empty except for Petunia, who followed them in and then stood in the doorway glaring at the stranger with all the ferocity forty pounds of lamb could muster, which was considerable.

Cyrus got Ilse onto the straw pallet in the corner, stripped off her sodden cloak, and piled every clean sack and blanket they owned on top of her. Then they crouched in the straw beside her, dripping, and did the thing they were best at in all the world: they listened without words.

It was a strange skill to describe to anyone who hadn't grown up with it. Not mind-reading—nothing so grand. More like standing very still until you could feel the shape of another creature's distress, the way you could feel weather coming in your teeth. Goats wore their pain plainly. Dogs wore theirs plainly. People wrapped theirs in language and manners until it was unrecognizable, and Cyrus had spent a lifetime learning to unwrap them anyway, quietly, because it was the one gift they had that never felt like a danger.

Ilse's distress had a shape. It was long and narrow and it pointed, always, toward the leather case.

"The case," she managed, when Cyrus finally asked aloud. "Don't open it. Not here. Not—" a cough, wet and terrible, "—not until you understand what you are."

"What I *am*?" Cyrus laughed, and hated the laugh immediately, because it came out brittle. "I know what I am. I'm a goatherd. I'm Maren's kid. I'm the person who's good with animals, which everyone thinks is a charming quirk and nobody thinks is—" They stopped. The sigils on her arm had caught their eye again, guttering, alive. "Nobody thinks it's worth killing someone over. Is it? Worth killing over?"

Ilse's eyes closed. For a horrible moment Cyrus thought she'd gone, just like that, mid-question—but her chest still moved, shallow and slow.

"Your hands," she whispered eventually. "Show me your hands."

Confused, Cyrus held them out, palms up, rain-cold and goat-smelling.

Ilse reached out with her free hand—one trembling finger—and touched the center of Cyrus's palm. Just touched it. And Cyrus felt it, unmistakable as a struck bell, a hum rising up through their wrist, their arm, their chest. Something answering. Something *recognizing*. As if a tuning fork had been held against a string they'd spent twenty-two years pretending wasn't there.

Inside the ward, where magic was supposed to be dead, where Cyrus had learned to keep their gift folded up small and silent as a moth in winter—something had just answered a stranger's touch.

"Don't," Cyrus said, and pulled their hand back, and their voice cracked on the single syllable. "Please don't do that. If anyone saw—if anyone ever—" They were shaking now, and it wasn't the cold. "You don't understand what it's like here. We don't talk about it. We don't *have* it. Whatever you think I am, I'm not, officially, and if you say otherwise I will deny it, and my mother will deny it, and the whole valley will back us up because that's how we survive, we survive by agreeing."

The woman watched them with an expression Cyrus had no name for. Pity, maybe. Or recognition. Or the particular sorrow of someone who has seen this exact fear before, in a mirror, years ago.

"I know," she said softly. "Believe me. I know exactly what it's like."

* * *

The storm eased toward midnight, though it never fully stopped—it settled into that steady gray drizzle that meant it intended to stay the week. Cyrus sat in the straw with their back against the wall, close enough to reach Ilse if she worsened, far enough that touching her would take a deliberate act. Petunia had given up her sentry post and fallen asleep across Cyrus's feet, which was warm and also meant Cyrus could not leave, which Petunia probably understood perfectly. Animals were manipulative like that. Cyrus respected it enormously.

Somewhere in the small hours, Ilse began to talk.

Not coherently. In pieces, the way ice breaks—chunks at a time, edges sharp. Cyrus pieced together what they could.

There was a court beneath a mountain. Far north and east, past the fens, past the trade towns, under a peak the old maps named Hollowspire. It was not a human court, whatever shapes its nobles wore. It kept things. Kept voices, kept names, kept souls—bottled them like preserves, shelved them like jars of plums. And it *traded*. That was the part that turned Cyrus's blood to well-water. You could buy things from it. Safety. Silence. Time. The prices were never written down, and they were never money.

"I bought something," Ilse said, staring at the rafters. Rain ticked on the roof above them like patient fingers. "Twenty-two years ago. I bought a child's safety. A whole childhood of it. Sealed and warded and hidden so thoroughly that even the court's own eyes slid off the valley like rain off oil."

Whose child?

She didn't say. She didn't have to. The hum in Cyrus's palm was still there, faint as a remembered bell, and it answered the question before she could.

"Why?" Cyrus asked. Their voice sounded like someone else's. "Why would you spend—whatever it cost—to protect someone you'd never met?"

Ilse turned her head. In the dark of the shed, her gray eyes caught what little light there was.

"Because someone spent the same to protect mine, once. And the debt passed to me. Debts like that don't die with the debtor, little goatherd. They wait." A pause. Her breathing hitched. "And now mine has come due, and I find I can't pay it myself anymore. So it falls to you. I'm sorry. I fought the arithmetic the whole way down that river."

"That's not—" Cyrus stood up too fast, dislodging Petunia, who complained. "That's not how debts work! You can't just hand a person a bill for a life they didn't know was bought! I didn't sign anything! I was a *baby*!"

"Yes," Ilse agreed mildly. "Terrible practice, infant contracts. I've often thought so."

Despite everything—despite the terror sitting in Cyrus's chest like a swallowed stone—something in the absurdity of it cracked a laugh out of them, short and unwilling. Ilse's mouth twitched in response, and for a moment she looked less like a dying courier and more like someone who had once been funny on purpose, regularly, as a lifestyle.

Then the moment passed, and her face went grave again, and she lifted the hand that clutched the case.

"Thirteen turns," she said. "When the thirteenth moon fills, the court renews itself. It feeds. Everything in its vault goes into the fire—all the kept voices, all the bottled souls, everything it holds. Including—" her breath caught, held, released, "—including mine. I sold it, do you understand? Not pawned. Sold. I have been walking around for two decades as a woman missing the middle of herself, and I only understood what was missing when I tried to remember my mother's face and found a smooth blank where the memory should be."

Cyrus sat back down slowly. The straw was cold through their clothes. Somewhere outside, the river kept talking to itself, high and brown and indifferent.

"So the map," they said. "In the case. It's a route. To the mountain."

"It's a route, and a key, and a confession, and a bill of sale." Ilse's fingers loosened on the buckles, just slightly. "All four. The court appreciates thoroughness. Take it. Don't read it tonight. Read it when—" Another cough, longer this time, bending her double. When it ended she was gray as ash, and Cyrus's eyes were stinging, and she gripped Cyrus's sleeve with sudden fierce strength.

"When I'm dead," she finished, plainly, because apparently she was the sort of person who didn't flinch from the word. "Read it when I'm dead. There's a letter inside addressed to a woman named Maren. Give it to her unread. Promise me. She'll burn it—I'd stake my remaining soul-fraction on it—but give it to her anyway. Some things need burning. It's how they stop being secrets and start being ashes, and ashes weigh nothing."

"That's the strangest thing anyone's ever asked me," Cyrus said.

"I'm a strange person. I contain multitudes and most of them are overdue at the library." Her head rolled back against the straw. "Promise."

"...I promise."

"Good girl. Good—" a pause, a flicker of uncertainty, the first time all night she had seemed unsure of anything, "—good *child*. Forgive me. Where I came from, we didn't ask."

Cyrus stared at her. *Where you came from.* As if it were a country. As if it were somewhere else entirely.

Before they could ask, Ilse closed her eyes, and this time the closing looked deliberate, chosen, a door shut from the inside. Her breathing slowed. Did not stop. But it slowed the way a clock winds down, and Cyrus, who had sat up with sick lambs and a foaling mare and old Bram's last winter, knew the sound of a body beginning to negotiate terms with leaving.

They sat with her until the sky went pale at the edges. At some point Petunia crept over and laid her head, absurdly, on the dying woman's blanket, the way goats did with anything cold and still—warming it, guarding it, refusing to concede. Ilse's hand came to rest on the lamb's wool without waking.

Near dawn, her eyes opened one last time. She looked at Cyrus for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her nod, once, as if confirming a delivery.

"The thirteenth moon," she whispered. "Count them. I counted twelve before I left the mountain. You have—" her voice failed, recovered, thinner than thread, "—you have one moon's turn. Less. Less than that, now. Find the vault. Find my soul. It's small and blue and it hums when it's frightened, and it has been frightened for twenty-two years, and it is *mine*, and I want it back."

The light strengthened. The rain thinned to mist. Out in the pasture, the goats began their morning complaints, and the world resumed being ordinary, relentlessly, as if nothing at all had happened at the ford.

Ilse Vane died an hour after sunrise, between one breath and the next, with her hand still resting on a lamb.

Cyrus buried her that afternoon under the rowan behind the goat shed, before Maren could ask questions Cyrus didn't yet have answers for—which was cowardice, they knew, and did it anyway. They marked the grave with a flat stone and no name, because names invited questions, and questions invited answers, and answers, in Highmoor, could get you killed.

Then they sat alone in the shed with the leather case in their lap, rain starting again on the roof, and their promise to a dead woman sitting in their chest like a second heart.

*Give it to Maren unread.*

*Read it when she's dead.*

Both promises. Both true. Both impossible to keep at once.

Cyrus's thumb found the first buckle.

They did not open it. Not yet. But they didn't take their hand away, either.
