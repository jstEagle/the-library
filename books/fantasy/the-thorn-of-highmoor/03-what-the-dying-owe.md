## 3. What the Dying Owe

Cyrus did not open the case that night.

They meant to. They sat in the shed with the buckle under their thumb until the rain stopped, until the goats complained from the yard, until Maren's lamp passed the window on her way to bed—and then they put the case in the flour crock behind the last sack, which was the stupidest hiding place in the house and therefore the best one, because no one who knew Maren would ever look for anything near the flour. Maren guarded her flour the way other people guarded their children.

Then Cyrus went inside and ate stew and said nothing, and Maren said nothing back, and between them the silence had the specific texture of two people each carrying a secret too heavy to set down at the same table.

*You're the one I paid for.*

The words followed Cyrus into sleep and stood there waiting when they woke.

* * *

Ilse died on the second morning, in Maren's good bed, with the rain gone and the light coming through the shutters in pale stripes.

She had lasted longer than anyone should have lasted, given what the river and the road had done to her. The sigils on her skin—those impossible marks that had no business existing inside a valley where magic went to die—had faded by degrees, like ink rinsed from cloth, and as they faded she grew quieter, as if whatever kept her talking was leaking out through them.

Maren tended her with a briskness that fooled no one. She boiled willow bark and changed the linens and spoke in the clipped, practical voice she used for sick animals, but Cyrus caught her once, standing in the doorway with a basin she'd already emptied, just watching the woman breathe. Watching her like you watch a candle you know is going out.

"You knew her," Cyrus said that morning, low, in the kitchen.

"I knew of her." Maren didn't look up from the bread she was kneading with more force than bread requires. "Eat your eggs."

"Maren."

"Eggs, Cyrus."

So Cyrus ate their eggs, and hated them, and went in to sit with the dying woman instead.

Ilse's eyes were open. Gray eyes, the color of the ford after a storm, and they found Cyrus with an effort that seemed almost physical—as if looking had become a chore she had to budget for.

"There you are," she whispered. "The one I paid for."

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm a—a bolt of cloth. Something bought."

Something moved at the corner of Ilse's mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, worn smooth. "You're angrier than I expected. Good. Anger keeps. Fear spoils." Her hand crept out from under the blanket, and Cyrus took it, and it was cold and light, like holding a bird. "I need you to listen now. My voice is nearly spent and I have spent twenty-two years being careful with it, which is a joke, if you think about it."

"I don't feel like laughing."

"No. You won't. Not for a while." She drew a breath that whistled. "There is a court beneath the mountain. Not your mountain—the high one, north past the fens, the one the old maps call Hollowroot. It is real, whatever the ward has told you about the world being flat and quiet and safe. It is real, and it keeps things. Voices. Names." A pause. "Souls."

Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "That's a story mothers tell. To keep children from wandering."

"Yes," Ilse agreed gently. "It is exactly the sort of story mothers tell to keep children from wandering. And like all such stories, it is true, which is why it works." Her fingers tightened, surprising strength in them, brief as a struck match. "Twenty-two years ago I made a bargain there. I sold them the only thing I had that they wanted. In exchange, they wrote your name out of their ledger. A child born with the gift, hidden inside a dead valley—they hate nothing so much as a thing they cannot count."

The room tilted, very slightly. Cyrus heard their own heartbeat.

"My gift," they said. Their voice came out strange. Flat. "You knew. Before I could walk, you knew what I—" They stopped. Started again. "What *am* I?"

"A beast-speaker." Ilse said it plainly, without flinching, and Cyrus realized they had never once heard the words spoken aloud—not by Maren, not by anyone, not even in the safety of their own head. Hearing them was like hearing your own name called from across a river. "One who speaks to the wild things without word or whistle. There were four of us left, when I was young. Now—" she coughed, wet and long, and Cyrus held the cup to her lips and watched her not drink, "—now perhaps fewer."

"The sigils. On your arms. They worked inside the ward."

"They were bought with the same coin. Everything I am has been bought or sold; that is what a bargain with that court makes of you." Her gaze drifted toward the window, toward the gray light. "But the debt comes due, Cyrus. That's the shape of all their bargains. Thirteen turns of the moon, they said. Thirteen, and the payment is theirs again, and this time they take it whole." Her eyes came back to Cyrus, sharp despite everything. "Twelve moons have gone. I ran the length of the thirteenth trying to reach you before it filled."

"Why?" Cyrus asked, and their voice cracked on it. "Why spend your soul on me? On someone you'd never met?"

Ilse smiled properly then, and for a moment the death in her face rearranged itself around something young.

"Because I knew your mother," she said. "And because someone once did the same for me, and I never got the chance to say thank you. Consider this both."

Cyrus sat very still. Outside, a goat sneezed. The ordinary world went on being ordinary, stubbornly, while everything Cyrus understood about themselves quietly changed owners.

"Your mother's name was Sefa," Ilse went on, softer. "She came to the boundary stone in the dead of winter carrying you wrapped in her own coat. I was running. I was always running, those years. She saw the marks on my arms and she knew what I was, and she didn't scream, didn't run, didn't sell me to the Warden's men. She gave me half her bread and asked only one thing—that if anything happened to her, someone would know her child existed." Her breath hitched. "Something happened to her. And I was the someone. And I was a coward, so I paid instead of stayed."

"Paid," Cyrus repeated. The word felt enormous. "With your soul. For a stranger's baby."

"For my friend's baby." Ilse closed her eyes. Opened them. "There's a difference. It matters. Remember that—it matters later, when you meet things that don't believe in differences."

* * *

She pressed the case into Cyrus's hands an hour before noon.

Not the leather case with its buckles—she'd never known about that—but a smaller thing, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the lining of her coat, which Maren cut open with scissors and a face like a slammed door. Inside was a map, folded eight times, soft as cloth at the creases from years of folding and unfolding along roads Ilse had apparently walked more than once.

And a letter. Sealed, addressed in a careful hand to *M. of Highmoor*.

Maren took the letter without a word and left the room with it, and Cyrus heard the kitchen stove door open and close, and did not need to look to know what had become of it. That was a wound to be examined later. Everything was a wound to be examined later. Later was becoming a crowded country.

"Listen," Ilse said. Her voice was nearly gone now, a thread of a voice, and Cyrus bent close enough to smell the sickness on her breath and did not care. "The map. The route runs north through the fens, then east along the spine of the hills to Vell's Crossing. From Vell's Crossing there are ways down. Smugglers' ways, mostly, and worse. Do not go alone."

"I'm going?" Cyrus said. "I haven't said I'm going."

Ilse looked at them. Just looked. Gray eyes in a gray face, patient as stone, and Cyrus felt the argument die somewhere in their chest, because they had already packed a bag in their head three times since dawn and both of them knew it.

"When the thirteenth moon fills," Ilse whispered, "they'll consume what's mine. Renew the mountain with it. That's what they do—that's all they are, in the end. A hunger wearing a crown. If it's consumed, it's gone forever, do you understand? Not resting. Not sleeping. Gone. No one comes back from being eaten."

"And if I steal it back?"

"Then you'll be the first." The ghost-smile again. "I'd apologize for leaving you such poor instructions, but the truth is I never planned to fail. Dying was not in my notes."

Cyrus laughed. It burst out of them, ugly and wet and completely involuntary, and Ilse looked pleased about it, which somehow made it worse.

"Find the raven," she said suddenly, gripping Cyrus's wrist with the last of her strength. "On the north road. Black as sin, rude as a tax collector. He owes me—he stole from the court and they took his voice for it, and he's been circling the wilds ever since, looking for a way back in. He knows the tunnels. He knows the doors. He's a liar and a thief and he will abandon you twice, but he is the only creature alive who can get you through the gates."

"A raven."

"A raven."

"You're sending me to rob a soul-vault beneath a mountain with a talking bird."

"I'm sending you with the only guide I trust and a map I bled over." Her grip loosened. Slid away. "Yes. Precisely that. I'm sorry it isn't an army."

Outside, the goats were making noise about something. The kettle was on. Maren's shadow crossed the yard, checking fences that didn't need checking. The whole ordinary machinery of Highmoor ground on around the shed-room where a woman was busy dying, and none of it knew, none of it cared, and Cyrus thought: *this is what the ward feels like from the outside. A valley that forgets. A valley built to forget.*

"Ilse," they said. "Thank you." The words felt far too small, coins tossed into a well with no bottom. "For the bargain. For staying alive long enough to—"

"Don't thank me yet." Her breathing had gone shallow and slow, each one arriving like a guest unsure of welcome. "Thank me when you're free of it. Both of us. That's the thanks I want—two souls walking out from under that mountain, and the mountain hungry and empty-handed." Her hand twitched toward the map in Cyrus's lap. "Look at it. Before I go, look at it, and tell me what you see."

Cyrus unfolded it.

The paper was fine stuff, vellum probably, worth more than the shed they sat in. Inked lines ran north in careful detail: the ford, the fen causeways marked in red where the ground would bear weight, the hill-spine, Vell's Crossing with its little cluster of roofs drawn almost lovingly. Beyond it the land opened into blankness—and there, in the upper corner, a mountain rendered in cross-section, its roots exposed like a pulled tooth. Beneath the roots, in chambers drawn small and precise, someone had written labels in a spidery hand. *Halls.* *Vaults.* *Gates.*

And along the bottom margin, in different ink, older ink, a single line of script:

*Paid: one soul entire. Held against delivery: Nystrom, C.*

Cyrus read it twice. Three times. The letters didn't change. The name didn't change.

"That's—" Their voice wouldn't work properly. They cleared their throat. "That's my name."

"Yes," Ilse said.

"It's written on a map to a—a soul-vault. Like an item. Like a debt in a ledger."

"It is a debt in a ledger." Ilse's voice was fading fast now, and she was spending what remained of it like a miser forced open. "When they couldn't hold my bargain over me anymore—when I broke the terms by coming here—they reverted to the original debt. Your life was purchased with my soul, Cyrus. Which means, in their arithmetic, your life belongs to them. When the moon fills, they will come to collect. Not me first. *You.*"

The vertigo hit all at once, the way it does when the floor turns out to have been water all along. Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years of hiding a gift they'd barely understood, of wordless conversations in goat sheds, of flinching from every Warden's rider on the ridge road—and the whole time, the danger hadn't been the gift at all. It had been arithmetic. A line in a book beneath a mountain, with their own name written on it in old ink, accruing interest.

Their whole life had been purchased. And purchases, it turned out, came due.

"I won't let them," Cyrus said. They didn't remember deciding to say it. It came up from somewhere below thought, below fear, from the same place the gift lived—the place that heard the elk grieving on the high slopes and the worms turning in dark soil. "The moon fills in—I don't know how many days—"

"Nine," Ilse whispered. "Perhaps ten."

"Nine days. And I'm going to walk into that mountain and cross your name off their ledger myself. Both names." Cyrus looked down at the map, at the little drawn chambers, at the mountain drawn like a tooth. "Both of us walk out. You said. Two souls, mountain hungry and empty-handed."

Ilse exhaled. It went on a long time, that exhale, and Cyrus understood halfway through it that it wasn't going to be followed by an inhale.

Her face, at the end, was calm. Whatever she saw with her last sight—whether the shuttered light, or the fen roads of her running years, or something kinder—she looked like a woman finishing a sentence she'd started twenty-two years ago and finally finding the period.

Cyrus sat with her a while. Then they closed her eyes, because no one else was going to, and folded the map along its soft old creases, and went out into the yard to tell Maren that the stranger was dead.

Maren was standing by the fence with her back turned, and when Cyrus said it—just the plain words, *she's gone*—her shoulders moved once, a shudder quickly arrested, like a horse refusing a spook.

"We'll bury her by the stone," Maren said. Her voice was steady. Almost. "Tonight. Quietly."

"Why tonight? Why quietly?"

Maren turned. And for the first time in Cyrus's life, they watched their mother decide, visibly, deliberately, whether to lie to them—and watched her lose.

"Because," Maren said slowly, "the Warden's riders crossed the south ridge this morning. Three of them. Heading north." Her eyes went to the oilcloth still clutched in Cyrus's fist, then to Cyrus's face, and something in her expression cracked open like ice on the spring river. "And because whatever she gave you, Cyrus—whatever she told you—you need to understand that I have spent twenty-two years keeping you alive, and I am asking you, before you do anything, before you open another fold of that map—"

She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter:

"—to ask yourself who else knew you were born."

Behind Cyrus, in the flour crock, in the stupidest hiding place in the house, the leather case waited with its two buckles—one promised to Maren unread, one promised to the grave.

Cyrus had a feeling they were going to break at least one promise soon.

They only didn't yet know which.
