## 4. Maren's Silence

The goats knew before Maren did.

She'd swear to that for the rest of her life. Twenty-two years later, standing in her kitchen with a dead woman's letter trembling in her hands, she could still remember the way the whole herd had turned its head toward the boundary stone one gray morning in the first week of thaw, all of them at once, like iron filings snapping toward a lodestone.

"Storm coming," she'd told them, because telling the goats things was a habit she couldn't break. "Nothing else it can be."

But it hadn't been a storm.

---

*Twenty-two years earlier.*

Maren had gone out to check the stone because the hum had changed.

Every child in Highmoor learned the sound of the ward before they learned their letters — a low, patient thrum you felt more than heard, like a hive sleeping under the turf. The elders said it was the mountain breathing. The practical folk said it was nothing at all and best left alone. Maren, who was nineteen and newly alone in her mother's croft, mostly thought about how the hum meant the world outside stayed outside, and she liked that just fine.

That morning the hum had gone thin. Wrong. Like a singer running out of breath mid-note.

She'd taken the crook and walked up through the wet grass with the mist still clinging to her ankles, and there, at the base of the boundary stone, wrapped in a blanket so fine it must have cost someone a month's wages, was a baby.

Not crying. That was the first thing she noticed, and it unsettled her more than anything else. Babies cried. This one lay on its back in the frost-rimed grass, staring straight up at the gray sky with eyes too old and too dark for its face, and around it the grass was *green*. In the first week of thaw, when everything else was brown and shivering, a circle of grass two arms wide around the blanket had come up bright as midsummer.

Maren looked at the stone. The stone hummed its broken note. She looked at the baby. The baby looked at her, unblinking, and somewhere behind her a crow landed on the wall and made a sound that was almost, almost a word.

"No," Maren told it firmly. "Don't start."

The crow ruffled its feathers, deeply affronted.

She should have fetched the elders. That was what a sensible person did: fetch the elders, let them decide, wash her hands of whatever strange business came down from the mountains. She stood there for a long moment with her breath fogging and her heart hammering, doing sums she didn't want to do — the empty cradle-shaped years ahead of her, her mother gone a winter past, the croft too big for one set of hands.

Then the baby reached up one tiny fist, grabbed a fistful of that impossible green grass, and pulled, and when it came away the grass went brown in its grip like something drinking the color out of it, and the baby laughed — a small, startled bark of delight at its own power — and Maren felt something in her chest turn over and settle, permanent as a foundation stone.

"Well," she said. "You're trouble, aren't you."

The baby agreed wholeheartedly.

She named him Cyrus, after her grandfather, who had also been stubborn, also been found in an inconvenient place, and also never once in his long life admitted to being wrong. She told the elders he was a cousin's orphan from the lowlands, and the elders — who were not fools, and whose glances at the boundary stone were longer than usual that spring — chose to believe her, which is its own kind of kindness, and sometimes its own kind of cowardice.

For three years it worked. It should not have worked. But Highmoor was a valley built on the agreement not to look too closely at things, and Maren learned to live inside that agreement the way the fish live inside the river.

Then, on a night of hard rain, a woman came to the door.

---

Maren remembered everything about Ilse Vey, because Ilse Vey was the sort of person memory refused to release. Small and wiry, with rain streaming off a hooded traveling cloak that had seen more roads than most maps, and eyes the color of wet slate that took in Maren, the firelight, the wooden spoon in Maren's hand, and the toddler visible through the doorway — in that order, and fast.

"You have his look already," the woman said, without hello or by-your-leave. "Good. That helps."

Maren put herself between the stranger and the child with the spoon raised, which even then she understood was not a weapon so much as a statement of intent. "I don't know you."

"No." The woman pushed back her hood. Her hair was cropped short and going gray at the temples far too young, and there were marks on her wrists — fine, intricate sigils inked or scarred into the skin — that made Maren's teeth ache to look at, the way a wrong chord aches. "You don't. You will, though. May I come in? I've crossed the ford twice tonight and I'd rather not die in your yard. It's muddy, and I'm told the view is poor."

Against every instinct, Maren stepped aside. Later she would understand why: because the woman had looked at Cyrus the way people look at a fire they started, with love and guilt sharing the same face.

Ilse sat at the table, accepted tea she didn't drink, and laid it all out in a voice flat as ledger arithmetic, as if plainness could make it bearable.

"I'm a courier. Not of letters — of *arrangements*. There is a court beneath the eastern mountains. They keep things. Voices, souls, gifts — anything that burns bright enough to be worth keeping. And they are always hungry." She turned her wrist over; the sigils caught the light. "These are payment marks. I sold them something once, long ago, and I have spent the time since paying installments on my own self. Do you follow?"

"No," said Maren honestly.

"Good. Sensible people shouldn't." Ilse leaned forward. "Three years ago, a child was left at your boundary stone. A child who, in certain company, would be called a beast-speaker. One who can hear the minds of animals, and speak into them. In the lands beyond this valley, such children do not grow old. The court collects them young, when the gift is tender and easy to bind."

Maren's hand had found the table edge and gripped it. Through the doorway, she could hear Cyrus chattering to himself in the other room — except he wasn't chattering to himself, was he, he'd never been chattering to himself, he talked constantly to the goats and the hens and the crows on the fence, and she had told herself for three years that it was a child's fancy, a lonely boy's invented friends—

"He talks to them," Maren said. Her voice came out very small. "They answer him. I thought—"

"You thought what mothers think. Whatever lets you sleep." Ilse's voice gentled by a degree, the first crack in the arithmetic. "It isn't a fancy. And the night he was born, his parents ran until they couldn't run, and they bought his hiding with everything they had left. Which was me. I owed them a debt older than he is. They called it in: carry the child past the ward, find a keeper, and seal the bargain so no one in the court's reach can ever trace where he went."

"How?"

"That's mine to know." Ilse smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as paper over an abyss. "Suffice it to say I am now worth considerably less than I was. What remains of me has been scattered across several ledgers. But the part of the arrangement that matters is this: while he stays inside the ward, he is invisible. The valley eats magic. His gift sleeps. No one can smell what he is. The day he crosses that stone, the day the gift wakes—" She shrugged. "Everything that hunts such children wakes with him."

"So he never leaves."

"For his sake, he never leaves." Ilse studied her tea. "He'll hate you for it, eventually. Somewhere around fifteen, I'd guess. Sixteen. They always do. The walls that keep the wolves out feel identical to the walls that keep the child in. You'll bear that. It's part of the price, and it's the cheapest part."

"And yours?" Maren heard herself ask. "What's your price?"

Ilse looked at her for a long moment, and some of the courier fell away, and underneath was just a tired woman with rain still in her hair.

"My soul sits in a vault under the mountain," she said. "It has for eleven years. When I die, they keep it. That was the shape of the original sale, and there's no clause in any contract that gets it back." She rose, buttoning her cloak. "I'm not here for pity. I'm here because arrangements decay. Wards shift. Debts get sold to new owners who read the fine print differently. If anyone ever comes asking — and someday someone will — you know nothing, you saw nothing, and above all, *he* knows nothing. A secret half-kept is worse than none. A child who knows what he is and hides it anyway leaks guilt like a cracked jug. Keep him ignorant and loved. Ignorant and loved survives. Knowing and afraid does not."

At the door, she paused. From the other room came the sound of Cyrus laughing at something — at nothing, at the air, at a joke only he could hear — and both women stood in the silence after it, listening.

"He'll be extraordinary," Ilse said quietly. "Whatever happens. Make sure he gets to be ordinary first. Ordinary is armor."

Then she was gone into the rain, and Maren stood holding the spoon she'd never needed as a weapon, understanding that she had just become the keeper of a secret shaped like a boy.

---

*Present day.*

The letter had come at dawn with a courier Maren didn't know, who hadn't waited for a reply, who had ridden off at speed as though the letter might change its mind.

Maren had recognized the hand on the wrapping before she recognized anything else. Nineteen years since Ilse Vey stood in her kitchen, and Maren had never forgotten the sharp, slanted script of the single instruction she'd left behind — a note that said only *Burn this unread*, tucked into Cyrus's swaddling, which Maren had burned within the hour, obedient to the last.

Now another one. Same hand. Same four words, pressed hard into the paper, the ink blotted in places as though written by someone whose fingers wouldn't stay steady.

*Burn this unread.*

Maren sat at the kitchen table with the letter in front of her and did not burn it.

Outside, the morning went on being ordinary. Goats complained. The kettle ticked toward boiling. Somewhere up the slope, Cyrus was doing his rounds, and if she listened — she always listened — she could catch him talking to the nannies in that low, easy murmur, telling them gossip they had no way of knowing, and the goats answering in their blunt, satisfied bleats, and the whole exchange so seamless that anyone would swear it was a man thinking aloud.

Ignorant and loved. She'd managed it for twenty-two years. She deserved a medal, or a cell, depending on who was judging.

And now a dead woman's handwriting on her table.

Because Ilse was dead. Maren knew it the way you know weather coming — a pressure behind the eyes. Couriers didn't send final letters unless there was nothing left to send them *from*. Something had gone wrong out there beyond the stone, something that had chased Ilse Vey all the way to the end of her scattered ledgers, and the letter in Maren's hands was either a warning or a confession or an apology, and she would never know which, because the instructions were clear and had always been clear and Ilse had paid for those instructions with—

With everything. With her whole scattered self. So that a boy could grow up ordinary.

"Oh, you stubborn woman," Maren whispered. "You absolute mule."

She thought of Cyrus at three, pulling the color out of the grass and laughing at his own power. She thought of him at nine, sobbing with rage because she'd told him the crows weren't really answering, that he was too old for fancies now, and watching him fold that part of himself away with a child's terrible efficiency, and hating herself even as she did it, because Ilse had been right: he'd slept easier after. Ignorant and loved.

She thought of him at sixteen, standing at the fence line looking east with an expression she'd pretended not to see, the expression of a boy pressing his palms against a wall he couldn't name.

The walls that keep the wolves out feel identical to the walls that keep the child in.

The kettle screamed. Maren got up, poured the water over the leaves, and stood with her hands around the warm pot, buying time the way Ilse had bought time, installment by installment.

If she read it, she'd know. And if she knew, she'd have to tell him, or lie to him face to face for whatever remained, and she had spent twenty-two years lying to him gently and did not know if she had another lie left in her. But if she burned it unread, she kept faith with a dead woman's plan — and the dead woman was dead, and plans made by the living for the dead's benefit have a way of rotting.

*He'll hate you for it, eventually.* Ilse had said fifteen, sixteen. He was twenty-two. Maren counted it privately as the year's one great victory.

She carried the letter to the stove.

It was, she told herself, an act of love. Every concealment had been an act of love; she had built a whole cathedral of silence out of acts of love, stone by patient stone, and lived in it comfortably, and called it home.

The paper caught. Flame ran up the corner, curling the edges, eating the sharp slanted hand line by line. Maren watched it with her jaw tight, and in the last second before the fire took the body of the letter, her eye snagged on a fragment — half a line, unburnt, the ink swimming:

*...the thirteenth moon turns, tell him the truth, he'll need...*

Then it was ash.

Maren stood over the stove for a long time. The kettle ticked. Outside, faintly, up the slope, the goats were making noise — not their ordinary complaining, but something sharper, clustered and agitated, and beneath it, unmistakably, Cyrus's voice, high with something that was not anger and not fear but some third thing she had no name for.

The cathedral of silence had just developed its first crack, and through the crack, distantly, she could hear the world outside beginning to talk.

Maren wiped her hands on her apron, took down her shawl, and went up the hill to see what her son was shouting at.

Behind her, on the stove, the last scrap of Ilse Vey's letter flared white and died, taking the rest of the sentence — *he'll need a guide, and he won't like him* — with it into the dark.
