## 5. Bramble Speals Plainly

The raven had been watching Cyrus for three days.

Cyrus knew this the way they knew most things about animals—not through words, exactly, but through a pressure at the edge of thought, like a name remembered just before it's spoken. Ravens were not subtle creatures. Even inside the valley where magic went to die, even muffled and half-asleep as every creature here seemed to be, this particular raven had presence. It perched on the fence post above the goat pen each morning with the posture of someone waiting to be introduced.

"You're staring again," Maren said from the doorway, flour on her hands. "That bird's been there since dawn. If it's after the eggs, throw something at it."

"I don't think it wants the eggs."

"What does it want, then?"

Cyrus looked at the raven. The raven looked back with one eye, then the other, in that unsettling way birds had of making you feel inspected rather than observed. Its feathers were black as spilled ink but for a single grey feather at its throat, like a scar it wore deliberately.

"Company," Cyrus said, which was true, though not the whole truth. The whole truth was that when Cyrus reached toward the raven with the wordless part of themselves—the part they kept folded up small and hidden, even from Maren—the raven *pushed back*. Not away. Back. Like a hand pressed flat against theirs through a wall.

Animals didn't do that here. Inside Highmoor's ward, the gift was a whisper of a whisper, a feeling more than a language. But this raven answered.

Maren snorted and went back inside. Cyrus stayed on the fence rail, splitting wood for kindling without much attention to whether they were making kindling or confetti.

*Well?* the raven said. *Are you going to sit there all day, or are we going to talk?*

The axe stopped mid-swing.

Cyrus did not fall off the fence, though they considered it briefly as an option. Talking was not what happened between Cyrus and animals. Animals sent pictures—warm, blunt, simple pictures. *Food. Fear. Go away. Stay.* They did not send words. They certainly did not send words with that particular dry, put-upon tone, as though Cyrus were a slow student and the raven a tutor who had given up expecting better.

"You can speak," Cyrus said aloud, stupidly, because their mouth insisted on confirming things their mind already knew.

*I can,* said the raven. *And you can hear me, which is far more interesting. Do you know how long I've been sitting on that post hoping you'd be worth the wait? Three days. Three days of eating grubs and pretending to be an ordinary corvid. I have been *humiliated*, and I want you to know I blame you.*

"Blame me for—"

*For existing interestingly. It's a lot to ask of a person, I know. And yet.*

Cyrus set down the axe very carefully, the way one does when the ground has begun to feel unreliable. The ward. Everyone in Highmoor knew the stories: nothing magical could live inside the boundary stone's reach. That was the whole point of the valley. Whatever Cyrus was, whatever leaked out of them despite the stone's dampening hum, it was small enough to pass unnoticed—or so they'd believed their entire life.

But a talking raven was not small. A talking raven was not subtle. A talking raven was a lit candle walking through a town built entirely of fear.

"How are you here?" Cyrus asked. "Inside the ward?"

The raven preened its wing, drawing out the moment with the shamelessness of a performer who knows the audience is already hooked. *I crossed it. Obviously.*

"That's impossible."

*Most things are, until they aren't. I crossed your little forgetting-stone eleven years ago, and I've been living in your woods eating pine nuts and regretting my choices ever since. The ward doesn't stop things from entering, girl-boy-whatever-you-are. It stops things from being *remembered*. From the outside. From within—" he cocked his head at Cyrus, "—it mostly stops things from being *noticed*. Which is why nobody in this charming valley has ever noticed that their goats are having conversations with you, or that the dogs tell you where the lost lambs are, or that old Widow Tetch's cat has been filing detailed reports on her for years."

"He hasn't."

*He absolutely has. She hides brandy in the flour bin. You knew that too, didn't you.*

Cyrus opened their mouth. Closed it. The cat had told them, yes—in the vague, smug way cats communicated everything—but they had filed it under ordinary knowledge, harmless, unexamined. Now every quiet conversation, every animal confidence they'd ever received, rearranged itself into evidence.

"You're not afraid," Cyrus said quietly. "That I'll—"

*Report you? To whom? The valley elders who'd drown you in the millpond if they knew? No.* The raven hopped down the fence rail closer, and up close his eyes were not bird-black but deep brown, almost warm, ringed in grey like storm clouds deciding what to become. *I'm telling you what you are because I need something from you, and because—though I will deny this later, possibly under oath—I don't think you deserve the millpond.*

* * *

His name, he said, was Bramble. He delivered this information with the air of a man presenting credentials, then ruined the effect by immediately asking if Cyrus had any cheese.

They talked in the birch stand behind the goat pen, out of sight of the house, while Bramble ate half a wheel of hard cheese that Cyrus would spend weeks explaining to Maren. He talked the way water runs downhill—constantly, finding every low place, pooling wherever Cyrus showed interest.

He claimed—he said *claimed*, and Cyrus noted that he never quite said anything so solid as *was*—to have lived in the court beneath the mountain. Not as a raven. As something else. When Cyrus pressed, he fluffed his feathers and became very interested in a birch beetle.

*A servant,* he admitted finally. *Of a sort. I was... let us say I was staff. Low staff. Staff so low I was practically geology.*

"What did you steal?"

Bramble froze mid-beetle. *Who says I stole anything?*

"You said 'exiled for theft' the moment I asked why you left. You haven't stopped talking since, which means you're nervous, and you mentioned theft before I asked, which means you wanted me to ask."

There was a long pause. Somewhere in the trees, a jay scolded them both, and Cyrus felt the jay's irritation arrive in their chest like a pebble dropped in still water—simple, honest, *go away, mine, mine*.

*You're going to be insufferable,* Bramble said, with something dangerously close to admiration. *Fine. Yes. I stole. I stole a voice.*

"A voice."

*My own, technically. Or—a voice. Mine now. Look.* He hopped onto a fallen log, turned to face Cyrus fully, and for the first time since the fence post, he stopped performing. What remained was smaller and older and tired. *In the mountain court, everything is kept. Souls. Songs. Names. Voices. The powerful take what they want and shelve it, and everyone below them spends their lives trying to climb high enough to take something back. I was nobody. I had nothing anyone wanted except my service. So I took the only thing I could carry: I stole a voice out of the vault and swallowed it, and I flew, and I did not stop flying until I hit your lovely gray curtain of forgetting.*

"And the voice was yours?"

*Does it matter?* he snapped—and then, catching himself, gentled in a way that seemed to cost him. *It matters to you. That's the trouble with people like you. Everything matters. Fine. It was mine. It was taken from me when I entered the court's service, the way all our voices are taken, and I stole it back, and the taking of it changed me into—this.* He spread one wing, black and unremarkable. *A thief in a borrowed body. Exiled twice over: once by the court, once by your ward, which won't let me leave without losing what I am. Eleven years, Cyrus Nystrom. I have eaten pine nuts for eleven years.*

The name landed like a stone in Cyrus's stomach. They hadn't told him their name.

"Ilse," Cyrus said. "You knew Ilse."

Something flickered across the raven's face—grief, quickly buried under practicality, like a coin slipped beneath a rug. *Everyone in the court knew Ilse. Ilse was the kind of person who learned names. Dangerous habit. Expensive one, eventually.* He fixed Cyrus with both eyes now, and his voice lost its performance entirely. *She came looking for you. Did she tell you what she paid?*

"She said I was the one she paid for."

*Then she told you true. Twenty-two years ago, a woman walked out of the mountain carrying a bargain in her teeth, and the price of it bought a warded cradle in a forgotten valley, and the goods purchased were you.* Bramble's voice softened by a degree. *You've spent your whole life being hidden, child. Ilse spent hers paying for it. The debt comes due when the thirteenth moon fills, and the court collects what it collects.*

"Souls," Cyrus said. "It keeps souls."

*Keeps them. Tastes them. Eats them, when it's hungry, and it is always hungry.* Bramble hopped closer along the log. *Ilse's soul is on a shelf right now, in a vault full of lanterns, whispering to itself in the dark. And when the thirteenth moon turns full, the vault opens, and the sovereign drinks, and Ilse becomes nothing but warmth in a monster's belly.*

The birches moved overhead. The goat pen smelled of straw and animal patience, and somewhere behind the house Maren was singing to herself over the bread, off-key and unbothered, and all of it—the whole soft valley—suddenly felt like a room with a locked door and something breathing on the other side.

"So I go," Cyrus said. Their voice came out steadier than expected. "To the court. Before the moon turns."

*You go to the court,* Bramble agreed. *Through country you've never seen, past a Warden whose entire profession is finding people exactly like you, into a fortress made of hunger, to steal from a vault guarded by the thing that owns the mountain. Alone. With no plan. And no idea where any of it is.*

"Do you?"

Bramble grinned, and it was a terrible, wonderful sight—a raven's beak attempting a human expression and mostly succeeding. *Child. I was staff. Low staff, geology-adjacent staff, but staff. I know the tunnels. I know which doors lie. I know the vault. I know the shape of the wards and the taste of the seals. I know the court better than anyone who isn't currently being eaten by it.*

"And what does this cost me?" Cyrus asked. "Nothing in your story has come free yet. Not Ilse's bargain. Not yours."

For a moment Bramble looked genuinely delighted. *Oh, I *like* you. Yes. There's a price, and it's simple, and you may hate it. My voice—" he tapped his grey-throated feather with one foot, "—is still written in the ledger. Stolen property, outstanding debt. So long as the vault stands and my theft goes unpardoned, I'm a fugitive wearing feathers, and the day I fly back over that mountain, the voice gets ripped out of me and I get shelved next to Ilse. So." He drew himself up. *Free me first. Strike my name from the ledger. Make my theft a pardon instead of a crime. Then I walk you through the front door of hell with a smile on my face and a song in my throat—which, incidentally, will be the first song I've sung in eleven years, so I apologize in advance for the quality.*

"And if I free every soul in that vault instead?" Cyrus asked. "Yours among them?"

Bramble went still.

It was a small thing, that stillness, but Cyrus had spent twenty-two years reading the small things—the flicked ear, the lowered head, the held breath—and they saw what passed over the raven then. Not calculation. Something rawer. Hope, maybe, arriving so unexpectedly that he didn't know where to put it.

*Every soul,* he repeated carefully.

"If I'm breaking in anyway. If the vault has to open. It seems wasteful to open it halfway."

The silence stretched. Above them the birch leaves turned their silver undersides to the wind, and in the goat pen one of the nannies bleated a question at Cyrus—*food? food now?*—and got no answer, which alarmed her more than any answer would have.

Then Bramble laughed.

It was a shocking sound, a real laugh, rusty and croaking and entirely undignified, and he laughed until he had to steady himself on the log, wings half-spread, wheezing. *Halfway. Open the vault halfway. Oh, Ilse would have loved you. She had the same disease—this compulsion to fix everything she touched, consequences be damned.* He wiped one eye against his shoulder, which accomplished nothing, and composed himself with visible effort. *Yes. All right. Every soul, if we can manage it, and if we can't, we die trying, which is at least a memorable way to go. Partners, then. You and me and no plan whatsoever.*

"Bargain struck," Cyrus said, and held out an arm.

Bramble regarded the arm with deep suspicion. *I don't do the shoulder thing. It's demeaning. I'll fly near you, at a respectful distance, in a manner that suggests we merely travel together.*

"You sat on my fence post for three days."

*That was surveillance. Entirely different.*

But when Cyrus stood and started back toward the house, the raven lifted off the log and settled on their shoulder anyway, light as a secret, and neither of them mentioned it.

That night, Cyrus lay awake listening to the valley's small voices—the drowsy mutter of goats, the owl's patient arithmetic, the river keeping its endless count—and for the first time the gift didn't feel like a wound to hide. It felt like a hand reaching back. Tomorrow they would have to find the map again, and study it, and begin to understand what Ilse had died carrying. Tomorrow there would be fear enough.

Tonight, on the windowsill, a raven was humming—badly, tunelessly, with enormous joy—an old song from a court beneath the mountain, practicing for a homecoming he claimed not to want.

And far to the north, though Cyrus couldn't know it yet, something with a Warden's cold eyes paused on a ridge line, turned its face toward a forgotten valley, and began, unhurried, to walk.
