## 6. Crossing the Stone

Cyrus told the goats first.

It seemed only proper. They had been the first creatures Cyrus had ever spoken to without words—back when they were small enough that Maren still believed the girl-child she'd pulled from the snow was simply quiet—and they would be the last to hear of it before it happened.

"I'm going tonight," Cyrus said, not with their mouth but in that other way, the way that lived somewhere behind the breastbone and traveled out through the hands and the eyes and the particular angle of one's shoulders. The way that did not exist, officially, inside the ward of Highmoor. "I'm going past the stone."

The lead doe, a gray-jawed matriarch named Sorrel who had opinions about everything from fence posts to weather, chewed thoughtfully for a long moment.

*Cold,* she sent back. *Dark. Wolves on the ridge two nights ago—I smelled them through your mother's dog, who is useless.*

"Thank you for the encouragement."

*You are welcome.* She bumped Cyrus's hip with her forehead, nearly toppling them into the trough, which in goat language was either a blessing or an accusation. Possibly both.

The barn settled around them, hay-dust drifting gold in the last of the lantern light. Beyond it, through the slats, Cyrus could see the valley lying under a fat white moon—the twelfth moon, Bramble had said, twelve gone and one turning. Somewhere out there, past the tree line and the high pasture and the humming boundary stone that every soul in Highmoor walked past without ever hearing, Ilse's soul sat in a vault beneath a mountain, counting down to its own devouring.

And Cyrus was about to walk out of the only home they had ever known, carrying nothing but a dead woman's map, a sardonic raven, and a gift that would see them burned if anyone in this valley ever learned of it.

"You're stalling," said Bramble from the rafters.

Cyrus jumped. "I was saying goodbye."

"You were telling livestock your travel plans. There's a difference, though I grant it's a narrow one." The raven dropped from the beam in a flurry of black feathers and landed on the stall rail, tilting his head at Sorrel with what looked suspiciously like professional respect. "At least the goats have sense. *Wolves on the ridge,* indeed. Your mother's dog is a disgrace to his lineage."

"He's very sweet."

"That is exactly what I said." Bramble ruffled his feathers and fixed Cyrus with one glittering eye. "The ward thins after midnight. If we're crossing, we cross now, while the stone drowses. Every hour we linger, some part of me remembers I am a coward, and I'd rather not spend more time acquainted with that fellow than necessary."

Cyrus took down their pack from its peg. It had been packed and repacked three times: bread and hard cheese, a skin of water, the map case wrapped in oilcloth against rain, Maren's good knife, a change of clothes that would be useless within a day and that Cyrus brought anyway because leaving them felt like leaving a piece of home. Underneath it all, folded flat, the letter Maren had burned—or thought she had burned; the corner that survived the hearth was tucked there now like a splinter Cyrus couldn't stop touching.

They paused at the barn door and looked back once. Not at the house—looking at the house would have undone them—but at the shape of the place itself: the low roofline, the crooked chimney, the pasture silvered by moonlight where Sorrel and her sisters stood watching with their strange, patient eyes.

Then Cyrus closed the door softly, as if closing it on a sleeping thing, and walked out of the valley of Highmoor.

* * *

The path up to the boundary stone was one Cyrus had walked a hundred times, but never at night, and never with intent. By day it was a shepherd's track, worn pale through the bracken, climbing the shoulder of the fell until the trees gave out and the wind owned everything. The villagers called the stone the Old Grandfather, and children were dared to touch it, and nobody ever quite explained why everyone who touched it came back looking faintly disappointed, as if they'd expected something and couldn't name what.

Cyrus knew why. The stone was a door, and the door was locked, and the lock had been thrown over Cyrus's whole life before Cyrus was old enough to know there was anything on the other side of it.

"It hums," Cyrus said as they neared it. They could feel it now, a vibration in the teeth, in the fillings of old scars, in the soles of the feet. "Why doesn't anyone else feel it?"

"They do," Bramble said, riding the wind just above Cyrus's shoulder. "They call it the mountain singing. They call it rheumatism. They call it the presence of the Almighty, depending on the village. People are marvelously inventive about not noticing things." He landed on the stone itself, casual as a lord taking his chair. "This is the part where I tell you what happens next, so listen, because I'll only say it twice and the second time will be in a mocking voice."

"Bramble—"

"Beyond the stone, your gift wakes fully. Inside, it's been muffled all these years—like a bell wrapped in wool. You've learned to live with the wool. Out there—" he tapped the granite with his beak, *tok, tok*, "—there is no wool. Every creature for miles will pour into you at once. Birdsong and worm-song and the great slow dreaming of trees. It is," and here the raven's voice did something unexpected, dropping its irony like a coat, "the most beautiful thing you will ever feel, and it will also try to drown you. So breathe. Anchor to your body. Count your fingers if you must. And do not—not—reach for everything at once. Do you understand?"

"No," Cyrus admitted.

"Good. Honesty. We'll survive yet." Bramble hopped aside, making room. "Whenever you're ready. Or run away. Both are acceptable answers; only one of them is interesting."

The stone rose before them, taller than Cyrus, older than the valley's oldest stories. Moonlight lay along its face, and up close Cyrus could see the carvings worn nearly smooth—a ring of interlocking shapes, spirals and knots, and at the center a single symbol that made the skin between their shoulder blades prickle: an eye, open, patient, blind.

Ilse had crossed here dying. Ilse had come *through* this door, trailing sigils that shouldn't have existed inside the ward, and fallen at the ford below with blood in her mouth and Cyrus's name in hers.

*You're the one I paid for.*

"Right," Cyrus whispered, and stepped past the Old Grandfather into the wild.

* * *

Later, Cyrus would never be able to describe what happened next in any order that made sense, because it did not happen in order. It happened all at once, everywhere, like being born.

One step, the silence. The valley silence Cyrus had lived inside for twenty-two years without knowing it was silence—the deep cotton-wool hush of a world seen through glass. One step.

Two steps—

Sound.

No. Not sound. Sound was too small a word. It was as if Cyrus had been deaf since birth and someone had unwound the bandages all at once, mid-stride, on a cliff edge above a waterfall.

The world *roared* in.

Every cricket in the meadow grass below, each one a tiny struck bell. The owls, hunting, their attention sharp as knives sliding through the dark. A vixen trotting the ridge line with three dreams in her belly and frost on her whiskers. Fish in the ford holding their cold thoughts like coins. Worms—*worms*—turning the soil in their patient millions, a vast subterranean murmur like the earth muttering in its sleep. An oak on the far hillside, four hundred years old, dreaming its slow dream of seasons. A hare's terror. A badger's contentment. A moth's brief burning want for the moon.

And underneath it all, threading through everything, the current itself: magic, raw and running, the way water runs under fields whether or not anyone digs a well. Cyrus could feel it the way they had always, secretly, felt the moods of animals—except now the animal was everything. The whole world was breathing against their skin.

Cyrus's knees hit the ground.

They hadn't decided to fall. Their body simply quit, overwhelmed, and Cyrus knelt on the frozen turf with their arms wrapped around themselves, gasping, while ten thousand small lives poured through them like light through a cracked window.

*Breathe,* said Bramble, from very far away. *Breathe. Fingers.*

"F-fingers," Cyrus chattered. They held up a hand. Ten. Held up the other. Ten more. Twenty fingers, which was arithmetically alarming, but the counting steadied them anyway. "Twenty. That's—too many."

"You're panicking. Numbers were never going to be your strong suit tonight. Again."

Cyrus breathed. In through the nose, out through the mouth, the way Maren taught them when nightmares came. Slowly the flood stopped trying to tear them apart and began, instead, to settle—layering itself into something almost bearable, almost *musical*. The crickets kept their rhythm. The owl's knife-sharp focus softened into something like companionship, a distant hunter aware now of Cyrus's warmth and unconcerned by it. The oak dreamed on, and Cyrus found that if they didn't grab at the dream, didn't clutch, it let them sit beside it like a fire.

"Oh," Cyrus said. It came out broken. They said it again. "Oh."

"Yes," said Bramble, gently, for him. "Oh."

Tears were running down Cyrus's face and they hadn't noticed starting. This was what had been taken from them. Not hidden—taken, smothered, walled off by a stone their adoptive mother must have begged for, paid for, in whatever coin such things cost. All those years of feeling half-heard even among the valley creatures, working through wool and gauze, straining for whispers—when *this* was what waited outside. This ocean. This choir.

They laughed, wetly, helplessly, sitting in the frost with a raven watching them like a disapproving uncle.

"It's horrible," Cyrus managed. "It's wonderful. I can hear a fox thinking about a mouse and I can feel how sorry she'll be afterward and I can feel the *mouse*, Bramble, the mouse is right there—"

"Then don't look. First lesson of the wild: you cannot carry every heart you hear, or yours stops beating. Grieve what crosses your path. Wish well what doesn't." Bramble hopped closer, and for once there was nothing sardonic in his bright eye. "That is the whole of beast-speaking, girl. Everything else is vocabulary."

Cyrus wiped their face with a sleeve that immediately froze stiff at the cuffs. They should stand. They should move—the Warden of Seals might already be following Ilse's trail, Bramble had said so, and every hour counted, thirteen moons and twelve gone. They knew all of this. They repeated it to themselves like a litany.

And then they reached, just slightly, toward the nearest living thing—a juniper shrug of a tree at the slope's edge, bent and stubborn—and the juniper greeted them.

Not in words. Junipers didn't have words. It was more like a nod across a room from someone who had also been waiting a long time in the cold. *Ah,* said the juniper, in the way of slow green things. *Another one. It has been ninety years since one of you passed this stone awake.*

Ninety years. Cyrus sat with that, and with the tree, and felt the shape of their own loneliness change. All those years in Highmoor, loving animals in whispers, hiding the best part of themselves in a valley built to erase it—and all that time, there had been others? Speakers? Crossing here, going somewhere, doing *something* with what they were?

"What happened to them?" Cyrus asked aloud, and the wind took the question, and the juniper's answer was a long slow shudder of branches that meant, unmistakably: *Some went up the mountain. None came back down.*

Bramble had gone very still on Cyrus's knee. When he spoke, his voice was carefully light, which from Bramble was the loudest alarm there was.

"Ninety years," he said. "Yes. Well." He shook himself, feathers rattling like a dealer stacking coins. "Sentimental hour's over. You wanted to turn back—I saw it in your face, don't deny it—so let us establish, for the record, that you didn't."

Cyrus looked back down the slope.

They hadn't realized they'd done it—turned their whole body toward home—but there it was: Highmoor below, small and lamplit and safe, the ward's silence spreading over it like a blanket pulled up to the chin. Maren was there, asleep or pretending to sleep, having watched Cyrus leave with that terrible composed face she wore when she was breaking. The goats were there. The ford was there. Everything Cyrus loved fit inside that silence, and the silence had been killing them slowly for twenty-two years, and both of these things were true at once, and no one had warned them that leaving would feel like tearing a page out of the middle of a book.

"I almost turned back," Cyrus said quietly.

"Of course you did. Turning back is always almost. It's the most popular road in the world and it goes nowhere." Bramble launched off Cyrus's knee, beat upward, and circled once against the enormous wheeling stars. "Come on, then. The mountain's north-northeast, the fens are east, and everything between wants to eat you or freeze you. I recommend we disappoint them all."

Cyrus stood. Their legs held. Behind them the boundary stone hummed its blind, patient hum, and ahead the wild opened—dark, roaring with life, terrible and generous and real.

For the first time in their life, Cyrus Nystrom stepped forward as the whole of what they were. The owl noted it. The fox noted it. The juniper wished them well in its creaking green way, and the wind coming down off the northern heights carried a scent that made Bramble swear roundly in three languages:

Smoke. Cold iron. And something else, something wrong, like a seal broken on a jar that should never have been opened.

"Someone's crossed our trail," Bramble said, flat and quick. "Recent. Moving fast. Girl—run."
