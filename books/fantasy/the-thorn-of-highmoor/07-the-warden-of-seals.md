## 7. The Warden of Seals

The dead woman's trail ended at a ford, and the ford was full of goats.

Warden Sable stood on the near bank in the gray hour before dawn, rain beading on her oiled cloak, and considered the goats the way one considers a bad joke told by a superior officer: patiently, and with a mental note to remember it later.

"Six of them," said Vetch, her second, crouched at the water's edge with his nose nearly touching the mud. He was twenty, earnest, and built like a well-meaning door. "Maybe seven. Hard to say. They've churned the whole bank."

"I can count, Vetch."

"Yes, Warden." A pause. "It's the *seventh* I'm unsure about."

Sable let the silence do its work. It usually did. Vetch had been assigned to her eight months ago by the court's Chamberlain, who had a talent for pairing her with people who talked enough for two, perhaps on the theory that a Warden who filled the air with someone else's words would have less room in it for her own thoughts. The theory had failed spectacularly, but Sable kept the boy anyway. There was something useful about a man who saw seven goats where there were six. The world needed its over-counters.

She knelt where he'd been looking. The mud held the truth beneath the hoofprints: a smear of blood, diluted to rust by the rain, and beside it the ghost of a sigil pressed into the clay by a dying hand — a soul-mark, the kind the court branded into the flesh of those who owed it everything. Ilse Vane's mark. Ilse Vane, courier, thief of small mercies, debtor of thirteen years' standing. Dead now, or mostly dead when she'd crawled through this ford, and gone entirely somewhere since.

"She came out of the valley," Sable said.

"The dead valley?" Vetch frowned. "Nothing magical comes *out* of there. That's the whole point of it. The Wardens' Charter says—"

"The Charter is a list of things the court is afraid of, dressed up as a list of things we're afraid of." She touched the blood-smear. Still faintly warm to her senses, though warmth had nothing to do with temperature anymore; it was the residue of a bargain unraveling, the taste of a debt coming due. She could read it the way she'd once read the moods of hawks — except that reading hawk-moods had required a gift, and her gift was thirty years in a lantern under a mountain, and what remained in its place was this: a cold, clean attention, like standing in an empty church.

It was enough. It was always enough. That was the trick of it, and the tragedy, depending on the hour.

"She crossed the ward," Sable went on, rising. "Which means either the ward is failing—" She stopped herself. She did not finish sentences about the ward failing. Not aloud, not within a hundred miles of the mountain, because the mountain listened, and things the mountain heard tended to become things the mountain wanted discussed, and discussions before the court ended with someone's name on a shelf.

"—or someone carried her through," Vetch supplied helpfully.

"No one carries anything through that ward. The ward doesn't stop bodies. It stops *power*. And she was soaked in it, at the end." Sable looked east, toward Highmoor, where the morning fog sat on the hills like a lid on a pot. Somewhere behind that fog was the boundary stone, humming its dumb lullaby, keeping the valley empty as a swept floor. She had stood at that stone once, young and desperate, and turned back. Everyone turned back. That was what the valley was *for*: a place where people like her could be erased politely, without anyone having to swing an axe.

And yet Ilse Vane had walked out of it carrying a map, a debt, and apparently a purpose — and had died at this ford whispering to a goatherd.

That last part was the part that kept Sable's jaw tight. The local reeve's report lay in her satchel, and she had read it four times by candlelight, which was three more times than any report deserved. *A stranger found at the ford during the storm. Taken in by the Nystrom household. Died before morning. Buried quick, no rites, the family being private folk.* Private folk. In twenty-two years, the Nystrom woman — Maren, goat-keeper, widow, unremarkable in every particular the court's records bothered to note — had never once appeared on a ledger, a tithe roll, or a suspicion. People who were never suspected were either innocent or very good, and Sable had stopped believing in the first category around the time she'd sold her own soul to buy her way out of it.

"You think the goatherd killed her," Vetch said.

"I think the goatherd *listened* to her, which is worse." She swung back onto her horse, a long-boned gray mare named Disdain who had earned the name honestly. "A dying courier doesn't waste her last breaths on strangers. She spends them on the person she walked out of a warded valley to find. Which means the goatherd is either the destination or the message. Either way, they're coming with us."

"To the mountain?"

"To wherever the trail goes. Ilse didn't crawl through a river to die quietly. She crawled through it to pass something on." Sable gathered the reins. "And whatever she passed on is walking east right now with a goatherd's boots on."

* * *

They made the trade-town of Vell's Crossing by nightfall of the third day, and Sable hated it immediately and comprehensively.

The Market of Small Magics sprawled along the town's muddy spine like a spilled jewelry box: charm-sellers crying their wares in voices sweetened by their own merchandise, sprite-cages glinting in windows, a soul-broker's stall hung with lanterns that flickered in patterns too deliberate to be flame. Ordinary people moved among it all haggling over pennies, and that was the thing Sable hated — not the magic, but the *prices*. Everything sacred, marked down. Everything holy, two for three.

She had grown up in a village like this one, before the court found her. Before the day she'd stood in her father's barn with a fox's death in her hands — the fox had simply lain down when she asked, trusting, terrible — and understood exactly what she was and exactly what the world did to such creatures. The court's offer had come a year later, delivered by a courteous man with a courteous smile: *Your gift, kept safe in our vault. In exchange — position, protection, purpose.* She'd told herself she was trading a death sentence for a life. She had even, for some years, believed it.

The price had been this: she could still feel animals, faintly, the way you feel a limb that's been cut off in winter weather. An ache shaped like everything she'd lost. And in exchange, the ache pointed. It always pointed.

It was pointing now.

"Vetch." She reined in at the market's edge. "The horses."

"What about them?"

"Look at them."

Vetch looked. The picket lines were chaos, as always — forty horses, donkeys, and one ill-tempered mule, all shifting and stamping in the market din. But Sable saw what the over-counter couldn't: the pattern underneath. Every animal on the eastern line had turned its head east. Not wandering. *Attending*. Like dogs hearing a whistle pitched too high for human ears, except there was no whistle. There was only a speaker moving through the wilds beyond the town, and every beast within five miles was turning toward the sound of him like flowers toward sun.

Her amputated gift ached so hard her teeth hurt.

"Oh," Vetch said slowly. "Oh, that's — Warden, that's not normal, is it?"

"No."

"That's the kind of thing from the old stories. Beast-speakers." He said it the way country folk said *witch*, with a shiver he tried to hide behind respectability. "I thought they were all gone. The valley—"

"The valley keeps them. It doesn't unmake them. And wards fail, Vetch. Wards are promises, and promises are only as strong as the thing keeping them." She dismounted and handed Disdain to a stable-boy whose eyes were too busy counting coins to notice the horses' strange congregation. "Get rooms. Then find the reeve and ask, very casually, whether any traveler has come through recently buying supplies. Someone on foot or with a poor horse. Someone who looked at the animals too long, or too kindly."

"And if they have?"

"Then we're three days behind instead of five, and I'll buy you dinner." She paused. "The good tavern, not the one with the stew that moves."

Vetch brightened like a lamp and hurried off, and Sable stood alone in the market crowd with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, against the ache, listening to it point.

East. Toward the high fens, and past them, the mountain passes. Toward the court.

Toward home, said a voice she hadn't permitted herself in thirty years, and she put it away the way she put away everything else: carefully, in the dark, where it couldn't see the light of day and start wanting things.

* * *

The soul-broker's stall was doing brisk evening business when she drifted past it. She hadn't meant to stop. She never meant to stop. But the lanterns caught her eye — rows of them, small glass vessels each holding a mote of light that pulsed like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of geology — and the broker, a soft man with soft hands, mistook her attention for a customer's.

"Warden," he said, bowing with the agility of his profession. "Something for yourself? We've a fine selection. A piper's soul — musical temperament, very cheerful. A midwife's, if you want steady hands and patience. This one belonged to a poet, though I'll be honest, it's melancholy company."

"Do you know what happens to them?" Sable asked. "In the end. On the shelf."

The broker's smile stayed exactly where it was while the rest of his face went careful. "They're kept safe, Warden. Kept *safe*. That's the whole promise of the court. Nothing is ever truly lost down there."

"No," Sable agreed. "Nothing is ever lost. It's only eaten slowly enough that no one hears the chewing." She set a coin on his counter — more than courtesy required, less than apology — and walked on before he could decide whether he'd been threatened.

But the lanterns stayed with her. One of them, near the stall's edge, had pulsed differently when she leaned close. Not the slow geological heartbeat of the others. Something quicker. Something that had almost felt like — recognition.

She was thirty years out of practice at being spoken to. But she knew the sound of her own name in a language she'd sold, the way you know a song from childhood even after you've forgotten the words.

Ilse Vane had carried a soul-mark. Couriers who dealt in debts sometimes dealt in other cargo. If the broker had bought stock off Ilse's route — if even one lantern on his shelf had passed through the dead woman's hands —

Sable stood in the lamplit mud of Vell's Crossing and let herself consider, fully and dangerously, the possibility that she was not hunting a fugitive at all. That she was following the same trail Ilse had blazed, toward the same vault, where her own gift sat on its shelf whispering to itself in the dark. That the difference between Warden and thief was, in the end, mostly paperwork and timing.

"Careful," she murmured to herself. The mountain listened. Even here. Especially here.

But the thought had teeth now, and thoughts with teeth do not let go just because you tell them to behave.

* * *

Vetch found her an hour later on the inn's narrow balcony, where she'd taken up a post with sightlines to both road and sky. He was carrying two bowls of stew — the non-mobile kind, bless him — and wearing the expression of a man about to deliver news he'd rehearsed the whole walk over.

"Three travelers," he said. "Buying supplies yesterday morning. A raven — actually, Warden, you'll like this — a raven that *talked*, according to the farrier, who is either a liar or deeply shaken. And two figures. One older, round, a peddler type, very friendly, bought rope and lantern oil. And one younger. Hooded. Paid in odd coin."

"Odd how?"

"Old coin. Pre-ward minting, the farrier said. Valley money." Vetch set down the bowls. "Warden, the valley doesn't trade. Nobody's minted there in a generation."

Sable looked at the bowl of stew and did not eat. Old coin, spent carelessly by someone who had never needed money before. A talking raven — and there was a story in that, because ravens did not learn speech inside the ward, and birds that spoke outside it had almost always learned it *beneath the mountain*, which meant theft, which meant exile, which meant the court's business leaking out of the court like water through a cracked jar. A peddler with rope and lantern oil. And a hooded figure paying in a currency dead for twenty years, walking east with every horse in town watching them go.

The ache in her chest pointed so hard it trembled.

"They left when?"

"Dawn today. The peddler told everyone he was going to the fens to catch fen-lights in jars." Vetch hesitated. "He also told the barmaid, the blacksmith, and two separate children. For a smuggler, Warden, he's got a remarkable relationship with the truth."

"He's not a smuggler. He's a *decoy with a personality*." Almost, almost, Sable smiled. There was a time — she was nineteen, maybe twenty — when she'd have found that funny enough to laugh at. The court had taught her that laughter was a leak. She'd patched it. "Which means the quiet one is the cargo, or the thief, or the heir to something Ilse Vane bled out at a ford to deliver. And they're a day into the fens."

"The fens in autumn." Vetch looked genuinely distressed. "The marsh-elk are moving. The ground swallows horses. Last season a wool merchant—"

"I know what the fens do, Vetch."

"Yes, Warden." He picked up his stew, then set it down again, working up to something. She waited. He always worked up to something; it was part of his function, like a boiler. "Can I ask — and you don't have to answer — but the way you look east. When you think nobody's watching. Is it the hunt? Or is it—"

"It's the hunt."

"Right," said Vetch, who had been assigned to her for eight months and had already learned that her silences were load-bearing. He took his stew and retreated, pausing at the door. "For what it's worth, Warden — whoever they are, I hope the fens are gentle with them. The elk especially. They calve this time of year, and a spooked herd..."

He left the sentence unfinished and went to bed, because he was twenty and merciful, and mercy still seemed to him like a plan rather than a liability.

Sable stood alone on the balcony a long while, the ache pointing east, the mountain at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades.

Once, she had been what that hooded figure was: young, hunted, gifted, alive in a way the word *alive* barely covered afterward. Once, she had run through wild country with her heart hammering and every bird in the trees telling her which way the soldiers leaned. Once, someone had offered her a bargain, and she had been tired, and afraid, and twenty-two years old, and she had said yes.

She wondered what the figure would say. She wondered what she hoped the figure would say — and then she examined that hope the way she examined everything the court might overhear, turned it over, found it dangerous, and buried it.

It surfaced again anyway. Hopes were like that. So were souls, allegedly. So were gifts in lanterns, whispering to themselves on shelves, waiting thirty years for someone to walk past and recognize their own name.

At midnight, the ache *moved*.

Not a drift — a lurch, sudden and unmistakable, like a compass needle kicked by a lodestone. East by northeast, deep in the fens, something had flared: power, raw and unguarded and enormous, the signature of a speaker using the gift openly for the first time. She felt it the way she once felt weather in her knees, and beneath the professional cold of her attention something ancient and mutilated in her lifted its head and *howled*.

There it is, said the buried voice. There's the sound we used to make.

Sable gripped the balcony rail until her knuckles whitened. Below, in the stable yard, every horse was screaming.

"Vetch!" Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was the entire art of her life. "Saddle the horses. Now."

Boots hit floorboards above. Sable turned east, toward the dark line of the fens, toward the flare fading on the horizon of her senses like a struck bell going quiet — and found, to her considerable irritation, that her hands were shaking.

Not with fear. She'd done fear; fear was familiar furniture.

With recognition. Because whatever had just roared awake out in the marshlands wasn't running from the court the way she'd once run, blind and alone.

It was running *toward* it. With a map. And a raven. And a head start of exactly one day.

"Thirteen turns," she said softly, to the dark, to the mountain, to no one. "You always did cut it close, Ilse."

Then she went down to the horses, and the night swallowed the road east, and somewhere ahead of her, in the black water and the whispering reeds, a beast-speaker was about to learn what the wild cost — and what Sable had paid, thirty years ago, to stop paying it.
