## 8. The Market of Small Magics

The town announced itself a full hour before they reached it—not by sight, for the road curved through pine shadow the whole way, but by smell. Woodsmoke, frying onions, tanned leather, and beneath it all something stranger: a sweetness like crushed violets that made Bramble shift on Cyrus's shoulder and mutter, "Soul-smoke. Someone's burning something they shouldn't."

"You can smell souls?"

"I can smell *waste*," Bramble said. "There's a difference, and it smells exactly like that."

Cyrus had never seen a proper market. Highmoor's valley held three farms, a mill, and a chapel where the priest spoke to a congregation of eleven and a suspiciously attentive cow. Trade came to them twice a year in the form of a peddler with a limp and prices that could make Maren weep into her flour. So when the trees finally opened and Vell's Crossing spread itself below—roofs of slate and rust-red tile, banners snapping, a river thick with barges—Cyrus stopped walking without meaning to.

"Close your mouth," Bramble advised. "You look like a fledgling."

"I feel like one."

"That's because you are one. A twenty-two-year-old egg that just cracked." The raven smoothed a wing feather with his beak, affecting boredom, but his eye stayed fixed on the market with an appetite that had nothing to do with food. "Rules before we descend. One: don't touch anything that glows. Two: don't answer anyone who asks what you are. Three: if a woman with silver rings on every finger smiles at you, walk away. She'll want your teeth."

"*My teeth?*"

"Tooth-magic keeps beautifully," Bramble said, as though this were common knowledge everyone else had simply failed to acquire. "Now come along. I haven't been cheated properly in forty years, and I intend to correct that record."

* * *

Vell's Crossing was loud in a way Cyrus's whole life had not prepared them for. Not the loudness of storm or river—those were loud the way sleep is deep, all one voice—but the loudness of hundreds of separate wants, all shouting at once. A fishmonger crying the day's catch. Children daring each other toward a fortune-teller's striped tent. A man arguing passionately with a mule, and losing.

And underneath, faint as a pulse under bandages, the animals. Cyrus felt them the moment they passed the first stall—a knot of horses in a paddock beyond the market square, their attention brushing against Cyrus like moths against a lantern. Inside the ward, their gift had been a whisper heard through stone. Out here it was a hand pressed flat against their chest.

They kept their head down and walked.

The market arranged itself in rings, and each ring sold something worse. The outer ring was honest: rope, apples, boots, knives, wool dyed the color of autumn. The second ring sold luck—a hundred little charms swinging from strings, iron horseshoes blessed by someone's cousin, knotted red thread "guaranteed against ill-wishing, refunds void upon death." Cyrus paused at a stall where an old woman sold bottled dreams, each vial swirling with a soft grey light, and felt Bramble tense on their shoulder.

"Don't even breathe near those," he murmured. "Dream-bottles are crueler than soul-lanterns, if you ask me. At least a soul knows what it lost."

The third ring was where the sweetness in the air grew thick enough to taste.

Cyrus saw the sprites first. They hung in cages no bigger than fists—wrought silver and glass, delicate as jewelry—and inside each cage burned a small furious light with a face. One sprite pressed its palms against the glass as Cyrus passed. Its mouth moved. No sound came out, but Cyrus's gift, uncoiling despite every caution, caught the shape of its wanting: *out out out out out*, repeated like a heartbeat.

Cyrus walked faster. Bramble said nothing, which from Bramble was a sermon.

It was the fourth ring that stopped them entirely.

* * *

The soul-broker's stall looked, at first glance, like nothing at all. It was smaller than the charm-sellers' booths, plainer, tucked between a spice merchant and a man selling singing bowls that sang only when nobody listened. There were no goods displayed. Just a table, a chair, a ledger bound in something too pale to be leather, and a broker who might have been any age between thirty and ninety—a smooth, unfinished face like wax left near a fire.

A sign above the table read, in careful script: **FAIR PRICES. FINAL SALES.**

"What do they sell?" Cyrus whispered.

"Purchases," Bramble said. "That's the trick of it. Everyone thinks the court buys souls with gold. Gold's for peasants. The real currency is simpler." His voice dropped until it was nearly lost under the market noise. "*Room.* Somewhere to belong. Talent recognized. A mother's illness cured. Whatever you're starving for, they sell it to you at the only price you have."

Cyrus watched a young man approach the stall—broad-shouldered, farm-calloused, wearing the hopeful expression of someone about to sign something. He spoke to the broker too quietly to hear. The broker nodded once, opened the ledger, turned it around. The young man read. His face went through several seasons of weather—hesitation, longing, resolve—and then he pricked his thumb on a small silver needle and pressed it to the page.

The page drank the blood. And something else left him—Cyrus felt it go, a warmth guttering out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. The young man straightened, smiling now, lighter somehow, and walked away whistling. He passed within arm's reach of Cyrus, and for half a heartbeat their eyes met.

There was nothing behind them. Not cruelty. Not emptiness, even. Just *less*. A room with the furniture removed.

"Cyrus." Bramble's beak tapped their ear, sharp. "Walk. Now."

Cyrus walked. Their hands were shaking and they couldn't have said why—the man still had his soul, didn't he? Most of it? Enough of it?

"He'll live a long happy life," Bramble said quietly, reading them with uncomfortable ease. "That's the horror of it. It works. That's how they've lasted a thousand years under the mountain. Nobody screams. Everybody *smiles*."

* * *

They needed supplies, and supplies required coin they did not have. What Cyrus had instead was a talent, and after the soul-broker's stall the irony sat in their stomach like a swallowed stone. Still. Ilse's map wouldn't follow itself, and the thirteenth moon wasn't waiting.

They found the paddocks on the market's far edge, where a liveryman named Grett was losing a war of attrition with a dun mare. The horse—Bramble named her "the Duchess" immediately, with deep respect—had thrown two riders, bitten a groom, and was currently expressing her opinion of bridles by standing perfectly still and radiating contempt.

"She's devil-blooded," Grett said, wiping his neck. "Three years I've had her. Won't let a soul near her."

Cyrus considered the mare. The mare considered Cyrus. What passed between them was brief and businesslike: she wanted the bit gone, the stable's corner stall left open, and the piebald gelding next door to stop staring at her hindquarters. In exchange, she would tolerate the bridle. Today. Once.

"She says the bit pinches her left jaw," Cyrus said, before they could stop themselves.

Grett stared. "She *says*."

"There's a sore spot. Under the strap." Cyrus pointed. Grett lifted the mare's lip, found the abraded patch, and swore with the fluency of a man whose professional worldview had just been gently tipped over.

What followed took ten minutes and consisted mostly of Cyrus standing very still while the Duchess delivered a lengthy complaint, nodding at appropriate intervals, and then fitting the bridle themselves, adjusting the cheekpiece with two fingers. When it was done, the mare sighed through her nostrils and lowered her head—an apology, or the closest thing to one a duchess offers.

Grett paid them in supplies: dried meat, a coil of good rope, a flint, a canvas cloak that smelled of sheep but shed rain, and a small purse of coppers "for looking at my other horses sometime, if you're ever back through." He also paid them in information, freely given over a shared cup of cider: the mountain road was watched; strangers were being asked questions at the toll bridge; a Warden's seal-stamp had been seen at the crossing inn two nights ago.

The word *Warden* went through Cyrus like cold water.

"Sable?" they asked, before thinking.

Grett blinked. "You know her name?"

"Everyone does," said Bramble smoothly, before Cyrus's face could finish betraying them. He hopped to Cyrus's other shoulder and fixed Grett with a raven's flattest stare. "Famous woman. Terrible reputation. We admire her from a great distance and keep moving. Good day."

* * *

It was at the edge of the third ring, counting coppers and trying not to count moons, that Cyrus heard the argument.

"—a *fair* price, you great walnut, which yours is not!"

The speaker was short and broad and wrapped in a coat with more pockets than fabric, and they stood before a charm-seller's stall defending a small brass lantern with both arms curled around it like a child shielding a sweet. The lantern glowed. Inside the glow, something turned over and over, restless.

"The sprite's dim," the charm-seller said, bored. "Half-dead. I'm offering you copper for charity."

"It's not *dim*, it's *sad*, there's a difference—" The seller waved them off. The short figure turned away, shoulders tight, and nearly walked directly into Cyrus.

"Oh! Pardon—sorry—" They looked up. Their eyes were quick and brown and did a rapid, expert inventory of Cyrus in about two seconds: the wrong-cut cloak, the valley accent, the bird. "You're not from here either. Nobody local wears a cloak like they're apologizing for it." A grin, sudden and disarming. "Odji. Sprite-seller. Well—sprite-*rescuer*, technically, but 'seller' pays the tolls."

"Bramble," said Bramble, before Cyrus could decide whether to lie. "Thief, exile, moral compass. The large quiet one is Cyrus. They're shy."

"I'm not—"

"They're shy," Bramble confirmed.

Odji laughed—a bright, barking sound—and Cyrus, who had spent twenty-two years being careful with strangers, felt something in their chest loosen by a single degree. There was something about Odji's face that made lying feel like kicking a dog.

"That lantern," Cyrus said, because it was easier than conversation. "Is it really sad?"

Odji's grin faded into something more careful, more kind. "You can tell?"

"The light moves wrong. Like it's turning in circles."

For a long moment Odji just looked at them. Then, quietly, they raised the lantern so the glow spilled across Cyrus's face, and inside it the sprite drifted close to the glass—and stopped. Its restless circling stilled. Its light, dim as the charm-seller had said, brightened by one small degree.

"Huh," Odji breathed. "It likes you." They lowered the lantern, and their expression had shifted into something Cyrus couldn't read—wonder, maybe, or calculation, or the exact place where the two shake hands. "Twenty years I've carried that thing. Bought it off a dead man's cart outside Harrow Fen. Never once seen it settle for anybody."

"Perhaps it has low standards," said Bramble.

"Perhaps it recognizes quality," Odji shot back, and then grinned at Cyrus again. "Look—I feed these little ones, right? Keep their lights up however I can. But I can't talk to them. Twenty years of guessing what they want, and along comes someone who reads them like a letter from home." They tilted their head. "You're heading somewhere dangerous. Don't argue, you've got the face for it—the face people get when they're carrying something heavier than luggage. I know roads. Back ways. Ways that don't have toll bridges or Wardens asking names."

Cyrus's whole body went still. Every instinct honed in a valley built on silence screamed that this was too fast, too convenient, too warm.

"And what do you want for it?" they asked carefully. "Nothing's free here. I've seen what this market thinks things cost."

Something flickered across Odji's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. If Cyrus hadn't been watching for it, they'd have missed it entirely.

"Fair question," Odji said lightly. "Fair price, fair answer. Come sit. There's a tea seller two rows over who waters the cups but not the conversation. I'll tell you what I want, and you can decide if you're poor or rich."

* * *

They sat on upturned crates beneath a striped awning, and Odji talked—about roads, about the mountain's smuggler tunnels ("every fortress bleeds; you just have to find the wound"), about which villages fed travelers and which sold them. Bramble heckled from Cyrus's shoulder, stealing a pastry with a precision that suggested long practice, and Odji stole it back and ate it themselves, and some old familiar rhythm seemed to establish itself between the raven and the sprite-seller, like dancers finding each other's step.

But Cyrus kept seeing the flicker. The fish under ice.

"Your price," they said at last. "You still haven't named it."

Odji turned the brass lantern slowly in their hands, watching the small light circle inside. "Him," they said. "Her. Them—it's never told me. Twenty years, I've guessed. Fed it honey-water and moonlight and sung it songs I invented because I don't know any real ones." They looked up, and for the first time the brightness in their face had a crack in it, and through the crack Cyrus glimpsed something tired and old and loving. "I want you to ask it why it stays."

"Why it—"

"In the lantern. It could dim out anytime, I think. Sprites fade when they give up—that's what the sellers mean by 'going dim.' This one hasn't given up, but it won't leave either. Lantern doors open on the third night of every month. It stays." Odji's voice roughened. "I could sell it tomorrow and eat for a season. I'd rather starve. But I need to know—am I keeping a prisoner? Am I a better sort of jailer, is that all I am? Ask it. Tell me true, whatever it says. That's my price."

The sprite pressed itself against the glass, close to Cyrus's hands, and its wanting came through clear as a bell rung in an empty room. Not *out out out* this time. Something longer. Sadder. A sentence it had been trying to say for twenty years.

Cyrus looked at the little burning face, and at Odji's waiting one, and understood suddenly that this was what the market had been teaching them all day: everything sacred had a price tag here. Charms. Luck. Dreams. Souls. Room to belong.

But maybe—maybe—some things could be bought with something better than blood.

"All right," Cyrus said. "I'll ask."

And the sprite began, at last, to speak.

Behind them, unnoticed at the market's edge, a woman with silver rings on every finger smiled, and turned toward the toll bridge inn—to pass along a description, a valley accent, a raven.
