## 9. Thirteen Moons, Twelve Gone

The raven did not shut up.

Cyrus had known this for three days now—three days of walking east out of Highmoor with a bird perched on their pack like a feathered judgment—but the full weight of it only landed on the second morning, when Bramble discovered that Cyrus could hear him and therefore had no excuse to stop listening.

"You walk like a funeral procession," the raven said, hopping along a fallen log to keep pace. "One foot. Then the other foot. Then a long pause in which you contemplate the futility of all motion. I've seen snails with more urgency, and I once knew a snail who was exiled too, actually. Long story. Involved a duke's lettuce garden."

"I'm thinking," Cyrus said.

"That's the problem. Thinking is what you do when you're afraid to ask questions. So ask."

Cyrus adjusted the strap of their pack. The map case pressed against their hip, Ilse's blood long dried to a dark stain on the leather. Three days of not opening it again, because every time they did, they saw the mark over their own name—a small circle, like an eye, like a coin, like something waiting to be spent.

"All right," Cyrus said. "Explain it to me. Properly. What happens at the thirteenth moon."

Bramble stopped hopping. For a moment he was still in the way that ravens almost never are, head cocked, one black eye fixed on Cyrus with an attention that felt less like a bird and more like a very old man who happened to be wearing feathers.

"So you *do* want the truth," he said. "Fine. But not on the road. Truths taste better with a fire under them. Old rule. My rule. I invented several rules; that's the sort of thing you can do when you've been exiled long enough."

* * *

They made camp in the hollow beneath a leaning beech, where the roots curled up like cupped hands and the ground was soft with years of leaves. Cyrus built a small fire—Bramble insisted on a fire, said the dark made his voice sound "too honest"—and dug out the last of the bread Maren had packed. She'd wrapped it in cloth embroidered with rowan sprigs, which was as close as Maren ever came to saying *I love you* out loud, and Cyrus held that thought against their ribs like a warm stone while they chewed.

Bramble settled on a root opposite, folded his wings, and looked, for the first time since the ford, entirely serious.

"Right," he said. "The court beneath the mountain. You'll want to sit down for this, but you're already sitting, so we're ahead of schedule."

"Get on with it."

"Eager. Good. Here's what your map won't tell you, because maps are drawn by people who want to arrive alive." He tapped one talon against the root, a small dry click. "The mountain isn't empty. It hasn't been empty since before there were names for most things. There's a court down there—the Undercourt, though its own members just call it the Court, because vanity is the one virtue they practice faithfully. A sovereign. Courtiers. Halls lit by bottled light." He paused. "Bottled souls, if we're being precise, and we should be precise, because imprecision is how they get you."

"Bottled souls." Cyrus's voice came out flatter than they intended. "Like Odji's lanterns? In Vell's Crossing?"

"Odji sells *charms* and caged sprites, poor man, half of them fake, all of them sad. The court deals in the real thing." Bramble's feathers ruffled, a shiver passing through him. "When someone bargains with the court—and people do bargain, oh yes, they come crawling down the smuggler ways with their desperate little wants—the price is always the same. Not gold. Not service. Gold runs out and servants die. The court takes the soul itself. It goes into a lantern. It sits on a shelf in the vault until the thirteenth moon fills, and then—" He clicked his beak shut.

"Then what?"

"Then the mountain eats."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, and Cyrus, without meaning to, listened after it until the wingbeats faded south. Even out here past the ward, even with the gift roaring in their chest like a second heartbeat, some habits of silence held.

"Tell me about the eating part," Cyrus said quietly.

Bramble hopped closer to the firelight. His voice, when it came, had lost its sardonic edge entirely, and somehow that was worse.

"The mountain is hungry. That's the first thing to understand. It's not evil—not the way your valley stories mean evil, with horns and cackling. It's *hungry*, the way winter is hungry, the way the sea is. Magic is its food, and souls are magic at its most concentrated—every hope and fear and stubborn little love a person carries, all of it fuel. When the thirteenth moon turns full, the court holds a feast. They open the vault. They take the oldest debts first." He looked directly at Cyrus. "Ilse's soul has been on that shelf for twenty-two years. It will be the oldest debt owed when the moon fills. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Cyrus set down the bread. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; everything behind the hands was not.

"How long?"

Bramble tilted his head back toward the sky, visible through the beech branches—a thin silver sliver, barely begun.

"Count," he said. "Tonight is the turn of the twelfth moon. Twenty-eight days, give or take, before the thirteenth fills full. Twenty-eight days to travel to the mountain, get inside a court that has spent centuries perfecting the art of noticing things, find a vault guarded by things I don't have words for and I have *many* words, steal a soul out of a bottle, and get back out." He ruffled his feathers. "I've done harder jobs. Once I stole an entire cheese. But the cheese wasn't guarded by grief-eaters, so."

"Grief-eaters."

"Later. One horror at a time. I find pacing helps."

Cyrus stared into the fire. The flames bent and reformed, bent and reformed, and in them Cyrus kept seeing the ford—the storm-gray water, Ilse's hand closing around theirs with the last strength she had, her lips shaping words that were mostly blood: *you're the one I paid for.*

"She sold herself," Cyrus said. "For me. A child she'd never met."

"Yes."

"Why would anyone do that?"

"Ah." Bramble preened once, twice—an old nervous habit, Cyrus was learning—and then went still again. "You're asking the wrong question. Everyone asks why. The court loves that question; it's how they recruit. Why did she do it, why does anyone do anything, wouldn't you trade a piece of yourself for something good, doesn't everyone eventually?" He shook his head sharply. "Ask instead: *what did it cost her?* Because that's the part the court never mentions. Your soul in a lantern isn't sleep, boy-girl-whatever-you-are. It's awareness. Bottled awareness. You know everything happening around you. You watch the shelf. You count the moons. And you feel the mountain getting hungrier as the thirteenth moon waxes, and you know—" His voice caught, an odd clicking rasp. "You *know* what's coming, and you cannot close your eyes, because lanterns don't have eyelids."

The fire cracked. Cyrus realized their hands had gone cold despite the heat.

"You talk like you've seen it."

A long silence. Long enough that Cyrus thought Bramble might simply refuse, might spin off into a story about the cheese or the duke's lettuce or any of the hundred deflections the raven kept holstered like knives. Instead he stepped out of the firelight, into the darker ring beyond it, where his black feathers made him nearly invisible except for that single gleaming eye.

"I told you I was exiled from the court for theft," he said. "That's true, as far as it goes. What I stole was my voice. It used to sit on a shelf in the vault, right beside a lantern I'd grown rather fond of, belonging to a woman who'd bought a child's safety with everything she had." The eye didn't blink. "I couldn't give her back her soul. I'm a thief, not a miracle. But I could make sure someone heard her story. So I took my voice off the shelf and flew, and the court sealed the mountain behind me, and I have spent twenty-two years being a very annoying bird in a very quiet valley, waiting for you to grow up and grow a spine."

The words landed one by one, like stones dropped into deep water. Cyrus sat with them. The owl called again, far away now, heading north.

"You knew Ilse," Cyrus said at last. "Before the ford. You knew her."

"I knew *of* her. We were neighbors, of a kind—her lantern and my empty space on the same shelf." A dry, terrible humor crept back into his voice. "Shelf conversation is limited. Mostly counting. She counted better than me. She never lost her place, even in the bad years."

"And the map—"

"Was hers. Drawn from memory, in the dark, over years, with no hands. Don't ask me how. Some things even I don't understand, and I pretend to understand everything." He hopped back into the firelight, businesslike again, as if he hadn't just rearranged the architecture of Cyrus's world. "She carried it out the day she died. Which means the court let her go—which means someone down there wanted that map delivered. Or wanted *you* delivered. Both possibilities are unpleasant. I recommend not thinking about either until breakfast."

But Cyrus was already thinking about it, because that was the trouble with being told you'd been purchased: every thought afterward came with a price tag attached. Ilse had paid. Maren had paid—in worry, in silence, in twenty-two years of burning letters unread. And somewhere beneath a mountain, a woman's soul sat on a shelf counting moons, and the count was nearly done.

"Twenty-eight days," Cyrus said.

"Twenty-seven and a half. I rounded generously. I'm told generosity is charming."

"It's not."

"No," Bramble agreed cheerfully, "it isn't. Now sleep. You'll want your legs tomorrow—we cross the fenlands, and the fens are where the Warden's trail runs freshest."

Cyrus looked up sharply. "The Warden. Sable. You said the court sends Wardens after escaped debts—"

"I said no such thing, but yes, well done, you were listening." Bramble tucked his beak under one wing, the picture of a bird going to sleep, though his eye stayed open a crack. "Ilse died running. Whatever killed her left a trail, and the Warden of Seals follows trails the way rivers follow downhill. She's behind us. Two days, maybe three. Maybe less." A pause. "Also possibly ahead of us. Warden's prerogative."

"That's supposed to let me sleep?"

"No," said Bramble comfortably. "That's supposed to make you sleep *fast*. There's a difference, and it matters."

* * *

Cyrus did not sleep fast. Cyrus lay on their back beneath the beech and listened to the night, because listening was the one thing they had never been able to stop doing, even in the dead valley, even when the listening brought back nothing but wind and goats.

Out here it was different. Out here the night was *loud*—not with sound, but with presence. A fox's dreaming two hillsides east, sharp and ginger-colored in the mind. Moths spiraling drunk around distant flowers. The slow green patience of the beech overhead, its roots holding water the way old women hold grievances. And underneath it all, far to the north and east, something vast and cold turning over in its sleep beneath a mountain, something with an appetite so old it had forgotten it was an appetite and called itself a kingdom.

*Twenty-seven days,* Cyrus thought at it. *I'm coming for her.*

The vastness stirred, faintly, like a sleeper aware of a fly.

*Cyrus,* said Bramble softly, from the darkness, in the voice he only used when he thought Cyrus was asleep. *Whatever you're doing, stop announcing yourself to the mountain. It notices. It always notices.* A pause, and then, gentler than Cyrus had ever heard him: *Sleep, thief-in-training. Tomorrow we teach you how to be quiet in a loud world. It's harder than it sounds. I've had practice.*

And high above the beech, past the thin blade of the twelfth moon, the sky wheeled on toward a fullness that was coming whether they walked or slept or screamed—twenty-seven days of sky left between Ilse's soul and the mountain's open mouth.

In the morning, Cyrus woke to Bramble standing rigid on their chest, feathers raised, staring west.

"Wake up," the raven said. "We have company. And it isn't the friendly kind."
