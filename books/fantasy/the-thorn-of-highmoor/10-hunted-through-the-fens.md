## 10. Hunted Through the Fens

The fens announced themselves a full day before Cyrus saw them.

It began with the smell—rich, green, and rotting all at once, like the world's compost heap—and then the birds changed. The tidy hedgerow songbirds of the farmland gave way to herons standing in the ditches like patient creditors, and then to things stranger: a bird that cried from the reeds in a voice exactly like a child calling *Mama*, and another that made no sound at all but watched Cyrus pass with one flat yellow eye and an air of having filed their description somewhere permanent.

"They're judging you," Bramble said from his perch on Cyrus's pack. "All of them. I can tell. Birds are terrible gossips."

"You're a bird."

"I'm a *raven*. We're not gossipers, we're archivists. There's a difference, and it's mostly dignity." He shuffled his feathers against the wind coming off the wetlands. "Also, I want it noted for the record that I objected to this route."

"Noted," Cyrus said. "Overruled."

"See, this is what happens when you put a farmhand in charge of an expedition."

They had been three days out of Vell's Crossing, following the map's thread eastward through country that grew steadily wilder and wetter, and Cyrus had learned two things about life outside the ward. First: their gift did not sleep out here. It hummed under everything, a low constant murmur—the worms turning in the dark soil, the fish holding their cold opinions beneath the streams, the horses in the last village they'd passed dreaming anxious horse dreams about bridles. It was like walking around with every door in the world open at once.

Second: they were being followed.

Cyrus had noticed it first as a wrongness in the birdsong—a stretch of hedge where the sparrows went abruptly silent, then resumed too carefully, as though someone had told them to act natural. Then the innkeeper at the last crossroads had mentioned, unprompted, that a woman in gray had come through asking after a young person with an animal-way about them, and had paid with a coin so old its king was someone nobody remembered.

"She's gaining," Cyrus said now, picking their way along a causeway of packed earth between two black pools. "Two days behind at the crossing. A day and a half yesterday. She'll have us by tomorrow night."

Bramble preened, which Cyrus had learned was what he did instead of agreeing. "The Warden of Seals doesn't hurry. Hurrying is for people who might fail. She walks like the world is going to hold still for her." He paused. "Which, historically, it has."

"Then we need to stop being findable."

"Mm. And how does a beast-speaker become unfindable, exactly? Every animal within a league already knows your name, your smell, your mother's face, and your embarrassing habit of talking to goats. You're less a traveler than a traveling rumor."

Cyrus didn't answer, because the answer had been forming in them since the crossroads, and they didn't like it.

Ahead, the causeway bent north toward higher ground—but the map showed something better. A wide shallow basin off the eastern path where the water spread thin over acres of sedge, and where, if the rumors in Vell's Crossing could be trusted, the marsh-elk grazed in herds of fifty and more: gray, heavy-shouldered creatures with wide splayed hooves and antlers like drowned branches. Big enough to make any pursuer think twice.

"Elk won't fight for you," Bramble said, when Cyrus voiced it. He sounded unusually serious. "I've seen speakers try. Beasts aren't soldiers. You can ask them, but you can't *make* them, and asking a herd to trample a Warden is asking a lot."

"I'm not going to make them fight," Cyrus said. "I'm going to tell them the truth. That something dangerous is coming into their calving ground. Let them decide what to do about it."

Bramble was quiet for three wingbeats' worth of walking. "That," he said finally, "is either very wise or very stupid, and the fens are famously bad at telling the difference."

* * *

They left the causeway at dusk, wading out into the shallows where the sedge grew chest-high on a person, and Cyrus called.

Not with words. Words were the small clumsy part of the gift; inside the ward they had been all Cyrus ever had, a whispered vocabulary shared with Maren's goats. Out here, with the magic running full and free, speaking properly was something else entirely—a pouring-out, a shape made of scent and feeling and knowing, understood bone-deep by anything that cared to listen.

The elk heard.

Cyrus felt them first as a shifting weight at the edge of awareness: forty-odd great bodies moving through the deeper water to the east, ears swiveling, the lead cow lifting her head from grazing with water dripping from her jaw. Cyrus opened themselves and let the truth flow out: *something hunts here. Something that smells of iron and old bargains. Your calves are in the reeds.*

The herd's answer came back as a slow tide of wariness sharpening into attention. Marsh-elk were not gentle minds—they were vast and practical and slightly annoyed, the mental presence of them like leaning against a warm stone wall that occasionally resented you. But they listened. The lead cow tasted the air where Sable would come, reading Cyrus's borrowed knowledge of the gray-clad figure, the old coin, the silence she dragged behind her like a net.

Then the herd moved—not toward Cyrus but around them, flowing through the shallows with surprising silence for animals their size, arranging themselves between the causeway and the calving grounds. Fifty heads lowered. Fifty racks of antler caught the last red light.

"It's working," Cyrus breathed.

"Don't say it out loud," Bramble muttered. "Stories punish that."

They waited in the sedge while true dark came down, mosquitoes singing their tiny hymns, until near midnight the causeway gave up a figure.

Sable came alone, on foot, her horse left back along the trail—which Cyrus found more frightening than any company she might have brought. She walked the narrow earth path without hurry, gray cloak beaded with mist, and stopped at the edge of the basin as though she'd known precisely where the ambush would be. Which, probably, she had.

"Cyrus Nystrom." Her voice carried flat and clear across the water. Not loud. It didn't need to be. "I know you're there. The herons told me."

*Traitors*, Cyrus thought wildly, and felt the nearest heron radiate serene unapology.

"You've used your gift tonight," Sable continued. "Do you feel it? The trail you're leaving? Every working rings like a bell to those of us who know the sound. Come out. Talk to me. I am not Ilse's killer, whatever you've decided, but I know who is, and I know what took her soul, and you are walking straight into its mouth."

The herd stirred. Cyrus felt the elk's patience fraying—the lead cow's mind pressing against theirs, asking *now? now? now?*

And Cyrus, exhausted, frightened, and wanting desperately to end it, answered: *now.*

What happened next happened fast and wrong.

The herd surged forward through the shallows—and the water, which had seemed ankle-deep everywhere, was not. Hidden channels cut the basin like veins, and the ground beneath the sedge was peat-hungry and treacherous. The lead cow hit a channel mid-stride and went down hard, and the cows behind, committed, panicked by the sudden cry of their matriarch, broke formation. Fifty animals became a stampede became a chaos, elk crashing through sedge in every direction, some wheeling toward Sable, most simply fleeing the panic itself, and the whole thundering mass of it tore through the calving grounds where the year's fawns lay hidden in the reeds.

Cyrus heard the fawn before they understood it: a small bright mind, bewildered, calling for its mother in the simple wordless way of very young things. Then the herd rolled over that corner of the marsh like weather, and the small bright mind—

stopped.

The silence afterward was worse than a scream. It was a hole in the world shaped exactly like a fawn, and Cyrus felt it the way you feel a tooth missing—with the tongue of the soul, endlessly, involuntarily.

The chaos spent itself. Elk scattered into the dark, blowing and plunging. Somewhere in the confusion Sable stood untouched—of course she stood untouched—gray and still amid the wreckage of Cyrus's clever plan.

Cyrus found the fawn by its absence, wading in circles through the freezing shallows until their hands closed on a small wet body in the flattened sedge. Its neck was wrong. Its eyes were open on the moonlight and seeing nothing.

They knelt in the water and held it, because there was nothing else to do, and the guilt came up through the marsh muck and into their chest like cold water filling boot tops—slow, total, rising.

*I did this.* Not Sable. Not the court. Not fate or Ilse's map or thirteen turning moons. Cyrus had asked a herd of wild creatures to be soldiers, and when the truth proved inconvenient, had pushed past *asking*. One push. That was all it took. One push, and a calf who had never done anything to anyone was dead in the weeds.

"You're alive, then." Sable stood ten feet away, mist beading her shoulders. Cyrus hadn't heard her approach; hadn't cared enough to listen. "I could take you now. I want you to understand that. Whatever you think of me, understand that I am choosing, every hour, not to."

"Get away from him." Bramble landed between them in a fury of feathers, wings half-spread, looking absurd and magnificent. "Get away from—"

"The fawn wasn't her fault either," Sable said quietly. "Or yours. Or mine. It was everyone's. That's the lesson the court never teaches, and the reason I—" She stopped herself. Looked at the small body in Cyrus's arms, and something moved behind her eyes that was not cold at all. "You'll want to bury it. Dig deep. The fen will give it back otherwise."

She turned and walked away up the causeway, and Cyrus, kneeling in the black water with a dead fawn in their arms, did not follow.

They dug until dawn, hands raw, at the base of a sedge hummock above the flood line, and laid the small body down, and said the only words that came, which were *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry,* spoken aloud in the human tongue because the gift suddenly felt like a weapon they weren't fit to carry.

When they climbed back to the bank, stiff and hollow-eyed, Bramble was waiting with something in his beak. He dropped it in front of Cyrus: a sprig of marsh rosemary, the little blue flowers beaded with dew.

"For the grave," he said gruffly. "The elk do it. Did you know that? They remember their dead. Lay flowers. The big cow's been circling the spot all night—she knows. She's not angry at you." He cocked his head. "Well. She's a *little* angry at you. But mostly she understands that young ones break things when they're learning to carry weight. She says—" Bramble paused, listening to something Cyrus couldn't hear, and his voice went softer. "She says the mistake isn't the shame. Refusing to learn from it, that's the shame. She says grow or go home."

Cyrus looked at the fresh-turned earth, at the blue flowers, at their own shaking hands. The gift murmured all around them—the worms, the fish, the waking herons, the whole vast murmuring world—and for the first time it didn't feel like an open door. It felt like a responsibility.

"Twelve days," they said at last. Their voice came out scraped. "Twelve days until the thirteenth moon fills, and Ilse's soul burns, and I'm standing in a swamp learning elk etiquette. We need to move faster. We need another way."

"There *is* another way." Bramble hopped closer, and there was something odd in his posture—wings tucked tight, head low, the look of a bird about to confess. "Under the mountain. Smuggler ways. But it means trusting someone, and not me for once." He glanced east, back down the trail toward Vell's Crossing. "Because we're being followed again. Only this time it's someone carrying a lantern that whispers."
