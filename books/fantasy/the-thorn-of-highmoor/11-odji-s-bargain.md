## 11. Odji's Bargain

Odji had been following the beast-speaker for three days, and in that time they had learned several important things.

First: the beast-speaker slept badly, curled against whatever tree or wall was nearest, one hand always loose near their belt like they expected the world to bite.

Second: the beast-speaker talked to animals constantly — quietly, under their breath, a running commentary of *move along, friend* and *not you, I said move along* and once, memorably, an extended negotiation with a badger over a rabbit carcass that Odji would treasure until their dying day.

Third, and most importantly: the beast-speaker was hopeless with money.

Odji watched from behind a stack of empty barrels at the edge of the fen-town of Marrow's Rest as Cyrus — for that was the name the sprite-seller gossip had eventually coughed up, along with a description and a rumor about a dead woman at a ford — paid four coppers for a heel of bread that any child in the market could have haggled down to two.

"Disgraceful," Odji murmured to the lantern hanging from their belt.

The lantern did not answer. It never answered. But the light inside it flickered in what Odji had long ago decided was a laugh.

"Oh, don't start," Odji said. "You'd have paid five."

The light flickered again, sharper this time, almost indignant.

"That's the spirit."

They had followed Cyrus out of Vell's Crossing partly for business and partly for something Odji refused to call conscience. Business first: word in the trade-towns traveled faster than anything with legs, and word now said there was a beast-speaker walking east toward the mountain, and word also said a Warden of Seals was walking the same road a half-day behind. Where those two roads met, there would be trouble, and where there was trouble, there were people willing to pay for tunnels around it.

But the other part — the part Odji kept folded away small — was the memory of the market stall. The way Cyrus had stood in front of Odji's caged sprites, not buying, not bargaining, just looking, with an expression Odji recognized because Odji wore it themselves every time they passed a mirror. The look of someone seeing a cage and feeling its bars on their own skin.

Nobody looked at caged things that way unless they knew cages.

So Odji had packed the cart, hitched the mule — a bad-tempered animal named Duchess whom Odji had bought cheap precisely because she bit everyone except Odji — and followed.

* * *

The problem, Odji reflected on the third night, was that beast-speakers noticed being followed.

Cyrus had stopped at the edge of the fens where the reed-beds went silver under the rising moon, and had not moved for the better part of ten minutes, and was very definitely looking directly at a stand of willows where Odji was crouched behind Duchess, who was eating Odji's hood.

"I know you're there," Cyrus called. Their voice carried strangely across the water, flat and clear, the voice of someone used to making themselves understood without shouting. "You've got a mule that smells like a tannery and you whistle when you walk. You whistled past me twice yesterday. Same tune."

Odji considered their options. There was only one, really. They straightened up, pried Duchess's teeth out of their sleeve, and led the mule out into the moonlight with the easy rolling swagger that had gotten them thrown out of four towns and welcomed back into three.

"Evening," they said. "Lovely fens. Very atmospheric. Full of disease."

Cyrus didn't laugh. They stood with the raven on their shoulder — the raven was new since Vell's Crossing, and it watched Odji with the flat gold appraisal of a customs officer — and waited.

"All right, all right." Odji raised both hands, palms out, the universal gesture of the honest merchant, which is to say the gesture of someone about to lie. "You caught me. I'm a simple trader, following a person of obvious means and mysterious purpose across dangerous country, purely in the hope of—"

"You tried to sell me a 'genuine dragon scale' in Vell's Crossing."

"A *replica*, friend, clearly labeled—"

"You told a farmer his cow was cursed so he'd buy a charm off you."

"The cow *was* cursed. By fleas. Fleas are a curse." Odji sighed and let the swagger drop, which took effort; the swagger weighed more than the rest of them combined. "Fine. True version, then. I followed you because of the Warden."

That landed. He saw it land — saw Cyrus go still in the particular way prey goes still, the stillness Odji had seen in trapped birds and cornered children.

"Sable," Cyrus said.

"You know her name. Good. Saves time." Odji dug in the cart and came up with a dried fish, which they offered to the raven as tribute. The raven took it without breaking eye contact, which Odji respected enormously. "She passed through Marrow's Rest two days ago asking after you. Asking politely, which is worse. Wardens who shout want your body. Wardens who are polite want your cooperation, and bodies recover from that a lot easier than cooperation does."

"She can't touch me here. No seals work inside the valley ward—" Cyrus stopped. Recalculated. "I'm not in the valley anymore."

"No, friend. You're in the wide world, where everything works, including the things that work on people like us." Odji let *people like us* sit there in the air between them. "Now. I'm going to make you an offer. And before you refuse it, I want you to notice that I followed you through mosquito country for three days wearing my good boots, which should tell you how serious I am, because these boots cost more than my cart."

The raven made a sound that was unmistakably a snort.

"Your bird agrees."

"Bramble doesn't agree with anyone," Cyrus said. "What's the offer?"

Odji took a breath. Here was the tricky part. Here was the part they'd rehearsed against the side of the mule every night, and every night it had come out wrong, because the truth was a shape that didn't fit comfortably in the mouth.

"There are ways into the mountain that aren't the front gate," Odji said. "Smugglers' tunnels. Old ones, older than the court's current tenants care to admit, because admitting them would mean admitting things get *in*. I know them. Not all of them — nobody knows all of them, the mountain rearranges itself like a drunk rearranging furniture — but enough. I can take you under. Past the gates, past the seals, down to wherever it is you're going, which I suspect involves the vault, because everyone who goes under the mountain eventually involves the vault."

Cyrus's eyes narrowed. "And the price?"

Ah. There it was.

"The price," Odji said, "is not money."

The moon had climbed high enough now to lay a road of silver across the fen water, and the reeds whispered, and somewhere far off a marsh-elk called out and got no answer. Odji unclipped the lantern from their belt and held it up.

Inside the glass, a small light drifted. Not flame — flame sat still and ate its fuel and died. This light *moved*, restless, pressing at the glass walls the way a hand presses at a window. It brightened when Odji lifted the lantern and dimmed slightly, sulkily, when Odji spoke.

"A sprite," Cyrus said softly. Their whole face changed — Odji watched it happen, watched the wariness fall away like a coat taken off. "A real one. Why do you carry it in a lantern?"

"Because that's where it stays."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got." Odji turned the lantern slowly, watching the light follow. "I bought it eight years ago off a broker in the southern markets. Caged sprites sell better than free ones; everyone knows a captive light burns brighter, and everyone's wrong, but everyone buys anyway. I meant to sell it. That was the plan. Buy low, sell high, keep the difference."

"And?"

"And it wouldn't leave." Odji laughed, but it came out crooked. "I opened the lantern. First week. Second week. Tenth year anniversary of owning it, last spring. I open the glass and hold it out over the river and say, *go on, then, you're free,* and it — " Odji gestured helplessly at the light, which had begun to pulse, slow and rhythmic, like breathing. "It flies to the end of my arm and sits there. On my knuckle. Like it's afraid of the sky. Then it comes back inside and shuts itself in and dims down like a sulking candle, and I close the glass because if I don't, it *cries*, and I don't know how I know it's crying, but I know."

The raven had stopped eating. Even Duchess had lifted her head.

"So here's my price," Odji said. "You're a beast-speaker. Everyone says you speak to beasts, but Bramble there isn't a beast, not properly, and neither is a sprite. You speak to *things that talk*. So: talk to it. Find out why it won't leave. Because I have carried this lantern for eight years and I cannot sell it and I cannot free it and I am so tired, friend, of carrying a prison that the prisoner built themselves."

The silence stretched. The light in the lantern pulsed faster now, quicker and quicker, and pressed itself against the glass facing Cyrus with an intensity that made the hair on Odji's arms stand up.

"It's never done that," Odji said quietly. "Eight years, it's never reached for anyone."

Cyrus stepped forward. Slowly, hands visible, the way you approach any wild thing. They knelt in the wet reeds and brought their face level with the lantern, and Odji saw them do the thing they must have done a thousand times in the hidden valley — the listening posture, head tilted, eyes unfocused, mouth slightly open.

Then Cyrus flinched as though struck.

"What," Odji said. "What is it? What did it say?"

"It didn't say anything." Cyrus's voice had gone strange. "It's *singing*. It's been singing the whole time, hasn't it. All eight years."

"Yes," Odji whispered. "Yes, it hums at night, I thought — I thought it was just noise—"

"It's a homesong. A calling-song. It's singing for someone." Cyrus looked up, and their eyes were wet, which undid Odji completely, because they had prepared for anger, for bargaining, for scorn, and had prepared nothing at all for kindness. "It's singing for another sprite. Someone it was separated from. And it thinks—" Cyrus stopped. Started again, gentler. "It thinks if it leaves the lantern, it stops singing, and if it stops singing, the other one will never find it. So it stays. In the smallest room it could find. Keeping the song safe."

The light pressed harder against the glass, pulsing, pulsing, and Odji heard it then — faintly, impossibly, beneath the wind in the reeds — a thread of sound like a struck glass, high and patient and unbearably sad, a note held for eight years.

"Oh," Odji said. It was a very small word for a very large collapse. They sat down in the mud, right there, boots and all, because their legs had resigned without notice. "Oh, you stupid brave little — I opened the door for you. Eight years I've been opening the door, and you weren't staying because you couldn't go. You were staying because you were *waiting.*"

The light dimmed to a soft ember-glow, almost shy.

Cyrus stayed kneeling. "There's more, isn't there," they said to the sprite. A pause. "She wants to know — she asks whether the other one might be in the mountain. Under it." Another pause, longer, and Odji watched Cyrus listen to something no one else could hear, and watched their expression fold inward with grief. "The courts keep lights," Cyrus said quietly. "Bottled ones. She's heard of the vaults."

Odji's heart dropped through the mud and kept going. "Her — whoever she's waiting for is *in the vault?*"

"I don't know. She doesn't know. She knows the songs stop reaching her when she's brought near the mountain. Something down there eats sound. Or keeps it." Cyrus rose, knees cracking, and looked at Odji with an expression that Odji suspected mirrored their own: hollowed out and lit from inside, both at once. "Take me under, and I'll find out. For her. For you. That's the bargain."

"That's not a bargain, that's charity—"

"That's the price I'm naming, and beast-speakers drive hard bargains, ask anyone." But Cyrus smiled as they said it, tired and real, and stuck out a mud-streaked hand.

Odji shook it. Their palm was sweating. Eight years, and the answer had been sitting in a lantern on their hip the entire time, singing.

"Well," they said, standing, hauling the moment upright by main force because someone had to. "Right. Excellent. Emotional breakthrough achieved, mutual tears shed, very moving, we'll bill it as dinner theater. Now — practical matters." They pointed east, where the fens gave way to grey hills, and beyond the hills, black against the stars, the mountain sat like a held breath. "We move tomorrow at dusk. Sable's half a day behind us and gaining, the tunnel entrance floods at high water, and Duchess hates everyone, so we'll travel in a mood. Any questions?"

"One," said the raven, from Cyrus's shoulder, in a voice like gravel poured over silk. "If your tunnels are so wonderful, why did you sell the route map to the court six months ago?"

The fen went very quiet.

Odji felt every muscle in their body lock, one by one, like a door being barred. They had known this question was coming. They had hoped, in the way one hopes the tide won't come in.

"How did you—" they started.

"I was in the court before I was a bird," Bramble said. "Thieves' intelligence travels. A sprite-seller named Odji sold the smuggler routes to the Warden's office. Winter before last. Price undisclosed." The raven tilted its head, gold eyes pitiless. "So before we all march trustingly into your tunnels, seller of caged lights: why should we believe the woman who freed her sprite wouldn't sell her friends?"

Cyrus hadn't moved. Wasn't looking at Odji with suspicion, exactly — worse, with *openness*, with the terrible willingness to believe. Waiting.

Odji looked at the lantern. The little light had gone still, watching them too, the way it always watched, patient as eight years.

"I sold the routes," Odji said finally, "because the court had my sister."

The words came out flat and fast, like coins spilled from a torn purse.

"Nima. Younger than me. Cleverer than me, which I resent. She worked the southern vaults as a lamp-keeper — tending the bottled souls, feeding the wicks, whatever horrible tendernesses that job requires — and she made a mistake, or she saw something, or she was simply *there*, and the court put her on a shelf." Odji's voice cracked and they hated it and kept going. "They sent me her lantern. With her light in it. And a message: bring us the smuggler maps, or we drink her when the thirteenth moon fills."

The pulse of the sprite-light slowed. Softened. It drifted to the side of the glass nearest Odji and glowed there, steady, warm, the exact temperature of a hand held without touching.

"So yes," Odji said. "I sold the routes. Every one I knew. And I have spent every day since telling myself I'll buy them back with interest — that I'll walk into that mountain and walk out with her, or die in a corridor trying, and either way the debt gets paid." They wiped their face with their sleeve, roughly, once. "I followed you because you're going where she is. That's the true version. That's the last true version I've got. Take it or leave it, beast-speaker."

For a long moment, nobody spoke. The reeds whispered. The mountain breathed its dark breath on the horizon.

Then Cyrus said, "Then we have matching debts," and held out their hand again.

And Odji, who had spent a lifetime selling small magics and keeping large truths locked in lanterns, took it — while beside them, unnoticed by either of them, the little light in the glass began, very softly, to sing a second song: higher, thinner, threaded through the first like a second voice joining a round.

Neither of them knew yet what it meant.

But the raven did, and it said nothing, and looked east, and counted moons.
