## 12. The Court Beneath

The smuggler ways began, as Odji had promised they would, behind a waterfall that smelled faintly of old cabbage.

"That's the wards," Odji said, wringing out their sleeves as Cyrus ducked through the curtain of falling water. "The mountain's wards. They taste everything that passes. Cabbage is what a lie tastes like to stone."

"And we're lying?"

"We're existing where we shouldn't." Odji shrugged, a gesture that seemed to take in the whole dripping tunnel, the whole mountain, possibly the whole world. "So. Cabbage."

Bramble hopped along the ledge above them, feathers slicked dark with spray, and muttered something under his breath that was either a curse or a recipe.

"What did he say?" Cyrus asked.

"He said," Odji translated, "that in his day, thieves had standards. Marble corridors. Velvet ropes. None of this—" they gestured at the passage, where the walls were raw granite veined with something that glowed a sickly, honeyed gold whenever Cyrus looked away from it and dimmed when looked at directly, like it knew it was being watched, "—this *ambiance*."

"In his day," Cyrus said, "he was caught stealing and exiled for it."

"In his day," Bramble said clearly, from above, "*the ceilings were higher.*"

Cyrus laughed — a small, startled sound that echoed down the passage and came back changed, flattened by stone. They were three days past Vell's Crossing now, two days since Odji had appeared on the marsh road with their lantern swinging and their terms already prepared, and Cyrus was still adjusting to the strange arithmetic of traveling with company. For twenty-two years their conversations had been one-sided: goats, herons, the slow opinions of oxen. Now there were two voices answering back, and one of them belonged to a bird who treated sarcasm as a native language.

It helped. It helped more than Cyrus wanted to admit. The deeper they went, the more the mountain pressed in around them — not just physically, though the passages narrowed until sometimes Odji walked sideways and Bramble flew ahead like a thrown stone — but in some older, heavier way. The weight of all that rock overhead. The sense of being swallowed slowly, politely, by something patient.

"Talk to me," Cyrus said, when the silence got too thick. "About the tunnels. How do you know them?"

Odji's lantern bobbed. Inside its glass, the sprite — a small blue-white thing like a held breath — drifted listlessly against the walls of its cage. It hadn't spoken since the fens. It hadn't spoken before the fens, either; that was rather the point of the bargain.

"Smuggling's the family trade," Odji said. "Third generation. My grandmother ran salt through these very halls when the old toll-keepers still lived up top. There are eleven entrances to the mountain between here and the sea, and the court knows about nine of them." They smiled, quick and crooked. "We use the other two."

"And the court pays you not to tell?"

"The court pays us *to* tell. That's the trick of it." Odji's voice went light in the way Cyrus had learned meant the opposite. "You sell them your routes, they stop watching the ones you don't sell. Everyone's honest about their dishonesty. It's very civilized." A pause. The lantern swayed. "Was. It used to be."

They walked on. The gold veins in the walls grew thicker, brighter, and Cyrus noticed something else as they descended: warmth. Not the honest warmth of depth or fire, but a warmth like standing too near another person's fever. It prickled along their skin and set their gift murmuring uneasily. Because that was the other thing — down here, for the first time since crossing the boundary stone, Cyrus could feel almost nothing. No mice in the walls. No bats overhead. The mountain around them was full of hollows and galleries and, presumably, courtiers, and every living thing in it was silent to their senses, muffled, as if wrapped in wool.

Except the veins. The veins felt like something. Like voices heard through several closed doors, saying the same word over and over, too quiet to make out.

"Bramble," Cyrus said quietly. "The walls. What am I hearing?"

The raven dropped from the ledge and landed on Cyrus's shoulder, which he never did lightly — he weighed himself like an opinion, deliberately, so you'd know you'd been chosen. His claws were cold.

"You're hearing the lights," he said.

"The lights aren't lit yet."

"They don't need lighting." He smoothed a wing feather with great care, not looking at Cyrus. "Keep walking. You'll see soon enough, and I'd rather you saw it than felt it first."

* * *

They camped once more in the dark — a shallow alcove off a gallery whose ceiling vanished upward into blackness, hung with stalactites like the teeth of something asleep — and then the passage began, unmistakably, to descend toward splendor.

Cyrus smelled it before they saw it: candle smoke and crushed flowers, hot metal and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft. Then the air itself changed, growing warmer, thicker, carrying a sound they had no name for — a murmur, many-voiced, that was neither speech nor music but sat in the ear exactly between them.

Odji stopped at a seam in the wall where the stone split around a slab of black marble, polished to a mirror by centuries of hands. Beyond it, warm light breathed.

"Service gallery," Odji whispered. "Runs above the Hall of Kept Light. We can look. Looking's free — down here, looking is usually free. It's the other things that cost."

Cyrus put their eye to the seam.

Below, spread out like a jewel dropped in a cupped palm, was the court.

The hall was vast and wrong in a way Cyrus couldn't immediately name, because everything about it was beautiful. Pillars of white stone rose into a vaulted dark painted with constellations that moved — actually *moved*, drifting in slow procession across the ceiling like fish under ice. The floor gleamed like black water. Courtiers filled it in their hundreds, gowned in colors Cyrus had no words for: a purple that tasted of plums, a green like the moment after rain. Jewels flashed. Laughter rang up in bright peals. Somewhere, a string instrument played a melody so sweet it made the back of Cyrus's throat ache.

And the hall was lit by bottles.

Thousands of them. They hung from the vaulted ceiling in chains and garlands, they lined the pillars in niches carved to hold them, they floated — *floated*, gently revolving — in a vast fountain at the center of the floor. Each bottle glowed with its own color and its own rhythm, some steady as candles, some flickering, some pulsing slow and arrhythmic like hearts remembering how.

Cyrus knew what they were looking at the way you know a face in a crowd you've been dreading. Ilse had told them. Bramble had told them. Knowing had been a word. This was the word made glass.

"Soul-lanterns," Bramble said softly, at their ear. His voice had lost every ounce of its sardonic polish. "That's the harvest. That's what the mountain eats by."

Cyrus's hand found the seam in the wall and gripped it. Below, the ceremony began.

A bell rang — a single low note that passed through the floor and up through Cyrus's boots and teeth. The crowd turned as one body, faces tilting up, and the music modulated into something slower, richer, the kind of melody that makes you believe, just for the length of a phrase, that nothing has ever gone wrong anywhere.

Three figures crossed the floor. Two were courtiers in silver, moving with the unhurried grace of people performing a dance they've done ten thousand times. Between them walked a third — and Cyrus's stomach clenched, because the third figure walked *wrong*. Smoothly, obediently, but with none of the others' pleasure in it. Its eyes were open and fixed on nothing. Its mouth was slightly parted, as if it had been about to say something for a very long time.

"It's alive," Cyrus whispered. "Odji. The person in the middle. They're—"

"A vessel." Odji's voice beside the seam was flat and careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut from inside. "Don't watch the face. Watch the fountain."

The silver courtiers led the vessel to the fountain's edge, where a dais rose like a lily pad. One of them lifted a lantern from the water — a small one, glowing a gentle amber, pulsing quick and frightened — and the music swelled to meet it. The crowd sighed, a single collective breath of anticipation, and the sound of it was the worst thing Cyrus had heard in their life. Not cruel. *Pleased.*

The second courtier produced a long spoon of pale crystal.

"No," Cyrus said. Not meaning to say anything. Just: no.

The spoon touched the lantern's glow, and the glow *climbed onto it* — that was the only way Cyrus could describe it, a thread of amber light rising willingly, hungrily, to meet the crystal — and the courtier raised it to their lips and tasted.

The music resolved. The crowd made a sound like a wave drawing back over shells. And the courtier who had tasted closed their eyes and smiled, and their smile was so full, so radiant, so utterly satisfied, that for one terrible half-second Cyrus understood it completely — understood why anyone would ever want this, why a hundred courtiers stood applauding in their impossible gowns — and then they saw the lantern.

Its pulse was slowing.

Slowing, and dimming, and the thread of light that had climbed the spoon left behind it a paleness, the way a field looks after harvest. The vessel standing on the dais swayed. The courtier steadied them with one elegant hand, absently, the way you'd right a piece of furniture, and the vessel straightened, and their mouth was still slightly open, and whatever they had been about to say was now a little less likely ever to be said.

"How much does a taste take?" Cyrus asked. Their voice came out very small in the service gallery.

"Depends on the vintage," Odji said. "On how much soul there was to start." They didn't look through the seam anymore. They were studying their own boots with tremendous attention. "My sister's lantern is a small one. She was eight when they took her."

Cyrus turned to look at them — really look — and found Odji already meeting their gaze, chin up, daring them to say something pitying. The lantern in Odji's hand swung gently. Inside it, the little sprite had pressed herself against the glass nearest to Cyrus, tiny hands flat against the barrier, watching them with an expression that was not fear.

It was recognition.

"She knows what this place is," Cyrus said quietly.

"They all do, at the end." Odji's jaw tightened. "That's the part nobody puts in the songs. It doesn't hurt, mostly. That's the cruelty of it. It feels like rest. It feels like being *wanted*. Why do you think the vessels walk so calm?" They nodded down at the dais, where the silver courtiers were leading the emptied vessel away through admiring crowds, and the fountain turned its thousands of bottled lights, and the constellation-painted ceiling wheeled its slow invented stars. "Nobody screams down here. That's what makes it a court instead of a slaughterhouse. Everything screams somewhere else, later, where it can't disturb the wine."

Bramble said nothing at all, which from Bramble was a scream.

* * *

They pulled back from the seam and sat in the dark of the service gallery for a long time, sharing hard bread and quieter thoughts. The murmur of the hall below seeped through the stone, softened into something almost like surf. Almost pleasant. That was the trap of it, Cyrus thought. Even the horror here came pre-beautified. Even the monster wore perfume.

"I keep waiting to feel it," Cyrus said finally. "Through my gift. All those souls — I should be able to hear them, shouldn't I? Like I hear animals. But it's all just... muffled. Doors everywhere."

"Muffled," Bramble repeated. He was perched on a nub of rock, head tucked, and when he spoke his voice had a careful evenness Cyrus had learned meant fury being stored for later. "Say more about muffled."

"It's like — you know how the ward felt, in the valley? Sound after deafness, but backwards. Everything wrapped in wool. Except the veins in the walls, the gold ones. Those hum. Like a word repeated too quiet to catch."

Bramble's head came up. In the darkness his eyes caught the distant gold light and held it, twin coins.

"Cyrus." He hopped closer, along the rock, deliberate as punctuation. "What color is the light down here? Really. Not gold. Look again."

Cyrus looked. At the veins threading the gallery wall, pulsing faintly, patiently. At the glow leaking up through the seams from the hall of bottled souls.

Amber. The veins were amber. The same amber as the lantern the courtier had sipped from. The same amber as the light that had climbed the spoon.

"The mountain," Cyrus said slowly, "is lit by the same thing the court is lit by."

"The mountain isn't lit by it." Bramble's feathers rose along his neck, making him briefly twice his size and half as dignified. "The mountain is *made* of it. Or fed by it. Or both — I was small when I was stolen, I only ever saw the edges. But I'll tell you what I've worked out in twenty years of flying circles around the outside of this place." He fixed Cyrus with one glittering eye. "The valley you grew up in. The dead ward. The magic that doesn't work there. You thought that was protection, didn't you? A wall keeping the wild out?"

"Wasn't it?"

"It's a *larder*," Bramble said. "Something down here reached up twenty-two years ago and drank your valley dry, and sealed the lid behind it, and called the seal a ward. Everything magic that's born inside that border belongs to the mountain the moment it crosses the stone." He paused, and for once the sardonicism fell away entirely, leaving something old and tired and oddly gentle. "Which means, little speaker, that the moment you stepped over that boundary — you weren't escaping the larder."

"You were coming home to it," Cyrus finished. The words tasted of copper.

Below them, distantly, the court's music started up again, sweet and endless, and the fountain turned its harvest, and the veins in the walls pulsed their patient amber, and Cyrus understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, why the map had carried their name.

They weren't sneaking into the court.

They were the delivery.

Somewhere far above, muffled by a thousand feet of hungry stone, a bell began to ring — not the low ceremonial note from the hall, but something sharper, quicker, urgent. Boots, somewhere in the galleries below. Voices. Odji was on their feet, lantern snuffed, hand finding Cyrus's arm in the dark.

"Someone's come through the north entrance," Odji whispered. "Someone's *early*. And they're asking the gate-wardens questions." A pause, and then, in a voice stripped of all its crooked humor:

"They're asking about a beast-speaker."
