## 13. A Mask Fits Too Well

The livery smelled of mothballs and old ambition.

Cyrus stood before a tarnished mirror in a servant's alcove off the Court of Small Honors, tugging at a collar embroidered with silver thread that had once, perhaps, been actual silver. The coat was deep green—the color of House Merrowvale, whose crest was a stag so ornately antlered it looked less like an animal than like a chandelier having opinions about itself.

"You look ridiculous," said Bramble from the rafter above.

"I look like a servant."

"You look like a *costume* of a servant. There's a difference. A real one wouldn't keep checking whether the collar sits right." The raven hopped down onto the mirror's frame and regarded his own reflection with frank approval. "I, on the other hand, make this room better simply by being in it. Which is why I'm leaving. If anyone asks, I'm your familiar."

"Ravens aren't familiars in the court's fashion."

"Then I'm a very well-dressed omen. Honestly, Cyrus, the beauty of nobility is that they'll believe anything if you say it with sufficient boredom." He ruffled his feathers, preened once more, and flew out through a gap in the stonework where a gargoyle's ear used to be.

Cyrus took one last breath and put on the mask—not a literal one, though the courtiers below wore those too, delicate porcelain half-faces that made everyone look faintly amused by everything—but the other kind. The one they'd been wearing since the ford: *I belong here. I have always belonged here.*

They had been three days in the mountain.

Three days of Odji's smuggler tunnels narrowing into maintenance passages, maintenance passages widening into servants' halls, servants' halls opening—without warning, like a held breath finally released—into vaulted galleries lit by lanterns that did not burn oil. Cyrus had learned not to look directly at those lanterns. Looking meant listening. Listening meant hearing the small voices inside them, saying things like *it's cold* and *how long has it been* and, once, terribly, *mama?*

So they kept their eyes on the floor, which was easier, because the floors were mosaic and beautiful and demanded no moral accounting.

And they had done what Odji's map instructed: found the beast-tamer's post going begging. House Merrowvale—a minor noble line, wealthy enough to matter, unimportant enough to be overlooked—had recently lost its beast-tamer to something called "the Sovereign's summons," which no one would explain and everyone flinched at. A replacement was needed before the Season's first Presentation. Questions were considered gauche.

The steward, a dry stick of a man named Pellwin, had looked at Cyrus for exactly four seconds and said, "You can handle creatures?"

"They handle me, mostly," Cyrus had said, which was true and therefore safe.

Pellwin had hired them on the spot. In the world above, Cyrus thought bitterly, that honesty would have been a death sentence. Down here it was apparently just good interviewing.

* * *

The menagerie of House Merrowvale occupied a gallery off the main hall, behind doors of black oak carved with the aforementioned opinionated stag. Inside, the beasts were arranged in cages of silver wire that hummed faintly—the same hum as the boundary stone back home, Cyrus realized with a lurch, only smaller. Caged wards. Someone had built a valley-sized prison and then, out of habit or cruelty, built prisons inside the prison.

There were six animals. Cyrus counted them twice, because their gift told them something impossible: there should have been seven.

A marsh-fox with fur gone grey at the muzzle, pacing her cage in perfect eights.

Two moon-moths the size of dinner plates, drowsing against the wire, dreaming aloud in colors.

A rock-lizard the length of Cyrus's arm who had given up on everything except eating.

A songbird whose song, when it came, came wrong—notes in the shape of words, almost, like a person remembering language from the far side of sleep.

And a hollow spot in the corner cage, where the humming was strongest and the straw was still warm, as though something had been taken very recently and no one had bothered to say so.

"Good morning," Cyrus said softly, to all of them at once.

Six heads turned. Six sets of eyes fixed on them with the particular ferocity of creatures who had been spoken to by humans many times and understood by them never.

*I am sorry,* Cyrus said, without sound, letting the gift flow out gentle as water finding a crack. *I'm new. I won't pretend I'm not one of them. But I will listen.*

The silence that followed had texture. The fox stopped pacing. The moths' wings shivered through three shades of blue. And then the fox spoke—not in words, but in the way she knew how: a picture pushed gently into Cyrus's mind of a door opening, of cold air, of running.

*You want out,* Cyrus said.

*We want OUT,* said the songbird, and its broken notes cracked into something almost like laughter. *We want out we want out we want out we want—*

"Hush," Cyrus murmured, glancing at the door. "Loudly wanting things is how creatures end up in smaller cages."

The songbird subsided, sulking musically. The rock-lizard opened one eye, assessed Cyrus as a potential food source, found them lacking, and closed it again.

It was, Cyrus reflected, the most honest conversation they'd had in three days.

* * *

The trouble began—or rather, the wonderful, terrible thing began—at the afternoon gathering.

House Merrowvale hosted a salon every third day, and every third day the beasts were brought out on silver perches and leads, the way other houses displayed porcelain. It was barbaric. It was also, Cyrus discovered, an audience.

Lady Vess Merrowvale herself presided: a woman of perhaps sixty with a face like a kindly hawk and eyes that missed nothing, wearing a mask of white porcelain trimmed in the family green. She introduced the animals to her guests with the fondness of someone describing grandchildren she was also, somehow, selling.

"And here," she said, gesturing to the fox, "is Ashivva, taken from the fenlands at great expense, though she refuses to perform. Stubborn creature."

The guests laughed politely. Ashivva bared her teeth in a way that suggested the stubbornness was mutual and long-standing.

Cyrus stepped forward before their brain could file an objection. "She isn't stubborn, my lady. She's grieving. The fen is her mother-ground. She paces in eights because that's the number of dens in her home territory, and she's counting them."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when a servant speaks unprompted.

But Lady Vess tilted her head. "And how," she said slowly, "do you know that?"

Cyrus's heart performed a maneuver that would have concerned a physician. Every instinct honed over twenty-two years screamed *hide, shrink, apologize.* But the mask was on. And underneath the fear, something else stirred—something that had waited two decades for someone to ask that question and mean it.

"Animals talk, my lady," Cyrus said. "Most people just don't listen."

Another silence. Then Lady Vess smiled, and it transformed her face entirely. "Pellwin. Where did you find this one?"

"A road, my lady."

"A road." She laughed—a real laugh, quick and delighted—and turned back to her guests. "You see? This is what the court forgets. We buy our marvels and lose the ability to recognize them walking in off a road. Tell me—" and now she addressed Cyrus directly, which several guests visibly found scandalous, "—what else does the fox say?"

What followed was the strangest hour of Cyrus's life, and Cyrus had once spent an hour explaining to a goat named Duchess why eating Maren's laundry was a betrayal of their entire relationship.

They translated Ashivva's grief into words the court could hear. They woke the songbird's memory of its original song and coaxed it out note by note until a guest wept into her wine. They explained that the moon-moths weren't sleeping but dreaming, and described the dreams—rain on leaves, a garden no one had tended in years—and watched hardened courtiers go soft-eyed behind their porcelain masks.

And they applauded.

That was the part that lodged under Cyrus's skin like a splinter. They *applauded*. Not the hollow applause of fear, not the careful applause owed to power, but genuine wonder. One old lord gripped Cyrus's sleeve and whispered, "Forty years I've kept birds, and never once—" and couldn't finish.

In Highmoor, this gift was a death sentence worn invisibly. Here, it was a *talent*. People asked its name. People wanted more of it. For the first time since the ford, Cyrus felt the strange, vertiginous pleasure of not hiding—and hated themselves for how good it felt.

*Careful,* said a voice in their head. It sounded suspiciously like Bramble, but it was their own. *Careful. So did she, once. The Warden. Remember?*

Cyrus remembered. And remembered, too, that Lady Vess kept six beasts in humming silver cages and called it fondness.

* * *

That evening, Bramble found them in the hay loft above the menagerie, where Cyrus sat feeding strips of dried fish to the fox through the wires while pretending to sweep.

"Well?" Bramble landed on the pitchfork handle. "How's life among the beautiful and damned?"

"They clapped," Cyrus said.

"I heard. Sound carries strangely in this mountain—it arrives everywhere eventually, like bad news." He cocked his head. "You liked it."

"I didn't say—"

"Your feathers are all puffed up, metaphorically speaking. You walked in here like a wet cat and you're preening like a peacock." He hopped closer, voice dropping to something almost gentle, which for Bramble was deeply alarming. "Listen to me, because I only say sincere things approximately once a decade and I'd hate for you to miss it. That feeling? Being seen? It's real. It's not a trick. The trap isn't that the admiration is fake." He tapped the wooden beam between them with his beak. "The trap is what it costs. Look at the cages, Cyrus. Look at the lanterns in the hall. Everyone down here who is admired is admired *inside something*. The court doesn't hate your gift. Why would it? Your gift is delicious. The court eats things like your gift. It just prefers you seasoned and willing."

Cyrus looked down at Ashivva, who pressed her grey muzzle against the wire against Cyrus's knee.

"I know," they said quietly.

"Do you? Because the Warden of Seals was the finest beast-tamer this court ever paraded. They wrote songs about her, back when she still had a name instead of a title. And she believed exactly what you're believing right now—that she'd found the one place her gift could breathe. Right up until the day she signed it away to keep breathing there." Bramble's black eye caught the lamplight. "The mask fits, Cyrus. That's the whole horror of it. Masks that pinch come off easily. These ones grow in."

Cyrus was silent a long moment. Below them, the songbird sang its recovered song to an empty hall, soft and uncertain, like someone practicing being themselves again.

"When we take Ilse's soul back," Cyrus said at last, "we're taking these too. All of them. The fox, the bird, the moths. Whatever's missing from that seventh cage."

Bramble went very still. "The seventh cage is mine," he said. "Or it was. My voice sat on a shelf beside your friend Ilse's soul for eleven years, and what's in that cage now is whatever the court keeps in reserve for things it intends to repossess." He smoothed his feathers with unnecessary care. "So yes. Agreed. Everything. Now go to sleep, you're sentimental and it's making me itch."

He flew off. But Cyrus lay awake a long while after, listening to the mountain hum around them like a throat clearing before a very long speech, and understanding—with the clarity that comes only at the worst possible times—that Bramble was right about the mask.

Because tomorrow was the Presentation of Beasts before the higher court. And Lady Vess had already promised the assembled guests that her new tamer would demonstrate the gift before the Sovereign's own representatives.

And Cyrus had already said yes.

* * *

They were woken past midnight by footsteps on the loft ladder—measured, deliberate, wrong for a servant.

Cyrus sat up, hand closing around the pitchfork, as a figure rose through the hatch: tall, grey-cloaked, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been told *no* in a corridor. Lamplight found a face Cyrus knew from wanted-whispers and Odji's fearful descriptions, and found it older and wearier than either.

The Warden of Seals looked at Cyrus across the dark loft, and said, in a voice like a door closing:

"We need to talk, beast-speaker. And you'll want to hear me out—because I already know how your story ends. I lived it."
