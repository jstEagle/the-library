## 14. Sable's Offer

The Warden of Seals had learned, over thirty years of service, that the most dangerous hour in any hunt was not the chase. The chase was simple—a matter of legs and lungs and patience, and Sable had outlasted marsh-elk stampedes, a wyvern's grudge, and one memorable decade spent tracking a soul-thief who could fold himself into other people's shadows. No, the dangerous hour was the quiet after. The hour when the quarry stopped running and started thinking, because thinking was where hope lived, and hope was the one thing Sable had been professionally trained to kill.

She found Cyrus Nystrom in the servants' cloister behind the Tallow Court's eastern galleries, sitting on a bench beneath a trellis of pale, luminous fungus that the courtiers called moonlace and pretended was fashionable. The beast-tamer's livery sat on them like a borrowed coat two sizes too hopeful. They were talking to a moth.

Not *to* it, precisely. That was the thing about beast-speakers—half the time they didn't know they were doing it. Their lips barely moved. But the moth had landed on Cyrus's knuckle and was fanning its wings in slow, deliberate rhythm, and Cyrus was watching it with an expression Sable recognized from her own mirror, thirty years ago and three lifetimes away: the look of someone who has just remembered they are starving.

*You're late for the evening feeding,* the moth was saying, or rather broadcasting, in the fuzzy way insects broadcast—less language than weather. *The lizards get cross. The little one bites.*

"The little one always bites," Cyrus murmured, and then froze, hearing their own voice in the empty cloister.

"Then you'd best go feed it," Sable said.

Cyrus came off the bench like a startled cat, which was, Sable noted with professional detachment, exactly the wrong instinct. Startled cats ran. Running things got chased. She stayed very still by the archway, hands folded at her belt, seals glinting dully on her cuffs—the lead seal, the one that tasted fear, hung quiet against her wrist. Good. The child wasn't afraid yet. Afraid would come later, when she explained what she'd actually come to say.

"You," Cyrus said. "You're the Warden."

"I'm the Warden." She let the silence stretch a moment, enjoying the way Cyrus's eyes flicked to the cloister's exits—one behind her, one to the left, both hopeless, and the moment they realized it, something in their shoulders settled into the particular stubbornness of the cornered who have decided to be difficult about it. "You're not going to run?"

"Would it help?"

"Not even slightly. The court's halls are sealed to your kind after dark. I made sure of it this morning." She tilted her head. "But I appreciate the impulse. It shows sense."

"I've been told I have very little."

"By the raven, presumably." Sable crossed the cloister slowly and sat on the far end of the bench, leaving a full arm's length between them—an old trick, giving prey room enough to feel uncaught. "Bramble. He was called Quillon, before. Did he tell you that? No, of course not. Ravens edit."

Cyrus didn't sit. "If you're here to arrest me—"

"If I were here to arrest you, child, we'd be having this conversation in the Sealed Gallery, and there would be four more Wardens present, and none of us would be sitting down." She patted the bench. "Sit. The moonlace bites if you loom over it. Another of the court's little jokes."

After a long moment, Cyrus sat. Not close. But they sat, and their eyes—sharp, wary, and underneath the wariness, unbearably young—stayed on her face as though reading it for traps. Which was correct. It was full of traps. It had been for decades.

*Careful,* said the lead seal at her wrist, tasting the air. *It wants something from you. You want something from it. One of those is a lie.*

Sable told the seal, silently and with some feeling, to mind its own business.

* * *

"You used your gift in the fens," she began. "Marsh-elk. Eleven of them, if my counters are accurate, and my counters are always accurate. You turned a herd against a Warden of Seals." She let that hang. "Do you know how many people alive can do that?"

"I'm guessing the number is small."

"The number is one." Sable watched that land. Watched Cyrus's breath catch and steady, catch and steady—the rhythm of someone deciding not to be flattered and failing at it. "Beast-speakers come in gradations. Whispers, mostly. A girl in Vell's Crossing who can calm a spooked horse. A drover who dreams what his dogs dream. Useful. Harmless. The court licenses them and files them under *amenities*, next to the musicians." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "What you did in the fens was not a whisper. It was a shout heard through stone. Every seal in the mountain rang like a struck bell. I felt it from three ridges away, and I have felt nothing through these—" she touched the cuff-seals, lightly, the way one touches an old scar "—in thirty years."

"So you're saying I'm loud."

"I'm saying you're *impossible*. And impossible things in this mountain have exactly two fates." She held up one finger. "They get eaten." A second finger. "Or they get kept."

"Eaten," Cyrus repeated flatly. "Kept. Those are the options."

"And a third, which is why I'm here, sitting on a bench under decorative fungus instead of dragging you to the Sealed Gallery: the option nobody offers, because offering it is treason." Sable leaned back and looked up through the moonlace at the vaulted dark above, where the ceiling dissolved into rock so ancient no one remembered whether the court had been built into the mountain or grown out of it. "I am about to commit treason, Cyrus Nystrom. I'd thank you to listen properly."

* * *

She took him—her, them; Sable had decided, somewhere in the fens, watching the child stand over the dead fawn with grief written all over their face like a confession, that *them* suited—took them down through the gallery of minor nobles' apartments, past tapestries woven with thread spun from moth-dreams, past a corridor where bottled music played itself eternally to an empty room. Cyrus walked half a step behind her, and every few paces their head turned—toward a caged firefly, toward a sleeping hound behind a screen, toward the faint, miserable hum of something small and sentient trapped in a wall niche—and each time, Sable saw them wrench their attention back, ashamed of looking, unable not to.

"You hear all of it," she said quietly. "Don't you."

"It's very loud here." Cyrus's voice was careful. "Everything in this place is either singing or screaming. Sometimes both."

"Yes." Sable did not soften it. Softening was cruelty, here. "That's what the court is. A larder that sings."

They descended. The stair was cut from living stone and the stone was warm, and Sable felt Cyrus notice the warmth, felt them flinch from it, the way one flinches from a hand on the shoulder that means nothing good. At the bottom stood a door of black iron banded with seals—her seals, forty of them, layered like scales—and she pressed her palm to the center one and spoke the word that was not a word, and the door thought about it for a long, insulting moment before opening.

Beyond lay the Vault of Kept Voices.

Sable had walked into that vault perhaps ten thousand times, and it had never once failed to take her breath—not with awe, never awe, but with the particular vertigo of standing inside the answer to a question you'd spent your whole life asking. Shelves rose into the dark, carved from the mountain's own bone, and on the shelves, thousands upon thousands of lanterns: glass and brass and stranger materials, each one holding a soft light that pulsed. Slow. Patient. Like breathing. And from each lantern, if you stood close enough, a whisper. Names. Fragments. A woman's voice reciting a recipe for plum bread over and over, because it was the last thing she'd chosen to keep. A man humming. A child counting stars.

Beside her, Cyrus had gone rigid.

"They're alive," Cyrus whispered. "They're all still *alive* in there."

"Alive is a strong word. *Persisting.* The court prefers *preserved*." Sable walked the aisle with the ease of long habit, her boots silent on stone, while Cyrus followed as though walking on the surface of a frozen lake. "Here is what I was like once, since you're wondering and too polite to ask. I was born in the Weald, north country, to a family of root-readers—hedge magic, small magic, the kind that keeps crops honest and babies healthy. And I had the gift. The true gift. I could talk to anything with a heartbeat. When I was nine years old I talked a river out of drowning our neighbor's boy, and my mother wept, and my father started building a hiding place in the cellar floor, because we both knew what the world does with speakers."

She stopped before a lantern of smoked glass, its light a deep, steady amber, and did not touch it. She never touched it.

"I ran for six years. I was good at it. Better than you—I had a whole family running interference, and I still got caught, in the end, at seventeen, in a snowstorm, with frostbite in three toes and nowhere left to be. And the court's steward found me and offered me tea." Sable laughed, short and dry, a sound like a lock turning. "Tea. In a cell. And he said: *you have a choice. The mountain eats what resists it. But it makes room for what serves.*"

"What does that mean?" Cyrus asked. "Makes room. What does that actually *mean*?"

"It means they took my gift out of me and put it in that lantern." She nodded at the amber light. "And then they gave me a different gift in its place. Seals. Authority. The right to walk anywhere and be feared by everything, including the things I used to call friends." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced level for thirty years; it was the one craft she'd truly mastered. "I can no longer hear a single bird, Cyrus Nystrom. I stood in the fens while you shouted through the stone, and I felt it only as pressure, like weather coming. Do you understand? I caught you because I remember what it feels like from the outside. That's all I have left of it. Memory."

The amber lantern pulsed. Once. Twice. And Sable would have sworn—had sworn, to herself, on sleepless nights—that the pulse quickened, just slightly, when she spoke near it. As though whatever remained in there leaned toward the sound of its own name.

"That's you," Cyrus said softly. Not a question.

"That's me." She turned away from it. "Now. Here is the treasonous part, so attend."

* * *

She led them deeper, past the shelves, to where the vault narrowed into a gallery of newer lanterns—brighter, their lights less settled, still flickering with the shock of recent keeping. Ilse's lantern was there. Sable had checked on it eleven times since the courier died at the ford; it was procedure, and also it was not procedure, and she knew the difference even if she filed it under the former.

"This one belonged to the woman who saved your life," Sable said. "Ilse Vey. She sold her soul to buy twenty-two years of wardlight around a valley, around a boundary stone, around a baby left in the reeds. Sentimental, for a smuggler. Stupid, definitely. Brave, in the way that gets people killed." She watched Cyrus's hand rise toward the glass and stop, trembling, an inch away. "When the thirteenth moon fills, the court will open that lantern and pour it into the mountain's heart, and Ilse Vey will become warmth and power and nothing else. That is the appetite you've walked into. That is what hunts you—not me. Me, you could outrun. The mountain, never."

"Then why—" Cyrus's voice cracked and steadied. "Why show me this? Why any of this? You could have taken me in the cloister."

"Because I am the only person in this mountain who knows both sides of the bargain." Sable faced them fully, and let them see her—all of it, the gray at her temples, the seals on her wrists, the hollow where a gift used to live. "Listen carefully, because I will say this once, and then I will deny it under oath if necessary. The court will offer you what it offered me. It will dress it beautifully. It will tell you that you'll be safe, honored, *made room for*. And part of you—the tired part, the part that spent twenty-two years hiding in a valley built to erase you—is going to want to say yes." She stepped closer. "Don't."

"You just said those were the only two fates."

"I said those were the only two fates *the court allows*. I am proposing a third, and it is a terrible plan, and it will probably kill us both, and it is still better than the tea." Sable allowed herself, for the first time in years, a real smile—thin, crooked, rusted with disuse. "You cannot beat the mountain, child. But mountains have hearts, and hearts have doors, and I have spent thirty years learning where every seal in this place is set and which ones I installed myself. There is a night, thirteen days from now, when the thirteenth moon stands full and the court opens its vault to feed. For one hour, every seal in this mountain turns to hold the ceremony. Including mine."

Understanding dawned on Cyrus's face slowly, like light through water.

"You want me to help you rob them."

"I want you to help me commit the largest theft in the history of the subterranean court," Sable corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that robbery is a crime and this is justice, and I intend to enjoy the distinction while the mountain is eating us." She held out her hand—palm up, the lead seal dark and quiet against her wrist, almost, she thought, shy. "Ilse Vey bought your life once. I intend to see the purchase honored. And somewhere in that shelf—" her eyes went, despite herself, to the amber lantern, and her voice, for one unguarded syllable, lost its level "—there is a piece of me I would very much like to hold again before I die. Even for an hour. Even just to hear one bird."

Cyrus looked at her hand. Looked at the shelves of whispering lights. And Sable saw the war behind their eyes—the valley and the vault, the hiding and the hunger, twenty-two years of being a secret weighing itself against the offer of becoming a weapon—and she waited, because she had learned patience from the mountain, and patience was nearly the same thing as faith.

Then Cyrus Nystrom reached out and shook the Warden of Seals' hand, and somewhere high above them, deep in the mountain's warm stone, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep—and smiled.

It had heard everything.
