## 15. The Vault of Kept Voices

Odji's map of the smuggler ways was drawn on a scrap of hide no bigger than a supper plate, and it was wrong in three places before they'd gone a hundred paces.

"Left here," Cyrus whispered.

"That's what the map says," Odji hissed back. "The map is a liar. I told you I sold my route to the court—I didn't say I sold an *accurate* route. You sell the accurate one, they stop needing you."

"Then how do we—"

"I remember it by feel." Odji pressed their palm flat against the tunnel wall, which was warm in a way stone had no business being this deep under the mountain. "Warm means near a vent. Warm and humming means near the halls. Cold and quiet means we're lost, and we've been cold and quiet for ten minutes, so congratulations."

Above them, Bramble made a sound that was unmistakably a laugh, even filtered through the strange borrowed channel between raven and girl. *You hired a guide who navigates by temperature,* he said into Cyrus's mind, dry as old paper. *Bold strategy.*

"You could be scouting instead of mocking," Cyrus muttered.

*Mocking is scouting. It tells you everything about a person.* A flutter of wings somewhere in the dark overhead. *Also, fine. There's a junction ahead. Two ways down. One smells like dust and old sorrow. The other smells like the court's kitchens, which means grease, which means guards, which means I vote for sorrow.*

They took the sorrow.

The smuggler ways had been carved long ago, if the tool-marks were any guide—long, careful grooves in the rock, older than the court above them, older maybe than the ward in Highmoor. Cyrus ran her fingers along them as she walked and felt something faint stir in answer, the way a sleeping animal twitches when touched. The mountain was not dead down here. That was the worst part of it. She'd expected the deep places to be empty, and instead everything was *listening*.

She'd stopped fighting that feeling two days ago. Down here, with the gift roaring through her like a river that had been dammed for twenty-two years, she heard everything: the blind pale things that scuttled in the cracks, the slow mineral thoughts of stone itself, and—always, everywhere—the whispering.

It had started an hour after they left Odji's tunnel-mouth. Faint at first, like wind through reeds. Then clearer. Then unbearable.

Voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Coming from everywhere and nowhere, murmuring half-sentences, names, apologies, songs cut off mid-verse.

"It's the lanterns," Odji had said, not looking at her. "The vault's close enough now that you can hear them leak. Don't listen too hard. My sister's voice is in there somewhere, and the first time I heard it I walked straight into a wall and cracked my head open. Not dignified. The guards found me and I had to pretend I was drunk."

Cyrus was trying very hard not to listen too hard. She was failing. Every whisper snagged on her gift like burrs on wool. *Mama. Mama, the bread's burning. I never meant—I only wanted—cold, it's so cold, why did I think the price was fair, it seemed fair then—*

"Bramble," she said quietly. "How much farther?"

*A hundred heartbeats past the next bend. Then a door. Then the thing itself.* He paused. *Little thief, when we're inside—you'll want to touch everything. Don't. Some of those voices will ask you for help. Some of them will sound like people you love.*

"They already do."

*I know,* Bramble said, and for once there was nothing sardonic in it at all.

* * *

The door was iron, and iron was a problem.

Not because of locks—Odji dealt with the lock in the time it took Cyrus to count to forty, muttering cheerful obscenities at the tumblers, and swung it open on darkness. The problem was what came through the opening: a wave of sound, sudden and total, the whispers swelling from murmur to chorus. Cyrus staggered. Odji grabbed her arm.

"Breathe," the sprite-seller said. "In through the nose. They can't hurt you. They're just—"

"Loud."

"They're just *sad*. Sadness carries. Now come on, before someone checks the door."

The vault was a cavern, and the cavern was full of light.

Soul-lanterns hung from the ceiling in cascades, tier upon tier, thousands of glass globes strung on silver chains, each one glowing with its own color—amber, rose, a green like deep water, one furious violet that flickered like a struck match. Below them rose shelving cut into the living rock, row after row climbing toward the dark of the roof, and every shelf held more lanterns, resting in velvet-lined cradles, each cradle labeled with a small brass plate.

And every single lantern was whispering.

Up close, Cyrus could almost make out individual voices—a woman singing a lullaby in a language she didn't know; a man counting, over and over, as though he'd miscounted something once and couldn't stop; a child asking, patiently, endlessly, *is it time yet? is it time yet?* The light moved with the voices, brightening when they strengthened, dimming when they trailed off. The whole cavern breathed with it, a tide of small griefs.

Cyrus stood in the doorway with her hand over her mouth until she trusted herself not to be sick.

"This is where Ilse's soul is," she said. It wasn't a question. "This whole room."

"One shelf among many," Odji said. "The newer acquisitions go up high—they rotate them down toward the tasting hall as the moon fills. Thirteenth moon's four nights out, so anything still up top is—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Is still just sitting there. Waiting. Like mine would have been."

Cyrus looked at them. In the lantern-light, Odji's face was carefully blank, the way faces got when blankness was all that was holding a person together.

"We'll get your sister too," Cyrus said.

"Don't make promises with your whole chest in a room like this," Odji said, but their hand found Cyrus's shoulder and squeezed, once, and stayed there a moment longer than necessary.

*Bramble's found the index,* the raven reported. *There's a ledger chained to a lectern near the far wall. And before you ask—yes, I can read. I was educated. Poorly, but expensively.*

"How do you know which shelf?" Cyrus whispered as they crept along the wall, hugging the shadows between the great root-columns that held up the cavern roof.

*The plates are numbered. The ledger maps numbers to names. And the newest arrivals are shelved by date, and Ilse died—* a pause; even a raven hesitated at some arithmetic *—twenty-two years and some months ago. Which means her lantern won't be up high with the new stock. It'll be deep in the old rows. Near the bottom. Where things go to be forgotten until they're needed.*

Twenty-two years. Cyrus's throat tightened. Ilse had sold her soul the year Cyrus was born—or the year Maren had carried an infant to the boundary stone and begged—and had spent two decades walking the wilds as a courier, carrying messages, keeping watch over a child she'd never met, while the truest part of herself sat in the dark under a mountain gathering dust.

And Cyrus had spent those same twenty-two years ten miles away, tending goats, never knowing there was a debt at all.

*Second row,* Bramble said. *Numbered forty-one through sixty. Her name should be on the plate. Assuming the court spells it correctly, which given what I've seen of their bookkeeping—*

"Bramble."

*Moving.*

The second row was low—shelves barely waist-high, built for storage rather than display, and darker than the rest. Here the lanterns were older, their glass smoky, their colors muted to embers. The whispering was softer too, worn down by years, like sea glass. Cyrus knelt in the dust and read plates by feel, her fingers tracing the engraved letters because there wasn't light enough to read by eye.

Thirty-eight: a name she didn't know. Forty-one: another. Forty-four—

Her fingers stopped.

ILSE VARGA.

The lantern sat in its cradle like a bird in a nest, small and round and dim. Its glow was the color of late afternoon through honey, and it pulsed—slowly, sleepily, but it pulsed. Cyrus lifted it out with both hands and it weighed almost nothing, and it weighed everything.

"Ilse," she whispered. "It's me. It's—it's Cyrus. From the ford."

The whispering inside the glass shifted. Coalesced. For a moment it was almost words, almost a voice: *...the little one? ...is she...?*

"I'm here," Cyrus said, and her eyes were burning and she let them. "I got your map. I'm getting you out. I swear it. On the ford water, on Maren's kitchen, on everything—I swear."

The lantern glowed a fraction brighter. Inside, the whisper turned over and over like a dog circling to settle, and though no words formed, Cyrus understood it anyway, the way she understood animals without language: *warm. safe. stay.*

"Not staying," Cyrus said fiercely. "Leaving. All of us. That's the whole point."

*Touching scene,* said a voice from the shelf beside her knee. *Genuinely. I'm moved. I'm weeping. Well—one of us is.*

Cyrus froze.

The voice hadn't come from her mind. It hadn't come from Odji, who was standing guard at the end of the row. It had come from the lantern in the neighboring cradle—plate number forty-five—and it was dry, and sardonic, and horribly, instantly familiar.

She turned her head slowly and looked at the label.

BRAMBLE. EXILE OF THE COURT. VOICE FORFEIT, YEAR OF THE LONG SNOW.

For a long moment nobody spoke—not Cyrus, not Odji, not the whispering hundreds around them. Somewhere above, Bramble's wings went silent in the rafters, which was somehow louder than any sound he could have made.

"No," Cyrus said finally. "No, no, no. You're—Bramble, you're *here*. You're flying around up there cracking jokes. How can you be—"

*Ah,* said the raven's voice in her mind, very quietly. *Yes. About that.*

"You KNEW?"

*I suspected,* Bramble said. *I hoped otherwise. There's a difference, and I clung to it with both feet.*

Cyrus stared at the lantern. The glow inside it was black-shot, shot through with threads of darkness like ink dropped in water—but beneath the dark, unmistakably, burned a spark of the same wry amber she'd come to know perched on her shoulder across five hundred miles of wilderness.

"When I crossed the ward," she said slowly, working it through, "you said you could speak because you'd crossed it long ago. You said the court couldn't hear you outside it. You said—"

*I lied,* Bramble said simply. *Mostly. I do have a body that flies and eats garbage and enjoys both. But the part of me that talks—that was taken the winter I stole from the sovereign's own table. They keep voices here the way they keep souls. A soul is the whole candle. A voice is just the flame.* A pause. *They let me keep enough of myself to walk and fly and hate them efficiently. The rest has been sitting on this shelf for sixty years, listening to the neighbors.*

Sixty years. The number landed in Cyrus's chest like a stone. Sixty years of whispers, and the thing beside Ilse's lantern had been him the whole time—had been *talking to her* through a body while his actual voice lay six inches from where Ilse's soul slept, and neither of them had known, and he had known, or suspected, and had said nothing because—

"Why?" she asked. "Why didn't you tell me?"

Silence from the rafters. Then, from the lantern, in that same dry voice, quieter: *Because I watched her die, child.* The glow flickered. *Ilse. Twenty-two years on the shelf next to me. Do you know what you hear, down here? Everything. Every bargain made upstairs, every lie, every courier who came in and never walked out. She used to talk to me. Couldn't hear my answers—her lantern only whispers, mine only speaks—but she talked anyway. Told me about a valley. About a ward that ate magic. About a child she'd bought a lifetime for, and whether the price had been worth it, and how she'd never know.*

The lantern brightened, steadied.

*So when I found you at the ford, following the trail she'd paid for with everything she had—when I saw the chance to finally be useful to somebody instead of just clever—* the voice roughened *—I thought perhaps I'd earned the right to one omission. One. I'm sorry. It was selfish. I wanted to be the raven who helped you, not the lantern who asked.*

Cyrus knelt there with two lights in her hands: one holding a woman who had bought her life, and one holding the voice of the friend who had guided her to steal it back. Around her, a thousand strangers whispered their small griefs into the dark, and none of them knew that anyone had ever come down here meaning to leave again.

"You could have told me at any point in the last three weeks," she managed.

*Yes. See previous statement regarding selfishness. Also I was enjoying being admired for my wit, and I feared the original was superior and would show me up.*

Despite everything—despite the tears and the terror and the ticking of the thirteenth moon—Cyrus laughed. It came out wet and broken, but it was a laugh, and the lantern flared warmly at the sound, as if it had been waiting sixty years to cause one.

"Right," she said, scrubbing her face with her sleeve. "New plan. We take all three of you. Ilse, your voice, and—"

"No," said Odji, from the end of the row. Their voice was strange. Tight. "Cyrus. Come look at this."

Cyrus tucked Ilse's lantern against her chest, wrapped both hands around Bramble's, and followed.

At the very end of the second row, half-hidden behind a pillar, sat a lantern unlike the others. Newer. Brighter. Its cradle bore no brass plate—only a strip of fresh parchment, ink still sharp:

HELD IN TRUST. NOT FOR SALE. NOT FOR TASTING. BY ORDER OF THE SOVEREIGN.

And beneath it, in smaller letters, a name that made Odji make a sound like they'd been struck:

*Yara.*

"My sister," Odji whispered. Their hands hovered over the glass without touching, the way you hover over a fire you're afraid will go out. "She's been here eight months. I sold your route to buy her off the tasting list, and they moved her anyway, and they *marked her*—" Their voice cracked clean through. "Held in trust. For *what*?"

Cyrus reached out with her gift, gently, the way she'd approach a frightened animal—and the lantern flared at her touch of attention, and inside it, a young woman's voice said, clear as a bell through the glass:

"*Odji? Odji, is that you? Oh, thank every star—run. You have to run. It knows you're here. It's known since the door.*"

Far above them, through miles of stone, something vast shifted in its sleep.

And every lantern in the vault, all at once, went silent.
