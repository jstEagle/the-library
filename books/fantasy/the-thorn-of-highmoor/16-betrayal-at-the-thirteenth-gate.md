## 16. Betrayal at the Thirteenth Gate

Odji had counted the lanterns twice.

The first time had been for the route—Bramble's whispered directions, memorized in the dark of the smuggler ways, each turn marked by a scratch of moss or a dead man's boot buckle nailed to the stone. The second time was now, standing at the edge of the vault with their heart hammering against their ribs like something trying to get out, counting because counting was easier than feeling.

Forty-one soul-lanterns on the long shelves. Forty-one small lights, each one breathing its slow, glassy breath. Each one whispering.

They had told themselves they'd come back for Sefa. They had told themselves that every night for three months, lying in cots and ditches and once, memorably, the hayloft of a very judgmental mule, whispering her name into the dark like a prayer that had gotten too tired to kneel.

*I sold the route,* Odji thought, watching Cyrus move between the shelves with that careful, listening stillness of theirs, Bramble a shadow on their shoulder. *I sold it to save her, and I told myself I'd fix it, and now we're here, and she is right there.*

Shelf four. Third lantern from the end. The light inside it flickered in a rhythm Odji knew better than their own pulse—the quick, impatient stutter of a girl who could never wait for soup to cool.

Sefa.

*Don't*, said the sensible part of them, the part that had kept them alive through Vell's Crossing and the fens and the long gray walk beneath the mountain. *Wait. Take Ilse's first. Get out. Come back with an army, a plan, anything.*

But the sensible part of Odji had been losing arguments to their heart since before they could talk, and the lanterns were right there, and the thirteenth moon was only two nights from full, and Sefa's light was stuttering faster now, frightened, the way she always got frightened right before she pretended not to be.

Cyrus lifted Ilse's lantern from the shelf with both hands, gentle as lifting a sleeping child, and Odji saw the moment the whispers around them changed—saw Cyrus's head come up like a deer scenting smoke.

"What is it?" Odji asked, and hated how fast the words came out, too quick, too bright, the voice of someone who already knew.

"Boots," Cyrus breathed. "A long way off. And—" Their eyes went strange, unfocused, the gift pulling sound through stone the way roots pull water. "Gates. Something's waking up in the gates."

Odji's hand was already moving. That was the terrible thing about it—not deliberation, not even decision, just their body choosing love over loyalty the way a falling person chooses to grab whatever's nearest. The little iron bell hung by the vault door, the court's alarm, the thing Bramble had warned them about with a raven's flat contempt: *ring it and the whole mountain comes running, so don't ring it, obviously, are you simple?*

Their fingers closed around it.

*I'm sorry*, they thought, to Cyrus, to Bramble, to the dying woman's map and the whole ridiculous chain of kindnesses that had carried them here. *I'm sorry. But you'd have done the same. Anyone who wouldn't has never had a sister.*

They rang it.

* * *

The sound did not travel through the vault so much as *arrive* everywhere at once, the way thunder does—a single brazen note that Odji felt in their teeth, in the floor, in the glass of forty-one lanterns shivering into a chorus of tiny screams.

"Odji." Cyrus turned, and there was no anger in their face yet, which was worse; there was only confusion, wide and open, the face of someone waiting to be told the world was still shaped the way they thought. "What did you do?"

"I—" Odji's voice cracked. Somewhere above them, stone doors were grinding open, and the boots Cyrus had heard had stopped being far away. "My sister. Shelf four. She's been down here eight months, Cyrus, eight months, and I sold your route to the Warden's agents in the spring because they had her lantern in a room with me and a knife, and I keep telling myself I was going to fix it—"

"You *sold*—"

"I was going to fix it!" It came out as a wail, undignified and true. "That counts for something! In some courts! Possibly this one!"

Bramble dropped from Cyrus's shoulder onto the shelf beside Sefa's lantern and peered into the glass with one glittering eye. "Huh," he said. "She looks like you. Same look of a person about to do something stupid."

"Bramble, *not now*."

"There won't be a later, featherless." He rapped his beak against the glass, twice, softly, almost tender. "Hello, little thief. Hold on. We're all thieves here tonight."

And then the doors.

There were three ways out of the vault—Odji's smuggler tunnel, breathing cold behind them; the great processional stair, which was where the boots were coming from; and the Thirteenth Gate itself, the ceremonial arch at the vault's far end, wrought in black iron and moon-silver, built for one night a year when the court came down to feed. Cyrus ran for the tunnel. Sable's seals bloomed across it before Cyrus had taken five steps—white sigils flaring up out of the stone like frost given opinions—and the passage simply closed, folding shut with a sound like a book ending.

"No," Cyrus said quietly.

"Yes," said Warden Sable, from the stair.

* * *

Sable came down the steps without hurry, because hurry was for people who weren't certain. Her gray coat caught the lantern-light and gave none of it back. Behind her, four wardens of the court fanned out along the stair, and behind *them*, drawn up along the gallery above, the courtiers had begun to gather—drawn by the alarm the way moths draw to a lamp, beautiful and hollow-eyed, craning to see what the mountain had caught.

She took in the scene the way she took in everything: completely, and with visible disappointment.

"The beast-speaker," she said, "with the courier's lantern in her hands. The sprite-peddler, ringing alarms like a child with a pot and spoon." Her gaze moved to Bramble, and something old and complicated crossed her face, quickly buried. "And a bird I exiled forty years ago, who apparently still can't resist coming home."

"Came back for my stuff," Bramble said.

"Your stuff is *in the lanterns*, Bramble."

"One of them is. The rest is metaphorical."

"Sable." Cyrus stepped forward, Ilse's lantern held against their chest, and Sable watched them do it—the instinctive curl of the body around another's soul, the refusal to set it down even with wardens' crossbows leveled—and felt something shift under her ribs in a place she had sealed over decades ago. "You showed me the vault. You *showed me*. You knew what I'd come for. Why let us get this far?"

"Because I wanted to watch you try." Sable's voice stayed level, but her hands, folded behind her back, had gone white-knuckled. "Because the sovereign demanded a demonstration, and I would rather it happen here, in front of witnesses, than have you dragged down to the feeding hall screaming. Because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I was on that side of the room once. Holding someone else's light. And nobody in this court offered me a choice with any dignity in it. So I am offering you one. Put down the lanterns. Kneel. Give the gift freely, and the court will make room for you, as it made room for me. You will want for nothing except everything that matters. It is," she added, with a bitterness so worn smooth it had almost become courtesy, "a genuinely generous offer."

"That's not an offer," Cyrus said. "That's a menu with one dish."

"It's the only dish on the table."

"Then I'll eat elsewhere."

Above them, the courtiers murmured—delighted, hungry, bored in the specific way of people who had watched a thousand souls go into the glass and needed this one to be different somehow. Sable raised one hand, and the wardens raised their crossbows, and the Thirteenth Gate at the far end of the vault began, slowly, to glow—the mountain waking up behind it, tasting the air, smelling the gift in Cyrus like smoke smells a forest.

It was Odji who broke first. Not with courage—with panic, which wears courage's clothes often enough that most people can't tell them apart.

"Sable—Warden—" They shoved forward, hands up, tears cutting clean lines through the vault dust on their face. "You got what you paid for. The route, the tunnels, all of it, that was me, months ago, I never lied about the price—I just need her. Shelf four. My sister. Take mine instead, take both, take—" their voice did something awful, "—she's nineteen, she got taken because she tried to steal bread for *me*, and I have spent every day since being the kind of person who lets other people pay, and I can't, I can't do it again, not with them, not with *her*—"

The vault went quiet. Even the lanterns seemed to hush.

Sable looked at Odji for a long moment. And Cyrus, who heard things other people didn't—heartbeats, grief, the small dishonesties of bodies—heard what moved under the Warden's stillness: not contempt. Recognition. The sound of a woman hearing her own voice come out of someone else's mouth, twenty years too late.

"You rang the alarm," Sable said at last, "to save her. Knowing it would seal the gates on yourself."

"I wasn't thinking. That's the whole point. I'm *done* thinking."

"Hm." Sable turned away, and for one suspended heartbeat Cyrus thought—they would swear to it later—that the Warden's shoulders dropped half an inch, like a coat settling wrong. Then she said, crisp as a ledger closing: "Seal the Thirteenth Gate. Full ceremony protocols. The sovereign feeds in two nights regardless; we may as well give it an appetite."

White fire crawled across the great arch. Beyond it, faintly, something enormous exhaled.

* * *

They put them in the antechamber off the vault—the holding room, though someone with the court's particular cruelty had made it comfortable. Cushions. Wine. A window that wasn't a window, just a sheet of polished obsidian showing the mountain's deep galleries, lit by bottled souls drifting overhead like slow, silent fireworks.

"They're showing us the lanterns," Cyrus said. They sat with their back to the wall, Ilse's lantern in their lap, refusing every cushion. "So we know what's coming. It's a threat dressed as hospitality."

"Everything here is dressed as something else," Bramble said. He was perched on the obsidian, studying his own reflection with distaste. "The courtiers are dressed as people. The hunger is dressed as a government. The prison is dressed as a guest room. Honestly, the wine is probably dressed as wine, but I'd chance it."

"Don't," said Odji, from the corner, where they had been sitting very still with their knees pulled up. "Don't be funny at me. I rang the bell. I sealed us in. I—" Their voice went thin. "You should hate me."

Cyrus looked at them for a while. Down the corridor, boots passed and passed; somewhere below, the mountain made a sound like a stomach.

"When Ilse found me at the ford," Cyrus said finally, "dying, in the rain, she gave me a map she could have used herself. Or sold. Or burned. She gave it to a stranger because she'd already spent herself paying for that stranger, years ago, sight unseen, and she wanted the payment to mean something." They turned the lantern in their lap; the light inside it steadied, as if listening. "You sold me out to buy your sister back. I'm not going to tell you it doesn't hurt. It does. But Odji—you did it *for* someone. Every person in this mountain did it *for* themselves. If that's the worst thing you ever do, you're better than this place deserves, and probably better than I'd be."

Odji made a sound that was mostly a sob wearing a laugh's coat. "I'm going to cry on you."

"Noted."

"And then possibly vomit."

"*Noted.*"

Bramble hopped down from the obsidian and landed on the floor between them, and for once he didn't preen or posture. He looked at the lantern in Cyrus's lap, and then at the ceiling, where the drifting souls glowed and glowed, forty-one of them down the hall plus hundreds more beyond, and his voice came out stripped of its usual varnish.

"Two nights," he said. "Moon fills, gate opens, sovereign feeds. That's the shape of it. Unless somebody in this room does something the mountain hasn't seen in four hundred years."

"Like what?"

"Well." Bramble cocked his head, and there—there it was, the old gleam, the thief's gleam, the gleam of a bird looking at an impossible lock and taking it personally. "I stole my own voice once. Badly. Got caught, got jarred, got exiled. But I've been thinking, these last weeks, watching you talk to goats and elk and one deeply unimpressive mule." He tapped the floor with his beak. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* "Every lantern in this vault is locked to a keeper's seal. And there's exactly one person in this court who knows every seal ever cut."

The room went still.

"She taught half of them herself," Bramble said softly. "Before she gave the rest of herself away. And I've been listening to her for two days, little speaker, and I'm telling you what ravens know and wardens forget: a seal is a promise. And promises"—his eye glinted—"can be *kept differently*."

Down the corridor, the boots stopped outside their door. A key turned. And Warden Sable stood in the doorway alone, no escort, no crossbow, her face in the lantern-light like a mask that had been left out in the rain—still shaped, but softening at the edges.

"Get up," she said. "Both of you. And bring the lanterns."

"Why?" Cyrus asked.

Sable looked past them, at the drifting souls beyond the false window, and when she answered, her voice was barely louder than the whispering glass.

"Because the moon rises tomorrow night," she said, "and I find, after forty years, that I would like to break a promise instead of keeping it."
