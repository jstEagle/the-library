## 17. The Mountain's Appetite

They did not drag Cyrus to the sovereign. That was the first strange thing.

After the gates sealed and Odji was pulled away—still shouting apologies that sounded more like warnings—the guards had simply waited. Then a door opened where no door had been, in the wall of the corridor, and a chamberlain made of too many joints bowed and said, "The mountain will see you now," as though Cyrus had requested an audience rather than been caught breaking into its pantry.

Bramble rode on Cyrus's shoulder, uncharacteristically silent. His talons gripped tighter than usual.

"You could fly off," Cyrus murmured under their breath.

"And miss this?" Bramble whispered back. "I've wondered for forty years what sits at the bottom of this place. Besides." A pause. "If it eats you, I want to know what the chewing sounds like. For my memoirs."

"Comforting."

"I'm a raven, not a nursemaid."

The corridor descended in slow spirals, and with every turn the light changed. The soul-lanterns of the courtier halls gave way to something older—veins of pale luminescence running through the stone itself, like marrow glowing through bone. The air grew warm. Not comfortable-warm; body-warm, the warmth of a throat. Cyrus realized, somewhere around the fourth spiral down, that they were not walking through a tunnel at all.

They were being swallowed.

*Don't think about it*, they told themselves, and thought about nothing else.

The guards stopped at a threshold of black stone polished smooth as still water. Beyond it, the passage opened into a vast hollow—a geode the size of a cathedral, its walls glittering with crystal growths that caught the light and broke it into colors that didn't quite have names. And in the center, half-embedded in the floor like a heart in a ribcage, sat the sovereign.

Cyrus had imagined a king. A courtly monster, perhaps, all silks and long fingers and beautiful cruelty. They had prepared speeches. Insults, even, held in reserve.

What sat before them was the mountain wearing a person like a coat.

The shape was roughly human—seated, draped, crowned with stalactites that might have grown or might have been placed—but the seams were wrong. Stone flowed into flesh at the wrists without boundary. Crystals budded along one cheekbone like frost forming. When it moved—and it moved slowly, terribly, the way glaciers move when no one is watching—the whole chamber creaked with the sound of settling rock. Its face, where there was one, was gentle. That was the worst part. It looked *kind*, the way a landslide is kind to the valley it flattens.

"Cyrus Nystrom," the sovereign said. The voice came not from its mouth but from everywhere—from the walls, from the floor beneath Cyrus's boots, from the crystals singing faint harmonics above. It was the voice of a cave giving an echo a name. "Twenty-two years old. Born under a waning moon. Found at a boundary stone by a woman named Maren, who loved you badly and well. Kept safe by a courier who sold herself piece by piece so that no one would come looking for you." It tilted its head, and gravel whispered somewhere inside it. "You are angry. Good. Anger is a kind of appetite too. Sit."

There was no chair. Cyrus stayed standing.

"I'd rather not."

"Of course you would rather not." Something that was almost amusement rippled through the chamber. "Standing suits defiance. I remember standing. It was four hundred years ago, but I remember." One arm—half stone, half something softer—lifted in a gesture of terrible hospitality toward the glittering dark. "Do you know what you are looking at, little speaker?"

"A very large room."

"The largest appetite you will ever meet." The sovereign leaned forward, and the motion took a full breath to complete, stone grinding against stone. "You've seen my vaults. Shelves of lanterns, each one whispering. You think I collect souls because souls are pretty, or precious, or because I am cruel. Cruelty requires wanting things to hurt. I don't want that." A pause, almost wistful. "I am simply hungry, and magic is the only food there has ever been."

Bramble's talons dug in. "It's true," he breathed, so quietly only Cyrus heard. "Gods below. It's true. I thought it was a story the exiles told to frighten fledglings."

"Tell me," Cyrus said, and hated how steady their voice came out.

"You felt it already." The sovereign spread its hands, and the crystals overhead flared, throwing shadows like a forest of knives. "When you crossed your valley's ward, magic rushed back into you like blood into a sleeping limb. Every creature for miles, crying out at once. Do you wonder why the ward existed? Why someone built a dead place in the middle of living land and hid a child inside it?" Its gentle face inclined. "Because I bit it first."

The words landed like cold water.

"Highmoor's ward wasn't raised to hide you," the sovereign continued, conversational now, a teacher with a favorite pupil. "It was raised to hide the valley *from me*. There was once a people there—weavers, singers, speakers like you, dozens of them, their gifts thick as honey in the air. I found them the way I find everything: by following the taste of it. I ate their magic, Cyrus Nystrom. All of it. Their songs went quiet. Their beasts went dumb. And when there was nothing left to eat, what remained was silence—and silence is such a good hiding place that even I lose track of it eventually. So the survivors built a ward over their own emptiness and called it protection, and it was, in its way. A scab over the wound I left."

Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "That's not—" But it was. Of course it was. The dead valley. The ward that hummed like a struck bell at the edges. The way no one in Highmoor ever spoke of what the valley had been before. *A valley that forgets.* They had grown up inside a scar and called it home.

"The ward is failing," the sovereign said. "You noticed, didn't you? The hum gone thin. Sigils showing through where Ilse walked. Because a ward needs feeding, and the people who knew how to feed it are two hundred years in their graves. In another decade, maybe two, the last of it goes out like a candle." It smiled—or the stones arranged themselves into something adjacent to smiling. "And then I finish my meal. Slowly. Politely. Goat by goat, gift by gift, until your mother's valley is as quiet as the day I found it."

"You're going to eat Highmoor." Cyrus heard their own voice as if from far away. "All of it."

"I'm going to eat everything," the sovereign said, with the mild patience of the very old. "Eventually. The world makes more magic than it knows what to do with—it grows back, like grass, like moss, given time. I am patient. I have been patient for longer than your language has had a word for *mountain*. Souls are merely... preserves. Winter stores. Ilse's will keep me fed for a season when the thirteenth moon turns. Yours—" the crystal eyes brightened, actually brightened, light leaking out of them like heat through a crack, "—yours is not a preserve at all. Yours is a feast. A beast-speaker born whole, never spent, never sold, never nibbled. Do you understand what a rarity that is? Everyone else in this court sold some part of themselves to get here. You arrive *intact*. Do you know how that tastes?"

"No," Cyrus said. "And I don't want to."

"No," the sovereign agreed softly. "The living never do. It's one of the many things that makes them delicious."

* * *

The despair came later, which surprised them. First came the questions, spilling out faster than fear could dam them—because if Cyrus Nystrom was anything, they were a person who needed to understand a thing before they could be afraid of it properly.

"How?" they asked. "How do you *eat* magic? Magic isn't—it's not food, it doesn't sit in a bowl—"

"Neither does sunlight," said the sovereign, "and yet look at the trees." It gestured, and the crystal walls shimmered, and for a moment Cyrus saw—not with their eyes, but with the gift that roared awake beyond the ward—threads. Thousands of them, fine as spider silk, running out from the sovereign's seated form into the stone, up through the mountain's roots, out across the whole dark country. Threads of light being drawn inward, always inward, a river flowing the wrong direction. "Every spell cast in this land feeds me a crumb. Every charm bought at Vell's Crossing, every blessing muttered over a sick ewe, every wild thing using its small knowing to find its den—I sip it all as it passes. Most of it I barely notice. But concentrated things. Bound things. *Kept* things." Its gaze drifted upward, toward the vault, toward the shelves of whispering lanterns. "Those I savor."

"That's what the court is for." Cyrus's stomach turned. "The nobles. the ceremonies. The *tasting*. It's not decadence. It's farming."

"Now you see it. My courtiers believe they serve a king. They are gardeners and don't know the crop. They bottle souls because bottled souls concentrate—grief and love and talent, pressed tight, like cider. And when the thirteenth moon fills, we drink." The sovereign settled deeper into its throne of itself. "Ilse understood. She came here willingly, twenty-two years ago, and traded pieces of herself to buy a child's silence. She paid in installments, as it were. Her memory of her own mother's face. Her ability to sing. Her talent for maps—which is why she got lost so easily at the end, poor thing, wandering a country she had drawn blind. And finally the last coin she had." A pause. "Her self. It sits on my shelf, third row, between a hedge-witch's courage and a minstrel's joy. It whispers your name sometimes. They usually whisper names. The recently kept always do."

Cyrus's hands had closed into fists without their permission. Bramble made a low sound in his throat, almost a growl, almost grief.

"You're telling me all this," Cyrus said slowly, "because you want something. Monsters don't monologue for free. What do you want?"

"Honesty. How refreshing." The sovereign's gentle face turned fully toward them, and the light in its eyes steadied. "I want to make you an offer. But not yet—not tonight. Tonight I wanted you to *understand*. Understand what I am, what your valley is, what your friend's soul costs to hold. Tomorrow, when the thirteenth gate opens and the court gathers, you will choose. And I wanted your choice to be made with open eyes, because a choice made in ignorance isn't a choice at all. It's just a stumble."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we shall see what you are made of," the sovereign said pleasantly, "when I start eating it."

* * *

They were escorted back up—not to a cell, but to a guest chamber, which somehow was worse. Velvet hangings. A bed wide enough for three. A window of thick glass looking out over the courtiers' hall far below, where the bottled souls glowed like a city seen from a hill at night. A tray of food Cyrus couldn't identify and didn't touch.

The door closed. The locks, Cyrus noted, did not engage. Where would they run?

Bramble hopped from their shoulder to the windowsill and stood there, feathers puffed, staring out at the glow.

"Well," he said at last. "That went poorly."

"Bramble." Cyrus sat down heavily on the edge of the bed. The mattress swallowed them like a bog. "Third row. Between a hedge-witch's courage and a minstrel's joy. Your voice is on a shelf next to hers."

"I heard." The raven's voice was flat in a way Cyrus had never heard it. Forty years of sarcasm, and underneath it, this. "I'd hoped—I don't know what I'd hoped. That mine had been eaten outright. That there was nothing left to find." He turned his head, one black eye catching the lantern-light. "It whispers my name, apparently."

"It whispered hers. It'll whisper yours."

"Yes." Bramble ruffled his feathers, shook himself all over like a bird deciding the rain is done. "Well. When we get it out, I intend to have strong words with myself about the company I kept."

Cyrus laughed—one short, cracked, involuntary laugh—and then found they couldn't stop the rest of it, the shaking, the hot pressure behind the eyes. They put their face in their hands. "It ate my valley, Bramble. Before I was born. Everything quiet about home, everyone who can't hear the goats the way I can—that's not nature. That's digestion. I grew up in its stomach and called it the world."

"Mm." Bramble hopped closer along the sill. "And yet."

Cyrus lifted their head. "And yet what?"

"And yet here you sit, intact, unbitten, unbought, in the belly of the thing, having broken into its pantry and gotten away with it so far." The raven cocked his head. "It wants to make you an offer tomorrow. Sovereigns only offer when they're uncertain. It talked for an hour, Cy. Do you know why powerful things talk? Because they need you to agree. If it could simply take your gift, it would have taken it while you were unconscious in the corridor. Instead it showed you threads and gave you a history lesson." His eye gleamed. "Something about you makes taking difficult. And I have known you for weeks and can tell you exactly what it is."

"What?"

"You're stubborn as a mule with a grudge, you ask questions like a magpie collects shiny things, and you have never once in your life done what you were told when what you were told was wrong." Bramble preened his wing, insufferably calm. "It thinks it's shown you despair. It hasn't. It's shown you the size of the job."

Cyrus stared at him. Outside the glass, the soul-city glowed, thousands of small lights, thousands of whispers. Somewhere on a shelf, third row, Ilse was saying their name into the dark.

"It ate the valley," Cyrus said again, quieter now. Testing the weight of it.

"And the valley sent you," Bramble said. "A beast-speaker, whole and furious, carrying a dead woman's map. Either that's coincidence—and I've lived too long to believe in those—or the world has opinions." He hopped onto the bed, then onto Cyrus's knee, and looked up at them with terrible directness. "Tomorrow it offers you comfort. Safety. Your mother protected, your valley spared, everything you've ever wanted, in exchange for the only thing you've ever been. And you're going to say no. I know it the way I know weather coming. The question is what you do *after* the no. So sleep, if you can. Eat something, if you're braver than I am about mystery stew. And let that great stubborn head of yours work on the problem it's good at."

"Which is?"

"Finding the crack," Bramble said. "Everything has one. Even mountains."

He flew up to the curtain rod, tucked his head under his wing, and fell silent.

Cyrus lay back on the vast soft bed and did not sleep. They listened instead—to the mountain breathing around them, slow and vast, the hunger in it patient as stone. And beneath the fear, beneath the grief for a valley that had been wounded before they were born, something else was hardening. Small and bright and unreasonable.

*You showed me your threads,* Cyrus thought at the dark ceiling. *You showed me exactly how you're woven into every stone.*

*Big mistake, teaching the shepherd where the fence is weak.*

Somewhere deep below, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the thirteenth gate began, very slowly, to open.
