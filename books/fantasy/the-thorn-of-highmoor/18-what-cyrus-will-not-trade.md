## 18. What Cyrus Will Not Trade

They gave Cyrus time to think.

That was the first insult. The second was the chair.

It arrived on its own legs—a thing of pale rootwood that walked into the sovereign's hall, turned, and sat down beneath Cyrus as though it had known them all their life. It was, Cyrus had to admit, the most comfortable chair they had ever occupied, which somehow made it worse. Everything in the court beneath the mountain was comfortable. The light was comfortable, honeyed and shadowless, poured from a hundred bottled souls hung along the walls like lanterns at a festival. The air was warm. Somewhere, out of sight, water moved through channels cut in the living rock, and the sound of it was exactly the sound of the stream behind Maren's cottage.

The court had studied Cyrus. That was the truth of it. Every detail of this hall had been arranged to remind them of home.

*It wants me to feel like this is a place I could live,* Cyrus thought, and their hands tightened on the chair's arms. *That's how it hunts. Not with teeth. With comfort.*

Bramble was nowhere to be seen. They had taken him at the thirteenth gate—two courtiers with hands like folded paper, and Sable standing apart, her face unreadable, saying nothing as the raven cursed them with a fluency that would have made a dockworker blush. Cyrus had screamed his name until the echo came back wrong. Now the silence where his voice should be sat in Cyrus's chest like a swallowed stone.

And Odji. Odji, who had wept even as the alarm bells sang, who had mouthed *I'm sorry, I'm sorry* as the gates sealed—Odji had been dragged away too, toward whatever shelf held their sister's lantern. Cyrus did not know if they would see either of them again. They did not know, in the honest dark behind their ribs, whether they could forgive Odji even if they did.

The chair shifted beneath them, adjusting to their posture, and Cyrus realized with a lurch that it was listening. Everything here listened.

"Stop that," they said aloud, and the chair went very still, like a goat caught eating the washing.

The hall was empty except for the lanterns and the long table of black glass where the court's nobles had sat during the tasting ceremony, days ago now—or hours; time moved strangely under the mountain—and the great dais at the far end, where the sovereign would appear when it wished to appear. There were no doors that Cyrus could see. There had been a door when they'd been brought in. There was not one now. The wall had simply healed over it, smooth as the inside of a shell.

Cyrus counted the lanterns instead. It was something to do with their eyes that wasn't weeping. Forty-one. Forty-one little suns, and each one a person, or what was left of one. Some burned steady. Some flickered, and when they flickered, Cyrus's gift—the gift they had spent twenty-two years strangling—reached toward them without being asked, the way a hand reaches toward a crying child in the night.

*They're whispering,* Cyrus thought. *They've been whispering this whole time. I just couldn't hear it over my own fear.*

One lantern, third from the left, flickered in a rhythm that was almost speech. Almost a name. Cyrus leaned forward despite themselves, and the whisper resolved—not into words, but into a feeling, old and patient and unbearably sad, the feeling of a woman standing at a river ford in the rain.

"Ilse," Cyrus said, and their voice cracked on it. "I came. I'm sorry I'm late."

The lantern flared, once, like a held breath released.

And then the wall at the far end opened.

* * *

The sovereign did not walk so much as arrive, the way weather arrives. One moment the dais was empty; the next, the mountain itself had gathered into a shape at its center—a tall figure robed in what was not cloth but stone, granite flowing like wool, and where a face should have been there was a suggestion of one, the way a suggestion of a face appears in a cliff if you stare long enough. Its voice, when it spoke, came from everywhere: from the floor, from the lanterns, from the bones of Cyrus's own skull.

*The beast-speaker of Highmoor.* A pause, and Cyrus could have sworn the mountain was smiling. *You have caused us such interesting trouble.*

"I've been told that's my best quality," Cyrus said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Somewhere in the last day, fear had burned down to its coals, and the coals, it turned out, were mostly anger. "Where's the raven? Where's the sprite-seller? If you're going to eat us, I'd like to at least see them first."

*Nobody is eaten here.* The sovereign drifted nearer, and the temperature of the hall changed, warmer, coaxing. *We are not the monsters your valley's stories make of us. We are custodians. Keepers. Everything that comes beneath the mountain is preserved. Cherished, even.* It gestured with a hand of flowing stone toward the lanterns. *Would you prefer they were dead?*

Cyrus looked at the forty-one lights, and the whisper of Ilse's lantern, and said nothing, because the sovereign was right, and they both knew it, and that was the most horrifying thing about the place.

*You have questions,* the sovereign said. *Ask them. We have been waiting twenty-two years for this conversation. We are very good at waiting.*

"Then you can answer one honestly." Cyrus stood. The chair tried to keep them; they felt its rootwood fingers loosen, reluctantly, at a flick of the sovereign's attention. "The ward around Highmoor. The one that's been dying. The one that kept me deaf and dumb and small my whole life. That was you."

*That was us,* the sovereign agreed, without shame. *The valley was our first meal. A whole region's wild magic, drunk down to the dregs. It bought the court a century of strength.* It tilted its suggestion of a head. *We did not know, at the time, that we were drinking down a child. The ward hid you from us as thoroughly as it hid you from the world. When the courier woman came to bargain, we assumed she protected some minor talent. A hedge-witch. A charm-monger.*

*Imagine our surprise,* it said, and the lanterns all flickered at once, *when a beast-speaker walked out of that dead valley wearing a gift like a bonfire.*

"Imagine mine," Cyrus said, "finding out my whole childhood was a transaction."

*And yet.* The sovereign's voice went soft, and softness from a thing like that was worse than any roar. *And yet the transaction held. Twenty-two years of safety. A mother who loved you. Goats, I am told. A garden. You were hidden, and you were happy. We offer you the same bargain, Cyrus Nystrom, only better. We offer you the truth of it.*

The hall changed.

It happened between one breath and the next—the black table was gone, the dais was gone, and Cyrus stood in Maren's kitchen. Not a copy. *Maren's kitchen*, down to the chip in the third floorboard and the smell of bread and the bundle of dried lavender over the window, and Maren herself at the hearth, younger than Cyrus had ever known her, turning with a wooden spoon in her hand and a smile that broke open like morning.

"Sit down, love, you're letting the cold in," Maren said. "I've made the honey cakes you like."

"Maren's in Highmoor," Cyrus said, and their voice shook. "She doesn't make honey cakes. She burns them. Every time. She says the oven has a grudge."

*We can make her oven behave,* the sovereign said, from everywhere and nowhere, and the kitchen rippled like water. *We can make anything behave. Listen to what we offer.*

And it offered.

The kitchen dissolved into a procession of futures, each one more reasonable than the last. Cyrus saw themselves as the court's Beastmaster—robed in silver, a hall of their own, nobles bowing as they passed, their gift worn openly like a crown instead of buried like a crime. They saw Maren, old and warm by a fire that never went cold, protected by treaties written in the mountain's own stone, wanting for nothing. They saw the valley of Highmoor warded again—not dead this time, but *sleeping*, gently, so that no Warden of Seals would ever come asking questions. They saw safety. They saw belonging. They saw, and this was the hook that went deepest, a life where they never had to hide again and never had to fight, either—where the gift was simply *permitted*, the way rain is permitted, requiring nothing of them but quiet gratitude.

*All of this,* the sovereign said, *for one small thing.*

The futures folded away, and Cyrus stood again in the honeyed hall, and the sovereign's stone face leaned close, close enough that Cyrus could see the lantern-light swimming in the granite like fish under ice.

*Give us the gift. Willingly. Freely. Not torn—given. A willing surrender feeds us a thousand times more than a stolen soul. And in exchange, everything you have just seen. Everything you have ever wanted. We will even throw in the raven's freedom, and the sprite-seller's sister, as a gesture of good faith.*

The silence that followed was very long.

Cyrus thought about the chair that had tried to hold them. They thought about the kitchen with its corrected floorboard and its obedient oven. They thought about twenty-two years of pressing their hand flat against a goat's flank and feeling *nothing*, nothing, nothing, and smiling anyway, and going to bed grateful for a life that was a beautifully furnished room with no windows.

And they thought about last week. Crossing the boundary stone at night, magic flooding back like sound after deafness—the whole world suddenly shouting its name. The marsh-elk turning as one animal at their word. The fawn, dead in the reeds, because of that word. Bramble on their shoulder, complaining about the quality of roadkill. Odji's lantern, and the small trapped light inside it, and the question Odji had asked them to ask.

*Why do you stay?*

"I have a counter-offer," Cyrus said.

The sovereign waited. It was very good at waiting.

"You can keep your chairs and your kitchens and your futures in a box. You can keep the treaties and the silver robes." Cyrus's voice was rising now, and they let it. Twenty-two years of whispering, and here, at the bottom of the world, in the belly of the thing that had eaten their valley, they were finally going to use their whole voice. "Here's what I'll trade you. *Nothing.* I will trade you nothing. Not the gift. Not one hair of it. Not one *note* of it."

*You would die for it,* the sovereign said. It was not a question, and for the first time, there was something in its voice that was not patience. Something older. Hungrier. Almost—wounded.

"I would *live* for it," Cyrus said. "That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and you can't see it, because you've never once in your long grey life been given anything—you've only ever *taken*, and taken, and told yourselves it was keeping." They pointed at the lanterns, all forty-one, their arm steady. "You call that cherishing. I call it a pantry with good lighting."

*The woman Ilse gave her soul willingly,* the sovereign said. *To buy your childhood. Was that taking?*

The words landed like a knife between ribs, and the sovereign knew it, and Cyrus knew it knew it.

"No," Cyrus said quietly. "That was love. And you've been *digesting* it for twenty-two years, which is what you do with everything anyone ever gives you. But I've thought about Ilse every day since the ford. And do you know what I've figured out? She didn't give you her soul because your bargain was good. She gave it because she loved a stranger's baby more than she loved her own skin. The bargain was just the shape the love had to take." They stepped closer to the dais, close enough that the granite face loomed over them like weather. "So no. I won't give you my gift. Not because it's *mine*—though it is, it's the truest thing about me, and I spent half my life hating it and I'm done, *done*, with hating it. I won't give it because everything you build is made of things taken, and I have lived in a valley made of things hidden, and I am finished with both. I'd rather be true and hunted than safe and hollow. I'd rather be *Cyrus*."

The hall went still. Even the water in its channels seemed to hold its breath.

Then the sovereign laughed.

It was not a kind sound. It was the sound a mountain makes when it decides to become an avalanche—deep, and vast, and terribly amused, and the lanterns swung on their chains, and the whisper of Ilse's soul rose to a keening, and the sovereign said:

*Then you will be Cyrus, and you will be ours, and we will take what the courier's love bought us and what your stubbornness has delivered. You walked into the mountain of your own free will, beast-speaker. Everything beneath it belongs to us. Even your refusal—* the granite face split into something like a grin, *—especially your refusal. A willing soul, even a defiant one. What a feast you will make.*

"I was hoping you'd say something like that," said a dry voice from the shadows near the ceiling, "because I've been picking the lock on your lantern-rack for an hour, and honestly, the suspense was killing me."

Bramble dropped out of the dark like a stone with opinions, landed on Cyrus's shoulder, and in his beak—glowing, humming, *screaming* with borrowed light—was a soul-lantern.

And behind him, slipping through a crack in the sovereign's own dais that had not existed a moment ago, came Odji, wide-eyed, soot-streaked, and holding a smuggler's crowbar like a holy sword.

"Smuggler tunnels," Odji gasped. "The court doesn't watch its own foundations. Nobody ever looks *down* in a place that's already underground—"

The sovereign turned, and the whole mountain turned with it, and Cyrus felt the floor begin, very slowly, to wake up.

* * *

Later—though "later" would prove to be a matter of minutes—Cyrus would remember that the sovereign's first mistake was not the bargain. The bargain had been clever. The sovereign's first mistake was bringing Cyrus into the vault-adjacent hall at all, within reaching distance of forty-one whispering souls, and letting a beast-speaker stand among them long enough to *listen*.

Because Cyrus was listening now. Truly listening, for the first time without fear, without flinching, with their whole gift open like a door flung wide.

And the souls in the lanterns were not whispering in fear.

They were whispering in *anger*. Forty-one voices, and beneath them, deeper in the mountain, hundreds more—every kept voice in the vault of Kept Voices, every stolen soul in the court's pantry—and they had all been waiting, some of them for centuries, for one thing.

Someone to speak *for* them.

"Bramble," Cyrus said, and their voice rang off the stone like a bell, "how many lanterns are in the vault?"

"Last I counted, before they exiled me for having better taste in jewelry than the Lord of Seals?" Bramble's feathers were standing straight up. "Three hundred and six. Why?"

Cyrus looked up at the sovereign's stone grin, and felt something in their chest that was not fear, and was not anger, and was not despair.

It was the feeling of a door opening.

"Because," Cyrus said, "I've changed my mind about the trade. I *will* give you something, sovereign. I'm going to give you back every single thing you've ever stolen."

And the mountain, for the first time in a thousand years, felt afraid.
