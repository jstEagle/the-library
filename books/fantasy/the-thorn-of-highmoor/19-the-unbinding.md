## 19. The Unbinding

The sovereign's grin did not waver, but the hall did.

It was a small thing at first—a tremor in the bottled light, the way a candle flame leans when someone walks past. Then the shelves of the vault, far below and yet somehow also all around them, shivered. Cyrus heard it through their boots, through their teeth, through the gift that roared in them now like a river un-dammed: the sound of three hundred and six souls noticing, all at once, that someone had come for them.

"You are one speaker," the mountain said. Its voice came from everywhere—the walls, the floor, the chandelier of caged moths that had once been something else entirely. "One frightened child wearing borrowed clothes. What will you do? *Whisper* at me?"

"Actually," said Bramble from Cyrus's shoulder, "they're going to break your shelves. I'd duck if I were you. Oh wait—you're a mountain. My condolences."

Cyrus did not answer either of them. They were listening.

That was the thing about the gift that no one in Highmoor had ever been able to teach them, because there was no one to teach: it was never about commanding. The marsh-elk had not charged Sable because Cyrus ordered them to. They had charged because Cyrus had asked, honestly, and the asking had been true. Every creature they had ever spoken to had answered the same question underneath all the others: *Will you help me?*

So Cyrus knelt down on the cold floor of the sovereign's hall—knelt, in front of the thing wearing the mountain like a coat—and pressed both palms flat against the stone.

And spoke to the vault.

*Hello,* they said, the way they'd say it to a spooked ewe, to a fawn caught in fencing wire, to Maren's old dog the winter his hips went bad. *Hello. I know you're frightened. I know some of you have been here so long you've forgotten what frightened was before it became everything. But listen—I need you to listen.*

The whispers rose. Hundreds of them, thousands, the soft rustle of souls pressed shoulder to shoulder in glass and iron, and beneath the rustle Cyrus felt the court's attention swing toward them like a lighthouse beam.

*There's a door,* Cyrus said. *It's me. I'm the door. And I am asking—not ordering, asking—will you help me open it?*

For a heartbeat, nothing. The sovereign began to laugh, a sound like an avalanche deciding to take its time.

Then a single lantern on the lowest shelf of the world flared white.

And every soul in the mountain screamed *yes.*

* * *

Warden Sable had sealed the thirteenth gate with her own hands eleven years ago, and she had felt nothing, because feeling was the first thing the court had taken and the last thing it returned.

She felt something now.

It started in her palms—in the seals themselves, the sigils burned into her skin the day she traded her gift away. They had been silent for thirty years. Dumb ink, dead weight, the receipt for everything she'd been. Now they were humming. Now they were *waking*, and the waking felt like the moment before tears, that ache in the throat you get when a song finds something you buried.

Down in the vault, a beast-speaker was doing the impossible. Doing the thing Sable had been told for three decades was impossible—that the vault was sealed with sovereign-work, that no power in or under the world could crack it, that resistance was arithmetic and the arithmetic always favored the house.

*Sealed with sovereign-work.* Yes. Sealed with the sovereign's seals.

Her seals. Given to her, woven into her, hers to command.

Sable stood at the mouth of the descending stair with her hand on the wall and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had spent thirty years being a lock and calling herself a key.

"Stop them," rasped the Lord of Seals behind her—or whatever wore him now; half the courtiers in the hall had stopped blinking an hour ago. "Warden. That is an order."

"They usually are," Sable said.

She drew a breath that hurt going in. And then Warden Sable, terror of three kingdoms, breaker of the fen-rebellion, hound of the court beneath the mountain, turned to face the court she served—and pulled.

She did not pull against the vault. She pulled against herself. Against thirty years of seals, of silence, of the careful deadness she had mistaken for peace. It tore coming out. Of course it tore. Everything honest does.

The sigils on her hands blazed—and then, obedient at last to the woman who carried them instead of the mountain that made them, they *reversed*. Every seal she had ever set on a gate, a shelf, a soul-lantern, a person, lit up along its whole history and let go.

Above her, the thirteenth gate groaned open. Below her, three hundred and six locks clicked as one. And somewhere in the middle of it all, Sable heard her own voice—her real voice, the one she'd sold—come back to her mid-scream, and discovered she had forgotten how much she had needed to scream.

* * *

The vault erupted.

Not broke—*erupted*, because three hundred years of kept souls do not leave quietly. They left the way water leaves a shattered dam, the way birds leave a field before a storm, the way song leaves a throat after too long held shut. Light poured off the shelves in rivers. The whispering became voices, the voices became names, the names became a sound that was not grief and not joy but the place where they meet, and Cyrus stood in the center of it with their arms out and their eyes streaming and laughed until it hurt.

Bramble's lantern burst like an egg.

The voice that came out hit the raven mid-flight—he had insisted on being present for his own liberation, "for dramatic purposes"—and he tumbled out of the air, righted himself, and hovered there, opening and closing his beak.

"Bramble?" Cyrus said.

"I—" The raven landed on a broken shelf, staggering. "*I can hear myself.* Do you know how long—three hundred years of talking and never once hearing—" His voice cracked, sardonic and rough and entirely, unmistakably his own. "Well. That's ruined my air of mystery forever."

"Your air of mystery was ruined in chapter five."

"Cruelty," Bramble said, wiping his eye with a wing, which is not something ravens can do, and did anyway. "From the person who freed me. There's gratitude for you."

A second lantern cracked near Cyrus's feet—smaller, older, its glass fogged with long keeping. Out of it rose a light shaped vaguely like a young woman, flickering, uncertain, blinking at the chaos as though waking from a nap she hadn't meant to take.

"Odji," she said. "Where's Odji?"

"Looking for you," Cyrus said. "For months. They sold us all out to the court to keep this lantern safe—they're probably being arrested right now—but every step of it was toward you. Go find them. Third corridor up, past the moth-chandelier. And tell them—" Cyrus hesitated. "Tell them the debt's paid."

The soul looked at Cyrus for a long moment, and then did something souls apparently could still do: smiled.

* * *

The mountain convulsed.

There was no better word. The sovereign had worn this peak like a coat for a thousand years, and now the coat was trying to stand up without its body. Stone screamed in a register only the gift could hear. The glittering halls split along seams no one had known were there. Courtiers scattered like beetles when a log is rolled, shedding their beauty in clumps, revealing underneath the gray, hungry, hollow things the court had spent centuries upholstering.

The Lord of Seals—what remained of him—lunged at Sable with a knife made of contracts. She broke his wrist, took the knife, and did not use it. "You're under seal," she told him, and pressed her palm to his chest, and he collapsed into a small, exhausted heap of man. "Someone should've done that to me thirty years ago. Consider us even."

Cyrus ran. Not away—toward. Because through the roaring and the cracking, through the avalanche-voice of a sovereign discovering fear, Cyrus could hear something else underneath: a small, persistent sound like weeping, coming from the deepest dark of all, where the first stolen things were kept.

The oldest shelf. The first bite.

And on it, dim as an ember under ash, a ward. A valley-shaped ward. A ward that hummed a boundary-stone hum that Cyrus had known since childhood, that they had fallen asleep beside, that had erased them gently for twenty-two years—

"Oh," Cyrus breathed. "Oh, it's *you*."

The valley's magic. Highmoor's deadness. The sovereign's first theft, bottled and shelved and fed upon for a thousand years, and it sounded—to Cyrus's gift, to the part of them that had grown up inside its silence—like home.

They wrapped both hands around the lantern of the ward, and the ward, recognizing something it had been shaped around, recognized them back.

"CYRUS NYSTROM." The sovereign's voice split the dark. The walls peeled back and there it was: vast, furious, diminished, a thousand-year appetite suddenly confronted with an empty plate. "YOU WILL NOT LEAVE THIS MOUNTAIN. NOTHING LEAVES. THE HUNGER DOES NOT END. IT ONLY WAITS."

"Yeah," Cyrus said. "I've heard. Ilse waited thirteen turns. You waited a thousand. Waiting's the family business." They lifted the lantern. "But here's the difference between us. I don't eat what I love."

Behind them, limping, scorched, grinning like a woman reborn, Sable raised her hands—and every reversed seal in the mountain rose with them, a constellation of letting-go. Bramble wheeled overhead, screaming in a voice three hundred years hungry. And far above, faint but rising, Cyrus heard the souls pouring free into the night sky over the mountain like a river finding the sea.

The sovereign lunged.

Cyrus did not run.

They brought the lantern of the valley's ward down against the vault floor—the floor that was the sovereign, the floor that was the hunger itself—and shattered it.

The mountain convulsed one final time. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Cyrus reached into the flood of returning magic with everything they were—every hidden year, every wordless conversation with goats, every whispered kindness to every creature that had ever leaned into their hands—and did not push the ward back out of the mountain.

They *anchored* it. Wove it through the stone, through the hunger, through the ancient gnawing dark, the way you gentle a snare into a fence, a cage into a shelter. *You will not devour,* they told the mountain, and the mountain, filled at last to bursting with a thousand years of stolen light, had no room left to argue. *You will hold. You will keep nothing. You will be a keeper instead.*

The sovereign's roar faded into a rumble. The rumble faded into something almost like breathing.

When Cyrus opened their eyes, they were kneeling in a quiet hall of ordinary stone. No chandeliers. no glitter. Just moonlight, falling through a crack in the ceiling that hadn't existed before, and dust motes drifting through it like slow silver fish.

"Well," said Bramble, landing on their shoulder. His voice was steady now, settled into itself like a bird folding wings. "You've gone and done it. You've turned the world's oldest predator into a very large guard dog."

"It seemed fair," Cyrus said. Their hands were shaking. They found they were laughing anyway, wet and helpless. "It seemed *fair*, Bramble. Nobody eats anybody. Everybody gets kept."

"Sentimental," said Bramble. He pressed his head briefly, warmly, against Cyrus's cheek. "Don't ever change."

Sable stood in the doorway, gray-faced, her hands bare of sigils for the first time in thirty years, looking at them like a woman reading a letter she'd sent to herself decades ago and only just received.

"The valley," she said quietly. "When the ward moved—it didn't die, did it. It changed shape."

"No," Cyrus said. "It learned."

Sable nodded slowly. Then, awkwardly, like someone remembering how: she bowed.

Far up the tunnel, footsteps—two sets, stumbling, urgent, one of them crying. Odji appeared in the moonlight with a flickering, laughing light clasped against their chest, and behind them, silhouetted against the broken gates, souls were still rising out of the mountain into the thirteenth-moon sky, hundreds of them, a slow bright migration.

Cyrus watched them go, and felt the anchor in their chest hum—steady, shared, alive.

"Maren's going to kill me," they said.

"Maren's going to *hug* you," said Bramble, "and then kill you. In that order. We should start walking."

Cyrus looked up at the crack of moonlight, at the souls streaming free, at the road home they could finally see the end of.

"Yes," they said. "Let's go home."
