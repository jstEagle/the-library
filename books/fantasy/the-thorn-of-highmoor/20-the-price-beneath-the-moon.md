## 20. The Price Beneath the Moon

The mountain was screaming.

Not with stone and thunder—Cyrus had heard avalanches before, distant white roars rolling down the high shoulders of the world. This was different. This was the sound of a thing that had spent centuries eating and had finally been made to spit it all back up.

They stood at the edge of the shattered vault, Ilse's soul-lantern cradled against their chest like a sleeping bird. It was warm now. Warm the way a hand is warm after you've held it too long in the cold, and inside the glass the pale light that had been Ilse Vey turned slowly, like someone stretching after a long sleep. Around them, hundreds of other lanterns lay broken or emptying, their whispers spilling out into the dark in a hundred borrowed voices—a child laughing, an old man humming, a woman saying *I never told anyone this* over and over, gentle as rain.

Bramble perched on Cyrus's shoulder, feathers singed, one eye swollen half-shut from flying through falling masonry.

"Well," he rasped, in his own voice now, his real voice, which turned out to be lower and rougher than his stolen one, with a burr in it like gravel in honey. "You've done something catastrophically irreversible. I want you to know I'm very proud of you and also we should run."

The floor shuddered. Somewhere far above, whole galleries of the court were peeling away like bark off a burning log. The beautiful hollow courtiers had fled screaming down corridors that were no longer there when they reached them, and the bottled light that had lit everything—that soft, terrible glow of kept souls—was guttering out hall by hall.

And beneath it all, under the floor, under the roots of the mountain itself, something enormous turned over and opened its eyes.

*Cyrus Nystrom.*

The voice did not come through the ears. It came up through the soles of their boots, through their bones, through the old scar-tissue of every secret they'd ever kept. It was the voice they'd felt humming through the boundary stone their whole life, except now they understood: the ward over Highmoor hadn't been a wall. It had been a *bite mark*. The court's first meal, swallowed whole, a valley's worth of magic chewed and swallowed and still being digested twenty-two years later.

"You freed what is mine," the mountain said. "You will give it back. Or I will take the valley instead. Every living thing that breathes beneath that stone. I am patient. I have eaten patience."

Cyrus looked down at Ilse's lantern.

The light inside pulsed once, twice—the rhythm of a heart remembering how to beat. Twenty-two years ago this woman had walked to the edge of a valley she'd never seen and sold the only thing she owned so that a stranger's child could grow up unremarked and unmarked, herding goats, arguing with Maren about salt, laughing at nothing by the river. She'd bought a life with hers and never once asked for thanks.

*You're the one I paid for,* she'd said. And then, softer, with the last warmth left in her: *Make it worth it.*

"I'm not giving anything back," Cyrus said aloud. Their voice cracked on the second word, and they let it crack. "But I'm not letting you eat Highmoor either."

Bramble's head swiveled toward them. "Cy. What are you doing."

"Thinking."

"That has historically gone badly for everyone within three miles."

"Hush." Cyrus knelt, set the lantern gently on the broken shelf, and put both palms flat against the trembling stone. The mountain's hunger rose up through the rock like heat from a forge—not rage, exactly. Rage would almost have been better. This was appetite, vast and impersonal, the hunger of a thing that had been built, long ago, to hold magic the way a granary holds grain, and which had learned somewhere along the way to *eat* it instead. To keep. To own. To taste.

Every soul in this vault had been food. Every gift Sable had ever sealed, every sprite in Odji's lanterns, every stolen voice—including Bramble's, sitting warm and returned on Cyrus's shoulder—all of it had gone down the same throat.

And the throat was empty now, and the thing around it was dying, and a dying thing with an appetite does not care whose children it swallows.

"There's another way," Cyrus said. "There has to be. You were built to be a keeper. That's what you're *for*. Someone just taught you to steal instead."

The mountain laughed, and the laughter brought dust down from the ceiling in grey sheets. *Keeper. I keep nothing. I am the keeping.*

"No," Cyrus said. "You're the *vault*. There's a difference. A vault holds things without eating them." They thought of Maren's kitchen, the smell of bread, the way Maren had hidden them for twenty-two years—love expressed as concealment, and how wrong it had turned out to be, and how much love had been in it anyway. "My mother kept me my whole life by hiding me. It nearly broke us both. Hiding isn't the same as holding. Holding means the thing stays alive inside you."

The hunger paused. Not convinced. But pausing—the way a starving animal pauses when something unexpected moves at the edge of its vision.

Bramble hopped down onto Cyrus's knee. His good eye was bright and sharp and, for once, entirely serious.

"Cyrus," he said quietly, and it was strange to hear him say the name plainly, without performance, "whatever shape this idea of yours is taking, I can see it forming behind your face like weather, and I need you to hear me first. I spent four hundred years as a whisper in glass because someone thought a voice was a small enough thing to take. Don't hand this thing a bigger piece of yourself than you can walk out with."

"It won't be a piece," Cyrus said. Their heart was pounding so hard they could feel it in their teeth. "That's the whole point."

*Speak your offer,* the mountain said, and the impatience in it was the impatience of a wound.

Cyrus closed their eyes.

Here was the truth they'd carried out of Highmoor and across fens and markets and smuggler-dark tunnels: the gift wasn't a possession. It had never been a possession. When they spoke to the goats, they didn't send words out—they opened a door, and something walked through both ways. When they'd called the marsh-elk against the Warden, they hadn't commanded; they'd *asked*, desperately, terribly, and the elk had answered, and a fawn had died, and the guilt of that was theirs forever because the door had been theirs to open.

A gift like that couldn't be taken. Only given. And it couldn't be given away, either—not truly—because it lived in the space between, in the opening, in the *listening*.

So they wouldn't surrender it. They would plant it.

"I'll anchor it here," Cyrus said. "In you. My gift—the whole of it, the listening and the speaking, the door itself—I'll bind it into your deepest stone. Not as food. As root. You'll stop eating magic because you'll stop needing to—you'll *grow* it. Every beast-speaker born east of the sea will feel you the way I felt the boundary stone, and if they come to you, you'll hold them the way a tree holds a nest. Not a vault. A hearth. And the valley—" their voice caught, steadied— "the valley's ward comes back too. Living this time. Mine. Fed from the inside instead of bitten off from the outside. No more forgetting. Anyone who hides there will hide *with* the mountain, not from it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears.

Then, softly, Bramble: "And what happens to *you*, Cy? In this pretty picture?"

Cyrus opened their eyes. The vault was dimmer now, most of the lantern-light gone home to wherever freed souls go, and the dark coming up through the floor was the dark of a huge creature deciding.

"They keep the gift," Cyrus said. "That's the bargain. Anchored doesn't mean amputated. I stay the door. But the door lives here now. I can't leave the mountain's reach—ever—or the binding unravels and we're all back where we started, except hungrier." They managed a lopsided smile. "So I guess I'm becoming a very well-traveled goat-herd with an extremely short commute."

Bramble stared at them. Then he did something he had never done in all the miles between the ford and this place: he pressed his forehead against Cyrus's cheek, feathers and bone, brief and fierce.

"You absolute fool," he said thickly. "You found the one trade this rotten place couldn't twist. It wants souls and gifts and *pieces* of people. So you offered it a *home*." He pulled back, sniffed wetly—an affectation, since ravens don't cry, but Cyrus let him have it. "Ilse would have liked you enormously. She'd have said something insufferably kind about it."

*It is not enough,* the mountain rumbled—but the rumble had changed key. Hunger again, yes, but underneath it, faint as a memory of music, something that might have been longing. *I was made to hold. I remember being made. I do not remember how.*

"I know," Cyrus said gently. "Nobody teaches keepers anymore. They only teach takers. That's why I'm not offering you a lesson." They stood, knees aching, and looked down into the dark seam where the floor had split, where the oldest stone of the mountain showed through like a heart through a ribcage. "I'm offering you practice."

They stepped off the edge.

Falling through that dark was nothing like falling—it was like being *remembered*. The mountain took them the way soil takes a seed, and Cyrus sank past galleries and drowned halls and the cold places where even the courtiers' ghosts feared to go, down to the first stone laid when the world was young, and there they stopped, and breathed, and began.

Binding a gift into a mountain is not like driving a nail. It is like teaching a giant to whistle. Cyrus started small—with the feeling of dawn in a goat pen, the warm animal drowse of a hundred bodies shifting in straw. They gave the mountain the memory of Bramble's first joke, and the taste of Maren's bread, and the exact sound the ford makes at midnight when the moon is thin. They gave it the marsh-elk, and apologized again to the fawn while they did it, out loud, into the stone, because some debts are paid in grief and should be. And the mountain, that vast starving engine, learned—slowly, clumsily, the way a rescued animal learns hands—to hold each thing without swallowing. To let the memory exist inside it and remain *itself*, alive, unconsumed.

It stumbled. Twice it clenched too hard and Cyrus gasped as something precious nearly crushed—and twice they said, patiently, *no, gentler, like this,* and showed it again, the way Maren had once shown a frightened child how to hold a newborn kid without squeezing.

By the time the last knot tied itself, Cyrus was no longer sure where their heartbeat ended and the mountain's slow stone-pulse began, and that was the point, and it was wonderful, and it was terrifying, and they had chosen it with both eyes open, which made all the difference in the world.

Above, far above, the shaking stopped.

When Cyrus climbed back up out of the seam—walking now, not sinking, the mountain lifting them gently as a mother lifts a child onto a wall—the vault was quiet. Whole. The broken shelves had knit themselves into something new, something less like a prison and more like terraced garden beds, and in the place where Ilse's lantern had rested, the lantern itself had opened like a flower, empty and shining, its occupant long since gone wherever freed things go.

Bramble sat waiting on the top shelf, and beside him, looking rumpled and furious and alive, stood a figure Cyrus knew from a thousand campfire descriptions: broad, dark-skinned, arms crossed, one hand still curled protectively around a small lantern of her own.

Odji's sister. Freed. And Odji himself bursting up the stairwell behind her, taking the steps three at a time, shouting her name in a voice that cracked clean in half.

Cyrus leaned against the new-grown wall of the vault—*their* wall now, in a way no wall had ever been theirs—and watched the reunion, and felt the mountain feel it too, curious and careful, learning what joy was like to hold.

"So," Bramble said, sidling over. "Home, then. Back to the valley. Goats. Bread. Your terrifying adoptive mother, who is going to kill you, resurrect you, and kill you again."

"The mountain's reach goes all the way to the boundary stone," Cyrus said. "Past it, actually. I checked on the way up." They smiled, and the smile ached, because it was the first smile in days that cost nothing. "I can go home, Bramble. All the way home."

"Mm." He ruffled his scorched feathers. "And when the Warden asks what became of her seals—since you appear to have absorbed the entire legal system of the underworld—what exactly are you planning to tell her?"

Cyrus opened their mouth to answer—

—and felt, through forty miles of living stone, a knock. Faint. Familiar. Two hooves and a stubborn heart, standing at the edge of the valley where the old ward used to hum, stamping impatiently at the ground.

Maren's lead goat had come looking for them.

And it was not alone. Behind it, small and scattered and unmistakable, came the shy press of other minds—wild ones, curious ones, drawn from every direction toward the mountain that had begun, at last, to listen.

"*Well*," said Bramble, eyeing the horizon. "I hope you weren't planning on a quiet homecoming."
