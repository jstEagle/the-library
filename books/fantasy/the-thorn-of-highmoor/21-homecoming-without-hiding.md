## 21. Homecoming Without Hiding

The valley of Highmoor looked smaller from above.

Cyrus had never seen it from above before—the ridge path along the eastern shoulder was a thing they'd only ever heard about from shepherds who claimed to have walked it in their grandfathers' day. Now they stood on it with the wind pulling at their hair and the whole of their childhood spread out below them: the grey-green quilt of pasture, the thread of the river catching morning light at the ford, the crooked chimney-smoke of home rising like a question mark against the pale sky.

Beside them, Bramble shifted on their shoulder, feathers ruffling.

"Smaller than you remember," the raven observed.

"It's exactly as big as I remember."

"No, no. I've been watching your face. You keep waiting for it to be more." He tilted his head, one bright eye fixed on them. "That's what hiding does. It shrinks things. Twenty-two years of quiet makes even a valley feel like a pocket."

Cyrus didn't answer right away. Below, a flock of sheep had gathered near the ford, and even at this distance—especially at this distance—they could hear them. That was the strangest part of coming home. The ward that had pressed down on Highmoor for longer than anyone living could remember was gone, unmade when the mountain convulsed and Sable turned her seals inside out. And where the silence had been, everything poured in: sheep muttering their dull contentment, a kestrel screaming mathematics at the hillside, the river itself murmuring its endless one-word conversation with the stones.

And beneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat felt through a floor, the mountain.

It wasn't gone either. Cyrus had made sure of that—or made something new of it. Their gift, which had spent a lifetime coiled small and secret in their chest, now ran down through the rock like roots through soil. The court's hunger was still there, deep below, but it was held now, cradled, spoken to every hour of every day by a voice it couldn't devour because the voice was everywhere and nowhere and belonged to the stone itself. A predator turned keeper. A leash woven out of listening.

Some mornings Cyrus woke and could not tell whether they were lying in their own bed or standing at the center of the world.

*Both,* the mountain would murmur, in that vast slow way it had learned from them. *Both is good.*

"You're doing the thing again," Bramble said.

"What thing?"

"The thousand-yard stare with the tiny smile. Very mystical. Very annoying for anyone standing next to you." He hopped closer along their shoulder and tugged gently at a strand of their hair with his beak. "Come on. Your mother's been watching the road since dawn. I could hear her kettle having an argument with itself from three ridges back."

Cyrus laughed—a real laugh, loose and easy in a way that still surprised them sometimes—and started down.

* * *

Maren was in the garden when they came up the lane, though anyone could see she'd been pretending otherwise. She had a basket on her arm and was inspecting the cabbages with the ferocious concentration of a woman who has checked the road eleven times already and refuses to check it a twelfth while someone might be looking.

She looked up anyway. Of course she did.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The wind came down the valley carrying the smell of rain on far hills, and somewhere behind the house a goat complained to no one in particular, and Maren Nystrom stood among her cabbages with her hands slowly tightening on the basket handle until her knuckles went white.

"You're thinner," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're thinner and there's ash in your hair and—" Her voice caught. She set the basket down on the ground very carefully, the way people do when they're afraid that if they don't occupy their hands with something gentle they'll use them for shaking instead. "There's ash in your hair, Cyrus."

"There was a fire."

"I know there was a fire. Half the valley saw the mountain breathe smoke for three days and everyone's been telling each other stories, and none of them are true, because nobody knows anything, because I never told them anything, because—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes for a moment. When she lowered it, her face had settled into something steadier, though her eyes hadn't. "Come inside. There's bread. We'll talk."

"We'll talk," Cyrus agreed quietly.

"And your bird can come too, if he must."

"She must," said Bramble, sweeping down from Cyrus's shoulder to land on the fence post with theatrical dignity. "And thank you. 'Must' is correct. I am a necessity."

"He stayed in Vell's Crossing for six years," Cyrus said, following their mother toward the door. "You get used to him."

"One does not get used to him," Maren said. "One endures him. There's a difference, and it matters."

Inside, the house smelled the way it always had—woodsmoke and dried herbs and the particular warm dustiness of a place where generations have slept. Cyrus stood in the middle of the kitchen and felt something in their chest crack open in the good way, the painful way, the way a fist unclenches after being clenched so long it forgot how to be anything else.

Maren put the kettle on. Cut bread. Set out butter and the blackberry jam she only made when something needed forgiving or celebrating, and Cyrus thought: *perhaps both.* They sat across from each other at the table like two people meeting after a long voyage, which, in every way that counted, they were.

"I got your letter," Cyrus said.

"I know."

"You burned Ilse's letter without reading it. But you wrote me one."

Maren's hands went still around the teapot. "Yes."

"Why?"

The question hung there, and Maren looked at it the way she might look at a difficult birth, or a storm coming over the ridge—something that had to be faced whether or not she was ready. She poured the tea. Sat down. Wrapped both hands around her cup as though the autumn air had gotten into the kitchen just for her.

"Because I owed her silence," she said, "and I owed you the truth, and I spent twenty-two years convincing myself those were the same debt." She took a breath. "They weren't. Sit still, Cyrus. This will take the length of a pot of tea, and I'd like to get through it without you interrupting, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve, and I've lost it twice already this week."

So Cyrus sat still, and Maren told them.

She told it plainly, the way she did everything—with a farmer's economy, no embellishment, the facts laid out like seed potatoes in rows. How she had found the infant at the boundary stone twenty-two years ago, wrapped in wool that was soaked through with rain and something darker. How a woman had been standing there in the storm, marked all over with sigils that glowed even through the ward's dead air, sigils Maren had never seen before and never wanted to see again. How the woman had said, *I bought this child's life. All I ask is that no one here ever asks why.* How she'd left a purse of coin that should have lasted ten years and lasted thirty, because somehow it never emptied until the night it needed to.

"She said the child was marked," Maren said. "Not with sigils—those were hers, her payment, her scars—but marked some other way. She wouldn't say how. She said if the wrong people ever learned of it, no wall in the world would be thick enough, and the only safe place in creation was a valley built to forget." Her thumb moved along the rim of her cup, around and around. "I didn't understand half of it. I understood enough. Someone powerful wanted a baby dead or kept, and either way, my job was the keeping. So I kept."

"You could have told me."

"When? At seven, so you could carry it like a stone through your whole childhood? At twelve, when you cried because the goats loved you more than other children loved you and you couldn't say why?" Maren shook her head. "Every year I waited, it got harder to say and easier to justify not saying. That's how secrets work, Cyrus. They don't sit still. They grow roots. By the time you were grown, telling you felt like tearing out a tree." She met their eyes. "I'm not asking you to say it was kind. It wasn't kind. It was love, but it wasn't kind, and I've finally learned those aren't always the same animal."

Outside, Bramble had gone conspicuously silent on the windowsill, which for Bramble was the equivalent of removing his hat.

Cyrus looked down at their tea. The anger they'd carried down the mountain—that hot, bright coal they'd nursed through every mile of the homeward road—was still there. They checked. It was. But it had cooled into something else, something with edges they could hold without burning.

"You let me think I was alone in the world," they said. "Twenty-two years. You watched me hide from *you*, Mom. You watched me flinch whenever you asked about the goats, and lie about the dreams I had, and you knew. You knew why."

"Yes."

"That's the part that hurts. Not the bargain. The watching."

Maren nodded slowly, and to her credit she didn't reach for excuses. "I know," she said simply. "I'd have hurt worse, if it were me. I know that too." She pushed the jam across the table. "I'm not going to ask for forgiveness today. I'm going to ask for Tuesdays. Come for supper on Tuesdays. Let the forgiveness take as long as it takes."

Cyrus was quiet for a moment. Then, despite everything—or perhaps because of everything—they felt the corner of their mouth move.

"Tuesdays," they said. "And you have to meet Odji properly. Last time you fed them stew and interrogated them about their intentions like they were courting me."

"Was I wrong?"

"Mom."

"They live in my barn, Cyrus. My barn. I have a right to know their intentions regarding my barn."

From the window, Bramble said, "For the record, they intend to reorganize your tool shed, and frankly it needs it."

* * *

Odji arrived at dusk, as promised, leading two pack-mules and wearing the expression of a person who has recently discovered that freedom smells like hay. They'd been in the barn four days and had already, according to Maren, "moved everything twice and put nothing back where it lived."

They came into the kitchen trailing the smell of road dust and lamp oil, took one look at the table—at Maren's red-rimmed eyes, at Cyrus's face—and said, "Ah. Is this a moment? Should I be elsewhere?"

"Sit down," said Maren, in a tone that permitted no elsewhere. "Have bread."

"This is how it starts," Odji murmured to Cyrus as they sat. "First it's bread. Then it's questions about the barn. Then suddenly you have a mother. I've seen it happen."

"How would you know? You've never had one."

"Exactly. I've studied the phenomenon closely. From a distance. With envy." They accepted the bread with both hands, suddenly and uncharacteristically sincere. "Is it done? The talking?"

"It's done," Cyrus said. "Or begun. Both is good."

Odji smiled at that—their own words, given back—and for a while the four of them ate in the comfortable clatter of a kitchen finding its evening rhythm. Maren asked Odji about their sister, whose lantern had come out of the shattered vault blinking and furious and now resided in a sunny window box in Vell's Crossing, refusing to discuss the experience. Odji asked Maren whether the south field really flooded every spring or whether that was a story she told to lower expectations. Bramble demanded and received cheese.

It was ordinary. That was the miracle of it. Cyrus sat in their mother's kitchen with a soul-broker-turned-friend and a thieving exile of the mountain court, and it was *ordinary*, and nobody hid, and the goat stuck her head through the door demanding her share, and Maren said "not tonight, Wrenna, you've had your supper," to a goat, in front of company, without embarrassment.

Afterward, Cyrus walked out to the boundary stone.

It stood where it had always stood at the valley's edge, but it was different now—dormant, its hum gone, just weathered granite with old carvings worn to suggestions. Cyrus pressed a palm against it out of habit. Nothing. Just cold stone and the memory of fear.

But underneath the ground, under the whole valley, the mountain breathed. And through the mountain, Cyrus felt the far edges of their gift stretched wide—not suppressed anymore, not hidden, but *distributed*, threaded through miles of rock and root like a second nervous system for the land. Every creature in Highmoor was audible to them if they listened. And every creature in Highmoor, they realized, could feel them too: a presence in the ground, warm and watchful, that the sheep had stopped fearing and the kestrels had started greeting.

The valley wasn't magic-dead anymore. It was magic-*rooted*. There was a difference, and it mattered.

"You're doing the thing again."

Bramble landed on the stone beside their hand, folding his wings.

"I'm allowed to do the thing. I earned the thing."

"Mm. Well, while you were communing, I flew the perimeter. Thought you'd want to know: travelers on the north road. Three days out, maybe four. A peddler, a family with too many children, and someone else." His feathers lifted slightly. "Someone with a staff. And the staff has a heartbeat."

Cyrus went still. "A staff with a heartbeat."

"Or the person holding it does, faintly. Either way—it's not a peddler, Cyrus. And word travels. You broke a mountain open and freed forty souls; the wilds noticed." Bramble cocked his head, and his voice gentled into something almost kind. "Your valley isn't a pocket anymore. Things are going to find it. Some of them will be wonderful. One or two may be trouble."

Behind them, up the darkening lane, lamplight spilled from the kitchen door where Maren stood watching, and Odji's laugh drifted out on the cooling air, and Wrenna the goat complained about the injustice of suppers ending.

Cyrus looked at the road, and the mountains beyond it, and felt—instead of the old dread—a strange, steady readiness, like a hand opening.

"Well," they said. "Then we'd better make room."
