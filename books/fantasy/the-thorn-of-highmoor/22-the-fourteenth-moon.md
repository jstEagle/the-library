## 22. The Fourteenth Moon

The morning the first strangers came to Highmoor, Cyrus was arguing with a goat.

"You cannot eat the washing," they said, with the particular patience of someone who had said this many times. "I know it's wool. I know you think that makes it yours by right of kinship. It doesn't."

Petunia, who was the worst of the eleven goats and knew it, regarded them with flat yellow contempt and began, slowly and deliberately, to chew on Maren's second-best shawl.

Cyrus sighed and let her have it. Some battles were not worth the dignity of winning.

It was the fourteenth moon since Ilse had died at the ford—fourteen turns since a stranger's blood had soaked into Cyrus's hands and everything in their life had changed shape. The valley had noticed. That was the strange part, the part Cyrus still hadn't gotten used to: the valley *noticed* things now. The ward at the boundary stone had gone from a dead hum to something alive and low and warm, like a sleeping animal's breathing. The goats were chattier. The river told them when the trout were running. And people—

People came.

Word travels strangely out of the mountains. It comes down with the tin traders and the pilgrims and the tinker caravans, and it changes a little every time it passes through a mouth. In Vell's Crossing they said there was a speaker in Highmoor who could call elk out of the fens. Further south, they said a beast-speaker had gone under the mountain and come back out again, which no one had done in living memory. By the time the story reached the coast, Cyrus suspected, it would involve dragons. There were no dragons involved. There was a raven, which was worse.

"Visitors," said a dry voice above them. "Three on the north road, walking. One is limping and pretending not to. Another is carrying more weapons than a pilgrim needs and less sense than either."

Bramble dropped onto the fence post beside Cyrus, feathers ruffled, eyes bright as wet ink. He had been scouting the road since dawn, because scouting the road made him feel important, and feeling important was the only thing he loved more than bread crusts and his own opinions.

"Three," Cyrus said. "That's fewer than last week."

"Last week's lot turned around when they saw your mother." Bramble preened. "Maren has a look. I've seen it stop avalanches. Metaphorically. I've never actually seen an avalanche stopped, but I'm confident she could."

Cyrus laughed. That was another thing that had changed—they laughed now in daylight, easily, without checking first whether anyone was listening. For twenty-two years they had lived like a held breath. They hadn't realized how tired it had made them until they finally exhaled.

"Should I go meet them?" they asked.

"You should absolutely go meet them," Bramble said. "You're the beast-speaker who tamed a mountain. You can't be found doing chores. Terrible for the legend."

"I was found doing chores last week. And the week before."

"Yes, and one of them asked if the great speaker would bless his mule, and you did it while holding a bucket. The bucket is now part of the story. People ask about the bucket." He hopped to their shoulder, claws gentle through the sleeve. "Go. I'll handle Petunia."

"You cannot handle Petunia."

"No one can handle Petunia. But I can watch her eat the rest of the shawl with moral disapproval, which is nearly the same thing."

* * *

The ford looked different in autumn light.

The river ran shallow and quick over its stones, brown and gold with fallen leaves, and the willows along the bank had gone the color of old coins. Cyrus stood where they always stood now—the spot where Ilse had collapsed into their arms, whispering words that had unmade a life and remade it. They came here most mornings. Not to grieve, exactly. Grief was still there, but it had settled into something quieter, something more like gratitude wearing grief's old coat. Ilse had bought a child's safety with her own soul, and the child had grown up and gone and gotten it back, and neither of those things canceled the other. Both were true. Cyrus had learned, under the mountain, that two true things could live in the same chest without breaking it.

They heard the travelers before they saw them, the way they heard everything now—not just hooves and boots but heartbeats, breath, the small nervous chatter of a dog's mind and the placid green boredom of the horses. Their gift sat differently in them these days. Before, it had been a shout they were trying not to make. Now it was more like hearing itself: always on, but gentle, a room full of quiet voices rather than a storm.

The mountain carried half of it anyway. That had been the bargain's true shape, though Cyrus was still learning all its edges. When they had bound their gift into the stone—not surrendered, never surrendered, *anchored*—the mountain had become something like a second self, or perhaps Cyrus had become something like a small walking piece of the mountain. Either way, they were never alone inside their own head anymore. On bad nights, that was the difference between drowning and floating.

The travelers crested the rise: three of them, as Bramble had promised. A limping man with a peddler's pack. A woman whose hand kept drifting toward the knife at her belt and then away, drifting and returning like a bird that couldn't decide on a branch. And a girl, fifteen maybe, walking slightly ahead of the others with her chin up in a way that fooled no one, because Cyrus recognized the set of those shoulders. They had worn that exact posture for twenty-two years. It was the posture of someone trying very hard to seem like nothing.

The girl saw Cyrus standing at the ford and stopped dead.

"You're real," she blurted, and then went scarlet.

"I'm real," Cyrus agreed. "Though some mornings I check."

"We came from Marrowdale. It took nine days. My aunt said—" She faltered, glancing back at the others, then forward again, deciding. Cyrus watched her decide. It was like watching someone step off a ledge and find out mid-fall that the air would hold. "My aunt said if anyone could hear what was wrong with our ox, it'd be you. He won't eat. The farrier says there's nothing wrong with him. But he looks at the barn wall like there's something behind it."

"There's a wasp nest between the boards," Cyrus said. "Second plank up, left of the door. He doesn't want to get stung in the nose."

The girl stared. Then she laughed—a startled, delighted sound—and the woman behind her put a hand over her mouth, and the tension that had been riding all three sets of shoulders simply fell away, like snow sliding off a roof.

"How did you—" the limping man started.

"They told me," Cyrus said gently. "Oxen aren't shy about their opinions. You learn to listen, and after that it's mostly manners."

This was the part Cyrus loved now, the part they would never have believed possible a year ago: this. Standing in plain sight, gift open, saying yes instead of nothing. Every stranger who came down the north road arrived braced like the girl—braced for refusal, for suspicion, for that careful blankness Cyrus themselves had perfected. And every time Cyrus answered plainly, warmly, something in the visitor's face unlocked. Word was spreading, but not the word they'd expected. Not *beware*. Just: *she'll talk to you. She'll tell you why your cow cries at night.* Small miracles. Mule blessings. Wasps in walls.

"Come up to the house," Cyrus said. "Maren's baking, which means there'll be too much of it, and Odji's fixing the well rope, which means it'll be fixed in a way that's technically better than before and will annoy everyone forever. You can wash at the ford first, if you like. The water's cold but honest."

* * *

The girl's name was Wren, which Cyrus decided privately was a good omen, and her ox was called Bartholomew, which Cyrus decided privately was a terrible name that the ox deserved to suffer under.

They walked up together through the lower pasture, and Wren talked the whole way, in the tumbling flood of someone who had been rehearsing questions for nine days and could finally ask them. What was it like, going under the mountain? Was it true the court ate souls? Was it true the Warden of Seals fought beside you at the end?

"That last one's true," Cyrus said. "Though 'fought beside' is generous. Sable mostly fought *near* me, in a manner suggesting she might change her mind at any moment. Which, to be fair, so did I."

"And the raven? Is the raven—"

"Here," said Bramble, dropping from the birch overhead to land on Wren's pack strap, making her shriek. "And listening, which is my primary occupation. Ask your next question carefully."

"He's exiled nobility," Cyrus said. "From the mountain court. Stole something he shouldn't have."

"I liberated something that was poorly guarded," Bramble corrected. "History is written by whoever can talk. Which, conveniently, is me."

"What did you steal?" Wren asked, wide-eyed.

"My voice," Bramble said, "and later, someone else's. It's a long story involving a lantern, a vault, and the single greatest act of larceny in the history of the subterranean world, and I'll tell it for bread."

"He'll tell it for free," Cyrus said. "He just likes being paid."

"A poet must eat."

The farmhouse came into view around the bend—smoke from the chimney, laundry on the line (minus one shawl; Petunia had shown no remorse), and Odji perched absurdly on the well frame with their sleeves rolled up and a look of profound grievance aimed at the pulley.

"It's crooked," Odji announced before anyone could say hello. "Your well. The rope runs against the stone edge, it'll fray within the season, I've rigged a guide, you're welcome, and also your goat is in the kitchen."

"She got in through the—"

"The window, yes. I've met your goat. Your goat and I have an understanding." Odji swung down, brushed off their hands, and bowed to the travelers with theatrical grace. "Friends of the famous? Come in, come in. Mind the third floorboard. It squeaks in a key that upsets the chickens."

The lantern hung at their hip as always, and Cyrus caught, as they always did now, the faint glow behind the glass and the small voice humming inside it. Liss. Odji's sister. Freed from the vault with all the others on the night the mountain shook, she had surprised everyone by choosing to stay—choosing the lantern, choosing Odji, choosing the road. *Some cages are homes,* she'd said, in a voice like struck glass, when Cyrus had asked. *The trick is knowing which kind yours is.* Odji had cried about it exactly once, denied it ever, and carried the lantern closer to their heart ever after.

Inside, Maren had, as prophesied, baked enough bread for a siege. She came out of the kitchen flour-dusted and formidable, took one look at the three dusty travelers, and began issuing instructions—wash basin, chairs, the good butter, sit, *sit*—with such brisk authority that Wren and her companions obeyed before realizing they'd been given orders.

Cyrus watched her mother work and felt the old ache turn over, but gentler now. A month ago Maren had confessed everything: the boundary stone, the dying woman, the bargain, the letter she'd burned unread because she couldn't bear what it would ask of her. Forgiveness hadn't come in a single moment. It had come the way the ward had woken—slowly, in pieces, a warmth returning to something long frozen. Last week Cyrus had found Maren sitting by the boundary stone at dusk, one hand flat against it, murmuring. When Cyrus asked what she was doing, she'd said, without embarrassment, "Getting to know it." As if the ward were a member of the family who'd been ill for years and was finally well enough to receive visitors.

Maybe it was.

"So," Maren said, setting down a plate and fixing Wren with a look. "An ox that stares at barn walls. That's why you crossed half the country?"

Wren, mouth full, nodded.

"Hm." Maren wiped her hands. "When Cyrus was small, they told me the neighbor's cows were sad about the thunder. Sad. About thunder. I thought the child was making up stories to avoid weeding." Her eyes crinkled at Cyrus, and there was no apology in it, because they'd been past apologies for a while now—there was just this, the truth, spoken lightly at last. "Took me twenty years to believe them. Don't be as slow as me."

"Maren," Cyrus said, "no one could be as slow as you."

"Flattery. More bread?"

* * *

They stayed three days, the travelers. Cyrus walked up to Marrowdale's edge with Wren to see Bartholomew, confirmed the wasps, and showed her how to move slow and breathe low so the ox would let her smoke them out herself. "He'll trust you after," Cyrus said. "That's most of it, really. Anyone can hear them. The gift is just getting them to believe you're worth talking to."

"Is that how it worked with the mountain?" Wren asked.

Cyrus looked east, where the peak stood clear against the afternoon sky, wearing its evening clouds like a mantle. Somewhere in all that stone, a hunger that had eaten magic for centuries now spent its days listening—to wind, to roots, to the small bright noise of living things. Not tamed, exactly. Given a better job.

"Something like that," Cyrus said. "It was starving. Nobody had offered it anything but fear in a very long time."

On the morning the travelers left, Wren hesitated at the gate, working up to something. Cyrus waited. They were good at waiting now.

"When I go home," Wren said finally, "people will ask what you're like. The great speaker." Her ears went pink. "What should I tell them?"

Bramble, from the fence post, opened his beak with the clear intention of dictating a flattering epic, and Cyrus raised a hand to silence him.

"Tell them I argue with goats," Cyrus said. "Tell them my mother makes too much bread, and my friend fixed the well in a way that's annoying, and the raven will lie to you for crumbs but tells the truth for free if you catch him tired. Tell them—" and here they paused, because they wanted to get it right, because once they had been a girl on a road too, carrying a question they didn't know how to ask, "—tell them the thing you're afraid of isn't the thing people will run from. Usually it's the thing they've been waiting for."

Wren blinked hard, nodded twice, and fled to her companions before her face could do anything embarrassing. Cyrus understood that too.

They stood at the gate and watched the three figures shrink down the north road, toward the ford, toward the world. The dog barked once, joyfully, at nothing. The wind moved through the willows. Underfoot, faint and steady as a heartbeat, the mountain listened.

"Well," said Bramble. "You gave a speech. It wasn't terrible. I've drafted several improvements—"

"Bramble."

"—which I'll share over breakfast, ideally the honey bread, don't think I haven't noticed that Maren hides it—"

"Bramble."

He settled on Cyrus's shoulder, warm weight, familiar claws, and for a moment said nothing at all, which from Bramble was practically a sonnet.

"They're going to keep coming, you know," he said quietly. "Travelers. Questions. The girl was only the first of hundreds. Your quiet life is over."

Cyrus looked out at the valley—at the pastures and the smoking chimneys, at the boundary stone glowing soft and patient on the hill, at the ford where a dying woman had once pressed a map into their hands and changed everything. The fourteenth moon was rising somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, round and new, beginning its next slow turn.

"Good," said Cyrus, and meant it. "Let them come."
