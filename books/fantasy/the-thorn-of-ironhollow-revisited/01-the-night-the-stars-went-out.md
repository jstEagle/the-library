## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The evening of the harvest vigil, Jude Winterbourne was arguing with a goose.

It was, in fairness, less an argument than a negotiation — the goose held all the leverage, being large and white and possessed of an opinion about the pen gate that it expressed by biting. Jude wrestled the latch closed with one hand while the other kept a respectful distance from that orange beak, and above them both the sky did what the sky over Ironhollow always did: nothing at all. It was the valley's oldest promise. The stars came out every night in their fixed and faithful ranks, the same stars their great-great-grandmothers had steered by, unblinking, unhurried, as constant as the wardstones that ringed the valley like sleeping dogs.

"You can hiss at me all you like," Jude told the goose. "Tomorrow there'll be grain enough for both of us."

The goose hissed anyway. Jude laughed — the short, easy laugh of someone who has never once had reason to look up in fear — and shouldered the feed pail, and that was the last ordinary thing they did for a very long time.

* * *

The cottage smelled of beeswax and baking squash when Jude got home, and of the particular sharp sweetness of cedar shavings where Mother's carving knife had been at work again. She sat by the hearth with her bad knee propped on a stool, transforming a knot of pale wood into the shape of a wren — or possibly, judging by the wings she'd roughed out, another wren; she had made eleven wrens this year and showed no sign of stopping. On the shelf above her hung the only other object in the house that mattered more than the carvings: the sword.

It was not much to look at. A plain blade, oiled and dark, in a scabbard gone soft at the mouth from two centuries of careful hands. It had belonged to Jude's grandmother, and her grandmother before her, back through a line of names Jude could recite like a psalm — Winterbourne after Winterbourne after Winterbourne, each one a swordspeaker, each one sworn. The oath-bound sword of Ironhollow. In Jude's whole life it had never done anything but hang there, heavy and quiet as a held breath, because swordspeaking was a ritual thing, a harvest-thing, a thing performed once a year with salt bread and old words and then put away. Theirs was a valley that had made peace with its own magic the way you make peace with a well: you draw from it carefully, at appointed times, and you do not ask what moves down in the dark water.

"You're late," Mother said, not looking up from the wren. "Marta Vess sent word the vigil loaves are ready and she wants the swordspeaker there early to bless them."

"The swordspeaker will be there when the swordspeaker has washed the goose off."

"Mm." The knife whispered. "She also asked if you'd speak the boundary-blessing this year instead of old Tammas. He's got wind in his chest."

"I always speak it."

"She wants you to *mean* it louder, I think." Mother smiled at the wood. "Everyone wants things from the last sword-speaker in the valley. That's what being the last means, my love. You're not a person so much as a porch everyone stands on."

Jude snorted and bent to kiss the crown of her head. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and the rosemary oil she rubbed into her hands. Later — in all the long and hungry years afterward — this was the moment Jude would return to, again and again, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth: the warmth of the hearth, the half-made wren, the ordinary miracle of an evening that believed it would be followed by another.

Outside, the first bell of the vigil rang down from the chapel hill, thin and silver in the cold air, and the whole valley seemed to lean toward it like wheat toward sun.

* * *

They walked up together through lanes strung with lanterns, past doorways heaped with gourds and braided straw, past children chasing one another with turnip-lanterns whose candlelight made jack-o'-lantern grins of their faces. The whole of Ironhollow had turned out for the vigil — farmers with flour-dusted hands, the miller's boys scrubbed pink, old Tammas Hollis leaning on his stick and already complaining about the cold. At the top of the hill stood the vigil fire, unlit, waiting, and beyond it the ancient wardstone that anchored the valley's southern border: a granite finger taller than two men, carved over with spirals so worn they were more memory than mark.

Jude had touched that stone a thousand times. Every child in Ironhollow had. It was warm the way a hearthstone is warm, faintly, patiently, and it hummed — not to ears, but to something deeper, the way you feel a train coming through your boot-soles. Two hundred years the wards had held without a single failure. Two hundred years the world outside — wars, plagues, kings, all of it — had rolled against the valley's borders like weather against glass, and found no purchase.

Jude laid a hand on the stone as tradition demanded and began the boundary-blessing, the old words rolling out over the hush of the crowd:

*"Steel remember. Stone remember. Let what is without stay without—"*

And that was when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud. They went out *at once*, every star in the sky above Ironhollow, as if a hand had passed over the world's candles in a single sweep. One heartbeat the vault of heaven was sewn thick with light; the next it was a black so total and so sudden that the crowd's gasp sounded like a single drawn breath, and the lantern-light below seemed suddenly the only light left anywhere.

For three full seconds nobody moved. Nobody breathed.

Then old Tammas said, comfortably, "Eclipse," and someone laughed, and the laugh had a nervous edge, because everyone knew — the way you know rain is coming in your knee — that eclipses do not fall upon every star at once, and that the moon was nowhere near the sky it would need to be in.

Jude's hand was still on the wardstone. And the wardstone was screaming.

There was no sound. That was the horror of it — a scream entirely inside the stone, inside the bones of Jude's hand, a vibration climbing up the arm and into the chest and setting the teeth buzzing in the jaw. The warmth drained out of the granite under their palm as though blood were leaving it. And high overhead, faint as frost forming on a window, something *cracked* — a sound heard not with ears but with the marrow, a splintering running around the rim of the whole valley, north and east and south and west, along the invisible ring where the wards had slept for two hundred years.

"They're breaking," Jude heard themselves say. Their voice came out very small. "The wards are breaking."

Around the vigil fire, faces turned upward into that starless black, and Jude watched a whole village understand, in the space of a few heartbeats, that the roof over their heads had just been taken away. Marta Vess clutched her vigil-loaves to her chest like a shield. A child began to cry and was hushed, and then the hushing broke and several children were crying, and their mothers' faces in the firelight had a terrible stillness, the stillness of people doing arithmetic with fear.

Mother's hand found Jude's shoulder. Her grip was iron. "Say the blessing again," she said quietly. "All of it. Now."

*"Steel remember. Stone remember—"* But the words came apart in Jude's mouth like wet bread, because the stone under their palm had gone cold as riverbed ice, and the humming was gone, and in its place was silence — the specific, ringing silence of something that has stopped.

* * *

It was on the walk home, down through lanes where nobody lit their lanterns anymore because every flame seemed suddenly precious and hoarded, that Jude felt the sword.

They had carried it to the vigil as always — slung across the back out of duty, kissed by tradition rather than use. All their life it had hung against their spine with the inert weight of any old thing. Now, halfway down the chapel hill, between one step and the next, the blade *hummed*.

Not rattled. Not shifted. Hummed — a note too low for ears, felt in the shoulder blades, in the sternum, in the backs of the teeth. Jude stopped dead in the lane. Somewhere ahead a dog began to howl, and then another, all across the valley, the way dogs sing when the ground is about to give.

"No," Jude said aloud, to the sword, to the sky, to no one. "You don't get to start now."

The sword hummed on, patient and low, like something waking after a very long sleep and finding the house changed around it.

Inside the cottage, Mother built up the fire though it was not yet late, feeding it log after log until the room roared with heat and light, as if brightness could be stockpiled against what was coming. She didn't speak. Neither did Jude. They ate the vigil supper standing up — salt bread and honey and the first of the new squash, food blessed for luck that neither of them tasted — and twice Jude caught their mother looking at the sword where it leaned against the wall, and twice she looked quickly away, as though meeting its eye.

Near midnight, when the fire had burned down to a red heart, Mother finally spoke.

"My grandmother told me a story once," she said, turning her carved wren over and over in her hands without seeing it. "About why Ironhollow is hidden. She said we weren't hiding *from* anything. She said we were hiding something *in*. I asked her what, and she boxed my ears and never spoke of it again."

"What are you telling me that for?"

"Because tonight the hiding stopped." She set the wren down on the table between them, unfinished, its wings half-formed, and looked at her child across the firelight with an expression Jude had never seen on her face — not fear exactly, but the terrible calm of someone who has finally been told the hour of an appointment they'd spent their whole life dreading. "Whatever comes through those broken wards is going to come looking. And you're the last swordspeaker in Ironhollow, my love. Whatever that means — whatever it has always meant, beneath the rituals and the bread — it means it's you they'll find first."

The fire ticked. Outside, the dog had stopped howling, which was somehow worse.

Jude looked down at their own hands — work-worn, flour in the creases, goose-down still stuck to one sleeve — ordinary hands, hands made for pens and loaves and the small honest labors of a hidden valley. Then they looked at the sword against the wall, dark and silent and awake, and understood with perfect clarity that the life they had been living was already over, had ended sometime between the first bell and the second, and that everything after this night would be counted differently.

Above the cottage, through the smoke-hole, the sky was a black with no holes in it. Jude had lived twenty-six years under those stars and had never once thanked them for burning. Now, staring up into the vacancy where they had been, Jude found they could think of nothing else — the whole lost constellation of them, gone like breath off glass, and the valley lying open beneath like a wound undressed.

The sword hummed against the wall, low and steady, keeping time with Jude's frightened heart.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the valley, at the ragged place where the wards used to be — something moved.
