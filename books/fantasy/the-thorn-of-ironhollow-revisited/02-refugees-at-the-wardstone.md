## 2. Refugees at the Wardstone

The morning after the stars died, the fog came down off the shoulders of the mountains and would not lift, as though the valley had pulled a sheet over its own face.

Jude had not slept. They had sat up all night with the oath-sword laid across their knees, listening to it hum. Not a sound, exactly — the hum lived in the bones of their hands, in the roots of their teeth, a vibration like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Twice they had set the sword in its chest at the foot of the bed and walked away, and twice the humming had followed them, patient and terrible, like a dog that knew it was wanted.

By the time the light came — gray, starved, nothing like a proper dawn — the bell in the village square was already ringing. Not the harvest peal. The alarm.

Jude dressed in the dark, buckled the sword on out of habit and then stood a moment with their hand on the door, feeling the weight of it pull at their hip the way it had never pulled before. For two hundred years the swordspeakers of Ironhollow had carried their blades to festivals and funerals, to the planting and the shearing, and the steel had been nothing but steel. Ornament. Inheritance. A story the valley told about itself.

Now it was awake, and it wanted something, and Jude did not know what.

The fog was thick enough to drink. Jude walked through it toward the square and heard the village before they saw it — the wet cough of cattle, a child crying somewhere with the flat, exhausted persistence of a child who has been crying all night, and under it all a low murmur of voices, dozens of them, all saying the same words in different mouths: *the wards, the wards, the wards.*

* * *

The wardstone stood at the valley's northern mouth, where the road to the outside world narrowed to a cart's width between two leaning cliffs. Jude's grandfather had called it the Doorpost. Every child in Ironhollow had pressed a palm to it at least once, dared by a friend, and every child had felt nothing — the faint chill of any old stone, no more. The wards were not for touching. They were for trusting. They had stood so long that no one living remembered them as anything but a fact of the world, like rain falling down or bread rising near the fire.

The wardstone was broken.

Not cracked. Not chipped. Broken the way a promise is broken — all at once, and from the inside. A wound ran down its face from crown to base, a seam of darkness like a vein of night had been exposed in the rock, and from that seam the fog was pouring out in slow ribbons, though the air on either side was still. Jude stopped ten paces away and felt the sword at their hip go taut with attention, a hound scenting something it could not name.

The crowd had gathered in a rough crescent before the stone, and it was larger than Jude had expected — not just Hollowbrook's folk but families from the outlying farms, people who had walked miles in the dark with their children bundled in blankets, carrying what they could. A woman held a hen against her breast like an infant. An old man had brought his anvil hammer, for reasons Jude could not begin to guess. They stood in the fog with their breath steaming, and when Jude came up the path, the crescent turned toward them the way a wound turns toward salt.

"There," someone said. "The swordspeaker."

Jude had been the last swordspeaker since they were nineteen years old, since old Master Ellery had died with Jude's hand in his and the oath passed between them like a coal too hot to hold. Nine years. And in nine years the title had meant harvest blessings and wedding rites and the annual recitation of the Vigil Words, all of it ceremony, all of it safe. The villagers had looked at Jude with fondness, the way one looks at a well-loved clock that no longer keeps time but is too beautiful to take down.

They were not looking at Jude with fondness now.

"The stars," said Bram Tuller, the miller, shouldering forward. He was a big man with flour in the creases of his knuckles and fear in his eyes. "The stars went out. All of them, all at once, like snuffed candles. My boy saw it. My wife saw it. The whole sky, dark as the inside of a sack."

"And the stone," said a woman behind him — Maren Ostler, whose family had kept the eastern farms for six generations. Her voice was steadier, but her hands, wrapped white-knuckled around a shepherd's crook, were not. "The stone's been humming since midnight. My dog wouldn't go near the north road. He howled until dawn and then he just — stopped. Went under the house and wouldn't come out."

"The wards are broken," said old Tammas Reeve flatly, from the front of the crowd. He had been the village's memory for forty years, the one who knew which field had been cursed and which wedding had ended badly. "I don't say it to frighten anyone. I say it because it's true, and because we've a right to hear what's to be done."

All of them were looking at Jude now. All of them, and behind their eyes the same question, simple and enormous: *you are the last of the ones who were supposed to keep us safe. What will you do?*

Jude opened their mouth and found it full of fog.

"I don't know," they said.

The word fell into the crowd like a stone into a well, and the ripples went out and out.

"You don't *know*," Bram repeated. "You're the swordspeaker. Your family's kept the valley's oaths since before the stone was raised. If anyone knows—"

"Then no one knows," Jude said, and heard their own voice come out harder than they intended, edged with the sleepless night and the humming sword and the terrible, starless dark they had lain awake inside. "Master Ellery taught me the rites. He taught me the Vigil Words and the Oath of the Steel and the names of every swordspeaker back to the Founding. He never taught me what to do when the sky goes out, because no one who wrote those rites ever thought it *could.*" They made themselves breathe. Made their voice level. "The wards have stood for two hundred years. Whatever broke them, it wasn't broken from inside the valley. And whatever it is, it's still out there."

A murmur ran through the crowd, the sound of two hundred people deciding at once to be more afraid.

"Then what good are you?" someone said — young Wren Ostler, Maren's son, sixteen and scared enough to be cruel. "What good's a swordspeaker whose sword doesn't—"

"Wren." Maren's voice cracked like a whip. But the boy's question hung there anyway, and everyone heard it, and no one contradicted it, and Jude felt it settle into their chest alongside the sword's humming, two weights where there had been one.

"I don't know what good I am," Jude said quietly. "But I know the wards are down and the valley is open, and standing here arguing in the fog won't close them. Go home. Bar your doors. Keep your children inside and your lamps lit. If anything comes up that road, you'll hear the bell, and I will be the one standing between it and you. That much I can swear. That much I *will* swear."

For a moment the crowd held its shape, a wall of frightened faces. Then Tammas Reeve nodded, slow and grave, and the nod went through the crowd like a current, and they began to move — not comforted, none of them comforted, but obedient to the shape of an order, because people would rather follow a plan they doubt than stand in a fog with no plan at all.

Jude watched them go and did not feel like the person who had just spoken. The words had come out of them the way the humming came out of the sword — from somewhere deeper than decision. *I will be the one standing between it and you.* They had sworn it without knowing if they could keep it, and the oath-sword at their hip had warmed against their leg as they said it, as if it had been listening. As if it had been *hungry.*

* * *

The fog thinned toward midday but never lifted entirely, and Jude spent the hours walking the north road's length, from the wardstone to the first bend where the cliffs closed in, cataloguing what could be seen and dreading what could not. The road beyond the valley's mouth was empty. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. An empty road, in a world where the stars had gone out, felt like a held breath.

They were on their third pass of the wardstone when the humming changed.

Jude felt it before they heard it — the vibration in their hands shifting key, rising from its low patient drone to something quicker, brighter, urgent as a struck flint. They stopped. The dark seam in the stone pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat remembering itself.

And then, out of the fog on the far side of the stone, out of the outside world, a figure came walking. Staggering. Walking and staggering and falling and rising again, a shape that resolved by degrees into a woman — young, or young-ish, wrapped in a traveler's cloak that had been gray once and was now mostly mud and soot. She was limping badly on her left leg. Her right hand was pressed against her side, and between her fingers something was *glowing.*

Jude's breath stopped.

It was small — no bigger than a knife's blade, a splinter, a shard — and it burned with a light that Jude's body understood before their mind did, because there was only one thing in all the world that light could be. It was starlight. A piece of a star, fallen and broken and still burning, cradled in a stranger's bloody hand like a coal carried from a fire that no longer existed anywhere else.

The woman saw Jude. Jude saw the exact moment she saw them — the way her eyes went first to the sword at their hip, then to their face, and how something in her expression shifted from desperation to a desperate, exhausted *recognition*, as though Jude were a door she had been crawling toward for a very long time.

"You're one of them," she said. Her voice was ragged, worn down to the thread. "The valley-folk. The hidden ones. You're—" She took another step and her leg went out from under her.

Jude moved without deciding to. They caught her before she struck the stone, and the moment their hands touched her arms, three things happened at once.

The sword at their hip rang — actually rang, a clear high note like a bell struck in an empty church, a sound Jude had never heard it make and had no name for. The shard in the woman's hand flared, throwing their two shadows huge and wavering across the broken wardstone. And the dark seam in the stone *leaned* — there was no other word for it — the way a plant leans toward light, the way a starving thing leans toward warmth, and Jude felt the whole valley hold its breath around them.

The woman looked up at Jude from the circle of their arms. Her face was gray with exhaustion, streaked with soot, and there was a cut above her eyebrow that had bled freely and recently. Her eyes were the color of deep water and just as cold and just as full of moving things.

"Are you the last one?" she whispered. "The last swordspeaker?"

The question went through Jude like the seam of dark had gone through the wardstone — all at once, from the inside. *The last.* They had carried those words for nine years like a stone in the pocket, and they had never once heard them spoken by a stranger, by someone from *outside*, by someone who knew what the word *last* actually weighed.

"Yes," Jude heard themselves say.

"Then you have to listen to me." She gripped Jude's wrist with a hand that had almost no strength left in it and spent all of it anyway. "The wards didn't fail. They were *broken*, on purpose, and the ones who broke them are coming, and I have to—"

She got no further. Her eyes rolled back, her grip went slack, and she collapsed against Jude's chest, unconscious, the shard of fallen star still burning in her open palm — a little captive dawn in a valley where the night had just begun.

Behind her, far up the empty road, deep in the fog and the starless dark of the outside world, something made a sound.

Not a footstep. Not a voice. A long, low, patient sound, like the hum of the sword, like the hum of the stone, like the whole wounded world clearing its throat.

Jude stood in the fog with a dying stranger in their arms, a burning star in her hand, and their oath-sword singing against their hip, and understood — with the calm, cold clarity of a person standing on ground that has just begun to tilt — that the safe world was over, that it had ended last night between one star and the next, and that they, the last of the swordspeakers, were the only door left standing in a valley with no walls.

The bell in the village began to ring again. Jude lifted their voice and shouted for help, and the fog swallowed the sound whole.
