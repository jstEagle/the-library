## 3. What Magic Cannot Follow

Sera dreamed of falling upward.

She rose through dark water that was not water, through a blackness that was not night, and above her the stars hung like wounds in a curtain — beautiful, bleeding light. In the dream she reached for them. In the dream she always reached for them. And in the dream, as in life, they went out at her touch, one by one, until she hung alone in a darkness with nothing left to name it by.

She woke to the smell of woodsmoke and feverfew.

The ceiling above her was low and hand-hewn, beams darkened by decades of hearth-cure. A bundle of dried herbs hung from a rafter, turning slowly in some draft she couldn't feel. Somewhere nearby, water moved — a kettle, or a stream, or rain beginning against a roof. The sounds of a world that did not know what was coming for it.

*Ironhollow,* she thought, and the word arrived like a stone dropped into still water, rings spreading outward through everything she knew. *I made it. I actually made it.*

She tried to sit up and her body filed a formal complaint. Her side had been bound — clean linen, careful knots, someone who understood wounds if not their causes — and beneath the binding the gash throbbed in time with her heartbeat, a slow dull drumbeat of wrongness. There was a cup on the stool beside the bed. She drank it without tasting it. Bitter. Feverfew and something sharper underneath, willow-bark maybe, and beneath even that a faint mineral tang she recognized and did not want to recognize: wardstone dust, ground fine, stirred into a healer's draught. The valley's whole self dissolved into tea.

The door opened without a knock.

Jude Winterbourne came in sideways, the way people do when their arms are full — an armful of split firewood, dumped into the box by the hearth with a clatter that Sera felt in her ribs. They were younger than she'd expected from the way the villagers had spoken of them. Younger, and tired around the eyes in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. They wore the oath-sword at their hip the way other people wore a debt: present, unavoidable, resented.

"You're awake," Jude said. Not a question. An accusation leveled gently at the fact itself.

"Barely." Her voice came out as gravel. She tried again. "Thank you for the binding."

"Don't thank me. Marta Hale did the stitching. I held the lamp and tried not to faint, which I'm told is traditional." They crouched at the hearth, fed the fire, and only then turned to look at her properly — and Sera watched them do what she herself had done a hundred times in a hundred rooms: take inventory. The stranger's hands. The stranger's eyes. The question of whether the stranger was going to be trouble. "You crossed the wardline carrying starlight. Do you understand how strange a sentence that is?"

"Understand it?" Sera managed something that was almost a laugh, and paid for it in pain. "I've spent six years trying to say it out loud to anyone who wouldn't burn me for it."

"And now you're saying it to me."

"Now I'm saying it to you."

Jude sat down on the stool across from her, slowly, like a person lowering themselves into cold water. Up close, their face had the kind of plainness that took effort to see past — broad cheekbones, wind-chapped skin, a small old scar through one eyebrow. But their eyes were doing something their face wasn't. Their eyes were afraid, and honest about it, in a way that Sera found more disarming than any blade would have been.

"The stars went out," Jude said. "Over my valley. Over the valley my family has kept for two hundred years. Then the wards broke — the wards that were supposed to hold until the sun burns out — and then *you* fell out of the hole where the sky used to be, bleeding, holding a piece of one of them." They spread their hands. "So. Start wherever you like. But start."

Outside, the rain had begun in earnest, soft on the thatch, patient. Sera looked at the fire instead of at them, because some things were easier said to flames.

"My order called it the under-court," she said. "The court beneath the mountain. You won't have heard of it. That was rather the point of you."

"Of me?"

"Of this valley. Of all of it." She shifted, winced, kept going. "Before Ironhollow had a name, before the wards, there was a thing under the mountains north of here that the old faiths served. Not worshipped, exactly — served is worse. It bought things. Promises. Souls. It has been buying for longer than your valley has existed, and every kingdom that ever touched it came away poorer in ways that don't show up in ledgers." She watched Jude's face carefully as she said it, watching for disbelief, and found instead a terrible kind of recognition — the look of someone hearing the explanation for a noise they'd been pretending not to hear all their life. "Your founders didn't build Ironhollow to be a home, Jude. They built it to be a hiding place. The wards aren't walls against weather or war. They're a blindfold. Magic cannot follow you in here because magic cannot *see* you in here. This valley is a hole in the world's memory."

The fire popped. Rain ticked against the thatch. Jude sat very still.

"What are they hiding?"

"I can't tell you that."

"You just told me everything else."

"I told you the shape of the secret," Sera said. "Not its contents. And there is a difference, and the difference matters, and you will have to trust me on that, which I know is a great deal to ask of someone whose entire inheritance just collapsed last night." She held their gaze. It cost her more than the wound did. "What I can tell you is this: the thing under the mountain wanted what your founders hid. Has always wanted it. Could never find it, because your ancestors built a blind spot so complete that even its hunters walked past this valley for two centuries without knowing there was anything to walk past. And then, three nights ago, someone sold the wards."

Jude went white. Not dramatically — no gasp, no stagger — just a slow draining, like water finding a crack. "Sold."

"Sold. Wards that old don't fail, swordspeaker. They're not machines. They're a promise, and promises don't wear out — they get broken, or they get *bought out*. Someone with the blood-right to speak for this valley went to the court beneath the mountain and traded the blindfold for something they wanted more." Sera let that sit in the room between them like a third person. "And whatever they got in exchange, it was enough to kill the stars. Because the stars, Jude—" and here her voice finally failed her, cracked down the middle, and she pressed on anyway, because she had come too far and bled too much to stop at the doorstep, "—the stars are not fires. They are bindings. Old ones. Lit by hands older than kingdoms, and they go out one at a time as the things they bind grow strong enough to pay their way free."

Silence. The kettle began, far off, to murmur toward boiling.

"So the hunters," Jude said at last, quietly. "You said hunters were coming."

"Hunters are already here." Sera pushed herself up against the headboard, ignoring the screaming of her side, because this part mattered and she would say it sitting upright or not at all. "The ones who took the payment will send others to collect the goods. Whatever your founders hid is now findable — the blindfold is off, the valley is a lit window in a dark country, and every servant of the under-court between here and the sea can feel it. I didn't cross the wardline by accident, swordspeaker. I crossed it running. And the things I was running from stopped at your border for exactly as long as it took the wards to die." She swallowed. "They'll come within days. Perhaps less. And they will not be gentle, because gentleness is not in the contract, and neither, anymore, are you safe behind anything your great-grandparents built."

Jude stood. Walked to the window. Stood there with their back to her, watching rain run down the glass, and Sera saw their reflection do something complicated — jaw working, hand drifting once to the sword at their hip and stopping short of touching it, the way you don't touch a dog that's been growling.

"Marta Hale asked me this morning," Jude said, to the glass, "if I could fix the wardstone. The whole village asked. They stood in the rain looking at me like I was the answer to a question nobody ever taught me." A breath. "Two hundred years my family spoke oaths over steel and kept the border, and we never once needed the magic to *work*, because the wards did everything worth doing. We were locks on a door that never got knocked on. And now—"

"Now the door is open," Sera finished softly, "and the lock wants to matter."

Jude turned. Something had settled in their face — not peace, nothing so kind, but a decision taking its first breath.

"Why you?" they asked. "Why does a wounded priest of a dead order crawl through a war to reach a valley full of farmers? What do you want from us? From *me*?"

It was the right question. Sera had prepared answers for it across six hundred miles — answers tailored to knights and merchants and frightened village councils — and she found, lying in a stranger's bed in a stranger's valley at the end of the world, that she was tired of all of them.

"I want to fix what my order helped break," she said. "And I can't do it alone, and everyone who might have helped me is dead or bought, and you are the last swordspeaker alive, which means you are the last person on this earth whose word, spoken over steel, still carries weight enough to matter to what comes next." She held up a hand before Jude could speak. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to believe your own eyes. The stars went out. Your wards died. I'm real, the shard is real, and the hunters coming for this valley are more real than either. Believe those three things, and the rest will follow you whether you consent or not."

For a long moment the only sound was rain, and the kettle, and the small animal noises of the fire.

Then Jude crossed the room, knelt, and lifted from beneath the bed the cloth-wrapped bundle Sera had carried unconscious across the threshold — the shard, still wrapped, still humming its note below hearing. They set it on the blanket between them without uncovering it, the way you set down something hot.

"If I help you," Jude said, "you tell me everything eventually. Not today. But eventually. What's hidden, why it matters, who sold the wards — all of it. No more shapes of secrets."

"Eventually," Sera agreed. "On my order's bones, I swear it."

"That means nothing to me. Your order is dust."

"It means everything to me," Sera said. "That will have to do."

Jude looked at her for a long moment — measuring her, she thought, the way you measure a bridge before you put your weight on it — and then nodded, once, and something passed between them that was not friendship, was nowhere near friendship, but was the raw material friendship is quarried from.

A horn sounded.

Far off, thin through the rain, from the direction of the western ridge — a single long note, and then another, and then the village bell beginning to ring again, fast this time, urgent, the alarm-pattern Sera didn't know but Jude plainly did, because they were already moving, already reaching for the sword on the wall, their face gone pale and set.

"They're not supposed to sound that bell for another month," Jude said. "Harvest vigil. It's the rider — someone's coming down the west road, hard, flying colors I don't recognize."

Sera closed her eyes and felt, beneath the floorboards, beneath the roots, beneath the patient stone of the valley itself, the first tremor of the world remembering that Ironhollow existed.

"Not colors," she said quietly, swinging her legs off the bed despite everything her body screamed at her. "Get me up, swordspeaker. If that's who I think it is — you'll want to hear what she has to say before Veyra's outriders burn your question along with everything else."

Jude froze with one hand on the door.

"How," they said, very carefully, "do you know that name?"
