## 4. The Knight Without a Banner

The dead stars had been dead for three days, and the village had begun to pretend otherwise.

That was the thing about Ironhollow, Jude thought as they split kindling behind the cottage that morning. The valley was built on pretending. Pretending the wards were eternal. Pretending the harvest would come because it always had. Pretending that a girl with an oath-bound sword hanging over her door was a guardian of something, rather than the last keeper of an empty lighthouse.

The axe bit into the wood, and the wood gave, and that at least was honest work. Sera sat on the step behind them, wrapped in one of Mother's old blankets — no. Not Mother's. The blanket had come from the market in Lowbrook two summers past; Mother had been dead six years. But grief reached backward and relabeled things, and lately everything in the cottage was becoming hers again: her chair, her kettle, her mended quilt. The house remembered its dead by dressing strangers in their clothes.

Sera's star-shard lay across her knees, and even in gray daylight it gave off a light that wasn't quite light — more like the memory of light, the way a bell remembers ringing after the sound has gone. She turned it over and over with fingers that were still scabbed along the knuckles from wherever she'd come from, whatever she'd run through to reach the wardstone three nights ago.

"You're staring," Sera said without looking up.

"I'm watching you hold a piece of a fallen star in a valley where magic can't live."

"Then you're staring."

Jude swung the axe. The log split clean. "It doesn't make sense."

"Most true things don't." Sera lifted the shard so it caught the sun, and for a heartbeat something moved inside it — slow, like smoke underwater. "The sky went out everywhere, not just here. That means the wound is large. Large wounds bleed slowly. We have time." She said *we* as if it were settled law, as if Jude had agreed to anything beyond giving her a bed and broth.

"We have time until what?"

Sera smiled the way people smile at children who have asked how long until winter. "Until the hunters stop following the shard," she said, "and start following you."

Jude set down the axe. They meant to say something cutting, something worthy of a swordspeaker — *I've kept this valley safe my whole life, I'll keep it now* — but before the words came, the words became unnecessary.

Because the dogs began to scream.

Not bark. Scream — high and ragged, all four of the village dogs at once, from the pens near the mill, and beneath their voices came another sound that Jude felt in the teeth before hearing with the ears: hoofbeats. Many. Fast. Coming up the valley road at a pace no honest traveler used, because honest travelers knew what the switchbacks did to horses' legs and slowed for them.

And underneath the hoofbeats — faint, almost nothing, like a plucked string heard through a wall — Jude's oath-sword hummed.

They had hung it above the door since the night the stars vanished, after it had hummed once in their hands like a living thing surprised awake. Two hundred years of silence, and then twice in three days. Jude crossed the dooryard in six strides and took it down, and the leather of the grip was warm, warm as a sleeping animal, and the humming climbed into their wrist bones.

"Sera."

"I hear it." She was already standing, the shard pressed flat against her sternum, her face gone the color of tallow. "How many riders?"

Jude listened the way they'd been taught before they could write — the way every Winterbourne was taught, kneeling on the floor of this same cottage while an older voice said *the valley speaks first, child, and you listen second*. "Eight. Maybe nine. Splitting at the fork — four toward the mill, five for us."

"They found me faster than I hoped." Sera said it without self-pity, the way one notes weather. "Get your people inside. Get the old ones down into the cellar tunnels. Now, Jude."

"And you?"

Something flickered across her face — guilt, maybe, or arithmetic. "I'm why they're here."

* * *

Ironhollow had never needed a militia. That was the quiet joke of the valley: the wards did the fighting, and the swordspeakers did the remembering, and between the two there hadn't been a raid in living memory. What Ironhollow had instead was a bell and a custom — ring the bell, everyone goes indoors, and whatever trouble has come up the road will find itself alone with the trouble it brought.

Old Maren pulled the bell rope with hands that shook, and the sound rolled down-valley like a warning passed mouth to mouth. Doors slammed. Children were scooped up like firewood. By the time the five riders came up the last stretch of road, the village stood empty in the noon light, shutters closed, smoke rising straight and innocent from chimneys, a stage set with everyone fled backstage.

Except Jude, who stood in the middle of the road with an oath-sword that would not stop humming, and Sera, who refused the cellar with a look that dared anyone to argue.

The riders reined in twenty paces out. Five horses under black-and-silver caparisons, and the sigil on them made the back of Jude's neck prickle: a mountain inverted, its peak driven downward into a crown. Veyra's mark. Everyone had heard of it — news crept into the valley twice a year with the salt traders, gossip of the capital, the queen dead, the regent crowned, wars in the east — but sigils belonged to the world outside, the world that ended at the wardline, the world that could not touch them.

The world that had, apparently, learned to touch.

The lead rider wore a visorless helm and had the bored competence of a man doing errands. He looked at Jude — at the sword — and his expression didn't change, but he raised a hand, and his four companions spread into a line with the smooth unhurried motion of professionals.

"By authority of Her Radiant Majesty Veyra, First of Her Name," he recited, "surrender the fugitive Sera Vael, apostate of the Order of the Veiled Star, wanted for heresy, theft, and treason against the crown." His eyes flicked to Jude again, lingering on the sword. "You'd be the local... speaker, yes? The last one. Her Majesty extends her protection to loyal subjects of the valley. There need be no unpleasantness. Hand over the woman and go back to your harvest."

The words hung there, absurd and enormous. *Her Radiant Majesty.* In Ironhollow. On the road where Jude had learned to walk.

"The valley is under its own protection," Jude said, and hated how young their voice sounded. "It always has been."

The soldier sighed, genuinely regretful, which was somehow worse than menace. "That was true," he said, "right up until three nights ago."

He never got to finish whatever came next, because that was when the sixth rider arrived.

She came down the valley road like something thrown — a bay horse blown white with foam, running on heart alone, and on its back a figure in armor that had once been magnificent and was now a ruin: dented breastplate, a cloak burned away at one shoulder, and no banner anywhere, only a torn strip of white cloth knotted at the saddle where a standard should fly. She rode straight through the soldiers' line without slowing, and it was only when one of them lunged to grab her rein that the violence started.

Jude had seen violence before — boars, a bull gone mad, once a knife fight outside the Lowbrook tavern that everyone talked about for a decade. Nothing had prepared them for this. The woman fought like water finding cracks. A warhammer came off her back and a soldier's lance broke against it; her horse shoulder-checked another rider into the fence rail hard enough to crack wood; and when the leader drew steel and came at her properly, she met him with a stillness that was worse than speed, turning his blade with a small precise motion and putting her fist — armored, terrible — into the joint of his neck so he folded like wet cloth.

Four seconds. Perhaps five. Then she wheeled the bay around, put herself between the remaining soldiers and the village, and held.

"Hold," she said, and her voice carried like the bell had, like it was accustomed to being obeyed on battlefields larger than valleys. "You have orders for the apostate. You have no orders to die in a ditch in a sheep valley for a woman whose name won't be remembered. Think about which one your captain will thank you for."

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then the leader — groggy, bleeding from the ear, hauled upright by his fellows — looked at the knight, and at the sword in Jude's hands, which had stopped humming and begun to sing, a thin clear note like ice about to crack, and made the calculation that sensible men make.

"This isn't finished," he said. It sounded like a formality, a phrase he'd say again at some other crossroads over some other corpse. They gathered their wounded and wheeled and went down-valley at a canter, unhurried, insolent in their retreat, and the dust of them hung in the air long after the sound was gone.

* * *

She dismounted like a sack of grain that had learned dignity late in life. Up close the ruin of her armor told a longer story: burn scars along the pauldron, a sword-cut through the gorget that had missed the throat by the width of a prayer, mud dried in layers suggesting days of hard riding without rest. She was perhaps fifty, gray-shot, square-jawed, with eyes the color of slate and a way of looking at Jude that was less greeting than assessment.

"Dame Corvel Ashgrave," she said. "Once shield to Queen Isolde. Currently, according to the crown I don't recognize, a traitor." She glanced at the torn strip of white at her saddle. "Hence the lack of banner."

"You brought them here," Jude said. Anger arrived late and all at once, the way it always did. "Your chasing, their chasing — you led them up our road."

"Yes." No apology in it. "And in three days they'd have come anyway, because the woman behind you carries the only proof in the world of what Veyra truly is, and Veyra knows it." Corvel unbuckled her gauntlets, finger by finger, wincing. "I'd rather they arrive tired and angry than rested and certain. So. Yes. I led them here. I'd do it again. I'm sorry for it."

"She's sorry for it," Sera murmured, to no one, in a tone that suggested she believed neither of them entirely.

Maren and half the village had crept from the cellars by then, drawn by the silence, and stood clustered at the lane's end with pitchforks and kitchen knives held like props in a play none of them had auditioned for. Corvel took in the scene — the frightened faces, the broken fence, the burned smell where a lance had scorched the rail — and something in her jaw tightened.

"Bring me to your headman," she said. "What I have to say is for everyone. Because in about ten days, give or take, everything you believe about this valley becomes a lie someone else tells, and you deserve to hear it from me first."

* * *

They gathered in the meetinghouse because there was nowhere else large enough, and it was strange and awful how quickly the village remembered how to do this — benches dragged into rows, lamps lit though it was barely afternoon, the oldest families in the seats nearest the door because the oldest families always sat nearest the doors. Tam's absence struck Jude then — no. Wrong name, wrong chapter of the future; there was no Tam, not yet, just the Holloway children pressed against their mother's skirts. Jude shook the thought off like a fly. The last few days had filled their head with strangers' futures.

Corvel did not sit. She stood before them in her ruined armor, and she laid three things on the table between herself and the village: a signet ring heavy with gold; a letter sealed with the inverted-mountain sigil, unopened; and a bundle of oilcloth that clinked.

"My queen died fourteen months ago," she said. "Not of the fever they announced. Of poison, administered over months by her own regent, in doses too subtle for any physicker to catch. I know because I was her shield, and a shield's first failure is the body, and the body failed on my watch." Her voice stayed level, but the levelness cost her; Jude could see it costing her, muscle by muscle. "Veyra took the throne within the month. The council objected. The council now numbers three living members, and two are in exile, and I am one of them."

Someone in the back whispered a prayer to stars that weren't there anymore.

"Here is what the histories will never print." Corvel unwrapped the oilcloth, and inside lay shards — dark glassy fragments that drank the lamplight rather than reflecting it, each one etched with fine lines of script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts through closed eyelids. "Contracts. Soul-bonds, in the old tongue. I stole these from the archive beneath Veyra's own seat, at the cost of four good soldiers whose names I carry and will carry. And they say—" her hand, steady as a surgeon's, touched the largest fragment "—that eight nights before her coronation, Veyra rode beneath Mount Cindral and stood in the court that lives under it, and she sold her soul to buy the throne."

The silence afterward had weight, texture, temperature.

"The court beneath the mountain," Sera said quietly, from the back, and every head turned to her, and she did not flinch. "So it's real. I prayed to it for eleven years and told myself it was metaphor."

"It's real," Corvel said. "And here is the part that concerns you — concerns all of you, every soul under every sky." She lifted the largest shard to the lamplight. "A soul is not nothing. A soul is a binding. Every oath ever sworn, every promise kept, every love that outlives its body — souls are the knots that hold the pattern together. The stars themselves are bound alight by the great bindings. And when Veyra sold hers — when the under-court took a whole human soul into itself—"

She turned the shard, and the script caught the light, and for one instant Jude saw — or dreamed they saw — a shape in the darkness of the glass, vast and coiled and patient, like a thing asleep at the bottom of a well.

"—the knots slipped. All of them. Everywhere. The stars didn't go out. They were *unbound*." Corvel set the shard down with the gentleness of someone handling a sleeping child. "And the court beneath the mountain holds the stolen soul, and the stolen soul is the thread that can tie them back on. Which means the fate of every star in the sky is currently sitting in the treasury of the worst ruler this land has ever known, who does not know what she's holding, and would burn the world before letting anyone else touch it."

Old Maren rose from the front bench. She was eighty-one and walked with two sticks and feared nothing that bled. "And what would you have of us, Dame Traitor?"

"Not you." For the first time, Corvel Ashgrave's certainty cracked — not broke, but cracked, like ice over deep water. Her slate eyes found Jude, and the assessment in them resolved into something heavier: recognition, apology, pity. "The star-priest carries the shard. The shard is the key. But keys need hands, and hands need protection, and the road to the free cities runs through a burning country." She drew breath. "There is exactly one swordspeaker left in the world, and she is standing in this room."

Every face turned. Jude felt the oath-sword's warmth through the scabbard at their hip like a hand closing.

"I'm not asking tonight," Corvel said. "I'm asking you to sleep on it, knowing what sleeps beside you. But understand this much, and understand it fully: those outriders will report back. Veyra's armies will come up this valley within the week, and they will not leave it as they found it, whether or not the apostate is here. Your wards are dead. Your ancestors bought you two hundred years of peace, and the bill has come due — and it is addressed to you, Jude Winterbourne, because your family wrote it."

The lamps guttered. Outside, where stars should have been wheeling toward midnight, the sky lay black and absolute, and somewhere far down the valley road, so faint it might have been imagination, thunder rolled from a cloudless night.

Or hoofbeats. More of them. Many more.

Sera rose from the back bench, the star-shard glowing against her chest like a small stubborn dawn, and looked at Jude with an expression that was no longer wary trust but something decided.

"They're coming sooner than she thinks," Sera said softly. "We leave tomorrow."
