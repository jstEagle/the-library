## 5. The First Skirmish

The smoke came before the soldiers did.

Jude smelled it at dawn, two days after Corvel Ashgrave rode into Ironhollow with blood drying on her cloak and a dead queen's name in her mouth — a thin gray ribbon rising past the eastern ridge, where no chimney stood and no shepherd camped this late in the season. They were splitting kindling behind the cottage when the wind shifted and brought it to them: pine resin, horse lather, and under all of it something sweeter and fouler that they could not name until Sera stepped out beside them, went very still, and said, "Pitch arrows. They mean to burn as they come."

The axe handle creaked in Jude's grip. The wood was old ash, worn smooth by their father's hands and his father's before him, and for one absurd moment Jude wanted nothing more than to keep splitting kindling until the world made sense again — until the stars came back, until the wards were whole, until the word *usurper* meant only a story told at winter firesides.

"They'll reach the wardstone by midmorning," Sera said. Her voice was flat the way water is flat before it freezes. She had slept badly; the bruise along her jaw from the road had gone yellow-green, blooming like something seasonal. "Corvel's outriders will have slowed them, but slowed isn't stopped."

"Then we run," Jude said. It was not a question, and it was not brave, and they said it anyway because someone in this valley had to be honest about what they were. Farmers. Smiths. A miller with bad knees. One reluctant swordspeaker who had never spoken a sword in anger, or outside ritual, or — if they were being entirely truthful — ever at all.

Sera looked at them for a long moment. In the gray light her eyes held the color of the star-shard she kept wrapped in wool at her breast: that impossible silver, that small captive morning. "You can't run," she said gently. "You know that."

And the worst of it, the part Jude would carry like a stone in the boot for years afterward, was that they did know.

* * *

The village gathered at the wardstone by the time the sun cleared the ridge, which was to say: what remained of the village gathered, some forty souls out of three hundred, the rest having fled south through Shepherd's Gap in the night with carts and children and whatever the arms could carry. Those who stayed formed a loose, frightened crescent around the shattered stone — Old Marta with her hay-scythe, the twins Bren and Col who had never shaved, Widow Anselm holding a carving knife like a rosary. Behind them, the first of the fires had already been set in the barley fields, a desperate old tactic: burn your own crop, deny the enemy forage. The smoke of it rolled up thick and acrid and turned the morning the color of old bruises.

Corvel Ashgrave stood apart from them all, armored in piecemeal scavenged steel, her broken shield slung across her back like a penance. She had spent the night sharpening everything with an edge and speaking to no one, and now she looked at the road winding down from the pass and said, quietly, "They're early."

"How many?" Jude asked.

"I count sixty. Perhaps eighty behind." She said it the way a physician names a disease. No drama in it. Just arithmetic. "Veyra's vanguard doesn't travel light, but she won't spend her best on a valley of farmers. These will be second-line troops. Hungry ones." A pause. "Hungry troops are the dangerous kind."

Jude's hands would not stop shaking, so they pressed them flat against their thighs and thought about the oath-sword hanging at their hip — their grandmother's sword, the Winterbourne blade, plain-hilted and unadorned, the kind of weapon that looked like it had been made to be forgotten rather than admired. Two hundred years it had hung silent on the family wall. Two hundred years of harvest vigils where the eldest Winterbourne touched the pommel and spoke the binding words and felt — so the stories claimed — the valley's wards settle like a blanket over sleeping sheep.

Two days ago the words had hummed instead of settled. Two days ago the wards had shattered like a dropped glass, and the sword had sung one long, low note that no one else seemed to hear.

It was singing now. Faintly. Like a kettle beginning to think about boiling.

"You don't have to do this," Sera said at their shoulder. She had appeared without sound, the way she always did; even wounded, even limping, she moved like a rumor. "Sixty soldiers. One untried swordspeaker. I've done the arithmetic too, Jude. There are other ways. We could take the shard and go now, while they're still forming up—"

"And leave them?" Jude nodded at the crescent of villagers. At Old Marta, who had once fished them out of the millpond when they were seven and drowning, and who was now tightening her grip on a scythe meant for grain. At little Col, whose chin trembled but whose feet stayed planted. "You said it yourself. Hunters are coming for what's hidden here. If I walk away, they burn Ironhollow looking for it anyway."

"That's not your burden."

"It became my burden the night the stars went out." They heard their own voice and were startled by it — steady, low, older than they felt. Then the steadiness cracked, and honesty came through the crack. "I'm terrified, Sera. I want you to know that. I'm not brave. I just can't think of anything else to be."

For a moment Sera's hand hovered near theirs — almost touching, not quite — and then the first horn sounded down in the valley, and there was no more time for almost.

* * *

They came up the cart-road in column, and they were not monsters, and that was somehow worse.

Jude had expected — had needed, maybe — demons. Black armor, burning eyes, the court beneath the mountain leaking upward through mortal men. Instead they saw tired faces under dented helms, mud-caked boots, a standard-bearer who couldn't have been twenty carrying Veyra's new banner: the iron crown over the crossed keys, sewn crooked, the gold thread already tarnishing. Soldiers of the usurper. Boys mostly. Boys with pitch-pots and torches and orders to make an example of the valley that had harbored a traitor knight.

"Hold!" The captain's voice carried up the slope, rough with dust. He was a broad man with a gray-streaked beard and the weary authority of someone who had done this before and expected to do it many times more. "In the name of Queen Veyra, First of Her Name, Protector of the Nine Cities! Surrender the traitor Corvel Ashgrave and the foreign witch, and your valley will be spared! You have my word!"

Widow Anselm spat. Old Marta laughed, high and terrible, the laugh of someone who has buried a husband and three children and has no fear left to give. And Jude stepped forward — one step, two — until they stood alone between the crescent of Ironhollow and the column of the crown, with the broken wardstone at their back humming its absence and the sword at their hip humming its presence, and the two notes tangling in their chest like birds fighting in a sack.

"The valley refuses," Jude called back. Their voice shook on the first word and held on the rest. "Take your boys home, Captain. Nobody here wants to bury you."

Something flickered across the captain's face — regret, maybe, or just the professional sadness of a man watching a negotiation end. He raised his hand. The column halted. Somewhere down the line, a torch hissed against pitch.

"Last chance, swordspeaker," he said, and the title was mockery now, soft and poisonous. "There's no magic left in this dirt. Whatever your grandmother's blade used to whisper, it's done talking. We've all heard the tales. Wards fall, stars die. The old world's over."

And that — *the old world's over*, said with such easy certainty, such contempt — was the thing that reached down through Jude's terror and found something harder underneath.

"No," Jude said quietly. "It isn't."

They drew the sword.

Later, they would try to describe the sound it made leaving the scabbard, and fail. Not steel-on-leather. Something closer to a struck bell underwater, closer to a held breath released. The blade came free gleaming dull silver, unremarkable, and then the humming in Jude's chest rose up their throat and out through their teeth before they understood they were speaking —

"*Keir-vash-anneth.*"

Edge.

The word left their mouth and became the air itself. Jude felt it go — felt the syllables tear loose from somewhere deeper than language and pour down their arm, through their wrist, into the blade, and the dull silver woke. Light ran along the edge like frost racing across a windowpane, and when Jude swung the sword — a slow, wondering, practice-yard arc — the air parted with a sound like tearing silk, and thirty feet away a fence post toppled, cut clean through, though the blade had never come within ten feet of it.

Silence. On the hillside. In the column below. Even the torches seemed to lean away from their own flames.

"Oh," Jude breathed, staring at their own hands. "*Oh.*"

The captain recovered first, because captains must. "Loose!" he roared. "Archers, loose!"

The world narrowed. Arrows came up the slope in a hissing black arc, and Jude's body did what two hundred years of buried instinct demanded: they spoke again, faster now, the words arriving like memories of a language they'd never learned.

"*Skarn!*"

Shield. The word hardened into a shimmer above the villagers' heads, faint as heat-haze, and the arrows struck it and burst apart like moths on a lantern. The recoil staggered Jude to one knee; every word cost something, drew warmth out of their bones like a hearth drawing flame. But the villagers were alive. Bren and Col stared at the fading shimmer, at Jude kneeling in the scorched grass with a singing sword, and Old Marta said, in a voice of absolute calm, "Well. About time, child."

Then the line charged, and there was no more time for wonder.

* * *

What followed, Jude would remember in fragments for the rest of their life — in shards, the way the wards had shattered, the way a life shatters when it turns out to have been built on a floor of glass.

They remembered the captain coming up the slope with a two-handed hammer, and meeting him in the center of the burning barley, and the strange grief of it — because he fought honorably, telegraphing his swings, giving a farmer with a magic sword every courtesy of the duel, and Jude still had to speak the edge-word again and take his arm off at the elbow, and he screamed, and they knelt beside him in the smoke and bound the stump with their own sash while the battle crashed around them both, and he stared at them with fever-bright confusion and whispered, "Why — you're trying to save me? We came to kill you—" and Jude said, "I know. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry," and dragged him behind a stone wall where the fire wouldn't reach.

They remembered Sera moving through the melee like water finding cracks, not fighting but redirecting, turning wrists, whispering words that made men stumble and lose their nerve — and the star-shard at her breast glowing brighter with every passing minute, drinking something from the violence, and Jude filing that away in the growing cabinet of things they were afraid to ask.

They remembered Corvel Ashgrave at last unslinging her broken shield and being, for three minutes, the thing she had been forged to be. The disgraced knight moved through Veyra's line like a verdict. She did not speak any magic. She did not need to. When it was over her pauldrons were notched like a butcher's tally, and she stood in the trampled barley breathing hard, and she did not look at anyone, and Jude understood that she was counting the men she'd killed and would be counting them for a long time.

They remembered the moment the line broke — really broke, the way lines break not when they are beaten but when they decide, collectively and wordlessly, that the story they were told is not the story that is true. The story was: a burned-out valley, a toothless swordspeaker, an easy example made. The truth was: a young person standing in the fire-light with a word-blade, cutting the air itself, weeping openly as they fought. Boys do not stay to fight truths like that. They ran, dropping torches and hammers, and the captain's surviving lieutenants blew the retreat, and the column dissolved back up the cart-road toward the pass like sugar in rain.

And they remembered — this most of all, this forever — standing in the ruins of the skirmish, victorious, barely, blood-spattered and shaking, as the wind changed direction and showed them what the pitch-pots had done.

The fire had jumped the plowed furrows. It had found the thatch. It was eating the village.

"No," Jude said. "No, no—" They ran. They spoke the shield-word until their vision grayed and their nose bled freely down their lip, throwing shimmer after shimmer over rooftops, and it was like bailing the sea with a cup. A spark had ridden the wind into the granary. The mill took next, that beautiful old mill with the wheel that had turned since before the wards were raised. They saved perhaps six houses out of forty. They saved the well and the shrine and Old Marta's cottage because Marta herself stood on her roof beating sparks away with a wet blanket, cursing with the fluency of generations.

By dusk, half of Ironhollow was charcoal and the other half was ash-dusted and silent. The survivors stood in the lane among the drifting flakes, and nobody cheered, because there was nothing to cheer. Sixty soldiers routed. Half a village gone. The barley that would have fed them through winter: smoke. And up on the ridge, the enemy was regrouping, and everyone knew — the way animals know weather — that sixty had been a probing finger, and the fist was still coming.

Jude sat down in the road. Just sat, right there in the muck and ash, the oath-sword across their knees, still faintly warm, still faintly singing. Their hands would not stop trembling now, and they let them.

Sera knelt in front of them. She smelled of smoke and old incense. "You saved them," she said. "Most of them."

"I killed people." The words came out small. "That captain. Others. I could feel every word leave me like — like pulling thread out of my own seams, and I kept doing it anyway, and I *liked* it, Sera. For a second each time, I liked it. The power fit me like a glove." They looked up, and their vision swam. "Is that what it means? Being the last swordspeaker? That it fits?"

Sera held their gaze, and did not flinch, and did not lie. "Maybe," she said. "Or maybe the fitting is why it's the last. Why your ancestor built this valley and hid the sword behind wards for two hundred years." Her hand rose, hesitated, then settled warm on their shoulder. "We should talk about your ancestor, Jude. Soon. There are things in the shard I didn't understand until tonight. Things I saw in how the words move when you speak them."

"Tomorrow," Jude said. "Let me be ordinary until tomorrow."

Above them, the sky went dark the way it had gone dark every night since the stars died — total, velvety, wrong. But far to the east, past the smoking pass, past the retreating army, past all the miles between here and the capital where a woman wearing a stolen soul sat a stolen throne, a single point of light trembled at the horizon's edge.

Not a star. Smaller. Colder. Moving.

Sera saw it too. Her hand tightened on Jude's shoulder, and her voice, when it came, was the flattest Jude had ever heard it.

"That's not a relief column," she said. "That's a hunter. And it's found the scent."
