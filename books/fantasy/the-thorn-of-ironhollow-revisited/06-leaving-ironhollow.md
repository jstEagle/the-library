## 6. Leaving Ironhollow

Sera had counted the dead twice, and both times the number had come back wrong — not because she miscounted, but because grief kept rearranging the arithmetic. Eleven. No, twelve; old Marta had died in the night and no one had thought to tell her until dawn. Twelve souls out of a village of two hundred, and somehow that felt like the cruelest kind of mercy: small enough to bear, large enough never to forget.

She stood in the smoke-hazed morning outside the meeting house, where the survivors had gathered to decide what remained of their lives. The air smelled of wet ash and charred barley, of the particular sweetness that followed a burn — the smell of things that had been food or home becoming neither. Above it all hung the sky, still starless in daylight the way skies always were, but Sera could feel the absence up there like a missing tooth. The stars had not come back. Whatever Veyra had done beneath the mountain, it held.

Inside, the village argued.

She could hear them through the plank walls — voices worn raw by three days of fear. Some wanted to stay. Of course they did. This was Ironhollow; the valley had kept them safe for two hundred years, and even broken wards left bones behind, foundations, root cellars, graves with names they knew. Others wanted to run east before Veyra's armies came back in force, because everyone understood now that the first skirmish had only been scouts. The soldiers who burned the village had been a probing finger. The fist was still coming.

And in the middle of it all sat Jude Winterbourne, last swordspeaker of Ironhollow, looking like someone who had been asked to hold up the sky and was quietly astonished to find it heavier than expected.

Sera slipped inside and took a place against the back wall, where the shadows pooled thickest. She had learned, in her years among the under-court's acolytes, the value of standing where firelight did not reach. Old habits. Old faith. She was trying very hard not to be either of those things anymore.

"—and what would we even carry?" a farmer named Osk was saying, his hands still black with soot from digging his neighbor out of a collapsed barn. "The valley is our living. You pull a man out of his valley, you might as well pull the roots off a turnip."

"The valley didn't save us," said a woman Sera didn't know, her voice cracking on every third word. "The valley *hid* us, and hiding's over. They found us. They'll keep finding us."

"Then we rebuild the wards," someone else offered, desperate. "Jude can rebuild the wards. Jude's the swordspeaker—"

All eyes turned, the way water finds a low place. Jude stood near the front, and Sera watched them take the weight of two hundred gazes and try, visibly, not to buckle under it. There was ash in Jude's hair. There had been ash in Jude's hair for three days; Sera suspected it had become permanent, worked into the scalp like a second color.

"I can't rebuild the wards." Jude's voice was quiet, but the room hushed for it anyway. That was the thing about being the last of anything — people leaned toward you whether you deserved it or not. "I don't know how they were made. I don't think anyone alive knows how they were made. And—" Jude hesitated, and Sera saw the truth arrive in their face a moment before it arrived in their words, the way pain arrives before blood. "And I don't think the wards failed on their own. Dame Corvel has seen things. Documents. I think someone sold them."

The room erupted. Sera let it. She watched Jude stand in the middle of the noise like a post in a flood, and she felt something she hadn't let herself feel since fleeing the under-court: guilt, sharp and specific. She had known about the sold soul for months. She had run instead of warning anyone. Every burned roof in this valley was downstream of her silence, and she carried that knowledge the way you carry a stone in your boot — privately, constantly, with a limp you learn to disguise.

It was Corvel who brought order, finally, by the simple expedient of standing up. The disgraced knight filled a doorway the way weather fills a horizon; even stripped of her banner and her order, even with the brand of traitor seared into her cheek, she had a presence that made argument feel briefly foolish.

"Here is what I know," Corvel said, and her voice carried the flat certainty of someone who had stopped hoping to be believed and started simply stating facts. "Veyra marches with four thousand. She will be here within the month, perhaps sooner. She wants the shard this woman carries" — a gauntleted hand indicated Sera without warmth — "because the shard is a piece of the binding that holds the stars alight, and whoever holds the binding holds the throne. If you stay, some of you will die. Not all. Armies are inefficient at slaughter. But some, and the ones who die will be the slowest, the oldest, the ones who couldn't run when running mattered."

"And if we go?" Osk again. "Go where? On what?"

"East. To the free cities. Marrowgate first, then Aurelspire, where the cities' council still meets, still argues, still believes — gods help them — that negotiation is possible." Corvel's mouth twisted. "They'll need convincing. They'll need a witness. Someone who stood in a burning village and can say: this is what Veyra's peace looks like."

Silence settled over the meeting house like snowfall. Sera heard the fire pop in the hearth. Somewhere outside, a child was crying in the thin, exhausted way of children who have cried themselves empty and started again from habit.

The vote, when it came, was not really a vote. It was more like a tide going out. Hands rose to stay — perhaps sixty of them, mostly older folk, the ones whose whole selves were rooted here. Hands rose to go — fewer, but younger, and carrying children. And in between stood the great gray mass of the undecided, waiting to see which way the current pulled.

In the end, maybe ninety would leave. The rest would stay and rebuild, and pray that the war passed over them, and bury their dead in the churchyard where their grandparents lay, which was its own kind of answer and Sera could not fault it. Home is not a place you argue people out of. She had tried once, in another valley, with another doomed congregation. It hadn't worked then either.

What mattered — what made her chest ache with something too tangled to name — was that when the question of the shard came up, when Corvel said plainly that the shard must reach the free cities and that the road would kill anyone weak or slow, three names stood together at the center of the room as if they'd rehearsed it.

Herself. Corvel. And Jude.

"You don't have to," Sera murmured to Jude afterward, in the doorway, while the village dissolved around them into packing and weeping and farewell. "You're not bound to this. Your oath doesn't cover—"

"My oath covers exactly one thing," Jude said. Their hand drifted to the sword at their hip — the oath-sword, the heirloom, the blade that had hummed on the night the stars went out and had not been fully silent since. "It says I speak for the valley. Well. The valley just voted." A ghost of a smile, tired and crooked. "This is what speaking sounds like."

* * *

They left on the fourth morning, before full light, because Corvel insisted that armies move at dawn and therefore fugitives should move earlier.

Sera walked at the rear, as she had walked at the rear of processions her whole acolyte life, and watched Ironhollow shrink behind them. It was a beautiful valley even ruined — she would give it that. The river ran silver through the middle of it, indifferent to everything, and the terraced fields climbed the slopes in green steps, and the rebuilt half of the village already looked stubbornly permanent, new timber bright against the char. Smoke rose from chimneys of the ones who stayed. Someone was ringing the chapel bell, though there was no service, no reason; they rang it because the bell was theirs and ringing it was a way of saying *we remain.*

Ninety exiles strung out along the east road, carts and bundles and children on shoulders. Most would peel away at the crossroads, scattering to cousins and old friends in towns that had never heard of Ironhollow. Only three were going all the way east. Three, and a fragment of a fallen star wrapped in wool in Sera's pack, humming faintly against her spine like a sleeping bird.

Jude walked beside her. They had been quiet most of the morning, and Sera recognized the quality of the quiet. It was the quiet of someone standing at the edge of something.

"The wardline," Jude said finally. "Where exactly is it?"

"Ahead. Maybe an hour." Sera nodded toward a fold in the hills where the road crested between two bald shoulders of stone. From the outside, the border of Ironhollow looked like nothing — a saddle between hills, a change in the wind. Two hundred years ago it had been the strongest working of concealment-magic on the continent, and now it was a scar you could only see from certain angles, in certain light, the way you can sometimes see the ghost of an old injury when the sun hits it right. "You've crossed it before? As a child?"

"To the high pastures. Never noticed anything." Jude's hand rested on the sword hilt, not gripping, just touching — the way you touch a railing. "Will I notice now?"

Sera considered lying, decided against it. Lying was the faith she was trying to renounce. "Yes. What's coming for you on the other side of that line — magic isn't a thing that lives out there, Jude. It's a thing that's been kept *out of here.* The valley wasn't shielding you from danger. It was shielding you from *everything.* When you cross, it's all going to arrive at once. Every thread, every current, every whisper the world makes when it's awake." She paused. "For someone with your blood, your oath, whatever sleeps in that sword — it may be a great deal."

Jude looked at her sidelong. "Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"No. Comfort is a priest's tool. I'm not a priest anymore." But she softened it with a small shrug. "It's supposed to prepare you. There's a difference. Priests want you calm. I want you upright."

Ahead, the column of exiles reached the crest of the saddle and began, one by one, to pass over it. Sera watched for it — she always watched for it, the moment of crossing, the little stagger travelers never knew to expect. The miller's boy went through and shivered like a dog shaking off water. A grandmother crossed and pressed her hand to her sternum and laughed, startled, at nothing. Each of them stepped out of two hundred years of silence and into the loud world, and none of them knew that was what they were doing.

Then it was Jude's turn.

Sera saw it happen and knew she would remember it for the rest of her life, however long the under-court allowed that to be.

Jude stopped at the line — actually stopped, one foot raised, like a wader at the edge of cold surf — and Sera watched the color leave their face and rush back twice as deep. Their breath went out of them in a sound that was almost a word and almost a prayer and almost a curse. Their free hand flew out and found Sera's shoulder and gripped it hard enough to bruise, and she let them, because she remembered what it was like, her own first crossing, the day she fled the court with her faith burning behind her like a city.

Magic flooded into Jude Winterbourne like cold water into a dry well.

She could see it — not with eyes, exactly, but with the trained inner sense she had spent twenty years cultivating and had sworn she'd never use again. The valley had been a held breath, and Jude had lived their whole life inside that held breath, and now the breath released and the world poured in. Threads of it. Currents. The road itself was faintly ensorcelled, old pilgrim-work, and the hills beyond were threaded with wilder stuff, hedge-craft and hearth-charms and the vast slow pressure of the mountains' own dreaming. All of it arriving in Jude at once, filling them, finding the hollow places — and there was a hollow place in Jude, Sera realized, a shaped and waiting emptiness like a lock, and the magic poured into it and *turned.*

The oath-sword sang.

Not hummed. Sang — a single clear note, silver and terrible, that made the nearest exiles flinch and look around for its source. Frost feathered along the blade despite the morning's mildness. And Jude stood in the middle of it with their eyes wide open and tears cutting tracks through the ash on their face, and Sera could not tell — she genuinely could not tell — whether they were weeping from grief or wonder, and she suspected Jude couldn't either.

"There you are," Sera whispered, and did not know if she meant the magic or the person.

Corvel turned her horse — she rode point, armored, watchful — and looked back at the sound of the singing steel, and her expression did something complicated. Recognition, Sera thought. The knight had served a queen who wielded artifacts of state, and she knew what power looked like when it woke up in someone who hadn't asked for it.

"It doesn't stop," Jude said. Their voice shook. "Does it — does it ever get quieter?"

"No," Sera said honestly. "But you learn which voices matter. Give it time. Grieve it. It deserves grieving — you're losing the world you were born in, even if you're walking out of it on your own feet." She hesitated, then added the truer thing underneath: "And it's not only loss. Look east, Jude. Tell me what you see."

Jude looked east. Beyond the saddle the land fell away in long folds of forest and field toward the distant glitter of the lowland rivers, and above it all — Sera watched Jude's face as they saw it — the sky went on forever, huge and unhidden, and somewhere in its blue depth, invisible by day but suddenly, unmistakably *present*, the absent stars left a shape of absence that was almost worse than darkness. A constellation of holes. A map of everything stolen.

"They're really gone," Jude said softly. "I always thought that was a story. Stars you could lose." Their hand tightened on the sword, and the singing faded to a hum, to a whisper, to something that lived in the steel the way a heartbeat lives in a wrist. "Whoever did this — Veyra, the court, whoever sold my valley's safety — they took the sky down. For everyone. Everywhere."

"Yes."

"Then we go get it back."

There it was. Sera had been waiting for it for three days, watching for the moment the reluctant villager became something else — not a hero, she didn't believe in heroes, heroes were a priestly fiction like comfort — but a *witness with a weapon.* Someone who had seen the wound and could not unsee it. The free cities would need that. The council at Aurelspire would sit in its marble hall and debate tariffs and borders until the end of the world unless someone stood in front of them who had breathed the smoke of a burned village and sung the song of a waking sword.

"Then we walk," Sera said, and shouldered the pack with its sleeping fragment of starlight, and stepped over the wardline herself.

The crossing hit her too, as it always did — the sudden loudness of the world, magic lapping at her like a tide she had once swum in and now only waded. She was an exile twice over: out of the under-court, out of Ironhollow, belonging nowhere except to the road ahead and the two people walking it beside her. Behind her, the bell in the valley rang on, growing smaller, growing sweeter, until the wind took it entirely.

Exile, she thought, was a strange country. It hurt like a wound and opened like a door, and you lived in both places at once for the rest of your days.

They crested the last hill at midmorning, and the road unspooled eastward through country none of them had ever seen — and there, far off at the edge of sight, where the forests darkened toward the passes, Sera saw something that stopped her breath and made her grip the strap of her pack until her knuckles went white.

Riders. Many riders. Moving fast along the eastern road, banners snapping, straight toward the crossroads where the exiles would scatter.

Corvel saw them a heartbeat later and swore with the fluency of a soldier. "Outriders. Veyra's vanguard is ahead of schedule." She wheeled her horse, voice dropping to iron. "We can't outrun them on foot, and we can't lead them to the others. Off the road — now. Into the trees. And pray whatever gods listen that the shard stays quiet."

Against Sera's spine, faint and warm as a heartbeat, the starlight began to hum.
