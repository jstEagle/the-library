## 7. The Road of Broken Bells

They rode east for three days before Corvel allowed them a road at all.

Before that, there had been the trees — two days of them, dark and close, the horses' hooves wrapped in sacking so the sound of their passing would not carry. Corvel led by dead reckoning and old habit, cutting across streams where she could, avoiding the shepherd tracks that looked safe because they were easy. Easy was a word written in other people's blood. She had learned that lesson under a different queen, on a different border, and it had cost her everything she had once been proud to wear.

Now she wore no colors at all. A plain mail shirt beneath a farmer's cloak gone gray with road dust. No banner, no badge, no blazon. When she caught her reflection in still water — and she avoided still water, mostly — she saw a broad woman with iron-shot hair cropped short for practicality, a face like a door left open too long in the weather. *The Knight Without a Banner*, Sera had called her once, half teasing, and Corvel had not laughed.

On the third morning the trees thinned, and beyond them lay the lowlands: a country of hedgerows and mill-races and small stone villages, the kind of country that fed armies without ever being asked. It should have been green. It was the wrong time of year for anything but green.

It was brown instead. Scorched in patches. And silent.

Corvel reined in at the tree line and studied it the way she'd once studied battlefields — for what moved, and for what didn't.

"Roads are clear," she said finally. "That's not good."

"Clear roads usually mean someone's using them," Sera said. She sat her borrowed horse badly, one hand pressed to her ribs where the wound from Ironhollow's threshold had only just closed, the other resting against the satchel where the star-shard slept against her spine. "Or clearing them."

"Both." Corvel nudged her mare forward. "We ride the ditches and we keep our mouths shut. Marrowgate's six days if the bridges hold."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we learn to swim, priestess, and I'll apologize to your god personally."

Sera made a sound that might have been a laugh in a healthier throat. Jude said nothing. The boy — no, not a boy; Corvel kept having to correct herself, though Jude Winterbourne carried youth like an ill-fitting coat, all elbows and wariness — rode behind with the oath-sword slung across their back, and every hour or so Corvel would catch them touching the hilt through the cloth, the way you touch a bruise to check whether it still hurts.

It did. She knew that kind of hurt. She'd been carrying her own for eleven years.

* * *

The first broken bell they found outside a village called Harrowmere, or what the signpost claimed was Harrowmere.

The village itself was half-burned and wholly empty — doors open, a cradle overturned in one doorway, bread gone blue with mold on a windowsill as if the whole place had simply stepped out of its own life midmorning and never come back. But it was the churchyard that stopped them.

The bell tower stood intact. The bell did not. Someone had climbed into the belfry with ropes and fire and pried the great bronze tongue out of its housing, then split the bell itself down the middle like a fruit, hauling the pieces down on sledges. The scar of the work remained — pale wood, cracked stone, the mounting yoke hanging empty and absurd, swinging faintly in a wind that had nothing else left to move.

"They took it," Jude said quietly. "A bell. Who takes a *bell*?"

"Veyra takes bells." Corvel dismounted and walked the churchyard slowly, reading the ground. "Bronze goes into cannon. Temple bronze especially — it rings true, it casts clean. Her foundries have been eating the gods out of the sky for two years." She crouched beside a sledge-track pressed deep in the mud, still sharp-edged. "Recent. Days, not weeks."

"But why the temples?" Sera had come to stand among the graves, and her voice had changed — thinner, careful. "There's plenty of bronze in the world. Kettles. Statues."

"Because it isn't about the bronze." Corvel rose and brushed the mud from her knee. "I've seen her requisition orders, back when I still had clearance to see such things. Every bell comes with the same annotation. *Render mute.* Not melt down — render mute. The cannons are real, but they're the excuse. She wants a country where nothing rings. Nothing calls people together. Nothing marks the hours except her drums."

Sera's hand had gone to the satchel again, unconsciously, the way a person touches a wound. "And the stars," she said softly. "She wants a country where nothing shines either."

Corvel looked at her and did not answer, because the answer was yes, and yes was a country Corvel had helped build before she understood the shape of it.

They camped that night in the shell of a tithe barn, no fire, cold rations. In the dark, Corvel heard Jude ask Sera, very low, what the under-court wanted with silence, and heard Sera say, "Silence is how it sleeps. Loud things wake it," and heard Jude lie awake a long time after that. Corvel stared at the roof beams and did not sleep at all, which was normal, and told herself the guilt was just an old companion come walking, which was also normal, and almost believed it.

* * *

On the fifth day they came to the river crossing at a town called Bellfound, which had once, Corvel guessed, been named for better reasons.

The bridge was gone — burned at the far end, cut at the near end, the stumps black and dripping. But the town had a ferryman's landing, and the landing had a boat, and the boat had an old man in it who looked at Corvel's sword-hilt the way men look at weather coming.

"Three coppers each," he said. "Four if the priestess is bleeding."

"She's not bleeding."

"Everyone from the east is bleeding somewhere. It's the fashion this season." He spat over the side, philosophically. "Coppers."

While Sera counted out coins with hands that shook slightly — she was still not eating enough, Corvel noted, and filed it away beside the other small worries she refused to speak aloud — Corvel scanned the far bank. Smoke rose from chimneys there, ordinary cook-smoke, and a market square showed bunting, faded but present. Life, persisting. It always did. That was the thing the ballads never managed: not the glory, just the stubbornness of people boiling water while empires fell.

"The free cities are past that?" Jude asked. Their voice had developed a habit of staying quiet, as if loudness might attract the same attention the bells had.

"Marrowgate's three days past. Then Aurelspire, if we're lucky enough to be refused there too."

"Refused?"

"You'll see." Corvel took the bow thwart, leaving the young ones amidships. "Free cities are free the way cats are domesticated. They'll feed us and argue about whether to help until Veyra's at the gates, and then argue about whose fault the gates were."

The river carried them across in silence except for water. Halfway over, the old man jerked his chin at the eastern horizon, where a smudge stood against the afternoon.

"Foundry smoke," he said. "You hear it some nights, when the wind's right. Like a bell ringing wrong. All the bells in the world, melted down into one long scream." He rowed. "My wife wouldn't leave, when the soldiers came for Saint Adelheid's bell. Stood in the churchyard and sang at them. They let her sing. Took the bell anyway. Said the Lady Veyra has no quarrel with singers." His jaw worked. "Just with things that ring true."

Corvel watched him row and thought: *I served that woman. I held her shield. I swore on her name before I knew what she'd sworn away.*

The guilt walked up and sat down beside her, familiar as an old hound, and she let it, because pushing it off in front of witnesses meant questions, and questions meant answers, and answers were the one cargo heavier than the shard.

But the river was long, and the old man's shoulders bent and straightened, bent and straightened, and somewhere in the middle of the crossing Corvel found herself saying, to the water more than to anyone:

"I knew. Some of it."

Jude looked up. Sera went very still.

"Not the soul-selling. Not — whatever she became under that mountain. But I knew about the foundry edicts, and the temple seizures, and I told myself it was wartime necessity, because I loved her." The word came out rusty. "Not the way the songs mean. The way a shield loves the arm it's strapped to. Queen Maren named me to the Queensguard at nineteen, and when Veyra — " She stopped. Started again, flatter. "When Maren died and Veyra took the regency, I stayed. Told myself someone loyal had to stay close to the throne, in case. In case of what, I couldn't have said. In case she needed stopping, I suppose. And then the night the wards broke — the night the stars died — I was standing guard outside her council chamber, and I heard her give thanks. Not to any god above. *Downward.* And I understood that the case had come, years ago, and I had spent it polishing armor instead of drawing steel."

The boat scraped gravel. The old man said nothing, holding the painter, watching them with the patience of someone who had ferried many confessions across this water and expected to ferry many more.

"That's why there's no banner," Corvel finished. "Not because they took it. Because I burned it myself, the night I rode west. You can't desert a queen you helped make."

No one spoke. Then Sera shifted, wincing, and said, "I ran from my faith instead of fighting it. Eleven years I called it survival. It was cowardice wearing a pilgrim's boots." She looked at the shard-satchel between her feet. "Perhaps we're all deserters of something. Perhaps that's what a found family is — the people who deserted the same war."

Jude, who had lost a home rather than abandoned one, said only, "The village sang too. At the harvest vigils. Before." And looked at the water.

Corvel felt something loosen in her chest that she distrusted immediately and completely, because hope was just another way of giving hostages to fortune, and she had sworn off hostage-giving along with banners.

* * *

They made the far bank as the light failed, and camped in a hollow behind a collapsed dovecote, and that was where the second bell found them — or rather its echo.

It came at moonrise: a sound rolling across the lowlands from the south, vast and cracked and wrong, a bronze voice with a hole torn in its throat. Once. Twice. A third time, ragged, defiant, dying.

"Somebody's bell," the ferryman had said. "Somebody always rings one, after. Takes the soldiers a week to find it."

In the hollow, the three of them lay in the dark and listened to it fade, and none of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the bell hadn't already said better.

It was Jude who finally moved — sat up, drew the oath-sword partway from its sheath, and looked at the exposed span of steel as though waiting for it to hum again, the way it had the night the stars went out. It stayed silent. Jude resheathed it slowly, and Corvel saw their face in the moonglow and recognized the expression: someone deciding, alone and irreversibly, what they were willing to become.

"We reach Marrowgate tomorrow," Corvel said, to break it, because breaking silences was the one duty she had left. "Sleep."

But sleep did not come, and near dawn it was Sera who woke them both with a hand on each shoulder and a voice stripped of everything but fact.

"Riders on the ridge," she whispered. "Torchlight. And they're not moving past us."

Corvel rolled to the lip of the hollow and counted torches — eight, maybe nine, fanning out down the slope with the unhurried discipline of hunters who had already run their quarry to ground. At their head, a rider bore a lantern high, and in its light Corvel caught the gleam of a device she knew too well: a bell, split down the middle, stamped in black iron.

Veyra's bell-breakers. The ones who silenced things that rang true.

Beside her, against Sera's spine, faint and warm as a heartbeat, the star-shard began to hum.
