## 8. Freehold of Marrowgate

The first thing Jude smelled was candle smoke — thousands upon thousands of candles, their wax and wicks burning sweet over the canals, so that the whole city seemed to breathe perfume. They had come down off the salt marshes at dusk, footsore and hollow-eyed, following the road as it sank between levees toward a horizon stitched with lamplight, and now they stood on the great sluice-gate bridge while Marrowgate unfolded below them like something from a story Sera might have told around a fire.

Water everywhere. That was the truth of the city. Canals ran through it like veins through a wrist — broad avenues of black, starless water where barges slid without sound, and narrow cuttings where laundry lines sagged between leaning houses and old women leaned out to shout at boatmen in voices like gulls. Every bridge wore its own constellation of lanterns. Every window held a flame. And above it all rose the candle markets, tiered terraces of stalls climbing the hillsides at the city's heart, ten thousand points of fire that almost — *almost* — made up for the empty sky.

Jude caught themself looking up anyway. Three weeks on the road and still the habit held: tilt the head back, search for stars, find nothing. The darkness overhead was total, a lid pressed down on the world. Corvel had stopped apologizing for it somewhere past the second ruined town.

"Close your mouth," Corvel said. "You look like a farmboy."

"I am a farm person."

"You look like one, then." The knight sat her horse with the ease of someone who had forgotten how to be anything but mounted; she'd barely let the animal rest since the Road of Broken Bells. Her armor was gone — traded two towns back for grain and silence — and without it she looked smaller, grayer, a woman of middling years with a dueling scar through one eyebrow and eyes that catalogued every rooftop before deciding any of them were safe. None of them were safe. She said so with a jerk of her chin. "Candle markets draw pilgrims, merchants, spies. Veyra's coin moves through cities like this faster than plague."

"Then why are we here?" Jude asked.

"Because Sera is about to collapse off that mule, and because the shard needs a reader, and readers need libraries, and libraries need cities." Corvel dismounted stiffly, one hand pressed to ribs that had never quite healed from the skirmish at the valley's mouth. "And because I promised you walls between you and the world, and these are the thickest ones left."

Sera slid off the mule without being asked to, which was how Jude knew how bad it was. She stood swaying on the bridge, gray-faced, one hand curled tight around the satchel where the star-shard rode wrapped in wool and oilcloth against her chest. Up close, in the lantern-light, Jude could see the shard's glow leaking through the fabric seams — a faint cold shimmer, like moonlight trapped under skin. Three weeks and Jude had learned to read that light the way farmers read clouds. Bright meant safe distance. Dim meant danger near. Tonight it pulsed slow and steady, a sleeping heartbeat.

*It knows we're home,* Sera had said once, on the road. *Starlight always knows.*

"It's beautiful," Sera said now, quietly. She was looking not at the markets but at the canals, at a barge stacked with cut flowers making its way toward the terraces, at a girl on the stern scattering petals into the black water. "I forgot cities could be beautiful."

"You fled an underground court," Jude said. "I imagine most things look beautiful after that."

"That's not what I mean." Sera's voice had the careful flatness she used when a memory pressed too close. "The under-court has no light that isn't stolen. Everything down there shines with someone else's fire. This—" she gestured at the terraces, the bridges, the ten thousand candles bought and paid for by ordinary hands, "—this is light people made themselves. For each other." She swayed again.

Corvel caught her elbow. "Inn first. Philosophy after sleep."

They took rooms at a canal-side house called the Drowned Bell, chosen by Corvel for the quality of its back stairs rather than the softness of its beds. The innkeeper, a broad woman with dye-stained fingers, asked no questions beyond the price, which was steep, and the length of stay, which was open. Marrowgate's neutrality was famous, she said, tapping the counter where three different currencies lay jumbled together. The free cities did not ask who you were. Only what you could pay.

That night Jude slept for twelve hours and woke to the smell of frying fish and the sound of Sera already awake below, murmuring to herself over the shard. By the second morning a plan had formed, the way plans did when Corvel made them: Sera would seek out the Athenaeum of the Terraces, the candle-market library, where scholars kept records older than Veyra's usurpation. Corvel would move among the veterans' halls and taverns, listening for news of armies and musters. And Jude —

Jude would carry the shard.

It had been decided over Sera's objection and Corvel's shrug. If hunters came for the starlight, they would look for the priest who'd carried it across half the known world, not for the tall, unremarkable stranger from nowhere who walked the markets buying candles like anyone else. So Jude went out into Marrowgate with a dead sword on their hip and a living star against their ribs, and tried very hard to be nobody.

It was harder than it sounded. Ironhollow had taught them invisibility of a kind — the village had been small enough that everyone knew everyone, and strangers stood out — but Marrowgate inverted the lesson. Here, *everyone* was a stranger. Crowds moved along the canal paths like schools of fish, and Jude moved among them, and no one looked twice, and there was a freedom in that that frightened them more than being watched ever had. In Ironhollow, being seen had been a form of safety. Here, unseen, they might as well not exist.

On the third day they found the black market.

Corvel had described it obliquely — *the underside of the terraces, where the candlelight doesn't reach* — and even so Jude nearly missed it: a stair leading down beneath the lowest market tier, into vaulted cellars where the canals had been roofed over generations ago. Down there the air was thick with tallow and secrets, and the light came from lamps shuttered to slits, and goods changed hands that had no business existing: relics looted from burned temples, letters bearing forged seals, a cage of songbirds whose songs, the seller whispered, could imitate any voice you named. Veyra's melted bells had ended up here too, Jude saw — recast into lockplates and knife-hilts, holy bronze sold by the ounce.

Jude hadn't wanted to come. But Sera needed a particular ink — star-ink, silver-ground, used for reading celestial script — and the only place in Marrowgate that would stock such a thing without asking questions was the underside. So Jude went, hating every stair, keeping one hand on the satchel strap, telling themself that the shard's steady glow meant nothing had found them yet.

The stall they wanted was near the far end, wedged between a fence for stolen signet rings and a woman selling bottled breath — actual breath, captured at the moment of dying, or so she claimed. The ink-seller was a thin man with spectacles made of smoked glass and hands that never stopped moving. He asked no questions either. He simply weighed out the ink in a tiny glass vial, wrapped it, and held it out — and then his gaze snagged, just for a moment, on the satchel at Jude's hip.

Just a moment. Just the way his head tilted, the way his nostrils flared, the way a hound marks a scent on the wind.

Jude paid and walked away slowly, not hurrying, heart hammering. Behind them the market noise swallowed everything. Above, through the cellar grates, candlelight fell in golden bars onto the wet stone floor, and Jude stepped through those bars of light and shadow and told themself the man had seen nothing.

The glow of the shard, when Jude checked it in an alley a hundred paces on, had begun to pulse faster.

* *

Tam found Jude the next morning, or rather Tam found the space next to Jude and occupied it, the way weather occupies a valley.

Jude was sitting on the edge of a canal eating fried dough from a paper cone, watching barges, trying to decide whether the ink-seller's glance had been paranoia or catastrophe, when a voice at their elbow said, "You're being followed."

Jude turned. A child stood there — eleven, maybe twelve, all elbows and sharp chin, hair cropped raggedly short, wearing a coat three sizes too big with pockets sewn into the lining, dozens of them, bulging with an inventory of other people's property. The kid's eyes were quick and brown and utterly unafraid.

"Since when?" Jude asked, because arguing with the statement felt less useful than interrogating it.

"Since yesterday. Gray coat, red scarf, walks with a limp he doesn't have. He followed you down the under-stairs and then he went straight to a counting-house on Silverquay and sent a pigeon." The kid held out a grimy hand. "Name's Tam."

Jude didn't take the hand. "How do you know about the pigeon?"

"Because I steal pigeons." Tam said it the way another child might say *I can swim*. "Messages cost a copper apiece at the roosts. Stealing them costs nothing. I've got six in my coat right now. One of them says—" the kid screwed up their face, recalling, "—*'Item located, Candlemarket district, traveling with armed escort, request instruction.'*" Tam shrugged. "Request denied, probably. Pigeons fly both ways. Whoever sent it will get an answer tomorrow."

For a long moment Jude couldn't speak. The fried dough had turned to lead in their stomach. Somewhere behind them a barge horn sounded, low and mournful, and the candle markets glittered on the hillside as if nothing in the world were wrong.

"Why tell me?" Jude managed finally. "What's it worth to you?"

"Nothing." Tam's chin lifted, defiant. Then, quieter: "Not nothing. You looked at me like I was a person. Most grown folk look through us like we're rats. And—" the defiance cracked, just slightly, "—there's kids. Under the terraces. Nine of us. If soldiers come through the underside asking questions, they don't ask nice. I've seen what Veyra's men do to places like ours."

Something in Jude's chest twisted. They thought of Ironhollow burning, of villagers scattered, of the weight of being the last of anything. They thought of Sera saying *light people made themselves, for each other*, and here was a child made entirely of other people's lost property, offering loyalty for the price of being looked at.

"Can you lose him?" Jude asked. "The gray coat?"

"I can lose anyone." Tam grinned, gap-toothed. "Question is whether you want him lost, or lost-and-watched. Watched is better. Watched tells you who his friends are."

"Watched," Jude agreed. "And Tam? Come to the Drowned Bell tonight. Back stairs. There are people you should meet — and food. There's always food."

The kid's grin widened into something startlingly young. "Back stairs," Tam repeated, tasting it. "Fancy."

* *

That evening, in the inn's low-ceilinged back room, Tam sat between Corvel and Sera like a sparrow that had blundered into a den of hawks and decided to make itself comfortable. The child ate with the focused ferocity of genuine hunger and talked with the same energy, laying out Marrowgate's underworld in terms so precise that Corvel twice stopped eating to stare.

"—and the counting-house on Silverquay is Hask's, and Hask sells to anybody, but his pigeons go to a roost on the north wall, and north-wall birds all answer to the harbormaster's office, and the harbormaster's office flies the two-headed flag since winter, so whatever your gray-coat friend wrote, it went up the chain to somebody wearing Veyra's colors." Tam wiped grease on an already-black sleeve. "Two days, maybe three, before instructions come back. Less if they pay for fast birds."

"Then we leave in two," Corvel said flatly.

"Not yet." Sera hadn't touched her food. She had the shard out on the table between them, unwrapped at last, and its light threw strange shadows up her face — pale, cold, restless. "The Athenaeum keeps its celestial folios under seal. The archivist won't open them until the new week. Two days may buy us nothing if we flee before I've read what's in them."

"What's in them?" Jude asked.

"The reason the wards failed." Sera met their eyes, and there was something in her face Jude had never seen before — guilt, maybe, or fear wearing guilt's clothing. "The real reason. Not the one I gave you on the road."

The room went quiet. Water lapped somewhere below the floor. On the table, the shard pulsed like a captive heartbeat, and its light seemed dimmer than it had been, though Jude couldn't have said why.

"We should hear this," Jude said slowly, "before we decide anything."

"After the folios," Sera said. "Please. I'd rather tell it once, and truly, than twice, and badly." She rewrapped the shard with hands that weren't quite steady.

Corvel looked at Jude. Jude looked at Tam, who was watching them all with the frank appraisal of someone deciding whether the adults in the room were worth the trouble, and who caught Jude looking and winked.

"Two days," Corvel said at last, heavily, like a woman signing a death warrant. "We spend them preparing. Tam — you know the ways out of this city that aren't roads?"

"Every sewer, culvert, and coffin-barge route in Marrowgate." The child tapped the bulging coat. "And I know which ones are watched, because I watch the watchers. It's a whole economy."

"Then you're hired," Corvel said. "One silver a day, plus food, plus—" she hesitated, glancing at the child's threadbare coat, "—plus a new coat. A respectable one. With fewer pockets."

Tam gasped in genuine horror. "Fewer pockets? What would I even *put things* in?"

Laughter broke out around the table — tired, surprised laughter, the first Jude could remember in weeks — and for a moment the room felt warm in a way that had nothing to do with the candles. Jude looked around at it: the disgraced knight, the fugitive priest, the pickpocket orphan, the last swordspeaker with a dead sword and a stolen star. A family assembling itself out of wreckage, piece by piece, none of them quite choosing it.

Outside, night had settled fully over Marrowgate, and the candle markets burned on, ten thousand flames defying the starless dark. Beautiful, Jude thought, and doomed-feeling, the way beautiful things always feel once you know they're being hunted. Somewhere across the city a pigeon beat its wings against the night, carrying words toward Veyra's throne. Somewhere beneath the terraces, nine children slept in a cellar, guarded by a child with too many pockets.

And on the third morning, when Tam came pounding up the back stairs of the Drowned Bell two hours before dawn, white-faced and breathless, hammering on the door hard enough to wake the dead — Jude knew, before opening it, exactly what the child was going to say.

"They're here," Tam gasped, gripping the doorframe, eyes wide. "Soldiers. Gray coats, red scarves — dozens of them — and they're not searching the city." The child swallowed. "They're surrounding the candle markets. All of them. Like they already know exactly where you'll run."
