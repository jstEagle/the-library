## 10. Betrayal at the Candle Market

Tam had learned to count in Marrowgate before learning letters. Coins first — copper clinked against copper in a palm and you knew your morning from your evening. Then footsteps: the heavy tread of the canal watch, the soft scuff of a cutpurse working a crowd, and now, for three days running, boots that didn't belong. Boots with iron heels and no mud on them, moving through the candle market like they had every right, though everyone who lived by the water's edge could smell the road dust and blood under their polish.

Veyra's people were in Marrowgate.

Tam sat cross-legged on the low wall above the Lantern Canal, watching them work the market below. There were four of them that Tam had marked, maybe six more unmarked, dressed as spice merchants and fishwives but standing wrong — weight forward, eyes sweeping doorways instead of wares. They weren't buying candles. Nobody bought candles at dusk; the whole point of the market was that it lit itself when the sun went down, ten thousand little flames along the canals until the whole city floated on its own small constellation.

*Constellation.* The word still tasted strange in Tam's mouth. Three days ago it had just been a thing other people had. Now there was a woman staying in the safehouse off Tanner's Row who talked about stars the way Tam talked about bread — like something you could be hungry for. Sera. The star-priest with the shard wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, the one who slept with her back to the wall and woke at every bell.

And the swordspeaker. Jude Winterbourne, who was supposed to be somebody important, the last of some old line of magic, and who mostly looked like a scared person trying very hard not to look scared. Tam had seen plenty of those. Saw one in the mirror most mornings.

"You're brooding," said Pip, materializing out of the crowd the way only Pip could, a small girl-shaped shadow with flour still on her cheek from begging rolls off the baker on Coppersmith Lane. "You get a look on your face like the gulls when the fish carts come through."

"I'm counting," Tam said.

"Counting what?"

"Boots."

Pip followed Tam's gaze down into the market. The candle sellers were calling their last prices, wax ghosts in the fading light, and among them moved the four who did not belong. One of them stopped at Mother Ives' stall — Mother Ives, who paid protection to nobody and told the watch exactly where to put their questions — and showed her something. A paper. Mother Ives shook her head. The man smiled in a way that wasn't a smile, dropped a coin on her table anyway, and moved on.

"They're looking for someone," Pip said, going quiet in the way she did when she'd figured something out faster than she wanted to. "They're looking for *them*, aren't they? Your new friends."

"Maybe."

"Tam." Pip sat down on the wall, knees drawn up, and Tam saw what Tam had been pretending not to see for two days: the hollows under her eyes, the way her wrists stuck out of her sleeves. It hadn't been just Pip either. There were eleven of the little ones sleeping under the tannery awning now, eleven mouths, because Veyra's war had rolled down the river valleys like a stone down a well shaft and children came loose from everything, the way pebbles come loose in a flood. Eleven. Last month there had been four.

"We're out of bread," Pip said. "We're out of nearly everything. Nixie's coughing again and we've got no honey and no coin and you said —" She stopped herself. She was good at stopping herself. That was the worst part of her, how young she was and how carefully she'd already learned to ration hope like it was the last roll in the sack. "You said your friends would help."

"And I meant it," Tam said. "But they don't *have* anything either. They're running too. That's the whole trouble with them."

Below, one of the iron-heeled men turned his head, slow as an owl, and looked directly at the wall where Tam sat.

Tam slid off the stones and pulled Pip down with them, heart hammering, and they were just two more street kids laughing over nothing by the time the man's gaze swept past. But Tam's hands were shaking, and not entirely from fear. Underneath the fear, quiet and horrible, another thought had been sitting all day like a stone at the bottom of a well:

*Four of them are looking. And there are eleven children who haven't eaten properly since Tuesday.*

The thought didn't shout. That was the trick of it. Bad thoughts never shouted in Marrowgate. They just waited at the bottom of the well, patient as drowned things, until you leaned over far enough to see your own face looking back at you.

* * *

The safehouse off Tanner's Row smelled of tallow smoke and Corvel's poultices, bitter green smells that didn't belong to any kitchen. Tam slipped in through the coal chute at the back — the front door was for honest people, and besides, Tam liked knowing every way out of a room before entering it.

Jude was sitting by the window with the oath-sword across their knees, not sharpening it, not touching it, just looking at it the way you'd look at a dog that had bitten someone once. Sera was asleep sitting up, which she seemed able to do anywhere, the oilcloth bundle of the star-shard tucked against her even in sleep, one hand resting on it like a mother's hand on a cradle. Corvel stood at the map nailed to the wall, armored from the waist up even indoors, her gray-streaked hair tied back tight, tracing roads with one scarred finger.

"The east bridges are watched," she was saying as Tam dropped down through the chute. "All of them. Which means our friend in the market this afternoon wasn't idle."

"What friend?" Jude asked, looking up.

"There was a woman selling salt fish who asked me where I'd gotten my sword-belt. Called it fine craftsmanship." Corvel's mouth went flat. "It's court-issue. She knew it. She wanted to see if I'd lie about it."

"So we move tonight," Jude said. "Before—"

"Tonight," Sera said, without opening her eyes, "we do nothing, because the shard needs to be re-wrapped in consecrated wax or it starts to sing, and a singing shard is audible for half a mile, and if you think the fishwife was bad, wait until you hear what comes for a singing shard." She yawned, opened her eyes, found Tam crouched by the coal chute, and smiled the tired smile she'd been giving Tam for three days, the one that made Tam feel like a person instead of a problem. "Evening, pickpocket. Steal anything interesting?"

"My dignity," Tam said. "Somebody left it lying around so I took it."

That got a laugh out of Jude, short and surprised, and Tam felt the familiar warmth of it, the dangerous warmth. That was the thing Tam kept forgetting, the thing that made this whole arrangement worse than any job Tam had ever run. These three didn't treat Tam like a tool. They treated Tam like a person who happened to know the sewers, and persons were a hundred times easier to betray than tools, because tools never fed you soup and asked your opinion.

Corvel turned from the map. Her eyes were the gray of canal water in winter, and they missed nothing. "You've been jumpy all day, Tam."

"I'm always jumpy. It's a professional quality."

"Mm." But Corvel let it go, which was its own kind of mercy, and Tam carried the stone at the bottom of the well back out into the dark.

* * *

It took until midnight to find the buyer, because Tam refused to sell to just anyone. If it had to happen — and the well-bottom face said it had to happen, because Nixie's cough had gone wet and deep, and Pip had counted the remaining food twice and gotten the same terrible answer both times — then it would happen with someone who'd pay in silver and ask few questions.

The broker worked out of the back of a chandler's shop near the Salt Gate, a soft-voiced man named Quillon whose hands smelled of beeswax and whose eyes smelled of nothing at all. Tam had sold him information twice before, small things: which merchant wagons ran unguarded, which warehouses had rotten locks. This was different. This was bigger than anything Tam had ever held in two grubby hands.

"They're at the old rope-walk off Tanner's Row," Tam heard themself say. Their voice sounded like it belonged to someone else. "Through the coal chute. Four of them. One's hurt already — the knight, the tall one — she took a wound at the border and it's not healed right, she favors the left side. The priest sleeps sitting up and wakes at bells. The swordspeaker keeps watch till the third bell, then the priest takes over."

Quillon wrote it all down without hurry. Then he counted out coins onto the table between them, and they were silver, real silver, more than Tam had ever held at once, and Tam looked at them and felt absolutely nothing, which was somehow worse than feeling guilty.

"That's for the location," Quillon said. "There'll be the same again when it's confirmed." He paused, and something almost human crossed his wax-smelling face. "You understand what happens after, boy. To them."

"I understand what happens tonight to eleven kids if I don't eat," Tam said. "Everything else is weather."

But walking back through the midnight streets with the silver sewn into the lining of a stolen coat, Tam understood perfectly well what happened next. Iron-heeled boots would come down Tanner's Row quiet as snowfall. There would be a knock, or there wouldn't. And the woman who called Tam *pickpocket* like it was a name anyone might be proud of, and the swordspeaker who laughed at bad jokes like they were worth laughing at, and the knight who noticed everything and punished none of it —

They'd be dragged out into the dark, and somewhere south of here a queen with a hole where her soul should have been would open them up like letters, looking for the words inside.

Tam stopped on a bridge over the Lantern Canal. Below, the last candles of the night market guttered on the black water, each one a tiny stubborn argument against the dark, and Tam stood there a long time doing arithmetic that wouldn't come out right no matter how it was done. Eleven children weighed against three strangers. Hunger tonight weighed against knives tomorrow. And underneath it all, the truth that made Tam's stomach turn: the silver hadn't been necessary. Tam had stolen enough that afternoon for bread and honey both, and had sold the strangers anyway, because the well-bottom face had whispered that silver was *safety*, that silver was a cushion between Tam and the world, and Tam had spent a whole life learning that cushions were the only prayers that got answered.

The candles burned on below, refusing to notice.

"Oh, rot it," Tam said aloud, to nobody, to the water, to whatever god kept accounts of street children. "Rot it all."

* * *

Tam came back over the rooftops instead of through the chute, which was itself an answer, and dropped into the alley behind the rope-walk in time to hear the first whistle.

It was a soft sound, two notes, like a nightbird with an opinion. Then it came again from the far end of Tanner's Row, and a third time from the canal side, and Tam's blood went cold, because Tam knew every whistle in Marrowgate and these belonged to nobody Tam had ever sold to — these were military whistles, iron-heeled whistles, and they were closing like a hand.

Inside the rope-walk, a lamp flared. Shouting — Corvel's voice, low and hard, then steel ringing free of a scabbard. The coal chute banged open and Sera came out of it backward, dragging Jude by the wrist, the oilcloth bundle clutched to her chest, her eyes wide and white-rimmed.

"They were *inside*," she gasped. "How were they inside?"

And Tam, standing in the alley with the silver weighing a thousand pounds against their ribs, said: "This way. Quickly. Don't ask."

Sera looked at Tam — really looked, the way she looked at everything, like she was reading small print by starlight — and for one endless second Tam was certain the priest could see straight through skin and coat-lining to the coins and the sin beneath. Then the whistle sounded again, closer, and Sera said, "Lead."

They went down. Down the rotted steps behind the tannery, down past the level where Marrowgate kept its respectable secrets, into the sewers — the great brick throats of the old city, where the Lantern Canal's runoff sang in the dark and the air was warm and thick and stank of everything the beautiful city preferred to forget. Tam knew these tunnels the way Sera knew her faith: by heart, by hunger, by long necessity. Left at the junction where the brickwork went red. Right where the ceiling dripped. Through the low arch the watch had sealed and forgotten twenty years ago.

Behind them, above them, the rope-walk erupted.

Tam heard it through the grate they passed — the crash of a door, shouts, and then that sound Tam had only ever heard described by soldiers home from the wars: a sword speaking. Not the ring of metal. Something else. Something that made the sewer walls hum, that set the standing water trembling in rings, as if a word had been given an edge and taught to cut.

"The swordspeaker," Sera breathed. She'd gone pale in the dark. "They're using it. They've never — they said it only worked in ritual, they said—"

Another word-edge fell, and this time Tam felt it in their teeth, and with it came a scream that stopped abruptly, and then Corvel's voice, raw and carrying, echoing down through the grate like a bell tolling underwater:

"*Go! All of you go! The rearguard is mine!*"

"She can't hold four of them alone," Jude said, twisting toward the ladder up.

"She's not holding four," Tam said, and hated the steadiness of their own voice, hated everything about being the one who knew. "She's holding however many they sent, and they sent enough. Because someone told them exactly who to send them for."

Sera stopped dead in the dripping tunnel. Turned. Above them, muffled by earth and brick, the fighting snarled on, and Corvel's voice rose again — a battle-cry in some old formal tongue, the kind knights used when they'd stopped expecting to win and started intending to matter.

"Tam," Sera said quietly. "Who told them?"

The water sang. The candles of Marrowgate were a mile away and a lifetime ago, and Tam stood in the dark with the silver pressing against their heart like a brand, and found that the lie — any lie, the easy ones, the professional ones — simply would not come.

"Me," Tam said. "I did. I sold you. For food. For—" Their voice cracked clean through. "For eleven kids who haven't eaten since Tuesday. And I'd do it again, that's the worst of it, I'd bloody do it again, except now I've heard her voice saying *the rearguard is mine* and I don't — I don't know what I'd do, and I can't breathe—"

Sera hit them. Not hard — a short blow across the ear, the kind you'd give a hysterical recruit, and it worked, snapped the spiral shut. When Tam could see straight again, Sera was holding their shoulders, and her eyes in the dark were bright and terrible and not unkind.

"Then you'll carry her out," Sera said. "That's your price. Not absolution — there's no such coin down here. You'll carry her out, and you'll tell her yourself what you did, and you'll live with her answer. Can you do that?"

Above them, something huge crashed down through the floor of the rope-walk, and dust sifted through the grate, and Corvel cried out — not a word, just a sound, the sound of armor taking what it couldn't give back.

"Yes," Tam said. "Show me where."

Jude was already climbing. "She's forty feet up and there's six of them."

"Then we'd best be quick," said Sera, unwinding the oilcloth from the star-shard, and in the black throat of the sewer the fragment of fallen sky began to glow — faint, cold, blue-white, lighting the water and the three faces bent over it like a piece of the murdered heavens come down to testify.

Tam led them toward the ladder, toward the noise, toward the woman bleeding out on behalf of a betrayal Tam had priced in silver — and overhead, faint through the grate, the fighting suddenly changed key. Fewer voices. A pause. And then, clear and cold through the broken floor of the world above, a stranger's voice, smooth as poured wax:

"The priest and the shard are not on the bodies. Find them. Burn Tanner's Row if you must."

Tam's blood turned to ice water. Because Tam knew that voice. Everyone from the Salt Gate to the tanneries knew that voice.

Quillon had come himself.
