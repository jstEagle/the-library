## 11. The Quiet Camp

The hills above the River Vell had a way of swallowing sound, and Jude was grateful for it.

They had made camp in the hollow of an old shepherd's ruin — three walls of dry-stacked stone, a roof long since fallen to moss and weather, one wall open to the west where the land fell away in folds of bracken and grey rock toward a river that glittered, far below, like spilled pewter. Corvel lay in the deepest corner, wrapped in every cloak they owned, her breathing shallow and quick as a bird's. The wound in her side had gone from angry red to a bruised, feverish purple, and the flesh around it was hot enough that when Jude's fingers hovered near, he could feel it radiating like a hearthstone.

* * *

The first watch was Sera's. Jude tried to sleep and could not. He lay on his back beneath the broken roof and watched the sky through the gap where tiles had once been, and the stars looked down at him — real stars again now, or at least the memory of them restored by the shard's slow warming — but they gave him no comfort. Every point of light felt like an accusation. Somewhere under the mountain, a soul was screaming. Somewhere behind them, Veyra's outriders were combing the river roads for three travelers and a fragment of fallen light. And here, in this ruined sheepfold, a knight who had once been the queen's shield was burning with fever, murmuring names into her blankets that belonged to people long dead.

He got up near midnight and took the second watch without being asked. Sera surrendered it without argument, which told him how tired she truly was; she had argued with him about everything else since Marrowgate — the route, the pace, whether to risk a fire, whether to eat the last of the dried plums. She curled up near Corvel instead, close enough that the knight's fever-heat would warm her through the night, and was asleep within minutes, her breath evening out into something almost childlike.

So it was Jude and the dark, and the sword across his knees.

* * *

He had taken to sitting with it drawn across his lap during watches, not because it needed tending but because he needed to know where it was. Since the skirmish at Ironhollow — since the words had come out of his mouth and become edges, since men had died with his voice still ringing in their ears — the sword had developed a weight that had nothing to do with iron. It lay against his thighs now like a sleeping animal, warm at the guard where his hands rested, and he could feel it listening.

That was the part he could never say aloud in daylight: *it listened.*

"You should wake me if you're going to brood," Sera said quietly, from the corner. "It's wasteful."

"I thought you were asleep."

"I was pretending." She sat up, hair loose and tangled around her shoulders, eyes catching what little light the shard threw off where it lay wrapped in oilcloth between them. In the darkness the shard glimmered faintly through its wrappings, like a lamp under a blanket, and its glow turned her face half-silver. "Corvel's fever broke an hour ago. Or broke partway. She's sweating, at least. That's supposed to be good."

"That's supposed to be good," Jude agreed, and then, because the night was large and the silence larger, he said the thing he'd been carrying all day: "I keep thinking about the man with the pike."

Sera didn't answer. She came and sat beside him instead, folding herself down onto the cold stone with her knees drawn up, close but not touching, the way she always arranged herself — near enough for warmth, far enough for retreat.

"He wasn't even looking at me," Jude went on. His voice came out low, roughened. "When I spoke. When the word became — when it *cut*. He was looking past me, at the village. At the burning. He looked surprised more than anything. Like he hadn't expected any of it either." He turned the sword slightly in his lap, and the firelight of the shard moved along the blade. "I killed a surprised man, Sera."

"Yes."

"And I didn't feel anything. That's the worst of it. I stood there afterward and waited to feel sick, or sorry, or — something, and there was just this —" He hunted for the word and hated every one he found. "*Quiet*. Like a room after everyone's left. And then I realized the quiet was coming from the sword. It was satisfied. It had done what it was for, and it was *content*, and I was standing inside its contentment like it was mine."

The wind moved through the bracken below the ruin, a long soft rush like a tide withdrawing.

"The oath-sword frightens me more than Veyra does," Jude said finally. "More than the armies. More than the court under the mountain, whatever it is. Because I can understand wanting to kill us — that's simple, that's human, I can hate someone for it. But this thing in my hands doesn't want. It just *is*, and it makes me into what it is for, and afterward it hands me back my body like I'm borrowing it." He looked at her. "I'm the last swordspeaker. Everyone keeps saying it like it's a title. But nobody asks what happens to the last of anything."

* * *

Sera was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had none of its usual deflection, none of the acolyte's polished calm she wore like armor.

"When I was young in the under-court," she said, "they taught us that power is a door. You knock, you're admitted, you walk through, and on the other side you are changed — permanently, irrevocably — into someone who has walked through. There's no walking back. The person who comes out the other side has your face and your memories and your name, but they aren't quite you anymore." She pulled her cloak tighter. "I used to think that was beautiful. A mystery worth surrendering to. Now I think it's just — true, in the most ordinary and terrible way. Everything changes you. The sword changes you. Grief changes you. Love changes you. The question isn't whether you'll be altered, Jude. It's whether you'll choose the alteration or have it chosen for you."

"Is that meant to comfort me?"

"No," she said, and almost smiled. "It's meant to be honest. You asked what happens to the last of anything. It becomes the thing itself. The last candle in a dark room isn't a candle anymore, it's the only light there is. That's not a burden anyone should carry alone." Her hand found his in the dark, briefly, and withdrew. "You don't have to carry it alone. That's all I'm saying. Whatever the sword makes you say next — you won't say it alone."

* * *

They sat together as the night deepened, and somewhere in that shared silence the conversation loosened, unspooled, became something neither of them had planned.

"What did you want," Sera asked eventually, "before all this? Before the wards fell?"

Jue laughed softly, surprising himself. "Rain," he said. "That's such a small thing. We got so little rain in the valley, and I used to stand outside in storms like a fool. I wanted —" He searched. "I wanted to learn to make bread properly. Mine always came out dense as a brick. I wanted to fix the south fence before winter. I wanted to see the ocean, maybe, someday, if the world ever let me. I never wanted any of this." He gestured vaguely at the sword, the shard, the whole wounded continent. "I wanted small things, Sera. Good bread and rain and a fence that kept the goats in. And now I'm going to die under a mountain for a stranger's soul, and the strange thing is I'm not even sure I mind, except —"

"Except?"

"Except I keep catching myself planning a garden. In the middle of everything. We were riding through that burned orchard two days ago and I caught myself thinking, *the soil's still good there, you could get apples off that ground in three years.* Three years! As if I'll be anywhere in three years. As if there will be a ground."

"There will," Sera said simply. "There will be ground, and there will be apples, and there will be rain. I've read the old prophecies, Jude, and I'm telling you as a former priestess and a current liar and thief: the ones who plan gardens are the ones who come home."

"And what did you want? Before?"

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Music. Real music, not the liturgy — we weren't allowed instruments in the under-court, only voices, and only approved songs. I wanted to hear a fiddle played badly in a tavern. I wanted to dance with someone whose name I didn't need to know. I wanted—" Her breath caught, and Jude understood suddenly, the way you understand things in the dark that you cannot understand in daylight, that she was crying, silently, without changing her posture at all. "I wanted to matter to someone who wasn't a god. That's all. Just one person. One person for whom I was not a vessel or a servant or a runaway, but simply — Sera."

"You matter to me," Jude said. It came out clumsy, too fast, and he didn't try to take it back. "Not as a vessel. Not as a guide or a priestess or whatever else. Just as you."

* * *

She turned her head and looked at him then, fully, in the silver-dark, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to decide something for her, because she leaned her shoulder against his — a small weight, deliberate, offered — and left it there.

"Tell me about the bread," she said.

"It's terrible. Dense as a brick. I told you."

"Tell me anyway."

So he did. He told her about his mother's kitchen, about the trick of letting the dough rest twice, about the year the harvest vigil had been rained out and everyone had crowded into the meeting hall and eaten slightly stale honey-cakes and sung until dawn. He told her about the goats, about Tam's laugh, about the way the valley smelled after the first frost. And Sera told him about the free cities in spring, about canal-water and cherry trees planted along the embankments by prisoners doing their penance, about a fiddle player in Marrowgate who played so badly the whole tavern sang louder to cover him, and how that had been the happiest night of her life.

And slowly, without either of them naming it, the space between them closed — shoulder to shoulder, then her head finding the hollow beneath his jaw, then his arm around her, careful, questioning, and her answering by settling deeper. They were not lovers yet, and might never be; but something passed between them in that ruin that was older and more necessary than desire. Two exhausted people, keeping each other company inside the storm's eye, agreeing without words that if the world ended tomorrow it would end while they were holding on to someone.

Above them, through the broken roof, the stars burned steady and patient. Below them, Corvel breathed — ragged, but breathing. The sword lay quiet across Jude's knees, and for the first time since Ironhollow, he did not feel it listening.

* * *

Near dawn, Corvel stirred.

They both heard it — the shift in her breathing, the rustle of blankets — and were on their feet before conscious thought caught up, hands finding weapons out of habit. But the knight only turned her head on the bedroll, eyes half-open, glassy with fever but focused, fixed on the eastern horizon where the sky was beginning to pale.

"Smoke," she rasped.

Jude followed her gaze. Far off, at the edge of seeing, where the river road curved north toward the lowlands — thin grey threads of it, rising straight in the windless morning air. Too many fires, too evenly spaced, to be farmers.

"Not smoke," Sera said quietly, stepping up beside him, her hand cold when it found his arm. "Signals. Look at the spacing. They're passing word along the road."

Corvel coughed, wet and deep, and pushed herself up on one elbow despite everything, because she was Corvel, and duty was a bone in her body that no fever could break. "Army's moving," she managed. "Veyra's not sending riders anymore. She's done chasing." Her eyes found Jude's, and in them was the flat, clear certainty of a soldier reading a field. "She's marching on Aurelspire. Ten days, maybe less. And everything we did in Marrowgate — everything we spent — bought us exactly enough road to get there *just* in time to watch the gates close."

Below them, far away, the signal fires burned on, one after another, like a message being spelled out across the whole dark body of the land.
