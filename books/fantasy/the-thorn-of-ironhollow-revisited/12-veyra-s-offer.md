## 12. Veyra's Offer

The herald found us on the ninth day of hiding, which told Corvel Ashgrave everything she needed to know about how well they were hiding.

She was on the second watch before dawn, wrapped in a wool cloak that smelled of woodsmoke and Corvel's own sweat, watching the treeline where the hills folded down toward the old shepherd's road. Frost had come early that autumn. It silvered the bracken and made the horses' breath hang like small ghosts above the hollow where they'd camped — a fold of earth beneath a leaning rowan, deep enough to hide firelight, shallow enough that Corvel had never once stopped listening for hooves.

She heard them now. Not coming. Waiting.

One rider on the road below, unhurried, a lantern hung from his saddlebow though the sky was still dark enough to hold stars in its western quarter — the few stars left, the ragged remnant that hadn't gone out with the rest two months ago. Corvel counted them out of habit, the way other soldiers counted their wounds. Seven. There had been eleven when they fled Marrowgate. The sky was going out like a house whose candles were being carried away one room at a time, and somewhere behind them Veyra's armies were marching, and here was a single man with a lantern sitting his horse in the frost as if he had all the patience of winter itself.

Corvel drew her knife, considered, and did not wake the others. Not yet.

She went down the slope alone, quiet as her old training could make her — and it surprised her, a little, how much of that training still lived in her body after everything, after the disgrace and the branding and the years of sleeping in ditches. Her knees complained. Her hands were steady.

"Hold there," she said.

The herald turned. He was older than she'd expected, gray at the temples, with the neat, unremarkable face of a man who delivered messages for a living and had learned to make himself forgettable. He raised both hands, palms out, empty.

"Dame Corvel Ashgrave," he said. Not a question. "I'm told you were once the finest shield in the queen's guard. I'm also told you're a traitor now. Forgive me if I don't bow."

"Who told you where we were?"

"The same person who tells everyone everything, eventually." He smiled without warmth. "Money, Dame Ashgrave. Money and fear. They move faster than cavalry." He reached slowly into his coat and drew out a scroll sealed with black wax — and Corvel's stomach tightened, because she knew that seal. She had knelt before it. She had held the banner of it over Veyra's head at the coronation, back when the woman had still been Lady Veyra of the eastern marches, back when Corvel had believed — gods help her, she had *believed* — that the realm would be safer under a hard hand than a gentle one.

"I'm not here for you," the herald said. "You know that. I'm here for the swordspeaker."

Behind her, up the slope, Corvel heard the soft scrape of a boot on frost. Jude Winterbourne had woken after all. Of course they had. The boy — no, not a boy, Corvel corrected herself again; a grown adult with a sword that spoke and a village's ashes behind them — had ears like a fox and slept like a sentry these days, and every night Corvel watched the guilt of it settle deeper into their shoulders. They blamed themselves for Ironhollow burning. They blamed themselves for every mile the three of them had dragged Sera's wounded hope across the free cities' borders.

They should not hear this offer, Corvel thought. And then: they have to. And then, worst of all: *what will they choose?*

"Jude," she said quietly, not turning. "Come down. Bring the lantern."

* * *

They woke Sera too, because it was her right. She sat propped against the rowan's roots with the star-shard wrapped in wool in her lap, and even half-asleep her hands curled around it the way a mother curls around a child. The shard gave off no light anyone else could see — Corvel had never been able to see it — but she always knew where it was in a room, the way you know where a fire is burning behind a wall. A warmth at the edge of perception. An attention.

Jude stood in front of the herald with their arms crossed and their oath-sword slung across their back, and Corvel watched them take the measure of the man the way they took the measure of everything now: carefully, wearily, with eyes that had aged ten years in ten weeks.

"Say what you came to say," Jude said.

The herald dismounted. He bowed — not deeply, but correctly, the bow owed to an oath-bound swordspeaker, and Corvel saw Jude flinch at it, because they had spent their whole life in a valley where the title meant nothing but ritual and harvest-blessing, and now the whole world outside kept trying to kneel to them.

"By order of Her Radiant Majesty Veyra, First of Her Name, Protector of the Nine Thrones—" Sera made a sound low in her throat, and the herald paused, adjusted, continued "—the crown offers terms to Jude Winterbourne, last of the swordspeakers of Ironhollow."

He broke the seal and read. He had clearly rehearsed it. That was the thing Corvel noticed first, and hated most: the words were polished smooth as river stones, worn by repetition, designed to sound inevitable.

"Full and unconditional pardon for all acts committed against the crown, past and future. Restitution and rebuilding of the valley of Ironhollow — timber, stone, seed grain, smiths, at the crown's expense, begun within the season. The wardstone shall be restored or replaced as the survivors see fit. No soldier of the crown shall enter the valley save by invitation." He looked up. "And a place at court. Honored. Permanent. Her Majesty remembers the old bonds between the throne and the swordspeakers, and wishes to renew them."

He rolled the scroll closed and held it out.

"In exchange, the crown asks only this: surrender the stolen fragment you carry, and cease your journey. That is all. No conditions on your persons. No pursuit. The free cities may do as they like — Her Majesty is confident of the outcome either way." His voice softened, and Corvel realized with a cold drop in her belly that the softness was genuine, or crafted so well it made no difference. "I've seen the muster rolls, Swordspeaker. I've seen what's coming to Aurelspire. You cannot stop it with a shard and a traitor and a priest who won't say what she believes. You can only watch it happen. Or you can go home."

Silence, except for the horses shifting, and the wind moving through the frost-killed bracken with a sound like paper being turned.

Jude didn't take the scroll. But they didn't look away from it, either.

* * *

Later, Corvel would turn the moment over and over in her memory like a coin she couldn't stop biting, looking for the false metal in it. Because here was the poison of it, the elegance: none of it was a lie. That was the craft of Veyra, whom Corvel had served and failed and followed into darkness without ever seeing the darkness until it was too late. Other tyrants threatened. Veyra *offered*. She offered so generously, so reasonably, that refusing felt like the unreasonable act — and somewhere in the offering, always, was the hook, thin as a hair, waiting.

Restoration of Ironhollow. Corvel had seen the faces of the villagers who'd voted to stay behind and rebuild among the ashes. She'd seen Jude's face when they'd spoken of them. Give Jude a promise of timber and seed grain and safety for the people who'd trusted them, and what oath wouldn't bend?

A place at court. Give a displaced swordspeaker — a person whose whole identity had been forged around a duty that had just been shattered, whose valley had burned, who slept badly and ate less and carried more grief than any one spine should hold — give that person a *home*. Walls. Purpose. Belonging.

And underneath it, the hook: *you can only watch it happen. Or you can go home.*

"Leave us," Sera said to the herald.

"I'll wait by the road," he said agreeably, and mounted, and rode back down to sit his horse in the frost with his lantern, patient as a debt.

* * *

They argued for an hour. Corvel stood apart, as she always did during the arguments between those two, close enough to intervene and far enough to pretend she wasn't listening — a skill from her court years, standing at a queen's shoulder while queens and ministers cut each other with velvet words.

"It's real," Sera said. She had unwrapped the star-shard and was holding it up between them, and in the pre-dawn gray Corvel could almost believe she saw something in it, a depth, a slow turning like water finding its level. "That's what makes it dangerous. The pardon is real. She'd honor it. She honors her bargains, Jude — that's the whole horror of her. She sold her soul and paid the price to the letter. If you take her offer, you'll get your valley back. Every stone of it."

"And the stars?" Jude said quietly. "Do I get those back too?"

"No," Sera said. "The stars stay dead. The soul stays buried. And whatever holds them alight stays in the dark with her bargain, feeding on it, growing stronger, year after year, decade after decade, until—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. When she spoke again her voice was gentler. "Until something worse than dead stars happens. I've told you what my order believed. What I *know*, because I ran from the room where they discussed it."

"You've told me stories," Jude said, and Corvel heard the crack in it, the exhaustion, the terrible fairness of the doubt. "Beautiful ones. Around fires. About courts under mountains and souls as currency and bindings older than kingdoms. And every day we walk, someone else bleeds. Corvel bled at Marrowgate. You can barely ride some mornings. Tam—"

"Don't," Sera said softly.

"—Tam is a child selling information to buy bread because we brought a war to their city," Jude finished anyway, because they were honest past the point of kindness, mostly to themselves. "And here's a man on a road saying: put it down. Go home. Everyone lives. And I want—" Their voice caught. Corvel looked away, giving them that privacy the way she wished someone had given it to her, twenty years ago, when a different offer had been made to a younger knight with tired eyes. "Gods help me, Sera. I want to go home."

The words fell into the frost and lay there.

Corvel should have stayed silent. It wasn't her argument; it wasn't her valley or her faith or her grief. But she had spent her life learning the weight of oaths, and she had broken the worst one of hers, and she knew — she knew in her scarred hands and her branded skin — what it cost to set a burden down, and what it cost afterward to pick it back up.

"Dame Ashgrave," Sera said, warning in it, but Corvel stepped forward anyway.

"When I was knighted," she said, "my oath-mistress told me the hardest vows aren't the ones that cost you blood. Anyone can bleed. It's the vows that cost you comfort. The ones where doing right means staying tired, staying poor, staying afraid, staying *unthanked* — forever, with no end in sight and no guarantee it matters." She looked at Jude, and let them see all of her, the failure included. "Veyra made me an offer once too. Not a scroll. A chair beside her throne, a place in her new order, a chance to fix everything I'd failed to fix the clean way, this time with power enough to do it. And I sat in that chair for six years, Jude, and every year the fixing got harder and the power felt better, and I told myself stories exactly as beautiful as the ones Sera tells you — except mine were lies, and hers are true, and that difference is the whole world."

"The herald said she honors her bargains," Jude said.

"She does." Corvel smiled, and knew it was a bitter thing. "That's how she takes everything. She doesn't break her word. She makes sure you *want* to give her what she wants, so her hands stay clean. Ask yourself why a queen with a hundred thousand soldiers sends one gray man with a lantern. Because she doesn't need to take the shard from you, Jude. She needs you to hand it over believing it was mercy."

Jude looked down at the scroll, still lying in the frost where the herald had set it, its black wax seal glinting.

* * *

They burned it. That was the answer Jude gave — not to the herald, whom they dismissed with a single sentence and no ceremony, but to themselves, in front of witnesses. Sera struck flint to the camp-fire's embers and Jude held the scroll over it, and Corvel watched the young swordspeaker's face as the black wax bubbled and the careful, generous, poisonous words curled brown and then bright and then were ash on the morning wind.

But Corvel had been a soldier long enough to know that fire does not burn ideas. It burns paper.

They packed camp in silence and moved north through the hills, toward the high road and Aurelspire, ten days distant if the weather held and less if Veyra's columns marched fast. Sera rode ahead with the shard. Tam's name came up once, quietly, and was let lie. Corvel took the rear guard, as she always did, and watched Jude's back the way she had once watched a queen's, and told herself the watching meant nothing, that the seed hadn't taken root, that refusal given in fire was refusal entire.

But she had seen Jude's eyes while the scroll burned. Not relief. Not resolve.

*Longing.* The clean, simple longing of a person who has just been shown, in perfect detail, exactly what peace would look like — timber and stone and seed grain, a door with their name on it, an end to walking — and has chosen, freely, to keep walking anyway.

That kind of longing doesn't die in a fire. It waits. It grows. And Corvel knew, riding rear guard through the frost with her hand on her sword, that somewhere ahead — at the council walls of Aurelspire, or in the dark under the mountain itself — there would come a moment when Jude Winterbourne would be tired enough, or grieving enough, or offered enough, to reach for that valley again.

She only prayed that when the moment came, the price would be theirs alone to pay.

Behind them, far down the southern roads, she could almost feel it: the army coming. Ten days, Tam's runners had said.

Nine, now.
