## 13. The Free Cities Muster

Aurelspire announced itself three days before we saw it.

We smelled it first — woodsmoke and salt and something sweeter beneath, like honey left too long in the sun. Then we heard it: a low, ceaseless hum that Corvel called the city's breathing. Ten thousand bells, she said, cast into the spires of the old temple quarter, ringing without ringers whenever the wind came down off the mountains. They had not been melted for cannon-metal here. Aurelspire guarded its bells the way other cities guarded their granaries.

But it was the light that stopped me on the road.

The city rose from a shoulder of pale stone like a fist of white candles, tier upon tier, and at its crown stood the Spire itself — a needle of glass and gold that caught the grey daylight and threw it back broken into a thousand colors. I had never seen so much light gathered in one place. Even with the stars dead above us, even under that bruised and starless sky, Aurelspire burned with its own small stubborn suns.

"It's beautiful," Sera said quietly beside me.

She had said little since the Rootways' edge gave way to open road, since the toll-gate and the memories we no longer carried. She rode with her hood up and her hands folded around the star-shard where it hung against her chest, wrapped now in wool and leather, humming faintly — a sound I felt in my teeth more than heard. Sometimes at night I woke to find her awake, holding the shard up to what little moonlight remained, reading it by touch the way a blind woman reads a face.

Corvel grunted from my other side. "Beautiful and useless, if the council inside is what the refugees say."

The road had been thickening for two days with people flowing toward the city — farmers with carts heaped high, merchants with armed escorts, whole families walking with everything they owned on their backs. And threaded through them, always, the same word passed mouth to mouth like a spark along a fuse: *Veyra. Veyra marches. Veyra has taken Harrowfen. Veyra's outriders were seen at the ford.*

Ten days, Tam's runner had told us before we parted ways outside Marrowgate. We had spent six of them getting here.

I touched the sword at my hip as we passed under the first gate — an old habit, or perhaps not a habit at all anymore. The oath-sword did not hum now. It was only steel, cold and quiet, the words that lived in it gone silent as stones at the bottom of a well. I had not yet told the others how much that silence frightened me. How every time I reached for it out of reflex, I found nothing there, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be.

The gate guards waved us through without question. War made everyone welcome who came armed and able.

* * *

Inside, the beauty continued, and so did the fear.

We climbed through streets where laundry hung between balconies like signal flags, past fountain squares where children still played — though their games had changed; I watched a boy chase another with a stick, shouting *"I'm Veyra's horse!"* while mothers looked away — until we reached the concourse below the council hall, and there the city's fear was laid out plain.

Refugees camped in the shadow of the walls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Tents of patched canvas and lean-tos of stripped branches, cook-fires smoking in long rows, and everywhere the faces — faces from Harrowfen and the river towns, faces grayed with ash, faces that turned toward every rider who entered the square with the same hungry question in them: *Is anyone coming? Is anyone doing anything?*

Sera pulled back her hood. "They're not being let inside?"

"The free cities don't let anyone all the way inside," Corvel said. "That's rather the point of them." She reined her mare toward a stable-boy and dismounted stiffly, favoring the side where the wound from Marrowgate had healed hard and slow. "Council sits at dusk. If we're to speak to them, we speak tonight."

"Will they even hear us?" I asked.

"They'll hear anyone," Corvel said, and something bitter moved behind her eyes. "Hearing costs nothing. It's the listening they hoard."

* * *

The council chamber of Aurelspire had been built to hold a thousand voices and held, that night, perhaps six hundred, packed onto tiered benches carved from white stone that glowed faintly with old enchantment. Delegates from nine cities sat below in a horseshoe of desks — Marrowgate's canal-lords in their blue silks, the merchant-princes of Dellhaven dripping pearls, the war-matriarchs of the eastern holds in armor they clearly wore more often than robes. Above them all, citizens crowded the galleries, and above *them*, through high windows of colored glass, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath.

I stood in the antechamber with Sera and Corvel and listened to the free cities fail.

"—and I say again, the eastern holds will not bleed for Marrowgate's canals—"

"—then perhaps the canals should learn to bleed for themselves—"

"—Harrofen is *gone*, Delegate. Burned. The survivors are camped beneath this very roof—"

"—survivors of what? A skirmish? Veyra took Harrowfen with two thousand men and a handful of those soul-cannon. She cannot possibly—"

"—cannot possibly *what*? March here? She sold her soul to the under-court, man. Do you imagine she bought it to sit on her throne and count her rings?"

Voices rose and tangled and drowned each other. Somewhere a delegate laughed, short and ugly, at something someone said about the western cities. In the galleries, the refugees watched, and I watched the refugees, and I thought about the boy playing Veyra's horse, and about the village green at home where I had learned to whittle and to dance and to bury the dead.

"You're gripping the bench," Sera murmured.

I loosened my fingers. "I'm fine."

"You're lying beautifully."

"I'm a swordspeaker," I said, before I could stop myself. The old joke fell flat in my own mouth. Sera's hand found mine briefly, warm, then withdrew.

A herald in silver called the chamber to order, and the din sank to a simmer. Then the First Voice of Aurelspire — an old woman with a voice like dry paper and eyes like wet slate — read the evening's petitions, and one by one they were received, tabled, deferred, referred to committees whose names sounded like places where good intentions went to die.

Then the herald spoke my name.

Not my name, exactly. "*Jude Winterbourne, last of the swordspeakers of Ironhollow, bearer of witness.*" Someone had coached the herald well. Corvel, I suspected. I stood, and six hundred heads turned, and I felt the weight of them settle onto my shoulders like wet sand.

I had fought soldiers in a burning village. I had faced the dark at the toll-gate and given up my mother's face to pass through it. None of it had prepared me for a room full of people waiting to be convinced.

* * *

I had planned a speech. Corvel had helped me plan it, late the night before, scratching notes by candlelight with her sword-hand bandaged: *Lead with the shard. Lead with the proof. Tell them what the soul-cannon are. Give them numbers.*

Standing on the floor of that chamber, with the horseshoe of delegates looking at me the way creditors look at a debtor, I abandoned all of it.

"Delegates of the free cities," I began, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted. I stopped. Breathed. Started again. "My name is Jude Winterbourne. I'm not a general. I've never commanded anything larger than a harvest crew. Three months ago I lived in a valley most maps don't bother with, and my biggest worry was whether the barley would come in before the frost."

Someone in the eastern delegation shifted impatiently. I kept going.

"Ironhollow was warded. Two hundred years of wards, so strong that magic couldn't cross our border — couldn't follow us in. We thought that made us safe. On the night the stars went out, the wards shattered like glass, and I learned that safety you didn't build yourself isn't safety at all. It's just a debt someone else took out in your name."

I saw the Marrowgate delegates lean forward slightly at that. Good. They understood debts.

"I'm not here to tell you about magic," I said. "You have priests and scholars for that. I'm here because someone has to tell you what I've seen between Ironhollow and this room, because I don't think anyone in this chamber has seen it, and because if you vote tonight the way you've been talking all evening, none of you ever will."

Silence now. Real silence. Even the galleries had gone still.

"Harrowfen," I said. "I walked through Harrowfen four days after it fell. I want to describe it to you, and I want you to understand that I'm choosing plain words because plain words are all that's left. There was a church with its bell still hanging in the tower, half-melted, drooping like tallow. There were fields where the wheat had been burned standing, so that when the wind moved, the ash stalks swayed like they were still alive. There were dogs. I won't tell you about the dogs."

A woman in the gallery began to cry, quietly, without shame.

"There was a door," I went on, "with boot-prints on it. Small ones. Children's. Going *in*, not out, because they ran inside when the riders came, because inside is where you go when you're small and afraid. And I stood in that doorway and I thought: somewhere, right now, there is a chamber full of important people arguing about whether this matters enough to inconvenience themselves. And I thought: I hope I'm wrong."

I let that sit. My hands were shaking. I let them shake.

"Veyra has soul-cannon. You know this — some of you have seen them, and the rest have heard enough stories to be frightened, and you've decided that being frightened in comfort is cheaper than being frightened in armor. I understand that. I do. But here is what your scholars may not have told you." I glanced at Sera, standing at the chamber's edge with the shard hidden against her heart, and she gave me the smallest nod. "Those cannon fire grief. That's the powder Veyra bought with her soul. Every shot carries a piece of the court beneath the mountain, and the court beneath the mountain is lonely, and loneliness is the hungriest thing there is. You cannot outlast it. You cannot pay it off. You can only stand together or fall apart, and Veyra is betting everything you own that you'll choose falling apart, because she has watched this council for years and knows how it votes."

Angry murmuring from three delegations at once. I raised my voice, not much — just enough.

"I'm not asking you to love each other. I've traveled with two people I'd die for and I still can't get them to agree on where to camp. I'm asking you to do what ordinary people do in a burning house — which is throw water, not argue about whose fault the fire is. Nine cities. One army. Ten days."

"*Nine days,*" corrected a delegate from the east, pedantically, and I almost laughed, because it was such a human thing to say in the middle of the end of the world.

"Then nine," I said. "I have nothing else to offer you. No armies. No gold. I gave up the only magic I had to buy back something better than victory, and I'd make the trade again. All I am is a witness. I saw Harrowfen. I saw Marrowgate burn. I saw what's coming, and it doesn't care which of you is richest or proudest or most right, because grief eats everyone at the same table eventually. Arm together. Or don't — and meet me in the ashes, and I'll teach you the plain words for what's left."

I stepped back. The chamber was quiet in a way that felt less like agreement than like a held knife.

* * *

They argued for four hours.

I sat in the gallery with my head against cool stone and listened to the free cities take my plain words and grind them through their mills of procedure and pride. The Marrowgate canal-lords committed first — blue silks rising to pledge two thousand pikes and their river galleys, to scattered applause. The eastern holds held out for an hour over command structure, then over supply routes, then over a slight delivered forty years ago that apparently still smarted. The merchant-princes of Dellhaven offered coin instead of men, and were hissed from the galleries for it, and revised their offer twice.

Sera dozed against my shoulder around midnight. Corvel stood at the rail the entire time, arms crossed, watching the horseshoe with the expression of a woman counting exits.

It was past the second bell of morning when the First Voice called the final division, and the delegates rose one by one, city by city, and the tally was read out in that dry-paper voice.

Nine cities. Seven in favor.

One abstention. One — the western port of Greywater — against.

"Seven of nine," Corvel said softly. "Barely. Ugly. Half-armed and badly led." She exhaled through her nose, and it might have been a laugh. "It'll do."

Below, the First Voice struck her staff once against the white stone floor, and the glow in the stone flared bright, and the old law of the free cities rang through the chamber: *What is mustered together stands together.* Six hundred voices took up the words, ragged and tired and suddenly, terribly hopeful, and the sound rolled up through the galleries and out the high windows into the starless dark.

Sera stirred against my shoulder. "Did we win?" she mumbled.

"No," I said, watching the delegates below already turning to each other, already beginning to bargain over the shape of the army that didn't exist yet. "But maybe we didn't lose."

Then the doors at the far end of the chamber banged open, and a rider in Greywater colors stumbled down the aisle, mud to the hips, blood on her sleeve, and shouted the words that emptied the chamber faster than any decree:

"Greywater is burning! Veyra's fleet came up the coast in the night — she's not waiting nine days — she never meant to give us nine days!"

Above us, through the colored glass, ten thousand bells began to ring — all at once, with no wind at all.
