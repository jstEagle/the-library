## 14. The Truth About the Wards

The star-shard woke at moonrise.

Sera had been watching it for three days — laid on a square of black velvet in the corner of the safehouse loft in Aurelspire, a room borrowed from a candlemaker who asked no questions and charged accordingly. For three days it had done nothing but glow with that faint, patient light, like an ember deciding whether to bother. She had copied its angles, measured its warmth against her palm, chanted over it in the dead liturgies of her old faith until her throat was raw. Nothing.

Then, tonight, as the moon cleared the rooftops and poured itself through the broken shutters, the shard began to *sing*.

Not sound, exactly. It was more like remembering — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like frost and iron at the back of the tongue. The light inside it shifted, deepened, turned from silver to something older, the color of the sky before dawn in the years before the stars were named. And in that shifting light, Sera saw letters.

They hung above the shard's surface the way reflections hang on water: thin, wavering, written in no alphabet she had ever studied at the under-court, and yet she could read them, because they were not meant for the eye at all. They were meant for the part of a person that remembered being prayed to.

She sat up so fast her cot creaked, and the sound of it was enough to wake Jude, who slept — when Jude slept at all, which lately was rarely and badly — on the pallet beneath the window.

"What is it?" Jude's voice came out rough, half-dreaming, hand already finding the hilt of the oath-sword out of habit rather than threat.

"It's speaking," Sera said. "Or — showing. Come here. Bring the lamp."

Jude crossed the loft barefoot, hair sleep-tangled, and knelt beside her at the velvet square. Up close, in the lamplight and the shardlight both, Jude looked younger and wearier than their years, the way people did after villages burned and roads went long. There was flour still under the nails of one hand, from the bread they'd insisted on baking that morning because, they said, if the world was ending it might as well end with decent loaves. Sera had come to love that about them — that stubborn insistence on ordinary things — though she would have died before saying so.

"Those are words," Jude said slowly. "I can almost—"

"Don't touch it yet." She caught their wrist. "It's keyed. I think it's been waiting for someone with old blood in them. Ironhollow blood."

Jude went very still. In the shard's shifting light, their face did the thing it always did when the past reached for them — the flinch of a person who had grown up believing history was something that happened to other valleys.

"Read it," Jude said quietly. "You read it. Tell me what it says."

So Sera leaned close, let the frost-and-iron taste flood her mouth, and read.

* * *

The first lines were a prayer. That was the cruelty of it — the thing opened like scripture, the cadences rising and falling in the old pattern every acolyte learned before they learned to walk. *Light of the high courts, keep what we entrust; dark of the deep court, take only what is freely given.* Sera had spoken those words ten thousand times in the singing halls beneath the mountain, back when she believed they were hymns instead of contracts.

Then the prayer became a ledger.

*I, Aldous Winterbourne, founder by fire and oath of the valley called Ironhollow, do set down in starlight what must not be set in ink.*

Sera felt Jude stop breathing beside her.

*Aldous Winterbourne,* she read aloud, and the name filled the little loft like smoke. *Aldous Winterbourne, who built the wards.*

"I know that name," Jude whispered. "It's carved on the wardstone. It's carved on the well. It's—" A small, disbelieving laugh. "There's a festival day. We used to put lanterns on the water for Founder's Day. Every autumn. My mother used to say we were descended from his brother's line, that it didn't really count, that everyone in the valley claimed it." The laugh died. "Sera. What did he do?"

She kept reading.

*I was keeper of the binding before I was founder of anything. Before the valley, I served the court beneath the mountain, as my mothers and fathers served before me, and I will tell the truth plainly since no one remains alive to shame me for it: the stars do not burn on their own. They never did. They burn because something below holds them up — holds them the way a dying man holds a candle, out of love, out of loneliness, out of a bargain struck so long ago that even the mountain has forgotten its terms.*

"The binding," Sera murmured. "The binding that holds the stars alight. This is what I fled from. This is what Veyra bought."

*Keep reading,* said the shard, in the pressure-behind-the-eyes voice, and she kept reading.

*In my service below, I learned what the court truly guards: not the stars themselves, but the soul that fuels them — the first soul ever given, freely given, in the world's youngest age, when the sky was going dark and something had to be done. The court does not hoard it out of greed. The court hoards it out of grief. It has kept that soul the way one keeps a letter from a dead beloved: unread, unopened, warm only because it is held.*

"But Veyra found it," Jude said. "Veyra took it."

*Not took,* the shard corrected, and the light flared, sharp as a struck flint. *Bought. As I once sold.*

The word landed in the room like a dropped blade.

*Hear me, descendant, whoever you are, reading this by whatever mercy of light survives me. When I left the court — when I ran, as cowards run, as I now understand all founders run from something — I did not merely hide myself in this valley. I purchased my hiding. I went to the border-stones and I paid for the wards with the only coin I carried: the truth of where the soul lay hidden. I gave the court the valley's silence, and the court gave the valley its blindness, and for two hundred years the arrangement held, because no one below wanted the soul found any more than I wanted it found. The wards were not a shield against the world. They were a seal upon a secret. And secrets, descendant, are only ever borrowed.*

Sera stopped. Her mouth had gone dry as ash. Above the shard, the letters trembled, waiting.

"Sera." Jude's hand closed around hers, and she realized hers were shaking. "What does it say? What are you not saying?"

"You should hear the rest," she managed. "All of it. You deserve— you need to hear it whole."

*Understand me rightly,* the founder wrote. *I did not betray the world. I betrayed it precisely, carefully, with love. The soul below was the most dangerous thing in creation — not because of what it could do, but because of what could be done with it. In the wrong hands, the binding could be unwound. The stars extinguished. The sky made into a weapon. So I hid the valley that knew its location, and I paid the court with the knowledge it already possessed, and I told myself the lie that founders tell: that I was protecting my children.*

*But here is the truth beneath the truth, and may the high lights forgive me for writing it: the wards did not fail the night the stars went out. They were never going to fail. A seal does not break. It is opened. And the key to it was never steel or sorcery — it was a purchase. Someone came to the court beneath the mountain and made an offer, and the court, which has been alone since the stars were lit, which has waited two hundred years for anyone to speak to it at all, accepted.*

*Sell your soul,* the court asked her. *And I will open the valley.*

*And she said yes.*

*So know this, last reader, last child of my valley: the wards fell because I taught the court how to sell them. Veyra only spent the coin I minted. Whatever ruin comes, whatever armies cross our borders, whatever darkness pours through the gap I made — the debt is mine. And yours. Because inheritance is also a debt, and you have lived all your life in the house my lie built, eating the bread my silence bought, sleeping safe behind walls that were never protection, only complicity wearing the mask of peace.*

*I am sorry. I was afraid. These are the truest words I have, and they are worth nothing.*

*The shard remembers what I cannot undo. If there is a way to mend what I broke, it begins with knowing — and knowing begins with you.*

The letters guttered, wavered, and sank beneath the shard's surface like stones into a well. The light steadied back to its patient ember-glow, as though nothing had happened, as though it had not just reached across two centuries and cut a living person to the bone.

* * *

For a long time, neither of them spoke.

The candlemaker's loft ticked and settled around them. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over canal-cobbles; somewhere farther, the city bells rang the third hour, cracked and off-key, because half the bells in the free cities had gone to feed Veyra's cannons and the rest rang with holes in their voices. Sera listened to all of it — the small honest noises of a world that had no idea what it owed to a dead man's cowardice.

Jude sat back on their heels. Their face, in the shardlight, had gone very quiet — not blank, Sera thought, but emptied. The look of someone standing in a burned house trying to remember which shadow had been the table.

"Two hundred years," Jude said finally. The words came out level, which was worse than shouting. "Two hundred years of harvest vigils. Of lanterns on the water for him. Children dressed as little founders, running through the square with paper crowns. We thanked him, Sera. We sang to him." Their voice caught on something and they swallowed it. "He sold us. He sold us before any of us were born, and he dressed the sale up as a blessing, and we — my mother, my grandmother, all of them — they lived their whole lives inside his lie and called it home."

"Yes."

"And now the bill comes due, and it lands on—" Jude pressed the heel of a hand against their sternum, hard, as if trying to hold something in place. "On me. On the last one. Of course it does. Of course it's the last Winterbourne who has to carry it."

"That's not what it says." Sera heard her own voice sharpen and didn't soften it. She was tired — tired down to the marrow, tired of carrying pieces of terrible knowledge like shards in her shoes — and the tenderness in her came out sideways, as bluntness. "Listen to me. He wants you to inherit his guilt. That's the last trick in the ledger — guilt passed down like an heirloom, generation to generation, each one holding it like it's theirs. It isn't yours, Jude. You didn't sell anything. You baked bread this morning. You pulled strangers out of a burning village. Whatever he was, you get to be something else."

"Do I?" Jude looked at her then, and their eyes were wet and furious and terribly young. "Because everything I am — the sword, the oath, the valley, the name — it all stands on top of what he did. I can't dig it out without digging out myself. When Corvel told me Veyra sold her soul, I thought: monster. When we learned she'd bought the stars' deaths, I thought: monster. But this—" Their hand rose, unsteady, toward the shard. "This says the first monster wore our face. Built our houses. Got a festival day." A breath. "How am I supposed to fight a war against complicity when I'm made of it?"

Sera reached out and took the oath-sword from where it lay across Jude's knees — gently, the way you take a blade from someone who might otherwise use it on themselves — and set it aside on the velvet, next to the shard. Steel and starlight, side by side, both of them silent.

"When I fled the under-court," she said, "I told myself I was brave. I wasn't. I was a coward with good timing — Aldous's own words, and they fit me like a glove. I ran, and I let the acolytes I grew up with go on singing lies, and I told myself survival was innocence." She turned her hands over, palms up, showing the faded ritual scars there, the fine silver lines every acolyte bore from the first time they held the censers. "I have spent every mile of this road learning that running from a sin doesn't cleanse you. But walking toward it — walking toward it with your eyes open — that's the only absolution anyone has ever actually offered me. Not innocence, Jude. Direction."

Jude stared at her. Outside, the off-key bell finished its third hour, and the silence afterward seemed enormous.

"He hid the valley," Jude said slowly, working it through, turning the truth like a stone to see all its faces. "To protect the soul. He says that. Protect it from unwinding, from being weaponized. And then Veyra comes along two hundred years later and buys it anyway, and uses the opening *he* created." A muscle worked in their jaw. "Which means the court agreed to open the valley. Which means the court *wanted* it open. After two hundred years of keeping its side of the bargain." Jude's head came up sharply. "Why? Why would it break a bargain that old?"

Sera felt the question slide into her like cold water into a boot. Because she had wondered the same thing, in the singing halls, in the years before she ran. Because she had seen the way the older priests spoke of the deep court — not with fear, exactly, but with the careful gentleness people used for the grieving. *It has been alone since the stars were lit.* The founder had written it himself, and she had read past it in her horror, but it sat in her now like a swallowed coal.

"I don't know," she said, which was true, and then, because Jude deserved better than the whole truth withheld twice in one night: "But I think — I think maybe the court didn't break faith. Maybe it was *waiting*. Two hundred years, holding a dead beloved's letter, alone in the dark, and the one thing it asked in exchange for its patience was that no one come knocking. And then Veyra knocked. And offered it the only thing it might have wanted more than solitude."

"Which is?"

"I don't know," Sera said again. But the shard, cooling now on its velvet, pulsed once — softly, faintly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall — and she found herself thinking, absurdly, unbearably: *company. Maybe it only wanted company.*

Jude picked the oath-sword back up. Held it across their lap, looking down at it — at the blade that had hummed the night the wards broke, that had turned words into edges in a burning village, that had frightened its bearer more thoroughly than any army. The heirloom. The inheritance. The complicity, forged and honed and oiled and loved.

"Founder's Day," Jude said. "Every year. Lanterns on the water." They laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "We were floating candles for our own jailer. We were thanking the lock for the cell." They wiped their face with the heel of their hand, briskly, the gesture of someone closing a door on a room they intended never to enter again. Then they looked up, and behind the grief and the fury something else had kindled — small and hard and bright, the same stubbornness that baked bread at the end of the world.

"Aldous Winterbourne hid this valley to protect the soul. Fine. Then the last Winterbourne is going to finish the job properly — not with walls and silence, but by carrying it back to whoever it belongs to. His debt. My direction." Jude stood, joints stiff, and crossed to the window, looking out over the sleeping rooftops of Aurelspire, where the free cities' banners hung limp over gates that did not yet know how much time they had. "First, though, we have to convince seven bickering councils to raise an army. And Sera—" They glanced back, shardlight still ghosting in their eyes. "Before we decipher another word of that thing, I want to know everything it knows about the court. Everything you know. Because if the court has been alone for two hundred years, and it opened the valley anyway..."

They didn't finish. They didn't have to.

Below them, in the candlelit streets of Aurelspire, a watchman's horn sounded — one short note, then another, then a third: the signal for riders at the gate. Not attack. Something rarer. An envoy, under parley banner, asking admittance.

Sera looked at the shard, and the shard glowed back at her, patient as a grave, and she thought: *the court sent something. Or Veyra did. And either way, the night is far from done.*

"Get Corvel," she said, rising, reaching for her boots. "Whatever's coming to that gate, we're going to want her eyes on it."

The horn sounded a fourth time, longer, and the shard on its velvet brightened — just slightly, just briefly — as if, two hundred miles beneath their feet and two hundred years beneath their grief, something ancient had heard its name spoken at last, and was listening.
