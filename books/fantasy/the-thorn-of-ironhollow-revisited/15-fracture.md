## 15. Fracture

Corvel Ashgrave had learned to read storms in thirty years of soldiering — the green tinge to a sky before hail, the hush before a cavalry charge, the way horses went quiet in their lines. She had never learned to read Jude Winterbourne until now, and what she saw frightened her more than any weather.

The boy stood at the edge of the camp with their back turned, staring down the long green throat of the valley they'd come through, the one that wound west and south and eventually, if a body walked it long enough, home. Their pack sat at their feet. Packed properly, she noted. Bedroll cinched tight, waterskins full, the star-shard wrapped and stowed in its oilcloth like any other piece of cargo.

That was how she knew. You didn't pack for a tantrum. You packed for a road.

"Jude."

No answer. Wind came off the high hills carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and somewhere below them an eagle cried once and gave up on the day.

"You're not going to say anything?" Corvel tried again, coming closer, her boots loud on the scree. "Fine. Then I'll talk, and you can pretend I'm the wind."

Still nothing. But she caught the movement of their jaw, the muscle working there like a fist under skin.

"They lied." The words came out flat and strange, as if someone else were speaking through Jude's mouth. "Two hundred years. My grandmother lied. Her mother lied. The whole village lies every harvest vigil when we sing about the wards holding us safe from *the wilds*, like we're sheep behind a fence instead of—" Their breath hitched. "Like we're a lockbox. Like we were *built* to be a lockbox, and nobody ever told us we were inside it."

"I know."

"You don't know." Jude turned then, and Corvel took a half-step back without meaning to. She had seen fury on battlefields; this was something colder and more patient than fury. This was grief that had found a place to stand. "You weren't born behind those wards. You didn't learn your letters on a stone that was really a seal. Every song my mother sang me was a wall. Every story about the founders was a lie with a tune in it. And the founder — the *founder* — was mine. My own blood sold the sky over our heads, Corvel. They looked up at the stars going out and called it safety."

"They called it protection," Corvel said carefully. "There's a difference, even if it's a small one."

"Not small enough!" Jude's voice cracked across the hillside, scattering stones downslope. The oath-sword at their hip hummed faintly — it did that now, when Jude felt strongly, a sound like a struck glass — and Corvel watched them flinch at it, watch them hate it. "And now Sera wants me to walk into the dark under the world and fix what my family broke, because I'm the last of the line, because the sword likes me, because *someone* has to—"

"No," Corvel said. "Because you choose to. There's a difference there too, and it's not a small one."

Jude stared at her. Rain finally began to fall, fine and cold, beading on the leather of her pauldron, darkening the dust around their boots.

"I'm going home," Jude said quietly. "Let Veyra have the shard. Let the stars stay dead. Ironhollow rebuilt itself once after the skirmish. We'll rebuild again. We'll grow barley under a black sky and sing our lying songs and maybe, if we're lucky, die before whatever's beneath the mountain gets hungry enough to climb. At least it's honest. At least it's *mine*."

They bent for the pack.

Corvel moved. Not fast — she was too old and too wise for fast — but she put herself between Jude and the downward path, and when Jude straightened with the pack over their shoulder, they found her standing square in the rain, arms loose at her sides, no hand anywhere near a weapon.

"Move, Dame Ashgrave."

"Dame Ashgrave is a brand they burned onto me in Aurelspire," she said. "The woman in front of you is just Corvel, and she's asking you to hear one thing before you go. One thing. Then I'll step aside, and if you still want the road home, I'll walk the first ten miles with you myself, so you don't go alone."

That got through. She saw it get through, saw the anger stumble on something underneath it. Jude didn't move, but they didn't move away either.

"When I was twenty-two years old," Corvel said, "I stood guard outside a door in the old palace, and Queen Maren — the true queen, Veyra's cousin, the one nobody remembers anymore — asked me to come inside with her. There was a man waiting. A priest of the under-court, though we didn't know that word then; we thought he was a scholar. He'd brought Veyra with him. Veyra, who I'd trained with. Who I'd carried on my shoulders when she twisted her ankle at the autumn games. Who used to steal apples from the palace kitchens and split them with me on the training yard wall."

The rain thickened. Jude hadn't spoken, but they hadn't turned away, and that was enough.

"Maren asked me to come into that room because she trusted me more than anyone alive. And I stayed outside. Because Veyra was in the hall, laughing, saying it was nothing, a formality, an old family matter, and staying out there meant another hour in her company instead of an hour of standing at attention in a stuffy room listening to lawyers. That's all it was, Jude. That's the whole of my great betrayal. I chose an apple and a laugh over my queen, because it was easier, because I was tired, because I told myself it wasn't my place."

She let the silence sit a moment. It hurt to hold it open. She held it open anyway.

"Three weeks later Maren was dead — officially of a fever — and the wards around her private apartments had been quietly dissolved by men wearing my order's colors. And I spent eleven years telling myself the same story you're telling me right now: that it wasn't mine to fix, that the rot was above my station, that going home and keeping my head down and surviving was the honest choice." She shook her head slowly, rain running off her jaw. "It isn't honest, Jude. It's just comfortable. Comfort wears honesty's clothes very well, but it has never once been honesty."

"You don't know what you're asking," Jude said, low. "You're asking me to spend my life fixing a crime my family committed. To carry their guilt up a mountain and pay for it."

"No." Corvel stepped closer, close enough that she could see the rain and the other thing mixing in Jude's eyes. "I'm asking you to do what your ancestor couldn't bear to do. Look at what they built and say: *this was wrong, and I will unmake it.* That's not their guilt. That's yours to refuse or take up, and either way it's yours — which is the one thing they never gave you. A real choice." She almost smiled, and it cost her. "Your family hid the truth to keep you safe. Everyone who loves anyone does some version of it. The only question left is whether you forgive them while you fix it, or after. Going home doesn't skip the question. It just makes sure you answer it badly."

For a long moment there was nothing but rain and wind and the far-off complaint of the eagle.

Then Jude set the pack down. Gently, deliberately, the way you lay down a weapon rather than drop it.

"If we lose," they said, "if we go under that mountain and fail — everyone I love dies knowing I chose a dead queen and a hole in the ground over them."

"Yes," Corvel said. "That's what choosing costs. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something." She put a hand on Jude's shoulder, and it trembled slightly, and she let it. "I've failed at everything except showing up, Winterbourne. Show up with me. It turns out it matters more than being right."

Jude laughed — a wet, broken sound, but a laugh. They wiped their face with a sleeve, though the rain made it pointless, and looked back down the valley one last time.

"My mother sang that song," they said softly. "About the wards. She had a terrible voice. She sang it anyway, every vigil, loud enough to embarrass me in front of the whole village." A breath. "I'm going to unmake the lie she sang. And then I'm going to learn every true song there is, and sing them worse than she did."

"That," said Corvel, "is the most sensible plan I've heard all year."

They were turning back toward camp when the whistle came — three short notes, Tam's signal, sharp through the rain. A small figure came pelting up the slope from the treeline, hood plastered flat, breath smoking, and skidded to a stop in front of them with the particular grin Tam wore when the news was bad and they were proud of having stolen it first.

"You're both soaked and miserable-looking, so here's something to match," Tam panted. "I got into the courier camp two valleys east — don't ask how, it involved a dead mule and a lot of dignity I wasn't using anyway. Veyra's army broke camp four days ago." The grin fell away. "They're marching on Aurelspire. Sixty thousand, give or take the ones deserting. And the council's still arguing about whether to arm the lower city."

"How long?" Corvel asked, though her stomach already knew.

Tam held up both hands, fingers spread, then folded one pair down.

"Ten days," they said. "*If* the rain slows them. Eleven if we're lucky, and I've stopped believing in luck, personally. So." They looked between the two of them — the traitor knight and the last swordspeaker, dripping on a hillside with the whole grey world coming apart — and jerked a thumb toward the distant, invisible shape of Aurelspire beyond the mountains. "Are we walking, or are we singing?"

Jue picked up the pack. Slung it. Drew the oath-sword an inch from its sheath, enough that it sang — a clear, thin note that cut through the rain like the memory of light — and slid it home again.

"We're walking," Jude said. "And Corvel? When this is done, you're teaching me that apple story properly. The version where you get the ending wrong."

"It doesn't have a good ending."

"Neither does ours yet." Jude turned toward the pass, toward the free cities, toward the mountain with the dark underneath it, and the rain seemed suddenly to matter less. "Come on. Ten days is plenty of time to save the world badly."

* * *

They walked.

The storm followed them down off the hills and into the foothills beyond, where the land opened into the long brown approach to Aurelspire — fields gone to seed because no one dared plant, roads rutted by supply wagons, villages shuttered. Refugees trickled along the verges, heading the same direction they were, and Corvel watched Jude slow each time they passed a family with too many children and too few bundles, watched something harden and settle in them with every mile.

By the second day the rain had stopped and the clouds had broken, and there was nothing overhead but the same starless black that had hung over the world since the night the wards died. Corvel caught Jude looking up at it, again and again, the way you probe a tooth that hurts.

"Sera says the shard remembers the sky," Jude said that evening, over a fire of damp gorse that smoked more than it burned. "Says it's a key, not a lamp. That when we bring it under the mountain—" They shook their head. "I keep trying to picture it. Stars. Actual stars. I've seen engravings. I don't think I understand what I'm fighting for."

"I saw them," Corvel said. "When I was young. Before the queen died." She fed a stick to the fire. "On the night Maren was crowned, the whole court went up on the walls to watch the stars come out, and Veyra stood next to me and said, *one day none of that will matter, and I'll be glad.* I thought she was being witty." She looked at Jude across the flames. "She was being honest. That's the worst part. She meant it."

"Do you think we can win?" Jude asked. Plainly, the way children ask, wanting the answer and dreading it equally.

Corvel considered lying. She had earned the right, she supposed, and it would be kinder.

"No," she said. "Not the way armies win. But I've seen battles turn on stranger things than a swordspeaker with a grudge against the dark. And I've read enough history to know the odds are always written after the fighting's done." She managed a smile. "Ask me again when we're behind walls with sixty thousand reasons to be certain."

Jude laughed despite themselves, and Sera stirred from her meditation by the fire's edge, and Tam came back from scouting the road with bread they had absolutely stolen, and for one hour the camp was almost warm.

But late that night, Corvel woke to find Jude standing alone at the fire's ashes, sword drawn, watching the blade glow faintly in the dark like a captive ember. Not speaking steel. Just looking.

"Couldn't sleep?" she asked.

"I was thinking about my mother's voice," Jude said. "And about the founder. My blood. And I decided something." They sheathed the sword with a soft click, and turned, and in the starless dark their eyes were steady for the first time since the wardstone shattered. "Whatever's under that mountain — whatever my family did, whatever Veyra bought — when we get there, I'm not going as their descendant or their debt. I'm going as me. Jude Winterbourne. Who sings badly and packs well and is very tired of other people deciding things on their behalf."

Corvel nodded slowly, and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were burning.

"Good," she managed. "Hold onto that. It's worth more than the sword."

Above them the sky stayed black and empty, but somewhere past the eastern ridgeline, Aurelspire waited with its ten days, and beneath the mountains the under-court waited with its patience, and between them walked four people with nothing left to lose and one thing left to prove.

The road bent east. They took it.
