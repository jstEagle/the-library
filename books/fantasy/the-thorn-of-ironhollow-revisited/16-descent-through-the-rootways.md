## 16. Descent Through the Rootways

The mouth of the Rootways did not look like a door. It looked like a wound.

It opened in the mountainside above the ruined shrine of Vel Ashen, a cleft in the rock no wider than a coffin stood on end, and from it came a breath of air so old that it seemed to carry the memory of every living thing that had ever exhaled into the dark. Moss hung at its lip like a green curtain drawn aside. The stone around the entrance was veined with something pale — not quartz, not bone, though it resembled both — and when I touched it, it was warm.

Warm stone, three days' climb above the snowline, on the last morning of autumn.

"That's the mountain's blood," Sera said behind me. She had her pack straps cinched tight, her star-shard wrapped in wool against her chest where it glowed faintly through the fabric, a second heartbeat she carried everywhere now. "The old faith called it *the patient warmth*. We used to say the world was not dead beneath us. Only sleeping. And that everything sleeping dreams of waking."

"And we're going to walk into its dream," Corvel said.

She stood at my shoulder with her sword slung low and her armor patched with river-iron, the scar across her ribs still angry-red beneath her shirt. She should have been resting another month. She had told us so herself, and then she had tightened her gauntlets anyway, because Dame Corvel Ashgrave had never once in her life done the thing she ought.

"We're going to walk under it," I corrected, and heard how small my voice sounded against all that stone. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" Corvel asked, and smiled the thin smile she saved for things that deserved no better.

* * *

We went down in single file, Sera first, because the tunnels answered her.

That was the strangest part, in those first hours — stranger than the dark, which was total and absolute once we left daylight behind; stranger than the cold that crept up through our boots like water wicking through cloth. It was the way the Rootways seemed to know her. At every branching she would pause, lay two fingers against the wall, close her eyes, and listen — truly listen, the way other people listened for birdsong or a child crying in the night — and then she would choose. Left. Right. Down. Always down.

"You can hear the way," I said, somewhere past the third hour, when my legs had gone wooden and the darkness had pressed so long against my eyes that they ached with the effort of staying open.

"Not the way," Sera said. "The faith. The acolytes built these passages, before the under-court turned inward, before anyone thought to fear what lived below. They carved prayers into the walls as they dug. Every junction is a verse. Every slope is a line of the litany." Her fingers trailed along the stone as she walked, reading it the way a blind woman reads a letter from someone she loves. "I forgot most of my faith when I ran. But your hands never forget a psalm. Not entirely."

"What does it say?" I asked. "The litany."

Sera was quiet for eleven steps. I counted them because counting kept me sane.

"It says: *we descend not to escape the light but to learn what the light is for.*"

Corvel snorted softly behind me. "Priests," she said. "They always make the hole sound holier than the digging."

But I noticed she walked closer to Sera after that. Closer to all of us. In the dark, the space between people becomes a thing you measure, and none of us wanted much of it anymore.

* * *

We slept in a chamber shaped like an egg, its ceiling lost somewhere above the reach of our little flame, its floor dusted with a powder so fine it rose around our ankles in ghostly halos when we moved. Sera said it was the shed skin of centuries — limestone weeping itself away grain by grain, building stalagmites in the deep places that would not be finished growing until long after everyone I loved was dead. There is a strange comfort in that kind of arithmetic. The mountain does not hurry. The mountain will outlast Veyra, outlast the free cities, outlast whatever bargain we meant to strike in the court below.

I lay awake longer than the others, listening.

At first I thought the singing was Sera, murmuring in her sleep. Then I thought it was water moving through some hidden vein. But when I pressed my palm flat against the floor, I felt it: a vibration too deep for ears, rising through the bones of my hand, my wrist, my arm, my chest, until it wasn't a sound at all but a feeling — a vast, slow, patient note, sustained as only stone can sustain anything.

The mountain was singing to itself. Or dreaming aloud. Or mourning.

I did not sleep well after that.

* * *

On the second day underground — if "day" means anything in a place where time is measured in torches rather than suns — we began to pass the sleeping things.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only oath I have left that hasn't been spent: I do not fully know what they were.

The first one lay in a side gallery we crossed without meaning to, a hollow off the main passage where the air moved differently, warmer, wetter. Our light caught something pale and enormous folded against the far wall, and for one terrible moment I thought it was a fallen statue — some ancient guardian carved in white marble, knees drawn up, head bowed, hands clasped over what might have been a face. Then the hands shifted. A fraction. The way a sleeper shifts, drawing a blanket higher, and settles again.

Sera pulled us past without stopping, her voice a thread: "*Don't wake it. Don't name it. Walk.*"

"What is it?" Corvel breathed.

"A pilgrim," Sera said. "Or what pilgrims became, in the last years of the faith. Those who came down to hear the court sing and never found their way back up. The dark takes them slowly. Kindly, maybe. I don't know." A pause. Her hand found mine in the blackness, brief and hard, then let go. "I knew one of them. Once."

We passed four more before the day ended. One had been asleep so long that moss grew on its shoulders, luminous moss, feeding on nothing we could see, casting a faint blue glow that made the whole chamber look like the bottom of a drowned sea. As we crossed it, the great shape sighed — a sound like wind through a canyon, like a bell struck underwater — and every hair on my body stood upright, and my hand went to the oath-sword's hilt before my mind could stop it.

And the sword hummed.

Not the hungry hum of the night the wards broke. Something gentler. Almost a greeting. And in the blue half-light I saw the sleeping giant's face turn, very slightly, toward the sound — toward *me*, toward the blade — and settle again, the way a child settles when it hears its mother's footsteps in another room.

"They remember swordspeakers," Sera whispered. "The old ones. Your order sang them to sleep, ages ago, when the world above was too loud. That's part of what the oaths were *for*, Jude. Not just war. Lullabies."

I walked the rest of that passage with my hand resting on the hilt, not gripping it, just touching it, the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of something frightened. Of something that trusted you.

I had spent my whole life thinking of the sword as a weapon I'd inherited. It had taken the underside of the world to teach me it was also a promise. And promises, I was learning, are the heaviest things there are.

* * *

The singing stone came on the third descent.

We heard it before we saw it — a harmonic threading through the mountain's low drone, high and clear and unmistakably intentional, a melody moving against the drone the way a voice moves against a drum. Sera stopped so suddenly that Corvel nearly walked into her back.

"That's not the mountain," Sera said. "That's the gate-singers. We're close."

The tunnel widened, and then widened again, and then opened into a cathedral of stone so vast our torchlight couldn't find its ceiling. Stalactites hung from the invisible heights like the pipes of some impossible organ, hundreds of them, thousands, each one humming its own note as the mountain's breath moved through it, and together they made music — real music, structured and mournful and gorgeous, a requiem that had been playing since before the first human city raised its first wall.

We stood in the entrance for a long time. None of us spoke. Even Corvel, who met beauty like an opponent across a dueling ground, had removed her helmet, and her face in the torchlight was young in a way I'd never seen it.

"The acolytes believed the stars were lit by a song," Sera said quietly. "That the under-court sang the first light into the sky, back when the court still loved what it had made. And that the stars stay lit only so long as the song continues." She looked up into the dark, at the sourceless music falling around us like rain. "When Veyra sold her soul, she didn't just buy a throne. She silenced a verse. And everything since — the wards breaking, the stars going out — has been the song stumbling."

"Then we give it back the missing word," I said.

"Yes." Sera turned to me, and in the torchlight her eyes were wet, though her voice was steady as bedrock. "And Jude — the court will not simply hand it over. Souls are not coins. What was bought must be *unbought*. There will be a price. There is always a price, at gates like this one. The old faith had a word for it." She hesitated, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to tell the truth — perhaps the last easy truth any of us would get. "*Toll*," she said. "The toll of the threshold. Paid in memory. Paid in self."

Corvel's jaw tightened. "You're telling us the door charges admission out of our own heads."

"I'm telling you what I was taught as a girl in the dark," Sera said. "And that I fled before I ever had to pay it. So I don't know what form it takes. I don't know if it can be refused. I only know that everyone who taught me spoke of the toll the way soldiers speak of a battle they barely survived." She drew a breath. "Whatever waits at the gate — go in knowing what you might lose. Decide *now* what you can't."

I thought about it the way you think about amputating your own hand: quickly, because slowly would be unbearable, and honestly, because lies would be worse than useless down here.

My mother's face. That was the thing. Two hundred years of Winterbourne oaths, and the whole inheritance had come down to a woman who baked bread badly and sang worse and died believing her child was safe inside the wards, and I could not — I would not —

No. That was exactly the wrong answer, and I knew it even as I thought it. If I went down clutching her face like a hoarded coin, I would fail her more thoroughly than any toll ever could. She had given me her face freely. Everything freely given can be offered again.

"I'll pay what the gate asks," I said. "All of it. Whatever it wants."

Corvel looked at me a long moment. Then she nodded once, soldier-short, and said, "Same," and I believed her, because she had already lost a queen, a banner, and a reputation; there was very little left in her inventory worth guarding.

Sera said nothing. She only closed her eyes, and I saw her lips move through some final verse of the litany — *we descend not to escape the light* — and then she shouldered her pack and pointed down the cavern's far throat, where the music gathered itself into a single sustained chord, deepening, darkening, becoming less a song than a held breath.

The gate was near. I could feel it in my teeth.

* * *

The last passage sloped down at an angle no stair could match and no rope ease, a slick chute of polished stone worn smooth by ten thousand centuries of water that no longer fell. We descended it on our backs, feet-first, letting ourselves slide by degrees, and the singing stone swelled around us until it filled my skull, until I could not tell whether I was hearing it or it was hearing me.

And then the slope ended, and I stood, and I saw the gate.

It was not a door. It was a *face* — or the suggestion of one, vast beyond reason, rendered in bas-relief across a wall of obsidian that drank our torchlight and gave nothing back. Closed eyes the size of shields. A mouth slightly parted, as if mid-word, mid-song, mid-sigh, frozen at the moment of speaking for so long that the moment had become geology. Before it stretched a floor of black glass, and written across that floor in lines of dim silver light — starlight, I realized with a jolt; trapped starlight, the same light as the shard against Sera's chest — was a script older than any alphabet I knew.

The silver lines were fading. Even as we watched, a character guttered and went out, like a candle in a draft.

"The binding," Sera whispered. She had the shard in her hands now, bare, unhidden, and its glow reached hungrily toward the dying script like a plant toward the sun. "The original binding. The one Veyra's soul anchors. It's almost spent. That's why the stars died — the words themselves are going out."

"How long?" Corvel asked.

Sera didn't answer immediately. She knelt at the threshold, laid the shard against the black glass, and read — really read, faster now, the old faith rising in her like water into a dry channel. When she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"Days. Perhaps fewer."

The great stone face hung above us, eyes closed, mouth open, waiting. And beneath its silence, beneath the mountain's endless drone, I felt — or imagined, or was chosen to feel — a single new note enter the music. Faint. Distant. Rising.

Like something far below, turning over in its sleep.

Like something noticing, at last, that it had visitors.

Sera rose to her feet beside me. Corvel set her hand on her sword. And I stepped forward onto the black glass, toward the open mouth of the mountain's oldest prayer, carrying nothing but my name, my oath, and the certain knowledge that whatever the gate asked of me, I had already decided to pay it.
