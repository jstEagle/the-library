## 17. The Price of Passage

The gate had been waiting for them longer than they had been walking toward it.

Sera felt that truth settle into her bones as they came up out of the last gallery of the Rootways and the tunnel simply *stopped* — not collapsed, not blocked, but ended, the way a sentence ends, with a finality that made her teeth ache. Before them opened a cavern so vast that the light of Tam's borrowed lantern died a few feet past its threshold, swallowed like a coin dropped into deep water. And in the middle of that darkness stood the gate.

She remembered it from before — from another life, another name, when she had worn grey wool and gone barefoot on cold stone and called herself an acolyte of the under-court without understanding what she served. The gate was two slabs of obsidian taller than the spire of any free city church, standing apart but leaning toward one another at their crowns, so that the space between them formed a shape: a doorway, or a keyhole, or — if you were foolish enough to look too long — a mouth.

The stone was singing.

Not loudly. Not even melodically. It was a sound below sound, a vibration Sera felt in the fillings of her teeth and the hollows of her knees, a note held so long it stopped being music and became weather. The singing stone of the Rootways had followed them all the way down, growing louder with each mile, and here at the gate it gathered itself into something almost like speech.

"It knows we're here," Corvel said quietly. She had one hand on the wall, and Sera could see by the lantern's gutter that the knight's knuckles had gone white. The wound in her side had closed days ago, but the fever had left her thinner, grayer, moving like a woman who no longer entirely trusted her own body to hold. "Does it always know?"

"Always," Sera said. "It's not a door, Dame Ashgrave. It's a listening thing."

Jude stood closest to the gate — of course they did; Jude always stood where the rest of them flinched from standing — with the oath-sword across their back and the star-shard wrapped against their chest, and Sera watched them tilt their head the way they did when they were hearing something the others couldn't. Swordspeaking had never worked down here. Jude had tried it once, three days ago, in a tunnel full of sleeping pale shapes, and their voice had come out flat and dead as a struck bell with a cracked lip. But something else answered in them now. Something older than the oath. Sera saw it in the way the dark around Jude seemed to lean in, attentive, curious.

"What's the toll?" Jude asked. No preamble. No prayer. That was Jude all over — they walked into holy places the way farmers walked into fields.

And because there was no use lying, because the gate would not be lied to and neither, anymore, could she, Sera said: "A memory. One apiece."

* * *

They made camp at the mouth of the last gallery rather than press on through the night, which Sera suspected was less prudence than cowardice dressed in practical clothes. None of them wanted to stand before the gate after dark. The lantern flame burned strangely here — slow, thick, orange as hearthlight instead of white — and the shadows it threw were patient.

Corvel built a fire out of nothing, because there was nothing to burn; she had carried a brick of compressed peat since Marrowgate for exactly this kind of place, and watching her strike the flint with steady hands Sera thought, not for the first time, that the disgraced knight was the only one among them who understood ritual. Jude spoke words that became edges. Sera herself had once believed that faith was a matter of doctrine. But Corvel knew that holiness lived in small repeated acts: the fire laid the same way every night, the watch rotated in the same order, the boots set side by side at the edge of the blanket-roll. When everything else is stripped away, Sera thought, what you keep doing is who you are.

"So explain it," Jude said, when the fire had caught and the dark beyond it had retreated to a respectful distance. "All of it. You said the gate takes memories. You've been through before. What does that mean, Sera? What actually happens?"

"I don't know what happens," Sera admitted. She sat with her arms around her knees, and the star-shard lay between them in its wrappings, glowing faintly through the cloth like a lamp under a bedsheet. It had grown brighter as they descended. It had been growing brighter for weeks, and none of them had said aloud what that might mean. "I went through as an acolyte, when I took my vows. Everyone does. The elders told us it was a blessing — *the court asks only what you can carry, and gives you back lighter.* We memorized that. We sang it." Her mouth twisted. "Nobody ever told us what we'd lost. That's the cruelty of it. You come out the other side feeling clean and empty and grateful for the emptiness, because you don't know enough to mourn."

Corvel fed the fire another twist of dried moss. "But you know now. You've had years to work out what's missing."

"Some of it." Sera looked into the flames. "I know there was a person. My teacher. The one whose death made me run. I know they existed because I wrote about them afterward — letters, half-finished arguments I kept having on paper. I read my own handwriting describing someone I couldn't remember, and I grieved them like a stranger's ghost." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice of someone handling broken glass. "That's what the gate takes. Not the memory whole. It takes the *livingness* of it. You keep the shape. The warmth goes into the stone."

"And it keeps it?" Jude asked. "Or eats it?"

"That depends," Sera said, "on what we find on the other side."

Silence, then. The singing of the gate filled it, that endless subterranean note, and Sera realized she had begun unconsciously breathing in time with it, the way she had as a girl, and made herself stop.

"We could turn back," Corvel said at last. It cost her something to say it; Sera watched the words come out of her sideways, like a soldier admitting a wound. "Aurelspire has ten days. Maybe eleven. If we turned back now—"

"We'd get there in time to die behind walls instead of in a hole in the ground," Jude said. There was no cruelty in it. Jude never dealt in cruelty; they dealt in arithmetic, the plain and terrible sums. "The shard doesn't open the way back up, Sera said. The only road to the soul runs through the court. And Veyra's army—" They stopped. Started again. "There isn't another road. There's just this one."

"There's one more thing," Sera said, and found that her hands had begun to shake, and made herself say it anyway, because they deserved to walk into the gate with their eyes open. "You should choose your memory tonight. Not at the threshold. Here, where you can think. Because the gate doesn't take what you offer." She met each of their eyes in turn — Corvel first, then Jude, holding longer. "It takes what you *least* want to give. It reaches in and finds the warmest thing in you. That's the price. Not sacrifice — sacrifice would be easy. This is surrender. You put your hand through the door and let something else decide what you are."

* * *

Jude slept badly, and Sera, keeping second watch, listened to them not-sleeping from across the dead fire.

Near midnight — if midnight meant anything a mile beneath the roots of mountains — Jude gave up the pretense and sat up, wrapping their blanket around their shoulders like a cloak, and came to sit beside her against the tunnel wall. For a while neither of them spoke. Below them, faintly, something vast turned over in its sleep and resumed its dreaming.

"My mother," Jude said finally.

Sera said nothing. Waiting was a discipline she had learned twice — once in the cloister, once on the road — and both times she had learned it too late.

"She died when I was nine. Fever, the winter the ward-keepers said magic couldn't reach us and the fevers proved them liars anyway." Jude's voice was level, reciting, the way people speak when emotion is a weight they're carrying very carefully across ice. "Everyone always says they remember their mother's face. I actually do. Not a story about it. Not a portrait. The face itself. Every time I close my eyes I can see her turning around in the kitchen with flour on her hands, saying my name." A breath. "If the gate takes the warmest thing — that's it. That's the warmest thing I have. I've been trying all evening to think of something warmer to give it instead, and I can't, and I'm starting to understand that maybe that's the point. Maybe it wants exactly that. The one thing that makes you *you*."

"You could still turn back," Sera said softly.

"No." And then, quieter, with a huff that was almost a laugh: "You know what frightens me most? Not losing it. Losing it and not knowing I've lost it. Walking out of that gate feeling lighter and calling it peace." Jude pulled the blanket tighter. "Promise me something. If I come out the other side smiling about it — tell me what I paid. Don't let me be grateful."

"I promise," Sera said, and hated the gate for making love sound like a contract, and promised anyway.

* * *

Corvel did not sleep at all.

Dawn, such as it was — a grayening of the air, a shifting of the dark — found her sitting alone by the cold fire, sharpening a sword she could barely lift, and when Sera crouched beside her the knight didn't pretend otherwise. She just kept working the whetstone along the steel, slow strokes, the rhythm of a woman praying in the only language left to her.

"The day I was knighted," Corvel said, without looking up.

Sera waited.

"Twenty-two years old. Kneeling in the Starfall Cathedral with the whole court watching, and Queen Maren laid the blade on my shoulder, and her hands were shaking — everyone thought it was age, but I was close enough to see it wasn't age, it was *fear*, she was afraid of the man standing behind her, the man whose shield-daughter I already secretly was." The whetstone paused. Resumed. "It was the best day of my life, and it was also the day I chose wrong, and those are the same memory, Sera. That's the trap of it. If I give up the knighting, I give up the moment I swore to protect her. If I keep it—" She laughed, short and bitter. "If I keep it, I keep the shame too, because they're braided together, honor and failure, same cord. The gate will take the honor. It'll leave me the failure, warmless. A shape. Like your teacher."

"Then give it freely," Sera said. "Take the choice away from the gate. Choose the loss before it chooses for you. That's all the agency we get down here — but it's not nothing. It's never nothing."

Corvel finally looked at her, and her eyes were dry and very old. "When did you learn to talk like that?"

"On the road," Sera said. "From a farmer's kid who talks to swords."

Something moved across the knight's face — brief, unguarded, gone again — and she nodded once, and began packing the camp.

* * *

As for Sera, she told no one what she intended to give. She hardly let herself think it. She kept it wrapped in the back of her mind the way the star-shard was wrapped in cloth, and all through the last meal, all through the striking of the camp, all through the long walk down the final gallery with the singing stone swelling around them until it was less a sound than a country, she held it shut behind her teeth.

Because the gate would choose anyway. That was what she had told them. The gate reached in and took the warmest thing.

And Sera had spent the whole descent pretending she didn't know which memory in her burned brightest, which one she touched in the dark watches of the night the way you touch a bruise, which one had driven her out of the grey wool and the bare feet and up through the Rootways into a world of mud and war. Not her teacher's death. That grief was real but it was old scar-tissue now, cooled to stone.

No. The warmest thing was smaller than that. Warmer.

It was the reason itself. The *moment*. Standing in the reliquary beneath the temple, seventeen years old, holding the ledger open in her hands and reading the entry in her own master's handwriting — *the binding holds the stars; the court has bought a queen; the stars will go out within her lifetime* — and feeling, beneath the horror, beneath the sick lurch of the world tilting, one bright treacherous coal of a thought: *now I know. Now I am the one who knows. Whatever I do next, it will matter.*

That was what she had fled carrying. Not knowledge. Purpose. The burning certainty that she had been chosen — by accident, by horror, but chosen — and that her life had a shape at last.

And she had built seven years on top of that coal. The running, the hiding, the finding of Corvel, the finding of Jude. Every step of the long road home to the gate had been lit by it.

The gate would take the coal. It would leave her the ash: a woman who had run, for reasons unknown, from a place she could not remember, carrying a shard toward a purpose she could no longer feel. She would come out the other side hollowed-out and *grateful*, the way she had been grateful at seventeen, and this time she would know enough to mourn.

Unless she gave it first. Unless she stood at the threshold and opened her own hand and said: *take this, I choose this, it is mine to lose.*

* * *

The gate filled the world.

Up close, the obsidian slabs were not black at all but deep, depthless violet, shot through with veins of dim gold that pulsed in time with the singing, and looking into them was like looking into water over an abyss. The space between the slabs shimmered — not with light, but with attention. Sera felt it settle over the three of them the way a great bird settles over a field: considering, unhurried, ancient.

"Say nothing unless it addresses you," she murmured. "And whatever it shows you — however it tempts you — keep your hand open. Closed fists it pries open. Open hands it only borrows from."

The singing changed.

It resolved, slowly, terribly, into something like a voice — not heard through the ears but arriving fully formed inside the skull, the way you sometimes wake knowing a dream was speaking to you. It did not use words. It used *knowings*. Sera felt it move through her like cold water through sand, touching everything, weighing everything, and she heard Jude gasp beside her, heard Corvel's armor creak as her spine went rigid, and then the gate said, in the language of things-understood:

***Three. Warm. Give.***

"I'll go first," Sera said aloud, and stepped forward into the space between the stones.

The shimmer closed around her like the surface of a lake. It did not hurt. Nothing about it hurt, and that was the worst part — it was gentle as sleep, thorough as drowning. She felt it find the coal. Of course it did. It was the gate; the coal was the brightest thing in her; there had never really been a choice, except the choice she had already made, on the road, in the dark watches, opening her hand in advance.

*I give it,* she said, in the wordless tongue the gate spoke. *Freely. Take it.*

And the moment left her.

She felt it go — felt it the way you feel a tooth leave the gum, a hot bright tearing that was over before the pain could arrive. The reliquary. The ledger. The trembling seventeen-year-old certainty of being *chosen*. Gone. In its place: smooth emptiness, a socket, a shape. She knew she had run from the under-court. She knew why in outline, the way you know a room you've only seen in a drawing — there had been a discovery, there had been a death, there had been a flight. The facts remained, arranged like pressed flowers. The livingness was in the stone.

She came out the other side of the shimmer and stood blinking in the violet light, feeling lighter, feeling *clean*, feeling — and she caught herself doing it, caught the gratitude rising in her like warm water, and bit down on it until she tasted blood, and held on to the mourning instead. Held on to the grief she'd promised herself. It was hers. It was all she had left of the coal.

Behind her, Corvel stepped through.

Sera turned in time to see it: the knight kneeling of her own accord before the gate, head bowed, sword laid flat across both palms like an offering, and the shimmer closing over her, and the long silence, and then Corvel rising again with a strange slackness in her face, a face suddenly missing its keystone. She staggered. Sera caught her arm.

"What did it take?" Corvel whispered. "What did it — I know I had a day. I know there was a day that mattered. I can feel the hole where—"

"The knighting," Sera said gently. "You gave the knighting."

Corvel stared at her own empty palms. "Then I have no oath," she said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Twenty-two years of service and the beginning of it is *stone* now, it's in the *wall*—" She stopped. Breathed. Straightened by inches, the old drill, spine and shoulders, the body remembering what the memory no longer could. "Keep telling me," she said. "Whatever you remember of me. Keep telling me until it grows back."

"I will," Sera promised. "Jude. Your turn."

Jude had not moved.

They stood before the gate with their hand resting on the hilt of the oath-sword — the sword that had been silent since the wards fell, silent as a grave-marker — and Sera saw their throat work once, twice, and saw them take their hand off the hilt deliberately, finger by finger, the way you unclench a fist around something precious so it can be taken cleanly.

"I know what it wants," Jude said. Not to Sera. To the gate. Their voice rang oddly in the vast dark, small and stubborn. "My mother's face. Flour on her hands. My name in her mouth. Take it, then. But hear me — I'm not giving it because you asked. I'm giving it because a soul is trapped under this mountain and stars are dead in the sky and someone has to pay, and I'd rather it be me than the world." They lifted their chin. "That's not tribute. That's terms."

The singing swelled.

For one vertiginous instant Sera thought the gate was angry — the note climbed and climbed, the gold veins flaring — and then she understood, with a shock that dropped through her like a stone through deep water: it was *delighted*. Something had spoken to it in its own grammar. Something had come to the door of the lonely thing beneath the world and offered not submission but *bargain*, and the gate, which had eaten ten thousand bowed heads, had never once been offered terms.

The shimmer rose around Jude like a tide.

Sera watched her friend's face as the memory left them. Watched the exact moment it went — a flicker behind the eyes, a softness collapsing, the particular grief of a hand closing on nothing. Jude swayed. Did not fall. Stood there breathing hard in the violet light, staring at their own open palm as though it belonged to a stranger, and Sera crossed to them fast and took their hand, because that was the other thing she had learned about gates, the thing the elders never sang:

What the stone keeps, the living can carry instead.

"Hold on to us," she said, and Corvel's gauntleted hand closed over Jude's shoulder from the other side, and the three of them stood locked together at the threshold of the deep court, hollowed-out, holding one another's losses like candles against a wind.

Beyond the gate, the dark went down and down, and somewhere at the bottom of it something turned — slowly, attentively, with the sound of mountains changing their minds — and began, for the first time in two hundred years, to climb toward the light.

The way in was open.

The way back, Sera understood, watching the shimmer seal itself behind them like water closing over a sunk ship, was not.
