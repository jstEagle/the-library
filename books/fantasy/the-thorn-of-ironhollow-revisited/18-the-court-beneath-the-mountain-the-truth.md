## 18. The Court Beneath the Mountain (The Truth)

The gate closed behind us without a sound.

That was the worst of it — I had braced for a boom, for stone grinding on stone, for some final pronouncement that we were now and forever swallowed. Instead the dark simply finished, the way a held breath finishes, and there was nothing behind us anymore. Sera stood at my shoulder with her hand pressed flat against my back, as if she could hold me upright by touch alone. Corvel was somewhere behind us both, breathing in short, careful pulls. Hollowed out, all three of us. The toll had taken its price from each of us, and what remained walked forward because walking forward was all that remained.

The Rootways had been narrow, close, lit by the shard's stubborn glow and by veins of something luminous in the rock that Sera called starblood and would not explain further. But here, at last, the tunnels opened.

I have no honest way to describe the court beneath the mountain except to say it was like standing inside a night sky that had been turned wrong side out.

Obsidian everywhere — not polished, not cut, but grown, in vast slow petals and pillars, black glass that caught the shard's light and multiplied it until the whole chamber seemed hung with small cold stars. A ceiling so far above us that it might have been sky. A floor that gave back our reflections threefold, fivefold, tenfold, so that an army of Judes walked where I walked, an army of Seras at their shoulders, and every one of them looked more afraid than I felt, which I hadn't thought possible.

And in the center of it, the throne.

I say *throne* because there is no better word, but it was less a seat than a wound in the world — a place where the obsidian bent inward, folding over itself like a flower deciding to become a fist. And in the fold of it sat the thing that bought a queen's soul.

It was smaller than I expected. That sounds absurd, but it's true. After weeks of Veyra's armies and burned villages and Sera's firelit stories — the under-court as a devouring dark, a hunger with teeth — I expected something vast. What sat in the fold of the glass was roughly the size of a person, if you were generous about it, and shaped like one only the way a shadow is shaped like the thing that casts it. It did not move. It did not breathe. Its edges kept sliding out of focus, the way a word slides when you say it too many times.

It was looking at us. I knew that without eyes, the way you know someone is standing behind you on a dark road.

"Well," said Corvel, behind me, very quietly. "We're announced, at least."

"Announced how?" I whispered.

"You're still alive," she said. "That's the announcement."

Sera moved first. Of course she did — she had been an acolyte here once, before she ran, before she forgot why she'd run because the toll had eaten that reason at the gate. She knelt. Not groveling; it was older than groveling, some posture learned in childhood, hands open on the black floor, head bowed but not fully down.

"We come freely," she said, and her voice echoed strangely, doubling off the obsidian until it sounded like a congregation of one. "We ask audience."

The silence went on so long I began to count my own heartbeats and lost track somewhere past two hundred. Then the thing spoke.

It did not speak aloud. There was no sound at all. The words arrived the way memory arrives — already inside me, complete, wearing my own voice like a borrowed coat.

***Visitors.***

One word, and the whole chamber shivered with it. Every reflection of me flinched at once.

"Yes," Sera said. "Three."

***Three.*** The word turned over slowly, the way you'd turn a coin found on a road, checking which face it showed. ***Two hollowed. One half-hollowed. And one who carries a piece of the sky in a box of bone.***

My hand went to the satchel at my hip before I could stop it — the star-shard, wrapped in its layers of silk and ward-cloth, warm against my ribs like a second heart. The thing's attention settled on it, and the warmth flared, and I felt the shard answer it, a low hum through the leather and my fingers and my bones, like a dog hearing its name across a valley.

"It isn't yours," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Not anymore. You sold it. Or — you took payment for it. Either way, the debt runs the other direction now."

The attention shifted to me. Fully. All of it.

I want to tell you I stood firm under that gaze. What actually happened is that every fear I owned came up out of me at once, uninvited: the sword's hum on the night the stars died, the village burning, Corvel bleeding in the candle market, my mother's face gone from me like a window painted shut, the terrible arithmetic I'd been doing since Aurelspire — *one oath, weighed against everything.* Under that gaze I was transparent, and the thing read me the way Sera read the shard: patiently, thoroughly, missing nothing.

Then it did something I will never be able to explain properly to anyone, including myself.

It laughed.

There was no sound. But I felt it — a ripple through the obsidian, through the floor, through the soles of my feet, a shaking that was unmistakably, horribly, *amusement.*

***You speak of debts.*** The words came slow, tasting themselves. ***You, whose blood wrote the contract.***

The chamber changed. Not dramatically — the reflections simply rearranged, and suddenly the army of Judes around me was not an army of the present. They wore old clothes. Old faces. One of them stood at a wardstone with his hands pressed to it, and even blurred across generations I knew the shape of that jaw, that stubborn set of the shoulders, because I saw it every time I caught my reflection in rainwater.

My ancestor. The founder. The man who built Ironhollow to hide the soul, and then — when hiding wasn't enough — sold the wards themselves to buy the hiding time. Sera had found it in the shard's light, weeks ago, and I had carried the truth since like a stone behind my sternum: my family's name on the deed of betrayal.

"He tried to protect it," I said. "Whatever else he did—"

***He tried to protect himself.*** No cruelty in it. That was the strange part. The voice corrected me the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. ***He was afraid. Fear writes most contracts. His fear bought your valley two hundred years. Her fear bought her a crown. Yours—***

It paused. Considered me again.

***Yours has brought you here. Fear makes poor architects, but it does make pilgrims.***

Corvel stepped up beside me. I heard the creak of her armor, the deliberate weight of her boots — she planted herself the way she'd planted herself at the rearguard in Marrowgate's sewers, between us and whatever came. "Enough riddling," she said. "You know why we've come. The soul Veyra pawned to you. We want it returned."

***Returned.*** Something moved in the fold of the throne — not the thing itself, but around it, a stirring like wind through a room that had known no wind. ***Do you know what she traded? Do you understand what sits in this court, knight?***

"No," Corvel said. "That's rather why we descended four days through singing stone. To learn."

The thing rose.

It didn't stand so much as unfold — the way darkness unfolds when a lamp goes out, filling corners you'd sworn were already full. It grew as it came toward us, or perhaps it had always been this size and the throne had made it look small, and I understood suddenly that scale meant nothing here, that this thing was as large or as small as its loneliness required. Sera made a small wounded sound. Corvel's hand found her sword hilt and stopped there, not drawing, because we had both understood at the gate: steel meant nothing to something that had never known a body to cut.

It stopped three paces away. Close enough that I could see — or thought I could see — that its surface was not smooth. It was crowded. Faint shapes moved within it, deep down, like fish under winter ice. Faces. Hands. Fragments of everything it had ever taken in trade, suspended and preserved, the way amber holds insects.

***Since the stars were lit,*** it said, and for the first time the voice had weight in it, a downward drag, ***I have kept the binding. The stars do not burn on their own. Nothing burns on its own. Someone must hold the flame to the wick of the world, always, without sleeping, without leaving. I have held it longer than your kind has had names.***

"That's a lie," Sera breathed. "The acolytes taught — they said the court devours, that it hoards light, that it—"

***Your teachers needed a monster,*** it said gently, and the gentleness was worse than any roar. ***Monsters can be served. Loneliness cannot. So they dressed my keeping as hunger and my custody as greed, and they worshipped the story instead of the truth, and when the truth inconvenienced them they fled it, as you fled, little runaway.***

Sera went white. I put my hand on her arm. She was trembling.

"And Veyra's soul," Corvel said, iron-steady, though I heard the effort under it. "What did you want with a queen's soul?"

***Nothing.*** The word dropped like a stone into water. ***She offered. I refused. She offered again, with terms written by her own ambition — power, succession, a throne secured against every rival living and dead. I told her the binding requires only maintenance. I told her souls are heavy things to store. She did not believe a god could say no. So I took the payment she forced upon me, because refusal, thrice offered, becomes theft of hope — and I am owed enough grief without adding hers.***

The ice-faces within it stirred. And among them — I saw it, and my stomach turned over — a woman's face, proud and sharp-boned, recognizable from the banners and the coins and the wanted-notices that had chased us across half the free cities. Veyra's soul, floating in the dark like a moth in resin.

"She's in there," I said. "Alive?"

***Souls do not die. They wait. Hers waits loudly.*** Another ripple of that terrible amusement, thinner now. ***She bargains even in storage. She has offered me the free cities. She has offered me the sun. She is very bored and very certain someone will come for her eventually, and she is right, because here you are.***

"So return her," I said.

***No.***

The word filled the whole chamber. The reflected armies of ourselves all spoke it together, ten thousand mouths.

"The stars are dying," I said, and my voice cracked and I let it. "The sky over my home has been empty for a season. Villages burn. Armies march under a black heaven and call it destiny. Whatever your keeping costs you — the cost of stopping is higher. Give her back."

The thing regarded me for a long moment. Then it drifted closer, close enough that I could feel a pressure like deep water, and it did the strangest thing yet.

It reached toward the shard at my hip. Not taking. Hovering. And the shard sang — a thin, clear note, like a wet finger on a goblet's rim — and the note passed through me, and for one heartbeat I felt what the thing felt.

Loneliness is too small a word. This was loneliness with geology in it. Loneliness measured in the birth and death of languages. An age of holding the flame alone, watching the small bright lives above flicker and cluster and pair and sing together, never once able to join them, because the binding does not release its keeper, and the keeper had long ago forgotten whether it had ever chosen the keeping or been chosen for it.

I staggered. Sera caught me. When I could see again, the thing had withdrawn, and its attention had gone soft and strange, and I understood with a certainty that frightened me more than any threat could have:

It had shown me that on purpose. Not as torture. As introduction.

"You're lonely," I said. Stupidly. Enormously. The way you'd say it to a child crying in the dark.

***I am the longest continuous act in existence,*** it said. ***And no one has ever asked me anything except give, take, spare, curse. You are the first to ask what I feel.***

"Is that what you wanted?" Corvel asked slowly. "When you took Veyra's soul. Company?"

***I wanted the noise,*** it admitted, and the admission cost it something — I felt the chamber dim slightly, the way a room dims when someone exhales after holding breath for years. ***A soul in storage is not silent. Hers argues. Schemes. Recites the genealogy of her enemies to pass the time. It is the first voice this court has held in an age. And when the cities' emissaries demanded her return, generation after generation, I refused — yes, I refused — because returning her meant returning to the quiet. Judge me for it. Everyone must have one small sin. Mine is that I liked hearing someone else breathe.***

Silence. Even the obsidian seemed to lean in.

Sera knelt again, but differently this time — not the old acolyte's posture, something new, invented on the spot. "Then don't go back to the quiet," she said. "Change the terms. The binding holds the stars — does it require solitude? Or has it just *had* solitude?"

The thing went very still.

I watched understanding move through it like weather, and I felt the floor of my own thinking shift beneath me, because I saw where she was going, and I saw the shape of the price waiting at the end of that road, and it had my oath's name on it.

"There are three of us," Sera went on, softly, relentlessly. "Keepers needn't keep alone. And above us, right now, an army marches on Aurelspire, and the only coin we carry that could interest you—"

"*Sera,*" Corvel warned.

"—is freely given," Sera finished. "If he chooses it. If *he* offers it."

Every reflection of me turned to face me at once.

The thing drifted near again, and its attention wrapped around me like a cloak of deep water, and it spoke directly into the space behind my sternum, where the oath lived — the last swordspeaker's binding, the words spoken over me as a child, the name-tie that had hummed awake the night the wards fell and had burned in my blood ever since.

***An oath freely given,*** it murmured, ***is the oldest currency there is. Heavier than souls. Souls are *taken*, usually. Oaths must be *handed over*. I have held exactly one, in all my ages — your ancestor's, the coward's, and it bought him a valley and bought me two centuries of listening to him regret it.***

"I haven't agreed to anything," I said.

***No.*** Almost kindly. ***But you are doing the arithmetic. I can hear it. You have been doing it since the gate. One binding, weighed against the sky. Tell me, last swordspeaker — what does your oath weigh tonight?***

Above us — impossibly, through miles of mountain — I swear I felt it: a faint tremor in the obsidian, rhythmic, distant. Drums. Or siege engines. Or the first assault breaking against Aurelspire's walls while I stood in the dark doing sums with my soul.

The shard pulsed warm against my ribs. The stars, asking.

I opened my mouth to answer — I still don't know what I meant to say — and the thing raised one shadowed hand, gentle as a mother hushing a feverish child.

***Before you speak,*** it said, ***know this: I will not take a bargain made in panic. Come. Sit. The court has waited an eternity; it can wait one hour more. Let me show you what your oath truly is — and what the sky truly costs — and then choose, not as a soldier out of time, but as the first visitor I have ever had who might, possibly, be a friend.***

And the obsidian around us folded outward, opening — I saw it clearly — a hearth.

A hearth, beneath the mountain, in the court of the thing that ate the stars' keeper's sleep. Small flames kindled in it from nowhere, and the light they cast was warm, and it was the loneliest light I have ever seen.

Corvel's hand closed on my shoulder. Sera's breath caught. Somewhere overhead, the drums went on.

I walked toward the fire.
