## 19. The Bargain of Names

The obsidian court did not have a floor, exactly. It had a surface that remembered being a lake, and Jude's boots left ripples in the black glass long after they had stopped walking, rings of faint silver spreading outward and dying at the edge of hearing.

Jude stood at the center of it with Sera a pace behind and Corvel — fever-bright, half-carried, insisting on standing — leaning against her like a wall that had decided to lean back. Above them, where a ceiling should have been, the mountain went up forever: vaulted dark shot through with veins of dim red light, like embers seen through a closed eyelid. The thing they had come to bargain with sat at the far end of all that darkness, and it was not what Jude had expected.

It was not a throne. It was a hearth.

A ring of stones, ancient and soot-blackened, set into the living rock, and within it a fire that gave no heat. The flames were pale — star-colored, if stars could be taught to burn low and sad. Around the hearth, arranged with terrible care, were chairs. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Carved from the same black stone as everything else, each one different — some tall-backed and thronelike, some little more than stools, some clearly made for shapes that were never human. All of them empty. All of them facing the fire.

"It doesn't want worship," Sera had whispered on the descent, and now Jude understood. It wanted *company*. It had built an audience out of loneliness and waited two hundred years for anyone to sit down.

The voice, when it came, came from everywhere the firelight touched — from the walls, from the ripples underfoot, from somewhere behind Jude's own ribs.

*You are the one who speaks to steel.*

"I'm the last one," Jude said. Their voice sounded small and human in all that dark, and they found they didn't mind. Small and human was honest here. "And you're the one who bought a queen."

*Bought,* the court said, tasting the word. *She came offering. We only accepted what was held out. That is the oldest law of any market, swordspeaker. Even yours.*

Jude's hand drifted to the oath-sword at their hip — old habit, though the blade had been singing softly ever since they passed the gate, a note like a struck bell fading too slowly. "We're not here to haggle over blame. We're here for the soul of Veyra of Aurelspire. You're holding it."

*Holding.* Again the taste, again the slow delight in language, as if the thing beneath the mountain had been starved of conversation the way other prisoners were starved of bread. *Yes. It is warm. It is the warmest thing we have owned since the stars were lit. Do you know what we did before the stars, little oath?*

"No one does," Jude said. "Sera says you don't remember."

*Sera is correct, and Sera is afraid of us, and both things can be true.* A ripple passed through the fire, and for one instant Jude saw shapes in it — not faces, nothing so kind, but suggestions of vast attention turning like whales under ice. *Before the stars there was no above and below. There was only the dark, and we were its whole population, and we were not lonely because lonely requires knowing something else exists. Then your ancestors climbed up and lit the sky, and every light they hung was a door closing. We have been knocking ever since. Two hundred years ago, someone finally answered.*

"The wards," Jude said. Their mouth had gone dry. "Ironhollow's wards weren't broken. They were sold."

*Sold by the man who built them,* the court agreed, gentle as a knife laid flat against skin. *He hid his valley to keep the soul safe inside it, and then he grew old, and tired, and frightened of what he had done, and he came down here and traded his descendants' safety for our silence. He thought he was buying peace. He was buying us time. And when his bloodline thinned to a single child who could speak to steel — when it thinned to* you *— the last ward failed, and the woman Veyra walked in with her soul cupped in her hands like water, and asked what we would give her for it.*

"And you gave her a throne."

*We gave her what she asked for. What she does with it is her craft, not ours.* The fire sank low, then rose. *But you should understand what you ask, swordspeaker. The soul is not a coin we keep in a drawer. It is fuel. It is the last warmth in this house. If we return it, we return to the cold, and the cold is very old, and we remember less of ourselves every century we spend in it. We will not give up the only warm thing we own for nothing.*

"Name your price," Corvel rasped from beside Jude. Her voice was wrecked from the candle-market rearguard, from the fever, from ten days of dying by inches on the road. She straightened anyway, the way she always did, the way knights do even when knighthood has been taken from them. "You want warmth. You want company. Name what equals a soul."

The silence that followed was long enough that Jude heard their own heart, heard Sera's breath, heard — distantly, impossibly far above — the first faint tremor of something that might have been thunder or might have been cannon.

Then the court spoke, and it spoke only to Jude.

*An equal binding. A soul is a name given wholly, without reservation, unto another. To buy one back, we require the same coin. An oath freely given, complete, irrevocable — not a promise, not a vow, but a binding of the whole self. Do you possess such a thing, last swordspeaker? Or do you come to us with empty hands, having already spent your courage on the road?*

Jude knew.

They had known since the gate, maybe since the Rootways, maybe — if they were honest, and honesty was suddenly the only currency they trusted — since the night the stars went out and the sword hummed against their hip like something waking. An oath-bound swordspeaker carried one oath, the founding one, spoken at thirteen years old in the harvest vigil smoke with the whole village watching: *my name in service of the steel, my steel in service of the valley, until death unbinds me.* It was not a promise. It was the shape their magic took. It was why the words became edges. Every swordspeaking they had ever done was drawn against that account.

It was also the last of its kind. No children had been born to the gift in forty years. When Jude died, whatever gods or ancestors had kept the tradition alive would let it go out like a lamp in an empty room.

If they surrendered the oath here, they would not die. They would simply become, forever after, an ordinary person. A person who could hold a sword and swing it and be cut down by any soldier in Veyra's army. A person whose words were only words.

A person who could go home to Ironhollow and belong there, actually belong, instead of standing slightly apart from it their whole life, marked by the village's need.

The thought arrived uninvited and shameless: *you wanted this. On the quiet nights, watching Sera sleep by the fire, listening to Tam's stories of street children who needed no oaths to love each other — you wanted to be free of it.*

That was the trap in the bargain, Jude realized. Not the price itself, but the relief. Any oath given in secret hope of its ending was already hollowed, and hollow coins would not spend. The court would know. The court could taste sincerity the way Sera tasted lies.

So Jude stood in the rippling dark and made themselves look directly at the fire that gave no heat, and they searched their own heart with the same unsparing attention the court turned on them.

Did they want to live? Yes. Did they want the war ended? Desperately. Did they want, somewhere underneath everything, to put the sword down?

Also yes. And that yes could not be allowed near this decision. This decision had to be made the way Corvel held a rearguard — knowing the cost, walking into it with eyes open, for the sake of people who would never know her name.

"Above us," Jude said slowly, "an army is reaching the walls of Aurelspire. Veyra's army. Ten thousand soldiers who follow her because they believe her crown is legitimate — because her soul-sold bargain makes her untouchable. If we return her soul to her, the spell breaks. They'll see it break. The stars coming back won't just be light. It'll be proof that her power was stolen, that her reign is void." Jude swallowed. "You'd be saving a city. Maybe five. Maybe the whole map."

*Flattery,* the court observed, *is also currency. But continue.*

"It's not flattery, it's arithmetic. But here's my offer." Jude drew the sword.

The sound it made leaving the scabbard was not the scrape of steel on leather. It was a chord — low, resonant, filling the whole buried dark — and the veins of red light in the mountain ceiling flickered toward it like moths. In two hundred years the blade had hummed once, on the night the wards broke. Now it sang.

"This is my oath," Jude said. "Spoken when I was thirteen. Bound into this steel. The last swordspeaker's oath in the world. It is not a promise I made; it is what I *am*. My name lives in it. Every word I've ever spoken to metal has been borrowed against it." They turned the blade so the firelight ran along it, and for a moment — Jude would swear this later, and Sera would confirm it — the pale flames leaned toward the steel like a crowd leaning toward a speaker. "I give it to you. Freely. Completely. Irrevocably. Take the binding and the name inside it, take the magic and the lineage and the last of it, and give back the soul of Veyra of Aurelspire, whole and unspent, so that she may carry her own weight again."

The court was silent.

Then, softly, with something that in any other voice might have been wonder:

*Do you understand what you offer? This is not warmth. This is the last lit lamp of a dead tradition. This is two hundred years of your ancestors' voices folded into a single sentence. We could drink it for a century.*

"You could," Jude agreed. Their hands had begun to shake. They let them. "Or you could keep it as company. Put it by your fire. Listen to it. It's used to talking."

* * *

"Sera." Jude didn't turn around. "Tell me if I'm wrong."

Behind them, Sera made a sound that was half breath and half prayer. Then footsteps on the ringing glass, and her hand — thin, acolyte-calloused, still carrying a splinter of fallen starlight in a pouch at her belt — closed on Jude's shoulder.

"You're not wrong." Her voice cracked on the second word. "The law of equal binding is real. I fled the under-court rather than serve it, but I never stopped believing its laws were true, because they're older than belief. An oath freely given is the only coin it will accept. And yours—" she stopped, started again. "Jude, yours is worth more than Veyra's soul ever was. Hers she gave away to get something. Yours you're giving away to give something back. The court knows the difference. That's why it's listening."

"That's why it's terrifying," Corvel said. She had slid down to one knee despite herself, one hand pressed to the bandages at her side, and her grey eyes were fixed on Jude with an expression Jude had never seen her wear — not pity, not admiration, but recognition. The look of one person who pays prices meeting another. "There'll be no taking it back. You know that. No clever loophole, no third-act reversal. You speak those words and the magic leaves you forever, and in six months you'll stand in a field full of Veyra's soldiers with nothing in your hands but iron."

"I know."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I know." Jude finally turned. Sera's face was wet. Corvel's was calm as winter. Somewhere above them — through miles of stone, through the whole weight of the mountain — came a second tremor, longer than the first, and this time none of them pretended it was thunder. The battle for Aurelspire had begun. Somewhere up there, Tam was running messages through streets that were learning what cannons sounded like. Somewhere up there, five bickering cities' worth of defenders were holding a wall because a witness with ordinary words had shamed them into standing.

Time was running out in both worlds. Below, the court's patience; above, the wall.

"Ask me one question," Jude said to the fire. "The question that matters. Ask it, and I'll answer honestly, and then judge whether my oath spends true."

The flames stood very still.

*Why?*

Not *what do you gain*. Not *what do you fear*. Just that, naked and enormous, the way only an ancient and lonely thing can ask it.

Jude thought of Ironhollow. Of their mother's garden, of the harvest vigils, of the wardstone the villagers had begged them to mend with tears running down their faces because they believed the last swordspeaker could fix anything. They thought of Marrowgate's candle markets, of Tam's crooked grin, of Corvel holding a bridge alone, of Sera saying *I won't say what's hidden* and then, eventually, saying everything. They thought of a court full of empty chairs arranged around a cold fire by something that had been alone since before memory, and they understood — with the terrible empathy the last chapter of every hard story brings — that the monster below and the girl above and the knight and the thief and themselves were all, in the end, asking the universe the same question: *does anyone stay?*

"Because an oath is a way of staying," Jude said. "That's all it ever was. My ancestors bound themselves so the valley wouldn't be abandoned. I bound myself so the village wouldn't be alone. And you—"

They looked at the empty chairs.

"You've been alone so long you forgot that's what an oath is. So I'll teach you. Take mine. Keep it by your fire. Let it remind you what staying sounds like. And give the soul back so that a woman who sold hers can learn to carry her own weight, and a city that never chose her can choose itself instead."

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats. Four. Five.

Then the fire rose — not in fury but in something like hunger, something like joy, pale flames climbing higher than the hearth-stones, and the voice came from everywhere at once, deep enough to shake dust from the mountain's ancient bones:

*THEN SPEAK IT, LAST SWORDSPEAKER. SPEAK YOUR NAME INTO OUR KEEPING, AND LET THE SKY REMEMBER WHAT IT OWES YOU.*

Jude raised the singing sword upright before them. Sera's hand tightened on their shoulder and did not let go. Corvel bowed her head. Far above, muffled by a thousand fathoms of stone, the first true roar of battle rolled down through the roots of the world like a wave deciding whether to break.

Jude breathed in. Breathed out. Found the thirteen-year-old still living inside them, waiting in the vigil-smoke, and took that child's hand.

"My name is Jude Winterbourne," they said, and began to unbind.
