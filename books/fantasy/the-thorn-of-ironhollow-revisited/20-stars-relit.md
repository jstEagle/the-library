## 20. Stars Relit

**Corvel**

The first assault broke against Aurelspire's walls like a wave against a cliff that had decided, after two hundred years of standing, to stop pretending it was stone.

Corvel Ashgrave stood on the eastern rampart with a borrowed spear and no banner, and watched the wave come apart. Veyra's soldiers came up the siege-slopes in their thousands, in ranks so deep the lantern-light could not find the back of them, and for one terrible quarter-hour they almost did not break. They had ladders and hooks and the flat certainty of the well-fed, and above them the sky was a black cloth pulled tight, starless, as it had been since the night the wards died.

*Hold,* Corvel thought, not to the wall but to the mountain behind her, to the dark beneath the world where three people she loved were doing something she could not see, could not help, could not even imagine correctly. *Hold, hold, you stubborn ghosts. Whatever bargain is being struck down there — strike it faster.*

The left bastion groaned. A section of parapet came away in a bloom of dust and screaming. She was moving before her mind finished deciding, shoulder into the shield-line, hauling a boy of maybe sixteen out of the gap while the gap filled with spears.

She had served Veyra once. She had held *her* shield once, on a day when the future queen's hands shook and Corvel had told her, laughing, that fear was only courage with its coat still on. She thought about that now, driving her spear into the press below, and wondered what coat courage wore when it had nothing left but this: a wall, a spear, and the hope that somewhere under the roots of the world, an oath-bound swordspeaker from a valley no map bothered with was saying yes to something enormous.

The horns changed. That was how she knew, later, that it had already begun — not with light, not yet, but with sound. The enemy horns faltered mid-note, all along the line at once, like a choir losing its nerve.

Then the sky opened.

* * *

**Jude**

The court beneath the mountain did not look at Jude. It looked through them, the way water looks through a dropped stone, and waited.

Jude stood on the black glass floor with Sera's hand in theirs and Corvel's absence like a missing tooth they kept probing, and felt the oath sit inside them the way it had sat for thirty years: quiet, patient, load-bearing. The last binding of their name. Their mother's voice speaking it over them when they were nine years old and proud of a wooden practice sword. *I am Winterbourne. I speak steel and steel answers. While I live, my word has an edge.*

The thing in the obsidian court had asked for company once, long ago, and been given a soul instead, and had not known the difference until the stars went out and it understood what warmth actually weighed. Now it wanted the debt unwound. Debts unwound both ways.

"Say it plainly," Jude said. Their voice echoed strangely off the glass, coming back wrong, coming back *older*. "What do you want from me?"

*An equal weight,* the court said, and the words were less sound than pressure, less pressure than cold. *A soul freely sold bought your queen her throne. A binding freely broken will buy the stars their fire. You carry the last oath ever sworn by your line, swordspeaker. Your ancestor built the valley to hide the warmth. You are the lock's final tooth.*

"And if I give it?"

*You will be ordinary.* The word landed like a stone in still water. *No edge to your word. No answer in your steel. The valley's wards will not return; nothing will protect Ironhollow again but the hands of the people in it. And the stars will burn.*

Sera's grip tightened. Jude could feel her wanting to say something — *don't, there has to be another way, we'll find another way* — and could feel her deciding, with the discipline of someone who had fled a faith rather than lie inside it, not to insult him with it. There was no other way. They had walked through the Rootways singing stone and sleeping things to stand here. There had never been another way; there had only been the road to this room.

Jude thought about the harvest vigils. About the way the whole village used to look up at the stars together, every autumn, faces tilted like flowers, and how Jude had always felt slightly outside it — useful, honored, *bound*, but never quite one of the upturned faces. The oath had been a wall around them too. A smaller valley, inside the valley.

They thought about Tam, selling their location for bread, and then crawling through sewage to make it right. About Corvel holding a rearguard alone so strangers could run. About Sera, who had lost her reason for fleeing at the gate and stood here anyway, hollowed out, choosing these people with everything that remained.

Ordinary. The word didn't frighten them anymore. Somewhere on the Road of Broken Bells, between the melted temples and the quiet camp and Sera's voice in the dark saying *tell me what you're afraid of*, ordinary had stopped being a punishment and started being a country they wanted citizenship in.

Above them — impossibly, through miles of mountain — Jude felt Aurelspire shudder. Felt, or imagined, or perhaps the oath itself reported it, the last duty of the last swordspeaker: the wall breaking, the wave coming over, the found family scattered across a battlefield they couldn't reach.

Time ran out in both worlds. It always did. That was what oaths were *for*.

"I give it," Jude said.

They knelt, because some grammar lived deeper than choice, and laid the oath-sword flat on the black glass, and spoke the binding aloud one final time — not as claim but as surrender, each word lifted off their name like a stone set down at last:

*I am Winterbourne. I unbind what I bound. Let the word go home. Let the edge rest. Let the stars remember.*

The court took it the way the earth takes rain.

It did not hurt. That was the unbearable part. It simply *left*, sliding out of them with a sigh like a sword returned to its scabbard after a lifetime drawn, and Jude felt the shape of themselves change — felt the place where the oath had been become just a place, open air, a window finally unlatched after thirty winters. They gasped. Sera caught them. Far away, overhead, something ancient and enormous remembered how to burn.

* * *

**Tam**

Tam was on the wall when the stars came back, which meant they got to watch it happen to everyone at once.

The fighting stopped the way a fight stops in a dream — all at once, everywhere, without anyone deciding. One heartbeat the eastern slope was a churning mass of pike and ladder and screaming; the next, ten thousand men had gone still, and then, as one animal, they had turned their faces upward.

The first star came back over the northern towers, small and white and absolutely certain, and Tam heard someone beside them start to cry and realized distantly it was themself.

Then the rest followed — not gradually, not shyly, but the way a city lights at dusk, district by district, until the whole black cloth was sewn through with fire. Constellations Tam had only ever heard old people describe. The Reaper's Wain. The Sisters. The long silver river of the Starfield that the temple maps showed in gold leaf, that half of Aurelspire had privately decided was a story grandmothers told.

Light fell on the battlefield. On the breached bastion, on the dead lying in the mud with their eyes open to the sky, on Veyra's soldiers standing in the wreckage of their own assault with their weapons hanging loose.

And Tam, who had grown up learning to read crowds the way other children learned letters, watched the reading change.

It wasn't fear that moved through those ranks. Fear they could have fought through; fear had marched them up the slopes in the first place. It was *arithmetic*. Tam could see it happening face by face, soldier by soldier: the usurper's bargain had been real, everyone had known it was real, the starless sky had been the receipt. And now the sky was full, and the receipt had burned, and every man on that slope was doing the same cold sum — *she sold her soul and got nothing, and I am dying on a wall for nothing* —

Someone threw down a pike. Just one man, near the front, a veteran with grey in his beard. He took off his helmet, looked up at the Sisters, and sat down in the mud.

The sound that went through the army was not a cheer and not a roar. It was quieter than that and far more fatal: it was the sound of ten thousand people exhaling at the same moment, and deciding, together, without a single order, that they were done.

The rout began at the edges and ate inward. Banners dipped and turned. Officers screamed and were not heard, because the stars were louder than any officer who had ever lived.

Tam leaned on the parapet and laughed until it turned into something else, watching the mightiest army in living memory be defeated by the night sky remembering its job.

*Come on, Jude,* they whispered, to the mountain, to the dark, to nobody. *Whatever it cost you — come home and let us pay it back.*

* * *

**Sera**

Sera had expected the relighting to feel like victory. It felt like a funeral with excellent lighting.

Jude lay against her shoulder on the glass floor, breathing, alive, whole — and emptied. She had seen acolytes come back from the deep rites hollowed before; she knew the look. The body fine, the architecture gone. When she touched his chest there was no hum in him at all. No resonance. He was, for the first time in his life, exactly as silent inside as any ordinary person, and the silence was so complete it rang.

"It worked," Jude said. His voice sounded like his own, which somehow made it worse. "Sera. It worked."

"Yes." Her throat was tight. "Yes, it worked."

"The stars—"

"Burning. All of them. You'd know if you could see the sky right now." She smiled and hated how it wobbled. "The whole Starfield. Even the Sisters. Someone up on that wall is going to have to describe it to you properly, and it will take them an hour, and they'll get it wrong."

He laughed — a thin, cracked laugh, but real — and then his face did something complicated, grief arriving late the way it does when the body is busy surviving. "I can't feel it," he said quietly. "The sword. It's right there on the floor and I can't feel anything from it at all. It's just — metal. It's just a thing."

Sera gathered him closer. Around them the obsidian court was changing — she could see it, the black glass warming at the edges toward something like amber, the cold pressure in the air loosening into something almost like attention, almost like relief. The returned soul sat somewhere in the deep of the court now, and its warmth was spreading through the dark the way lamplight spreads through a house at evening, room by room. The thing that had been alone since the stars were lit was not alone anymore. Whatever that meant for it — whatever it became — would be a story for later. Possibly a beautiful one. Possibly a terrifying one. Sera found, exhausted beyond measure, that she could hold both possibilities in her hands at once and neither one crushed her.

That, she supposed, was what the free cities called faith, and why she had left the under-court to go looking for the honest version of it.

"Jude Winterbourne," she said, "last swordspeaker of Ironhollow, first ordinary person to walk out of the court beneath the mountain. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else who comes down here comes back *less*. You came back free."

He looked up at her, and in his face she saw the dread and wonder of the night the stars went out, transfigured — the same feeling, inverted, the safe world ending quietly and a truer one beginning the same way.

"Help me up," he said. "We should go home."

* * *

**Corvel**

By dawn the field before Aurelspire was strewn not with the wounded of a battle but with the abandoned equipment of an army that had ceased to believe in itself — pikes stacked in surrender-piles by men who wept while stacking them, banners folded with strange tenderness, helmets arranged in rows as if the soldiers expected to come back for them and knew they wouldn't. Veyra herself had not fled east, as Corvel had bet. She had sat down in her command tent and sent out a herald under a white cloth to say she would receive the cities' judgment, and Corvel, watching her escorted up the hill under guard, saw a woman walking lighter than she had walked in years, as though a great weight had been lifted off her whether she wanted it or not.

The gates opened. The defenders came down among the surrendered, warily, and then not warily at all, sharing water, staring upward together whenever anyone could bear to look away from the ground.

Corvel stood in the middle of it with her borrowed spear planted in the earth and let the starlight fall on her face.

She was forty-nine years old and she had never seen the Sisters. She had been knighted under a full sky, true, but she had been young and terrified and mostly watching the queen-to-be's face. Now she tipped her head back and looked until her neck ached, and the long silver river of the Starfield poured into her eyes, and somewhere in the crowd a child asked her mother what the brightest one was called, and the mother began, hesitantly, to tell a story she was making up as she went — and Corvel thought: *that. That is what we bought. That is worth every mile.*

Boots behind her. Two sets, uneven. She turned.

Jude came across the churned field leaning on Sera, moving like a man who had carried something heavy for thirty years and set it down and was still walking in the old carrying-shape. Tam appeared out of the crowd as if conjured, running the last stretch, skidding to a stop in front of Jude with the particular restraint of someone who very badly wanted to hug a person and was checking first whether hugging was permitted.

Jude hugged them. All three of them, actually — dragged Tam and Sera in with both arms, and looked at Corvel over their heads with wet eyes and a crooked smile, and mouthed something she chose, generously, to interpret as *shut up and join us*.

She joined them. Four people in the ruin of a battlefield, holding each other under a sky that burned.

"You gave it up," Corvel said into Jude's hair. "The oath. All of it."

"All of it."

"Was it—" She stopped. Started again, more honestly: "Does it hurt?"

Jude considered. Above them, the Sisters wheeled slow and bright.

"No," he said at last. "But I'm going to miss it. Both things are true, and I'm going to spend the rest of my life learning how to hold them at the same time." A pause. "Starting with breakfast. Does this city have breakfast?"

Tam laughed — a wet, cracked, glorious laugh — and grabbed his sleeve. "Best candle-market griddle cakes in the free cities, and today they're free, because I know the vendor and he owes me and also because the *stars are back*, Jude, everything is free today—"

They went up the hill together toward the smoking, singing, starlit city, and behind them the field lay quiet for the first time in a generation.

Only Sera glanced back once, toward the mountain — toward the warm dark underneath it, where something ancient was holding a soul's warmth for the first time in centuries and learning, slowly, what to do with it.

"We'll have to go back someday," she said softly. "To see what it becomes."

"Someday," Jude agreed. "Not tomorrow."

"Not tomorrow," Sera said, and meant: *we have earned our tomorrows, all of them, one by one, starting now.*
