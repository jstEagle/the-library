## 22. Homecoming

The road into Ironhollow had not changed, and that was the first miracle.

Jude had expected ruin to have a taste — ash on the wind, char in the soil, something bitter to announce itself miles before the wardline. Instead there was hay dust and woodsmoke and the green smell of the river shallows where it braided over stones, the same as every autumn of their life. The valley opened around them like a hand uncurling: terraced fields gone gold at the edges, the orchards heavy with late apples nobody had picked yet, the crooked line of rooftops along the water catching the afternoon light.

And above it all — this was the second miracle, the one Jude still could not look at directly without their throat tightening — above it all, the stars were already out, faint as salt scattered on blue slate, waiting for full dark the way they always had. As if they had never left. As if two hundred years of borrowed darkness had been only a long blink.

"They're early," Sera said beside them, walking with her staff slung across her shoulders like a yoke, her pack riding light now, half its weight given away along the road. "The first ones. They always come early in autumn. I used to watch for them from the acolytes' gallery, through a slit no wider than my hand." She said it easily, which was new. The memory-toll at the gate beneath the mountain had hollowed her, but the hollows were filling in — with stories, mostly, told by firelight, by the people who loved her. That was the arrangement they had made with one another, somewhere on the long road east of Aurelspire: *I will remember for you until you can remember for yourself.*

"Two hundred years," Jude said, "and you're telling me they come early."

"Stars keep better time than kings." She glanced sideways at them, and her mouth did the thing it did when she was deciding whether to say the kinder thing or the truer one. She usually chose the truer one. "You've stopped checking your hands."

Jude looked down anyway. Bare hands, ordinary hands, knuckles scarred from kitchen work and hedging and one bad winter with an axe. They had drawn steel perhaps a thousand times in the months since Ironhollow burned, and each time the words had come — *edge* and *answer*, *hold* and *hallow* — and each time since the bargain, nothing. Silence where the speaking had been. A sword that was only a sword.

They had wept about it exactly once, alone, three days out from the mountain, and then discovered that the grief had a floor to it, and stood on that floor, and found it bore their weight. You can live in a room with no magic in it. People always had. What you cannot live in is a world with no stars, and the stars were back.

"I stopped checking," Jude said, "because I finally believe it."

Ahead, Tam whooped — a thin, sharp sound that scattered birds off the fence rails — and broke into a run.

* * *

The village was not what it had been, and it was more.

The fire had taken the eastern third of Ironhollow that night Veyra's soldiers came: the granary, the smithy, eleven houses, the meeting hall with its ancient roof-beams carved with names Jude's grandmother had traced for them once, finger on finger, saying *this one planted the orchard, this one argued with the founder, this one was hanged unjustly and we still say his name.* All of it gone to black timber and smoke. Jude had carried embers of that grief across half the known world, expecting to return to a scar.

But the scar had been worked. New timber stood pale against old, unpainted, silvering slowly in the weather. The granary had been rebuilt on taller posts, cats already installed against the mice by unanimous village vote. The smithy's chimney rose crooked and proud, and even at this distance Jude could hear someone hammering, keeping the hour the way the bells of the free cities kept theirs. And the meeting hall — the meeting hall was half-raised, scaffolds climbing its flanks, and swarming over those scaffolds like bright insects were children.

Not village children. Not only. Children in coats cut down from soldier's wool, children with Marrowgate vowels and Aurelspire manners, children who had learned to sleep in doorways and now, apparently, slept in lofts and barns and the half-finished hall, because Tam had brought them home the way other people bring home stray dogs — one at first, then a handful, then a tide.

Tam reached them at the foot of the lane, skidding in the dust, twelve years old and suddenly taller, hair hacked short with what looked like shears and no supervision. Behind her came a dozen smaller shapes pouring downhill like spilled marbles.

"You're late," Tam announced, breathless. "You said harvest vigil. It's harvest vigil. Barely. The apples are going over."

"The apples are fine."

"The apples are *going over*, I checked." She caught herself mid-scold, and Jude watched the bravado wobble, watched her look up at them — at all three of them — with the fierce, unschooled hunger of a child confirming that things come back. That some things come back. Her hand shot out and gripped Jude's sleeve, hard, as if to check they were real. "You came back," she said, quieter, accusing.

"We came back," Jude said.

"I sold you," Tam said, to the ground. "In Marrowgate. For bread."

"And then you got us out through the sewers," Jude said, "and Corvel says the rearguard would have failed without you, and I say so too, and we're not doing this accounting again. We settled it on the road. Interest paid."

Tam sniffed, scrubbed her face with her wrist with tremendous dignity, and then turned and bellowed at her charges: "Well? Don't just stand there gawking, help with the bags!" And they fell upon Sera and Corvel and Jude like starlings on a spilled sack, chattering, tugging, asking questions in overlapping waves — *is it true you went under the mountain, is it true the stars went out everywhere, is it true she's a knight* — until Corvel laughed, actually laughed, deep in her chest, and swung the smallest one up onto her shoulder as if she had been doing it all her life instead of for six weeks on the road east while her wound healed to a pale seam below the collarbone.

Dame Corvel Ashgrave, traitor, queen's shield, rode into Ironhollow leading her horse rather than riding it, because — she said — a place deserved the courtesy of arriving on foot the first time. She wore no banner still. But the brand on her cheek had faded to an old-battle smudge, and there were new letters tucked inside her coat, sealed ones, from the surviving chapters of the shattered orders, asking her counsel, asking her name, asking — this was the word Jude had seen in the one letter Corvel would let them read — asking her to *come and help us begin again.*

She would ride east tomorrow. Everyone knew it. She had said it plainly at supper outside Aurelspire, the way she said everything: *There are orders with no masters and masters with no orders, and I know how both feel, and I'm tired of being the only one holding either.* No one had argued. Arguing with Corvel was like arguing with a stone wall that loved you.

Now she set the small child down, straightened, and surveyed the valley with the long measuring look she gave everything — walls, roads, hearts.

"It'll hold," she said. "Ironhollow. Good sightlines, water, timber. Stubborn people." Her eyes found Jude's. "It always was."

Jude's throat closed. Corvel crossed the distance between them and did the thing she almost never did: took Jude's face in both gauntleted hands — gently, the way she'd once held a wounded bird Jude remembered but she no longer could; the knighting day was gone from her, traded at the gate, and sometimes she reached for it and found only the shape where it had been, and the rest of them filled it in aloud — and pressed her forehead to theirs.

"You gave everything," she said, low enough that only Jude heard it. "I watched you do it. I want you to hear it said in the place you did it for."

"Sera gave memories. You gave blood. I gave a job I wasn't using properly anyway," Jude managed, and Corvel huffed, and let go, and the moment folded itself away into the day like a letter into a pocket.

* * *

Sera stayed.

It surprised no one, least of all Sera, though she made a show of deliberating for two entire days, standing on the new bridge with her staff, looking north toward the cities and south toward the mountains, before announcing that she had considered all her options carefully and chosen the one with the best apples. Tam, who had appointed herself the valley's minister of intelligence, reported that Sera had been seen measuring the foundation stones of the ruined shrine by the river with strips of knotted cord, muttering numbers.

"She's building something," Tam said, perched on Jude's fence rail, swinging her boots. "A temple, probably. Or a school. Or both. She says the under-court isn't evil, it's just lonely, and lonely things need somebody to visit them regular, and somebody's got to teach people how to knock."

"Does she now."

"She says you especially owe it a visit." Tam's swing slowed. Her voice dropped out of its reportorial register into something younger. "Do you? Owe it?"

Jude thought about the obsidian hall, the vast slow attention of the thing beneath the mountain, older than kingdoms, alone since the stars were lit — alone because lighting them had required a binding, and bindings require a binder, and the binder had walked away and left it in the dark with its one duty. Thought about how it had not wanted worship, only company. Thought about the voice like deep water saying, at the very last, *you will forget to come.*

"I owe it a visit," Jude said. "Once a year. Maybe twice."

"That's what she said you'd say." Tam grinned, satisfied, and vaulted off the rail. "Also she says your cottage roof leaks."

The cottage roof did leak. Jude had discovered it their first night home, lying awake under the familiar beams listening to the rain find the one weak seam above the bed, drip by patient drip, and had lain there smiling like a fool, because a leaking roof was a problem a person could fix. A person with a ladder and some thatch and no destiny whatsoever. It was, Jude reflected, the finest problem they had ever owned.

* * *

On the fourth morning, with frost on the fields and the rebuilt village smelling of bread and sawdust, Jude carried the sword up from the riverbank where it had spent the journey wrapped in oilcloth on top of their pack.

It was not much to look at. It never had been. A straight blade, honest steel, a plain guard worn smooth by generations of hands — hands that had held it in ritual only, because inside Ironhollow the speaking had never worked, and outside it none of them had ever gone. Two hundred years the sword had hung above the founder's hearth, then above Jude's mother's, then above Jude's own, silent as furniture, until the night the wards broke and it had hummed against the wall like a struck bell and Jude had known, with dread and wonder tangled together in the throat, that the safe world was ending.

Jude climbed the steps of the cottage — their cottage, patched roof and all — and stood a moment in the doorway looking back down the lane. Sera was at the shrine foundations with her cords and her chalk, surrounded by an audience of children who asked questions she answered with terrifying precision. Corvel was at the stable saddling her horse for the morning's drill with the village's four able riders, teaching them, patiently and mercilessly, how to hold a line. Tam was on the hall scaffold directing operations with the authority of an emperor, none of the adults below appearing to notice that they were taking orders from a child, all of them apparently content to do so.

Ordinary life, everywhere. Hard-won, everywhere. Jude turned the sword over once in their hands — felt its familiar weight, its complete silence, the absence where the oath had lived, an absence that had healed over like a clean wound — and drove two nails into the beam above the door, and hung it there.

Not point-down, as a warning. Not crosswise, as a barrier. Just hanging, the way tools hang in a house that expects to use them again someday, for ordinary purposes: cutting kindling rope, splitting a stubborn hinge, defending the doorstep if it ever came to that. A sword among the tools. A person among the people.

Their reflection ghosted faintly in the flat of the blade, and for a moment — habit, hope, imagination, Jude couldn't say — the steel seemed to hold not just their face but the faces behind it: the founder who had sold the wards and hidden the valley to protect the stolen soul, complicity and love twisted together down the generations like roots; the mother whose face Jude had given away at the gate and whose smile the whole village now returned to them daily, in a hundred strangers' kindnesses, filling the hollow the way the others' stories filled Sera's and Corvel's. *We will remember for you,* the faces seemed to say. *Go be ordinary now. It's harder than it looks, and better.*

The wind moved down the valley, through the orchards, setting the apples nodding. Overhead, in a sky still rinsed-pale with morning, the first evening stars were not yet visible — but Jude knew they were there, all of them, burning whether anyone watched or not, lit at last by an oath freely given and a soul brought home warm.

Jude stepped through the doorway, under the silent sword, and went down the hill to help with the apples.
