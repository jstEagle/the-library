## 1. The Taster's Spoon

There were three rules to surviving as the king's taster, and Petra Okafor had written all of them himself, in the manner of a man who had learned his lessons the expensive way.

One: never be interesting.

Two: never be full.

Three: never, under any circumstances, let them see you enjoy the food.

He was thinking about rule three that particular morning because the kitchen had produced a venison stew so good it ought to have been illegal — and as it turned out, it was, though not for reasons of flavor. The second cook, a round-faced woman named Bess who hummed off-key and salted everything twice, had laced the pot with enough foxglove to stop a horse's heart at forty paces. Petty treason, the guild called it. Poisoning the king's dinner out of spite, or greed, or — in Bess's case, as it would emerge by afternoon — because Lord Veyran of the western reaches had promised her widow's sister three acres and a goat if the old man's digestion stopped permanently.

Petra stood at the long oak table in the antechamber beyond the kitchens, the morning light falling through windows glazed with the amber grime of ten thousand cooking fires, and tasted the stew with a small silver spoon.

Venison, juniper, rosemary. Salted twice — hello, Bess. And underneath, folded into the richness like a whisper in a crowd: foxglove. Sweetish, faintly numbing on the tongue's edge, a bitterness that arrived late, the way bad news does.

He took another spoonful. Then a third.

This was the part no one understood about his work — the part he intended to take to his grave, which, if he managed things correctly, would be a very distant grave indeed. A taster did not merely detect poison. Detection was the easy half; any tongue could learn that, given time and a tolerant stomach. No, the true art — the art Petra had been building, swallow by careful swallow, for nine years — was the other half. The body's slow education. A grain of this, a drop of that, each dose measured against the last until his blood learned the alphabet of every poison the realm could produce and learned, moreover, to answer each letter with its own quiet word.

Immunity, the old texts called it. The guild called it tolerance. His mother, who had been a hedgewitch before the purges and who had taught him the first principles of it over a kitchen fire while the world outside still pretended such knowledge was folklore — his mother had called it *the garden you carry inside you*. You planted a little poison. You tended it. And one day, without anyone noticing, you were a meadow where death came to graze and went away hungry.

The gift itself was forbidden, hunted, burned out of bloodlines wherever it showed. But immunity was subtler. Immunity looked like nothing. Immunity was a servant's trick, a professional necessity, the sort of thing a sensible crown paid for rather than feared.

*Provided*, Petra thought, setting down the spoon, *nobody ever looks too closely.*

"Is it good?"

Bess had come around the corner with a tray of bread rolls balanced on one hip, her face wearing the particular brightness of a woman who has recently committed treason and would very much like everyone to behave normally about it.

"The stew?" Petra said. He kept his voice flat, bored, the voice of a man discussing weather. "It wants more thyme."

"More thyme," Bess repeated, wounded. "I put in a whole sprig."

"A whole sprig is for soup. This is a stew. It wants two." He picked up the small bell beside his place — brass, worn smooth by generations of tasters' hands — but did not ring it yet. "Also, I'd let the cook know the juniper's gone slightly bitter at the bottom of the pot. She may want to stir from lower down."

"I *am* the cook," said Bess.

"Then you'll want to stir from lower down."

She huffed off toward the ovens, muttering, and Petra allowed himself one private moment of grim amusement. There was a poetry to it, he supposed: the poisoner being advised on seasoning by the man she'd meant to murder. He'd have to report the foxglove within the hour — that was procedure, and procedure was armor — and the guild's men would come for Bess with their little vials and their little questions, and Lord Veyran's name would surface eventually like a drowned thing, and somewhere a widow's sister would lose her three acres and her goat. None of which was his affair. His affair began and ended at the rim of a bowl.

His affair was the spoon.

He rang the bell. Somewhere deeper in the palace, a page took up the signal, and the machinery of the king's dinner resumed its grinding.

* * *

The midday service passed without incident, which Petra noted the way a sailor notes calm water: gratefully, and with suspicion.

By the afternoon sitting, the kitchens had recovered their dignity. There was pheasant in a sauce of plums and red wine, a white loaf still steaming from its baking, butter stamped with the royal crest, and a selection of small sweet things that the king ignored entirely and the queen pretended to ignore while eating four of them when she thought no one was counting. Petra counted. Counting was part of invisibility — a taster who noticed things was useful; a taster who *mentioned* things was dead.

He worked through the courses in his established order, which had taken years to refine and looked, to observers, like mere fussiness. First the smell, eyes closed, three seconds — poisons announced themselves differently through the nose, some of them, and three seconds was long enough to catch a whisper and short enough to seem like piety. Then the touch of the spoon against the inner lip, waiting for tingling, numbness, heat. Then the smallest possible taste, held on the tongue for a count of five. Then, if the world remained agreeable, a proper mouthful, and a wait of two hundred heartbeats before he nodded and rang the bell.

Two hundred heartbeats. He'd trained himself to feel them, a drummer in his own chest, and he used the time the way he used all his waiting: by being furniture. Servants forgot furniture. Guards forgot furniture. Once, memorably, the captain of the household guard had spent a full quarter hour complaining about his wife to Petra, in detail, apparently under the impression that he was alone, and had afterward looked straight through him in the corridor without a flicker of recognition.

That was the day Petra had truly understood the shape of his safety. Not walls. Not steel. *Forgettableness.*

The pheasant was clean. The bread was clean. The plum sauce was clean, though he made a note to check whether the new supplier of plums had changed, because there was an almond note in the sweetness that hadn't been there last week, and almond notes in fruit were occasionally innocent and occasionally cyanide's shy cousin. Probably innocence. Probably.

Then came the wine.

Now — here was the thing about the wine. The wine was always sampled separately, by the taster alone, in the small stone chamber adjoining the king's private study, because wine was where clever people hid clever things. Wine forgave flavors. Wine was complex and dark and gave alibis to whatever dissolved inside it. Every taster in the guild's history who had died at his post, Petra was fairly sure, had died by the cup rather than the dish.

So he treated the wine with respect. He poured a measure into the crystal sampling glass — real crystal, because the guild claimed glass of lesser quality could mask certain mineral poisons, a claim Petra privately suspected existed mostly to justify the crystal budget — and he lifted it, and he smelled it.

Black cherry. Oak smoke. Wet slate from the eastern vineyards, that cold stony breath the good cellars carried.

And underneath — thin as wire, high as a whistle —

Something new.

Petra did not move. This was rule one in action: never be interesting, and a man who stiffened over a glass of wine was suddenly the most interesting man in Ironhollow. He breathed once more, slowly, cataloguing. The bitterness wasn't vegetal, like foxglove, or metallic, like the salts. It was — green. Almost floral. A taste like the memory of a flower pressed between the pages of a ledger.

He knew every poison the guild taught. He knew eleven the guild didn't teach, harvested from his mother's letters and from a smuggler's runner's education in what people will pay to make other people stop existing. This was none of them.

Which meant either the vineyards had invented something remarkable, or someone had introduced something remarkable, and both possibilities required the same response: absolute calm, and a sample.

He drank the ritual mouthful. Held it. Five heartbeats. Ten. Swallowed.

Nothing happened, which proved only that nothing happened quickly. Some poisons were patient. He poured the rest of the decanter's contents — a full cup, more than he'd ever risked of an unknown compound, but the moment demanded evidence and his body, that well-tended meadow, had earned its keep — into the small clay vial he kept for exactly this purpose, tucked in the lining of his sleeve where a seam met a seam and no one's eye ever went. He sealed it, wiped the glass, refilled the decanter from a clean bottle of the same vintage that he'd verified that morning, and rang the bell.

The steward appeared, satisfied. The king drank his wine, complained about the harvest, and signed three documents. Petra stood in the corner with his hands folded, being furniture, being forgettable, being — beneath the practiced calm of nine years — quietly, thoroughly afraid.

* * *

That evening, in his narrow room above the taster's quarters — a room with one window, one candle, one bed, and no decorations whatsoever, because decorations were memories and memories were leverage — Petra lit his second candle of the day, the one he permitted himself after dark, and sat with the vial.

He turned it in the light. The wine moved, dark and ordinary. Whatever rode in it left no sediment, no cloudiness. Refined, then. Expensive. Someone had paid real money for this, or had access to real knowledge, and neither of those people sent their work into a king's decanter for practice.

He thought about the timing. Three weeks ago, the treasury auditors had visited the palace — unusual, unannounced, tight-lipped. Two weeks ago, the king had dismissed his physician of nineteen years with no explanation offered and none requested, because kings do not explain themselves to furniture. Last week, the guildmaster, Corvane, had walked through the tasting chamber unaccompanied — Corvane, who never went anywhere unaccompanied — and had paused behind Petra's chair for exactly long enough to be felt, and had said, in his dry paper voice: *"You've been with us nine years now, haven't you, Okafor. Time does go on."*

Time goes on. As if it were a compliment. As if nine years of swallowing death in measured doses were a vintage worth noting.

And now a new bitterness in the wine, in the one cup that touched the king's lips alone.

Petra set the vial down and rubbed his eyes. He was thirty-one years old and had the hands of a much older man, stained faintly silver at the fingertips from years of handling assay salts, and he was tired in the specific way of people whose vigilance has become their skeleton — invisible, structural, load-bearing.

He should destroy the vial. That was the cautious play. Report the taste, note it in the log — *unidentified bitterness, vintage 743 east slope, recommend cellar inquiry* — and let the guild's chemists earn their salaries.

But.

But the guild's chemists reported to Corvane. And Corvane's pause behind his chair had lasted three heartbeats too long. And in nine years, Petra had learned one law above all others, a law his mother had taught him in a different kitchen, in a different life: *when the powerful start tasting strange things, it is the powerless who end up swallowed.*

He got up, crossed to the loose floorboard beneath the bed — third from the wall, under the knot, a hiding place older than his tenancy — and lifted out the flat tin box that held everything he owned that mattered. Inside: his mother's letters, tied with string. A map of the Thornfell passes, drawn in a smuggler's hand, soft as cloth from handling. Eleven gold pieces. And a spare vial, empty, waiting.

He placed the sample beside the map. Then he sat back on his heels and looked at it for a long time, the candle guttering, the palace settling around him with its usual night sounds — doors, boots, the far-off clink of the guard changing.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would begin doing something he had never done in nine careful years.

Tomorrow, he would start finding out what the guild knew about *him*.
