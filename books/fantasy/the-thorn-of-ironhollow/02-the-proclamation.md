## 2. The Proclamation

The morning the king announced the Gift-Hunt, Petra Okafor was in the scullery teaching a kitchen boy named Wren how to tell arsenic from salt by smell alone.

"Lean closer," he said, holding out the little clay saucer. "But don't sniff like you mean it. You're not courting it. You're just passing by and happen to notice."

Wren leaned past the saucer, wrinkled his nose, and recoiled. "Like burnt almonds?"

"Very good. Salt smells of nothing at all, which is why poisoners love to hide behind it. Remember that: nothing is a disguise too." Petra swept the saucer away before the boy could be tempted. "Now go scrub the roasting pans before Cook remembers you exist."

Wren grinned — he had a gap in his teeth and an appetite for forbidden knowledge that reminded Petra painfully of himself at that age — and darted off through the steam. Petra watched him go and felt the small, familiar ache that came whenever he let himself notice someone. Nine years as the crown's food-taster had taught him one law above all others: *be furniture*. Furniture had no friends. Furniture was not remembered fondly by name in sculleries.

And yet here he was, teaching children poisons. He sighed at himself. Old habits of being human were hard to kill, and he'd never quite managed the execution.

He returned to his station in the tasting chamber — a narrow stone room off the royal kitchens where every dish bound for the king's table passed beneath his spoon. It was a comfortable little cell, all things considered. A brazier. A shelf of clean spoons ranked by size like soldiers. A window high on the wall that admitted exactly enough light to see what you were eating and no more.

The midday service arrived: a partridge pie, a soup of leeks and barley, honeyed pears, and a carafe of the dark southern wine the king favored with his fish course. Petra worked through them in order, methodical as prayer. The pie was innocent. The soup was innocent. The pears carried nothing worse than a cook's heavy hand with clove.

The wine was another matter.

Petra held the cup to the light. The wine itself was right — that deep garnet color, the legs sliding slow down the cup's belly — but there was something riding underneath it, something faintly *bitter*, like the ghost of a green walnut. He swirled. He sipped, letting it sit on his tongue the way a jeweler lets a stone sit in the scale.

There. Beneath the plum and the smoke: a bitterness with a shape to it. Not a natural tannin. Natural bitterness sprawled; this stood up straight. This had been *put* there.

Petra did not react. This was the first rule of his craft and the first rule of his life, and they happened to be the same rule: you never let your face tell anyone anything. He swallowed, counted his own pulse the way other men counted sheep, and set the cup down with the same mild care he'd give any cup.

Then, while the under-steward's back was turned, he tipped a measure of the wine into the little vial he kept sewn into his sleeve and palmed the stopper.

It wasn't the first time. Over nine years he'd built a private apothecary in a locked box beneath his cot: vials of everything the crown had ever slipped into his food, labeled in his own cramped hand. His colleagues thought him eccentric — the taster who kept samples, hoarding his work like a miser. Let them think it. What none of them knew was why.

Because Petra Okafor had been born with a body that shrugged off poison the way a duck shrugs off rain, and he had spent nine years feeding that gift carefully, patiently, drop by measured drop — building his immunity the way a mason builds a wall, one stone at a time, always careful never to build so high that anyone might notice the wall existed.

The gift itself was the crime now. But the *practice* of it — the daily quiet swallowing, the ledger in his head of doses and effects — that was a second crime, and a more dangerous one, because it required intention. A man could be born wrong. Only a man could choose to stay that way on purpose.

Petra sealed the vial, slid it home against his wrist, and called for the next course.

* * *

They heard the proclamation before they saw anything of it — the bells, first, all seven towers of the palace ringing at once, which meant either fire or festival or king, and the kitchens went still as a held breath. Then the horns. Then the voice of the herald, magnified by some clever acoustic trick of the balcony above the great square, rolling over the rooftops and down the chimneys into their flour and fat-scented world:

*"By command of Aldric, King of Ironhollow and the Thornward Marches—"*

Cook dropped her ladle. Nobody picked it up.

Petra set down his knife. Around him, forty faces turned toward the yard door, toward the strip of grey sky visible beyond it. He moved with them, unhurried, a man simply curious about weather — but inside, some animal part of him had already gone flat-eared and still.

*"—let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of the forbidden gift is treason most foul, poisoning the wellsprings of loyal blood. And therefore: a Hunt is declared. From this day, any soul found bearing the gift shall be taken for examination."*

*Examination.* The word landed in Petra's stomach like a swallowed stone.

*"To any loyal subject who gives true name of a gift-bearer: ten crowns of silver."*

Ten crowns. Petra did the arithmetic without meaning to — it was a taster's habit, weighing things — and the sum was obscene. Ten crowns was a year's wages for a scullery hand. Ten crowns was a winter's coal, a doctor's fee, a coffin done properly. Ten crowns was a neighbor's price, and everyone in that square would learn within the week what a neighbor cost.

The herald's voice continued with its brass-and-parchment cadence, but the kitchens had stopped listening as words and started listening as animals do — to tone, to danger. Someone near the buttery began to cry quietly. Somewhere else, someone laughed, one sharp bark of a laugh with terror in it, quickly smothered.

"We should get back," muttered the under-steward. "Service won't hold itself."

Nobody moved. Then Cook, big hands planted on her hips, said in a voice pitched to carry, "Well. I've known every soul in this palace nine years and better, and I'll say plain: I know no witches. Never met one. Wouldn't know what one looked like if she curtsied to me." She looked around the room, meeting eyes. "Anybody here know different?"

"No, Cook."

"No, Cook."

A chorus, ragged but real. Petra added his own "No, Cook," and hated how easily it came, how practiced, how much like the truth it sounded even to himself.

They filed back to work. Service proceeded. The king ate his partridge pie, and Petra tasted the evening courses, and the palace performed its great pantomime of normalcy while below stairs the whispering ran like water finding cracks: *who? who? who?*

That night, Petra lay on his cot in the tasters' dormitory and listened to the palace breathe, and understood with cold clarity that the floor of his careful life had just opened beneath him like a trapdoor he'd been standing on for nine years without knowing it was a door.

Because here was the thing he could not say aloud, the thing shaped like his own heartbeat: the Gift-Hunt would not come for him with accusations. It would come with a *test*. They would feed him something bitter in a cup of wine, watch his face, and wait for him to die. And Petra Okafor would not die. He would sit there calm as Sunday while the poison did its polite, useless work, and every eye in the room would widen, and someone would whisper the word *gift*, and ten crowns of silver would change hands before his body finished deciding what to do with the dose.

His immunity — built stone by patient stone across nine years — would read, to any examiner in the realm, as exactly what it functionally was. The gift. Or close enough. Close enough to hang a man.

He stared at the ceiling beams and thought, with a strange detachment: *I have been invisible for nine years, and invisibility only works while nobody is looking. Tomorrow, everyone will be looking.*

Sleep came late and thin, like broth stretched too far.

* * *

It was three days later that they took the boy.

Petra was crossing the lower court on an errand for the guild steward — fetching a fresh ledger of tasting records, ink still tacky — when the commotion started at the servants' gate. He didn't break stride. That was the discipline. Whatever happened, you walked, because running men attracted eyes, and eyes were death.

So he walked, and thus he had a clear view of the whole ugly tableau: two of the king's new Hunt-wardens — men in grey livery with silver pins shaped like crossed daggers, appointed barely a day past — dragging a boy between them. The boy was maybe fourteen. Small for his age. Kitchen-scalds on his forearms.

It was Wren.

Petra's feet wanted very badly to stop walking. He made them keep going.

"—found him practicing, my lord, honest, I *saw* him—" A scullery woman, wringing her apron, her voice pitched high with the particular eagerness of the frightened trying to prove their loyalty. "He had a candle and he made the flame bend, I swear it on my mother's grave, I *saw* it—"

"I didn't!" Wren's voice cracked in the middle. "I didn't bend anything, I was just looking at it, I swear, I only—"

One of the wardens cuffed him across the ear, casual as flicking a fly, and the sound cut through the court. The boy staggered. Blood showed bright at his lip.

"He'll be examined proper at the Citadel," the warden said, bored, to no one in particular. "If he's clean, he comes back. If he's marked—" He shrugged, and the shrug said everything the law hadn't bothered to write down yet.

"If he's *clean*, he comes back," Wren repeated wildly, twisting to look at the gathering servants, at Cook, at anyone. "Tell them. Tell them I'm not — I only ever watched the flames, everybody watches the flames, don't you? Don't you ever watch the—"

The gate closed behind him with a boom that Petra felt in his teeth.

The court emptied slowly, the way water drains, everyone suddenly busy, everyone suddenly unable to meet anyone's eye. Cook stood by the well with her arms folded tight, staring at nothing, and Petra saw that her jaw was working around words she would never say, and that she was crying without letting a single tear fall, because tears in front of the wrong witness cost ten crowns.

Petra delivered his ledger to the guild steward. He thanked the clerk. He commented on the weather. Every word emerged correctly shaped, and somewhere behind his own face a man was screaming, and the screaming man and the correct-speaking man were both him, and the distance between them was nine years wide and growing.

*Examination*, they called it. Petra knew what examination meant, because he'd read the old statutes once, out of professional thoroughness, the way a surgeon reads about wounds. Examination meant a locked room and escalating doses until the subject either demonstrated resistance — proof of gift — or died innocently. There was no third outcome. A boy who could bend candle flames would be lucky. At least his guilt would be quick.

And Wren — gap-toothed, poison-curious, *alive* Wren — had been taken because a terrified woman needed ten crowns more than she needed her soul.

That night, Petra did not sleep at all. He sat in the dark with his knees drawn up and made himself look directly at the thing he'd been circling for three days:

*You are already marked. You have been marked since birth, and everything since — the guild, the spoon, the careful years — has only been you hiding in plain sight inside the one profession where your strangeness looks like diligence. The Hunt doesn't need to find you. It needs only to test you. One cup of wine. One raised eyebrow. One colleague with a grudge and a memory.*

And the taster's guild employed dozens of men who watched him eat every day, and any one of them might notice that Petra Okafor never flinched, never sickened, never so much as sweated after a suspect dish — and wonder why.

The floor was gone. There was only the fall now, and the question of how he landed.

* * *

He waited until deep night, when the dormitory breathed in unison and the watchman's boots had made their round. Then he knelt, drew out the loose flagstone under his cot, and lifted the oilcloth-wrapped bundle from its hollow.

His mother's letters.

Twelve of them, written across the last year of her life, in the looping hand of a woman who had taught herself letters by copying herb-lore manuals. He'd read them so many times he no longer needed light, but he lit the stub of candle anyway. Some farewells deserve ceremony.

*My Petra. If you are reading this I am dead, and if I am dead, then hear me: what is in your blood is not a curse, whatever your father's family says. My grandmother had it. Her mother before her. We are a long line of women who could drink what kills and walk away smiling, and we survived every purge and every burning by one rule only: never let them see the cup touch your lips twice.*

*You are going to the capital to be a taster, and I am frightened, but it is the cleverest hiding place in the world, my love. Who suspects the poison-drinker of drinking poison? Be dull. Be forgettable. Be the most boring man in Ironhollow.*

*And keep the letters somewhere safe, because if I am gone I want you to remember you were loved by someone who knew exactly what you were and thought you were wonderful anyway.*

Petra read them again anyway. All twelve. Slowly. Letting each line land like a stone in still water.

Then he fed them to the candle flame, one page at a time, holding each until the fire nearly kissed his fingers. His mother's handwriting curled and blackened and went to ash in the chipped bowl he used for washing his spoons. The herb-lore instructions. The jokes. The recipe for elderflower cordial she'd promised to teach him in person and never had the chance. The last letter, the one that ended *be boring, my love, and live long, and put flowers on my grave when it's safe*.

When the last of it was ash he stirred the bowl with a spoon and poured the grey remains into the slop bucket, where they joined eggshells and tea leaves and vanished among a thousand indistinguishable scraps.

No letters. No evidence. No line of inheritance for an examiner to trace.

It should have felt like safety. Instead it felt like burying her a second time, and this time alone.

Petra sat back on his heels in the dark and listened to his own heart doing its steady, poison-hardened work, and admitted at last what he'd known since the herald's voice rolled down the chimney: staying was dying slowly. Staying was waiting for the cup to be offered in a room with locks on the door. Staying was ten crowns of silver and a woman's frightened testimony, sooner or later, from someone who'd seen him not flinch one time too many.

There were roads out of the city. He'd know; he'd run contraband along half of them in his wilder youth, before the guild and the spoon, back when he was fast and anonymous and stupid in the ways only nineteen-year-olds are stupid. The Thornfell passes. The smuggler caches. Old routes that the king's riders had forgotten because the king's riders had never been hungry enough to learn them.

Thornfell. Wild country, thick with thorn and fog and old bad memories. The kind of place a marked man could disappear, if disappearing were still possible anywhere in Ironhollow.

Petra wrapped the empty space where his mother's words had lived, replaced the flagstone, and pinched out the candle.

In the morning, he would begin making arrangements. Salt, silver, secrets — the three coins that opened any gate in this kingdom, including gates that officially did not open.

Somewhere below, in the palace undercrofts, a boy named Wren was facing his examination, and there was nothing — *nothing* — Petra could do for him tonight except survive long enough to become someone who could do something.

He lay down and closed his eyes and began, silently, to count the days until the spring caravans left for the passes.

Fourteen days. He could be invisible for fourteen more days.

He hoped.
