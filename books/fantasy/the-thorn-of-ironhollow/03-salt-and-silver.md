## 3. Salt and Silver

The salt came to Petra in a barrel marked *pickled herring*, because everything worth having in Ironhollow arrived disguised as something no one wanted.

He'd paid a fishmonger named Doss three silver pieces and the promise of a story — not a true one, necessarily, but an entertaining one — to look the other way while the barrel was rolled off the quay cart. Now it sat in his rented room above the chandlery, lid pried up, filling the space with the smell of the sea that had nothing to do with herring. Salt was better than gold in the Thornfell passes. Gold you could only bribe people with, and bribed people talked. Salt you could eat, preserve with, trade for mules. Salt kept a man alive when his cleverness ran out.

Petra had learned that at nineteen, running goods through the mountains for a smuggler called Half-Face Hesk, who had taught him two things: how to read a pass like a sentence, and how to disappear from a conversation without anyone noticing he'd left. Both lessons had served him well in nine years of court tasting. The second one especially.

He measured out salt into oilskin packets by candlelight, hands steady, mind doing what it always did: listing.

Salt for Ilsa. Salt for the mule man in Bracken Hollow, if he still lived — and if he didn't, salt for whoever had inherited his mules, because mule men were like tavern stools; remove one and another appeared within the week. Salt enough to buy silence at two river crossings. And in the false bottom of his satchel, wrapped in wool: the vial from last night's wine.

He took it out now and held it up to the candle. It caught the light wrong — too yellow, too slow. Wine should move like a gossip. This moved like syrup with secrets.

Nine years of tasting had given him a vocabulary most poisoners would have wept over. Bitterness had dialects. There was the flat bitterness of spoiled bark, the bright bitterness of certain roots, the green bitterness of things picked too early. This was none of those. This was metallic underneath, old-fashioned, the kind of bitterness that came from compounds ground and aged on purpose. Someone had put something in that wine knowing — *knowing* — that only one mouth in the whole banquet hall would touch it.

His mouth.

He'd swallowed a half-measure, as always, and pocketed the rest, and spent the walk home cataloguing what his body did with it. A faint numbness at the back of the tongue. A slight loosening in the knees, gone within twenty steps. His heart, traitorous organ, beating exactly as calmly as it always did, because nine years of measured doses had built walls in his blood that most poisons couldn't climb.

*Immunity,* the guildmasters called it. A professional asset. But the king had stood on his balcony yesterday and called a different name for it.

*Gift-bearer.*

The word sat in Petra's chest like a swallowed stone. He thought of the kitchen boy — Tam, someone had said his name was, Tam with the flour on his ears — dragged out of the palace kitchens by two guards while the criers outside shouted about silver for names. The boy hadn't even done anything visible. Someone had simply *said*. That was all it took now. A whisper, a grudge, a neighbor's envy, and silver changed hands, and a person became property of the crown.

And if they tested Petra — if some guild physician pricked his finger or made him drink a measured dose before witnesses and watched him shrug it off — what would they call that? Skill? Or gift?

They would call it whatever was most useful to them. That was the first rule of power, and Petra had learned it secondhand through his stomach.

So: escape. Through Thornfell, over the Shepherd's Tooth, down into the free valleys beyond the king's maps. He knew those passes the way other men knew their mothers' faces. He had drawn them once, every switchback and goat track, in a little book he kept sewn into his coat lining — and then, four years ago, he had burned that book page by page in a palace hearth, because a food-taster could not afford to be interesting.

He regretted the burning now. But the map lived in his head, where fire couldn't reach it. Mostly.

A knock at the door. Three quick, two slow — the rhythm of the fishmonger's boy, which meant the message had been delivered, which meant Ilsa Vane would be expecting him at the Salt Gate come nightfall.

Petra finished packing, ate a supper he tested out of pure habit (nothing, tonight, but honest bread and older cheese), pulled his hood low, and went out into streets that felt different than they had a week ago. Same cobbles. Same lamplighters. But now there were ears everywhere, hungry ones, fed on silver. He walked the way Hesk had taught him: unhurried, forgettable, a man going nowhere worth following.

* * *

Ilsa Vane had been a gate-captain for eleven years, which meant she'd survived eleven years of promotions, purges, and budget cuts, and had done it by being exactly corrupt enough to be useful and exactly loyal enough to be trusted. She met Petra in her office above the gatehouse, a room that smelled of lamp oil, leather, and the particular staleness of paperwork nobody intended to file.

She didn't look up when he entered. "You're late."

"I'm early," Petra said. "Your sergeant just hasn't told you yet."

That got a glance. Ilsa was fifty-odd, built like a door, with gray coming in at her temples in a pattern that suggested she'd earned every strand individually. "Okafor." She said his name like she was checking it against a list. "The taster. Come to bribe me at the worst possible moment in the history of this city."

"I come bearing pickled herring."

"You come bearing a barrel of salt pretending to be pickled herring, which is worse, because it means you think I'm stupid enough to need it disguised." She leaned back. "Sit down, Petra. Let's have this conversation properly, since we're both going to pretend afterward it never happened."

He sat. He set one packet of salt on the desk between them, and beside it, folded paper — not coin. Secrets. "The eastern tax wagons reroute through your gate on the ninth. Your predecessor skimmed from them for six years and never got caught because he never touched the wine shipments. You could retire on what passes under your nose, if you knew where to look. I'm telling you where to look."

Ilsa looked at the paper a long time. Then at him. "Why do you know that?"

"Because I taste for the people who count the taxes, and counting men talk over dinner."

"And the salt?"

"For the pass road. I'll need provisions at Bracken Hollow, and quiet at the ford."

"The Thornfell passes." She said it flatly. "Through the hunting grounds."

"The Thornfell passes," Petra agreed, "which are wide enough to get lost in and high enough that criers' voices don't carry."

"They're also crawling." Ilsa pushed the salt packet back toward him — then, after a moment, left it where it lay, which was its own kind of answer. "The Gift-Hunt isn't a proclamation, Petra. It's an industry. Riders on every road east of the river. And you—" she tapped the desk, once, hard— "you're not just any man leaving town the week after the balcony speech. You're the king's own taster, walking out the same week the king announces he's hunting people whose blood does strange things. Do you understand what that looks like from where I sit?"

"It looks like a man taking leave to visit his mother's grave."

"Is his mother's grave in Thornfell?"

"He never knew his mother. The grave is wherever he says it is."

Something moved behind Ilsa's eyes — not warmth, exactly, but the recognition of a fellow practitioner. They had both spent careers being underestimated by men who signed their pay. "Here's my trouble," she said. "I can open a gate. Gates are easy. What I can't do is make you safe on the other side of it. And I'll tell you something for free, since you brought me salt instead of silver, which I respect." She lowered her voice. "The taster's guild keeps ledgers."

"I know. They record doses. It's procedure—"

"Not dose ledgers." She shook her head slowly. "*Swallow* ledgers. Every measured drop that's crossed your lips in nine years, recorded, dated, compounded. My husband worked guild security before he died. He used to say the taster's hall had more ink than the treasury. They don't just test poisons on you, Petra. They *keep accounts* on you. Why do you think that is?"

Petra's hands, resting on his knees, went very still. He made himself shrug. "Record-keeping. Guilds love records."

"Guilds love records," Ilsa agreed. "But there's a clerk — guild man, soft hands, comes through my gate twice a year with a courier case chained to his wrist. Never opens it. Never lets it out of his sight. Two years ago he got drunk at the Wayfarer's Rest — soft-handed men drink badly — and he said a thing I've thought about ever since." She leaned forward. "He said, 'It takes nine years to bring a palate to ripeness.'"

The candle flame bent in a draft from under the door. Somewhere below, a guard laughed at something.

"Ripeness," Petra repeated. The word tasted like the wine from last night. Metallic. Old-fashioned. Ground and aged on purpose.

"That's what he said." Ilsa sat back. "Now, I don't know what it means, and I've got children, so I don't go asking questions with teeth. But I know what it sounds like, and it doesn't sound like a man being protected from poison. It sounds like a man being *prepared*."

*Prepared.* Like a dish. Like a vintage. Petra thought of every measured half-dose, every careful swallow, every morning he'd woken proud of his steadiness, his discipline, his invisible craft — and wondered, for the first time in nine years, whose craft it had actually been. He'd thought he was building his armor one dose at a time. What if he'd been building something else? What if his body was a larder someone else had stocked?

He found his voice. "The gate. Ninth bell tonight. Can you?"

"I can look away for the length of a prayer," Ilsa said. "One prayer, not two. Take the south postern, not the main arch — the main arch has a new man on it who's eager, and eager men cause trouble." She slid the salt packet back across the desk toward him, and this time she pushed it firmly into his hand. "Keep this. You'll need it more than me. The secrets I'll take, because secrets don't weigh anything and I've got room for one more sin."

"Why?" Petra asked. "Truly. Why help me?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw — the tired man under the taster's calm, the boy who'd run smuggler roads, the body full of nine years of somebody else's ledger — seemed to settle something in her.

"Because my grandmother was a hearth-witch," she said simply. "Nothing grand. She could keep bread from burning and fevers from climbing, and when the last purge came she hid it so deep even my grandfather never knew. She died in her bed at eighty, warm and safe, because she was *small* about it." Her jaw tightened. "Then yesterday some boy got dragged out of a kitchen for less than my grandmother ever dared, and I watched the guards do it, and I thought: small isn't working anymore. Small is just dying slower." She stood. "Go home, pack, and don't be seen buying anything else. Ninth bell. South postern. If anyone asks, you went to visit your mother's grave."

"She doesn't exist."

"Neither does my integrity. We're even." At the door, she stopped him with one hand. "Petra. The riders aren't just on the roads. Guild men ride with them — clerks, the soft-handed kind. They're looking for something specific out here, and they're checking names against lists. Whatever's in that wine they gave you—"

"You know about the wine?"

"I know you pocketed a vial at the banquet because I watch everyone, it's my job." She opened the door onto the stairwell dark. "Whatever it was, someone paid good money to put it in *your* cup and no one else's. Ask yourself why a guild that's dosed you for nine years suddenly changes the recipe the same week the king starts collecting gifts."

Petra stepped out into the stairwell dark, the salt heavy under his arm, the question heavier under his ribs.

Ninth bell. South postern. One prayer's length of looking away.

Behind him, Ilsa blew out her candle, and the gate-captain's office went dark, keeping its secrets the way it always had — until, someday, someone paid for them.
