## 4. What the Ledgers Say

The taster's guild kept its ledgers in a cellar beneath the Hall of Palates, and Guildmaster Corvane liked to say that the cellar was the true treasury of Ironhollow — more valuable than the king's vaults, though nothing in it glittered. Rows upon rows of bound volumes, their spines cracked with age, their pages crowded with entries in a dozen different hands across three generations of scriveners.

*Subject 14. Morning draught, two grains of bitterroot. No reaction.*

*Subject 14. Midday broth, essence of hemlock, quarter-measure. Subject reports "a faint pepperiness." Scrivener's note: subject's palate remains exceptional.*

*Subject 14. Banquet wine, distilled wyrmshade. Full measure. No reaction. Note from Undermaster Drell: this should not be possible.*

Corvane ran one gloved finger down the column of dates and felt something close to affection. It was not a warm feeling — Corvane did not have warm feelings, or if he had once had them, he had filed them away somewhere in these same cellars and lost the key — but it was steady, and proprietary, and in its own grey way it served him as well as warmth served other men.

Nine years. Nine years of measured doses, each one logged, each one calibrated, each one a small deposit into an account no one but Corvane and the king knew existed. Other guilds kept books on coin. The Tasters' Guild kept books on bodies. And of all the bodies in the ledger, none had performed so beautifully as Subject Fourteen.

The boy — man now, Corvane supposed, though in his own mind Petra Okafor would always be *the asset*, and assets did not age, they merely matured like casks — had come to them at nineteen with nothing: a dead mother, a smuggler's past he thought well hidden, and a palate so fine he could distinguish river water from well water by smell alone. Corvane had seen the potential immediately. Not the gift itself; gifts were vulgar things, fireworks for peasants. No, what Corvane saw was a vessel. A clean, unclaimed body that could be filled, year over year, with exactly what the crown required.

And the boy had swallowed everything without ever once suspecting why.

Corvane turned the page. The lamplight caught the marginalia, and he permitted himself the smallest smile at the entry from Year Three:

*Subject 14 requests additional salt ration. Denied. Subject sulks for two days. Note: sulking does not affect tolerance levels. Recommend indulgence in future to maintain morale.*

They had indulged him, after all. The honey cakes on his name-day. The good boots when his wore through. Corvane believed in caring for livestock; it was simple economics. A neglected asset bolted, and a bolted asset was worth a fraction of a contented one. He had even, in a moment of sentimentality he would deny under torture, sent the boy a new whetstone when word came that his old knife had chipped. The knife had been returned to the armory within the week — assets were searched, after all — but Corvane liked to think the whetstone had been used.

He reached the final volume, the current one, and his smile faded.

*Subject 14. Banquet wine, private reserve. Wyrmshade compound, refined batch seven. Measure: full. Result: pending.*

Pending. That single word had sat in the ledger for six days now, because the tasting had never been confirmed complete. The cup had been found emptied, yes. But the report from the under-taster assigned to observe was maddeningly thin: subject appeared pale, left the hall early, was seen speaking briefly to the gate-captain Ilsa, and then —

And then nothing. Subject Fourteen had walked out of the palace the following morning with a pack on his shoulder, as he did twice a year on his sanctioned leave, except that this time he had not returned by evening, nor the next day, and the runner sent to his lodging had found the room swept clean, the bed made with military precision, and a coin left on the sill. Payment, Corvane understood, for the window he'd climbed out of. The boy had broken nothing. Had stolen nothing. Had even, gods rot him, straightened the bedclothes.

Corvane closed the ledger gently, which those who knew him recognized as the most dangerous thing he could do with his hands.

* * *

The king's summons came at dusk, as it always did, delivered by a page who waited outside the guildhall door without meeting anyone's eyes. Aldric received Corvane in the map room rather than the throne room, which meant business rather than theater, and Corvane appreciated the distinction. Theater cost money. Business only cost other people.

"You've read the ledgers," Aldric said. It was not a question. The king stood with his back turned, studying the great painted map of the realm that covered the eastern wall, though Corvane noted his eyes were fixed not on any border but on the blank space beyond the Thornfell passes, where the cartographers had given up on detail and simply written *mountains*.

"I have read them every night since the asset absconded," Corvane said. "Your Majesty."

"And?"

"And the harvest is nearly ripe." Corvane chose the words with care, as he chose all words, the way a vintner chooses a cork. "Nine years of cultivation. His blood carries compounds now that our alchemists cannot synthesize and our gardens cannot grow. The last three elixir batches drawn from him extended Your Majesty's vitality by — " he consulted memory, though he did not need to — "fourteen months, eleven months, and thirteen months respectively. The next drawing, properly timed, should yield the largest quantity yet. His body has been building toward it. One more harvest, sire, and we will have enough refined gift-blood to see you safely through another decade."

Aldric's shoulders eased by a degree. On most men the motion would have been invisible. On a king it was a landslide.

"Then find him."

"He is making for the mountains. The evidence is thin but consistent — he bought passage-goods, he drew wages early, and the gate logs show him passing the Salt Gate on foot the morning after his leave began." Corvane paused. "There is also the matter of Captain Ilsa."

"The gate-captain?" Aldric turned. "She took your silver and let him walk?"

"She took my silver, yes. Whether she let him walk or merely failed to stop him, she has been — unhelpful. I questioned her yesterday. She claims the asset showed her papers she did not have authority to verify, and that she is a servant of the city watch, not of the guild." Corvane's voice stayed level, pleasant, almost mild. "I have taken the liberty of arranging her reassignment to a coastal garrison. Very far away. Effective tomorrow."

"Is that necessary?"

"It is hygiene, sire." Corvane folded his hands. "One loose thread invites pulling. And there is a second matter, less comfortable, which I must lay before you regardless of how it reflects on my guild."

The king's attention sharpened. Corvane had learned long ago that the fastest way to keep a monarch's trust was to hand him bad news wrapped neatly in competence. Kings forgave failures they witnessed; they never forgave surprises.

"Speak."

"The dose we administered in the banquet wine — the refined wyrmshade — was designed to provoke a measurable response. In a common man it kills. In a gift-bearer it marks the blood for weeks afterward, visible to any trained alchemist. In Subject Fourteen, it produced neither death nor the expected marker pattern. His blood burned through it in a manner our records associate with only one other class of subject." Corvane met the king's eyes. "The old line. The dragon-bonded."

The silence in the map room was long enough that Corvane could hear the candles burning. Somewhere below them, the palace undercrofts hummed with the quiet industry of the alchemists' wing, distilling, refining, measuring — the machinery of the king's immortality grinding on whether or not its raw material cooperated.

"A taster," Aldric said at last, "with the old blood in him."

"A taster we built," Corvane corrected, softly, and heard the possessiveness in his own voice and did not trouble to disguise it. Possession, in Corvane's experience, was not a sin. It was an accounting principle. "Whatever he was born with, sire, nine years of our dosing refined it. Concentrated it. He is, quite literally, the finest vintage this guild has ever produced. Which is why I must impress upon you the urgency. If he crosses into Thornfell, he passes beyond the reach of writ and warrant both. The mountain clans do not honor the crown's law, and the passes are closing with the season. We lose him past that ridge, we may as well pour nine years down a drain."

Aldric looked back at the map. His finger rose and traced the road eastward, out of the capital, along the river, up through the fog country to the thin brown lines that stood for passes.

"How many riders can you field quietly?"

"Twelve. My best. They travel as wool merchants." Corvane had already chosen them, already briefed them, already provisioned them — he mentioned this not as confession but as reassurance, the way one mentions that a house was built on stone. "They will ride through the night and reach the river ford by the second dawn. After the ford, the roads narrow to goat tracks, and twelve men become conspicuous. But before the ford — " he tapped the map where the river bent — "we take him."

"And if he resists?"

Corvane considered the question honestly, because the king deserved honesty in matters of accounting. "Then we take what remains. But I would prefer him breathing, sire, and preferably calm. Frightened blood sours the draw. Our alchemists are very particular." He allowed himself a dry note. "As am I. I have spent nine years perfecting this particular wine. I do not intend to serve it bruised."

Something flickered across the king's face — not quite unease, but the shadow of it, the look of a man who has just remembered what his dinner is made of. It passed quickly. It always passed quickly. Hunger was a marvelous solvent for squeamishness.

"Go," Aldric said. "Bring me my vintage."

* * *

The guild stables smelled of hay and lamp oil, and Corvane stood in the doorway while his twelve riders mounted up, saying little, because little needed saying. They were a mixed lot — two ex-soldiers, a poacher turned tracker whose loyalty Corvane owned outright through debts the man would never finish paying, and a soft-spoken clerk named Fenwick whom everyone underestimated until they saw him work. Corvane addressed them as a group, brief and unhurried, a man discussing freight.

"The cargo is one man. Dark hair, grey eyes, walks softly, eats carefully. He is not armed in any way that will concern you, but he is cleverer than any of you, and I include Fenwick in that assessment, Fenwick, don't argue. He knows the Thornfell roads better than any living soul — better, I suspect, than the smugglers who think they own them. He ran goods through those ravines at fifteen. So do not assume the obvious route is safe, and do not assume the safe route is obvious. He will expect pursuit from the moment he sees dust behind him. Give him none until the ford."

He handed the tracker a small oilskin packet. Inside was a description, a sketch — Corvane had commissioned it from memory and it was, he felt, quite good, capturing the asset's habitual wariness around the mouth — and a vial of testing reagent.

"If you must shed blood, shed it after you confirm the mark. I want certainty before I want speed. And if he offers you food or drink on the road — " here Corvane paused, and something that was almost humor moved behind his eyes, dry as old paper — "decline. He is, whatever else he may be, the best poisoner this kingdom has ever employed, and he doesn't even know it yet."

The riders laughed politely. Corvane did not. He watched them wheel out of the yard in pairs, torches hooded, hooves wrapped in sacking against the cobblestones' noise, until the last tail disappeared down the lane toward the river road.

Then he went back inside, down the stairs, into the cellar of ledgers, and opened Volume Nine to the final page. He uncapped his pen. For a long moment he sat with the nib poised above the entry that still read *pending*, and then, in his small precise hand, he wrote:

*Subject 14. Status: overdue. Recovery initiated. Expected return: within the fortnight.*

*Note to successor, should one be required: the asset responds to kindness. Use it.*

He blotted the page, set the pen down square with the ledger's edge, and banked the lamp. In the dark of the cellar, nine years of swallowing sat shelved and silent, row upon row, patient as graves.

At the ford, he estimated, the riders would make their move on the second dawn — which gave his runaway vintage four days' head start, a swollen river, and a lifetime of knowing exactly how the world poisoned people.

It would not, Corvane reflected as he climbed the stairs toward his supper, be nearly enough. Assets always believed, in the end, that they belonged to themselves.

The ledgers said otherwise.
