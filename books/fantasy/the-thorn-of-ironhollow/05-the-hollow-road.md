## 5. The Hollow Road

The fog in the Thornfell ravines did not drift so much as *settle*, like a great grey cat deciding where to lie down, and Petra had learned long ago that you did not argue with a cat that size.

He climbed out of the river valley before dawn, when the mist was thickest and the world was reduced to six feet of wet stone and whatever memory could supply beyond it. Memory supplied quite a lot. Nine years ago — no, ten now, gods, had it truly been ten? — he had run these ravines for a smuggler named Doss Marrow, carrying bolts of salted silk and kegs of unlicensed brandy on his back while his knees were still young enough to forgive him. He'd been seventeen and invincible and paid in coin he spent too fast. Now he was twenty-seven and invisible by profession, and the coin in his purse was guild silver, which felt like carrying a confession.

*Left at the split birch,* he told himself, feeling for it in the dark. *Then down into the Hollow Road proper.*

The split birch was still there, older and more splintered, but unmistakable: two trunks leaning apart like a man caught mid-shrug. Petra allowed himself a small, private smile. It was absurd how much comfort a tree could give. Ten years, and the ravines hadn't forgotten him either.

He moved the way Doss had taught him — heel first, weight low, never hurrying even when hurrying felt like survival. *Panic is loud,* Doss used to say, picking his teeth with a fishhook because he believed this made him memorable. *Loud men die in ravines. Quiet men die in bed, which is better.* Petra had taken the lesson and discarded the fishhook, and in the years since he had refined quietness into something close to an art. A food-taster who flinched at bitterness did not last a season. A taster who flinched at anything did not last at all.

So he walked calmly through the fog with a stolen vial of wine-bitterness in his pocket and the king's hunt ringing in his ears, and he permitted himself exactly one moment of exhilaration.

It came at the top of the second ridge, when he looked back — which Doss would have boxed his ears for — and saw nothing behind him at all. No riders. No torchlight. No Ironhollow. Just fog folding over the world like a page being turned. He was nobody's taster until further notice. He was a rumor moving south.

"Ha," he said softly, to no one, and the fog swallowed even that.

* * *

The first cache was where he'd left it, which surprised him more than if it had been robbed.

Doss had kept supply caches all through the Thornfell passes — wax-sealed crocks buried under cairns, each holding hardtack, dried plums, a twist of salt, and usually a note insulting whoever found them. Petra had helped stock this one himself, a lifetime ago, wedged beneath an overhang above the old charcoal burners' path. He cleared away ten years of leaf-rot with his boot heel, pried up the flat stone, and there it sat: the crock, intact, its seal unbroken.

"You beautiful paranoid man," Petra murmured, meaning Doss, or possibly the cache, or possibly himself for remembering the exact cairn after a decade.

Inside: hardtack gone soft as cake but edible; plums shriveled to leather but sweet; salt, still salt; and a scrap of oilcloth with writing on it. He held it up to the grey light.

*If you're reading this, you owe me four crowns, Petra. Interest accrues.*

Petra laughed — actually laughed, alone in a fog-choked ravine with a bounty presumably on his head — and tucked the note into his coat next to the vial. It was ridiculous. It was also, he noticed, the first time in nine years anyone had asked him for something as ordinary as money. Not his blood. Not his tongue. Four crowns. He resolved, absurdly, to pay it back if he ever saw Doss again, plus interest, plus a fifth crown for the plums.

He ate walking, rationing like a professional, and let himself plan the route in the comfortable arithmetic of distances. Two days down the Hollow Road to the ford at Greywater. Another day to the high pass Ilsa had unlocked for him with salt and secrets. After that, the southern vales, where a man with no name and no gift could vanish into orchard work and be forgotten by spring.

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, an excellent plan, and Petra would think about that later — about how good plans are the ones the world most enjoys ruining.

* * *

The rockslide announced itself only as a wrongness in the sound of his own footsteps.

He was three hours past the second cache, deep in the Narrows where the walls pinched close and the fog pressed in like wool, when he realized the scree underfoot had changed pitch. Loose stone has a voice; any smuggler's runner learns it young. This stone spoke in a higher register, dry and restless, the sound of rock balanced on rock with nothing decided yet.

Petra stopped. Stood very still. Looked up.

Above him, half-glimpsed through the fog, the slope of the Narrows rose steep and jumbled — and across it, faint but legible to a man who had once been paid to notice such things, ran a seam of pale scarred stone. A cut face. Fresh-cut, geologically speaking, though "fresh" here might mean five years or fifty.

He knew that seam. Gods help him, he knew it intimately, because on a cold autumn night ten years ago he had stood at the top of it with a pry bar and three other runners, levering loose the keystone of a slope so that it would come down across the road behind them and bury the road ahead of the tax collectors' horses. Doss's idea. Doss's orders. Petra had been the smallest and lightest, so they'd tied a rope around him and sent him out along the lip of the slide to place the wedges, and he had done it, laughing, terrified, seventeen, while the mountain hummed under him like a struck bell.

They'd ridden the slide down on the far side and drunk Doss's brandy and never once spoken of it again, because that was how Doss's people handled things.

And now Petra Okafor was standing directly below it, in the fog, having just spent the last hour cheerfully kicking loose stones downhill.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, no."

He did not run. Running was loud, and loud men died in ravines, and besides, his body understood before his mind did that running would shake the slope like a dog shaking off water. Instead he went down onto hands and knees and began to crawl, smooth and slow, toward the wider ground he remembered being perhaps forty yards south. The fog gave him nothing to aim for. The stone under his palms whispered its high dry whisper. Somewhere above, with terrible patience, the mountain considered its options.

He was maybe halfway when the wedge he himself had placed — or its ghost, its grandchild, some descendant of that long-ago night's meddling — finally lost its argument with gravity.

There was no roar at first. There was a *click*, distinct and intimate, like a lock turning. Then the whole slope above him exhaled.

Petra pressed himself flat into a fold of the hillside, arms over his head, and the world became noise. Stone poured past him on both sides — not at him, thanks to the fold, thanks to luck, thanks to the seventeen-year-old who had apparently placed his wedges with more care than he'd ever given himself credit for — a river of rock running down the throat of the Narrows where his legs had been moments before. Dust filled the fog until there was no difference between them. Something the size of a sheep struck the ground close enough to spatter grit against his cheek. He tasted stone, and blood where he'd bitten his lip, and underneath both, absurdly, dried plum.

Then it was over, and the silence rang louder than the slide had.

Petra lay there breathing in careful, measured draughts — the same breathing he used after swallowing something that might kill him, waiting to learn whether today was the day — and began, slowly, to laugh. It came out shaky and half-hysterical and it kept going longer than dignity allowed.

"I rigged that," he informed the dust. "I *rigged* that. Ten years ago. With my own hands."

Because that was the joke, wasn't it? The trap on this road wasn't the king's riders or Corvane's ledgers. It was Petra himself, younger and dumber, reaching across a decade to nearly kill him. He had fled his own past and his own past had tried to drop a mountain on him. If he survived all this, he decided, he was going to write Doss a very strongly worded letter.

He dug himself out, inventoried himself — bruised hip, scraped palms, nothing broken — and stood at the edge of the fresh slide field, looking at the new scar across the Hollow Road. Going forward meant climbing over it. Going back meant everything he'd burned his mother's letters to escape.

He climbed over it. His knees complained. He ignored them.

* * *

He smelled it before he saw it.

Not smoke, exactly — smoke was alive, it curled and breathed. This was the ghost of smoke, old and cold, soaked so deep into stone that the rain of years hadn't washed it out. Char and sulfur and something else beneath, mineral and strange, like a forge that had burned a metal no smith would name.

The Narrows opened, as he remembered, into a broad hanging valley — the Shepherds' Rest, the maps called it, though no shepherds had rested there in living memory. The fog thinned here, pooling in the hollows instead of drowning the world, and Petra came down into the valley floor and stopped walking entirely.

The grass was dead. Not winter-dead, not drought-dead — *scoured*, blackened down to the root in a great ragged swath a hundred yards wide, as though something enormous had knelt here and exhaled. At the edges of the burn, the surviving grass grew in a strange, stunted fringe, bent outward, combed away from the center like iron filings around a lodestone.

And across the scorched earth, pressed deep enough to hold last week's rainwater, were tracks.

Petra had seen bear tracks. He had seen elk tracks, wolf tracks, and once, memorably, the tracks of a tax collector's horse after it had eaten a barrel of fermented apples. Nothing in his experience had prepared him for prints the size of cartwheels, three-toed, each one sunk eight inches into hard ground, spaced in a deliberate stride that said *this creature was not fleeing*. It had walked. Slowly. In pain, maybe — the right hind print dragged, just slightly, a shallow tail of earth pulled along behind it.

He followed the track-line with his eyes to where it emerged from the burn and crossed the valley toward the northern cliffs, where the old mine adits gaped in the hillside — the copper workings, abandoned before his grandfather's time, their timber mouths rotten and slumped.

The largest adit had collapsed. Recently. The rubble at its mouth lay bright and unweathered against the dark of the old spoil heaps, and part of the collapse, Petra saw, was not stone at all but charred timber, beams burned through from within and then brought down by something pushing outward from inside. Or pulling inward. Or simply too heavy, and too tired, to be careful anymore.

The tracks led straight to it and stopped.

Petra stood at the edge of the scorched ground for a long time, doing the arithmetic he didn't want to do. Fire hot enough to scour earth. Feet big enough to fill cartwheel prints. A mine brought down around something that had crawled inside to die, the way animals crawl somewhere dark and quiet to die, the way everything eventually crawls somewhere dark and quiet —

Every story he'd ever heard as a boy came up out of the fog at once. *Dragons in the Thornfell. Dragons in the deep places. Gone now, boy, all gone, the last fires went out before your grandmother's grandmother.* Stories told in the same tone as stories about heroes and honest kings: pretty, impossible, safely finished.

The tracks were not finished. The tracks were three days old at most, and they dragged.

His heart was doing something complicated — fear, yes, thick and animal, but threaded through it a wire of something else, something that felt disgracefully like hope. Because if one story was true, walking wounded through the Shepherds' Rest, then the world was larger than the one Aldric ruled and Corvane catalogued, larger than ledgers and proclamations and the price of a kitchen boy's name. And a larger world, Petra thought, might have room in it for a man whose blood refused to behave.

The fog was thinning toward evening. On the cliffs above the collapsed adit, the last light caught something — a gleam, brief and vast, like lamplight moving behind a shuttered window. Then gone.

Petra looked at the dead grass. At the dragging print. At the dark mouth of the mine, breathing its cold char-smell into the dusk.

A sensible man, he thought, would walk south. A sensible man would put this valley behind him and be in the orchard country by spring, nameless and safe and forever wondering.

"Well," Petra said aloud, to the fog, to the tracks, to the seventeen-year-old who had apparently survived long enough to get him killed after all. "I was never sensible. It's practically the only thing I've got left."

He crossed the scorched earth — the ground still faintly warm under his boots, which answered several questions and raised worse ones — and stood before the fallen timbers of the mine mouth. From somewhere in the deep dark below, slow as a tide, came the sound of breathing.

It was not the breathing of anything small.

Petra took one step into the dark, and the darkness took a breath, and said his name.
