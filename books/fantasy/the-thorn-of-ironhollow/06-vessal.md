## 6. Vessal

The voice did not echo. That was the first wrong thing about it.

Petra had spent nine years learning how sound behaved in enclosed spaces — the palace undercrofts where he'd been trained, the wine cellars where he'd worked, the smuggler tunnels of his youth. Sound in a stone throat bounced. It came back to you wearing different clothes, arriving late, arriving changed.

This voice arrived exactly on time and dressed only as itself, as though the mountain had simply decided to pass it along without comment, the way a servant passes a letter they have not dared to read.

*Petra Okafor.*

Not "who goes there." Not a roar, not a challenge. His name — his whole name, the one his mother had stitched into the hem of his coat when he was six because she said a boy should carry his name somewhere it couldn't be taken from him. A name he had not spoken aloud in nine years. A name that existed now on no ledger, no roll, no document between the coast and the capital.

He stood very still in the mine mouth with one hand braced against a fallen timber gone soft with rot, and he thought, with the strange detached clarity that had kept him alive through two hundred and eleven controlled doses: *Well. Either I have finally eaten something that disagrees with me, or I am about to die in a hole in the ground, and honestly, Mother always said it would be one of the two.*

"Who's there?" he asked. His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

The darkness breathed again. The breathing was the tide-sound he'd heard from outside — slow, vast, and wrong in a way it took him three more cycles of it to name. It was labored. There was a catch in it, high up, like a bellows with a split seam. Whatever was down there was not merely large. It was *failing*.

"You are the one who smells of deaths," the voice said.

Petra considered this. "I get that a lot," he said. "Occupational hazard."

"Do you?" Something moved far below — a shifting of weight so enormous that grit sifted down through the broken timbers above him like black snow. "No. You do not understand what you carry. Come down, taster. My eyes are not what they were, but my nose has never once lied to me, and it says you are the most interesting thing to walk into this mountain in four hundred years. That is not a compliment. Four hundred years is not long enough for anything interesting."

Every sensible fiber of Petra's being — the fibers he had spent nearly a decade weaving into armor — advised him to turn around, walk back out into the fog, and never speak of this again. He catalogued them respectfully, the way he catalogued the taste of almond in a cup of wine, and then he climbed down over the fallen timbers into the dark, because he had already established, seventeen years ago and confirmed just yesterday at the rockslide, that he was never sensible.

* * *

The shaft descended in switchbacks, half-collapsed, and Petra navigated it by memory and touch — old smuggler instinct, hands reading the walls like a ledger. Someone had mined here once, long ago; the timber-work was ancient and strange, joints cut in patterns no Ironhollow crew used. Deeper, the tunnel widened, and the air changed. Warmer. Faintly mineral, like the smell of a struck flint held just under the threshold of sparking.

And there was light. Not firelight. Nothing so kind. A dull red glow, guttering, coming from around the last bend — pulsing slowly, in time with that split-bellows breathing, like the coals of a fire nobody had tended in days.

Petra turned the corner and stopped.

The chamber beyond had not been dug by men. Men had merely *found* it, once, and their diggings were a scratch of candle-glow at its edge — an abandoned cart no bigger than a child's toy against the thing that filled the cavern floor. She lay curled around herself like a question mark, scales the color of old iron shot through with veins of tarnished copper, and she was — Petra did the arithmetic automatically, the way he did everything — she was the size of the chapel in Ironhollow village. Larger. Her wings were folded against her flanks, and where the membrane caught the glow of her own fading heat, he could see the thin places, the scars, the patches gone gray as ash.

She was dying. He knew death the way a priest knew prayer, and there was no mistaking it. This was what a cathedral looked like as it fell.

"Oh," Petra said. It was not a word he had planned. It was simply what fell out of him.

Her head lifted. It took a long time — he watched the muscles of her neck engage in sequence, effortful as a ship raising anchor — and turned toward him, and one eye opened.

It was gold. Old gold, the gold of coins buried so long the face on them has worn away, and it fixed on him with an attention so total that Petra felt, absurdly, like a specimen under glass. Like something being *sampled*. He knew that feeling from the other side of the table; he had spent nine years putting it into other mouths. He had never imagined it could be done to him with a look.

"There you are," the dragon said. And then, in a tone of mild scholarly disappointment, entirely at odds with the terror of her: "Hm. Younger than I expected. And thinner. Do they not feed you?"

"They feed me fine," Petra said, before he could stop himself. Then: "How do you know my name?"

"I know many things I should not." Her eye half-lidded, and the glow of her dimmed with it, and for a moment Petra thought she had fallen asleep mid-conversation, which would have been almost funny if his heart weren't trying to exit through his ribs. "Your mother gave it to you twice. Once at your birthing, once at the river shrine at Coldharrow, when you were nine, when she begged whatever was listening to keep you. Something was listening."

The floor of the world tilted gently, the way it did after a bad dose. Petra sat down. Not gracefully — his knees simply filed their resignation and he followed them to the cold stone, landing hard on his backside with his pack clinking.

"My mother," he said carefully, "has been dead eight years."

"I am aware," Vessal said. "I was listening then too. Grief carries, little taster, farther than you would believe. It is one of the few human things worth carrying."

*Little taster.* He seized on the practical questions the way a drowning man seizes driftwood, because the alternative was to sit in a collapsing mine and weep in front of a creature who could eat him like an oyster. "You've been watching me. Nine years. Why?"

"Not watching." The great head shifted, settling lower on forelegs folded like the root-systems of felled oaks. "Smelling. Every swallow you took, every measured poison they fed you in that pretty room with the silver spoons — do you think it vanished? It went into you. And you —" the eye opened fully again, and the gold in it seemed, briefly, to brighten — "you did the one thing none of them intended. You made their poisons yours. You built a lock out of every key they tried."

"The guild calls it tolerance."

"The guild," Vessal said, "calls a stolen harvest by many gentle names. Tolerance. Service. Duty." Something moved in the cavern — not her body; the air itself, a faint stirring of warmth off her flanks, carrying that struck-flint smell stronger now. "Tell me, Petra Okafor. When they hand you the cup, and you drink, and you do not die — what do you feel? Truthfully. No one has ever asked you truthfully, I think."

Petra opened his mouth to give the answer he'd given the guild physician a thousand times. *Nothing. A slight bitterness. A mild warmth in the chest, gone in a minute.*

What came out instead was the truth, and it startled him more than the dragon had.

"Like I'm *more*," he said quietly. "For about a minute afterward. Like all the versions of me that might have died are standing just behind my shoulders. And then it fades, and I go back to being invisible, and I tell myself invisible is safe." He laughed — a small, cracked sound in the vast dark. "Why am I telling you this? I don't tell anyone anything. It's practically a religious discipline."

"Because I am the last creature alive who can hear what you actually are," Vessal said, "and some part of you has known that since you smelled the sulfur on the wind outside this mountain and walked in anyway." She drew a breath — long, laborious, the split-seam catch in it worse than before — and let it out, and the red glow of her guttered low, so low that for a horrible moment the whole cavern went nearly black and Petra thought, with a lurch of grief he had no business feeling yet, *oh, she's going now, right here, mid-sentence.*

Then the glow steadied. Dimmer than before. But steady.

"You felt that," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Yes."

"Yes. The others feel nothing. The hunters who passed my ridge last autumn felt nothing. They stood on my scorch-marks and felt nothing, and rode on to burn a farmstead whose only crime was a grandmother who could coax beans out of bad soil." The gold eye reflected him, small and upright in all that vastness. "But you knelt down in my dying light like a man kneeling at a bedside. Your blood answered mine. Do you know why? Have they told you nothing? Have you guessed nothing, in nine years of swallowing their quiet crimes?"

"I've guessed plenty," Petra said. "Guessing is free. Being right tends to be fatal, so I try not to finish the sums."

A sound came out of the dragon. It took Petra a moment to identify it, and when he did, he sat back on his heels, astonished. She was laughing. It was a low, rolling, ruined-bellows sort of laugh, and it raised dust from the cavern floor in little ghost-whorls, and it was, unmistakably, delighted.

"Ohh," she rumbled. "Oh, they picked their final ingredient well and never knew it. Finish the sums, little taster. Tonight. Because my fire is going out — you can see it, you can smell it, you who smell everything — and when it goes, it does not go alone."

"What does it take with it?" he asked. And then, because her silence had texture, weight, the specific gravity of a thing withheld: "Vessal. What does it take with it?"

Her name in his mouth surprised them both. He hadn't known it until he said it — it had simply arrived, the way her knowledge of his mother's prayers had arrived, out of some place where names were stored. The dragon went very still. Stillness, in something her size, was its own earthquake.

"You know my name," she said softly.

"It seems we're even," Petra said. His voice shook slightly. Everything about him was shaking slightly. "Shall we trade the rest? Because I'm sitting in a dead mine talking to a dying dragon, and I left sensible behind at the rockslide, and I find I'd rather have the truth than the dark."

The gold eye regarded him for a long moment. Outside, distantly, the fog pressed against the mountain, and somewhere in it, he did not doubt, Corvane's riders were making their own cold arithmetic. But that was tomorrow's poison. He had survived nine years by taking things one dose at a time.

"Then listen," Vessal said, and settled her head down until that ancient golden gaze was level with his own, close enough that he could see the fine cracks running through the iris like a glaze on old porcelain. "Listen, Petra Okafor, son of Adaeze, taster of poisons, keeper of a gift you did not choose and do not yet understand — listen, and I will tell you what the king has been drinking, and what your blood was grown for, and what dies when the last fire goes out of the world."

She drew in breath — the deepest yet, her whole vast body swelling, the red light rising with it — and the mountain leaned in to listen too.

"The gifts were never magic," Vessal said. "They were *inheritance*. And I am the last will and testament."

* * *

Far above, at the mouth of the mine, the fog thickened. And far below the fog, on the smuggler road that threaded the pass, a rider checked his horse and turned his hooded head toward the mountain, toward a scent on the wind that his masters had paid nine years to cultivate — and put his heels to the horse's flanks.

But inside the mountain, there was only the glow, and the story, and the two listeners: one who had waited four hundred years to be believed, and one who had waited nine years to stop being invisible.

Vessal began to speak, and the dark drank her words like water.
