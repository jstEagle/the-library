## 7. The Bargain of Breath

I had forgotten what it was to be smelled.

Not smelled as dragons are usually smelled — the sulfur-and-storm reek that sends villages bolting for their cellars — but smelled *closely*, intimately, the way one creature reads another's blood. The small one crouched at the edge of my collapsed mine with a torch guttering in his fist, and his scent told me everything before he said a word.

Fear, of course. Fear smells like wet copper and cold sweat. But beneath the fear: nightshade. Wolfsbane. Arsenic layered on arsenic like sediment in old stone. Bitterweed. Three compounds I did not recognize at all, which is its own kind of remarkable, because I am older than most poisons.

Nine years of swallowing death in measured spoonfuls, and here he stood, breathing, heart steady as a millwheel. A walking contradiction. A living ledger of every toxin the crown had ever slipped past his tongue.

And something else besides. Something faint and golden underneath all that poison, like a coin buried under river silt.

*Gift-blood,* I thought, and my old heart turned over in its ruined chest like a man rolling to face a wall he cannot climb.

"Vessal," I had said to him. His name for me. Not the Old Tongue name, not the burning title my kind once carried through the sky like banners. Just *Vessal*. Two syllables, worn smooth, the way a pebble gets worn by someone carrying it in a pocket for years.

He had known my name. No human living should have known it.

I let him stand there in the torchlight while I gathered myself. Gathering takes longer these days. There was a time — and I say this without vanity, or rather, with exactly as much vanity as the truth requires — when I could have unmade this mountain. Now I could barely make my voice carry past the fallen beams.

"You may come closer," I rumbled. "Or you may run. Most of your kind runs. It is a sensible instinct and I bear no grudge against it."

"I'm not your kind," he said. "I'm nobody's kind."

Ah. That I understood.

* * *

He came closer. Of course he came closer; his kind always does, the curious ones, the doomed ones, the ones who smell a secret the way sharks — no, forgive me, I have been listening to fisherfolk too long — the way moths smell a flame. He picked his way over the rubble with the careful feet of someone who has spent his life learning which floorboards betray him, and he stopped just beyond the reach of my breath, which was wise, because my breath these days could not light a candle but could still sour milk at forty paces.

"You're dying," he said. Not a question. Tasters learn to read bodies the way priests read scripture.

"I am," I agreed. "Have been for some decades now. Dying is slower work than your poets claim. Mostly it is waiting."

"For what?"

"For an ending, or a reason." I shifted, and the mine groaned around us, shedding grit from the ceiling. He flinched. I did not apologize. Dragons do not apologize for their size any more than mountains do. "You walked into a dying dragon's den with nothing but a torch and bad judgment. Ask your question properly."

His jaw worked. I watched him decide how much truth he could afford, which is a calculation I know well; I have made it ten thousand times across centuries.

"The tracks outside," he said finally. "They led here. And there were places in Thornfell where the earth was scorched in circles — old burns, but not lightning. Lightning doesn't spiral." He swallowed, and I tasted his fear sharpen into something finer. "The gifts are fading. Everyone says so. Hearth-witches who used to keep a whole village warm can barely light a candle now. My mother could—" He stopped himself. "People say the gift is dying out of the world."

"People say many things," I said. "Most of them while looking away from the true thing standing in front of them."

"And the true thing is you."

"The true thing is *fire*, little taster." I let the word sit between us and watched him go very still. Yes. I knew what he was. Let him chew on that. "Your gift, his gift, every gift in every bloodline of this realm — it is not yours. You did not make it. You do not generate it, any more than a lamp makes oil. You only burn what is given to you."

"Given by what?"

"By us." I drew a breath, and even guttered as I am, the darkness behind me seemed to lean toward the sound. "Dragon-fire is not heat, human. Heat is the least of it. Fire is the oldest current in the world, and when we flew — when we *burned* — that current ran through the land like water through roots. Your ancestors drank from those roots. Every gift-bearer since is a leaf on branches fed by dragon-fire. We kept the current moving. We kept your strange little talents alive the way a hearth keeps a kettle singing."

The torch trembled in his hand. "And now you're dying."

"Now we are dying. All of us. Hunted, or hollowed out, or simply fading like coals no one tends. And as we fade—" I gestured with one claw, a slow arc taking in the mountain, the valley, the whole gray breadth of Ironhollow, "—the gifts fade with us. The hearth-witch's fire gutters. The iron-hearer's songs go quiet. The king grows old and frightened and does desperate, monstrous things to stretch his years."

Something moved behind his eyes at that. Recognition. He had seen the desperation up close, I judged. He had tasted it, in fact, off a silver spoon.

"That's why you know my name," he said quietly. "You've been... watching? Listening? How does a dying dragon in a collapsed mine know what I am?"

"I did not always live in mines." The old grief rose in me then, vast and slow, like a whale surfacing. "There was a time when humans and dragons kept company. Properly. Knowingly. There were bonded pairs — a human and a dragon, two lives wound together, the fire flowing freely between them. Your gift-bearers were our friends, our partners, sometimes our family. And then your kings decided that a friend with wings was a rival for the throne, and the hunts began, and the bonds were broken one by one, and we went into the deep places to die alone." I looked at him. "But the old names linger. In songs. In stones. In blood. Yours carries echoes. When you spoke at the mouth of this cave, I heard a voice I knew in it. That is how I knew your name, Petra Okafor. It was given to me long ago by someone who loved a human very much, and humans repeat themselves more than they know."

He sat down. Right there, on a broken beam, in a dead dragon's parlor, as if his legs had simply resigned from the conversation. "Who?" he asked. "Who gave you my name?"

"A girl," I said. "Four hundred years ago. She was a hearth-keeper, and her line ran warm with fire-gift, and she used to bring me honey-cakes because she believed, quite sincerely, that dragons liked sweets." I paused. "We did. We do. It has been four hundred years since anyone offered me a honey-cake, and I want you to understand that this is among the great tragedies of my life."

A laugh escaped him — startled, cracked, real. It changed his whole face. Underneath the fear and the caution there was a person, young-ish, tired, wry, carrying grief the way he carried that vial in his pocket: close, always close.

"Honey-cakes," he repeated.

"Do not mock the honey-cakes. Mock the king. Mock the guild. Mock your own terrible survival instincts, which appear to consist entirely of running toward danger with a torch. But the honey-cakes were sacred."

* * *

He stayed the night. I pretend this did not please me.

He built a small fire — a real one, wood and flint, the ordinary kind — near the cave mouth, and shared out dried meat and hard cheese, and ate half before, with visible internal struggle, setting the rest on a flat stone and pushing it toward me.

"For the road," he said. "Or whatever. You probably eat whole cows."

"I used to eat whole cows. These days I would settle for a robust goat." I took the offering with great dignity. It was not honey-cake. I did not mention this. Some sacrifices must be borne silently.

While he chewed, I talked. That is the bargain I offered him, though neither of us called it that yet: talk, freely given. Truth, unmeasured. For nine years this man had been dosed and drained and documented without his knowledge or consent; the least the world owed him was information he did not have to steal.

So I told him about the bonding-stones, the great cracked hearts of mountain-crystal where human and dragon oaths were once sealed. I told him about the First Fading, when the last great flights ended and the gifts began their long dimming. I told him that his immunity — the poison-proofness the guild thought they had manufactured in him — was not manufactured at all. They had merely watered a seed that was already there. His body refused toxins because somewhere far back, his blood had learned refusal from fire itself.

"They think they made me," he said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man holding something heavy very level so it doesn't spill.

"They think they made bread because they baked the loaf. The wheat was never theirs." I let the silence do its work. "They have been cultivating you, Petra Okafor. Do you understand? Nine years of measured doses is not cruelty alone. It is *husbandry*. They are growing something in your veins, and when it ripens, they will harvest it."

"I figured out some of that." He stared into his small fire. "The vial I took from the wine. The new bitterness. I think they're preparing the final dose. I think I'm nearly ripe."

"Yes."

"And you? What do you get out of telling me all this? Dying dragons don't gossip for charity."

Direct. Good. I have never had patience for creatures who circle their questions like shy horses.

"What do I get," I mused. "Company, perhaps. Four hundred years is a long time to talk only to bats, and bats are dreadful listeners — all squeak and no follow-up questions. But if you want the honest answer: I am the last keeper of a fire that is going out, and I have been looking for someone worth passing it to. Not handing it over. Passing it. There is a difference. One is theft dressed in ceremony. The other is a choice, made twice — once by the giver, once by the receiver."

"Bonding," he said.

"Bonding," I agreed. "Though I warn you, the last ones who tried it were hunted to extinction, and I am currently the approximate size and vigor of a large sick cow, so the benefits package is less impressive than it once was."

He almost smiled. Then didn't. "And if I refuse?"

"Then you take your cheese and your vial and your excellent instincts over the pass tomorrow, and you survive, which is more than most managed, and I finish my waiting alone, which is how I expected it to go anyway." I settled my chin onto my forearms, joints crackling like green logs. "I am not in the business of trapping people, human. I watched a realm do enough of that."

He was quiet a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind worried at the scree slopes.

"My mother burned letters for me," he said at last, apropos of apparently nothing. "Before the proclamation. Her letters. She said paper remembers things, and remembering is dangerous now." He rubbed his face. "She was a hearth-keeper. Small gift, barely anything — her soup never went cold, that was all. But it was hers. And I watched the gift fade out of her over twenty years like a tide going out, and she pretended not to mind, and I pretended to believe her." His voice caught, steadied. "If what you're saying is true — that it faded because your fire is dying — then it wasn't age. It wasn't fate. Someone killed it. Something is killing it still."

"Yes."

"Then refusing isn't safety. Refusing is just dying slower, in a smaller room." He looked up at me, and his eyes in the firelight were the exact color of strong tea, and utterly unafraid for one bare moment. "I'm not saying yes, Vessal. I've been owned by institutions before. I'd like to know what I'd be choosing."

"*Choosing,*" I repeated, and the word tasted like the first rain after drought. "Do you know how long it has been since a human asked what choosing would involve, instead of asking what it would cost them, or what they could take?"

"What would it involve?"

"Not tonight." I closed my eyes. Even this much talking cost me; I could feel my fire flickering low, a candle cupped in a shaking hand. "Tonight you should sleep, and I should hoard my breath, and tomorrow you will decide whether to cross the pass alone or whether—"

The words stopped in my throat.

Because I felt it. Far off, down at the river ford, through stone and soil and the thin gold thread of my failing senses: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast. And riding ahead of them, carried on the wind like a foul perfume, the stink of cold iron and guild-oil and men who have been paid to find something and will not come home without it.

They had found the tracks. Of course they had found the tracks. A dragon leaves tracks the way a flood leaves mud.

"Petra," I said, opening my eyes.

He was already on his feet. Whatever else the guild had cultivated in him for nine years, it had not been calm — it had been *readiness*, the reflexes of a prey animal kept perpetually in season. He had heard nothing. But he read my face the way he read a suspicious stew.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

"We?" I echoed.

He was already kicking dirt over his little fire, already pocketing the vial, already moving toward the cave mouth with his head cocked like a bird's. "I haven't said yes," he said. "But nobody's hunting *you* to the ford for a polite chat, and I'm not leaving a four-hundred-year-old invalid to be carved up for elixir ingredients. That's not a bond. That's just not being a monster."

From somewhere deep in my ancient chest, beneath the ash and the ache, something that had not stirred in four hundred years produced a single spark of warmth.

"Ford," I said. "Riders. A dozen or more. Less than an hour."

He swore — fluently, creatively, with the vocabulary of a man raised around smugglers and kitchens — and then he looked back at me, and there it was again, that terrible, wonderful, doomed curiosity.

"Can you walk?"

"No," I said. "But I can fall downhill with tremendous authority, and if we reach the water, I know something about rivers that the guild does not."

"Then tell me on the way." He grabbed a burning branch from the embers for a torch, and held it up, and for a heartbeat he was silhouetted against the night like something out of the old murals — the small warm creature and the great dark one, moving together into danger.

It was not a yes. But it was not a no.

It was, I thought, hauling my ruined body up out of four hundred years of waiting, a beginning.
