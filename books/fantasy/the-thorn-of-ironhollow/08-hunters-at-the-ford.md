## 8. Hunters at the Ford

The river called Greywater for reasons that became obvious the moment you looked at it.

Petra had crossed it eleven times in his smuggling days, and every one of those crossings had involved either a bribe, a lie, or a very cold swim. Now, with an ancient dragon limping at his shoulder like a grandmother who insisted she didn't need help up the stairs, he was hoping for option one or two. He was running low on both.

"Your river smells wrong," Vessal said.

"It always smells wrong. That's why it's useful." He crouched at the treeline and studied the ford below—a wide, shallow sprawl of grey stones where the water spread itself thin and lazy, as if it couldn't be bothered to hurry anywhere. On the far bank, the road climbed toward the Thornfell passes. Behind them, somewhere in the fog-choked ravines they'd spent two days crawling through, were riders.

"You said this crossing is watched."

"I said it's *watchable*. There's a difference. A watched ford has men on it. A watchable ford just means I'd have posted men on it, if I were the sort of person who employed men." He chewed the inside of his cheek. "Which Corvane is."

Vessal lowered her great head beside him, close enough that her breath stirred his hair. It smelled of ash and old coals and, underneath, something like rain on hot stone—the ghost of fire. Two days ago her flame had been a candle in a drafty room. Yesterday it had been a match struck and immediately dropped. She hadn't breathed so much as a spark since morning, and when she tried, the sound she made was less a roar than a cough.

"How many?" she asked.

He scanned the far bank. The willows there grew thick and yellow-green, drooping into the water. The road beyond ran straight for half a mile before bending—good sightlines, bad cover. And there, at the bend, a glint. Not armor. Armor glinted constantly; amateurs wore it proudly. This was a single flash, brief as a coin flipped. A spyglass, maybe. Or a helmet someone had forgotten to keep still.

"At least four," he said. "Probably six. They'll have the crossing covered and a rider watching upstream in case we get clever."

"We are not clever?"

"Oh, we're extremely clever. That's exactly why they'll have a man upstream."

* * *

They retreated into the pines to argue about it, which is what Petra now thought of their conversations: arguments conducted in whispers between two creatures with nothing in common except a mutual interest in not dying.

The problem was simple and horrible. Vessal could no longer fly. Her wings worked, technically, the way a rusted gate works—you could move it if you hated it enough—but the muscles along her spine had gone slack as old rope, and twice yesterday she had stumbled on flat ground, catching herself with a sound like a felled tree. She could swim, probably. Dragons swam. But swimming required entering the water, and entering the water meant being visible from the far bank for the length of the crossing, which was forty yards of nothing to hide behind, and dragon-hide was not subtle even dimmed and mud-caked.

"You should go without me," Vessal said, for the fourth time.

"And you should stop saying that, because my answer hasn't changed."

"Your answer is sentimental."

"My answer is tactical," Petra snapped, then winced, because it wasn't, entirely. "Also sentimental. Both things can be true. I'm a complicated man."

Her eye—large as a dinner plate, gold going grey at the rim—regarded him with an expression he was beginning to learn. It was her unimpressed expression. She had several. This one meant she thought he was lying to himself but found it mildly entertaining.

"Say your plan, taster."

"I'm still building it."

"You have been 'building' it since noon."

"Some plans are slow-cooked." He pulled a stick from the ground and started drawing in the pine duff. The ford. The banks. The willows. "Here's what we know. Corvane's riders are guild men, which means they follow guild procedure, which means they've read the same manuals I have. Standard ford interdiction: two archers on the far bank under the willows, one outrider upstream, one downstream, rest held back as reserve. They don't rush. They wait. They know we have to cross eventually, and they think patience favors them."

"Does it not?"

"It would," Petra said, "if the river were behaving honestly."

Vessal's nostrils flared. "You are going to make it misbehave."

"I'm going to *ask* it to misbehave. Politely. With engineering." He drew a line above the ford, upstream. "Half a mile up there, the river runs through a gorge. Narrow walls, deep channel. In my runner days, we kept a cache near there—rope, oil, tools. And more importantly—" he tapped the gorge "—there's an old logjam. Beavers started it years ago, and smugglers improved it. When the tax collectors got too curious about a certain warehouse downstream, we'd cut the jam loose and the Greywater would rise three feet through the ford in about ten minutes. Nobody drowned. Everybody got wet. Very discouraging for people standing *in* the ford."

"You built a weapon out of a river and beavers."

"The beavers built most of it. We were consultants." He sat back. "Problem is, cutting the jam takes time, and I can't cut it and be here when it comes down. So the timing has to be exact. Which means I need you to do something terrifying."

"I am a dragon. Little terrifies me."

"You'll be standing in the open water looking helpless."

A pause. "That does not terrify me. It offends me deeply, however."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

Dusk came down blue and cold, and the fog off the river thickened until the world shrank to twenty yards of grey in every direction. Good. Fog was a friend to the desperate.

Petra walked Vessal down to the water's edge, murmuring nonsense to her the way you'd soothe a horse, though the horse-sized comparison ended abruptly past her shoulder. At the cache site he'd found most of what he remembered: a coil of hemp gone soft but serviceable, a hatchet with a handle wrapped in oiled leather, and a clay jar of lamp-oil that had somehow survived nine years of squirrels. He'd also taken the rope up to the gorge alone, in the last light, and done the work that made his hands shake—not from fear of the work, but because he knew what came after it.

Now he stood in the shallows with Vessal looming over him and gave her the instructions one final time.

"You wade out to the middle. Slowly. You look exhausted."

"I am exhausted."

"Perfect, lean into it. Don't perform it. Just be a big sad dying thing in a river, which, no offense—"

"None taken. It is accurate."

"—and let them come to you. Archers won't shoot unless ordered; guild procedure says gift-bearers are to be taken alive. *You* especially. You're worth more than the whole eastern province to Corvane, and he knows it. So they'll wade in with nets and hooks, feeling clever, and they'll all be standing in the ford when the river stands up."

"And you will be upstream, playing with beaver architecture."

"With beaver architecture," he agreed. "When the water comes, don't fight it. Let it push you toward the south bank—downstream, away from the reserve riders. Get to the willows on the far side and stay down. I'll meet you there."

"And if the timing fails," Vessal said, "if the water comes early or late—"

"Then I improvise. Improvising is also in the manuals. Chapter twelve. Mostly it says 'don't.'"

She made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could rumble through bedrock. Then she turned and waded out, and Petra watched the grey water climb her flanks and felt something tighten in his chest—not fear for himself, for once. Fear for her. Nine hundred years, Tam's stories said the dragons had lived. Nine hundred years, and here she was, bait in a river for men with nets.

*Don't think about it. Think about knots.*

* * *

Upstream, the gorge swallowed the sky. Petra moved through it by memory, hands finding rock holds his body remembered better than his mind did. At the logjam he knelt in the dark and did the last of the arithmetic.

The jam was a mess of pine trunks wedged between the gorge walls, silted and rooted, holding back years of debris. He'd tied the release line an hour ago: a main rope around the keystone log, rigged through a friction hitch so that one hard pull, with weight behind it, would walk the knot loose and drop the whole structure. But rope alone wouldn't do it—the jam had settled too well. Hence the oil. He'd soaked the heart of it, and now he struck flint to steel, three times, five, cursing quietly, until a bloom of orange caught and began to eat into the dark.

Fire in a dry season was a crime against everything. Fire at the base of a waterlogged logjam in autumn fog was merely ambitious. It caught slowly, sullenly, smoking more than burning, exactly as intended. Smoke would drift downstream and over the ford. Smoke would read, to nervous men in the dark, like signal fire. Like ambush.

Good. Nervous men bunch together. Bunched men stand in rivers.

He checked the moon. An hour past setting. Time to go.

* * *

From his perch on the gorge lip, Petra could see almost nothing of the ford, which was the point—he wasn't supposed to see it, he was supposed to trust it, and trusting things was not among his talents. He counted in his head instead. Vessal should be wading out now. The pickets should have seen her. The shout would go up—

It did. Thin across the fog, a voice cracked with the particular panic of a sentry who has just seen nine hundred years of legend emerge from a riverbank. More voices. A horn, two notes, guild signal for *target sighted, hold position*. Then torchlight bloomed along the far bank, and through the fog-glow Petra saw shapes moving onto the stones of the ford. Four. Five. Six, wading in, torches high, nets coiled on shoulders. Somewhere a sergeant was bellowing about taking it alive, careful, careful, it's worth more than your pensions—

And then the smoke arrived.

It rolled down the gorge and out over the water like weather, acrid and sudden, and the torches became smudges, and the voices rose into that specific confusion Petra had heard once before, years ago, when a rival crew tried to raid a warehouse during a fog and discovered that fog plus panic equals everyone fighting their own friends. Men bunched together, as nervous men do. Bunched men stood in rivers.

Petra took the release rope in both hands, braced his boots against stone, and pulled with everything his slight body had, throwing his whole weight backward, again, again—and the knot walked, and groaned, and the keystone log shifted with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

The river stood up.

It came down the gorge as a brown wall, not tall—three feet, maybe a little more—but *fast*, carrying ten years of collected logs and silt and beaver ambition, and it hit the ford like a fist. Petra heard it before anything else: a roar that swallowed the shouts, the horn, everything. Torchlights vanished all at once, snuffed or swept away. Through the fog he glimpsed chaos in flashes—one rider's horse screaming and rearing, a netted shape tumbling, a man clawing at a willow branch as the current took his legs. Vessal, magnificent, terrible, *committed to the role*, let herself be slammed sideways and rolled with it, sweeping downstream toward the south bank in a churning wave of grey water and grey hide, gone into the willows.

One figure did not go with the flood.

The sergeant—or what Petra guessed was the sergeant—had been nearest the north bank, and when the wall hit he'd caught a boulder, hauled himself half out of the current, and now clung there in the churn with a hooked netting-pole still strapped across his back. Even from the gorge lip, even through fog, Petra could see him fighting toward shore with the grim competence of a man who had done this before. And Petra could see, with the cold clarity that sometimes visited him in bad moments, exactly what happened next. The man would reach the bank. He would regroup with the reserve riders downstream. He would report a flooded ford, a dragon on the south side, and a smuggler's rig upstream—which meant Corvane would know every cache, every trick, every route Petra had ever used, because the guild had taught him all of it and could therefore anticipate all of it.

Unless the sergeant reported nothing.

Petra was already moving before the thought finished forming, sliding down the scree into the gorge, hatchet in hand, telling himself the lie he suspected every soldier tells: *I'll just stop him. I won't hurt him. I'll tie him up.* The current shoved him downstream alongside the boulder, and the sergeant's face appeared out of the fog—ordinary, thirtyish, terrified, a farm boy's jaw under a guild helm—and for one hanging moment they simply looked at each other, two men in a river, equally astonished to be there.

Then the man's hand went to the whistle at his collar.

Later, Petra would try to remember making the decision, and fail. There was no decision. There was only the hatchet coming around, heavy and wet and faster than mercy, and the impact jarring up his arm, and the sergeant's eyes going from terror to something else, something surprised and far away, and the river taking him gently, almost politely, spinning him once and folding him under.

The whistle sank with him. It never sounded.

* * *

Petra did not remember climbing out of the gorge. He found himself walking along the south bank with wet hands and an empty grip, and the hatchet was gone, lost somewhere in the flood, and he supposed that was fine, he supposed he never wanted to hold it again anyway.

Vessal found him before he found the willows. She came out of the fog like a hill deciding to relocate, and folded herself down beside him on the shingle, and said nothing, which was the correct thing, because he could not have spoken yet. The river rushed past them both, high and triumphant and indifferent.

"He had a whistle," Petra managed finally. His voice came out strange, flattened. "If he'd blown it, the reserve riders would have known everything. Every route. They'd have been ahead of us at every pass. It was—it was the right call. Tactically it was the right call."

"Yes," Vessal said.

"He had a farm boy's face."

"They often do," Vessal said, "on both sides. The ones who hunt and the ones who run." She lowered her head until her breath warmed his shoulder. "I have killed, taster. Wars' worth. I will not insult you by saying it becomes easy. It becomes *heavier*, if you do it rightly. That weight is not a wound. It is tuition. You are paying it now so that some future version of you makes fewer such calls, or makes them better, or knows exactly what they cost when he must make them anyway."

"Guilt as tuition," Petra said. "The guild never charged me this much."

"The guild charged you in blood. At least mine bills honestly." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone she used perhaps once a day: "You saved my life tonight. A dying dragon, standing helpless in a river, and a small quick man with a smuggler's river-trick. I have not been saved by anything smaller than a mountain in a very long time."

"You're welcome," Petra said, and then, because the guilt needed somewhere to go and humor was the only drain he trusted: "Although technically the beavers deserve most of the credit. I was consulting."

Something rumbled through her, faint but unmistakable—the ghost of a laugh. And then her head came up sharply, nostrils wide, and all the warmth went out of the moment at once.

"What is it?"

"Smoke," she said. "Not ours. Not theirs." Her grey-gold eye fixed on the dark mass of the Thornfell foothills, where the fog was thinning toward dawn. "Woodsmoke. Cook-fires. Many of them, banked low, hidden in a valley east of here." A pause. "And under the woodsmoke, taster—I smell herbs. Hearth-magic. Old, practiced, frightened."

Petra followed her gaze. People. Hidden people, in the hills ahead of them—hidden the way he had hidden for nine years.

Or hidden the way people hide when the Hunt is already circling them.

"We should avoid it," he said. It sounded weak even to him.

"We should," Vessal agreed, neither of them moving.
