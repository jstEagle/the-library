## 9. Ironhollow's Orphans

The village announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and bread and, underneath it all, the green mineral breath of the river. Petra came down out of the tree line at dusk with Vessal's presence a low ache behind his ribs—the bond was still new enough that he wasn't sure where her tiredness ended and his began—and stopped at the edge of the first barley field like a man at the edge of deep water.

He had been in villages before. He had eaten in villages, drunk in villages, tasted for lords who owned villages down to the nails in their doors. He knew exactly how invisible he could be in one: less than a day. Villages had eyes like hawks and memories like elephants, and a stranger with a scarred throat and no trade was a story by morning.

But Tam was asleep against his back, boneless with exhaustion, his small hands locked in Petra's collar even in sleep. The boy hadn't let go since the ford. Since the flooding water took the riders. Since Petra had—his mind slid away from it, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth—since the first kill.

"You can't stand there until spring," said a voice.

Petra turned. A woman stood at the field's edge with a lantern held low, so its light fell on the barley and not on any of their faces. She was sixty or so, built like a fence post that had decided to be stubborn about weather, with iron-gray hair braided crownwise and flour to the elbows. Her eyes went to Tam, then to Petra's face, then did something he hadn't seen anyone's eyes do in nine years: they softened.

"He's iron-touched," she said quietly. "I can tell from here. They hum around him." She lifted the lantern a fraction, letting light climb her own face on purpose—a gift of trust. "I'm Maren. I bake bread and I keep the hearth and I know what you're carrying, taster, because I can smell poison on a person the way other folk smell rain coming."

Petra's hand moved toward his knife before his manners caught up with it.

"Ah," said Maren. "There it is. Well. Come inside before you make it worse, and put the boy down, he weighs nothing and you've been carrying him wrong for miles—I can see it in your shoulders."

* * *

Ironhollow sat in a fold of hills like a coin dropped in a purse: thirty-one roofs, a mill, a shrine to a god whose name had been worn off the lintel, and a single street that wandered instead of going anywhere, as if the village had never quite agreed on what it wanted to be when it grew up. Maren's house was the largest after the mill house, stone below, timber above, and it smelled—Petra catalogued it automatically, the old habit—of yeast, rosemary, tallow, dried nettle, and something faintly metallic that made his teeth ache in a way he couldn't name.

Maren fed them barley soup without asking questions, which Petra understood immediately as a professional courtesy among people who lived by not asking questions. She cut thick slices from a dark loaf, buttered them without being asked, and set the plate in front of Tam, who looked at it, then at Petra, then ate with the desperate politeness of a child who has learned food can be taken away.

"There's a loft," Maren said. "It's cold but it's dry, and the roof doesn't leak over the bed, only over the place nobody sleeps. You'll have it until—" She paused, ladle hovering. "Until," she finished, in a tone that suggested she had decided not to finish the sentence ever.

"Until we're gone," Petra said. "Three days. Four. We'll take nothing but what we brought."

"Mm," said Maren, and gave him more soup.

That night, in the loft, Tam slept in an actual bed for the first time since the ford, and Petra lay awake listening to the house settle and counting exits the way other men counted sheep. Front door. Kitchen door. The window over the woodshed with the loose shutter. Old habits, laid down like bricks over nine years of swallowing death by measured spoons.

Around midnight, the floorboards below creaked, and he heard Tam sit up in the dark and whisper, wonderingly: "The pots are singing."

And faintly, from the kitchen below, so faintly Petra would have sworn he imagined it if the boy's face hadn't been glowing with reflected joy—they were.

* * *

In the morning, Ironhollow pretended very hard not to notice them, which was how Petra learned the whole village already knew everything.

A woman drawing water nodded at him and said, "You'll want the far well, love, the near one's got a frog in it this week," which was either hospitality or a warning delivered in code, and with Maren's people it was impossible to say. Two children followed him and Tam for half a street at a distance of exactly eleven paces, whispering, until Tam turned around and said, "Your bucket hoop is going to crack, the left side," and the children fled shrieking with delight.

"They think he's marvelous," Maren said, appearing beside Petra with the inevitability of weather. "Nothing interesting happens here except births and the mill pond freezing, and now we've got a boy who talks to iron and a stranger with a swordsman's walk pretending to be a clerk. It's better than the midwinter mummers."

"I'm not pretending to be a clerk."

"You told Widow Anselm you were a clerk."

"I told her I worked with documents."

"You told her your hands were stained with ink." Maren looked pointedly at his hands, which were stained with charcoal from the maps he burned two days ago, and raised one gray eyebrow with the precision of a fencer's lunge.

"The ash," said Petra, "was very fine."

"Mm." She handed him a basket. "If you're going to be a liar in my village, you can be a useful one. Bread wants delivering to the miller and the shrine-keeper, and you've got legs, and Tam wants to come, and don't let him near the mill race because he'll start telling the wheel its axle is worn and the wheel gets sulky when he's right."

"The wheel gets sulky."

"You'd be sulky too, if someone spent forty years telling you your axle was worn."

* * *

So began the days that Petra would later count as the strangest of his life—not because anything happened in them, but because nothing did.

He delivered bread. He mended the shutter over the loft window, which had been loose, Maren admitted, since the flood year, because every man she'd asked to fix it had gotten distracted. He learned that the shrine-keeper, Doss, was deaf in one ear and compensated by shouting at everyone equally, which Petra found restful. He learned that the miller's wife won at cards by cheating so transparently it was practically a form of honesty. He learned that Ironhollow's entire militia was four men and a dog, and that the dog was considered the most reliable of the five.

And everywhere, quietly, gifts.

Not blazing ones. Small ones, banked like coals, hidden the way his own had been hidden. The ferryman who never missed a day of good weather, because he asked the river politely. The girl of ten whose cuts healed overnight if her mother sang to her. Old Anselm, the widow, who dreamed true twice a season and had learned to keep her mouth shut about both times. Nobody spoke of it directly. It lived under the village the way water lives under a millstone, and Petra understood within three days that Ironhollow was not an ordinary village that happened to contain gifted people—it was a place where the hidden had drifted together for generations, the way iron filings drift toward a lodestone, and had agreed, wordlessly, across decades, to be dull together.

"It's why the king's criers never stop here," Maren said on the fourth evening, shelling beans into a bowl while Tam did his homework—that is, lay on the floor with his ear pressed to the poker, reporting its opinions. "No silver to be had from us, because nobody sells anybody. Not because we're saints. Because the first person who sold somebody would have to look the rest of us in the eye at market forever, and that's a worse fate than hanging, in a village this size." She snapped a bean. "Also we'd drown them in the mill pond. But mostly the looking-in-the-eye thing."

"That's supposed to reassure me?"

"No, love. It's supposed to explain why you slept eight hours last night for the first time since you walked through my door. Don't argue. I heard you not having nightmares. Very loud, not having nightmares."

Tam laughed from the floor, and the poker rang softly, like a struck glass, and Petra felt something in his chest do a thing he refused to name.

* * *

On the fifth day, Vessal.

He had gone up each dawn to the scree hollow where she lay, bringing what meat the village could spare without noticing its absence—Maren noticed, of course; Maren noticed everything, but she only raised the eyebrow once and then started setting aside the tough ends of the mutton without comment, which was its own kind of sentence he chose not to finish either.

Vessal was worse. He could feel it through the bond before he saw it: her fire guttering like a candle in a doorway, her thoughts slower, grander distances between them, as if her mind were a country and winter were crossing it border by border. But when he scrambled down into the hollow with the wrapped mutton, she lifted her head, and one vast amber eye fixed on him with an attention that made the morning seem suddenly thin.

*You smell different,* she said. Her voice in his skull was like a bell heard through a hillside. *Less afraid. Why are you less afraid?*

"I'm sleeping," Petra said. "There's a village."

*A village.* She pronounced it the way a queen might pronounce *a hovel*. *Of the soft creatures.*

"They've been feeding you. Indirectly."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—if dragons could be said to have mouths' corners; the effect was geological, and terrifying, and unmistakably a smile. *Then I amend my judgment. Slightly. Eat, little taster. You carry enough deaths in your blood. Do not add hunger to the list.*

He sat against her foreleg, which radiated heat like a dying hearth—he tried not to think *dying*—and told her about the village. About Tam teaching the mill wheel self-respect. About Maren. About the ferryman and the singing mother and the widow who dreamed true. He didn't know why he told her. There was no tactical value. But the words came out of him like water finding a downhill grade, and Vessal listened the way mountains listen, and when he finished she was quiet a long moment.

*Once,* she said, *there were places like this everywhere. Where our fire warmed their blood, and their blood remembered our fire. Bonded lines. Whole valleys of them.* Her eye dimmed, some inner lamp turning down. *I forgot, these last centuries, what it looked like. Thank you for reminding me what I am dying of.*

"Don't say that."

*I am a dragon, little taster. We say true things. It's one of our few virtues and I refuse to abandon it in my dotage.* But her wing shifted, very gently, until it sheltered him from the wind, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

On the sixth evening, Maren taught him to bake.

This was not presented as a kindness. It was presented as a labor dispute. "Doss broke his wrist falling off his own roof—don't ask, he was chasing a cat that owed him money—and the shrine bread won't bake itself, and you've been eating my loaves for six days like a tax collector, so. Apron."

"I'm a fugitive," Petra said.

"You're a fugitive with clean hands. Apron."

And so, in the blue hour before dawn, in a kitchen warm as a held breath, Petra Okafor—who could detect seven poisons by scent, who had swallowed death in measured doses for nine years, who had flooded a ford and killed a man and bonded with a dragon—learned that he could not tell when dough had been kneaded enough.

"It's ready when it stops fighting you," Maren said.

"It isn't fighting me. It's just... resistant."

"So's a mule, love, and you get a mule to move by knowing when to lean and when to let go. Dough's the same." She watched his hands a moment, then said, lighter than the words deserved: "You taste things for a living, yes? Then taste this." She handed him a scrap of raw dough. "Tell me what's missing."

He chewed. Considered. "Salt. And..." He frowned. "Something bitter. A herb? Just barely."

"Aldergreen. Wild, grows along the north wall. Bitter keeps the loaf honest—sweet bread lies to you, tells you it'll keep longer than it will." She looked at him sideways, flour-dusted, firelit. "Your mother baked."

It wasn't a question, and Petra's hands went still in the dough.

"How—"

"You knead like someone who learned standing on a stool too small to reach the table properly, watching someone else's hands. That's not guild-trained. Guild training sits in the shoulders." Maren turned back to her own loaf, giving him the mercy of not being watched. "My mother baked too. Worst bread in three parishes. I keep her technique alive out of spite."

Petra laughed—an actual laugh, unguarded, startling them both—and the sound hung in the warm kitchen like the aldergreen in the dough: bitter, and keeping it honest.

That was the moment. He knew it even as it happened. Standing in a stranger's kitchen with dough to his wrists, laughing about mothers, while a hunted boy slept upstairs and a dying dragon slept on a mountain and a guildmaster somewhere sharpened his ledgers into knives. That was the moment the floor of his careful life stopped being a floor and became a home, which is a much more dangerous thing to stand on, because you can survive the floor dropping out from under you. Everyone knows how to fall. Almost no one knows how to stay.

* * *

The seventh day was market day, and Petra almost belonged.

He carried baskets. He argued cheerfully with the miller about the price of bran and lost, because the miller cheated everyone including her husband and it was somehow part of the festival of it. He bought honey he didn't need because Tam had never had honey on bread and Petra had discovered, to his private horror, that he wanted to be the one to give it to him. Doss shouted compliments at his shrine-bread from a distance of four feet. The ferryman tipped his cap. Somebody's baby grabbed his finger and declined to release it for the length of an entire conversation, and Petra stood there, hostage, feeling his heart do the unnamed thing again.

Almost belonged. The *almost* was the whole trick of it, and he knew that too. Belonging was a debt, and debts came due.

He was walking home past the shrine in the long gold light, Tam ahead of him walking the fence rail with his arms out because the fence nails were "singing happy today," when Tam stopped. Mid-rail. Perfectly still, the way he went still when the metal told him something bad.

"What?" Petra said.

Tam's head tilted. His eyes had gone distant, listening to something under the world. "Horseshoes," he said. "Up on the ridge road. A lot of them. And—" the boy swallowed, and his voice came out small, "—they're not moving like farmers, Petra. Farmers let horses pick their feet. These ones are making them march."

Behind them, in the west, the sun was going down over Thornfell, gilding the barley, warming the kitchen chimney where Maren was already banking her fire for the night.

Six riders. Maybe more. Coming down toward Ironhollow with the dusk.

Petra took Tam's hand and began, very calmly, to run.
