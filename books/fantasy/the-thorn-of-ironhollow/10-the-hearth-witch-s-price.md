## 10. The Hearth-Witch's Price

Maren Ashfoot knew every fire in Ironhollow the way a shepherd knows her sheep. The fat crackling one in the bakery, greedy and sweet-tempered. The sullen peat-smoke in old Doss's cottage that needed coaxing like a sulking child. The thin blue thread in Widow Pell's hearth that had burned on its own for three days after she died, as if reluctant to let her go.

And so, when a stranger walked into her village with something *wrong* flickering under his skin — not fire, exactly, but the memory of fire, banked down small and desperate — her fires told her about it before his boots ever touched her doorstep.

"He's here," she said to the kettle, and the kettle, being a sensible object, merely steamed.

* * *

She found him at the mill pond, of all places, crouched at its edge with the dragon-boy Tam beside him, both of them washing river mud off their hands like a pair of truant schoolboys. Maren had watched them come in two nights past, half-dead and limping, and had made the very deliberate choice to look away. Looking away was a discipline. She'd practiced it for years, since the soldiers took Briony, and she was very good at it.

But good wasn't perfect, and the thing this man carried was loud.

"You," she said.

The tall one — Okafor, the miller's wife said his name was, though nobody believed it was his real name any more than anyone believed the miller's wife's pies were her own recipe — straightened up slowly, the way men do when they've learned that sudden movements attract attention.

"Ma'am."

"Don't *ma'am* me. I've buried three husbands and outlived two tax collectors, and I can smell a lie the way you smell rain coming." She planted herself in front of him. Up close he had the kind of face that worked hard at being forgettable: brown eyes, brown hair, features arranged with an almost professional blandness. It was a good disguise. It would have worked on anyone whose laundry hadn't spent nine years whispering to her about poisons.

Because that was the other thing. Her gift was hearth-craft, humble as bread — fire and warmth and the small domestic magics that kept a village alive. But everything that passed through heat left its signature with her, and this man's clothes had been washed in water heated over flames that had touched *poison*. Not once. Dozens of times. Hundreds. Arsenic and belladonna and things with names from guild manuals she'd only seen once, years ago, before she stopped letting strangers see too much of her.

Nobody washed that many poisons out of their sleeves by accident.

"You're going to tell me who you are," Maren said, "and then you're going to leave."

"I'm Petra." He said it easily enough, but she saw the calculation behind it, quick as a card sharp. "We're just passing through. We'll be gone by week's end."

"Week's end." She laughed, short and bitter. "Man, do you know what week's end looks like from where I'm standing? Week's end is when the baker's boy finishes counting his silver and decides your name is worth more than his conscience. Week's end is when somebody's cousin in Lowmark mentions riders asking after a taster and a dragon, and somebody else remembers strangers came through with burn scars and no luggage. You've been here two nights. By week's end you'll have roots."

Tam, at the pond's edge, went very still. The boy did that — went still the way iron goes still, all weight and listening. He'd been doing it since they arrived, tilting his head at fences and horseshoes and the nails in Maren's own doorframe, hearing things in the metal that Maren chose not to ask about. Asking invited answers, and answers were dangerous.

"Where would we even go?" Petra asked, and there it was — the first honest note in his voice, worn smooth by use but still true underneath. "The passes are closed. The lowland roads are crawling. There's nowhere—"

"There's *away*," Maren snapped. "Away exists. Away has always existed. That's the whole trick of staying alive with a gift in Ironhollow: you keep moving toward away."

"And how long does that work?" His voice didn't rise, which somehow made it worse. "How many years? How many villages? Because I've done the math, mistress witch. Nine years of it. Invisible, careful, quiet as a held breath. And it lasted right up until the day it didn't."

Maren's hands closed around each other, knuckles whitening. Behind her, the mill pond lay flat and grey as hammered tin, and the village beyond it sent up its little ribbons of chimney smoke, each one a heartbeat she knew by name.

"You think I don't know what it costs?" she said quietly. "I've been paying that price longer than you've been alive."

* * *

She fed them first, because she was angry, not stupid, and because anger sits better on a full stomach — hers as much as theirs. Stew, dark bread, the last of the autumn apples baked soft with honey. Her hearth did most of the work while she pretended to cook, coaxing the pot to simmer faster than any pot had a right to, warming the bread with a thought. Let them think she was merely generous. Generous women weren't watched.

Petra ate like a man cataloguing ingredients, which told her more than his name had. Tam ate like a boy, which nearly broke her heart, and she had to turn to the fire and busy her hands with nothing for a moment until her face behaved.

"My daughter had a gift," Maren said, without deciding to say it. The words simply left, the way sparks jump a grate.

Petra set down his spoon.

"She could make plants grow. Nothing grand — no orchards overnight, no roses in January. But she could put her hands in sick soil and it would remember how to be healthy. The barley came up thick around her. Weeds grew *away* from her, polite as courtiers." Maren smiled at the memory, and hated how much the smile cost. "Briony. Named for the bramble, because she was sweet fruit on a thorny stem and I was fool enough to say so out loud."

"What happened to her?"

"The Hunt. Eleven years ago now. Before Aldric dressed it up in proclamations and silver, when it was just the guild's men riding circuit with their ledgers and their little brass scales." Her voice stayed level. Practice will do that. "A neighbor sold her name for six crowns. Six. I've bought worse pigs for more. They came at dawn, and I—" The fire popped. She gripped the poker. "I burned their wagon. Both horses. Two men's eyebrows and one man's entire beard, which I regret less than I should. And it changed nothing. They rode out with my daughter anyway, and I stood in my own doorway and discovered that a woman who can throw fire is precisely as powerless as a woman who cannot, if the men holding the chains don't care what burns."

The kitchen was silent except for the stew.

"So don't sit at my table," Maren said, "and tell me about the math of running. I did the math eleven years ago. I ran the numbers every way they could go, and every road ended with Briony in a cage or Maren in a grave, and I chose to stay and bury my gift so deep under soup kettles and warming pans that even I forget it's there some days. That's the price of belonging somewhere, taster-man. You stop being what you are so the place can keep you."

"I'm not a taster anymore."

"No," she agreed. "You're something worse. You're a taster with a dragon sleeping in the quarry cave above my village, and a boy who hears iron singing, and poison soaked into your blood like dye into wool. You're a bonfire wearing a beggar's cloak, and every day you stand in my village the wind gets a little closer to finding you."

* * *

He didn't argue. That was the maddening part. She'd prepared arguments all afternoon while pretending to mend a basket — she'd built whole rhetorical fortifications — and he simply sat there across her table, turning his cup slowly, absorbing her fury the way sand absorbs rain, and when she finally ran out of breath he said:

"Show me."

"Show you what?"

"How you hide it. The burying." He leaned forward, and for the first time she saw past the practiced blandness to the man underneath — tired, precise, and desperately hungry for exactly one thing. "Nine years I survived by dosing myself and keeping count. Method. Ledgers in my head. I never met anyone who hid a gift by *choice*, in a village, in plain sight, for over a decade, without going mad. Teach me how you did it, and I'll take Tam and go."

"And the dragon?"

The pause was very small, but Maren had spent a lifetime reading the silences around fire, and she read that one perfectly.

"She can't fly far," Petra said finally. "Her wing — she was hurt before I found her. And she's..." He looked at his hands. "She's dying, Mistress Ashfoot. Slowly. Like a fire settling into ash. I don't know how long she has. Weeks, maybe. So yes, I'll take the boy and go, but I won't drag a dying dragon over a mountain pass to die alone in the snow, and I won't pretend that leaving her behind is anything other than what it is."

*Leave her behind.* As if Vessal were luggage. Maren felt, absurdly, a flash of sympathy for the dragon, wherever she lay in the dark of the old quarry. Imagine surviving three hundred years of kings and hunters only to be discussed in the conditional tense by a man who couldn't decide which direction to run.

"You love her," Maren said. "Already. After a fortnight."

"I owe her my life."

"That's not what I asked."

Petra's jaw tightened. "I owe her my life, and she owes me hers, and where I come from that adds up to more than love does. Love is what people say when they want something. Debt is what you can actually pay."

"Mm." Maren rose and stirred the stew she didn't need to stir. "My third husband said something similar. About debts." She let that hang. "He was wrong, for the record. But he was also dead within the year, so his opinion lost some standing."

That startled a laugh out of Tam — a real one, rusty, the first sound she'd heard from him that belonged to a boy instead of a survivor. Even Petra's mouth twitched before he mastered it.

*There,* Maren thought, and hated herself a little. *That's how it starts. One laugh at your table. One bowl of your stew. One night they're too tired to move on, and then another, and suddenly they're not strangers passing through, they're yours, and you're standing in a doorway again watching chains.*

* * *

She walked them out at dusk, because the evening air was sharp and because she wanted to see, one more time, exactly what she was asking them to carry out of her village. The sky over Thornfell was going violet and iron-grey, and the first stars pricked through above the ridge where the quarry cave held its dying ember of a dragon.

They stood in her garden, among the bean rows Briony had once coaxed into bearing a month early, and Maren said what she had come to say.

"I'm asking you to leave. Tomorrow. Before dawn. Take the north sheep track — it doesn't touch the ford, and the ford's the first place riders would look. I'll pack you food and I'll ward the track against scrying, such as my small craft allows, and I will never speak your names to a living soul." She heard her own voice shake and steadied it. "That is what love looks like, in a village like this one. It looks like telling people to go."

"I know." Petra's voice was gentle, and gentleness was somehow harder to refuse than argument. "I know that's what it costs you. And I am sorry — truly — for the wind I'm carrying into your fields." He looked back at the lit windows of her cottage, at Tam's silhouette already inside, already hanging his threadbare coat on her pegs like it lived there. "But I'm not taking him over a winter pass to die of cold so that your village can be safe for another season. And I'm not leaving Vessal. So either I go alone and the boy stays under your roof, or we all stay and I spend every hour making us harder to find. Those are the choices. I've done the math."

"You arrogant, impossible—"

"Yes," he agreed mildly. "All of that. But I'm right about the pass. Ask your fire. Ask it what the north track is like after first snow."

She *did* ask her fire, that night, alone, feeding a curl of birch bark into her hearth and listening to what the flames knew of the high country. The flames knew rockfall. The flames knew white silence. The flames knew, in their flickering way, that a boy who heard iron would hear nothing at all up there except the ringing cold, and that a body gives up its heat faster than its hope, and that neither survives long without the other.

Maren sat by the fire until it burned down to coals, and she thought about Briony at fourteen, hands in the dirt, laughing because the weeds were being polite again. She thought about six crowns. She thought about doors and doorways and the particular cowardice of loving people safely, at a distance, by sending them away.

Then she banked the fire, and went to find paper, and began writing down everything she knew about which villagers could be trusted, which ones might sell a name, and which path out of Ironhollow no rider had ever thought to watch — the old drain tunnel under the mill, the one her second husband had sworn was bricked up, and wasn't.

If they wouldn't leave, then they would be *hidden*. Properly. Even if she had to bury them so deep in ordinary life that the Hunt walked past their door a dozen times.

It was while she was grinding ink, three hours past midnight, that she heard it: hooves. Many hooves. Coming up the valley road at a canter, unhurried, the way men ride when they are certain of their welcome.

Maren pinched out every candle in her house with a thought, and went to wake the taster.
