## 11. Betrayal by Bread

The bread told me before anyone did.

That's the thing about living among gift-bearers for a fortnight: you stop being surprised by omens and start being annoyed by them. Tam heard iron the way other boys hear rain. Old Fenwick's granddaughter could make a candle gutter just by scowling at it. And I — well, I could taste a lie in a loaf of rye, which is a less useful talent than it sounds, unless you happen to live in a village where the baker has recently begun lying with his whole body.

Bram's bread had been good since I arrived. Honest bread, dense and dark, the crust singing when you broke it. For nine years in the palace I had eaten food made by frightened people, and fear has a flavor — coppery, thin, like water left too long in a pewter cup. Bram's bread had never tasted of fear.

Three days ago it had started.

I stood at his counter that last evening, turning a heel of loaf over in my hands while he wiped down boards that were already clean.

"New flour?" I asked.

"Same as ever." His eyes went to the window. To the road. Back to his boards.

"There's ash in this," I said gently. "Not from your oven. From a fire built in a hurry, somewhere upwind." I set the bread down. "And something else. Tallow. Candle smoke. Someone sat with you late into the night, Bram, and neither of you slept."

The cloth stopped moving. He was a big man, Bram, forearms like hams, a face made for laughing — and watching that face try not to crumble was worse than any interrogation I'd ever sat through.

"Petra," he said, and then couldn't go on.

"You don't have to tell me."

"I have three children." It came out of him like a stone from a wound. "Marta. The twins. You've seen them. You taught Marta to cheat at cards, God help me, she's *good* at it now—"

"I know what I've done," I said quietly. "Sit down, Bram."

He didn't sit. He gripped the counter as if the floor might open under him, which, given our company lately, was not an unreasonable fear. "They came through two nights back. Not riders. A tinker. A friendly man, bought a dozen loaves, asked after strangers. And I thought — I thought if I said nothing, they'd pass by. But he knew, Petra. He knew there was someone here who'd come from the capital, someone the criers are calling a *traitor*, and he knew about Maren, about what she can do, and he said—" Bram's voice dropped to almost nothing. "He said they already had names. He said the only question was whether my children's names went on a list or didn't."

*The floor dropping out of a careful life.* I had felt that once before, on a balcony in Ironhollow's capital, watching a kitchen boy dragged away for a trick of light in his hands. I had sworn I would never be the reason another person felt it.

Here I was anyway. The reason arrived wearing flour.

"Bram." I kept my voice steady, the way I used to keep it steady handing the king's cup to the king. "Look at me. Did you tell them where Maren keeps her stores? Where we sleep?"

"No." His jaw worked. "No. But I told them enough. I told them the valley narrows at Shepherd's Rest. I told them where the ford runs shallow. I told them—" he swallowed "—I told them you'd run for the pass if trouble came. Because everyone runs for the pass. There's nowhere else to *go*."

I closed my eyes. In the darkness behind them I saw the map of the valley the way I'd learned to see all maps during my smuggler years: not as geography but as intentions. Narrow ground. Shallow water. One road out.

"How long do we have?"

"A day. Maybe two. They said dawn after next, so the light would favor them."

Dawn after next. Which meant, because the world is cruel in its bookkeeping rather than its arithmetic, that we had one night, and that night would be spent arguing.

* * *

We argued. Of course we argued.

Maren wanted to stand. That shouldn't have surprised me — everything about her was standing; she was a short, broad woman built like a doorframe, with gray shot through her black hair and hands that smelled of woodsmake and rosemary — but hearing her say it aloud still landed like a blow.

"This is our home," she said, in the low room behind her hearth, where eleven of us had crowded in to decide whether to live. "Nine families. Some of these people have hidden what they are for thirty years. If we scatter, we're hunted piecemeal. If we hold the pass—"

"Maren." I said her name the way you set down something fragile. "There are maybe forty riders coming. Corvane's riders, if the tinker was guild — and he was, he smelled of ink and horse liniment, that particular guild stink. Forty armed men against a village of farmers and a handful of small gifts."

"Fenwick's boy can freeze a lock solid. Tam can feel their armor coming a mile off. And I—"

"And you can burn," I said. "I know what you can burn."

She looked at me then, and I saw she knew that I knew — that I had guessed, weeks ago, why her cottage chimney never smoked no matter how cold the night, why the winter storms always seemed to break around Ironhollow like waves around a stone.

"My daughter," she said, "was taken eight years ago because I ran. Because I picked her up and I ran instead of standing, and they caught us at a river crossing, and they took her and let me live because a dead witch is worth nothing and a broken one is worth a lesson." Her voice never rose. That was the terrible thing about it. "I will not run across another river, Petra Okafor."

So we compromised, which is what tired people do instead of choosing. We would send the children out tonight — the little ones, Marta and the twins and half a dozen others, with two adults who could walk fast — south along the drover's track where there was no road worth naming. The rest would prepare to move at first light. And Maren, over every objection I could raise, would go up to Shepherd's Rest at dawn to "watch the road," which we both understood meant *hold the pass if it came to it*.

Tam found me afterward, sitting on the woodpile behind the baker's, doing sums I hated. He was twelve, wiry, missing a front tooth, with a habit of tilting his head as though listening to a conversation happening underground — which, in fairness, he often was.

"The mountain doesn't want them to come," he announced.

"No?"

"It's grumbly tonight. All that iron in the ridge, it hums when folk are scared." He sat beside me, close enough that his shoulder bumped mine, the way boys sit when they've decided you belong to them. "You're leaving again, aren't you. I can hear it in you. Your bones go quiet when you're deciding things."

"Do my bones now."

"They're very quiet." He kicked his heels against the woodpile. "Take me with you this time. When you go."

"Tam—"

"You said if the Hunt comes, the ones who can move fast should scatter wide. Maren said it too. Nobody moves faster than me, because I know where the ground is hollow. I can hear the old mine workings under Shepherd's Rest, the whole spiderweb of them. If riders take the pass, I could find ways through the mountain they'd never—" 

"Tam." I turned to look at him properly. "If it comes to running, yes. You and me both, together. But don't wish the running closer. It always comes soon enough on its own."

He considered this with the grave seriousness he brought to everything, nodded once, and said, "Your bread's gone bad, you know. Bram's bread. I heard it yesterday — all the yeast sulking. Bread knows when the house is frightened."

"I know," I said. "I tasted it."

* * *

They came before dawn after next, not dawn after. I should have known better than to trust a frightened man's arithmetic; fear always rounds down.

Tam woke me — or rather, Tam shook me awake while already speaking, which was his way. "Iron," he whispered. "Lots of iron. Coming fast. Coming *now*."

The village erupted around us with the awful efficiency of people who had rehearsed disaster in their heads for years. Mothers pulled cloaks over sleeping children. Fenwick's boy froze the larder lock out of pure nerves and had to be thawed loose. I got Marta and the twins onto the drover's track with the first group — they'd been meant to leave hours ago, and hadn't, because Maren had kept saying *one more hour, one more hour* as if time were a thing she could knead — and I held Marta's face in my hands and lied to her beautifully, the finest lie of my career, telling her we'd all follow by midday, telling her Bram would be right behind her.

Then the horns sounded at the north end of the valley, and there was no more time for anything.

From the lane I saw them: torchlight strung along the ridgeline above Shepherd's Rest like a necklace of embers. Forty riders, maybe more, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who believed the trap already worked. Below them, small against the gray sky, stood a single figure on the narrow spine of the pass.

Maren.

I ran. I'll not pretend I made some reasoned choice; my legs decided, and my legs had apparently concluded that nine years of swallowing poison had earned me one moment of magnificent stupidity. I got as far as the foot of the pass trail before Tam caught my sleeve — how he kept pace I'll never know — and pointed up.

"She's not afraid," he said. "Listen. The iron's gone quiet. She's *calm*."

Above us, Maren raised her arms, and the mountain answered.

I had suspected what she carried. Suspecting and seeing are different countries. Fire poured off her the way water pours from a burst dam — not leaping flame but a slow, vast, orange bloom that filled the notch of the pass wall to wall, a curtain between the villagers below and the riders above. The horses screamed. Torchlight scattered. And through it all Maren stood unmoving at the center of her own conflagration, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that she had not come to watch the road.

She had come to spend herself. All of it. At once.

"Hold them," I breathed. "*Hold them, Maren.*"

She couldn't have heard me over the roar. But her head turned slightly, down toward the village, toward her home, and I would swear until I die that she smiled.

"Come on." Tam's hand was hard on my arm. "Petra. *Come on.* She's holding it so we can go. If we stand here, it's for nothing."

It took everything I had — everything the taster had ever taught me about walking away from a cup — to turn my back on that fire. We went down through the village lanes as the eastern sky went from gray to the color of a healing bruise. Behind us the pass burned steady as a beacon. Ahead of us the drover's track climbed south into hills Tam swore were full of hollow places only he could hear.

At the edge of the fields I looked back once. The fire had begun to gutter — she was burning out, exactly as I'd feared, feeding the pass everything she had — but it still stood, a wall of gold against the dawn, and beneath it, streaming south along the track, were the shapes of children running free.

Bram had stayed with his ovens. I saw him at his doorway as we passed, arms wrapped around himself, watching the pass, watching the road, waiting to learn what his terror had purchased. Our eyes met for half a heartbeat. I did not forgive him. I hope someday I will. He watched his children escape because of what he'd done, and he watched a woman immolate herself because of it too, and I suspect those two sights will keep each other company in him for the rest of his life.

Then the hills took us, and Ironhollow vanished behind the morning fog.

* * *

We walked all day. Tam navigated by some sense I couldn't follow — cutting upslope where I'd have gone along, ducking through a rockfall I'd have sworn was solid, pausing twice with his head cocked to say *"riders, west, far"* long before any sound reached my ears.

Near dusk he stopped dead on a bare hillside of gorse and slate, tilted his head, and frowned.

"What is it?"

"There's something under us." He knelt, pressed his palm flat to the ground. "Big. Old. It's not iron exactly — it's like iron but *alive*, the way a bell is alive compared to a nail." His frown deepened. "It's sad. Why would metal be sad?"

Every hair on my arms rose. I thought of scorched earth in a high ravine, of tracks wide as dinner plates, of a voice in the dark of a collapsed mine saying my name — my true name, the one no living person should have known — in a whisper like wind through a cathedral.

"Tam," I said carefully, "how far down?"

"Not far." He looked up at me, and his eyes were enormous in his dirty face. "Petra... it knows you're here. It just woke up. It says—" he listened, lips moving faintly "—it says it's been dreaming about you. It says the fire's almost out, and it needs to talk to you before the dreaming stops."

The wind moved through the gorse. Somewhere far behind us, Ironhollow burned or didn't, Maren lived or didn't, and Corvane's riders combed valleys for a taster who had finally become impossible to overlook.

Tam stood, dusted his knees, and pointed at a shelf of rock half-swallowed by hillside — and I saw now what I'd missed: not rock at all, but worked stone, ancient, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth.

"It says to come in," he said. "It says we don't have much time."

I looked at the boy, at the buried door, at the dying thing underneath that had somehow waited centuries to speak my name — and I stepped forward into the dark.
