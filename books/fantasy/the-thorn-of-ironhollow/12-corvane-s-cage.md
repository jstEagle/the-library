## 12. Corvane's Cage

Guildmaster Corvane believed in the virtue of good lighting.

Torture, done properly, was not about darkness. Darkness was for amateurs and priests, men who needed mystery to feel important. No — interrogation required light. Clean, even, merciless light, the kind that left a man nowhere to hide his own face from himself. Corvane had designed the undercroft rooms of the Taster's Guildhall himself: white lime walls, no shadows deeper than a thumb's width, a single iron chair bolted at precisely the angle where a seated prisoner could see the door and therefore spend every hour hoping and dreading in equal measure.

Hope, Corvane had found, did most of the work. The irons were merely punctuation.

He stood now behind the one-way slit of the observation gallery, a cup of small beer in his hand — he never drank wine on duty; a taster's guildmaster who dulled his own tongue was a carpenter who sawed off his fingers — and watched the third villager of the morning weep into her apron.

"Again," said Sergeant Vell, from below. He was a patient man, Vell, with the temperament of a well-trained hound and roughly the same conversational range. "The hearth-witch. Where did she go?"

"I told you." The woman's voice came out sanded flat. "I don't know. She held the pass. We ran east, down through the birches, and she stayed. I heard the fire. That's all I know, may the Twins have mercy, that's *all*—"

"The Twins," Vell said, without inflection, "have none to spare."

Corvane sipped his beer and made a small note. Not of the woman — she was spent, a squeezed rind, useless for another hour at least — but of the phrasing. *She held the pass.* A hearth-witch, burning herself out. Sentimental. Predictable. The gift-bearers always organized themselves like families rather than armies, which was why they lost. Families fed their weakest first. Armies ate them.

He turned to the clerk at his elbow, a narrow young man named Peth who took dictation in a hand so small it could only be read by candlelight or spite.

"Peth. Cross-reference the eastern birch stands against the old charcoal-burner maps. If there's a cave system within two miles, flag it."

"Yes, Guildmaster." A pause, quill hovering. "And if there isn't?"

"Then they're in the open, and the open is where my riders are already standing. Next prisoner."

* * *

They brought him the boy next, and Corvane felt the particular lift he always felt when the day offered him something delicate.

Tam, the records said. Eleven years old. Apprehended at a ford three days' ride south of Ironhollow with a stolen map in his shirt and no family name worth writing down. The apprehending sergeant had noted, in the margin of his report, in handwriting that suggested the man had written it while looking over his shoulder: *the boy hears things. Knew our horses were coming before the pickets did.*

A listener. Iron-hearing, if the villagers were to be credited, though villagers credited anything with a story attached.

Corvane descended to the room itself. He liked to sit across from certain prisoners personally — not because he enjoyed it, whatever Vell's rumors said, but because precision demanded proximity. You could not read a face through lime plaster.

The boy was small, filthy, and sitting in the iron chair with his ankles hooked around its legs like a bird on a perch, which was either terror or defiance and was almost certainly both wearing each other's clothes. His eyes tracked Corvane from the door.

"You're the one," Tam said.

Corvane settled into the facing chair, laid his hands on his knees, and smiled the smile he reserved for children and bankers: warm, unhurried, entirely without content. "I'm the guildmaster. Do you know what a taster does?"

"Eats poison so the king doesn't have to." The boy's chin came up. "Petra says you're worse than poison. Poison only kills you once."

Something flickered behind Corvane's sternum — not hurt, nothing so soft as hurt, but the small cold satisfaction of confirmation. The boy said the name easily. Naturally. As though it belonged in his mouth.

"And where is Petra now?" Corvane asked gently.

Tam's mouth shut like a purse-string.

"There's no trick in it," Corvane went on, conversational, pleasant. "You were taken at a ford. He wasn't with you — we know that; my riders are not idiots, whatever the quality of their penmanship. Which means either he abandoned you—" he watched the boy flinch, filed the flinch, "—or you're carrying something for him. A message. Proof. Something meant for eyes further up than mine."

"I don't carry anything."

"Of course not. Your shirt is simply fond of maps." Corvane leaned back. "Here is what I admire about you, Tam. Loyalty is rare. Most people, by this point in the morning, have told me everything including their grandmother's recipes. But loyalty has a shelf life, like bread, like milk, like courage. And unlike those, it can't be bought fresh."

"I'm not scared of you."

"No," Corvane agreed. "That's the tragedy. Scared boys lie badly and confess quickly. Brave ones make me do arithmetic." He rose, straightened his cuffs. "Keep him comfortable. Bread, water, no conversation — isolation loosens tongues faster than fear, and I want his tongue *loose*, not broken. A broken tongue tells you what you want to hear. A loose one tells you true things by accident."

At the door he paused. "One question before I go, since you've been so generous with your opinions of me. The night you fled Ironhollow. Did anyone speak of a dragon?"

He watched it land. He had spent nine years reading faces for the micro-terrors of poison — the half-second lag before a liar's swallow, the pupil's confession — and the boy's face performed the whole liturgy: blink, stillness, blood draining from lips too young to have learned to hide it.

"There's no such thing as dragons anymore," Tam whispered.

"Mm," said Corvane. "That's what I'd hoped you'd say."

* * *

In his study that evening, with the ledgers open and the lamps trimmed, Corvane allowed himself the luxury of thinking in numbers.

Nine years of dosing records lay before him in their matched volumes, bound in grey calfskin, each entry in his own cipher. *Subject O. Batch 14. 3 drams arsenic-laced, tolerance holding. Batch 22, hemlock derivative, doubled dose, subject reports only headache. Batch 31 —* and here the entries grew interesting — *subject metabolizes compounds lethal to baseline humans within hours. Gift expression: immunity-class. Stable. Ripening.*

Ripening. He had chosen the word early, back when the elixir program was a rumor and a requisition slip, and he had never regretted it. You did not torture a grape into wine. You tended it. You gave it exactly what it needed, in exactly the amounts it could bear, season after season, until the fruit was ready.

And Petra Okafor — invisible, punctual, methodical little Petra, who had built his own immunity brick by careful brick believing it was his secret, his private rebellion, his one possession — had been ripening in Corvane's cellar the entire time.

There was a poetry to it. Corvane appreciated poetry in others; it saved him the expense of having any.

The king's physicians wanted the final harvest by midsummer. One more year of dosing would have been ideal — the vintage improved right up to the last possible moment, and Aldric's body had begun requiring stronger draughts every season — but ideal was for men without rivals at court. Lord Chancellor Bray had a cousin on the Guild council, and Bray had recently begun asking cheerful, terrible questions about program costs. Corvane intended to deliver the ingredient before Bray finished sharpening his smile.

Which meant Petra Okafor had to be taken alive, unspoiled, and soon.

Corvane poured a measure of the current elixir batch — the king's weekly draft, refined from the last harvest, a girl from the fen-country whose gift had been small and whose screams had been brief — and held it to the lamp. Amber-gold, faintly luminous, like sunlight trapped in honey. He did not drink it. That was the king's privilege, and Corvane's curse: nine years building the thing and never once permitted to taste the fruit. There were days he understood Adam better than scripture allowed.

A knock. Vell entered, helmet under his arm, mud to the knee.

"Report from the Ironhollow sweep, Guildmaster. The witch is dead — burned out at the pass, as the woman said. We have eleven villagers in the cage-wagon, the boy, and—" Vell hesitated, which Vell did perhaps twice a year, and each instance was memorable. "We have shepherd statements. From the high fells. Three separate men swear to a dragon."

Corvane set down his cup. "Dead men swear stranger things."

"Not dead, Guildmaster. Alive. Wounded, maybe — the tracks drag on the left foreleg — but alive. Big as a tithe-barn. It went to ground somewhere north of the ruined Delve at Hollowmere, and the shepherds won't go near the place. Say the ground hums."

Silence stretched, filled only by the tick of the cooling lamps.

Corvane had planned for many contingencies in his career. Bandits, blizzards, bureaucratic rivals, the occasional zealot with a torch. He had not planned for a dragon, because dragons were extinct in the way that honest magistrates were extinct: officially, tidily, in the histories everyone agreed not to examine too closely.

And yet. The gift did not come from nowhere. Every guild scholar knew the old theory — the one the crown suppressed, the one written in the margins of forbidden herbals — that gift-bearing blood had once been kindled by dragon-fire, that the gifts were embers of an older blaze. If that was true, then the fading of the gifts and the dying of the dragons were the same death. And if one dragon lived—

If one dragon lived, then the elixir was no longer the ceiling of Corvane's ambition. It was the floor.

An elixir prolonged a life. A dragon's heart, the old texts murmured, *renewed* one. And a living source of fire — a well that never ran dry instead of a cellar of harvested children — that was not medicine. That was empire. That was a reign that outlasted dynasties, outlasted memory, outlasted the very concept of rival.

Ambition, Corvane reflected, was like the poisons he dealt in: harmless in the vial, transformative in the blood, and fatal only in the dose. He had been taking small doses all his life. Tonight he found he wanted the whole cup.

"Vell," he said, and his voice sounded calm even to himself, which was how he knew it mattered. "Double the watch on the cage-wagons. Move the boy to a sealed cell — alone, no visitors, and search him again, seam by seam; I want whatever map he's hiding before it rots in some wall." 

"And the shepherds' dragon?"

Corvane looked at the amber light in his cup, at the ledger open to nine years of patient cultivation, at the word *ripening* in his own neat cipher.

"Send a fast rider to the palace tonight. The king will want to hear this from me, not from some garrison gossip with flour on his boots." He closed the ledger. "And wake the master of stables. By dawn I want twelve picked men, the quiet ones, provisioned for the high fells."

"We're hunting it? With twelve?"

"We're *finding* it," Corvane said mildly. "Killing is the crown's business. Harvesting is ours."

Vell saluted and went out, and Corvane sat a while longer in the lamplight, doing sums. A taster who had gone missing. A village that had sheltered him. A boy who carried his secrets. And now a dragon, wounded, grounded, somewhere in the dark north — drawn, if the old theories held, to the same blood the guild had spent nine years seasoning.

It occurred to Corvane, and he recorded the thought in his journal with genuine professional pleasure, that Petra Okafor might lead them to her without any rider ever needing to track a track. The man had spent his whole life being swallowed by other people's purposes. He would hardly notice one more.

Somewhere north, in the dark, the ground hummed.

By week's end, Corvane intended to find out why.
