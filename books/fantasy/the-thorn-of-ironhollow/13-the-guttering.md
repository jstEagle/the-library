## 13. The Guttering

Fire remembers everything. That is the first truth of dragonkind, and it is the last one I will be able to keep.

I remember the first fire. Every dragon does, though none of us were there for it. It is a memory passed down the way human mothers pass down lullabies—except that our lullabies are heat, and they live in the blood, and when we close our eyes we can feel the world young and molten and generous. The old ones said fire was not something dragons *had*. It was something dragons *agreed to carry*, the way a river agrees to carry light.

I have carried it for longer than Ironhollow has had a name. And now I carry it the way a dying man carries a candle up a long staircase: carefully, reverently, watching the flame shrink with every step and pretending he is not counting.

The collapsed mine smells of cold iron and old grief. I lie in it because I can no longer lie anywhere else. My wings are ruined lace. My scales, once the color of storm-light on the sea, have gone the gray of hearth-ash three days cold. When I breathe, the breath comes out as smoke that cannot decide whether it wants to be anything more, and mostly it doesn't.

But I am not alone anymore, which is its own kind of miracle, and miracles, I have found, are exhausting company.

* * *

The small one—Petra, my tongue had known his name before my mind did, which should have frightened me more than it did—returns at dusk with two hares and an expression I have come to think of as *carefully cheerful*, the way humans get when they are hiding a wound or a plan, and in his case usually both.

"Rabbit," he announces, dropping them by my foreclaw like tribute to a very unimpressed god. "You need to eat."

"I need," I say, "a great many things. Eating is merely the one within your power."

He crouches beside the hare carcasses and begins skinning them with quick, practiced hands. He does not flinch at the work. He has never flinched at any of the work, this one—not at my smell, which is admittedly the smell of a battlefield left out in rain; not at my size; not at the way my voice makes the rubble hum. Nine years of swallowing poison for a king will apparently cure a person of most reasonable fears.

"You're thinner," he says. He means *me*. He says it lightly, the way he says everything, but I have spent centuries learning to read the small soft creatures who once rode on our shoulders, and underneath the lightness is a ledger. Everything about Petra Okafor is a ledger. He keeps accounts of what things cost, and he pays, and he does not complain about the price until long after the transaction, if ever.

"And you've noticed," I say. "Observant. That bodes well for your continued survival."

"How much time?" he asks.

There it is. No circling, no gentle approach. Humans do not circle when they already know the shape of the thing they're approaching; they only pretend to, for politeness's sake, and Petra gave up pretense somewhere around the rockslide he nearly died in.

I could lie to him. Dragons lie beautifully—it is half of what our voices are *for*. But lying to him would be like lying to a mirror, and I am too tired for mirrors.

"A season," I say. "Perhaps less. Perhaps rather less, if the weather turns cruel, which it will, because the weather has always been a petty creature."

The knife stills in his hands. "A season."

"Spring into summer. The fire gutters, small one. It does not go out all at once—that would be a mercy. A guttering candle spends its last hours deciding how to die, and so do I."

He sets down the knife. He wipes his hands on his trousers, though they were already clean, which tells me he needs something to do with them. Then he sits down in the dirt in front of my snout, cross-legged, absurdly small, and looks at me with those dark eyes that catalog everything and surrender nothing.

"Tell me what happens," he says. "When it goes out."

*Ah,* I think. *There it is.*

I had wondered when he would ask. I had hoped he wouldn't. Hope is another thing dragons carry badly.

* * *

"When the last fire dies," I say, "it does not simply go dark. Fire is a thread, and threads run through cloth. Your kind was woven with ours, in the old forges, in the years before your histories began. We breathed our flame into your bloodlines—not as a gift, exactly. Gifts can be refused. More like... a graft. A cutting from our tree planted in yours."

"The gift," he says quietly. "What the king is hunting."

"What the king is hunting," I agree. "Your hearth-witches. Your iron-hearers. Your poison-eaters." I let that last one sit between us for a moment, because he deserves to sit with it. His immunity is not an accident. It never was. "Every strange bright thing in your realm is a leaf on that grafted branch. And a branch cut from its tree..."

"Dies back," he says. He knows plants. Of course he does—he was a smuggler's runner through mountain passes; you learn what grows where and what kills you if you eat it wrong.

"Dies back," I confirm. "Slowly at first. Then all at once. The gifts will fade over a generation, perhaps two—the strongest going first, because the brightest flames burn their wax fastest. But before the fading ends, something else happens. Something your guild-masters and your king have not yet understood, because they think the gift is a treasure to be harvested rather than a root to be kept watered."

He waits. He is good at waiting. It may be his single greatest skill, and I include his talent for not dying.

"When dragon-fire dies," I say, "everything it touched becomes mortal again. Fully mortal. Vulnerable in ways it has forgotten how to be vulnerable." I lower my great head until my eye—clouded now, filmed at the edges like an old window—is level with him. "You have been dosing yourself with death for nine years, Petra Okafor, and building a wall against it stone by stone. Do you know what mortar holds that wall?"

His face does something very small and very controlled. "The gift."

"The gift. Whatever ember sits banked in your blood—and it is banked deep, banked like a coal under ash, banked better than any I have sensed in a century—it is fed by the same fire that feeds me. When mine goes out..." I exhale, and the smoke that leaves me is thin as a widow's veil. "Your wall comes down. All at once. Every poison you ever swallowed, every tolerance you built, every immunity you paid for in sweat and shaking—all of it reverts. You will be, in a single breath, the most poisoned man alive and no longer proof against any of it."

Silence, except for the drip of water somewhere deeper in the mine, keeping its own patient time.

"That's why the ledgers matter," he says at last, and his voice is steady, which costs him, and I honor the cost. "Corvane's records. Nine years of doses. If I lose the immunity—"

"Then every entry in those ledgers becomes a debt collector," I say. "Yes."

He laughs. It startles me—I confess I expected rage, or despair, or that particular human silence that is worse than either. Instead he laughs, short and sharp, there in the dark with a dying dragon.

"Of course," he says. "Of course. Nine years of careful, methodical, invisible work, and the whole foundation is sitting on top of a candle that's about to go out. You couldn't design a better joke if you tried."

"I could," I say. "I once knew a dragon who bet a mountain against a mouse. But continue."

* * *

He builds a fire—his own, small and ordinary, the kind made of sticks and patience—and roasts the hares, and eats his share, and pushes the rest toward me without being asked. I eat. It is courtesy more than hunger; my body has begun declining invitations. But the meat is warm, and warmth is warmth, and I take what I am given these days.

"Vessal." He says my name carefully, the way humans handle an egg. "The bond. The thing between us. Is that part of the fire? Does it die too?"

I consider lying again. I seem to consider it frequently around him, and frequently decline, and I begin to suspect this is what humans mean by *trust*, though I would never use so undignified a word aloud.

"No," I say. "The bond is older than the fire. Or younger. The old ones argued about it. The bond is the thread itself, small one—the fire is only what runs along it. When the flame goes out, the thread remains. Cold, dark, useless for burning. But remaining."

"Useless," he repeats.

"Mostly," I say, because I am a coward in exactly one direction and it is this one. "A thread can hold weight without light. But it cannot warm anyone. It cannot wake anything. It would be—" I search for the word and dislike all of them. "It would be a door that opens onto a wall."

He is quiet for a while. The little fire pops. Somewhere above us, wind moves through the pass, and I track it out of habit, the way I once tracked armies.

"My mother used to say," he says finally, "that the worst thing about knowing a thing was going to end wasn't the ending. It was knowing the date." He turns the spit. "She was a taster too. Did I tell you that? Before me. She died of it, eventually—slowly, by inches, the poisons accumulating. She wrote to me about it. Matter-of-fact. Like reporting weather." His jaw tightens. "I burned her letters after the proclamation. All of them. I thought—I thought if anyone found them, they'd prove something. About the gift. About me."

"And now you regret the burning."

"Now I regret the burning." He looks at his hands. "Funny how you can save yourself and lose something anyway. Seems to be the whole business of living."

"Yes," I say. "We invented that observation. Dragons. Several thousand years before your kind learned to write it down. You're welcome."

That gets a real laugh out of him, brief and surprised, and I find I have been rationing my words all evening to earn exactly that sound, and I despise how easily pleased I have become, and I do it anyway.

* * *

Later, when he sleeps—curled against my foreleg because the mine is cold and I am the largest source of residual heat for miles, a fact I intend to hold over him indefinitely—I stay awake, because sleep and I have recently entered negotiations and I am losing.

And I do what dying dragons do. I take inventory.

The fire is smaller tonight than it was yesterday. I know this the way a miser knows his coins. There was a time when a single sneeze from me could glass a hillside; then a time when I could boil a lake; then a time, embarrassingly recent, when I struggled to char a rabbit. Now I look inward, at the hearth at the center of me, and I see embers. Beautiful ones. Old ones. Embers with memories folded inside them like letters in drawers.

Here is what I do not tell him, because telling him would change nothing and frighten him much:

The guttering is faster than a season. Seasons are for candles in calm rooms. Mine burns in a draft, and the draft has a name, and the name is *alone*. Dragon-fire feeds on company—on bonded hearts, on shared sky, on the particular music of a rider's breathing against a dragon's pulse. I have been alone for two hundred years. Two hundred years of banking coals and telling myself tomorrow I would go looking, and tomorrow, and tomorrow. The fire has been eating itself to keep me walking, the way starving travelers eat their boots, and now there is very little boot left.

A season. Perhaps. Perhaps rather less.

Unless—

And here is the thought I keep turning over like a stone with something glinting underneath, here is the heresy I have not spoken in two centuries because speaking it got the last dragons killed:

The fire does not have to end with me.

It was given, once. Grafted, planted, breathed into bloodlines. What was given can be given again. Not taken—taking is the king's method, and look how well that works; you cannot drink a river and become wet forever. Given. Freely. From a willing hearth to a waiting wick.

But I am so weak. What remains of me might not survive the giving. And the small one sleeping against my leg, with his poison-thickened blood and his ledger-keeping heart and his mother's steadiness in his jaw—what would remain of *him*?

I do not know. That is the honest answer, and honesty, I am discovering late in life, is terribly inconvenient.

The old bonding-stones could answer such questions. The forges where our kinds were first woven. Most were broken in the purges, shattered by men with hammers and fear. But stones are stubborn things. Stones remember. Somewhere in these mountains—

The thought arrives fully formed, the way true thoughts do, and I almost dismiss it, and then I feel it settle into place with the rightness of a key finding its lock.

Somewhere in these mountains there is a forge-city that the crown never found, because the crown has never once looked *down*.

* * *

Morning comes gray and thin. Petra wakes, stretches, winces at whatever new bruise the rockslide gifted him, and starts assembling breakfast from the supplies he carries with the thoroughness of a man who has twice nearly starved and intends never to make it a third time.

"Taste that before you eat it," I say.

He pauses, hare jerky halfway to his mouth, and gives me a look. "It's jerky, Vessal."

"It's also from a village whose baker sold you to the Guild, packed by hands you didn't watch. Old habits, small one. Indulge me."

He tastes it. Dramatically. Eyes closed. "Alarming notes of salt pork. A finish of despair. I'll live."

"You'd better," I say, and the words come out heavier than I meant them to, and he hears it, because he hears everything, curse him.

He lowers the jerky. "How bad is it? Really. Not the polite version."

I could prevaricate. I have prevaricated with emperors. But his eyes are dark and level and full of that terrible human arithmetic, and I find I cannot add one more false entry to his ledger. He has enough debts.

"Worse than a season," I say. "I told you a season because it was kinder and because I am old and vain and wanted one more kindness on my record. But fire feeds on company, Petra. On bond. And I have been alone for two hundred years, and my fire has been eating itself to keep me breathing. It is nearly finished eating."

His face goes very still. Then, because he is who he is: "How long?"

"Weeks. A handful of them. Fewer if I fly, and I must fly, because there is something we need."

"We?"

"There is a place," I say, "in the deep roots of these mountains. A forge-city, from the First Bonding, where human blood and dragon-fire were woven together at a stone older than either of our peoples. The stones remember what was done and how. And if anything in this world can teach a dying fire how to give instead of gutting itself—" I pause. "It is there. If it still stands."

He is quiet. I watch him do his accounting: the risk, the distance, the hunters behind us, the weeks ahead of us, and the price written in both our bodies.

"If we don't find it," he says.

"Then I die a few weeks early, comfortably, in the mountains I love, having spent my last strength on a fool's errand with a poison-taster." I let one eyelid droop in the expression humans call a wink and dragons call *insufferable*. "Frankly, small one, there are worse deaths. I've caused several."

He laughs despite himself, then sobers, looking east through the mine's broken mouth toward the low hills, toward Ironhollow, toward everything hunting us.

"Then we'd better move fast," he says. "Because I just realized something."

"Oh?"

"Tam." His voice drops. "The boy hears iron, Vessal. He hears what's buried. He heard the mountains talking back at the ford—I saw it in his face. If anyone alive knows where a lost forge-city sleeps under the stone..."

He trails off, but the hook is set, and I feel it pull through the cold dark thread that binds us, that thin stubborn line of bond the fire can no longer warm but cannot quite sever. Somewhere south and east, a boy who listens to metal is running ahead of us with nothing but the clothes on his back and the whole buried memory of the mountains in his ears.

"Well then," I say, and struggle upright, joints grinding, embers shifting in the hearth of me like coals roused by a bellows I no longer own. "It seems we are going to need a boy who talks to iron."

"And a dragon who can still fly."

I spread my ruined wings. They catch the morning light like torn banners.

"One flight left in me," I say. "Let us make it count."
