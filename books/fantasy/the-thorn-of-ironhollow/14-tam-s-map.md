## 14. Tam's Map

The mountain had been talking to Tam all his life, but it had never talked this loud.

He'd learned young to keep the iron-song quiet. When he was small, he'd told his mother that the pots in the kitchen hummed when she was sad, and she'd looked at him the way you look at a child who has said something dangerous without knowing it, and after that he kept his mouth shut and let people think him slow. Slow was safe. Slow got fed. The boy who flinched at hammer-blows three streets away, who knew which horseshoe would crack before the smith did — nobody suspected him of anything but dullness, and dullness was the best cloak a gift-bearer could wear.

But out here, past the ford, past the burned pass, past the place where Maren had stood in the smoke and bought them their lives with everything she had, the mountains didn't whisper anymore. They *sang*.

"You're doing the face again," Petra said. He was walking a few paces ahead on the goat track, one hand trailing along the rock as if he could read it by touch, which — Tam supposed — maybe he could, in his own way. "The one where you look like you're listening to something I can't hear."

"I am listening to something you can't hear."

"Is it telling you we're about to walk off a cliff?"

"No." Tam paused. Behind them, Vessal moved with the terrible slowness of a thing that used to be swift, her scales gone the grey of old ash, her breath rattling like a bellows with a torn seam. Even her silence was heavy. "It's telling me there's a road under us."

Petra stopped. "There's no road under us. There's scree and goat droppings and a drop on my left I'm trying very hard not to think about."

"There's a road *under* the scree," Tam said. "Old one. It goes down into the mountain, and the mountain is — " he hunted for the word, the way you hunt for a coin dropped in deep grass — "it's *hollow* there. Not empty-hollow. Built-hollow. Like a pot. Somebody made it, a long time ago, and the stone still remembers being made."

Vessal's head came around. It was the first quick movement she'd made in two days, and it scared Tam more than any slow one had. One ancient eye, copper-ringed and dim, fixed on him.

"Say that again," Vessal said. Her voice sounded like a forge going cold.

"The stone remembers being made?"

"*Hollow.* Built-hollow." The dragon's nostrils flared, drawing air in long and deliberate, the way she did when she was tasting something older than weather. "Little iron-hearer. Where."

Tam turned slowly, letting the song come up through his boots instead of holding it down. It was louder here than anywhere he'd ever been — a deep chord under the scree, layered, many-voiced, the way a village was many-voiced at market morning. And underneath the chord, faint as a name called across a valley: *fire*. Old fire. Fire remembered in metal the way a scar remembers the wound.

"That way," he said, pointing up-slope, where the track bent around a shoulder of granite. "Past the split rock. There's — I think there's a door."

* * *

The door had been a door once. Now it was a mouthful of collapsed rubble with one leaning slab still standing, carved all over in lines that might have been letters or might have been decoration or might have been both, the way old things often were. Petra put his palm against it and held it there for a long moment.

"This is guild-work," he said quietly. "Or — no. Older. This is what guild-work was copied from." His jaw tightened. Tam had noticed Petra did that sometimes — touched a thing, went somewhere else behind his eyes, came back angry. He did it less since the mine, since Vessal, but he still did it.

"Can you hear if anything's inside?" Petra asked him. "Man. Trap. Bear."

Tam crouched and laid both hands flat to the fallen stones, cheek nearly touching them, and listened the way Maren had taught him to listen to bread — patiently, with your whole body, ready to be surprised.

"Water," he said. "Far off. Dripping. And wind, moving through big spaces, like breath in a chest. And..." He frowned. "Metal. A lot of metal. But it's all asleep. Nothing's moving. Nobody's home."

"Nobody's been home for a long time," Vessal said. She lowered her great head to the gap beneath the leaning slab, and her breath stirred dust that hadn't been disturbed in centuries, and something in the carved lines — Tam heard it, actually *heard* it, a faint ringing like a wet finger round a bowl's rim — answered her.

She said a word then. It wasn't in any language Tam knew, and it wasn't in any language anyone living knew, and yet he understood it perfectly, the way you understand a lullaby your mother sang before you were born. It meant *I am come back late.*

The rubble groaned. Stones shifted, rolled aside — not pushed so much as persuaded, the way a knot persuades itself loose when the right hand finds it — and a dark passage opened beyond, breathing cold mineral air into their faces.

"Well," Petra said, after a moment. "That's one way with a door."

* * *

They went in single file, Vessal folding herself impossibly along corridors built for her kind and hers alone — Tam realized, watching her move through arches tall enough for her to walk upright beneath, that this place had never been built for humans at all. Humans had been guests here. Maybe less than guests.

Then the corridor opened, and Tam forgot how to breathe.

The mountain opened its ribs. A cavern vast enough to swallow Ironhollow whole spread below them, lit by shafts of pale daylight falling through cracks in the ceiling high above, and it was full of city. Terraces cut into the walls. Stairways wide as roads. Foundries with their great chimneys cold and silent, forges ranked in rows like sleeping animals, anvils the size of carts. And everywhere, everywhere, metal — worked metal, singing metal, even asleep, even rusted, a hundred thousand small voices humming the same long note.

"It's a forge-city," Petra breathed. "The stories — I thought they were stories. The Bondsmiths. I thought they were stories."

"They're not stories," Vessal said. "They're graves."

Her voice cracked on the last word. Not the way a voice cracks from weakness, though she was weak; the way it cracks from carrying something too heavy for too long. She descended the great stair slowly, each step placed like an apology, and Tam followed with his hand on the wall because the wall was singing and he couldn't bear not to touch it.

* * *

The murals started halfway down.

They covered the terraces in bands, painted in pigments that had somehow refused to die — deep reds, golds, a blue like high-altitude sky. Tam couldn't read the lettering spiraling through them, but he didn't need to. The pictures told it plain.

Dragons and people, standing together. Not dragons ridden, not dragons fought — standing, side by side, wings over shoulders, human hands laid flat against scaled flanks. Then smaller pictures, hundreds of them: a smith at an anvil with a drakeling curled asleep at his feet. A woman laughing as a young dragon snapped at a ribbon she held. Lines of figures — human, dragon, human, dragon — walking into fire together, unburned.

And then, further along the terrace, the colors changed. Red became the red of blood rather than clay. Dragons flew away in the murals, winging toward the top edge, and the humans below reached up, and some of the reaching hands held weapons.

"They left," Tam said.

"Some were driven," Vessal corrected softly. "And some left because staying meant watching what was done." She stopped before one mural larger than the rest. In it, a circle of humans knelt around a stone — a stone shaped like nothing so much as a great egg, veined with channels that glowed gold in the painter's pigment — and above them a dragon lowered its head, and light ran from the dragon's open jaws into the stone, and from the stone into the kneeling figures, and Tam felt his own skin prickle, because he knew that picture the way you know your own face in a mirror.

"What's happening there?" he asked, though some part of him already knew.

"Bonding," Vessal said. "Fire given freely into stone, and stone sharing it out to those who choose to kneel. That is how the gift lived in your bloodlines, little hearer. Not as theft. As *gift*. Freely offered, freely received." Her tail dragged as she turned. "Your king takes blood. The old way gave it. That is the whole difference between a bond and a harvest, and your world has forgotten it, and forgetting has a price, and I have been paying it for nine hundred years."

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind moved through the cavern, breathing through the dead chimneys, and the sleeping metal hummed.

* * *

The bonding-stone sat at the heart of it all, in a round chamber at the city's center where every terrace, every stairway, every corridor seemed to converge.

It was cracked clean through.

Not broken — cracked, like a bell with a flaw, like a cup that will hold water but only just. It stood twice Tam's height, egg-shaped, its surface webbed with those golden channels, most of them dark now, a few still holding a glow so faint you had to squint and doubt your own eyes. Fallen masonry lay around it. Someone, long ago, had tried to shore it up — timber braces rotted to lace, iron bands rusted to stains.

Tam walked up to it and, because it seemed the only polite thing to do, put his hand against it.

The stone was warm.

Not sun-warm, not fire-warm. *Alive*-warm, faint as a candle seen through a wall, but alive, and through his palm he heard it — not the long sleep-note of the rest of the city but something thinner, slower, stubborn. A heartbeat with a year between beats.

"She's still in there," he whispered. "Barely. Like — like embers under ash. Like Maren's stove banked for the night."

Petra had gone very still. Tam watched him look at the stone, then at Vessal, then at the stone again, and Tam was learning to read the taster's face, which showed almost nothing except to people who had learned to watch for the almost-nothing. What Tam saw there now was fear and hope fighting to a draw.

"Banked embers can be rekindled," Petra said carefully. "If you have fuel."

"Fuel," Vessal repeated.

"A taster learns chemistry whether he wants to or not." Petra's voice was level, but his hand had drifted to his own forearm, where the guild's needle-marks had long since faded to nothing anyone could see — though Tam had seen Vessal scent them often enough. "Nine years of dosed blood. If the stone shares fire the way your mural says — gives rather than takes —"

"No." Vessal's voice came out flat and final as a slammed door. "You do not bleed for stone. You have bled enough for thieves."

"I haven't bled at all. That's rather the point. They *took*."

"And I am telling you the answer is no, and I am old enough that 'no' is the oldest word I own."

But Tam, hand still on the stone, felt it shift — the thin heartbeat stuttering, quickening, the way a sleeper stirs when familiar footsteps cross the floor. And he heard something else, far off, carried down the mountain's bones through iron and stone and the soles of his feet.

Hooves. Many hooves. High up, on the goat track, coming fast.

"Um," Tam said. "The mountain says we have company."

* * *

They doused what little light they'd kindled and climbed the terraces in the dark, Vessal moving on sheer stubbornness now, her breath sawing. From a high gallery behind a fallen pillar, Tam looked back the way they'd come and saw, far below at the broken door, torchlight spilling through the gap like spilled soup.

Riders. Six, maybe eight. And even at this distance, Tam's ears caught the sound he dreaded most: chain-mail, and the particular creak of a cage-cart axle.

"They followed us," Petra murmured beside him. "From the ford? No — they'd have given up. Someone sold the route." His voice went very quiet. "Someone always sells."

"The stone," Tam whispered suddenly. His heart was hammering. "Petra, the riders are going to find the stone. And it's — it's barely alive, and if they break it, if Corvane breaks it —"

"He doesn't know what it is."

"His *smiths* will." Tam thought of the ledgers, of the vials, of nine years measured out in spoonfuls. "Everything the guild knows, they stole from here. Don't you see? This is where it all started. This is the original crime scene."

Below, a rider's torch swept the mural terrace, and the painted dragons seemed to flinch in its light. Tam pressed himself flatter against the cold stone floor, and the mountain sang its warning through his bones, and the cracked stone at the heart of everything beat its slow ember-beat, waiting — nine hundred years patient, and running out of time.

Petra touched his shoulder. "Tam," he said softly. "How good are you at hearing where a man keeps his keys?"
