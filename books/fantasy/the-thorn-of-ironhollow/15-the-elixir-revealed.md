## 15. The Elixir Revealed

The documents came out of Tam's satchel smelling of mold and lamp oil, which was fitting, because they had been rescued from a burning guildhouse and would end in fire themselves if Petra had any say in it.

"Careful with those," he said, and then heard how stupid it sounded. "I mean—careful with your hands. The ink will be leaded. Old guild habit."

Tam pulled his fingers back from the topmost page as if the paper had teeth. He was eleven, small for it, and had not slept properly since Ironhollow. Neither had Petra, but Petra had nine years of practice at looking like a man who had. That was half a taster's job: standing at the king's left hand at dawn, spoon steady, face smooth as poured cream, while inside him every nerve screamed or wept or both.

Outside the ruin of the old forge-city, the wind moved through broken colonnades and made a sound like slow breathing. Vessal lay curled in the great cracked bowl of the bonding-square, her hide gone dull as unpolished copper, her breathing so shallow that the snow near her nostrils did not even stir. She was sleeping more than waking now. Petra tried not to do the arithmetic that arithmetic kept doing anyway—how many days, how few—and failed, as usual.

He turned back to the papers and spread them on a fallen slab, weighting the corners with four chunks of tumbled masonry. Then he read.

* * *

The first document was innocuous, which was the first horror. It was a supply ledger, dated eleven years back, and it recorded deliveries to the Taster's Guild under the bland heading of *kitchen requisitions*. Salt. Honey. Wine vinegar. And, in a clerk's cramped hand, item after item of things no kitchen had ever wanted:

*Essence of belladonna, 4 drams.*
*Prepared aconite, 2 drams.*
*Waters of hemlock, clarified, 6 drams.*
*Compound unknown (eastern), 1 dram — handle with tongs.*

Petra's mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with recognition. He knew those doses. He had tasted them. Not all at once—he had never been given anything all at once—but across years, in wine and broth and the little ceremonial cup the guild called *the loyal sip*, each poison arriving at intervals precisely calculated so that his body would learn it, survive it, and then be made to learn a little more.

He had thought he was clever. Nine years of stealing sips from unwatched cups, of cultivating his own immunity in secret, of congratulating himself on outwitting the guild one measured drop at a time.

He set the page down very carefully, because his hands were not entirely his own just then.

"They weren't testing me," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the ruins, too loud against the stone. "They were feeding me."

Tam looked up from where he sat cross-legged by the cold fire-pit, whittling nothing in particular out of a stick. "Feeding you what?"

"Apothecary's garden." Petra laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Every poison I ever survived, Tam. Every one. They chose them. They scheduled them. There are annotations." He tapped the margin, where a neat hand had written *subject tolerates 3 gr. aconite; increase next quarter.* *Subject.* As though he were a field of barley. As though he were a barrel in a cellar, turned at the proper season, racked and bottled and brought up when the drinking was good.

Because that was exactly what he was. The second document proved it.

* * *

It was bound in grey leather, guild-stamped, and titled only *Vintage Records — Subject O*. Someone had once thought that funny. Petra could imagine the clerk who'd done it, some ink-stained little man in a warm room, pleased with his own wit while a boy of nineteen swallowed hemlock water and thanked the guildmaster for the honor of serving.

The vintage records were meticulous. The guild kept better books on Petra Okafor than the crown kept on its taxes. Date of birth. Mother's name—*deceased, gift-lineage suspected*. Father unknown. Recruited at fourteen as a runner, tested at sixteen, *blood shows dormant gift-marker; candidate accepted for cultivation*. And then year upon year of entries in columns so regular they might have been prayers:

*Year 3: Marker activity rising. Blood drawn monthly, 4 oz. Refined yield improving.*
*Year 5: Subject reports no symptoms. Excellent. Recommend continuation without interruption; gaps in regimen cause marker recession.*
*Year 7: Yield sufficient for quarterly delivery to palace undercrofts.*
*Year 9: Final harvest anticipated within the season. Subject remains unaware. Praise to the Guild.*

*Subject remains unaware.*

Petra read that line four times. The words did not change. That was the terrible thing about words; you could stare at them until your eyes burned and they simply went on saying what they said.

"Final harvest," Tam repeated. The boy had crept close despite himself; he always did when reading was involved, hungry for letters the way other children were hungry for bread. "Harvest of what? You're not a wheat field."

"No," Petra said. "But my blood is."

He explained it the way you explain something to a child because explaining it to yourself directly is unbearable. The gift—the true gift, the thing that let Maren coax flame from cold hearths and let Tam hear iron singing through six feet of stone—lived in the blood. Everyone had always said the gifts were fading, dying out generation by generation, a candle guttering in a drafty hall. But a candle's light can be captured. Distilled. Drawn off month by month into vials, refined by guild alchemists in the palace undercrofts, and carried up in a silver flask to a king who was seventy-one years old and had commanded armies at fifteen and should, by every law of nature, have been ten years in his tomb.

Aldric the Steadfast. Aldric the Ageless, the ballads called him now, and the balladeers meant it as praise.

"He's been drinking me," Petra said. "For seven years. Me and—" he flipped pages, and there they were, other designations, other letters, most of the entries ending in a small notation that he understood only slowly, and then all at once, and wished he never had: *yield declined*, *marker recession*, *subject expended*. Expended. Like a candle burned to its socket. Like a field ploughed to dust.

"How many?" Tam asked quietly. The boy heard iron, but he heard grief too; he always had.

Petra counted. He made himself count. Fourteen names' worth of letters before his own. Fourteen people who had stood at tables and drunk from ceremonial cups and believed, perhaps, that they were serving the realm. Some of the entries went back thirty years. One subject—*M*, recruited 40 years prior—had been cultivated for twenty-two years before the ledgers noted, in the same tidy clerical hand, *source depleted; replacement sought.*

Replacement. The word sat in Petra's chest like a swallowed coal. He had been a replacement. And somewhere, right now, in some guild office, some clerk was keeping a file labeled with the letter that would come after O.

* * *

He walked away from the documents after that. He told Tam he needed air, which was true, though what he actually needed was to move, to put his legs between himself and the paper, because if he sat still one more minute with those neat little columns he was going to do something destructive and undignified.

The forge-city took him in like it took in everyone: without comment. He climbed the ramp of the bonding-square, past the murals Tam had found days ago—the ones showing men and women standing hand in hand with dragons, their arms inked with the same thorn-rose pattern that now crawled faintly, invisibly, along Petra's own veins whenever Vessal's breath touched him. In the mural-painters' world, the gift had been a shared thing, a living current between species, and no one had needed to bleed anyone into a flask to keep a king alive past his allotted span.

At the top of the ramp he stopped, because Vessal's eye was open.

One eye. The size of a shield, gold going grey at the rim, fixed on him with the terrible patience of a creature who had watched empires compost themselves. She did not speak. She rarely bothered with words anymore; words cost her something, he could tell. But she looked at him, and she breathed—one long, slow exhalation, smokeless now, carrying only the smell of hot stone—and Petra felt it settle over him like a blanket, that warmth, that attention, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the first page of the vintage records loosened by a single degree.

"I was livestock," he said to her. His voice cracked on the last word, and he let it. There was no one here to perform calmness for. That was the strange luxury of dragons: they had no use for a smooth face. "Nine years. They fed me poisons like a goose being fattened for pâté, and I thought I was beating them. I used to lie awake proud of myself, Vessal. Proud. Every drop I survived, I thought, another victory. And they were writing it down in a ledger and calling me a *vintage*."

Vessal's nostrils flared. The old dragon made a sound low in her throat—not speech, not quite, but acknowledgment, the way a mountain acknowledges rain.

"And the worst of it," Petra went on, because now that the dam had cracked he could not stop it, and he had learned long ago that some things must be said aloud or they rot you from inside, "the worst is that it worked. Whatever they brewed out of me kept him alive, didn't it? Seven years of Aldric the Ageless, and every year some village burns and some child gets dragged away in a cart, because the king needs his wine." He laughed again, that awful laugh. "I'm the wine, Vessal. I'm the vintage. Nineteen years old when they started drawing my blood and I *thanked them*. I bowed. I said it was an honor to serve."

The dragon's eye held him. And in it, distorted but unmistakable, he saw his own reflection—a thin man with wind-burnt cheeks and a fugitive's beard, standing small and furious on ancient stone—and for the first time since Ironhollow he did not look away from himself.

* * *

He went back down when the light went copper. Tam had organized the documents into piles, which was a very Tam thing to do, and had built a small fire, which was a brave thing, since smoke could be seen for miles and both of them knew it. Sometimes courage looks like a boy deciding that warmth matters more than safety, just for one evening.

"There's more," Tam said. He said it carefully, watching Petra's face, and Petra appreciated that—the boy had learned to read people almost as well as documents. "The last packet. I saved it because the seal was different. It's not guild. Look."

The seal was the royal crest: the crowned stag of House Aldricson. Inside was a single letter, brief, in an elegant hand Petra recognized from proclamation copies nailed to every post in three kingdoms' worth of towns.

*Guildmaster Corvane—*

*The Vintage O approaches ripeness. My physicians say the present stock will not carry me through the winter. See that the final harvest is taken whole and soon. I am given to understand the subject has fled toward Thornfell. Retrieve him intact. Damage to the source diminishes the yield.*

*I trust this finds you prompt. You know my taste.*

*A.R.*

*Damage to the source diminishes the yield.* Petra read it twice. Then he did something he had never once done in nine years of court service, nine years of swallowing poison with a smile and calling it loyalty.

He tore the letter into pieces. Then he tore the pieces into pieces. Then he fed them into Tam's small fire one fragment at a time, watching each curl and blacken, and it helped less than he wanted and more than he expected.

"Whole," Tam said softly. "He wants you *whole*."

"Yes."

"So we keep you whole." The boy said it with the flat practicality of someone who had already lost a village and a hearth-witch and had decided, somewhere in the ashes, that losing one more person was not on this season's menu. "We run farther. We—"

"No."

The word came out of Petra with a force that surprised them both. He knelt by the fire, and the fury in him—which had been a storm all day, all noise and tearing—suddenly settled into something colder and far more useful. A taster's fury. The kind that measures.

"No," he said again, calmer. "Running is what they expect. Corvane's riders will sweep every pass looking for a man fleeing. And every mile we run, Vessal gets weaker, and the Hunt burns another village looking for us, and Aldric drinks whatever's left of the old stock and waits for his delivery." He picked up the vintage ledger—the grey leather book, the nine years of his life reduced to yields and quarters—and weighed it in his hand. "You don't run from men who think you're property, Tam. You make yourself expensive."

Tam frowned. "Expensive how?"

"You said these ruins have archives. Sealed rooms. The forge-city's record-halls." Petra's mind was moving fast now, the way it moved over a suspicious dish, tasting every component separately. "Corvane keeps ledgers. So did whoever ran this place, once. If there's proof here—not rumor, not whispers, but *records*—of what the crown has been doing, then I'm not a fugitive anymore. I'm evidence."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, wind sighed through the colonnades, and beneath the wind—faint, vast, tired—came the sound of Vessal breathing.

"And if the records say what I think they say," Petra went on, turning the grey book over in his hands, feeling the weight of nine stolen years in it, "then Aldric isn't just a murderer with a wine cellar. He's a thief who stole something that belongs to every gift-bearer in Ironhollow. To Maren's daughter. To every child in those carts." He looked up, and Tam flinched a little at what he found in the taster's face—no fear in it anymore, only a bright and patient heat, like coals banked for morning. "And you don't beg a thief to give it back, Tam. You take the ledger to his own table and you read it aloud in front of everyone he's lied to."

Tam stared at him. Then, slowly, the boy grinned—the first grin since Ironhollow, crooked and young and fierce. "The archives are under the east colonnade," he said. "I heard the doors. Iron hinges. They've been waiting a long time."

"Then tomorrow we open them." Petra set the vintage ledger down beside the fire—deliberately, gently, the way you set down a weapon you intend to pick up again. Above them, the great shadow of the dying dragon shifted against the stars.

He did not yet know that the decision had already been made for him—that even now, in the dark passes below, Corvane's riders were climbing, and that the choice between running and reckoning would soon be taken out of his hands entirely. He knew only that for the first time in nine years, Petra Okafor was not afraid of what the morning would bring.

He was angry enough to look forward to it.
