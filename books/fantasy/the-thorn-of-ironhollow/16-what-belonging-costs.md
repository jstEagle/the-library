## 16. What Belonging Costs

The forge-city had no name anymore, but Tam called it the Humming Place, because when he pressed his palm to the old bonding-stone he could hear something underneath the silence—like a spoon left ringing in an empty bowl.

Petra had learned, over nine years at court, to measure time in courses. The fish course was when you watched for almond bitterness. The meat course was when you watched the king's cup-bearer's hands. Here, in the ruined hall with its collapsed roof and its murals of winged shadows, time measured itself differently: in how long Vessal's breathing took between one slow rise and fall. In how many days of bread Maren's flour had bought them. In how much longer the proof in Tam's satchel would stay dry.

He sat on a fallen pillar that some ancient mason had carved into the shape of a dragon's tail, eating hard cheese and watching the boy sleep against Vessal's foreleg. The dragon's hide had gone from banked-coal red to the grey of a hearth swept out too early, but where Tam's head rested against her, a faint warmth still bloomed.

"You're doing it again," Vessal said. Her voice came like wind through a chimney, low and hollowed. "The counting."

"I'm always counting."

"Yes. But this time you're counting *away*." One amber eye, dim as a lamp about to gutter, opened. "You've looked at the eastern road four times since dawn. You've touched your collar twice, where Corvane's men grabbed you at the ford. And you have not once asked Tam what he dreams of, which you do every morning, because it makes him laugh, and because you are a sentimental creature pretending very hard to be a ledger."

"I am not sentimental."

"You wept into my scale last week and told me it was rain."

"It was raining."

"Underground," said Vessal, "Petra. It was raining underground."

He huffed and went back to his cheese. Below them, in the cracked bowl of the bonding-stone, dawnlight pooled like spilled honey. The murals on the walls showed what the stone used to do: humans standing palm-to-palm with dragons, fire passing between them like a shared cup. Nine hundred years ago, someone had decided that was dangerous. Nine hundred years later, King Aldric drank refined gift-blood in his cellar and called it medicine.

Three days ago, Tam had pulled the guild documents from a sealed archive beneath the eastern stair—documents Petra had spent two nights reading by candle stub until the words stopped being words and became a sentence, and then became a verdict. *Subject O. Dosage maintained at harvest-grade. Vintage approaching peak yield. Final extraction authorized upon capture.*

His body was a wine cellar. His suffering had been agriculture.

He'd thrown up behind the archive wall, quietly, the way he did everything, and then he'd come back and read it again, because a taster learns that the only way past a poison is straight through the middle of it.

* * *

Corvane's riders found the valley road on the fourth day.

Petra heard them before anyone—nine years of tasting had sharpened nothing so much as his hearing for trouble, the small wrongnesses in a room. Hooves on wet stone. Too many of them, moving too carefully. Not a patrol. A hunt with patience.

He woke Tam with a hand over the boy's mouth, which was a kindness compared to what the riders would do, and together they climbed to the ledge where Vessal slept her grey sleep.

"They've brought nets," Tam whispered. His eyes were shut, his face turned toward the sound the rest of them couldn't parse. "Iron chain in the nets. And—" the boy flinched— "*fire-oil*. Lots of it. They're going to burn her even if she can't wake up."

Vessal opened both eyes then. Something moved behind them that was older than kingdoms.

"No," she said.

"They're coming for all of us—"

"No." She dragged herself upright, and the effort cost her visibly; her breath whistled like a kettle taken off the heat too late. "They are coming for *him*. I am incidental. A heart they'd like, but a rumor they'd settle for." Her gaze settled on Petra with terrible gentleness. "You heard them too. Didn't you?"

He had. He'd been listening without admitting it, the way he'd tasted without admitting he was learning poisons. The riders weren't spreading out to search the ruins. They were holding formation around the single eastern approach—the pass that led back down toward Ironhollow, toward the village where Maren's people had scattered after the burning of the pass, toward the families hiding in root cellars and shepherds' huts who had taken Petra in when he had nothing to offer but company and good cheese-cutting.

And Corvane wanted him alive. That was the whole arithmetic of it. Alive, and unharvested, and delivered whole. Which meant that every rider out there was under orders to take him breathing—and every hour they spent hunting him was an hour their torches weren't being carried toward Ironhollow's orphans.

Unless he gave them a reason to stop hunting.

"You're making the face," Vessal said.

"What face?"

"The one you made when you fed yourself the poison in the stew pot instead of spitting it out. The one that says *I have done the sums and I hate the answer*."

Tam looked between them, and Petra watched understanding arrive on the boy's face the way frost arrives on a window—all at once, and impossible to wipe away. "No," Tam said. "No, you can't. They'll—"

"They'll take me, and they'll be so pleased with themselves that they'll forget everything else for a while." Petra kept his voice light. It was the voice he'd used at court when the dish was fine and the danger wasn't; it had never once fooled anyone worth fooling, and it didn't fool Tam now. "Corvane is a proud man, Tam. Proud men ride hard and look at nothing else. While they're looking at me—"

"I'm not going." The boy's chin came up, iron-stubborn, iron-hearing, twelve years old and furious. "You said—we're going together, you said you'd teach me the roads, you *said*—"

"And you're going to hold me to everything else I ever say, so let's start with this." Petra knelt, which put them nearly eye to eye, though Tam was growing like a spring weed and wouldn't permit that equality much longer. "There's a satchel under the third pillar. Documents. Ledgers. Corvane's own hand ordering the dosing, the harvesting, the children. If anything happens to me—if I'm very clever and it goes wrong anyway—that proof has to reach people who can use it. There's a printer in Lowmark named Odell who owes me a debt from my runner days. He owes me so many debts that he'll print anything I ask just to feel square again. You give him those papers. He prints ten thousand copies. And then the whole realm knows what the crown has been drinking."

"But—"

"You hear iron, Tam. You hear mountains. Do you know what that means?" He tapped the boy's chest, gently, right over the heart. "It means the gift isn't dying in you the way it died in most bloodlines. It means somewhere back in your family there was a bond, and the bond left an echo, and echoes are how things come back. You're not a messenger. You're a *seed*. Seeds don't stand and fight. Seeds travel."

"That's a horrible thing to call a person."

"It's the finest thing I know how to call one." He smiled, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

* * *

They argued until the hoofbeats were close enough that arguing became a luxury, and then they argued more efficiently.

Tam wanted to stay hidden and let the riders pass. Petra explained, with smuggler's patience, that Corvane's men had dogs now—the guildmaster had learned from the ford, had learned from every escape, and dogs would find the ruins within the day regardless. Hiding was no longer among the options. The options were: run east and be run down tired, or walk west with his hands open and buy the others time.

Vessal said nothing for a long while. Then she lowered her great head until her brow nearly touched his, and her breath washed over him, hot as an oven door, smelling of cinders and, beneath that, faintly, of roses.

"When I first spoke your name," she said, "I told you that names are debts. Yours was owed to you by someone dead, and I paid it forward. Now you're paying it forward too. This is what bonds are, little taster. Not the stone. Not the fire. Just this—two creatures deciding each other's debts are their own."

"If I die, does the bond die?"

"The bond," Vessal said, "is the only part of you they cannot put in a cellar."

* * *

He dressed for it the way he'd dressed for banquets. Clean shirt. Hair combed with his fingers. The vial he'd pocketed on his first day of dread, all those months ago, still sewn into his collar—he left it. No. He took it out and pressed it into Tam's hand along with the satchel.

"What is it?"

"Bitterleaf concentrate. Tastes like nothing if you're expecting wine, tastes like the end of the world if you're not. If Odell doubts your papers—which he will, because doubting is how printers stay alive—you tell him to taste it and then read page eleven, which describes exactly what the guild has been putting in my wine for nine years. He'll believe you after that." Petra closed the boy's fingers around it. "Don't drink it. Obviously."

"Obviously," Tam echoed, wet-eyed and trying to be stern about it.

They walked down together as far as the broken stair. Beyond it, the valley opened toward the western road, gray-green and indifferent, and somewhere on it, thirty riders and their patient dogs.

Petra hugged him once, briefly, the way you pick up something precious and set it down carefully. Then he stepped back before either of them could make it harder.

"Lowmark is six days if the weather's kind."

"Seven," said Tam. "The ford's been raining high. I can hear it."

"Seven days. Don't take shortcuts you haven't walked. Don't trust anyone who smiles first. And when Odell asks who sent you—" Petra paused, and found, to his surprise, that he knew the answer, had known it since a kitchen boy was dragged off a palace floor and a careful life ended. "Tell him the taster sent you. Tell him the taster finally swallowed something that matters."

Tam laughed—a cracked, terrible, wonderful sound—and ran east, small and swift as a spark off a grindstone, until the ferns took him.

Petra stood alone on the stair for exactly as long as it took to compose his face. Then he walked down into the valley, raised both hands above his head, and called out in the carrying voice of a man who had spent nine years announcing dishes to kings:

"Guildmaster Corvane! Your ledger is missing its final entry!"

* * *

They took his weapons, which took some time, because he'd hidden weapons in places that surprised even him. They bound his wrists with cord rather than iron, because Corvane's orders were explicit about the merchandise arriving undamaged, and Petra noted, professionally, that the lead rider's hands shook slightly—fear of the man they served, not of the man they'd caught.

Good. Fearful soldiers follow loud orders, and loud orders can be heard by everyone.

They rode him west all afternoon. Toward Ironhollow. Exactly as designed. He counted the riders—twenty-eight, plus two scouts ahead—and the dogs—four, miserable creatures—and the hours, and the turns of the road, building the map in his head the way he'd built immunity in his blood: patiently, dose by dose.

At dusk, the lead rider leaned close. "Guildmaster wants you comfortable," the man said, in the tone of someone repeating words he didn't believe. "Says you're to dine well tonight."

"Then I'll taste everything first," Petra said pleasantly. "Old habit."

Something flickered across the rider's face—superstition, maybe, or the memory of rumors about the taster who couldn't be poisoned. Good. Let them wonder. Let every man in this column lie awake wondering whether the food was safe, whether the water was safe, whether the quiet man with the court manners had already done something to the well. A taster's reputation was a weapon with a longer reach than any blade, and Petra intended to swing it all the way to Corvale's camp.

Because here was the sum he'd finished on the broken stair, the answer he hated: Corvane would interrogate him, and Corvane would gloat, and gloating men talk—about elixirs, about schedules, about the king's cellar—and every word spoken near a taster was a word a taster could use. He'd walked into poisonings before. He simply intended, this once, to be the poison.

Far behind him, faint as a heartbeat under a floor, he felt Vessal begin—very slowly, very stubbornly—to crawl east after the boy.

"Hold on, old mother," he murmured to the darkening hills. "I'll leave a door open."

Ahead, torchlight bloomed at the tree line, and a voice he remembered from ledgers and nightmares called his name like a man greeting a vintage finally ready for bottling.

"Master Okafor," said Guildmaster Corvane. "We have *so* much to discuss."
