## 17. The King's Cellar

They took his boots first.

Petra supposed he should have expected it. Boots had laces; laces made rope; rope made hanging. But there was something uniquely humiliating about being marched down six flights of spiral stair into the palace undercrofts in his stockings, the cold of the stone climbing through the wool like a slow gray tide.

"You could have left me the boots," he said to the guard at his elbow. "I'm a food-taster, not an acrobat."

The guard said nothing. Guards who escorted prisoners into the King's Cellar were chosen for their silence the way choirboys were chosen for their voices. This one had cheeks like ham and eyes that never moved from the middle distance, as if the middle distance might offer him a better life.

The undercrofts smelled of candlewax and cold stone and, beneath it all, something sweet and wrong — a smell Petra knew intimately, the way a widow knows her husband's cologne. Honey and iron and old blood boiled down to syrup. The smell of the guild's work. His work. Nine years of it, distilled.

His cell was not a cell. That was the first thing he understood, and it frightened him more than chains would have. It was a room with a bed, a washstand, a chair, and a table set with a single cup of water so clear it could only have come from a mountain spring. A room for a guest. A room for an asset.

On the table, beside the water, lay his own vial — the one he'd pocketed from the wine tasting, a lifetime ago, back when his biggest worry was bitterness in a Rhenish red. Empty now, of course. They'd have emptied it. But they'd kept it, and they'd placed it where he would see it, which was its own kind of message: *we know everything you know.*

Petra sat on the bed, put his stocking feet flat on the cold floor, and did what he always did. He took inventory.

Two exits: the door behind the guard, and a ventilation grate near the ceiling too narrow for anything larger than a cat or a very determined loaf of bread. One window: none. Weapons: none, unless you counted his tongue, which was honestly sharper than most things the armory issued.

Time: unknown. That was the dangerous one. In the dark under the palace, time went soft as overcooked onion.

He counted his breaths instead, four hundred of them, and then the door opened, and King Aldric came down himself.

* * *

Petra had seen the king perhaps forty times in nine years, always at distance — a figure on a balcony, a silhouette against banquet candles, a man whose soup Petra had slurped with professional detachment. Up close, the strangeness was how ordinary he looked. Gray at the temples. A crease between his brows like a man perpetually doing sums. You could have passed him in any market in Ironhollow and thought, *there goes someone's uncle, the one who haggles.*

Except for the eyes. The eyes were the eyes of a man who had stopped aging somewhere around his fiftieth year and intended to keep it that way indefinitely.

"Master Okafor." Aldric waved off the guards, and the door closed behind them with a sound like a held breath released. "Sit. Please."

"I'm more comfortable standing," Petra said. "Occupational habit. Tasters learn not to get too settled anywhere."

"Then we'll both be uncomfortable together. I find honesty begins there." The king sat in the room's single chair himself — a small courtesy, or a demonstration of power, that he needed no throne here — and studied Petra the way Petra studied unfamiliar stews. "Nine years. Do you know how many tasters survive nine years?"

"Three," Petra said. "Myself, a woman in the western court named Sef who I'm told has developed a nervous condition, and historically, no one."

Aldric laughed. It was a real laugh, warm even, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "You're funny. Corvane said you were funny. He also said you were the most valuable man in my kingdom, which is saying something, because I own a dragon's worth of mountains and a navy."

"Corvane flatters everyone he intends to bleed."

"Yes." The king's humor folded away, neat as a napkin. "He showed me the ledgers, Master Okafor. All nine years. Every measured dose. Every calculated exposure, escalated by grains, by compounds, by season. You've been given arsenic two hundred and eleven times. Wolfsbane sixty-four. Something the alchemists won't name in front of me, which means it's either obscene or expensive." He leaned forward. "You knew."

Petra considered lying. Then he considered that the man across from him drank distilled gift-blood every morning like a country squire taking cream in his tea, and decided the truth was at least interesting enough to buy time with.

"I suspected by the third year," Petra said. "Confirmed by the fifth. Nobody builds tolerance to *that many* poisons by accident. Somebody was curating my diet the way a vintner curates a vineyard." He shrugged. "I kept tasting anyway. The alternative was refusing, and refusers stop being tasters. Tasters who stop being tasters become examples."

"And you didn't run."

"Where?" Petra spread his hands. "Your face is on every coin. My face is on nothing, which is the whole point of me, but even invisible men need roads, and your guildmaster owns the tolls."

Something flickered across the king's face — not quite respect, but the shadow respect casts. "And yet you ran when the Hunt began."

"I ran when you started paying silver for names," Petra corrected gently. "There's a difference. A man can live under a bad law. He cannot live under his neighbor's arithmetic."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, the palace went about its evening — a cart in the yard, a bell, laughter from some hall where people still believed the world was shaped like a floor.

"You should know why you're here," Aldric said at last, "before Corvane comes down with his questions and his little instruments. I dismissed him for this conversation because Corvane sees a vintage where I see a man. I want to talk to the man." He rose, crossed to the door, opened it, and spoke briefly to someone outside. When he returned, he carried a tray, and on the tray was a decanter of deep garnet liquid and two glasses.

The smell reached Petra before the tray touched the table. Honey and iron and old blood. The elixir.

"Do you know what this is?"

"I know what it smells like," Petra said. "I've been smelling it for nine years without knowing why. Like meeting a stranger and realizing you've been dreaming about them."

"It is thirty-one years of my reign," Aldric said simply. "Every morning, a measure of this. Without it—" he turned the glass in his fingers, watching the light crawl through it like something alive, "—I am sixty-three years old, Master Okafor, and sixty-three arrives quickly once it starts arriving. With it, I am fifty, forever. The alchemists say the next batch will hold me at forty-eight." He smiled. "The next batch needs an ingredient. You've probably guessed."

"My blood."

"The distillation of nine years of your survival. Corvane calls it cultivation. I've asked him not to use the word around you, but Corvane has never once done anything I've asked, which is why he's useful." Aldric poured two measures and pushed one across the table toward Petra. "But here is what Corvane doesn't know I'm going to say. I don't want your blood *taken*, taster. I want your help making it. Partnership. You understand poisons and tolerances better than any alchemist in my employ — you've *lived* the curriculum. Refine the process with me. Improve the yield. And drink it alongside me."

Petra stared at him. "You're asking me to be immortal with you."

"I'm asking you to stop being *disposable*," Aldric said. "That's the whole tragedy of your profession, isn't it? You exist so that others may eat safely, and no one ever asks whether *you* are safe. I'm asking. Sit at my right hand. Taste for me forever — and be untasteable yourself. No poison will touch you. No blade, if the stories about gift-blood are true. And when I die — centuries from now, if we do this properly — the process lives on. You'll have built something. Isn't that what men like you want? To build something that outlasts the swallowing?"

It was a good speech. Petra gave it full marks. There was even truth in it, which was the best seasoning a lie could ask for. He felt the pull of it — the honest pull, the part of him that had spent nine years invisible and tired and hungry for exactly this: to matter, to be seen, to sit at a table rather than beneath it.

And underneath the pull, quiet as sediment, sat the memory of Maren burning herself out on a mountain pass so strangers could run. Of Tam's hand in his, small and iron-cold. Of Vessal's eye, vast and amber, closing like a sunset he couldn't afford to lose.

"They took a kitchen boy during the proclamation," Petra said. "Fennel. His name was Fennel. He could make bread rise by humming at it. Eleven years old." He looked up. "Was he an ingredient too? Or does the process only take adults with nine years of curated poisoning?"

The king's expression didn't change, which told Petra everything. "Collateral refinement," Aldric said. "Small gifts, small yields. Corvane manages the collection."

"There it is," Petra said softly. "Partnership with a man who calls children *collection*."

"Aldric—"

"No." Petra stood. His stocking feet made no sound on the stone, and somehow that made the word land harder. "Your Majesty. I've tasted for you for nine years. Every meal, every feast, every diplomatic visit from neighbors who'd love to see you dead. I swallowed things that would kill a cathedral. And in nine years you never once asked my name until you needed my veins." He tapped the ledger-vial on the table. "You don't want a partner. Partners have opinions. You want a cellar that talks."

For a moment — just a moment — the king looked genuinely wounded, and Petra felt the terrible old tug of sympathy for powerful sad men, and strangled it like a snake in a henhouse.

"Corvane will come tomorrow," Aldric said, rising. The warmth was gone from his voice; what remained was smooth as a frozen pond. "He'll ask his questions. Think about my offer overnight. Men in cells are very reasonable by morning."

"I'm unreasonable before breakfast," Petra agreed. "It's the hunger. Sharpens the wit."

The king paused at the door. "One more thing, taster. The dragon. Vessal. Corvane believes she lives, and the boy confirms it. My alchemists tell me a living dragon's heart would make the elixir obsolete — permanent. True immortality, not a daily ration." His eyes found Petra's, mild and bottomless. "If you know where she is, telling me tonight would go a long way toward making tomorrow unnecessary."

"She died in the mine," Petra said. "I watched her go. Very sad. Enormous funeral. You weren't invited."

Aldric held his gaze for three long breaths. Then he nodded, almost respectfully, and left.

* * *

Petra did not sleep. Sleep was for people whose plans didn't depend on the next six hours.

He sat cross-legged on the bed in the dark and let his mind do what it had been trained to do for nine years: taste everything. Every word the king had said, rolled across the palate, checked for aftertaste. The offer — genuine, mostly. The threat — genuine, entirely. The mention of Vessal — that was the poison in the dish. Not because the king wanted her heart, though he clearly did, but because of *when* he'd mentioned it. Last. After the offer. After the appeal to Petra's loneliness. A man saves his true question for last, the way a poisoner saves the bitter herb for the bottom of the cup.

Which meant the king believed Vessal lived. Which meant the riders Corvane had sent into Thornfell hadn't reported back. Which meant, somewhere north of here, a dying dragon with ruined wings was either hiding well or already moving.

*Please don't come here,* Petra thought, and then immediately, *please do come here,* and then he laughed at himself, alone in the dark, in his stockings, a prisoner arguing with his own heart. It sounded slightly unhinged. He decided to allow it.

Because here was the thing the ledgers had taught him, the thing nine years of measured doses had written into his body in letters finer than any script: *the dose makes the poison.* Arsenic in grain-measures kills. Arsenic in careful hands cures. Water kills, in quantity. Everything depends on amount, timing, and intent.

And a taster — a *good* taster — carries knowledge in his mouth the way other men carry knives in their belts.

Petra had known, since the fifth year, about a compound. Rare, expensive, untraceable — the alchemists' own darling, used in their refinements, present in trace amounts in every batch of elixir that had ever crossed his palate. He knew its taste the way he knew his mother's voice. And he knew something else, something he'd learned in a smuggler's cache two winters ago from a dying herbalist who owed him a favor: that compound had a partner. A common weed-alkaloid, bitter as regret, that slept harmlessly in the human body until it met the first compound — and then woke up *hungry*. Together they did something ugly to the liver, slowly, over weeks, in a way no physician alive could distinguish from simple royal excess.

He had carried a pinch of it in the lining of his collar for four years. Not because he'd planned anything. Because he was a taster, and a taster's creed was simple: *know what's in the cup.* And if someday the cup was aimed at you—

They'd taken his boots. They'd taken his belt, his laces, his coat buttons. They'd searched him with the thoroughness of men who feared their king's paranoia.

Nobody had cut open his collar. Why would they? It was wool. It was a collar. Collars were harmless.

Petra peeled back the stitching with his thumbnail, working by feel in the dark, and drew out a twist of waxed paper smaller than a thumbnail. Inside: a dust so fine it was almost a rumor, faintly, familiarly bitter.

He sat with it in his palm for a long moment. His heart was loud in the quiet. This was the line, and he knew it was, and he stepped over it the way you step off a riverbank — one moment solid ground, the next moment current.

"Not for myself," he murmured, to whoever kept accounts of such things. "For Fennel. For Maren. For the bread-rising boy and every name in Corvane's ledgers."

The decanter of elixir sat on the table where the king had left it, garnet-dark, patient. Tomorrow Corvane would come with his instruments, and there would be tasting — there was always tasting; the guild tested every batch against a calibrated palate, and Petra was the finest palate the guild had ever cultivated. They would hand him a measure of the new batch and watch him drink and read his face for verdicts.

He rose, padded silently to the table, unstopped the decanter, and stood over it breathing slowly, the way he did before every risky swallow. Then, with the steadiness of nine years' practice, he tipped the waxed paper and let the bitter dust fall into the king's immortality, stirring it with his smallest finger until it vanished — because it would vanish, because it was made to vanish, because the whole art of his life had been learning what disappears and what endures.

He restoppered the decanter. He returned the paper to his collar. He washed his finger at the washstand, dried it on the sheet, and got back onto the bed.

Somewhere far above, a bell rang the third hour past midnight. Somewhere north, if the ache behind his breastbone meant anything — and lately it had meant things, strange warm pulling things, like a second heartbeat tuned to a different sky — something enormous was turning its ruined body toward the capital.

"Hold on, old girl," Petra whispered to the ceiling, to the mountains, to the dark. "Don't save me. Just arrive. Arriving is enough."

He closed his eyes. For the first time in nine years, the taster slept without counting breaths — and dreamed of fire coming down out of a broken sky.
