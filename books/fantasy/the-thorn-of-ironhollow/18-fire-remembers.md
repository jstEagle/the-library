## 18. Fire Remembers

Dying, it turned out, was a great deal like sleeping in a drafty cave: mostly waiting, occasionally shivering, and full of dreams that were not quite dreams.

Vessal had done very little else for the better part of a season. She lay in the collapsed mine where the human with the careful hands had found her, her wings folded against her sides like a mourner folding their arms, and she counted the embers of herself going out one by one. There were not so many left. A dragon's fire was not a flame, exactly — it was more like a memory of the sun, kept alive in the blood by stubbornness and feeding — and hers had been starving for a hundred years. Longer. She had stopped counting when counting became an insult.

The humans called this mountain range Thornfell, which she found amusing, because she remembered when it had no name at all, only teeth of stone and the long cold rivers between them. She remembered when dragons had names for everything, because everything had belonged to us, little one, we simply hadn't gotten around to writing it down.

She did not say this aloud. Saying things aloud cost breath, and breath cost fire, and fire was the last coin in her purse.

But there was one thing she said aloud, or nearly, every day. She said it into the bond.

The bond was new. That was the strange mercy of it. Dragons had carried bonds to humans since before the first kings of Ironhollow, since the forge-cities in the deep mountains where the cracked bonding-stones still sat in their cradles of dust, waiting. The bond was how dragon-fire had kept the gift alive in human bloodlines, generation upon generation — a slow transfusion across species, warmth passed hand to hand down the centuries. And when the dragons began to die, the gifts in the bloodlines had begun to gutter too, like lamps whose oil was shared too thin.

Petra Okafor's gift had not guttered. Petra Okafor's gift had been *farmed*.

She had smelled it on him the first day, when he crawled into her ruin smelling of fear and river water and nine years of measured poison. Any dragon could have smelled it. His blood sang with compounds no body should have survived, layered like sediment, each dose a ring in a tree. Someone had been cultivating him the way orchardmen cultivate a rare fruit: patiently, annually, with an eye toward harvest.

She had told him some of this. Not all. There is a cruelty in telling a man his whole life has been someone else's garden, and Vessal was old enough to ration her cruelties.

What she had not expected — what no dragon in the old songs had managed, as far as she knew — was that the bond would take root anyway. Between a dying dragon and a man who tasted poison for his supper. The songs always had the bond forming in glory, in battle, in the shadow of burning towers. Hers had formed in a collapsed mine over the course of shared dried meat and one-sided conversation, which she supposed was fitting, since most true things in her long life had arrived without any dignity whatsoever.

Now she lay in the dark and felt him through it, the way you feel a coal through a blanket. A small warmth. Far away. Getting farther.

*Go,* she had told him, when the iron-hearing boy led them out and the choice came. *You cannot carry me and your cause both.*

He had looked at her with those taster's eyes — eyes that weighed everything, trusted nothing, and loved anyway, which was the most dangerous combination she knew of — and he had said, "I'm not carrying you. I'm coming back for you."

Humans lied beautifully. It was one of their finest qualities. But this one had not been lying, and that was worse, because now she had something to lose besides time.

* * *

The days after he left were the longest of her dying.

She passed them talking to the boy — Tam, who heard iron the way other children heard birdsong — when the boy dared come near, which grew braver by the day. He would sit just beyond the reach of her snout and ask questions in the flat, practical way of children who have already survived too much to waste breath on wonder.

"How much do you weigh?"

"More than your village."

"Could you still burn a village?"

"No." A pause. "A barn. On a dry day. If I hated the barn."

Tam had considered this seriously and then asked whether she hated any barns in particular, and Vessal had discovered that a dying dragon can laugh, if the joke is small enough. Laughing cost almost nothing. She resolved to do more of it.

But at night, when the boy slept curled against her foreleg for the warmth — and she gave him what warmth she had, deliberately, spending coin she could not spare, because what else is warmth *for* — she reached down the bond toward Petra and listened.

The bond was thin. Nascent, the hearth-witch Maren would have called it, before she burned herself to ash holding a pass so that children could run. Vessal had felt that death too, distantly, a flare of gift-fire spent all at once, generous and terrible. Humans burned so fast. It was why dragons had learned to love them slowly.

Through the thin thread she caught fragments. A road. A decision made in a cellar of documents — she felt the shape of his fury even without words, a fury with architecture, the kind that builds rather than burns. Then distance, and distance, and then a wrongness.

She woke from a shallow death-dream to find the thread pulled taut.

Not broken. Taut. Like a fishing line with something heavy on the end.

* * *

Here is what a nascent bond feels like from the dragon's side, for those keeping records, as humans love to do:

It feels like a second heartbeat that is not yours. It feels like knowing, without being told, that somewhere a person has just made a decision that will kill him, and has made it calmly, the way he does everything, with those careful hands.

Vessal felt it in the hour before dawn, in the mine, with Tam's small weight against her leg and the last of her fire banked down to a sullen red glow behind her breastbone.

She felt Petra walk into a room underground. She felt him pick up a cup.

And she felt him — this was the part that made her open her eyes, this was the part that rang through the thread like a bell struck with a hammer — she felt him *choose* to put something in the cup. Deliberately. With a taster's precision. Using the one poison his nine years of immunity had taught him to carry invisibly in his own blood and sweat, the way some flowers carry their defense in their perfume.

Sacrifice, answering sacrifice. That was the oldest pattern in the world, older than dragons. The hearth-witch had spent herself for strangers; now the taster spent himself for a realm that had never once seen him. And Vessal lay in the dark and understood, with the terrible clarity of the dying, that she was the third line of the pattern, and the pattern was waiting for her.

*No,* she told the bond, or tried to. *No, you foolish, brave, insufferably methodical man, I am the one who is already dying, the arithmetic is not difficult—*

The bond did not answer. Bonds did not carry words well yet; they carried weather. What came back to her was not argument but sensation: a banquet hall being prepared above his head, candle-smoke and roasting meat and the sour excitement of a court that smells a spectacle. And beneath it, steady as a metronome, the taste of the tainted elixir on his own tongue — he had sampled it, of course he had sampled it, the man could not help himself — and beneath *that*, quiet and unhidden because he wanted her to feel it, his farewell.

He was not planning to survive the night. He had known the odds when he walked in. He had walked in anyway, because Corvane would otherwise turn his riders back toward Ironhollow, toward Tam, toward the survivors, toward everyone Petra had finally, at ruinous cost, decided belonged to him.

Vessal lay still for a long moment.

Then she laughed — a real laugh, deep in the ruined bellows of her chest, which hurt, which she let hurt — because of course. Of *course*. Nine years swallowing poison in measured doses, and the man's final act was to measure one last dose, for a king. He had turned the guild's own vintage against its cellar-master. He had made his body, that cultivated field, into a weapon at the moment of harvest. Somewhere, Guildmaster Corvane was about to learn what every farmer eventually learns: that crops remember being planted.

"Tam," she said. Her voice came out like a landslide remembering it used to be a mountain. "Wake up, little iron-ear. I need your legs and your ears, and I need them now."

* * *

Getting out of the mine was the first miracle, and miracles, Vessal had found, were mostly just stubbornness wearing a good coat.

Her wings would not open properly. One of them had been ruined since the fall of the forge-cities — torn in the wars men had started because they feared what they themselves had asked for — and the membrane had healed crooked, scarred into ridges like old wax. The other wing worked, mostly, in the way that a door works when the hinges are rusted: with complaint.

But wings, she reminded herself, were not for walking. And she had walked before. Dragons had crossed the world on foot in the lean centuries, heads low, pride lower, eating what could be eaten and sleeping in ditches that had once been valleys. Dignity was a young creature's concern.

So she walked. Out of the mine, down the scree, through the treeline where the thorn roses grew wild and vicious along the old smuggler paths — Petra's paths, she realized; the man had mapped these hills foot by foot, and though he was a hundred miles away and walking into a cell, his maps walked with her, in Tam's head, in the boy's uncanny sense for stone and iron and the ways through.

Tam ran ahead and listened to the mountains. The mountains, apparently, answered.

"There's a pass," the boy said, pointing with the absolute confidence of the very young. "North-east. It drops to the river road. The river road goes to the capital."

"The river road," Vessal said, "is where men with crossbows wait for large slow targets."

"Is there another way?"

"There is always another way. That is the first law of staying alive, and the second law is that the other way is always worse."

Tam grinned, which Vessal chose to interpret as agreement.

They went by night. She moved at the speed of grief, which is faster than the speed of strength and leaves you far more damaged. By the third night her feet were split, her breathing sounded like a smithy with the bellows failing, and the glow behind her breastbone had dimmed to something you would have mistaken for a dying cookfire if you were feeling charitable.

By the fifth night, she could smell the capital.

Cities have a smell. This one smelled of woodsmoke and horse and sewage and ambition, and under all of it, faintly, a scent she knew from a century ago: the alchemical reek of the guild-houses, where they boiled gift-blood down into gold-tasting lies for a king's cup. The smell had not changed. Kings rarely did.

And through the bond, closer now, she felt Petra — alive, still alive, held somewhere below ground, in a cellar, among barrels. Waiting. Stalling. Being, with magnificent patience, the most inconvenient prisoner in the history of the realm.

*Hold on,* she pushed down the thread, not words, just heat, just the image of her own fire, banked and waiting. *Hold on, taster. I am slow, but I am coming, and I have never once in four hundred years arrived politely.*

* * *

The last obstacle was the city itself, and the last problem was the sky.

She had planned to approach at night, low, over the river, where the watchtowers faced outward toward enemies with armies rather than upward toward one exhausted lizard with a grudge. It was a good plan. It failed immediately, because her body had opinions.

She launched from the ridge above the river road at moonset, and for eleven glorious seconds she flew.

Eleven seconds. She counted them. The ruined wing tore along its oldest scar on the third second; the air came through the wound with a sound like tearing linen; and the ground rose up with the patient certainty of everything in this world that waits for dragons to finish falling.

She did not crash so much as arrive violently, plowing a furrow through a willow stand at the river's edge two miles downstream of the city walls, flattening a fisherman's shed, and coming to rest nose-first in the mud with all the majesty of a shipwreck.

The fisherman, to his eternal credit, did not scream. He stood in the shallows holding his lantern and staring, and after a long moment he said, in the tone of a man negotiating with fate, "You'll be paying for that shed."

"I'll be paying for the shed," Vessal agreed, wheezing.

"And you're the dragon. From the songs. The one the king wants."

"I am the dragon the king wants," she confirmed. "Everyone wants something. It's the great flaw of your species."

"He's dying," the fisherman said suddenly, and his voice changed. "In the palace. The king. They rang the alarm bells an hour past — sickness, they're saying. Poison, others are saying. There are soldiers everywhere and nobody knows who to point their spears at."

Through the bond, Vessal felt it land: the cup, raised; the toast; the swallow. Felt the elixir go into Aldric like winter going into a river, felt Petra's controlled heartbeat spike once — *it's working* — and then steady again into that unbearable calm.

Alarms rising. Bells over the city like a heart in fibrillation. And somewhere beneath it all, a taster in a cellar, buying minutes with his composure, spending them one by one on a realm that didn't yet know his name.

Vessal got her feet under her. It took three tries. The willow grove was ruined around her, and the mud stank, and her wings hung like wet flags, and none of it mattered, because she could see the palace now over the rooftops — candles in a thousand windows, torchlight boiling in the square, tiny figures running with the purposeful panic of people whose certainties are dying faster than their king.

One flight. That was all she needed. Not a flight of the sky — her sky-flying days were spent, sold off second by second across four hundred years. A flight of the old kind. The kind dragons made once, at the forging of the bonds, when a dragon gave its fire to a human line and asked nothing back except remembrance.

*Fire remembers,* her mother had told her, in the last summer of the old world. *Fire is the only thing that remembers everything, because it is in everything, waiting.*

"Tam," she said. The boy was already scrambling up onto her shoulder, finding the grip-points between her scales as if he'd been born to it, which, she supposed, in the way of bonded lines, he half had. "When we get there, whatever happens — get the stone to him. The bonding-stone fragment. You know which pocket."

"You're going to give him your fire." It wasn't a question. Children who hear iron hear everything.

"I'm going to try to give him my fire. Whether it works depends on him. It always depended on him." She gathered what remained — the last coins, the last embers, the whole poor treasury of her long diminishing life — and held it behind her breastbone like a breath she refused to release until the right moment. "Now hold on, little one. And if we die, aim for something expensive."

She hauled herself up the riverbank, planted her ruined feet in the mud of the world, and threw herself at the burning city — one last, ragged, impossible arc, wings tearing, alarms ringing, the bond pulling her forward like a hook of light —

— and below her, in the square, the crowd looked up, and pointed, and began, in thousands of voices at once, to scream her name.
