## 19. The Last Toast

They dressed me for the occasion.

I want that understood, because it matters: they *dressed* me. A taster is furniture. Furniture does not get a doublet of dove-grey silk with silver thread at the collar, or a barber who smells of rosewater and fear, or a guard outside the door who will not meet my eyes because he has been told what I am now and cannot decide whether to be disgusted or afraid.

"His Majesty wishes you presentable," the barber said, working a comb through hair he seemed surprised to find clean.

"He wishes me *visible*," I said. "There's a difference."

The barber said nothing more. Smart man. The smart ones had all stopped talking around me since Corvane's riders brought me back through the palace gates in an unmarked cart, wrists bound in silk so as not to bruise anything the elixir-masters might need. I had counted four days in the undercrofts. Four days of Aldric visiting me like a man visiting a vineyard, running his fingers along the barrels of my life, murmuring about vintages and harvests and how remarkable it was, Petra, truly remarkable, that you never suspected.

But I had suspected. That was the whole art of it. And suspicion, I had learned in nine years at the tasting table, was mostly a matter of patience.

Tonight was the Feast of the Long Reign. Forty-one years of King Aldric the Steadfast, and every noble house in Ironhollow crammed into the Grand Hall beneath banners gone soft with age, eating food I would not be asked to taste.

That was my first clue that something was wrong.

* * *

The hall smelled of roast swan and beeswax and too many bodies in wool. I stood at the king's right hand — *stood*, where a taster should have been seated behind him at the low table with the other servants — and Aldric introduced me, twice, to men who looked at me the way farmers look at a bull with good lines.

"Our finest instrument," the king said, hand heavy on my shoulder. "Nine years in my service. There is no poison in the realm his blood cannot master."

"There are several," I said mildly.

Aldric laughed. It was a good laugh; I'll grant him that. It had carried him through three wars and a famine and the quiet disappearance of perhaps two hundred gift-born children, and it made everyone within earshot feel that whatever came next would surely be reasonable.

"You see?" he said to Lord Vessany of the Marches. "Modest, too. Corvane, you've trained him well."

Guildmaster Corvane stood across the table, grey as wet slate, hands folded. He had not smiled once all evening. He had been watching me drink water. Just water — I'd insisted on it, playing the humble servant — but each cup I raised, his eyes tracked it the way a moneylender tracks a debtor leaving town.

"The guild trains obedience," Corvane said. "Everything else is the crown's property."

"*Our* property," Aldric corrected pleasantly. "We are one body, Corvane, you and I." He turned back to me, and for a moment the warmth drained out of his face and left something older underneath, something patient and hungry, a face I imagined had watched children being carried out of villages while it thought about breakfast. "After the toast, Petra, you'll come below with me. There's work tonight. The new batch is ready, and this time—" he squeezed my shoulder, almost tenderly— "this time we take the full measure."

The hall roared with some joke at the far end. Somewhere a fiddler tuned. My heart was doing its work quietly, the way I'd taught it, beat after beat after beat, no fuss, because panic is just another flavor and a taster learns to swallow them all.

"Of course, Your Majesty," I said. "Though I'd advise against the batch."

His eyebrows rose. "Oh?"

"I tasted it yesterday. In the cellar. You had me verify the draw from the cask." This was true, in the way that all my best lies were true — built on real stones, mortared with detail. "There's a bitterness in the finish. Faint. Beneath the iron and the honey."

"A bitterness," Aldric repeated.

"From the last harvest," I said. "The girl from Saltmarsh. Her blood carried something — grief does strange things to the humors, Your Majesty knows this — and when it met the preservative salts, it soured. Slowly. Over days. By tomorrow the whole cask will taste of it. Tonight it will only be at the finish." I paused, and let my eyes drop, the perfect picture of a servant afraid to overstep. "You may find it... unpleasant."

Silence, a long one. Corvane had gone very still, and stillness, from a man like Corvane, was a scream.

"You tasted it," Corvane said carefully, "yesterday. In the cellar. Alone?"

"With the cellar-keeper present until you dismissed him, Guildmaster. As your own protocol requires." I spread my hands. "I am obedient. Ask anyone. Ask the ledgers."

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes — the particular horror of a meticulous man realizing that meticulousness can be turned, like any blade, by whoever holds the handle. Nine years of records proving I drank everything set before me. Nine years of proof that I could be trusted to taste.

He knew, then. Or he knew enough. But there were four hundred nobles in the room, and the toast was already being called for, and Aldric — vain, ancient, forty-one years hungry — wanted his moment.

"To the Long Reign," the herald cried, and four hundred cups rose like a field of silver wheat.

Aldric lifted the small black crystal goblet that only he ever touched. I had poisoned it myself, eleven hours ago, with the one compound nine years of immunity had taught me to carry in my own blood and sweat — a taster's trick older than the guild, the last lesson my mother's letters ever taught me before I burned them: *the body keeps what you feed it, little fox, so feed it carefully.*

He drank it down like a man who had been drinking forever. Because he had.

"To the Long Reign," I echoed, raising water, and smiled at him with everything I had, which after nine years of smiling was quite a lot.

* * *

It took time. Good poison always does; the amateur poisons kill, and the professional ones merely *begin*.

Aldric toasted the army. He toasted the guild. He toasted his dead queen, and his voice cracked on her name in a way that even now I couldn't entirely hate him for — monsters are still people; that's the trap of them. He ate three courses, laughing, gesturing, and I stood at his elbow cataloguing the signs the way I'd once catalogued arsenic in a consignment of plums: the sheen of sweat at the temple, slightly early. The way he pressed two fingers to his wrist between courses, counting, and frowned, and stopped frowning when he caught me watching.

By the fish course, the hall had noticed nothing, but Corvane had moved.

He drifted around the table's curve like weather coming in, and when he reached my side he did not look at me. He looked straight ahead, lips barely moving, voice pitched for me alone beneath the fiddles.

"What did you use?"

"You'll want to write it down," I said. "You're the meticulous one."

"Petra." My name in his mouth sounded like an entry in a ledger. "Whatever you imagine this gains you—"

"It gains me a court," I said. "Four hundred witnesses. You taught me that numbers protect. Every dose you logged, every child you weighed and bled and entered in your neat little columns — you kept records because records are power. Well." I lifted my water cup in a small salute. "I read very well, Guildmaster. Tam found your archives. Every ledger is already out of Ironhollow and into hands you don't control. So the question isn't what this gains me. It's whether you'd like to hear the list now, or wait for the trial."

For the first time in nine years, I saw Guildmaster Corvane afraid.

It was not edifying. It was not even satisfying, really — it was small, the fear, mean and cramped, a man discovering the cage he'd built had been measured to himself. He glanced at the king, flushed and laughing and beginning, very faintly, to sweat. He glanced at the doors, where his own guild-guards stood. He did arithmetic. I watched him do it, and I knew the answer before he did, because I had done the same sums every day for nine years at that table: *what survives, what serves, what can be sacrificed.*

Corvane chose sacrifice. He always had. It was practically his signature.

"Lords and ladies!" His voice cracked across the hall like a whip, and the music died. "Forgive the interruption. There has been — an incident. This man—" his finger came around and fixed on me like a compass needle finding north— "is a gift-bearer. An unregistered one, harbored for years under guild supervision, and tonight he has committed treason against the crown. Seize him!"

And here is the thing I had counted on, the thing I'd bet nine invisible years upon: nobody moved.

Because a taster is furniture, and nobody fears furniture. Because Corvane's guards were guild-men, and half the nobility in that hall owed the guild money, grudges, or silence, and none of them loved it. Because the king — the king was mopping his brow with a napkin now, and frowning at it, and the frown had confusion in it, and confusion is contagious at banquets.

"Seize him," Corvane said again, sharper.

"Guildmaster," said old Lady Vessany, dry as August, "the king looks unwell. Perhaps before we arrest anyone, someone might call a physician."

"He is *poisoned*," Corvane snapped, and heard himself, and stopped, and I watched the sentence land in four hundred minds at once like a dropped tray.

Poisoned. At the royal banquet. Poisoned how? By whom? And why would the guildmaster know—

"He means," I said, and my voice carried further than I expected, further than any voice I'd owned in nine years, "that the king drinks refined gift-blood. Every night. Has for six years. Harvested from children the Hunt took from your villages. I know because I am the harvest. Nine years of dosing, gentlemen, ladies, meticulously recorded — shall I recite my own ledger? Shall I start with the girl from Saltmarsh, aged eleven, who yielded four pints before her heart gave out? Or the boy they called Whistler, whose gift was making bread rise, and who was worth less than a barrel of wine on Corvane's books, item thirty-seven, column six?"

The silence was total. Even the fire in the great hearth seemed to lean in.

"You're lying," Corvane said, but softly now, because loud lies die fast in big rooms.

"Search my cell below," I said. "Search the guild archive. Search *your memory*, my lord Duke — didn't your granddaughter go missing at Midwinter fair, three years past? Didn't the criers tell you she'd run off with a tinker?" I let that sit. "She didn't run anywhere. She was weighed."

Aldric stood up.

He stood up too fast, and the hall saw it — saw the greatest steadiness in the realm sway like a mast in weather — and he gripped the table and smiled his good smile and said, magnificently, "The taster has taken ill. Imagination. A servant's fancy. Someone take him—"

And then he stopped, mid-word, because something was happening inside him that he had never once in forty-one years allowed to happen in public, and his face went white as plate, and he sat back down without deciding to.

Guards wavered. I watched it happen along the walls — crowns on one side, truth on the other, spears shifting in unsure grips, young faces looking at old officers and old officers looking at the king and finding, where a kingdom used to be, only a sick old man sweating over his wine.

Corvane made his move then. Of course he did. He drew the slim guild-knife — the one we used for opening veins, I knew its shape better than my own — and he came around the table at me, quick for a grey man, and said, almost gently, the words meant only for me:

"The vintage spoils either way, Petra. Come below. We'll finish you first and sort the rest after."

"No," I said.

It's a short word. I'd been practicing it for nine years without knowing. I stepped back exactly one pace — the pace every taster learns, the distance that puts the table between you and a panicking chef — and the guild-knife meant for my throat instead opened the air where I'd been, and four hundred nobles gasped in unison, and somewhere in that gasp the spell of the crown broke for good, because everyone in that hall had spent their lives pretending not to see things, and here at last was a thing too large to pretend around.

"Guards," said Lady Vessany, rising, "arrest the *guildmaster*."

And they did. Some of them. While others hesitated, and one young spearman actually took a step toward me and then toward Corvane and then simply sat down on the dais steps, overwhelmed, head in his hands — and I understood him completely. I had been him for nine years. Choosing is a skill, and most people never get to practice.

Above it all, Aldric slid slowly from his chair, clutching the black goblet, and the court physician came running, and the king's eyes found mine across the chaos — furious, disbelieving, betrayed, as though *he* were the injured party, as though nine years of my swallowed doses had been a friendship — and he mouthed one word at me.

*Why?*

I held his gaze, and I gave him the only honest answer I had.

"You kept ledgers," I said. "I read them."

Then the windows exploded inward.

Not all of them. Just the tall eastern row, glass raining across the flagstones in a glittering tide — and through the gap, blotting out the stars, came a shape out of the oldest songs, wings torn and smoking, dim as an ember wrapped in ash, dragging herself through the night sky on sheer refusal alone.

The hall screamed. The guards wavered anew between every fear at once. Corvane twisted free of two soldiers, eyes wide, staring up at the impossible silhouette and whispering, "*a dragon*, they said it was dead, they said—"

Vessal.

Dying, burning-out, ruined Vessal, come across half the realm on wings that should have failed her — because she felt it through our bond, the way I'd known she would, the moment the poison left my hands and did its work. She had come to finish what I started. Or to save me from what came after. With Vessal those had always been the same gesture.

She wheeled once above the palace — a slow, terrible, laboring turn, trailing sparks like a dying bonfire — and began to fall toward the square below, toward the crowd, toward the bonding-stone fragment that Tam carried in his pack somewhere in that screaming dark.

I ran for the broken windows.

Behind me, the King of Ironhollow convulsed once in his chair, and the last toast of the Long Reign ended the only way it was ever going to end.
