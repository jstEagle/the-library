## 20. Dragonfire Reborn

*Vessal*

The sky had forgotten her.

That was the cruelest part of dying, Vessal thought as she beat her ruined wings against the cold morning air above the capital. Not the pain—pain was an old companion, older than the city below, older than the king who had drunk a century of stolen fire. The cruelty was that the sky no longer remembered how to hold her. Every stroke of her wings cost her something she could not name, some last coin from a purse long emptied, and the wind passed through her membranes like she was already smoke.

Below her, Ironhollow's capital sprawled in its winter grays, all slate roofs and chimney smoke and ten thousand small lives going about their small mornings. Somewhere beneath that sprawl, in a cellar that smelled of dust and old gold, a boy was doing something reckless with poison and a wineglass. She felt it through the bond—the thread between them, thin as spider silk, trembling like a plucked string.

*Now,* the thread said. Not in words. In urgency.

Vessal had not answered a summons in two hundred years. The last one had come from a bonded rider whose name history had misplaced, calling her to a war that had eaten everything, gifts and dragons and trust alike. She had sworn never again.

She banked toward the palace anyway.

Her left wing tore at the second descent. She felt it happen—a wet, ripping sound inside her own body—and then she was falling more than flying, the palace square rushing up at her like an open mouth. She saw faces turning upward. She heard screaming begin, that particular human scream that means *the world has broken open*, and she thought, with a flicker of her ancient dry humor: *Good. Let them scream. They have been quiet too long about what is done to their children.*

She hit the flagstones on her breastbone. The square cracked beneath her like a dropped plate.

For a long moment there was only dust, and the ringing in her skull, and the taste of blood—her own, copper and old magic, pooling under her chin. She tried to lift her head. She managed half of it.

Through the settling dust she saw them coming out of the palace doors: guards with spears, courtiers pressed against windows, and at the top of the great stair, a king swaying on his feet with his hand pressed to his belly, his face gone the color of tallow.

And running across the square, small and fast and carrying something over his shoulder like a sack of turnips—a boy. Tam. The iron-hearing boy, whose footsteps rang in stone the way bells ring in bronze. He was heading straight for her.

*The stone,* the bond-thread said, fainter now, fraying. *He has the stone.*

Vessal laid her head down on the cracked flagstones and waited to learn whether she had crawled across a kingdom to die in front of strangers, or to live in front of them.

She found, to her surprise, that either would do. That was what giving your last strength for someone bought you: a strange lightness. A willingness.

* * *

*Petra*

Petra Okafor had spent nine years learning exactly how much poison a body could carry, and he had never once imagined the answer would be *all of it, if you carry it for someone else.*

He stood at the edge of the square with Tam's bonding-stone fragment under his arm—heavy, cold, humming faintly the way a struck bell hums after the sound stops—and watched Aldric die by inches at the top of his own stairs.

It should have felt like triumph. It did not. It felt like the tasting spoon: necessary, bitter, and leaving a film on the tongue that no amount of water would wash away.

The elixir had been Aldric's toast. *To a hundred more years,* he'd said, lifting the golden cup before the assembled court, and Petra had stood at his elbow in his plain gray taster's coat and said nothing, which was the most eloquent speech of his life. Corvane had been three steps away, close enough that Petra could smell the ink on his hands. For one terrible heartbeat, the guildmaster's eyes had met Petra's—and Petra had seen the exact moment Corvane understood. The vial. The batch Petra had tasted and approved. The taster's art turned, at last, to weapon.

Corvane had lunged for him then, shouting something about treason, but the king had begun to scream before the guards could move, and screaming kings outrank shouting guildmasters. By the time the court sorted itself into factions—the loyal, the terrified, and the opportunistic—Petra was out the servants' door with a fragment of ancient stone under his arm and Tam waiting in the square below with a plan the boy had heard in the mountains themselves.

Now Aldric collapsed against the balustrade, and the court's screams changed key—from outrage to something older and less certain. Kings were not supposed to sicken. Kings were supposed to be the one body in the realm that death had to negotiate with.

"Petra!" Tam's voice cut across the square. The boy had reached Vessal's side and was standing on her foreleg like it was a dock, waving both arms. "The stone! She says it needs blood given, not taken! She says—" Tam cocked his head, listening to something no one else could hear, and his face went strange and bright. "She says it always needed that. That's why it broke. Everyone forgot."

Everyone forgot. Nine years of ledgers, nine years of measured doses, an entire guild built on the principle that gift-blood was a thing you harvested from the unwilling—and the whole rotten architecture rested on forgetting the first lesson, the one carved into a cracked stone in a dead forge-city: *freely given.*

Petra walked into the square.

Guards watched him go. Some raised spears; none lowered them, but none advanced either. Later, Petra would learn that half the palace guard had brothers or daughters or neighbors on the Hunt's lists. For now he simply walked, gray-coated and unremarkable, the invisible man made visible by the size of what he carried.

Vessal's eye tracked him as he approached. It was the color of a forest pool in autumn, deep and still and ringed with gold, and it held him the way his mother's eyes used to hold him when she was deciding whether to scold or laugh.

"You came," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "You shouldn't have flown. You're—"

*Dying,* her voice said, not through ears but directly into the marrow of him, vast and slow as weather. *Yes. I have been dying since before your grandfather's grandfather learned to fear me. But I am not dead, little taster, and neither are you, and there is a stone in your arms that remembers what we both forgot.*

Tam had set the fragment down against her foreleg. Up close, Petra could see the carvings worn nearly smooth—a human hand, a dragon claw, and between them, in the old script Maren had taught him to read in another lifetime, a single word repeated like a heartbeat: *given. given. given.*

"The elixir," Petra said. "What I put in it. It's working because my blood is in the base. My gift-blood. The corruption will kill him, but—" He swallowed. "When he dies, they'll burn every record, kill every witness, and start again. With someone else. With some other village."

*Then we must give them something else to look at,* Vessal said.

And Petra understood.

"It'll take my immunity," he whispered. "All of it. Everything the guild built in me. If I pour it out—"

*Yes.* The great eye blinked, slow as a season changing. *You will be able to be poisoned like any ordinary man. You will be able to be hurt, and age, and die. You will be, at last, only yourself.* A pause, and something moved through the bond that Petra had no word for—tenderness, maybe, or its dragon equivalent, which might have been the same thing. *I know what they took from you for nine years. I am asking for the rest. And I am asking, not taking. That is the whole difference between us and them. That is the entire point.*

Petra looked up at the stairs, where Aldric had slid down to sit on marble steps built by men who had believed in dragons once. He looked at the crowd pressing against the palace gates—servants, cooks, a washerwoman he recognized from the laundry yards, faces he had served beside and never truly seen. He looked at Tam, who was watching him with absolute, uncomplicated faith, the way only children and dogs can manage.

His mother had written, in the letters he burned: *A gift hoarded is just weight. A gift given is wings.*

He knelt at the stone and drew the small knife from his boot—the one he'd used for nine years to prick his thumb before every tasting, the guild's ritual, the guild's leash. He set his palm flat against the bonding-stone fragment.

"For Ironhollow," he said quietly. "For Maren. For the kitchen boy they dragged away in front of me while I kept my head down." His voice steadied. "And for you," he told Vessal, "because someone should give you something freely, after all this time."

He cut, and gave.

* * *

*Tam*

Tam heard it before anyone.

He heard it the way he heard everything—not with his ears, which were ordinary enough, but with the iron in the earth and the iron in the stone and the iron in the blood of everyone standing near him. What he heard was a bell. An enormous bell, buried for centuries, and just now beginning to swing.

The stone drank Petra's blood and rang.

Not aloud—or yes, aloud too, a tone so low Tam felt it in his teeth and his toes and the fillings in his teeth—but mostly in that other hearing, the one that lived in metal and memory. Light came up out of the cracks in the fragment like dawn through shutters, gold shot through with green, and it ran along the ground in veins, following the old roads the forge-city's builders had laid, reaching for—

Reaching for Vessal.

The dragon inhaled. Tam had never heard anything inhale like that—it was a landslide in reverse, it was the square itself drawing breath—and then the light poured off the stone and into her, into the seams of her scales, into the hollow places where fire had guttered down to ash, and Vessal *burned*.

Not the sickly ember-glow Tam had seen in the mine. Fire. True fire, white-hearted and gold-edged, roaring up her throat and out into the winter air in a column that turned the falling ash to sparks and lit every window in the palace like festival lanterns. The heat washed over Tam's face and he laughed, actually laughed, because it didn't burn—it warmed, it *blessed*, it was the hearth-fire feeling of Maren's kitchen multiplied until it filled the sky.

Above them, the bell-note climbed and broke apart into a thousand smaller notes, and Tam's hearing went wide open, wider than it had ever been, and he heard the strangest thing of all:

He heard the gift moving through the crowd.

It went where the light went. The washerwoman by the gate suddenly pressed her hands to her mouth as steam rose gently from her wash-water, boiling without flame. A guard dropped his spear and stared at his own palms, where tiny motes of light gathered like fireflies. A child on her father's shoulders began, very softly, to sing in a language nobody had spoken in two hundred years—and everybody somehow understood.

The gift, freed from its ledgers and its vials and its harvested children, pouring back into the people it had always belonged to.

"Look," Tam said, tugging Petra's sleeve. "Look what you did."

But Petra wasn't looking at the crowd. He was looking at his own hand, at the shallow cut already closing, at the faint green shimmer fading from beneath his skin like a tide going out. He looked—Tam searched for the word—*lighter*. Like a man setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing.

"I can feel it leaving," Petra said. His voice wobbled and he didn't try to hide it. "Nine years of poison, and it's just... going home."

*Not leaving,* Vessal rumbled, her throat still glowing. *Rooting. What was hoarded in one man becomes a garden in ten thousand. That was always the shape of it. That is why dragons and humans were bonded, once—to keep the giving flowing both ways.* Her great head swung around, and her autumn-pool eye fixed on Tam with unnerving precision. *Which brings me to you, small bell-ears.*

"Me?" Tam squeaked.

*You heard the stone before it woke. You heard the mountain paths when no map remained. You will hear much more, I think, in the years to come—if you are willing.*

Tam looked at Petra. Petra looked at Tam. Somewhere behind them, the king's chamberlain was shouting for order and getting none, and somewhere above them Vessal stretched her wings—whole now, blazing at the edges—and the shadow she cast over the square was, for the first time in living memory, a shadow people ran *toward* instead of away from.

"Well," Petra said, and there was that dry note Tam loved, the one that meant he was terrified and delighted in equal measure. "I suppose someone has to teach the first class."

* * *

*Vessal*

Fire, Vessal discovered, remembered everything.

It remembered the forge-cities and the bonded riders and the long bright centuries before the harvesting began. It remembered her name in the mouths of people who loved her. And now, coursing renewed through her ancient body in the middle of a human square, it remembered something new: a gray-coated man kneeling at a broken stone, opening his veins without being asked, and meaning it.

She rose.

It took effort—she was old, and even reborn fire could not make her young—but she rose, wingbeat by deliberate wingbeat, until she hung over the capital like a second sun, and every face below turned up to her. Ten thousand upturned faces. She had seen crowds flee from that shadow for two hundred years. This was the first time she had ever seen one *gather* beneath it.

On the palace steps, the king was dead. She could smell it—the elixir's corruption finishing what pride had started—and beside the body stood the guildmaster, Corvane, pale as paper, backing toward the doors with his ledgers' worth of sins finally visible on his face.

Vessal let the silence stretch. Then she spoke, not to Corvane but to all of them, in a voice that rattled every window in the city:

"People of Ironhollow. Your king drank nine years of stolen children. Ask your taster what else the ledgers say."

Below, a gray figure stepped forward onto the lowest step of the palace stair, into the full light, in front of everyone—an invisible man choosing, at last, to be seen. Beside Vessal's shadow, Tam whooped like a festival drummer, and somewhere in the crowd a woman's voice began to sing the old song, the bonding-song, and the crowd caught it like fire catches dry grass.

Vessal folded her wings and descended, slowly, to land among them—not above them, *among* them—for the first time in two hundred years.

The age of hiding, she decided, was over.
