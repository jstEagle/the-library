## 21. Ashes of the Ledger

The trial of Guildmaster Corvane was held in the market square of Ironhollow, because Petra insisted it be held where the ledgers had done their accounting.

Not in the palace undercrofts, where Aldric had kept his cellar of stolen vintages. Not in the capital's high court, with its wigs and its Latin and its cushions for the important. Here. In the square where criers had once read out the Gift-Hunt proclamation, offering silver for names. The same flagstones. The same cold wind off the mountains. Different words now, though the format was grimly familiar: a list of names, read aloud.

It took three days to read them all.

Petra stood at the front through every one of them, because someone had to, and because he had discovered that his body no longer knew how to be invisible. Nine years of practice, undone by one public testimony and one dying dragon crashing into a royal square. People looked at him now the way they'd once looked at the king — waiting to see what he would do next — and he found it exhausting in a way that poison had never been.

Corvane sat in a chair on the guildhouse steps, wrists bound with plain rope, because Maren's daughter's friend — a girl named Odile who had survived the pass Maren burned herself out holding — had refused to let them use iron shackles. "We're not him," she said. "We don't take people's hands from them before we've even heard what they did."

Corvane heard what they did. All three days of it.

He listened the way he must have listened to nine years of dosing records: perfectly still, gray-faced, taking nothing personally. That was the horror of him, Petra thought. He wasn't a monster in any shape storybooks prepared you for. He was a man with excellent posture and a tidy mind, who had once said *your body is a vintage* in the tone other men used for discussing weather.

On the second day, they read the entry for a boy called Fen. Six years old. Taken from a farmstead near Saltmere. Dosed for four months. Died in the undercrofts when the harvest went wrong, which in Corvane's ledger meant only that the yield was insufficient, noted in a margin in a small, patient hand.

Fen's mother had come all the way from Saltmere. She was a small woman with sun-cracked hands, and when her turn came she didn't weep or curse. She walked up to the steps, looked at Corvane for a long moment, and asked him one question.

"Did you taste anything you made us drink?"

The square went very quiet.

"No," Corvane said.

"Nine years," she said, to Petra, not to Corvane. "They made him swallow it all. And this man never tasted a drop." She turned back. "That's your whole crime, isn't it? You never once thought anything should go into your own mouth."

She walked away before anyone could decide whether she'd asked a question or delivered a sentence, and Corvane sat there with his excellent posture, and Petra watched something move behind his face that might have been the first crack in nine years of bookkeeping.

* * *

The verdict took less time than the reading.

The council that judged him was drawn by lot from the survivors and the villagers and even, at Tam's stubborn insistence, two iron-hearing boys from the forge-city ruins, who arrived with soot on their faces and opinions about justice that sounded suspiciously like things Tam had told them. There was no king to pronounce judgment. Aldric was eleven days dead, and the realm was discovering, with the tentative wonder of a bruised animal testing its legs, that it could govern itself without drinking anyone.

They sentenced Corvane to the rest of his life in Ironhollow.

Not a cell. Ironhollow had no cells, and the council voted down the proposal to build one — Odile again, arguing that a village that starts building cages has already lost the argument. Instead, Corvane would live among the people whose names filled his ledgers. He would carry water. He would dig graves in winter. He would sit in the square every morning while the roll of the taken was read, until every name had been read so many times that the town grew sick of it, and then he would keep sitting anyway.

"You could hang me," Corvane said, when it was pronounced. He said it without drama, the way he'd said everything. "It would be cleaner."

"It would be shorter," said Odile. "We're not interested in short."

Later, helping load the cart that would take him to his lodging — an actual lodging, with a window, because the council was like that — Corvane paused beside Petra and spoke quietly enough that only he could hear.

"You understand I would have harvested you anyway. Even after everything. The bond changes nothing about the value of your blood."

"I know," Petra said.

"And yet you're leaving me alive."

"You're the only person alive who knows how the dosing schedules worked," Petra said. "Every gift-bearer the guild touched is going to spend years wondering what was done inside their bodies. You're going to answer those questions, Corvane. One at a time. For free." He patted the man's bound wrist, almost kindly. "Consider it a vintage note."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — not quite pain, not quite respect, but some bitter alloy of the two — and Petra climbed onto the cart feeling lighter than he had any right to feel, and decided not to examine why.

* * *

Dissolving the Taster's Guild turned out to be mostly paperwork, which Petra hadn't expected, and grief, which he had.

The paperwork happened in the guildhall itself, that imposing stone building in the capital with its tasting chambers and its locked cabinets and its long tables where he'd spent nearly a decade learning to swallow death gracefully. A provisional assembly had taken over the palace and most of its machinery, and they needed someone who knew the guild's inner workings to help unwind them. So Petra spent a strange fortnight in rooms that still smelled faintly of silver polish and fear, opening cabinets with keys he'd stolen copies of years ago and never thought he'd use for honest purposes.

Inside the cabinets: the ledgers. Hundreds of them. Nine years of his own swallows lived in one of them — dose, compound, observed effect, yield estimate — written in a clerk's neat hand, with margins full of Corvane's patient annotations. Petra read his own life as data and felt strangely little. It was like reading about a brother he'd been separated from young.

What hurt more were the others' books. The children's. The harvest records from villages he'd never heard of. The correspondence between the guild and the crown, polite as dance cards, discussing *supply continuity* and *quality variance* and, once, memorably, *the Okafor asset shows promising resilience; recommend extending cultivation period.*

Asset. Cultivation. He put the letter down, walked to the window, breathed for a while, and came back. That was the trick of it. You put it down, you breathed, you came back. Nine years of being poisoned taught you at least that.

They burned the ledgers in the guildhall courtyard on the last day — except for one copy of each, sealed and sent to the forge-city archive, because Tam had argued, red-faced and iron-voiced, that burning evidence was how men like Corvane got away with things for centuries. *You keep the truth so it can testify,* he said, sounding so much like Odile that several adults laughed and then felt bad about laughing. *But you take its power away.*

So one copy went east into the mountain dark, to sit among murals of dragons and their bonded dead. And the rest went into the fire, and the crowd around that fire was quiet, and somewhere in the middle of it Petra realized he was crying, which annoyed him enormously, because he'd planned to get through the day with dignity.

Maren was not there to see it. They buried her ashes at the head of the pass she'd held, in sight of the road, so she could keep an eye on travelers. Tam had carved the marker himself, badly, in letters that leaned like drunk soldiers: *SHE HELD THE DOOR.* Petra visited it before leaving the capital, alone, and stood there for a long time not saying anything, because what do you say to a woman who spent her last fire buying strangers ten minutes of life?

He settled, finally, on: "I watered your roses." Which was true. Someone had to.

* * *

The immunity was gone. He confirmed it the way he confirmed everything: methodically, with notes.

Vessal's rebirth had cost more than either of them admitted at first. When her fire came back — fed by his blood given freely at the bonding-stone fragment — it hadn't just rekindled her. It had passed *through* him, taking with it whatever the guild's poisons had slowly built in his veins, the armor he'd spent nine years forging one agonizing swallow at a time. The gift that was also a cage. Dragon-fire remembered everything, Vessal told him, and it had remembered him clean.

He tested it in the spring, with a compound the provisional physicians supplied and watched him like hawks while he took. Half a dose of something that once would have made his tongue itch. It laid him flat for two days. Sweat, shaking, the whole humiliating theater of an ordinary body meeting ordinary poison.

Tam hovered the entire time, bringing broth and commentary in equal measure. ("Your face looks like a boiled ham. Is that normal? Should it look like that?")

"Yes," Petra croaked. "That's what it looks like when you're mortal, apparently."

"That's terrible. Why would anyone want that?"

"Ask me again in a week."

He asked himself in a week, lying in the grass behind the rebuilt hearth-house, weak as a kitten and absurdly, inexplicably content, and the answer surprised him: because it was *his* now. The immunity had never been his. It had been cultivated, harvested, annotated, owned — an asset with a recommended extension period. His body had been a field someone else farmed. But this — the weakness, the boiled-ham face, the risk — this belonged to him alone. No ledger recorded it. No vintage note waited at the end of his suffering. If he died of poison now, he would die of it as a free man, stupidly, entirely his own.

There was worse freedom too, and he didn't pretend otherwise. Every meal now required thought. Every cup of wine was a small act of faith. He caught himself, sometimes, reaching for the old habits — the careful sips, the pause, the reading of his own pulse — and having to remind his hands that the war was over, that caution was now just manners instead of survival.

And once — only once, at a festival in Ironhollow, when a stranger pressed a cup of mulled wine into his hand with a smile that lingered half a beat too long — he felt the old dread rise like floodwater, and had to stand very still until it passed. The stranger was just a stranger. Probably. He drank the wine. It was too sweet and too cloved and absolutely wonderful, and he laughed at himself afterward, quietly, where no one could see.

Mostly, though, it was lightness. He hadn't known how heavy he'd been.

* * *

Vessal was diminished, and burning.

Those two facts coexisted in her the way mourning and lightness coexisted in him, which was probably why they understood each other. Her fire had come back smaller than it left. Where once she could have melted the palace gates — and had, briefly, gloriously, during the landing nobody in the capital would stop talking about — she could now manage a cookfire's warmth, a lantern's glow, a flame about the size of an indignant candle. Her wings healed crooked. She could fly short distances, low and laborious, looking less like a legend and more like an enormous exhausted goose.

She pretended not to mind. She minded enormously.

"The chronicles will say I fell upon the palace like the wrath of heaven," she said, from her sprawl in the warm ash of the old kiln yard outside Ironhollow, where she'd appointed herself resident dragon. "They will not mention that my landing broke a fountain, two carts, and my remaining dignity."

"The chronicles will say you saved the realm," Petra said, sketching in his notebook. He sketched constantly now — flight paths, wind patterns, the geometry of her ruined wings. "A little indignity is good for the historical record. Keeps future generations humble."

"I was forty thousand years old when your king was a gleam in his grandfather's eye. Humility is for creatures with time to spare." She shifted, and her scales caught the afternoon light, dull bronze shot through with new bright seams where the reborn fire ran — scars that glowed faintly, like embers under ash. "How goes the schooling of your fledglings?"

"Fledglings" was her word for his students, and she deployed it with maximum condescension, and both of them knew she adored them. Three of them now, come spring: Tam, who heard the mountains; Odile's youngest cousin, who dreamed true and hated it; and a solemn miller's daughter named Bree whose gift nobody could name yet, except that plants grew slightly sideways toward her when she sang.

"They're hopeless," Petra said. "Tam tried to map a flight path today using a chicken as a model."

"A bold pedagogical choice."

"He threw it. The chicken's fine. Tam is not allowed within thirty feet of poultry until further notice."

Vessal made the sound that was her laugh, a deep rumble that set the ash dancing, and then went quiet in the way she went quiet when a thought was settling on her like a bird on a branch. Petra had learned to wait for these.

"When I was dying," she said at last, "in the mine, before you came — I believed the fire ended with me, and the gifts with it, and that the age of bonds was simply over, finished, folded up like a wing." Her ember-seams pulsed softly. "I did not imagine a human giving blood freely. We designed the bargain, my kind and yours, long ago. We built in payment, and exchange, and price. We never built in *gift*. Your species keeps inventing things outside the specifications. It is deeply unsettling."

"Get used to it," Petra said. "Apparently we're going to be doing this a long time."

"Yes," Vessal said, and there was something in the old voice that was almost shy, which for a creature of forty thousand years was its own small miracle. "I have been considering the matter. Of the long time. Of whether a diminished dragon with crooked wings might be permitted... company. For the duration."

Petra looked up from his notebook. She was studying the sky with tremendous concentration, as if the clouds required supervision.

"You're asking if you can stay," he said.

"I am asking nothing. Dragons do not ask. I am *observing aloud* that this valley has adequate thermals."

"It has terrible thermals. I've mapped them. You complained about them yourself, twice, Tuesday."

"Then it seems," said Vessal, with great dignity, "we are both staying somewhere inadequate."

Petra grinned and bent back over his notebook, and the ash-dust drifted gold in the slanting light, and for a moment — a real moment, unguarded — everything was simple. A dragon in a kiln yard. A notebook full of flight paths. Spring coming on.

* * *

It was Tam who brought the letter, because Tam always brought trouble; it followed him like a loyal dog.

He came pelting up the hill from the village road, waving a piece of paper, out of breath, fourteen years old and vibrating. "Rider from the capital. From the assembly. Petra. *Petra.*"

"If it's another request for testimony—"

"It's not." Tam shoved the paper at him. "Read it."

Petra read it. Read it twice. Then sat down slowly on the warm stones at the edge of the kiln yard, because his legs had opinions.

The assembly had ratified the Bond Accords — the framework Odile and the forge-city elders had been drafting since winter. Recognition of bonded pairs. Protection under law. Provision for a school. Provision, specifically, for *a founding instructor of the bonded line*, such instructor to be *named by consent of dragon and human together*.

They wanted him. Formally. In writing. With a seal on it and everything.

"They know I'm not a teacher," he said faintly. "I'm a smuggler who learned to taste poison. My qualifications are crimes."

"Vessal already said yes," Tam said, grinning. "Yesterday. While you were napping. She says you're contractually obligated to observe aloud about thermals for the next fifty years minimum."

"She *what*—"

Above them, from the kiln yard, came a rumble of ancient, shameless laughter, and the smell of woodsmoke, and the sound of a diminished dragon burning brightly anyway.

Petra looked at the letter. At the boy. At the crooked-winged shape against the spring sky. Somewhere east, in the mountain dark, the truth sat archived and testifying; somewhere west, a man carried water in a village that refused to hate him cheaply; somewhere under the grass, a hearth-witch kept watch over a road she'd bought with fire. None of it was finished. None of it would ever be finished. That, he understood now, was what belonging actually was — not a safe place at last, but a place worth the endless work of keeping.

"Well," he said, folding the letter into his pocket, next to a vial he no longer needed and hadn't had the heart to throw away. "I suppose I'd better learn the thermals properly, then."

He picked up his notebook, and went down the hill toward the first lesson of the rest of his life.
