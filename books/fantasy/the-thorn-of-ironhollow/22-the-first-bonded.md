## 22. The First Bonded

The trouble with teaching children to fly was that they kept wanting to do it immediately.

"Petra," called Sera from the top of the watchtower, her voice carrying across the training yard with the particular brightness of a girl about to do something inadvisable. "Petra, what happens if I jump and *believe* hard enough?"

"You break your ankle," Petra called back without looking up from his charts. "And then Maren's granddaughter heals you, and then she lectures you, which is worse."

"I've got the *feeling* this time!"

"That's what Bren said. Bren is currently eating his supper through a straw." He turned a page. The morning sun caught the ink still drying on the newest map spread across the trestle table before him — the eastern ridge line, drawn in his own careful hand, annotated in three colors: red for updrafts, blue for danger, green for the places where Vessal said, in her dry and ancient way, that the air "tasted right."

Two years ago he had mapped smuggler roads. Now he mapped sky.

It was spring in Ironhollow, the third since everything ended, and the village had grown fat on the shame of the crown. Where the guildhouse had stood — that squat, windowless place where ledgers recorded every swallow of his life — there was now a market square with a fountain, because Ironhollow had decided collectively that the best revenge on Corvane's memory was commerce. But Petra had asked for one thing. One small plot at the corner of it, where the old guildhouse foundation still ran under the cobbles like a scar under skin.

There, he planted roses. Thorn roses, black-stemmed, wild things that bit back when you touched them wrong. They bloomed viciously in spring, and the villagers had learned not to prune them, and children learned early that some beautiful things were also sharp, which Petra considered the most honest lesson in the village.

He was rolling up the chart when Tam came barreling into the yard, sixteen now and all elbows, with the look of a boy who had heard something in the mountains and run four miles downhill to say so.

"They're moving," Tam gasped. "North of the pass. The deep stone's singing — you know how it goes when they're coming home? It's doing that. A lot of thats."

Petra set down the chart. His heart did the thing it always did now, the lurch that was equal parts joy and arithmetic. "How many?"

"Three. Maybe four. Young ones, by the sound. Shallow voices." Tam pressed his palm flat against the yard's stone wall out of habit, listening with the part of him no one could see. "Vessal will want to know."

*Vessal will want to know.* Two years ago those words would have been unthinkable. Now they were Tuesday.

* * *

They found her in the high meadow above the terraces, where she had claimed a sunny shelf of rock the way an old queen claims a chair by the fire — through sheer accumulated dignity. She was smaller than she had been at the ford, smaller than she'd been even a year ago; the fire that Petra's blood had rekindled burned steady in her now but modest, a hearth-fire rather than a forge. Her scales had gone from storm-grey to a soft silver at the edges, like frost that never melted. The villagers had stopped being afraid of her sometime during the second winter, when she began letting the smallest children nap in the warmth between her forepaws while their mothers worked the looms nearby.

She heard them coming, of course. She heard everything within ten miles when she bothered to listen.

"The boy is excited," Vessal observed without opening her eyes. Her voice was gravel poured slow, and beneath it, faintly, something that might have been amusement. "He has been running since dawn. Humans are so brief and so *loud*."

"Dragons are so long and so smug," Petra said.

"One of us has earned smugness." She opened one eye, gold-ringed, ancient. "Well? What does the mountain say?"

Tam told her, breathless, hands shaping the shapes of young dragons in the air. Three, maybe four, flying north of the pass, coming home along the old migration lines that had been empty for a century. And Vessal listened with her whole body, the great wings shifting, her tail curling and uncurling in the grass, and when he finished she was quiet for a long moment.

"Kessa's brood," she said at last. "Or what survived of it. They were in the southern crags when the Hunt burned through the lowlands. Hiding. As we all hid, one way or another." The gold eye found Petra. "You understand what this means."

"That our flight school is about to be very crowded?"

"It means the bond is spreading, taster. Every gift in the realm woke the day you bled for me — you know this, you've watched it happen. And where gifts wake, dragons hear. They are coming because the world is *audible* again." She lowered her great head until her breath stirred his hair, warm and smelling of hot stone. "They are coming because of you. I hope you are prepared to be responsible."

"No one has ever accused me of being prepared."

"And yet here we are." She settled back onto her haunches, immensely satisfied with the universe. "Tell me about the new one. The miller's daughter who hears weather three days off."

"Sera? She jumped off the watchtower this morning on the theory that believing hard counts as flying."

"Mm. When I was young, we pushed each other off cliffs to learn. With slightly more cliff." A rumble that was her version of laughter. "Put her on my back tomorrow. Low circuits over the lake. Fear teaches faster than faith, but faith keeps them alive once fear runs out."

"You're enjoying this," Petra said. "You're a two-hundred-year-old legend of the elder world and you're *enjoying* this."

"Petra." She fixed him with both eyes now, and there was something underneath the humor, something old and warm and slightly astonished, as if she still couldn't quite believe it herself. "I spent a hundred years dying alone in a hole in the ground. Every dragon I loved went quiet one by one, and I lay in the dark cataloguing my own extinction. And now there are children in my meadow. There are *maps of the sky*. Do not begrudge an old creature her enjoyment."

* * *

That evening he walked down into the village with the chart under his arm and stopped, as he always did, at the corner of the market square. The fountain burbled. Someone had left baskets of early strawberries unattended on a bench, which in the old Ironhollow would have been theft waiting to happen, and in this one meant simply that everyone trusted everyone, which still made Petra's chest ache in a way he hadn't learned to name.

The roses were blooming. Black stems, dark leaves, and the flowers a deep bruised crimson that seemed to hold the light rather than reflect it. He knelt by the largest bush and did the year's first pruning, carefully, letting the thorns find his fingers the way they always did. He had stopped wearing gloves for this. It felt important that it cost a little.

Under his knees, beneath the cobbles, the old guildhouse foundation hummed its emptiness. Nine years of ledgers had been carried out of that building and burned in the square, page by page, while the people whose names were written in them watched. Some had wept. Old Wenna, the hearth-witch's sister, had thrown her ledger-page into the fire herself and then stood there a long time, watching her own name curl and blacken, saying nothing at all.

Petra had kept exactly one document. It lived in a case around his neck now: a single page, the first entry ever made in his file. *Subject P.O., age fourteen. Palatability: acceptable. Compliance: total.* He kept it not to grieve but to remember the boy who had been described that way, so that he would never mistake the man for anything other than what he'd chosen to become instead.

"You're talking to the roses again," said a voice behind him.

Maren's granddaughter, little Odile, eight years old and already able to close a wound with a touch, stood there in the last light with her fists on her hips. She had her grandmother's exact disapproving face, which Petra suspected was hereditary.

"I'm pruning them."

"You're talking to them. Grandmother said you used to talk to poisons too."

"Your grandmother," Petra said gravely, "should spend less time gossiping and more time teaching you manners."

"She says the same about you but with worse words." Odile crouched beside him, fearless of the thorns in the way of children who healed fast. "Is it true more dragons are coming?"

"It's true."

"Will there be enough for everyone who hears one?" And there it was, the real question, hiding inside the small voice. Because half the children in the valley had woken in the past two years with something new in them — weather-sense, iron-song, green-thumbs that made gardens riotous — and half the adults pretended not to notice, and everyone knew that the number of dragons in the world could be counted on one clawed foot.

Petra put down the shears. "Come here," he said, and when she sat beside him he pointed at the rosebush. "See how many blooms?"

"A lot."

"Count them."

She counted, lips moving. "Twenty-two."

"Now see the thorns. More than the blooms, yes? The plant makes more defenses than flowers, because that's how it survives." He tapped one dark stem gently. "Not every gift gets a dragon. But every gift gets *this* — a village that knows your name and doesn't sell it. That's the part we planted here. The dragons are just the blooms."

Odile considered this with the ferocious seriousness of an eight-year-old. "Grandmother says you used to be invisible."

"For nine years."

"And now you teach flying and talk to roses."

"People change."

"She says you changed *first*, and the world just took a while to catch up." Odile stood, brushed off her skirt, and added, with devastating childhood precision: "She cried when she said it, though. She cries easy now. Everyone does."

She ran off toward the smell of supper, and Petra stayed kneeling among the thorns a moment longer, letting that settle into him. *Everyone cries easy now.* Yes. The whole valley had learned that grief shared weighed less. It had taken a kingdom falling apart to teach them, but they had learned.

* * *

Three days later, the young dragons arrived.

Petra stood in the high meadow with Tam and Sera and eleven other students arranged behind him in what was supposed to be a calm and orderly line and was actually a vibrating mass of adolescents, as three shapes came down out of the northern clouds — lean, iridescent, unmistakably young despite their size, flying in the loose ragged formation of creatures who had spent years hiding and were only now remembering that sky was theirs.

Vessal rose to meet them. Even diminished, even silver-edged, she filled the sky the way winter fills a valley, and the three newcomers circled her twice, crying out in voices like cracked bells, before landing in the meadow with the gracelessness of the exhausted.

The greeting between Vessal and the eldest of them went on a long time, foreheads touching, wings mantled, throats working in the subsonic language that made the students' back teeth itch. When it was done, Vessal turned her head and looked directly at Petra across the whole length of the meadow.

*Taster,* she said, into his mind and, he realized a heartbeat later, into the minds of every bonded student present, deliberately, because she believed in pedagogy through overwhelming experience. *These are Rill, and Ash-from-the-South, and the small angry one is named for a mountain I have forgotten. They come seeking the bond-line. They come seeking you.*

"Why me?" Sera whispered, awed, from somewhere near Petra's elbow. "Why does it always start with him?"

Because, Petra thought, someone had to swallow the poison first so that no one else would have to. Someone had to be the vintage before anyone else could be the vineyard. But aloud he only said, "Because I owed a dragon a debt, and dragons collect in interesting currencies," and then he stepped forward into the shadow of three new wings, heart hammering, mouth dry, absolutely terrified —

— and found, as he always did, that terror and belonging had somehow become the same feeling, and that this was fine.

* * *

That summer the maps multiplied. Petra drew flight paths until his fingers were permanently stained with red and blue and green ink, taught thermals and crosswinds and the etiquette of approaching a sleeping dragon (loudly, from downwind, with snacks), and watched the first bonded generation grow into their strange new inheritance with the same methodical patience he had once spent building immunity, drop by drop, dose by dose.

Some nights he still dreamed of the cellar under the palace, of Aldric's smiling offer, of the vial of poison hidden in his sleeve. Some mornings he woke and, out of pure habit, tested his tea with a grain of caution before remembering — again — that he could be poisoned now like anyone, like any man, and that the vulnerability still felt less like loss than like finally being allowed to bleed.

On the first anniversary of the young dragons' arrival, he walked down at dusk to the corner of the market square with a sapling in one hand and a spade in the other. The thorn roses had gone woody and vast, a black hedge guarding the fountain's corner, and he had decided they needed company. Beside them, in the crack between cobbles where the old guildhouse threshold used to be, he planted an apple tree.

Odile found him there, patting down the soil. "Apples? Why apples?"

"Roses remember what we buried," Petra said, standing, brushing dirt from his knees. "Apples are for what comes next."

Above the valley, three young dragons wheeled in the gold light, and Vessal flew with them — slower than she'd been, but burning, always burning — and far below, in the yard of a rebuilt village, a miller's daughter felt wind arriving from three days away and smiled.

Petra picked up his spade and started home.

*Here ends The Thorn of Ironhollow.*
