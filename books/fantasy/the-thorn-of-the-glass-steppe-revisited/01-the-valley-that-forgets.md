## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bees woke before Odile did, as they always did, humming their small gold complaints against the cold of early spring, and he lay a moment in the dark of his cottage listening to them through the wall the way another man might listen to rain.

Seven years now in the valley of Vell Hollow, and still he counted the mornings like coins hoarded against some imagined theft. Seven years since he had climbed out of his master's tower with a grimoire strapped beneath his coat and soot on his hands and the taste of burnt feathers in his mouth, and run — not bravely, not cleverly, just relentlessly, the way water runs downhill — until the road ended at a pass between two gray mountains and he had dropped down into this green silence and felt, for the first time in his life, nothing reach for him.

That was the whole miracle of Vell Hollow, and it never stopped being miraculous no matter how many springs he survived to see: magic could not follow him here. It pooled against the ridgelines the way fog pools against glass. Spells unraveled at the pass. Charms went dumb. The old bloodlines who'd fled here generations ago had come for that reason, and their descendants stayed out of habit, tending goats and orchards and each other with a fierce, unmagical competence. No scrying could find you in the Hollow. No curse could cross the stones. Whatever waited outside those mountains — and Odile knew better than most what waited — it could not come down into the valley and take him.

He had built a life the size of his two hands. Bees. Herbs. A garden laid out in straight rows because straight rows asked nothing of him but weeding. A neighbor, Marta Ostrevik, who brought him cheese and gossip in equal measure and never asked why a man with ink-stained fingers and a scholar's stoop chose to spend his days hauling honeycomb. He told her he'd been a scribe in Corvale. It was nearly true. Scribes wrote things down; so had he, once, in margins he should have burned.

He rose, banked the stove, put water on, and dressed in the woolens Marta's sister wove for him. Outside the air had the clean bite of late frost, and the light was doing its slow gray work on the eastern ridge, picking out the last rags of snow in the high folds of the mountain. He walked the garden path with his breath smoking ahead of him and checked the rosemary under its straw blanket, the comfrey crowns, the sage that had wintered better than it deserved. Routine upon routine. He performed each small task the way a monk performs an office, and if there was devotion in it, he declined to name it.

Dread, after seven years, learns to dress itself in ordinary clothes. It looked like checking the latch twice. It looked like glancing up the goat track toward the pass each morning — one glance, no more, a man merely admiring the view — before turning back to his hives. It looked like the way he flinched, very slightly, when a bird burst from the hedge, or when someone knocked too hard.

The knock, when it came that evening, was barely a knock at all.

* * *

He was rendering wax over the stove when he heard it: a sound so faint he first took it for a branch dragging on the door in the wind. Then again — a soft, arrhythmic tapping, like knuckles that had forgotten how knuckles worked. He stood with the wooden paddle in his hand and let the wax cool around it while he listened. The wind was coming off the western slope tonight, which meant anything at his door had come from the village side, from the orchards and the lane. Nothing came from the pass. Nothing ever came from the pass.

"Hello?" he said, and hated how carefully casual he made his voice, how rehearsed the ordinariness sounded even to himself.

He opened the door on a child.

A boy, perhaps eight years old, perhaps younger — it was hard to tell, because he was swaying on his feet the way a candle flame sways, upright only through some stubbornness beyond strength. Barefoot in the snow-melt of the doorstep. His feet were gray-white with cold, split at the heels, leaving prints on Odile's threshold that were more water than blood but were partly blood. He wore a shift of some pale cloth gone filthy, and his hair was white — not old-man white but true white, snow white, the white of something grown without sun — and his eyes were the color of deep ice, and they did not look at Odile so much as *through* him, as though Odile were a window left open in a wall.

The boy's lips moved. What came out was not a word Odile knew, and yet —

And yet it landed somewhere behind his sternum like a struck bell.

He knew that sound. Not the language — he had never learned it, had only ever heard fragments of it, overheard through a door in a tower, syllables that had made the candles gutter and the mortar weep dust — but he knew what it *did*. It reached into the room and pressed against things. The flame of his lamp leaned toward the boy. The bees, seven hundred of them drowsy in the hive-wall, rose into a single low note, a held tone like the hum inside a seashell, and did not stop. Frost feathered across the inside of the windowpane in patterns too regular for weather — spirals, rings, concentric and precise, blooming outward from where the child stood as though the cold were being written there.

Odile's body knew what to do before his mind caught up. Seven years of practiced calm dissolved and underneath it the apprentice was still there, the boy who had learned to read danger in the behavior of flames. He got the child inside. He got him to the chair by the stove. He wrapped him in the good blanket, the one Marta had woven with red borders, and chafed his feet, and set warm honey-water in his hands, and all the while the boy spoke — softly, continuously, the way other children whimper in sleep — in a tongue that made the lamp flame bow and the frost write its patient spirals and something in Odile's chest ache like a bruise he had spent seven years convincing himself was healed.

"Hush," Odile said. "Hush now. You're safe." Safe was a strange thing to promise; he didn't examine it. "Where did you come from? Who — " He stopped himself from saying *who sent you*, which was the question that lived in his mouth like a stone, and asked instead, "What's your name?"

The boy looked at him then, truly looked, and his ice-colored eyes focused for the first time, and he said a word.

It was not a name. Odile understood that immediately, the way one understands a burn is a burn. It was a word from the same buried language, and it had weight — actual weight, a pressure in the air, a pressure in Odile's teeth — and every jar on his shelf rang faintly in sympathy, and the bees fell silent all at once, and the fire in the stove flattened to a blue, attentive stillness.

Then the boy shuddered, closed his eyes, and said nothing more, and within moments he was asleep — truly asleep, slack and small and terribly young, his white head lolling against the chair-back, his ruined feet trailing the blanket.

* * *

Odile did not sleep.

He sat across the room with his back to the wall — an old habit, from the tower, from the nights after lessons when his master's experiments went long and wrong — and watched the child breathe. The frost spirals on the window had not melted. Outside, the night was utterly quiet, and he realized after a long while that it was too quiet: no owl, no fox scream, none of the small rustling traffic of a valley evening. As though everything alive within earshot had stopped to listen to the boy's breathing.

He made himself think it through the way he made himself do everything now, step by careful step, a man crossing ice.

Children did not wander into Vell Hollow. The valley had maybe two hundred souls, every one of them born here or accounted for, and the roads out of it were three days' walk from anywhere, over passes that killed travelers in a mild autumn. A barefoot child in a fine linen shift, half-dead, arriving at dusk at the door of the one house in the valley whose occupant had fled the world of magic — that was not chance. Chance did not have aim. And the language — he pressed his palms against his knees and made himself look at it squarely — the language was not any language. It was the oldest thing he had ever heard, older than kingdoms, and he had heard it exactly twice before in his life: once through a locked door in his master's tower, and once from his master's own mouth, on the night he ran.

The boy murmured in his sleep. One syllable of it slipped free, soft as a sigh, and the stove-fire leaned.

Odile got up quietly and took down the lamp and carried it to the window, and looked at the frost spirals for a long time. They were not weather. Weather did not make concentric rings. Weather did not make marks that, if he half-closed his eyes and let his old training rise despite itself, resolved almost into — script. Binding-script. The kind of writing that was not written *on* a thing but written *as* a thing, the kind his master had spent whole chapters of the stolen grimoire circling without ever quite describing, the way one circles a wound.

"You're not a message," he whispered to the sleeping boy. "You're the seal the message is written on."

The words came out of him unbidden and true, and hearing them frightened him more than anything else that night, because he did not know how he knew them — only that some buried part of him, the apprentice he had starved for seven years, had sat up the moment the child spoke and recognized the shape of the thing in his kitchen.

Outside, far up on the black shoulder of the western ridge, where no path ran and no villager had reason to go, a light appeared. Small. Orange. Moving. Torchlight, descending — unhurried, deliberate — toward the valley floor, toward the village, toward his door.

The bees began to hum again, low and endless, like a held breath.

Odile stood in his kitchen with the lamp shaking very slightly in his hand, and admitted it, finally, to the empty room: peace had been a story he told himself, and stories, his master used to say, are just spells you cast on yourself — and this one, tonight, had broken.
