## 2. A Tongue Older Than Crowns

The boy slept in the space between the hearth and the wall, curled like a comma of punctuation in a sentence Odile could not read. He had given him broth and a blanket and the only soft thing in the house that wasn't his own pillow, and now Odile sat across the room with a beeswax candle burning low and watched him the way one watches a door left ajar in a storm.

He had not spoken since dusk. Not to Odile, anyway. To himself — or to something — he had spoken plenty. The words came out of him even in sleep sometimes, a low murmur like water finding its way under stone, and every syllable landed in Odile's chest with the weight of a hand pressing an old wound.

Because he knew them. Some of them.

Not all. Not even most. But enough that recognition had arrived in him not as surprise but as sickness — the way a body knows spoiled meat before the mind consents to it. He had heard this language before, seven years ago, in a tower room that smelled of hot iron and tallow, while his master stood over a kneeling man and *spoke*, and the man's shadow moved wrong, and Odile had run before the last word was finished.

*It is not speech,* Master Veyra had told her apprentices once, when she taught them the history of tongues instead of the practice of them. She had been almost gentle that day, which should have warned him. *Every language you know — Lowmark, Court Cant, the river dialects — is a language of description. It says what things are. There were older tongues that said what things must be. We call them binding-languages now, because that is all anyone used them for.*

She had smiled then, and it had been the worst smile Odile had ever seen on her face, because it looked like grief wearing a mask it hadn't bothered to fit.

*And if you ever hear one, children, you will feel it here.* She had touched her own sternum. *That ache is your soul being addressed by name. Do not answer it.*

Odile's sternum had been aching since sundown.

* * *

From inside the boy's sleep — though *inside* was the wrong word, there was no inside and no outside where the boy was concerned, there was only *held* —

*— held: the small warm box with its fire-smell and its bee-sweetness; held: the tall sorrowing shape that watches and does not come near; held: the words beneath the floor of the world, the ones I carry, the ones that press outward against my ribs like winter wheat against snow, wanting sun, wanting ground, wanting —*

*— no. Wanting is mine alone. That is the rule. The words do not want. The words wait. I am the door they wait behind, and doors do not dream, except that I dream, I dream of a woman's hands and cold water and a word said over me that felt like being folded shut —*

*— the tall one is afraid of me. Good. Fear keeps the tall ones at a distance, and distance keeps the words quiet, and quiet is all I have ever been able to give anyone —*

The boy turned over. In his sleep his mouth shaped three syllables, soft as breath on glass, and outside the window the frost on the pane rearranged itself into a pattern too regular to be accident: a ring within a ring, like a seal pressed into wax.

Odile blew out the candle so he would not have to look at it.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless. Odile did the chores because chores were the architecture of his sanity — feed the hens, check the hives' winter wrappings, chip ice from the trough — and the boy followed him at a distance of exactly six paces, silent, barefoot still, though Odile had left out a pair of wool socks that went ignored.

"You'll lose toes," Odile said, not turning around.

The boy said nothing. But Odile heard — felt — the absence of an answer, the way one hears the gap in a familiar song.

At midmorning he sat the boy down at his table, set out bread and salt honey, and tried the ordinary tongue. "What is your name?"

The boy looked at him. His eyes were pale gray, the color of weather deciding, and they did not blink often enough. Then he spoke — and it was the language again, but slowly this time, deliberately, each word placed like a stone in a stream so Odile could step across it.

He understood perhaps one word in five. But the one word in five was enough to make his blood go cold and stay there.

*Bound. Carried. Sealed. King. Crown. Hungry.*

"No," Odile said aloud, to the kitchen, to himself. "No, absolutely not."

But the ache in his sternum had already begun arranging itself into memory. Seven years ago. The tower room. The kneeling man whose shadow had rippled like disturbed water while Veyra spoke over him in this same tongue, and the moment before Odile fled — the half-second he had spent seven years pretending he hadn't seen — when the man had looked up at Odile with eyes gone flat and polite and said, in perfect ordinary speech, *"There is nothing wrong. There has never been anything wrong."*

Veyra had called it a mending, afterward, when Odile asked — once, carefully, the way you probe a tooth. "A loyalty-mending," she'd said. "Some men see things that unmake them. It is a mercy."

It had sounded like mercy. It had taken seven years and a starving child speaking in a dead grammar for Odile to hear what it actually was: a theft. Someone had reached into a man's mind and rewritten what he knew to be true, and Veyra had done the reaching, and Odile had watched, and then he had packed his satchel and stolen her grimoire and run to a valley where magic could not follow, and he had called it peace ever since.

Interest, he thought now, watching the boy eat bread with the mechanical thoroughness of someone who had been taught hunger was a debt. Interest comes due.

* * *

They came at noon.

Odile saw them first from the herb garden: four riders on the valley's single road, moving at the unhurried pace of men who already knew their destination. They wore no livery, which was itself a kind of uniform — the crown's business traveled plain in the provinces, or so the stories said. Gray cloaks, good horses, and the particular stillness of people who are not looking for trouble because they intend to be the trouble.

Odile wiped his hands on his apron and walked out to meet them, heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't let it play since he'd crossed the valley's edge seven years ago.

"Good day," he called. His voice came out steady. Small mercies.

The leader reined in. A woman, fifty or so, silver at her temples, with a face like a well-kept ledger — nothing wasted, everything recorded. Her eyes moved over Odile the way a customs officer's move over cargo.

"Good day." Her voice was pleasant and completely without warmth. "We're searching for a runaway ward of the royal household. A boy, eight years old, dark hair, underfed. He will not speak sense, if he speaks at all. Have you seen him?"

"I keep bees," Odile said. "I see my hens, my neighbor Sarn when he wants salt, and the occasional lost shepherd. No boys."

"You're certain." It was not a question, but she made it one anyway with a tilt of her head.

"I'm certain."

Something flickered behind her eyes — not suspicion exactly. Something worse. Recognition, almost, as if she were comparing him against a record. Against a *file*. "Your name, master beekeeper?"

"Corren," Odile said, and was distantly amazed that the lie came out whole, seamless, without the stammer he could feel building behind his teeth. Corren was the name of a dead man who'd lived in Marrow's Ford thirty years ago; it was the best he could do on short notice, and it seemed to suffice.

"Master Corren." She drew out the name like thread from a spool, testing its strength. "Strange valley, this. No magic reaches here. Did you know that? Most people find it restful." A pause, delicately weighted. "Others find it useful."

"I wouldn't know anything about magic," Odile said. "I know about bees."

"Yes," the woman said softly. "So you say." She gathered her reins. "If a child comes to your door — hungry, frightened, speaking nonsense — remember that nonsense can be dangerous, and that we are patient, and that we always, always find what we're looking for." She smiled. It did not involve any part of her above the mouth. "Good day, Master Corren."

They rode on up the valley road, toward the pass, and Odile stood in his garden until they were specks, then until they were gone, then longer.

Behind him, six paces away as always, the boy stood among the frozen bean rows, watching the road with an expression Odile had no ordinary word for. If the boy had been a door, the expression was the sound of something large beginning to lean against it from the other side.

*Runaway ward of the royal household.* Ward. Not prisoner, not experiment, not vessel — *ward*. The crown claimed him. Which meant the crown knew what he was, or suspected, or — worse — had *made* him and merely mislaid him.

Odile thought of Veyra's voice, gentle as rot: *binding-languages.* Thought of the mended man in the tower. Thought of hunters in unliveried gray asking after a child in a valley chosen precisely because nothing magical could reach him — nothing, apparently, except the child himself, walking out of the mountains with a language in his mouth that ached to hear.

His silence had kept him safe for seven years. That was all it had ever bought — safety, purchased with another man's flattened eyes and whatever else he'd refused to look at long enough to forget.

The interest, he thought again. Here it is. Presented for payment.

The boy looked up at him and spoke, quietly, in the old tongue, and this time Odile caught the shape of it well enough to translate the gist if not the letter:

*They will come back. And next time they will bring the thing that listens through their ears.*

Odile looked north, toward the pass, toward the world he had buried.

That night he took the key from around his neck, unlocked the chest under his bed, and set his master's stolen grimoire on the table beside the bread and the salt honey.

He had sworn never to open it. He opened it.
