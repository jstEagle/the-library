## 3. What the Hedge-Witch Knew

The rule had kept him alive for nine years, and it was a simple one: *never open the book*.

Not the grimoire itself — that lived wrapped in oilcloth and beeswax beneath the third floorboard of the hearth, and Odile had not so much as touched the wrapping since the night he'd fled his master's tower with it pressed against his ribs like a second heart. The rule was broader than that. It was this: *do not think in her grammar*. Do not reach for the old words when the bees swarmed wrong. Do not count things in sevens because seven was a number with teeth. Do not, above all, wonder whether the valley's silence was peace or merely the absence of anything worth saying.

He had broken the rule in small ways before, of course. Everyone who survives does. He'd let himself remember the smell of the tower — cold stone and hot iron and the particular sweetness of rendered fat used in binding-wax — and he'd let himself feel, once a year on the anniversary of his running, the specific shape of his cowardice, which was long and narrow and fit perfectly down the back stair.

But he had never opened the book.

On the third morning after the boy came, he went to the hearth while the child slept in the loft, prying up the floorboard with his thumbnail the way you'd pull a splinter from your own flesh — slowly, wincing ahead of the pain.

* * *

The oilcloth came away stiff as bark. Beneath it the grimoire waited, black goatskin gone greenish at the corners, its spine cracked into a fan of pale pages where some previous owner — his master, or hers before her — had forced it open too often at the same place. Odile set it on the kitchen table beside the honey jars and the drying bundles of yarrow, and for a long moment the ordinary objects seemed to lean away from it, the way water leans away from a hot iron.

The boy slept on. In sleep he made no sound at all, which was its own kind of wrong; children breathed loudly, snuffled, whimpered. This one lay like something set down carefully by hands that expected to pick it up again later.

Odile opened the cover.

The first thing that struck him was how *familiar* it was — the cramped slanted hand, the diagrams drawn with a draftsman's cruelty, every circle closed so tightly no mercy could leak out. Master Veyra's hand. He had spent six years copying that hand until his own began to imitate it without his permission. He still caught traces of it sometimes in the labels on his herb jars, and each time he scraped them off and rewrote them clumsily, deliberately ugly.

He turned past the early chapters — *On the Nature of the Unspoken*, *Concerning Names Given Freely*, the foundational cruelties dressed up as philosophy — looking for what he did not yet know he was looking for. The valley outside the window held its breath. Snow fell without sound. Even the bees, wintering in their skeps, were silent in a way that felt attentive.

It was near midday when he found it, in a section headed with a title that stopped his thumb dead on the page:

*On Living Seals: Their Making, Their Cost, and the Ethics Thereof*

The word *Ethics* underlined twice, and beside it, in the margin, in Veyra's hand: **there are none. proceed anyway.**

He read.

A seal, the text said, could be wrought from wax, from stone, from salt, from blood dried on parchment. All such seals failed eventually, because all materials remembered being other things. Wax remembered flowing. Stone remembered fire. A made thing always strained toward unmaking, and a seal was nothing but an argument against time, and time won every argument given enough centuries.

But there was one material that did not fail. One vessel whose memory could be emptied first, so that it held nothing else, and therefore held the binding whole.

*A living anchor,* Veyra had written. *Take a soul young enough that its own story is shallow — a story only a few winters deep can be poured out and refilled. Hollow the vessel. Speak the Old Binding into the emptiness until the emptiness learns the words by heart. What remains is not a child. What remains is a door shaped like a child, and behind the door, whatever you could not bear to let walk free.*

*Such vessels do not age as others do. They cannot die while the binding holds. They do not dream, because dreams would give the bound thing somewhere to go.*

*They do not forget, because forgetting is the one mercy we could not teach them.*

Odile sat very still. Somewhere above him the floorboards creaked as the boy turned over, and the sound went through Odile like a hook through soft tissue.

Seals are made, then. Not born. Somebody had taken a child — this child, or one like him — and hollowed him the way you'd hollow a gourd, and filled the emptiness with a language instead of a life, and the language was holding shut a door, and behind the door —

Behind the door was the thing in the crown. He didn't know how he knew that. He knew it the way he knew the taste of smoke meant fire: totally, and without evidence.

He read on. There was a passage near the end of the chapter, shorter than the rest, written faster, the pen pressing hard enough to tear in places:

*If the anchor is found, know this: it will try to speak to you. Not in words — words are the lock, not the key. It will speak in proximity, in need, in the terrible gravity of the unfinished. Every instinct of the craft says destroy the anchor, break it, end the argument. But a broken seal does not free the bound thing. A broken seal releases it unbound, unshaped, hungry in a way that has forgotten even hunger's name.*

*There is only one way to open a living seal safely. Someone must take the burden willingly. The binding must be transferred mouth to mouth, word to word, and the receiver must mean it.*

*No one has ever meant it.*

In the margin beside that last line, someone — Veyra again, but older, the letters shakier — had written a single word: ***yet?***

And below that, much fainter, almost erased, as if she had dragged her palm across wet ink in disgust at herself: *or is that why I keep him—*

The sentence stopped there. Whatever it had been about to confess, she had scraped it away with a knife, and the knife had gouged clean through three pages underneath.

Odile looked up at the loft, at the sleeping weight of the boy above his head, and understood suddenly and completely that his master had known. Had known about the child in the crown, or something like him, or something exactly like him. And had built — or been building — an answer. And had taught Odile just enough of the grammar to copy it and not enough to understand it, and then Odile had run.

* * *

That night he did not sleep either. He banked the fire and sat in its dying light, and the memory he had spent nine years not-having rose in him whole, the way a drowned body rises when the river finally lets it go.

It had been autumn. He remembered that because the tower's single window had framed a maple going red, and he had stared at it the entire time, cataloguing its colors with a desperate clerk's attention, because looking anywhere else was unthinkable.

There had been a man in the workroom. A big man, gray at the temples, wearing a carter's coat — someone local, someone who'd have had a family in the town below, someone whose laugh Odile might have heard through the market on fair days. He stood very quietly in the center of a chalked circle while Master Veyra walked around him, speaking. Speaking the language. The old one, the binding one, the one the child spoke now — and hearing it again across nine years, Odile felt the same ache bloom behind his sternum, sweet and awful, a homesickness for a home that had never existed. That was the trick of the tongue. It sounded like being loved.

The man's shadow was behaving badly. That was the detail Odile had buried deepest: the shadow did not lie flat. It pooled upward along the man's legs like rising water, and at its edges it moved independently, tasting the air, and once — once — it lifted a corner of itself off the floor entirely, a black flag testing the wind, and Veyra snapped a word at it the way you'd crack a whip, and it flattened, sulking.

"What's happening to him?" Odile had asked. His voice had come out very small. "Master — what's *wrong* with him?"

"Nothing that isn't already true," Veyra had said, without turning around. Her voice was calm the way deep water is calm. "This man carried something home from the wars. Something that wears its hosts. I am putting it where it can be watched."

"Watched," Odile repeated. "Or fed?"

She had turned then. He remembered her face precisely: not angry, not cruel. *Pleased*. The pleasure of a craftsman finding her apprentice asking better questions than expected. "Both," she said. "Eventually those are the same thing. Everything eats, Odile. The question is only ever *what*, and *how politely*." She had smiled at him. "You'll want to run now. Most of them do, the first time. Go on, then."

And he had. Down the back stair, out through the herb garden, over the wall with dew soaking through his shoes, the grimoire under his coat because stealing it had felt, in that moment, like the one act of courage available to him — taking the evidence, saving the truth for later, telling himself *I'll come back with soldiers, with magistrates, with someone*.

He had never come back. The truth had sat under his floorboard for nine years while he tended bees and called it peace, and somewhere in the world a man had walked around with a devouring thing stitched into his shadow, polite or otherwise, and a king had worn a crown that ate its kings, and a child had been hollowed out and filled with locks.

Odile put his face in his hands. The fire ticked down to embers.

He thought: *she let me steal it.* The thought arrived uninvited and refused to leave. She had smiled. She had said *go on, then*. What kind of master lets her apprentice run with her grimoire — her whole life's work — unless the running was part of the design? Unless the book in the valley was a seed, planted in the one place magic couldn't reach, waiting for the day the door-shaped-child came knocking?

Or unless she wanted him to think that. Rewriting was what the crown's servants did. He had no way left to tell a plan from a trap from a memory that had been tidied by hands other than his own.

But it didn't matter. That was the terrible clarity of it, sitting in the ember-light with guilt gone hard and bright as forged iron: it did not matter whether his cowardice had been her design. It was still his. And debts of that kind collected interest, and the interest had walked barefoot through the snow to his door.

Above him, the boy stirred. Odile heard him wake — that absolute silence breaking, a small body sitting up in the dark — and then heard the language, soft, half-asleep, a few syllables drifting down through the floorboards like ash.

The bees answered.

Every hive in the yard, every wintering cluster, hummed one low note in perfect unison, held it for the length of the boy's sentence, and went quiet again. As if listening. As if the valley's stillness were not empty at all, but a held breath — and something far away had begun, very patiently, to exhale toward them.

Odile stood up. He took the grimoire off the table and wrapped it back in its oilcloth, but he did not return it to the floorboard. He set it by the door, with his boots, with the good cloak, with the satchel he hadn't packed since the night he arrived.

"Get your shoes," he said to the loft, to the small silence up there. "We're leaving in the morning."

From the dark above, the child replied in the old tongue — one word, gentle as a hand on the head.

Odile didn't know what it meant.

He suspected, from the warmth of it, that he soon would have no choice.
