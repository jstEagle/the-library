## 5. The Loyalty of Liars

Marrow's Ford smelled of fish smoke and river mud and, beneath both, the green bite of healing herbs — a smell Odile had not encountered since he'd run from his master's tower with a stolen grimoire pressed against his ribs like a second heart. He stood at the end of the ferry landing with snowmelt soaking through his boots and let the smell do what smells do best: drag him bodily backward through the years.

He had been twenty-two when he last saw Sef. Sef had been nineteen, all elbows and fury, with a burn scar on his left forearm from the day Master Veyra's demonstration of cauterization had gone wrong and she had blamed *him* for flinching. They had fled the same night, separately, without ever speaking of it — two apprentices who each believed the other too frightened to go. Odile had gone north into stillness. He had assumed Sef was dead, or worse: caught, rewritten, walking around with someone else's loyalties stitched behind his eyes.

And now here was a painted sign above a crooked doorway on Tanner's Row: **SEF AMBROSE — TONICS, SALVES, BONES SET**. Unlicensed. The little caduceus beside the name had been defaced twice and repainted twice, defiantly crooked.

Odile's chest did something complicated and painful.

The child shifted against his back where he rode in the sling of Odile's cloak, and murmured one syllable of that old language — just one, half-asleep — and the frost on Tanner's Row windows bloomed outward in perfect spirals, every pane on the street, all at once, like a held breath released.

*Not here,* Odile thought, with a panic that felt like drowning. *Not yet.* He pulled the boy's hood lower and knocked.

* * *

Sef had gone soft in the way that meant hard years done well. He was broader, bearded, with ink stains on his fingers instead of scar tissue and gold rings — small ones, honest ones, the kind healers earned — on three fingers. For a long moment after opening the door he simply stared, and Odile watched recognition arrive like weather: confusion, disbelief, then a grief so old it had fossilized into something that looked almost like anger.

"You absolute bastard," Sef said. "You're alive."

"You're fat," said Odile, because there were things men like them could say instead of weeping.

"I'm *prosperous*, you north-country hermit, come in before the whole street sees you." Sef hauled them both inside, bolted the door with three separate bolts, and then — this was the part that undid Odile — turned around and embraced him, fierce and crushing, the child squashed between them and making a sound of protest.

"You found a kid," Sef said into Odile's shoulder.

"It's a long story."

"Everything about you is a long story. That was always your problem." Sef pulled back, wiped his eyes briskly as if annoyed by them, and looked down at the boy. Something moved across his face — quick, quickly mastered. "Who is he?"

"His name isn't safe to say. Maybe not even to think."

Sef's eyebrows went up. "Dramatic. You always were." But he didn't push, and that too was new; the Sef Odile remembered would have pushed until something broke.

* * *

They ate stew in Sef's kitchen while rain began against the shutters, and Odile told him everything he could bear to tell: the valley, the boy arriving barefoot in the snow, the hunters who served not the crown but the thing wearing it. He told him about the road reshaping itself under the child's words, the salt steppe they had crossed at night to avoid the Remembrancers' patrols, the way the boy's language was not speech but binding — an anchor-chain made of grammar.

Sef listened the way good healers listen: completely, without interrupting, his hands still around his bowl. When Odile finished, Sef set the bowl down and was quiet for a moment.

"Veyra," he said at last. "Is she—"

"She found us on the road. She leads them now. The Remembrancers." Odile watched his old friend's face carefully. "She claims she let me escape. Claims my silence was her design."

"And do you believe her?"

"I don't know. That's the worst part. She taught me that memory can be edited. So now I can't trust my own past, let alone hers."

Sef laughed — a short, unhappy sound. "Gods. Twelve years running and we both ended up in the same story anyway." He leaned forward. "Listen to me, Odile. I have a house. I have coin buried under the third floorboard of the back room — don't look at me like that, every unlicensed healer in the river country buries coin. I know the marsh families east of the Ford, people who owe me more than money, people who can make a child disappear into a life so ordinary no Remembrancer would ever sniff him out. A fishing family. New name, new history, real enough to survive questioning. And you—" he jabbed a ringed finger at Odile's chest "—you stay here. I need an assistant who knows hedge-craft from actual craft, and you clearly need somewhere the world can't find you. Again."

It was, Odile thought, the most beautiful offer he had ever heard. Safety. Purpose. Sef. The valley had been peace the way a held breath was peace; this could be peace the way breathing was. He could tend herbs again, real ones, in company. The boy could grow up gutting fish and forgetting whatever had been done to him.

"Yes," he heard himself say. "Yes, Sef, I—"

The child sneezed.

It was such a small thing. But the sneeze carried a fragment of the old language with it, the way a cough carries blood — a single syllable, involuntary, and the rain against the shutters stopped mid-fall for half a heartbeat, every drop hanging outside the window like beads on a string, before dropping all together with a sound like scattered rice.

And Sef flinched.

It was nothing. It was everything. A twitch of the shoulders, a blink held one beat too long, his hand rising halfway toward the boy and then stopping as if the air near the child were hot. He covered it instantly — laughed, said something about the boy being a storm in miniature — but Odile had spent twelve years learning to read the space between actions and their reasons, and he knew what he had seen.

Fear. Not of the boy. Of the *language*.

"Sef," Odile said carefully. "When did you first hear that tongue?"

"I haven't." Too fast. "Never."

"The syllable he just spoke. Did it mean anything to you?"

"No." Sef stood, gathering bowls, moving with sudden busy energy. "It sounded like a cat yowling. Kids make noises."

The child, sitting on the floor by the fire, looked up at Sef with those grey ancient eyes and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

* * *

Odile lay awake that night in the guest room above the surgery, listening to the rain and to Sef pacing downstairs, and took the memory out and examined it the way he examined a wound: gently, thoroughly, looking for what lay underneath.

A flinch. A lie — clumsy, immediate, unlike everything else Sef had said all evening. And something else, something he hadn't wanted to see at dinner: when the boy first arrived, before any word was spoken, Sef's gaze had gone to the child's *throat*. Not his face. His throat. As if checking for a brand, or a scar.

As if knowing where such children were marked.

Odile rose quietly and went down the stairs he knew creaked, avoiding them out of old apprentice habit, and stood in the dark of the surgery doorway. Sef sat at his own desk with a candle burned nearly to a puddle, writing. Not case notes — the script was wrong for case notes, too careful, too practiced. And he was writing with his left hand, though Odile had watched him ladle stew and mix salves right-handed all evening.

On the desk, half-buried under a ledger, lay a letter. Sealed with grey wax. Stamped with a sigil Odile knew from the road, from the hunters' livery, from his nightmares:

An open eye inside a closed crown.

*Sef has already been visited.*

The knowledge settled over him with terrible gentleness, the way snow settles on a roof — silently, weightlessly, until the timbers groan. He thought of Sef's offer, its warmth, its perfect reasonableness. A plan to hide the boy forever, delivered by a man whose hand shook when the boy spoke. Either the Remembrancers had rewritten his friend already, threading new loyalties through the old affection — or they had visited and *left him intact*, using him as bait, letting love do the work that force could not.

There was no version of it, Odile realized, standing in the dark with his heart breaking quietly in his chest, in which Sef was simply Sef anymore. The fracture ran through everything. Through the stew, the rings, the embrace at the door. Perhaps even through twelve years ago — perhaps Sef had never escaped at all.

The candle guttered. Sef looked up.

For a moment neither man moved. Rain ticked against the glass. Down the hall, in the room where the child slept, something hummed faintly — the seal dreaming, holding the world's oldest lock.

"You saw," Sef said. Not a question. His voice was very tired, and very young, and for one raw instant Odile thought he caught the nineteen-year-old underneath, the one with the burn scar, the one who had also run.

"Sef," Odile said, stepping into the light, "who wrote to you?"

His friend looked at the letter, and then at him, and his face did something Odile had never seen it do in all their years — it came apart, slowly, like wet paper.

"They know he's here," Sef whispered. "Odile — they've known since the moment you crossed the bridge."
