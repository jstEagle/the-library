## 6. The Crown That Eats Its Kings

The boy did not dream the way children dream.

Odile understood this by the second night in Sef's house above the healing rooms, when he woke to find the child sitting upright in the narrow bed, eyes open, lips moving in that old tongue — not speaking to anyone, Odile thought at first, but reciting. Counting. Keeping something wound tight the way a shepherd keeps count of a flock in the dark, by feel, by habit, because if he stopped counting even once, everything would come apart.

And then Odile stopped thinking of it as *the boy's* language at all, and began to understand what was happening: each night the words poured out of the sleeping child like water finding a crack, and they pooled in Odile, who had spent years learning how to listen without flinching. The language wanted a translator. It had chosen him — or rather, it had chosen the part of him his master had trained and never managed to burn out of him.

So when the memories came, they came in both voices at once. The child's knowing, and Odile's grief for it.

---

*In the beginning there is a name.*

That is how the memory begins every time. Not with faces, not with the gold and grey of the palace, but with a name — small, warm, spoken by a woman with flour on her hands, because kings' wives bake bread on feast days and this one laughed while she did it. The name belongs to a boy of four years who is afraid of the swans in the lower pond and loves the smell of the tallow-vats more than any incense, and who has a father whose crown is too heavy so the father wears an ordinary circlet indoors and lets the boy try the real one on once a year, on his birthday, just for a moment, just long enough to feel how cold gold is and how quickly it warms against skin.

*Once a year,* the memory says. *Only once. Because a crown should always be slightly strange to you. Father says that is what keeps a king kind.*

Odile lay in the dark of the guest room and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and let the memory pour through him anyway, because someone had to carry it, and the child could not carry it alone anymore.

The parasite came two reigns ago. The boy's father was already dead by then — a fever, honest and unremarkable — and the crown had passed to the boy's uncle, a broad laughing man who hunted boar badly and loved his niece and nephews loudly and had no idea what was coiled beneath the throne room, in the oldest foundations, where the palace's first builders had poured wards into the stone the way you pour footings for a tower.

It had waited a long time. It was patient the way rot is patient.

It took the uncle first. Odile felt this through the child's grammar, which has no word for *died*, only *was unwritten*: the uncle was unwritten over the course of a single winter, so gradually that even the man himself never noticed. His laughter stayed; whatever had chosen to laugh left. His love stayed; whatever had been loved went elsewhere. By spring he sat the throne and signed the orders and everyone who had known him all their lives said, *he seems well, doesn't he? Tired from the winter, but well.*

Then it needed heirs. Not living ones — *sealed* ones. Because the thing beneath the palace was old magic, older than the kingdom, older than the valley where Odile hid, and old magic obeys only the law of seals: nothing bound may move unless the binding breaks, and nothing unbound may be kept out unless something stands in the doorway.

A seal must be made of royal blood. A seal must be willing. And a seal —

*A seal must have somewhere else to be,* the child-grammar said, in its terrible gentle way. *You cannot seal a door using the door itself. So they took me out of myself, and put me in the doorway, and left my body walking around the palace wearing my face.*

The king's firstborn. Four years old when the Remembrancers came with their soft grey robes and their soft grey instruments and their soft grey voices saying *this will not hurt, little prince, this will not hurt at all.* And the worst of it — the part that made Odile get up and stand at the window with the frost ferns on the glass until his legs ached — the worst of it was that it was true. It did not hurt. Hollowing a child does not hurt. It is like being carried out of a burning house: you feel yourself lifted, and the warmth falls away, and then you are standing outside yourself forever, watching your own windows glow.

They made him into a lock. They taught him the binding-tongue — or rather, they woke it in his blood, because it was already there, sleeping in the royal line since before the kingdom had a name — and they set him in the doorway between the world and the thing that eats its kings. Two reigns now. Eight years in a doorway. Watching through his own stolen face as the crowned thing learned to smile like an uncle, grieve like a brother, rule like a king. Watching the Remembrancers go out among the people, quietly, season after season, to anyone who remembered too much.

*The hunters do not hunt runaways,* the grammar said, and Odile felt the truth of it settle into his chest like a swallowed stone. *Hunters want bodies. Remembrancers want memories. They take the memory of you from everyone who ever knew you, and then they take you, and no one looks for you, because no one remembers there was anyone to look for.*

That was why the child had run. Not toward freedom — a seal cannot be free, any more than a door can walk away from its frame — but toward stillness. Toward the one country in the world where the old magic could not reach him and could not call him home. He had walked barefoot out of the capital in midwinter, following some instinct older than his making, eight years old and carrying a doorframe inside his chest, until he fell down in the snow outside a beekeeper's hut in a valley that forgets.

Odile stood at the window a long time. Below, the town of Marrow's Ford murmured in its sleep. Somewhere in the house, Sef turned over in bed, and the floorboards spoke her name the way old houses do.

*She flinched,* the grammar reminded him, quiet as frost forming. *When I speak. Did you see? She flinches.*

He had seen. He had told himself it was nothing.

---

He found Sef in the kitchen before dawn, which was unlike her; she was a creature of noon and lamplight, never of grey beginnings. She had a cup of chicory in both hands and she was not drinking it, and when she heard him on the stairs she started — actually started, shoulders jumping — and covered it with a smile so quick and practiced that it frightened him more than the start had.

"You're up early," she said. "The little one keeping you awake?"

"Sef." He sat across from her. Ten years since they'd fled Master Veyra's house within a season of each other, and he had never once lied to her, and she to him, because liars recognize the value of one honest witness. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to remember that I ran from the same house you did, and that there is nothing you can say that will make me think less of you."

Her hands tightened around the cup. "Odile—"

"Have the Remembrancers been here?"

The silence lasted three heartbeats. In those heartbeats Odile watched ten years of friendship hold its shape, and watched the hairline fracture in it widen into something that would not close again.

"They don't announce themselves," Sef said finally, and her voice was flat and careful, the voice of a healer describing a symptom. "That's the horror of it. You don't get a visit. You get a *gap*. I was tending the Ferrow girl's burns — I remember the burns, I can describe every one of them — and I cannot remember the mother. There was a mother. The burns need a story and the story needs a mother and there is simply *nothing* where she should be. Just smoothness. Like a river stone where a face used to be." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Three days before you arrived, Odile. Three days before you came to my door with a child who speaks in the old binding-tongue, someone reached into my head and took a woman out of it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? They were *here*. This town. My house. Maybe my *head*, other parts of it, parts I can't check because I don't know what's missing—"

"That's why you flinch," Odile said softly. "When he speaks."

"Of course I flinch!" The cup cracked in her grip; chicory spread across the table like a dark stain of accusation. "Every time that child opens his mouth I hear the sound my own missing memories would make if they had a voice. He is made of the same thing that emptied me. Don't look at me like that — I know he's a child, I *know*, I've fed him and bathed him and I'd gut anyone who touched him. But Odile, listen to me, please—" She leaned across the table and gripped his wrist, and her hands were shaking. "My plan is still the plan. We hide him. I have coin, I have friends upriver, there are caves past Harrow Fell where even Remembrancers lose the thread. We take him and we vanish and we never speak of crowns or parasites or any of it, and we live. All three of us. Isn't that worth — isn't that enough?"

And Odile felt the whole warm weight of it, the temptation of it, ten years of exile teaching him exactly how sweet a small safe life could taste. He wanted it so badly his teeth ached.

But the child's grammar was still in him, patient as frost, and it laid one fact beside another the way it laid everything, gently, inevitably:

*A seal cannot be hidden. A seal can only be moved, broken, or passed to new hands. If we run to Harrow Fell, the Remembrancers do not stop hunting. They rewrite everyone along our path. Every village. Every friend. Sef again, and again, until there is nothing left of her to protect.*

"The caves won't save us," Odile said. "Sef. Hiding him doesn't end anything. The thing under the crown will keep sending them, and they'll keep emptying people until the world is smooth as a riverbed. There's only one place this ends." He swallowed. "The throne room."

Sef stared at him. Then she laughed — a short, awful, disbelieving sound — and let go of his wrist.

"You're going to the capital. With *him*. To the *palace*." She pushed back her chair. "Odile, they'll unwrap you both like gifts. You're an apprentice runaway with a stolen grimoire and he's a — a *doorway* — and you want to carry him to the one place in the world built to keep him."

"I want to carry him to the one place in the world where he can be *unmade*," Odile said. "Properly. Freely. Before the seal frays on its own and everything comes out at once."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Sef looked past him, toward the stairs, toward the room where the child slept — and her expression changed, softened, cracked into something so tender and so hopeless that Odile understood she had already decided to help them anyway, whatever she believed.

"He talks in his sleep," she whispered. "Did you know that? Last night he said a name, over and over. Not his. An old man's name." Her brow furrowed. "Why would he be dreaming about some gardener?"

The frost on the window seemed suddenly very loud.

Because the grammar stirred in Odile, turning over a fact he had not yet asked it for, and offered it now, gently, like a hand opening:

*The gardener who smuggled me over the wall. They took him yesterday morning. He is in the cells beneath the palace, and the crowned thing is asking him who helped me, and he does not remember helping me, because they rewrote him first — but they keep asking.*

"They'll kill him," Odile said, standing. "Sef, they've taken someone who helped him, and he's running out of time — and so is the seal."

Outside, far down the river road, grey-robed riders had begun to gather at the ford.
