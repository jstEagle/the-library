## 7. Remembrancer

The note came pinned to the door of Sef's house with a silver pin shaped like an eye, which was the first wrong thing, because Marrow's Ford had no silversmiths and no reason for anyone to write to a runaway healer in a hand so beautiful it looked like it had been paid to do it.

*The girl is our guest. She dines well. Bring the boy to the old toll bridge at moonrise. Come alone, or she will forget you entirely — and worse, you will forget her.*

Odile read it three times by candlelight while the child slept upstairs, curled in Sef's spare bed with his fists tucked under his chin like a much younger boy than he was. The words *she will forget you* did not mean ransom. Odile knew what forgetting meant now. He had seen it in Sef's eyes over the last two days, that hairline fracture widening: the way Sef had stopped humming while she ground her herbs, the way she'd looked at the child's sleeping face with something like hunger, or fear, and said, *Odile, who is this again? Why is he here?*

He had lied to her then. He was getting good at lying; the valley had taught him nothing if not how to bury things.

Now he sat at her table with the note in his hands and understood that they had taken her not for leverage but because she had already been touched — visited, rewritten, left half-finished like a seam someone would come back to. They wanted the boy, and they wanted Odile to bring him, and every instinct that had kept him alive since the night he ran from his master's tower screamed that the bridge was where he would be unmade.

But the child stirred when Odile climbed the stairs, and opened his eyes, and spoke.

Not words. Not quite. A sound like frost forming on glass, like the first syllable of a sentence that had been going on since before there were mouths to say it. Odile had learned enough of it on the road — enough to hear its grammar, which was not the grammar of speech but of binding, of *this shall be so*. And what the child said now, looking past him toward the window and the dark beyond it, translated itself in Odile's bones rather than his ears:

*They are coming whether you go or stay. Better to choose the ground.*

"Sentimental advice from a living lock," Odile said hoarsely. "You're supposed to keep things shut, not hand out strategy."

The child blinked slowly. He never smiled, but sometimes, when Odile talked to him like a person instead of a relic, his whole face went still in a way that felt almost like one.

"I'm going to get her back," Odile told him. "And I'm not bringing you."

The stillness deepened. Then the child shook his head once, small and absolute, and pressed his palm flat against Odile's chest, over the heart, and said a word that meant — as near as Odile could render it — *together-shaped*. As in: two things cut from the same intention. As in: whatever you decide, we have already decided it.

"You're six years old," Odile said.

The child looked at him with eyes that remembered being made.

* * *

The old toll bridge crossed the Marrow where the river widened into brown glass, its stones furred with moss, its toll-house collapsed into a roofless suggestion of walls. Moonrise came up red behind the hills, the color of a wound seen through water, and Odile walked out onto the bridge alone because the child had refused, with that terrible quiet certainty, to be left anywhere else. He waited in the shadow of the toll-house, wrapped in Sef's gray traveling cloak, and Odile hated him a little for it, and loved him more.

They came without horses. Six figures in undyed wool, moving with the unhurried precision of people who had never once needed to hurry, and their leader walked ahead of them with her hood back, letting the red moonlight find her face.

Odile's body knew her before his mind consented to. His knees tried to soften. His mouth went dry in the specific way it had gone dry at fourteen, standing outside a door he should not have been listening at, hearing sounds from within that no binding-lesson had ever described.

"Mistress Veyne," he said. It came out steadier than he deserved.

"Apprentice," she said, and the word was warm, and that warmth was the most frightening thing on the bridge. "You've kept my grimoire in good condition, I hope. The marginalia matters."

"You know about—"

"I wrote it. I bound it. I let you steal it." She folded her hands, a woman settling in for a lesson she had prepared for years. "Do you imagine, Odile, that the most careful woman in the capital loses track of a stolen book, a stolen apprentice, and a stolen memory all in the same night? You were never loose. You were planted."

The river moved beneath them, indifferent. Somewhere behind him in the toll-house shadows, the child made no sound at all, which was its own kind of roar.

"You're lying," Odile said. "Or you've been rewritten yourself and believe it. Either way—" He gestured at the five silent figures. "Either way, you took Sef."

"We interviewed her." Something flickered across Veyne's face — regret, or the memory of regret, filed away for later use. "She'll be returned mostly intact. We only need the parts that concern the crown."

"The thing wearing the king isn't the king."

"No," Veyne agreed pleasantly. "It's older than the king. Older than crowns. It eats its hosts and rewrites the loyal into furniture, and it has ruled this realm for two reigns while everyone who noticed forgot noticing. Except us. Remembrancers. We keep the true ledger." Her voice softened, and that softness found the place in him where guilt lived, the room he'd built around the night he ran. "I needed one seed outside my reach, Odile. One apprentice whose silence I could not command, because he'd fled me. Someone the crown-eater's web could never trace back. You thought you were running from me. You were running *for* me. Every year you stayed hidden was a year the parasite spent searching a valley it cannot enter, for a man who wasn't there."

It was beautifully made. That was the horror of it. Every piece fit: the grimoire's marginalia, the timing, even his own guilt sharpened into resolve — *she would say exactly this*, whispered the part of him that had survived his apprenticeship by doubting everything. *She would say precisely this, in precisely this voice, because she taught you that obedience begins with a story you want to be true.*

"Prove it," Odile said. "Bring Sef out. Let her tell me herself."

"She doesn't remember you fondly just now. We can mend that, given time—"

"*Bring her out.*"

A pause. Then Veyne inclined her head, and two of the figures turned back down the road, and returned leading Sef between them.

She looked small. She looked *edited* — her eyes moved across Odile's face like a stranger's, courteous and blank, and when she spoke her voice had the pleasant hollowness of a house after the furniture's been sold. "Good evening," she said. "I'm sorry, do I know you?"

Something broke in Odile then, quietly, the way ice breaks — along a line that had been there all along.

"You do," he said. "You stitched my arm when I came off the ferry. You fed a starving child for three days and never asked why. Your name is Sef and you hum when you grind comfrey and you hate the taste of your own tea but drink it anyway because waste is a sin." His voice cracked. "That's yours. Nobody gets to hold that."

Veyne watched him with an expression he could not read, and that unreadability was the answer to his question, though he didn't yet know which question. "You feel strongly," she said. "Good. Feeling is the one thread rewriting can't fully pull." She extended her hand, open, patient. "Give me the boy. The seal comes to the capital, the girl goes home whole, and you may even come with us — see it ended properly, the seed returning to the sower. Or refuse, and we take him tonight anyway, and Sef keeps only the memories we allow her, forever."

Behind him, soft as snowfall, the child stepped out of the toll-house shadow onto the bridge.

"No," Odile said — to the child, not to Veyne. "No, don't—"

But the boy was looking past them all, downriver, into the dark where the water ran, and he raised one small hand, and spoke.

One word. The oldest one Odile had yet heard from him, a word that made the river stutter against its banks and the red moonlight bend, a word that meant roughly, impossibly:

*I remember you.*

And out on the black water, something answered.
