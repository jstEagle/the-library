## 8. The Glass Steppe

The salt flats began where the last green thing died.

Odile felt the change before he saw it — a thinning in the air, as if the world had been wrung out and hung to dry. Behind them, Marrow's Ford's smoke still smudged the horizon; ahead, the Glass Steppe stretched white and blinding to the far mountains where the capital sat like a splinter driven into the land's palm.

They had one day's lead on the Remembrancers. Perhaps less.

"Hold on," he told the boy, tightening the rope that lashed them together across the back of Sef's borrowed mule. The child did not answer in words Odile knew. He answered in the old language instead, and each syllable came thinner than the last, like water through cracked clay.

*Sev-ah-neth,* the boy said. *I am coming undone.*

"I know." Odile had learned enough of the grammar by now to hear it: the fraying. The child was a knot, and knots pulled taut for two reigns do not hold forever. "I know. Just a little farther."

He did not let himself think about what *farther* meant. The capital. The throne room. The thing wearing the king's crown, wearing the king's face, wearing thirty years of rewritten loyal memories like a coat sewn from other people's skin. And him — a runaway apprentice who could barely read his own stolen grimoire without his hands shaking — walking straight toward it with the last seal in the realm tied to his back with mule-rope.

The mule picked its way down onto the flats. Salt crunched under its hooves like crushed glass, which he supposed was where the name came from. Under the crust the ground rang hollow. Nothing grew here. Nothing decayed here either, he realized; there were bones half-buried along the old road, white and unweathered, cattle and men indistinguishable at this distance. The steppe preserved what it killed.

"Keep your mouth shut if we're caught," Odile said. "Your language is worth more than both our lives."

*It is not my language,* the boy said, with the flat certainty of a child correcting an adult. *It is what I am made of.*

That was worse. Odile said nothing and rode on.

* * *

The storm found them at midday.

There had been no clouds. That was the first wrongness — the sky simply darkened in the east, a bruise spreading against the sun, and the light turned the color of old pewter. Then Odile felt it in his teeth, in the scar tissue over his heart where his master's binding-sigil had been cut and healed badly: magic, raw and loose and enormous, rolling toward them across the flats like weather with intent.

"The Remembrancers?" he asked, though he already knew. No human working smelled like this. This was older — this was what magic was before anyone taught it manners.

*Unbound,* the boy whispered. His small hands gripped Odile's coat. *When they unmake a witness, the taken memory does not vanish. It goes... here. It has nowhere else to go.* He looked up, and his eyes were doing the thing Odile hated, going silver and depthless, the seal looking out through the child. *This whole steppe is a grave for stolen remembering.*

The storm hit them like a wave.

It was not wind, exactly, though it nearly took them off the mule. It was *meaning* — fragments of other people's lives scouring past, a baker's name, a soldier's mother's voice, a wedding, a betrayal, forty years of someone's childhood shrieking by in a second and gone. Odile clamped his arms around the boy and pressed his face into the salt-smelling hide and endured it, and somewhere inside the roar he heard himself screaming, or thought he did, because one of the passing fragments was *his* — the smell of his master's workshop, beeswax and iron — torn loose from him like a tooth and flung away into the whiteness.

Then it passed. The light returned. The mule stood trembling, ears flat.

Odile counted what he still had. His name. The valley. The bees. His mother's face — yes, still there, thank every quiet god. But when he reached for the night he'd fled the tower, the details slid like fish. He remembered running. He could not remember what he'd been wearing.

"Boy," he said hoarsely. "The seal. Is it—"

*Cracked,* the child said. And then, smaller: *I am sorry.*

* * *

By evening the boy could barely sit upright.

They sheltered in the lee of a waystone — one of the old markers from before the kingdom, carved with script that predated the script the child spoke, if such a thing was possible. Odile built no fire; fire drew eyes. He fed the child dried plums and melted snow and watched the silver flicker behind the boy's eyes grow steadier and then gutter again, a lamp running dry.

*If I break,* the boy said quietly, *it goes free. Everything I have held. All of it comes out at once, and it will find the crown on its own. It always finds the crown.*

"How long?"

*I do not count time. I count what I am holding.* A pause. The child looked at his own hands as if reading them. *Less than yesterday.*

Odile sat back against the waystone. The stars over the steppe were viciously clear. Somewhere behind them, the Remembrancers were riding, and somewhere ahead, the parasite waited in its gilded chair, patient as rot. And between those two jaws lay a cracked seal shaped like a child.

There had to be something. There was always something, in the grimoire, in the margins, in the desperate arithmetic of apprentices who were never taught enough. He racked his memory — and noticed, with a cold drop in his stomach, how much easier that racking had become. How much of his past now felt smooth and featureless, like the flats themselves. The storm had taken more than he'd counted. Or the seal was taking it now, quietly, the way a wound takes blood.

Unless.

He remembered the fragment of binding-grammar he'd spoken aloud crossing onto the steppe, just three syllables to steady the boy — and how vividly he'd suddenly recalled a memory that wasn't his: a woman laughing in a kitchen that belonged to no house he'd ever known. The language didn't just bind. It *carried*. It was load-bearing speech. Whoever spoke it bore what was bound.

"Oh," Odile said aloud, very softly. "Oh, you clever, terrible old tongue."

*What?* the boy asked.

"You can't hold it all anymore. But the language doesn't care whose mouth holds it." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, high and frightened. "It only cares that someone speaks it."

*You cannot learn it,* the child said. *No one learns it. You are born into it or you are—*

"—or you are hollowed and remade, like you. Yes. I've read that chapter." Odile rubbed his face. His hands smelled of salt and plum juice and, faintly, of beeswax — the valley, the hives, the life he'd built out of silence. *But I don't have to be remade. I just have to hold it for a while. Speak it. Carry it in my mouth until we reach the throne room.*

*And then?*

"And then we'll see." He would not tell the boy the rest — that everything spoken in that tongue lodged in the speaker, that the seal's weight would settle into his bones like silt into a riverbed, that he could already feel, testing the grammar silently behind his teeth, how each phrase displaced something of his own. A word for a word. A memory for a memory. The oldest trade in the world.

He thought of his master, all those years ago, saying: *Every binding is paid for, Odile. The only question is who pays.* He'd fled rather than learn what she meant.

He was done fleeing.

"Teach me," he said.

The boy stared at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, he began — not teaching, really; the language could not be taught, only poured — speaking the anchor-litany of the seal, the great slow verse that held a devouring god folded up inside a child's body, and Odile opened his mouth and repeated it.

The first line cost him the smell of rain on the valley's south slope.

The second cost him the name of the innkeeper in Marrow's Ford who had liked his honey.

The third cost him — he searched, and searched, and could not find it — whatever it was, it was gone before he knew he'd owned it, and that was somehow the worst part, the thefts he would never even detect.

*Stop,* the boy said, distressed. *You are paying too fast—*

"Faster than that won't help. Slower won't either." Odile spat salt dust and kept speaking. The words moved in his mouth like living things, cold and exact, and he felt them take root, felt the seal's vast weight shift, redistribute, a mountain leaning from one shoulder to another. Around them the night air changed. The waystone's dead carvings began, faintly, to glow.

*Why?* the boy whispered. *Why would you carry it?*

Odile considered lying. He was, after all, getting good at it.

"Because someone should have carried mine," he said. "Years ago. Someone should have stood in front of me and said *this thing being done to you is wrong*, and no one did, and I have spent seven years telling myself the valley was peace." He looked out at the endless white steppe, at the road to the capital glowing faintly now beneath their feet, lit by his own mouth. "It wasn't peace. It was just a smaller cage with better light."

The boy said nothing. But he leaned against Odile's side, and the silver in his eyes dimmed to something almost like an ordinary child's tiredness, and together they watched the dawn come up over the Glass Steppe.

* * *

They saw the capital at noon on the second day, and Odile saw the Remembrancers ten minutes later.

Black riders on the southern horizon, moving fast, too fast for horses alone — the things they rode left no hoofprints in the salt. Between the riders and the city walls, the flats shimmered, and Odile understood with sick clarity that the Remembrancers did not need to catch them. They only needed to reach the gates first, and speak to whoever guarded them, and rewrite the truth of two travelers into the truth of two traitors before Odile ever set foot inside.

The boy swayed in the saddle. In Odile's chest, behind his breastbone, the borrowed seal pulsed like a second heart — heavier now than his own heartbeat, colder, and growing colder, full of a god's hunger folded very small.

*We are not going to make it,* the child said. Not a lament. An observation, in the tone children use for the weather.

Odile looked at the black riders. At the distant walls. At the storm clouds gathering again over the eastern steppe, fat with everyone the crown had ever erased.

"No," he agreed. "Not like this."

He gathered the reins, and began, quietly, to speak the old language into the ground itself — asking the steppe, in its own buried grammar, for the one thing it had never given anyone.

A road.
