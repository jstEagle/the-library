## 9. The Unbinding

The throne room doors were older than the dynasty, and the thing behind them had eaten every hand that built them.

Odile felt that truth in his teeth before he touched the wood — a hum like a struck bell that never finished ringing. The boy stood beside him, small hands buried in Odile's coat hem, and where his fingers pressed, the fabric had gone grey and stiff, as though a decade of winters had passed through it in an afternoon.

The seal was fraying. Odile could see it now without trying: threads of light pulling loose from the child's skin, drifting upward like pollen, drawn toward the doors the way smoke leans toward a draft. Each thread that escaped was a word unspoken. Each word unspoken was a lock with its key turning in it.

*Hold on,* he wanted to say, but he had learned on the steppe that even comfort spoken carelessly near the boy could reshape things. He squeezed the small shoulder instead.

Behind them, down the long corridor of the Glass Steppe's making, came the sound of the Remembrancers' boots. His master among them. Veyra Montrose-Asch, who had taught him letters and lies in equal measure, who claimed now — with a face he could not read — that she had let him run. That his silence had been her seed. He still did not know if it was true. He suspected he would never know, and that this not-knowing was itself the wound she had made in him years ago, dressed as an education.

The doors opened on their own.

* * *

The throne room was vast and cold and wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the eyes follow you. Banners hung from rafters high enough to hold weather, each one stitched with the faces of kings — and every face, Odile realized with a lurch, was the same face. The same heavy brow, the same generous mouth, repeated down two reigns of history like a signature forged so many times it had become someone.

On the throne sat the king.

He was not what Odile expected. The parasite did not wear horror; it wore dignity. It wore a grey-bearded man of perhaps sixty, broad through the shoulders, kind at the corners of the eyes, dressed plainly as a farmer at his own coronation. It looked tired. It looked good. That was the trap of it, Odile understood — it had eaten so many hosts that it no longer needed to pretend. It simply offered you the best version of whatever you most wanted to trust.

"Odile Dravenmoor," it said, and its voice was warm gravel, a hearth-voice, a voice for telling children stories. "And the little anchor. Come all this way. You must be exhausted."

Beside him, the boy made a sound Odile had never heard from him — not the binding-language, but something smaller, rawer. Fear in the grammar of any kingdom.

"You can speak to me," the crowned thing said gently. "I remember being small."

"No," Odile said. "You remember *eating* small. There's a difference."

Something flickered across the king's face — amusement, or hunger, or both wearing the same mask. "Do you know what I remember? Everything. Every host. Every life I have carried and been carried by. King Aldous loved honey-cakes and hated his brother. King Ferren wept at music. I keep them all." The eyes fixed on Odile, and they were kind, and they were bottomless. "I could give them back, you know. To anyone. I could give you *him* — the man whose body I am wearing. His whole memory, whole and warm. His childhood. His first snow. The way his mother smelled. All of it, freely given, if you walk away. If you take the boy and go back to your valley and let a kingdom keep its shape."

The offer landed in Odile like a stone dropped into deep water. Because that was the horror of it — he *wanted*. He wanted the king's memories the way a starving man wants bread he hasn't earned. Whole lives. Whole loves. A completeness his own thinned existence had never possessed.

"That's not giving," Odile said, and heard his own voice shake. "That's bait."

"It is *mercy*," said the thing, and for one terrible instant the warmth fell away, and what looked out through the king's eyes was old beyond reckoning, patient beyond forgiveness, hungry beyond hunger. Then the kindness returned, seamless. "But then, you've always preferred running to accepting gifts."

The doors boomed behind them. Veyra entered alone, hands empty and raised, the other Remembrancers held at the threshold by some signal Odile hadn't seen. She walked the length of the hall like a woman approaching an altar she had helped build.

"Enough," she said to the throne. "It ends here."

"Veyra," the thing crooned. "My oldest friend. My first keeper. Did you bring me another apprentice to ruin?"

"I brought you an ending."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Odile would spend the rest of his thinning life remembering it out of order.

Veyra spoke first — not to the throne but to the boy, and the words she used were the old language, the binding-tongue, rolling off her tongue with the fluency of decades. An unmaking. A transfer. *A seal needs an anchor,* she had told him on the steppe, when the storm scoured the salt flats white and the boy's language began pouring into Odile's mouth whether he willed it or not. *Anchors are made. I have made three.*

"I will carry it," she said now, arms open to the fraying child, her voice cracking on the words for the first time since Odile had known her. "Let me atone. Let me be the valley. I built this cage; I should be its last bar."

The parasite laughed — a real laugh, delighted, horrible. "Oh, *yes*, Veyra. Come here. Come home."

And Odile understood, in the space of a heartbeat, that it didn't matter whether her repentance was true. Whether she had let him escape or engineered him, whether her guilt was genuine or another rewriting worn so long it had grown roots — none of it mattered. If she took the seal into herself, she would be standing ten feet from the thing it bound. Anchors failed at close range. He had seen it happen to the boy for weeks. A seal pressed against its prisoner is not a prison; it is a door left ajar.

"No," Odile said.

"Odile—" Veyra began.

"You don't get to atone by standing next to it." He stepped forward, between her and the throne, and pulled the boy's hands from his coat and knelt, so their eyes were level. The child's face was grey as ash, the loose threads of light streaming off him faster now, and beneath the ancient weight in his gaze there was still, impossibly, a child looking out — a firstborn prince hollowed before he could name himself, given a purpose instead of a life.

"I'm going to speak," Odile told him quietly. "When I do, it will hurt. When I finish, it will stop. Both things. I promise."

The boy's lip trembled. And then — deliberately, gravely, the way a king might knight a subject — he reached up and pressed two fingers to Odile's lips, and breathed a single syllable against them. Permission. Consecration. *Speak.*

Odile stood. Turned to the throne. Opened his mouth, and let the old language come.

It poured through him the way the storm had poured across the steppe — vast, structural, a language not of words but of load-bearing truths. He felt it rewriting him as he spoke, felt memories slough away like frost off a warming branch: he lost the name of his village; he lost his mother's humming; he lost, somewhere in the middle clauses, the taste of the honey Sef had once stolen for him in Marrow's Ford. He kept speaking. Each sentence cost him something irreplaceable and he paid it gladly, because the alternative was letting this thing eat kingdoms until the sun burned out.

The parasite screamed. Not with the king's voice — with all of them at once, Aldous and Ferren and hosts without number, a choir of the devoured crying out through one throat as the bindings closed around it. The banners snapped in a wind that existed nowhere. The crown rose from the king's head, black fire licking off it, straining toward Odile like a drowning thing toward shore.

*A seal needs an anchor,* the language reminded him, speaking itself through his mouth. *Choose.*

"Take me," Odile said, in the old tongue, in his own. "Bind to me. I am going somewhere you cannot feed."

He caught the crown.

* * *

Cold. Cold like the space between stars. He felt it pour into him, coil around his ribs, sink rootlets toward his heart — and felt the valley in his mind, the magicless stillness of it, and understood that he was carrying the thing there the way a bee carries a sting: once, and fatally.

The king sagged.

For a moment the grey-bearded man simply sat, breathing, blinking, as though waking from a very long dream. Then his eyes cleared — truly cleared, human and frightened and free — and found the boy standing small and shaking in the ruined hall.

"Aldric," the king whispered. "My son. You stayed. All this time, you *stayed*."

The child made a sound that was not language at all.

The king smiled — a whole, unborrowed smile, the first true thing his face had done in twenty years — and then the emptiness the parasite left behind did what emptiness does. He died between one breath and the next, upright on his usurped throne, free.

The crown slid from Odile's fingers and rang against the marble, rolled, and lay still. Empty. Ordinary gold. Around the hall, the identical faces on the banners began, slowly, to fade.

Odile swayed. The world had gone strangely quiet inside his skull — rooms where memories had been, swept clean, their contents already forgotten by the only witness. He could not remember why he was crying. He only knew that he was, and that it felt earned.

Small arms wrapped around his legs. The boy looked up at him, unbound, unburdened, blinking in the strange lightness of a self he'd never been allowed to have — and asked him something in plain trade-tongue, halting and new:

"What happens now?"

Odile looked down at the crown on the floor, at the dead king, at the door where Veyra stood with tears cutting lines through the dust on her face. He thought of a valley where nothing followed, where a starving thing would waste away inside him winter by winter until nothing remained but a scar shaped like a choice.

"Now," Odile said, "we go home."
