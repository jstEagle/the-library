## 10. Where Magic Cannot Follow

The steppe had no mercy in it, but it had honesty, and Odile had come to prefer honesty.

He walked west with the crown wrapped in his cloak, bound against his chest like an infant, and with every league the salt grew whiter and the sky wider and the thing inside the glass quieter. It did not sleep, exactly. It *waited*, the way a tapeworm waits, the way winter waits under the first snow — patient because patience had always been its greatest weapon. He could feel it tasting him as he walked. Not his flesh. His past.

*The smell of beeswax,* he thought deliberately, each morning as the sun rose red over the flats. *My mother's kitchen. The hum of the hives at dusk.* He recited them like prayers, like inventory, because he had learned on the second day what the parasite could do when he let his mind wander: it found a memory and leaned against it, gently, gently, until the memory bent. By the third night he had lost the name of the village where he was born. By the fifth, his mother's face had gone soft-focus, a portrait left out in rain.

So he walked and he named things to himself. *Sef's laugh, too loud for sickrooms. The child's small cold hand. The word for "stay" in the old tongue, which is not a word at all but a pressure behind the breastbone.*

The frost of him thinning. That was how he thought of it, walking the white miles: he was a window in deep winter, and something warm-breathed stood close on the other side, and every hour the clear places grew smaller.

Once, near the middle of the steppe where nothing lived, the thing spoke with the king's voice. It had learned that voice across two reigns; it wore it now the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell.

*Apprentice,* it said, kind as a grandfather. *You could set me down, you know. Just here. The steppe is empty. No one would find me for a hundred years.*

"I would know," Odile said aloud. His voice cracked from disuse. "That's enough."

*You are losing your mother's hands,* it observed. *I can show you them again. I held ten thousand minds, boy. I remember everything my hosts remembered. Give me a crack and I will give you back your whole childhood, every hour of it, in perfect order.*

"That's how you got the crown," Odile said. "Someone grieved, and you handed them their dead."

Silence, then, and the long wind off the salt, and Odile kept walking, and that night he wrote in the ash of his cookfire with a charred stick: *Odile. Bees. Sef. The boy. Master Maren lied about letting me go — or she didn't. It doesn't matter now.* He read it every dawn until the stick broke, and then he cut the words into the strap of his satchel, and when the strap wore through he carved them into his own forearm with a fishhook, shallow and neat, because some archives should be written where even a devourer must look twice before feeding.

* * *

The valley took him back the way a mother takes back a prodigal: without ceremony, without question.

One moment the world was full — humming, layered, alive with the old magic pressing at the borders of him like water against a dam — and the next it simply stopped. The silence of the valley came down over him like a bell jar, and inside it, the thing in the crown went still with something very like horror.

*No,* it said. *No — what is this—*

"Nothing," Odile said. He stood in the pass with snow to his knees, breathing hard, and laughed until he cried, which confused him for a moment until he understood he was doing both. "This is nothing. This is the place where nothing follows. You ate kings. You unmade a child into a lock. And you are going to starve here in the quiet, in the company of bees."

He felt it strain against the binding he had spoken into it, the seal-grammar he still carried behind his breastbone like a swallowed coal. It pressed. It begged. It offered him his childhood again, entire, illustrated, and then it offered him threats, and then — this was the worst — it offered him nothing at all, just the vast offended silence of a predator discovering it has swallowed a stone.

He built his hut where the old one had rotted, on the same shelf of hill above the same dead-straight stream. The first summer, the thing in the crown raged. The second, it bargained. By the third winter it had grown so quiet he sometimes unwrapped the glass just to confirm it was still there — a dull grey light, guttering, no larger than a candle flame seen through fog. It looked up at him with what remained of its attention, and there was nothing kingly left in it. There was barely anything parasitic left. There was only hunger, and hunger alone, with nothing to feed it.

"You'll have a long time to think about the boy," Odile told it. "I recommend starting early."

It said nothing. Outside, the bees droned in their hives, indifferent as gods, and the valley held its perfect, magicless peace around them both.

* * *

They came in autumn, three years on, when the larches had gone the color of struck matches.

He saw them from the herb garden: two figures on the far trail, one tall and striding, one small and running ahead, and his heart did a thing he had trained it not to do, which was hope. Sef reached the gate first and gripped his shoulders hard enough to bruise, and Odile saw at once how the road had changed her — not aged her exactly, but *settled* her, the way sediment settles water. She had been all quicksilver when he'd known her in Marrow's Ford, all charm and deflection. Now she met his eyes and held them, and there were grey threads in her hair she did not bother to hide.

"They made me forget," she said, without preamble. "After the exchange. Wiped the last six months clean and sent me home with a story about bandits." Her jaw tightened. "But I'd treated a Remembrancer once, before any of it — a woman with tremors in her writing hand. I remembered the tremor, and I remembered that she didn't have one anymore, and lies leave scars if you know where to palpate. So I dug. Took me two years and most of my savings, and what I dug up is going to burn the Remembrancers' college to the ground, slowly, legally, witness by witness." A fierce, tired smile. "Turns out a healer makes a better revolutionary than a liar does. We're honest work, us healers. People tell us everything."

"And the crown?" Odile asked. "The capital?"

"The throne stands empty. There's a regency council arguing in circles, which is more freedom than that city has known in forty years." She glanced toward the hut, toward the dull grey ember sleeping under its wrappings, though she could not have seen it. "And you? What did it cost you? Your eyes went somewhere else just now, when I said *crown*. You do that."

"Little things," Odile said. "My mother's face. My seventh winter. Whether I ever liked honey or merely kept bees because they were there." He shrugged, and the shrug cost more than he let it show. "A fair price. I've seen the alternative invoice."

Then the boy came through the gate, and everything else in the world politely stepped aside.

He was taller. Ten now, perhaps eleven — it was impossible to say, and Sef had stopped trying. His hair was braided in the river-town fashion Sef's neighbors wore, his boots were good leather, and when he looked up at Odile his eyes were entirely, ordinarily human. Whatever ancient grammar had anchored itself in him had been spoken out, spent, transferred. He was just a child. He was the most miraculous thing Odile had ever seen.

The boy stopped an arm's length away and studied him with grave attention, the way he studied everything, and then he said, in plain trade-tongue with a slight accent he'd gotten from Sef:

"You're smaller than I remembered."

"You're larger," Odile managed.

"I practiced words on the road," the boy said. "Sef taught me. But I saved one for you." He took a breath, and behind his breastbone — behind both their breastbones — Odile felt a faint familiar pressure, an echo of grammar rather than the thing itself, a word worn smooth by handling. "*Stay,*" the boy said, in the old tongue, in the language older than crowns, and then immediately, grinning, ruining it, in trade-speech: "That means stay. In case you forgot. Sef says you forget things."

"Sef says correctly," Odile said, and sat down right there in the dirt of the gateway, and the boy sat down beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and Sef stood over them both with her hands on her hips and called them idiots in a voice that shook slightly at the edges.

* * *

The choosing of the name happened in winter, by the fire, as names should.

They had offered him a dozen. Sef suggested her father's name; Odile offered the names of saints and beekeepers. The boy considered each with the seriousness he brought to everything, turning them over like stones, and rejected them all. Names given by others, he explained patiently, were just another kind of binding, and he had been bound enough for several lifetimes.

He chose his own on the eve of the solstice, standing at the window while snow came down soft and endless beyond the glass.

"Bramble," he said.

Odile waited. There was more coming; he knew the boy's silences well by now.

"Brambles grow where nothing else will," the boy said. "You plant them to keep things out. And the berries are good, if you're patient, and everyone complains about the thorns except the people who pick them anyway." He turned from the window, firelight on his face, and Odile saw in him no ghost of kings, no hollow where a firstborn prince had been scooped out and repurposed — only a child, wary and whole and deciding, freely, for the first time in two reigns, where he belonged.

"Bramble," Odile repeated. "Welcome home."

Outside, the snow fell on the hives, on the buried crown, on the starving grey thing beneath it that would press against the valley's edge forever and never pass, and inside, the fire burned, and Sef laughed too loudly at something, and Bramble fell asleep in his chair before the dishes were done, the way children are supposed to.

Justice, Odile thought, watching him. There had been no trial. No court. No proclamation read from the steps of a palace. There had only been a runaway who finally stopped running, and a child who chose his own name, and a monster learning the taste of its own emptiness in the dark.

Some nights, the parasite still whispered up through the frozen ground, offering him his mother's face, his seventh winter, the smell of beeswax. Some nights he was tempted. Most nights he found he didn't need it — because Sef wrote every week now, filling in the gaps with other people's memories, kinder ones; because Bramble drew pictures of everything and labeled them in careful letters, *bees, hut, you, me, stay*.

And on the last night of the year, carving the annual notch into the doorframe where they measured the boy's height, Odile noticed something that stopped his hand.

Beneath the oldest notches, in wood gone silver with weather, there were marks he did not remember cutting. Three words, shallow and neat, in a hand that was unmistakably his own.

*Remember the boy.*

He stood a long time in the cold doorway, snow gathering on his shoulders, trying to remember making them — and could not. But whoever he had been, that thinner, frost-worn man crossing the steppe with a crown against his chest, had known exactly what to leave behind.

Odile smiled, and went in to the fire, and closed the door against the dark.
