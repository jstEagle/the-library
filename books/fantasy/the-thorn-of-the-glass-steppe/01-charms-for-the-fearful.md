## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The night market in Vell smelled of tar and frying onions and the particular salt-sweet rot of a harbor city pretending it wasn't dying. Edmund Almeida moved through it the way she moved through everything: unremarkably, unhurriedly, a woman shaped like a shadow among shadows, her stall no wider than a door and her wares arranged with the studied carelessness of someone who had rehearsed carelessness for five years.

She had learned that the trick to invisibility was not darkness. Darkness drew the eye. The trick was ordinariness—being so thoroughly, aggressively mundane that a man could look straight at you and see nothing worth remembering. So her stall was ordinary. Ribbons and buttons, secondhand combs, tin whistles with dented mouths. A basket of knotted cord charms she sold as *sailor's keepsakes*, three coppers each, and if a dockworker bought one and found his luck turned afterward—found his hands steadier on the rope, his lungs easier in the black water below the hull—well. That was between him and the cord.

Edmund never claimed magic. Claiming was how they caught you.

The edict had gone up on the palace gates the spring of her nineteenth year, nailed there by men whose faces she could no longer quite recall, though she remembered the nails going in one by one like a heartbeat slowing down. *By Order of King Aldous: The Practice of Spellcraft Within City Bounds Is Forbidden. The Penalty Is Fire.* Five years now. Long enough that children born after the edict thought of magic as a story mothers told too quietly, a word you mouthed rather than said. Long enough that Edmund had folded her gift down small and small and smaller until it fit inside knotted cords and whispered charms, inside the space between a button and a coat.

"Oi. The knot-woman."

He was a big man, the kind the harbor made—shoulders like cargo, forehands scarred white from line-work—and he came to her stall with the shuffling gait of someone who wanted very much not to be seen wanting something. Edmund knew the gait. She'd worn it herself once, before she'd learned to walk without wanting at all.

"Knots are three coppers," she said. "Whistles are five. I don't do repairs."

"I don't want a whistle." He glanced over his shoulder. The market churned behind him: a fishwife screaming prices, two boys chasing a cat through the trestles, lantern-light swimming on wet cobblestones. He leaned in. His breath smelled of cheap brandy and fear, which was a combination Edmund knew well; fear had its own flavor, and she'd been tasting other people's for half a decade. "My girl's got the lung-fever. Third winter running. The physickers want eight silver just to look at her, and I—" His jaw worked. "My mother used to say there was a woman off Cordwainer Lane who tied healing knots. Before."

*Before.* The word hung between them like smoke.

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. This was always the moment—the hinge of it, the terrible little door. She could send him away. She should send him away. Every rule she'd written for herself said so: never take a custom you can't disguise, never treat what needs treating, never let them look at you twice. A fever-charm was different work from a luck-knot. It took real craft, real power, and power left residue the way a fire left ash. The patrols had hounds for residue. Not dogs—hounds, the pale eyeless things that walked beside the gray-cloaked men and turned their blind faces toward whatever had been touched by working.

And yet.

"How old is your girl?" Edmund asked, which was already a surrender and she knew it.

"Seven." His voice cracked on the number like a hull on rocks.

Seven. Edmund had been seven once. There was a hole where most of that year should have been—one of many holes, smooth-edged and inexplicable, gaps in her memory like missing teeth—but she remembered fragments. Her mother's hands tying a cord around her wrist. *This is a holding-knot, little thorn. It holds what matters.*

"Sit," Edmund said. "Look at the whistles. Pick one up. Make it look like you're buying."

While he handled the dented tin with enormous, trembling concentration, Edmund reached beneath her table into the false bottom of a crate of buttons. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay her working kit: cords of three colors, a needle of bone, a stoppered vial of river-water blessed by nobody because blessing had been outlawed too, and a small brass thimble that had belonged to her mother and that Edmund had never once let another hand touch.

She worked fast, fingers moving on their own knowledge. Red cord for blood and breath. A triple bight, then a weaver's loop, then the knot her mother had called *the lung's door*—a slipknot that opened when the breath opened, closed when the breath closed, holding the rhythm steady the way a keel held a boat. Into the knot she fed the smallest thread of her power, barely a whisper, barely a breath of warmth. It was like opening a window in a house where you'd kept every shutter shut for years: light spilled out of her before she could stop it, golden and familiar and achingly like home, and she slammed the window again and knotted the charm off and felt the residue prickle along her skin like static.

Three seconds of exposure. That was all she allowed. Three seconds, and then the charm was dead-seeming, just cord, and she was just a woman selling trinkets.

"For your daughter," Edmund said aloud, sliding the red knot across the table under cover of the whistle. "Sailor's keepsake. For luck. You hang it above her bed, and—" she met his eyes, and let one syllable carry more than it should, "—you leave the window open a crack at night."

He understood. They always understood; that was the cruel joke of it, that the people who needed magic most were also the people best at reading what wasn't said. His fist closed around the knot like it was the last plank of a wrecked ship.

"What do I owe you?"

"Three coppers for the whistle. Knot's thrown in. Buy one every time you come to market and nobody looks twice at me."

His throat moved. "Woman—"

"Don't," Edmund said, not unkindly. "Don't thank me, don't remember me, don't tell anyone, even your mother, especially your mother. You bought a whistle and a bit of string. That's the whole story, start to finish." She smiled, and made sure the smile didn't reach anywhere true. "Go home to your girl."

He went. She watched him go, this mountain of a man clutching a scrap of red cord, and felt the familiar ache open under her ribs—the ache that had no name she was willing to give it. It wasn't guilt exactly. She'd done the right thing, or near enough. It was more like the loneliness of a lighthouse keeper: you saved people from the dark, and the dark was all you ever got to touch.

*One day,* she told herself, the way she told herself every night, *one day the edict burns instead of us.* But she'd stopped believing it somewhere around year three, and now the words were just another charm, knotted tight to keep the despair from bleeding through.

She was restacking the whistles when the hounds began to scream.

* * *

Everyone in Vell knew the sound. It came once or twice a season, usually from the Warrens or the tanners' quarter—a high, keening cry with no dog in it at all, a sound like a hinge made of grief. When the hounds sang, the market emptied. Not slowly, not with conversation trailing off. All at once, like a held breath released: fishwives snuffing their lanterns mid-word, pickpockets abandoning purses still warm from the lifting, the whole bright organism of the night market collapsing inward and going dark, because everyone in Vell understood the arithmetic. Whatever the hounds had found, it wasn't yours. Be elsewhere.

Edmund stood very still among the scattering crowd, one hand flat on her table, and did the thing she'd trained herself to do instead of panic: she counted.

Residue. She'd worked tonight. Three seconds of golden light in a city full of pale blind noses, and the hounds were singing from the harbor end of Rope Walk, which meant they'd scented something, which meant—

No. Maybe not her. The harbor was full of residue that wasn't hers: sea-magic in the brine, the old drowned wards beneath the pilings, a hundred small illegal warmths. The patrols swept the market every few weeks whether they smelled anything or not, because fear was cheaper than hound-feed and lasted longer.

But her fingers were still prickling. And the singing was getting closer.

"Pack up," muttered the candle-seller next to her, an old woman with a face like a walnut and eyes that missed nothing. "You heard them. Pack up, girl, go home."

"You go first, mistress. I'm nearly done."

It was a lie. Edmund wasn't nearly done; she hadn't started. But she watched the old woman shuffle off into the dark and felt a small, grim satisfaction in having lied well. Then she flipped the tarp over her table in three practiced motions, hoisted the crate of buttons onto her shoulder, and became, in the space of ten heartbeats, not a vendor but a woman carrying laundry home late, head down, shoulders rounded, utterly forgettable.

The gray cloaks came up Rope Walk four abreast, lanterns hooded to slits, and beside them walked the hounds.

Edmund had seen them only twice before, from distance, and both times her body had reacted before her mind: a cold flood, a tightening behind the sternum, the animal certainty of prey. They looked wrong the way deep water looks wrong—not monstrous, exactly, but *uninhabited*. Pale as peeled skin, legless, moving on a ripple of muscle beneath them, eyeless heads sweeping side to side. As she watched, frozen in her laundry-woman's slouch, the nearest hound's head swung toward the market stalls.

Toward the residue of three seconds of golden light.

Edmund turned and walked into the alley.

She didn't run. Running drew the eye and the ear, and the gray cloaks had whistles of their own. Instead she walked, quick and quiet, into the labyrinth behind the harbor front that she had spent five years learning the way other people learned scripture: the alley behind the chandlery where the drain grate lifted if you pulled left; the passage between the cooperage and the tanner's yard that stank so foully even the hounds slowed there; the stair of thirteen steps behind Mother Hessel's lodging-house that climbed to a roof-line connecting nine buildings like beads on a string. Vell had been built by people who expected to be besieged, and the poor had spent centuries carving the siege-city into a second city of cuts and crawls, and Edmund Almeida had memorized every vein of it.

Behind her, faint but gaining, the hounds sang.

She took the chandlery grate at a crouch, dropped into the drainage cut, and moved through blackness by touch and memory while her mind performed its cold arithmetic. *They'll sweep the market. They'll find the spot where I worked—I can't help where the residue settles, it pools like spilled wine. They'll question the candle-seller. Walnut-face saw me leave. Direction, at least.* Which meant she couldn't go home. Going home was the one move they'd expect, and the one place they'd wait, patient as tide.

Which meant the roof of the sail-loft, and a night of cold, and a morning of deciding who she would pretend to be tomorrow.

Her chest hurt. Not fear—or not only fear. Something older and less nameable, the ache of the lighthouse keeper again, rising in her throat. She had given that dockworker a knot to hold his daughter's breath steady, and she had done it knowing the cost might be everything, and she would do it again tomorrow, and the next night, because that was the whole rotten beautiful truth of her: Edmund Almeida could trust no one, could love no one openly, could keep no friend longer than a season, but she could not stop mending strangers. Her mother had left her two inheritances. One was the craft. The other was the compulsion to use it on people who needed it, edict or no edict, fire or no fire.

*This is a holding-knot, little thorn. It holds what matters.*

The memory surfaced and sank again, smooth-edged, incomplete. Her mother's voice, yes. Her mother's hands, yes. Her mother's face—

Nothing. A blank, soft as river fog. It was the largest of the holes, the one she never probed because probing it felt like pressing a bruise the size of her whole childhood. She had a mother the way she had a homeland: technically, on paper, in fragments. The woman had been there, and then there were the holes, and then there was Vell, and five years of careful nothing.

Edmund climbed out of the drainage cut behind the sail-loft, hauled herself up the drainpipe with a thief's economy she'd never intended to learn, and settled onto the rooftop with the crate of buttons hugged to her chest like a child. Below, torchlight swarmed the market square. Voices barked questions. Somewhere a woman was crying, maybe the candle-seller, maybe no one important, maybe everyone in Vell eventually.

The hounds had gone quiet. They hadn't found her. Not tonight.

But they had found *something*—she was almost sure of it now, replaying the sweep in her mind, the way the gray cloaks had split at the mouth of Rope Walk with purpose rather than routine. A raid, not a patrol. Someone had been taken, or was being taken, someone with residue strong enough to sing the hounds awake.

Another hedge-worker, then. Another name she'd never know, another neighbor in her invisible country of one, harvested out of the world while Edmund sat on a rooftop counting her own heartbeats.

She stayed until the torches dwindled, until the cold soaked through her coat and the crate of buttons pressed its geometry into her arms. Then she whispered the small charm that warmed her fingers—two seconds' light, no more, the cheapest coin in her purse—and looked out across the sleeping city, at the palace on its hill with its windows burning yellow and certain, at the harbor breathing below.

Tomorrow, she promised herself, she would be more careful. Smaller. Quieter. Tomorrow the knots would be only knots.

Tomorrow, she would believe it.

She almost did.

It was three days later, on an evening of clean cold air and early stars, that Sera found her—or rather, that Edmund walked into the Lamplighter tavern to meet the archivist she'd been persuaded, against every instinct, to have a drink with, and felt the strange sensation of a locked door in her chest swinging open an inch. But that was later. Tonight there was only the roof, and the cold, and the city, and the red cord traveling home in a dockworker's pocket toward a sickbed and a small girl's steady breath.

Tonight that was enough.

It had to be.
