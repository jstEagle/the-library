## 3. A Century of Tomorrow

Sera kissed me at the corner of Bellwether Lane, quick and shy as a struck match, and then said the words that would end my life as I knew it.

"There's a book I want to show you."

I should have gone home. That's the thing I return to, in the long nights after—the clean, ordinary exit I didn't take. She'd had a lovely evening. So had I, which was its own small catastrophe; I hadn't let myself have a lovely evening in five years, and now that I'd had one, some buried part of me was already greedy for another, already planning how to arrange it. Sera stood under the gaslamp with ink on her fingers and rain in her hair and a book-shaped secret behind her eyes, and instead of saying *goodnight*, I said:

"What kind of book?"

"The kind no one can catalogue." She laughed, but it came out crooked. "Sorry. That sounds mad. It's just—I've been trying to file it for three weeks and it keeps refusing to be filed. And tonight you did the thing with your hands. The knot. The way you tied my scarf when the wind picked up." Her eyes were steady on mine, guileless and terribly perceptive. "My grandmother used to tie knots like that. She called them charms. She died before I could learn what they were, so maybe I'm wrong. But I don't think I'm wrong about you."

The lamp above us hissed. Somewhere down the lane, a drunk was singing an old harvest song with the words worn off it, just the tune left, like a body without bones.

Five years is a long time to practice a single skill. Mine was stillness. I could stand in a room where someone spoke the word *witch* and keep my face smooth as pondwater. I could sell luck-knots to dockworkers and blessing-candles to grandmothers and never once let the craft show through my hands. Stillness had kept me alive in Vell, where the edict turned spellcraft into kindling and gifted folk into smoke.

But stillness has a weakness, and its name is wanting.

Because I *wanted*—God help me, I wanted—to be seen by this woman with ink-stained fingers who kissed like a question she wasn't sure she had the right to ask. I wanted someone to know the whole shape of me, the hedge-witch sigils pricked into my inner wrist, the grammar of cantrips my mother sang into my bones before I could walk. I wanted it so badly my stillness cracked straight down the middle.

"Come inside," I heard myself say. "I'll make tea. And you'll tell me about the book."

* * *

Sera's rooms were above the archive annexe, three narrow floors of stone stair winding up through the smell of old vellum and lamp oil. Her flat itself was a miracle of order: books shelved by height and then by color, which offended every archival instinct she claimed to have, and which she defended with the fierce embarrassment of a woman caught being human. A jar of dried lavender on the sill. A chipped blue cup she apologized for twice.

She pulled the book from beneath her bed.

I knew it was trouble before I touched it. Some objects carry weather inside them—I'd learned that young, sorting through my mother's things—and this one carried a storm two centuries deep. It was bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, tooled with no title, no press-mark, no censor's stamp. In Vell, every printed book bore the crown's stamp by law. This one wore nothing at all, the way a fugitive wears no name.

"Where did it come from?" I asked.

"A bequest. An estate clearance in the Glass Steppe—a farmhouse nobody had lived in for generations. Most of the lot was mildewed ledgers and farm accounts. This was wrapped in oilcloth, hidden under floorboards." Sera set it on her table between us like a card played face-up. "No accession number matches it. No printer's mark. The paper tests older than the binding, which shouldn't be possible unless someone rebound it deliberately to hide its age. And the strangest part—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide to trust me, watched it happen in real time, her shoulders dropping half an inch, "—the strangest part is that it won't stay catalogued. I enter it into the index, and by morning the entry is blank. Not deleted. *Blank.* As if the book were never entered at all."

"That's not possible," I said automatically.

"No," Sera agreed mildly. "Neither is what I'm about to show you. Shall we do impossible things together? It seems only fair after tonight."

I put my hand on the cover.

Cold went up my arm—not the dead cold of stone but a living cold, a cold with attention in it, like wading into a river that knows you're there. My mother's voice rose unbidden from wherever I kept it: *Everything made by hands remembers the hands. Be polite to old things, little thorn.*

Little thorn. She'd called me that because I'd been born in late autumn, all sharp elbows and fury, and because—

Because I couldn't remember why. The thought slid sideways as I reached for it, the way my childhood always did, a corridor that lengthened as I walked it. I frowned. Sera saw me frown.

"Edmund?"

"Open it," I said.

She did.

The first pages were watercolors.

Whoever had painted them had worked with a delicate, patient hand—landscapes mostly, at first. The Glass Steppe rendered in washes of green and silver, its glass-topped hills catching light the painter must have mixed from actual dawn. Villages. A river. Then people began to appear in the margins: a baker, a soldier, a girl carrying water. Portraits, each one labeled in a fine antique script with a name and a date. 1112. 1113. 1114. A century ago and more, someone had sat down with paper and pigment and recorded—what? Faces. Just faces, scattered across decades like pressed flowers.

"Nobody knows what it is," Sera whispered. "I've shown it to three senior archivists. One said a family album. One said an artist's study. The third looked at it for a long time and then told me to stop asking questions, and he was *pale*, Edmund. He was pale as milk."

She turned pages faster now, past farmers and midwives and children, and I confess I'd almost relaxed—almost sunk back into the pleasant fog of a second date, a strange curiosity, nothing more—when she stopped.

"There," she said, very quietly. "That one. That's why I asked you here. That's why I couldn't sleep."

I looked at the page and the world tilted.

It was Sera.

Not a likeness. Not a resemblance. *Sera*—the same narrow jaw, the same dark brows that met in concentration, the same small scar over her left eyebrow that she'd told me, hours ago over wine, came from falling out of a plum tree at nine years old. The painter had even caught the particular way her hair escaped its pins, one stubborn strand curling beside her ear. She sat in the portrait with a ledger open on her knees, ink-stained fingers curled around a quill, looking up at the viewer with an expression I recognized intimately because I'd been the recipient of it all evening: wary, hopeful, deciding whether to trust.

Beneath the portrait, in that fine antique hand, a date.

*Painted in the year 1112.*

One hundred and twelve years ago.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The rain against the window seemed to fall in silence. I became aware of my own heartbeat the way you become aware of a stone in your shoe—suddenly, painfully, unable to think of anything else.

"Sera," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "This isn't funny."

"I know it isn't." She was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten her knuckles. "I've checked the date four ways. The pigment analysis, the watermark, the provenance of the estate. It's genuinely a century old. And I—" she laughed, high and thin, "—I don't have any family stories. I was raised in a foundling house. For all I know, I've been walking around for a hundred years and simply failed to notice."

"You would notice," I said. "You'd—"

But I'd stopped listening to myself, because my eye had caught the verse beneath the portrait.

The script changed there, tightening, urgent, the letters leaning forward as if running. It was written in Old Vellish, the liturgical tongue they'd taught us fragments of in the parish schools before the edict burned most of those books too. My lips moved as I translated, slow and cold:

*Beware the year the thorn comes flowering,*
*for she shall come to unmake the crown.*
*What was hollowed shall be filled,*
*what was stolen shall cry aloud from the vault,*
*and the king who is not a king*
*shall meet the hand that ends him*
*wearing a face he once loved.*

The last word blurred. Or perhaps my eyes blurred. I read it again. *A face he once loved.*

"Unmake the crown," Sera repeated softly. "That's the phrase that got the third archivist so frightened. He read it over my shoulder and crossed himself, Edmund. In the archive. Where nobody has crossed themselves in thirty years."

"He's right to be frightened," I said. "In the edict's language, 'unmaking the crown' is treason. It's the phrase they burn people for. Literally—it's the formal charge. *Uttering words tending toward the unmaking of the crown.*"

"And yet a hundred-year-old book says it plainly, next to a picture of me." Sera turned to face me fully, and I saw that underneath the fear she was angry—which I liked about her enormously, and which frightened me more than the fear did. "So either I'm a century-old ghost playing an elaborate joke, or this book is something other than an album. Either way, I need help, and the only person I've met in five years in this city who might understand what this book actually *is*—"

"Don't," I said.

"—is a woman who ties charms into scarves," she finished, relentless, gentle, terrible. "Who moved her fingers tonight in patterns my grandmother's hands used to make. Who flinched when the patrol passed the market, flinched like someone who has been hunted before." Her voice dropped. "Edmund. I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm asking you to look at the margins. Please. Look at the margins of the verse."

I looked.

And my safe life ended on that page.

There, threaded through the illuminated border like ivy through a fence, ran a line of small sigils—protection marks, warding signs, the humble practical alphabet of hedge-witchery. Any hedge-witch in the Steppe would know them. Any hedge-witch would know *this* one especially, drawn at the head of the marginal line like a signature: a vertical stroke crossed by two curved hooks, closed at the base by a single dot.

My sigil. The mark I pricked into charm-knots so the magic knew whose intent it carried. The mark my mother had designed herself, altered from her own mother's version by one dot, one small dot, a family variation as distinctive as a fingerprint.

It was in the margins of a book painted a hundred and twelve years before I was born.

The room receded. I heard rain, heard the lamp hiss, heard Sera saying my name, and none of it reached me properly, because my mother's voice had surfaced again from whatever drowned place I kept it, and this time it wasn't a fragment. It was clear as bells, clear as the day she said it, kneeling in front of me in a kitchen that smelled of bread and hot iron:

*"One day someone will show you a page with our mark on it, little thorn, and when they do, you must run. Do you hear me? You run, and you don't look back, and you don't open the door for anyone wearing the crown."*

"Mama," I whispered. "Mama, what did you know?"

"Edmund!" Sera caught my wrist—my unmarked wrist, the left one—and I came back to the room gasping like a swimmer breaking surface. Her face was very close, her eyes wide and frightened and fixed on me. "You went somewhere. Where did you go?"

"I remembered something," I said stupidly. "I don't remember things. That's—there are holes. In my memory. Whole years, whole—my childhood is mostly—" I stopped. I had never said it aloud to anyone. Saying it aloud made it real in a new and horrible way. "Five years ago is a blur. Before that is worse. I have my craft. I have my mother's voice in pieces. I don't have *her*. I don't have her face. I couldn't draw my own mother's face if you paid me in gold."

Sera didn't pull away. Her grip on my wrist tightened, steadying, an anchor-line thrown to someone going under.

"And your sigil is in this book," she said. "A century early."

"Yes."

"Alongside a prophecy about unmaking the crown."

"Yes."

"Next to a painting of me."

"*Yes.*"

We stared at each other across the open book, two women on opposite sides of a table that had become a border between countries: the country where my life had been, careful and small and survivable, and the country we were apparently standing in now.

I should have run. My mother had told me to run. Every instinct honed through five years of stillness screamed at me to make an excuse, retrieve my coat, vanish into the rain, change my name by morning and my district by noon. That was the protocol. That was how I had survived while others burned.

But Sera's hand was still on my wrist. And beneath the fear in her face was the other thing, the thing I'd spent the whole evening trying not to drown in: she had shown me her impossible secret before I showed her mine. She had trusted me first. Nobody had trusted me first since my mother died.

"Close the book," I said.

Something flickered across her face—disappointment, resignation, the graceful beginning of letting go.

"Wrap it in the oilcloth," I went on, "and hide it somewhere that isn't under your bed, because under your bed is the first place anyone will look. Then sit down, and drink your tea while it's still hot, and listen to me. Because if that book says what I think it says, then neither of us gets to pretend anymore. And Sera?" I took a breath that shook on the way in. "You need to know what you've gotten yourself attached to. There are things I haven't told you. About what I am. About what happened to me five years ago—or rather, what didn't happen, what I can't remember happening. And I think—" my voice nearly failed, thinking of the verse, of vaults, of a king who was not a king, "—I think the holes in my head and the thing in that book are the same story."

Outside, the rain thickened against the glass. Somewhere below, on the annexe stairs, a door opened and closed—just a clerk leaving late, probably. Probably.

Sera wrapped the book with hands that trembled only a little. When she finished, she looked up at me across the bundled grey leather, and said the sentence I would carry through everything that followed, the sentence I would remember even when I could no longer remember my mother's face:

"Then tell me the story from the beginning. We have all night. Apparently we may have considerably less time than that."

She was right on both counts.
