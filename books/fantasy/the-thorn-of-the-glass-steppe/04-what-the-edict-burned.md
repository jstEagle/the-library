## 4. What the Edict Burned

Sera had learned to read a room the way other clerks learned to read ledgers: by what was missing.

The Lamplighter's Rest was half-empty on a Tuesday, which was normal. What was not normal was the way the empty tables sat—too clean, their chairs pushed in with the neatness of rooms no one had argued in for weeks. She knew three of the people who used to drink here. A cooper named Anselm who sang badly and often. A widow called Petra who mended nets and told fortunes she swore were only jokes. And a boy, Tam, sixteen, who could quiet a frightened horse with a touch of his palm.

All three were gone now. Not dead—dead left paperwork. Dead left grief, and grief was loud, and Vell's grief had always been loud, spilling into the streets with candles and wailing and the particular theater of mourning that dockside districts performed so well. No. These disappearances were quieter than death. They were *administrative*.

Sera would know. She had spent four months finding the seams where people had been unpicked from the world.

She sat in the corner with her back to the wall, a cup of cider going cold in front of her, and watched Edmund Almeida come through the door.

* * *

The date had been Sera's idea, technically—or her pretext, which amounted to the same thing and which she was not proud of. Three weeks ago, Edmund had come into the palace archive requesting a shipping manifest from forty years back, some dull customs record for a cargo of dyestuffs. Ordinary request. Ordinary clerk at the desk. Except that when Edmund signed the access ledger, Sera had looked down at the name and felt something turn over in her chest like a key in a lock she hadn't known was there.

Because Sera had seen that name before. In a book that should not exist.

She had spent two years as the archive's unofficial keeper of impossibilities—the misfiled, the uncatalogued, the things that arrived without provenance and resisted every system Master Corvane built. It was Sera who found the prophecy book wedged behind a false panel in the sub-archive, its watercolors still wet-bright after a century, its verses written in a hand that made her eyes slide off it unless she read it aloud. It was Sera who had stared at the portrait on page nine—a girl with ink-stained fingers and a crooked smile—and felt the floor tilt under her, because the girl on the page was wearing Sera's own face, down to the small scar on her chin she'd gotten falling out of a pear tree at seven years old.

And it was Sera who had noticed, in the margin beside the verse about the thorn and the crown, a small sigil drawn in faded violet ink. A knotwork mark. She had copied it into her notebook without understanding it, carried it for a year, and then one gray morning an ordinary woman buying a luck-knot at the night market had worn that exact sigil stitched inside her cuff.

Edmund Almeida.

So yes. The date was a pretext. But somewhere between the third glass of wine and the argument about whether the moon was a lantern or a hole, pretext had become something else. Edmund laughed with her whole body, shoulders first, head tipped back like she was daring the ceiling to object. She listened the way poor people listen—attentively, hungrily, as though attention were a currency she couldn't afford to waste. When Sera talked about the archive, about the smell of vellum and the way dust settled differently on books that were loved, Edmund had leaned forward with her elbows on the table and said, "Tell me what you love about it," and meant it, and waited for the answer like the answer mattered.

No one waited for answers. Not in Vell. Not anymore.

Which was why Sera was here tonight, in this half-empty tavern, having sent a note that said *dinner* when what she meant was *confession*. She watched Edmund thread between the tables, and she catalogued her the way she catalogued everything now: the quick eyes doing a sweep of the exits before they found Sera; the way her hand stayed near her coat pocket, where Sera happened to know she kept charms disguised as ordinary trinkets; the deliberate ordinariness of her, the practiced grayness, a woman painted into the background of her own life.

"Sorry," Edmund said, sliding into the chair opposite. "Market ran long."

"You're never sorry," Sera said. "You're always exactly on time for things you pretend are accidents."

Something flickered across Edmund's face—wariness, quickly smoothed over, but Sera caught it. Good. Let her be wary. Wary people were honest people; it was the comfortable ones who lied beautifully.

"I brought you something." Edmund set a small bundle on the table, wrapped in brown paper and twine. "Don't open it here."

"What is it?"

"A candle that burns blue if someone's lied in the room within a day. For your office." Edmund's mouth curved. "Archivists should have tools too."

It was a joke, mostly. But underneath the joke Sera heard the offer: *I am giving you a way to test me.* And that was how she knew Edmund already suspected why they were really here. Clever women recognized each other the way ships recognized weather.

Sera turned her cider mug slowly on the table, watching the ring of moisture it left, thinking about how to begin. There was no gentle version. There was only the truth, laid out like a body on a slab, and the hope that Edmund would see what Sera saw: not a monster, but a pattern.

"The cooper who used to sing here," Sera said. "Anselm. Do you know him?"

"Knew him. Bought a joint-ease charm off me last winter." Edmund went very still. "He's gone."

"He's gone. So is Petra the net-mender. So is Tam, the boy who calms horses. Eleven people in the Dock Ward since midwinter. Fourteen in Tanner's Row. I've counted nineteen across the city that I can prove, and I think there are more I can't." Sera kept her voice low, even, the voice she used for reading inventories aloud. "And here is the part that keeps me awake. They weren't just taken. They were *erased*."

"Erased how?"

"Erased the way only paper can erase a person." Sera pulled a folded sheaf from her satchel—copies, all copies, the originals locked in a drawer she did not trust—and spread them flat. "Anselm paid guild dues for eleven years. His name is in every ledger from 1189 forward. As of the third of Frostmonth, his dues ledger shows a blank line. Not crossed out—*blank*, as though the ink had been drunk out of the page. Same with his marriage record. Same with his mother's will, where he's named as beneficiary. The blank moves through the records like rot through fruit. Someone went document by document and unmade him."

Edmund's fingers had stopped moving entirely. "Ink doesn't do that."

"No," Sera agreed quietly. "Ink doesn't do that. Paper doesn't do that. But memory-work does. I've been reading everything the archive has on the old pre-Edict practices—heresy, I know, but the archive predates the edict and nobody audits the dead shelves. Memory-work was a discipline. Extraction, storage, binding. And there's a rite in one of the confiscated grimoires—" her throat tightened around the words— "a rite for *unwriting*. Removing a person's trace from records by consuming the memories attached to those records. Everyone who remembered Anselm well enough to write his name... the writing forgets."

The tavern noise seemed to recede. Somewhere behind the bar a kettle whined; a dog scratched itself under a table; the world went on being ordinary, obscenely ordinary, while between them on the scarred wood lay proof that ordinary was a costume the city wore over something with teeth.

"And the palace?" Edmund asked. Her voice had changed—flatter, careful, the voice of someone standing at the edge of a drop.

"That's the question, isn't it." Sera folded her hands so they wouldn't shake. "Here's what I know. The erasures require archive access. Deep access—not just the public rolls, but the sealed sub-archive beneath the Hall of Records, the one that requires a writ signed by the Crown itself. Every erasure window in the access logs—yes, I've been reading the logs, yes, that's above my station, no, I don't care—every single one falls on nights when the palace claims no business was conducted. Ghost entries. Stamped, authorized, and empty. Either someone in the palace is lying, or the palace itself is..." She hesitated on the word, because the word was madness, and madness was dangerous to say aloud in a city where saying things got you burned. "...hungry."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. The lamplight caught the violet thread at her cuff, the one she thought no one noticed.

"Why are you telling me this?" Edmund said finally. "You've known me three weeks. You don't know me. For all you know, I'm the one feeding names to whoever's doing this."

"I considered that," Sera admitted. "For about a day. Then I decided I didn't care, and that scared me more than the erasures did." She laughed, short and unhappy. "I'm not a brave person, Edmund. I'm a careful one. I have survived six years in that archive by being furniture—quiet, useful, invisible. I have watched men twice my age disappear into the edict's fires for less than what we're saying out loud right now, in public, over cider. I am risking my life sitting in this chair. And I did it anyway, because—" she stopped, because the true reason was embarrassing, enormous, and true.

"Because?"

"Because of the book." Sera lowered her voice until it was barely more than breath. "The one no one can catalogue. The one with my face in it, painted a hundred and twelve years ago. I haven't shown it to you yet. I've been carrying it around in my chest like a stone. And when I saw your sigil in its margins, I understood—I *understood*, the way you understand a fall only once you're falling—that whatever is happening to this city, whatever is eating people out of the world, the book knows about it. The book has *always* known about it. And it put your name next to mine."

Edmund's face did something complicated. Fear, Sera thought, and under the fear, terribly, hope—the same treacherous hope Sera herself had been drowning in for a year.

"I didn't ask for this," Edmund said. It came out rough. "Whatever the book says I am, I sell luck-knots to dockworkers. That's the whole of it. That's all I've let myself be for five years."

"I know."

"Do you? Because everyone in this city is pretending, Sera. The whole city is one long held breath. And you want me to exhale with you, and I'm telling you—" Edmund's hand flattened against her sternum, unconsciously, the gesture of someone protecting something fragile behind their ribs— "when people like us exhale, we burn."

The tavern door banged open. Cold air, a gust of river-smell, and two figures in the gray-and-black of the city watch, truncheons at their belts, shaking rain from their cloaks. Neither Sera nor Edmund moved. But Sera felt Edmund go taut as wire beside her, felt the small motion of her hand sliding toward her pocket, and Sera did the only thing she could think of: she reached across the table and took Edmund's hand, openly, plainly, the way lovers do, and turned her body so the watchmen would see exactly what they expected to see—two women sharing a table, sharing a touch, nothing worth a second glance.

The watchmen bought ale and argued about a football match and left.

When the door closed behind them, Edmund's hand was still in Sera's, and neither of them pulled away, and Sera realized she was trembling and that Edmund must have felt it, and that somehow this was worse and better than anything she'd planned to say.

"That," Edmund said quietly, "was either the bravest or stupidest thing anyone's ever done for me."

"It'll keep," Sera said. "I'll decide which later."

* * *

They walked afterward, because neither wanted the evening to end at a door, and doors had ears in Vell, and the streets at least belonged to the rain. It came down soft and steady, silvering the cobbles, filling the gutters with light from the few lamps the city still bothered to light. They walked with their hoods up and their arms linked, which was cover and comfort both, and Sera talked because if she stopped talking she would start thinking, and thinking, tonight, felt like standing on ice and hearing it speak.

"I was eight," she said, "the year the edict passed. My father was a chandler. He had a gift—small thing, hedge-craft, he could make tallow candles burn steady in any wind. Sailors paid extra for them. Called them *father's lights*. After the edict, he just... stopped. Snuffed the gift like pinching a wick. He never spoke of it again. He died when I was fourteen, and on his deathbed he gripped my wrist and said, 'If you have it, bury it deeper than I did.' And I said, 'Papa, I don't have anything,' and he looked at me with such relief that I understood, even then, that I had just told him the only lie he ever needed from me."

Rain ticked against her hood. Edmund said nothing, but she had stopped walking, and Sera stopped too, and they stood under the dripping eave of a shuttered chandlery—her father's old shop, though she didn't say so, though the coincidence sat in her throat like a stone.

"So I became a clerk," Sera went on. "Paper is safe. Paper doesn't burn people; people burn paper. I told myself that for six years. And then I started finding the blanks in the ledgers, and I understood that I had chosen the wrong hiding place. Because the thing doing this—it isn't burning people. Burning is honest. Fire leaves ash, and ash leaves grief, and grief leaves names. This thing takes the *names*. It takes people out of the story entirely. And the story is kept in paper. In archives. In places like mine." Her breath fogged. "I hid in the library, and the wolf learned to read."

Edmund made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could bleed.

"You sought me out," Edmund said. "Before the book. Before any of it—you picked me. At the market. Why?"

"Your sigil matched the margin drawing. That was the reason I gave myself." Sera turned to face her fully, rain running between them, and told the rest of the truth because there was no longer anywhere to put it down. "But that wasn't the reason. The reason was that you looked lonely in exactly the way I feel lonely. That specific kind. The kind that comes from being careful for so long that careful becomes the whole shape of you. I saw a woman selling safety to strangers who couldn't know what she was, and I thought: *there*. There is the only person in Vell who would understand what I am. And I was ashamed of wanting that, so I dressed it up as investigation. I'm sorry. I'm not sorry. Both."

For a moment Edmund just stood there, rain darkening her shoulders, her face unreadable in the hood's shadow. Then she reached up and pushed Sera's hood back, deliberately, letting the rain hit Sera's hair, her cheeks, her eyelashes—cold and clean and undeniable.

"If we do this," Edmund said, "we do it knowing what it costs. Not someday. Now. If the palace is taking people, then you and I are already in its ledger. We just haven't been read yet. Everything we do from tonight makes the ink darker. Do you understand? There's no version of this where we stay safe. There's only versions where we're safe *together*, briefly, and versions where we aren't."

"I understand."

"Say it anyway. I need to hear a witness say it. I need this to have happened in front of someone."

Sera took Edmund's hands in both of hers. They were work-worn, charm-scarred, warm despite the rain. "I, Sera Voss, clerk of the royal archive, do witness that on this night I chose conspiracy over survival. I accuse the Crown of Vell of making its people disappear. And I name Edmund Almeida as the only person in this city I trust enough to accuse it with."

Edmund exhaled—a long, shaking breath, five years of held breath, and Sera watched it leave her and thought: *so that's what she looks like unarmored. Terrified. Beautiful.*

"There's one more thing," Sera said, and her heart was hammering now, hammering, because this was the part she had rehearsed a hundred times and botched in every rehearsal. "Tomorrow night. The archive closes at dusk. I can get us into the sub-gallery—the restricted stacks, not the sealed vault, that needs a writ—but far enough. I want to show you the book. The real one. Page nine. Your sigil in the margin. The verse about the thorn."

Edmund's grip tightened hard enough to hurt. "The thorn," she repeated, like tasting something spoiled.

"'The thorn shall grow where least is grown,'" Sera quoted, because she had read it so many times it lived in her mouth now, sleeping, waking with her. "'And the thorn shall unmake the crown.'" She paused. "There's a second verse, Edmund. Mirrored. Written backwards. I've been trying to decipher it for a year and I've barely managed a line. But the first line of it says: *what the crown eats, the thorn must feed*."

The rain filled the silence between them, patient as paper.

"Tomorrow," Edmund said. "Dusk. Bring the book."

"I can't bring the book out. It stays in the archive—if it leaves, it gets logged, and logged means seen, and seen means—" Sera gestured vaguely at the rain, the dark, the whole watching apparatus of the city. "You come to it."

A muscle moved in Edmund's jaw. Sera watched her weigh it—watched her count exits, risks, the price of walking into the belly of the institution they had just sworn together to accuse—and Sera braced for refusal.

Instead Edmund stepped closer, under the eave, out of the rain, close enough that Sera could see the violet thread at her cuff and the small scar on her knuckle and nothing else, nothing else at all, because the rest of her had gone still as a held page.

"Dusk tomorrow," Edmund said. "Leave the side gate unlatched. The one by the coal chute. And Sera—" a pause, and then, softer, almost wondering, almost afraid of its own softness— "don't catalogue me. Whatever happens with that book. Some things shouldn't go in ledgers."

Sera laughed into the rain-dark, and it came out cracked and glad. "First entry in the new ledger," she said. "*Neither of us sleeps tonight.*"

They parted at the corner where Copperlane met the river road, and Sera watched Edmund walk away until the dark took her, and then she stood alone in the rain with her father's old shop at her back and the whole drowned city breathing around her, and she thought about the blank lines in the ledgers, and the ghost-stamped nights in the access logs, and the backwards verse she had never finished reading.

*What the crown eats, the thorn must feed.*

She had assumed it was a warning about Edmund.

Walking home, boots splashing, she began—for the first time—to wonder whether it might be a warning about her.
