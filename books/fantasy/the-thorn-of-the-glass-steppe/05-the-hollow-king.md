## 5. The Hollow King

Edmund had not slept. She had tried—she had banked her hearth, drunk the bitter tea of dried valerian root, lain in the narrow bed above her shop with her eyes closed and her breathing made slow and deliberate—but sleep would not take her, and by the third hour she stopped pretending it was a negotiation and got up instead, and lit a candle with a snap of her fingers because no one could see, and sat at her workbench turning the memory of the prophecy book over and over like a stone she could not stop rubbing.

*A century of tomorrow.* That was what Sera had called it. The book was older than either of them; the watercolor portrait had been painted before their grandmothers were born, and it showed a girl with ink-stained fingers and an expression of startled recognition that Sera wore like a second face. And beside it, in faded sepia ink, the verse about *the thorn* who would *unmake the crown*.

And in the margin, small and careless as a signature, a sigil Edmund knew better than her own heartbeat: three interlocking circles with a thorn drawn through them. Her mother's mark. Her mother's mark, on a page bound one hundred and twelve years ago.

She had spent the night doing what she always did when the world grew teeth: working. There were four luck-knots on the bench, half-plaited, waiting for the night market. There was a charm-bag for a fisherman's wife who believed her husband's boat had been cursed by a rival—though Edmund suspected the curse was simply bad maintenance and a wife who read omens into every storm. And there was the scrying bowl, which she had not touched in five years, sitting under a cloth at the back of the shelf like something dead.

Or like something buried alive.

She took the cloth off it now. The bowl was dark glass, rimmed in tarnished silver, and her mother had called it the *quiet eye*, and had taught her to use it before she taught her letters. *Scrying is listening, little thorn,* she used to say, warming the water with her cupped hands until it steamed. *You don't look at what you want to see. You listen for where it hurts.*

The memory came whole, which surprised her—so much else was missing, whole years of her childhood gone like pages torn from a ledger, five years ago blurred to nothing—but this remained: the smell of rosemary and hot copper, her mother's hands, the particular patience of her voice. Edmund clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood, and hated herself a little for the clinging, because she did not know yet how much of it she could trust.

A knock at the door. Two soft raps, then one, then two again—their pattern, agreed upon yesterday in the rain outside the archive, and Edmund felt something loosen and something else tighten in her chest all at once, because she had given Sera Vell the knock-code without thinking, which meant she had decided to trust her somewhere below the level of decision, and that frightened her more than the prophecy did.

She opened the door on grey morning light and rain-washed streets. Sera stood there with a satchel over one shoulder and shadows under her eyes and ink still ghosting the side of her right hand, and she looked, Edmund thought, like someone who had also spent the night losing arguments with a ceiling.

"I couldn't catalogue it," Sera said, without greeting. "I stayed up all night trying. The book won't take a number. I write the entry and by morning the ink has faded from the register. It doesn't want to be filed." She paused. "It wants to be *read*."

"Then we'd better find out what it's saying," Edmund said, "before it says it to someone else."

Sera's gaze went past her shoulder, into the shop, to the shelf where the scrying bowl now sat uncovered, catching the light like a pupil dilated wide.

"You're going to look at him," she said. Not a question. The king. The rite. Everyone in Vell knew: today was the Feast of the Unbroken Seal, when Aldous the Steadfast would appear on the balcony of the Glass Steppe and renew the ancient vow between crown and city, and half of Vell would crowd the riverfront to see him, and the other half would watch from rooftops and windows, and the patrols would be doubled and everyone would smile.

"The whole city will be looking at him," Edmund said. "Ten thousand pairs of eyes. Ten thousand hearts full of love or fear or boredom. All that attention, all pointed at one man." She touched the bowl's cold rim. "Attention leaves residue. Even hedge-craft can ride it, if you know how. My mother called it swimming downstream."

"And if the patrols catch you scrying on the king—"

"They won't catch me," Edmund said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and Sera heard it too, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"They caught your nerve, though," Sera said quietly. "Somewhere along the way. Five years ago?"

Edmund didn't answer. Sera didn't push. That, too, was becoming familiar between them—the careful stepping around each other's locked rooms, the courtesy of people who both kept secrets for a living.

"He'll be at the rite at noon," Edmund said instead. "I want to see him before the edict's next name lands on a list. If the palace is taking mages, I want to know what kind of thing takes them."

"And if you see something wrong?"

"Then wrong is finally useful," Edmund said. "Wrong gives us a thread to pull."

* * *

They watched from the roof of the chandlery on Tanner's Stair, a flat tarred space between two chimney-stacks where the smell of tallow and pitch covered the smell of the scrying-work, which was considerable. Rosemary and hot copper and something else, something older—burnt sugar, almost, the scent of magic worked hard and close. Edmund had drawn the circle in chalk that would wash away with the first real rain, had set the bowl on a folded cloak, and had filled it with water carried up from the river in silence, because her mother had taught her that living water remembered being a sea.

"Now," Edmund murmured. "Don't speak while I'm under. Whatever you see me do—don't touch me."

"What will you do?"

"I don't know." She smiled, and it wasn't much of a smile. "That's usually the interesting part."

She set her palms on the water's surface—not touching, hovering, the way you hover your hand over a sleeping child—and began the descent-words, the grammar of deepening that her mother's tongue had worn smooth with use. The water stilled. The rain-sound of the city receded. And the bowl filled, not with reflections of grey sky, but with light.

She swam downstream on ten thousand points of attention. It was like sinking through a school of fish, through flickers and glints—here a baker watching from his doorway with his arms crossed, here a child on a father's shoulders, here a soldier bored at his post, here an old woman praying. Attention has texture. Love feels like warm wool. Fear feels like needles. Boredom is a flat grey silt. Edmund moved through them all, letting the current carry her toward the Glass Steppe, toward the balcony, toward the man at the center of it all.

And then the current simply *stopped*.

Not ended—stopped, the way blood stops in a limb held too long. Edmund hung in the sudden silence and understood that she had reached some boundary, some skin stretched around the king and everything near him, and that the ten thousand streams of attention flowing toward him flowed *in* but nothing flowed *out*. He drank it. He drank all of it, the warm wool and the needles and the grey silt, and gave back nothing, and the nothing he gave back was the most frightening thing Edmund had ever perceived, because everything gives something back—a stone gives heat, a corpse gives rot, even the dark gives quiet.

She pressed against the boundary, gently, the way you test ice.

And the boundary noticed her.

Below her—in the vision, in the bowl, in the place behind the world where she was swimming—the king turned his head. Slowly. With the terrible unhurried grace of something that has never once needed to hurry. His face was Aldous the Steadfast's face, broad and bearded and weathered, the face on the coins and the customs-house seals and the mural in the Temple of Records. But behind the face, beneath it, around it like light around an eclipse—

Edmund had no words for it, then. Later she would find them: it was like looking at a house where every window is lit but no one lives there. It was hunger wearing patience. It was a vast pale presence folded impossibly small inside a human shape, flickering at its edges—not flickering like a flame, but like a picture that keeps nearly resolving and failing, a thousand faces almost surfacing and sliding under, faces that might have been memories, that *were* memories, stolen ones, harvested ones, worn like scales.

One of those faces almost surfaced, and Edmund's heart slammed against her ribs, because it was a woman's face, young, with dark hair and a crooked smile, and Edmund knew it, knew it the way you know a room you grew up in—

And the thing in the king's shape looked directly at her.

Through the bowl. Through the miles and the chalk circle and the wards of her own craft. It looked at her with Aldous's eyes, and Aldous's eyes were not Aldous's, they were holes punched in the face of the world, and Edmund heard—or did not hear, felt, received, the way you feel a door slam in another room—a sensation that arrived in her mind already translated, already dressed in meaning:

*There you are.*

She broke the connection. She came up out of the vision gasping, knocking the bowl askew, water slopping across the tarred roof, and she was on her hands and knees shaking and Sera was holding her shoulders saying her name, her actual name, *Edmund, Edmund, come back,* and the city roared around them—cheering, she realized distantly, the whole riverfront cheering, the Feast of the Unbroken Seal, long live the king, long live the king.

"It saw me," Edmund said. Her voice sounded scraped. "It—it looked at me. Through everything. It said—" She swallowed. "*There you are.* Like it was expecting me. Like I was late."

Sera had gone very pale. "What did you see? What is he?"

"That's just it." Edmund pushed herself upright, wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "He isn't. There's no *he* in there. There's something wearing him. Something made of—" She stopped. Started again. Made herself say it plainly, because plainness was armor. "Made of memories. Thousands of them. They're woven together into—into a mind, a presence, and it's inside the king like a hand inside a glove. The face flickers. Underneath the face. Like it can't quite hold the shape."

"Flickers how?"

"Like faces." Edmund hugged her knees, suddenly cold in the morning air despite the season. "Surfacing and going under. And Sera—one of them—I *knew* one of them. A woman. Dark hair. A crooked smile. I knew her the way you know—" Her throat closed. She forced it open. "The way you know family."

The wind came off the river and flattened the steam rising from the spilled bowl-water, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and below them the crowds were dispersing, happy and fed and unaware, streaming home through streets that smelled of roast chestnuts and river mud.

"Memory-work," Edmund said at last. The words came slowly, dragged up from somewhere deeper than thought. "That's what my mother called it. The discipline. She said it was the oldest craft and the most forbidden—older than fire-calling, older than storm-speaking. Taking memory out of a person. Weaving it. Wearing it." She stared at her own hands, at the faint burn-scar across the left palm she'd had since childhood and could not remember earning. "She said there was only ever one reason anyone wove stolen memories into a living core."

Sera waited. When Edmund didn't continue, she asked, softly: "What reason?"

"To live longer than a life allows." Edmund lifted her head. "You don't harvest memories for power. Power burns out. You harvest them for *continuance*. Every mind you weave in is another self to wear when yours wears thin. Another set of years. Another face." She laughed, and there was nothing of humor in it. "My mother taught me the theory so I'd know it to refuse it. She said memory-work always ends the same way. You keep eating tomorrows until there's nothing left of you but appetite."

"And the thing on the throne—"

"—has been eating for a very long time." Edmund looked out over the city, at the smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, at the Glass Steppe gleaming wet and pale in the distance like a blade laid on the horizon. "The vanishing mages. The erased records. It's not persecution, Sera. It never was. The edict, the burnings—that's just the net. It's *harvesting*. It's been harvesting this city for years, quietly, and now it's running low, and it's hungry, and it saw me scrying and it *recognized* me."

"Recognized you how?" Sera's voice was careful. "Edmund. How would it recognize you?"

And there it was, the question Edmund had been circling all night, all morning, all the long grey hours since the prophecy book, and she found she could not dodge it anymore, not with the taste of burnt sugar still in her mouth and the afterimage of that flickering face burned behind her eyelids.

"My mother's sigil is in that book," she said. "A book written a hundred years ago. My discipline—the scrying, the knots, the grammar of deepening—it comes down through her line, unbroken. And when that thing looked at me, it didn't look curious. It looked *satisfied*." She stood, knees unsteady, and pulled the chalk circle apart with the edge of her boot, breaking it, ending it, though the gesture felt absurdly small. "Whatever my mother was. Whatever she did, whatever happened five years ago that I can't remember—it's connected to that thing on the throne. Maybe it's afraid of her line. Or maybe—"

She stopped. The word she did not want to say sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Maybe what?" Sera asked.

"Maybe it's been saving room for me."

The gulls cried over the river. Somewhere below, a patrol drum beat its slow afternoon rhythm, marking the streets the way a pulse marks a body—alive, alive, alive—and Edmund stood on the tarred roof with her dead mother's grammar in her mouth and her missing years gaping inside her like a wound, and felt something shift in her that she had not felt in five years.

Fear had kept her small. Fear had kept her plaiting luck-knots and selling safety to strangers and trusting no one, invisible, inviolable, alone. But fear, she understood now, had been a misreading of the situation. The thing on the throne was not a danger she could avoid by being careful. It had known about her before she had known about it. Her hiding had never once hidden her.

So fear could go hang. What replaced it was colder and cleaner and far more dangerous.

Purpose.

"Sera," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The sub-archive. The sealed levels. You said the records don't add up—mages erased, blank nights in the access logs. If that thing is harvesting, there's a ledger somewhere. There's always a ledger. Memory-workers are hoarders—it's the first symptom of the discipline. It can't help cataloguing what it takes."

"You want to break into the palace archives."

"I want to read its shopping list." Edmund turned to face her, and the wind pulled at her hair, and the city glittered below, blind and beloved and being eaten. "You know the locks. I know the wards. And neither of us," she added, with a crooked smile that felt, for the first time, like her mother's, "has anything left to lose by knowing."

Sera looked at her for a long moment. Rain was coming again, blotting the far shore, walking up the river in grey columns, and the chandlery roof smelled of tallow and burnt sugar and beginning things.

"If it recognized you," Sera said slowly, "then it knows where you are. It may already know about me. Walking into its house—"

"Is safer than waiting for it to walk into mine." Edmund held out her hand. "Well, archivist? Do we read backwards, or do we wait to be read?"

Sera took her hand. Her grip was ink-stained and cold and did not let go.

"There's one more thing," she said, as the first drops came down around them. "In the book. The verse I told you about—the part I didn't finish reading last night. I copied it out before the ink faded." She pulled a folded scrap of paper from her satchel, damp-edged, and did not give it over, only held it, staring at it. "I ran the translation three times, Edmund. Three different dictionaries. And the second half doesn't say what the first half says. It's mirrored. Reversed. And the reversed verse says—"

Lightning walked the horizon, white and silent, over the Glass Steppe.

"—it says the thorn must feed."
