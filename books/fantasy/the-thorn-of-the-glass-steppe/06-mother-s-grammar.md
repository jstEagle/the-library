## 6. Mother's Grammar

The floorboard under the bed still remembered how to lie.

Edmund knelt on the cold boards of her rented room above the dye-shop, fingers hooked into the seam she had cut five years ago and never touched since, and felt the wood's old reluctance give way like a scab. Beneath it: a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a knot her mother had taught her when Edmund was—when Edmund was some age she could no longer name, an age that existed now only as a shape without a season.

She lifted it out. Dust rose in the lamplight, gold and slow, and for a moment she simply held the weight of it against her chest, the way you hold something you are afraid to open because opening it will make its loss official.

*You don't have to,* said the careful part of her, the part that had kept her alive in Vell for five years. *You saw what the king is. You know enough to run.*

But running was all she had ever done, and last night Sera had turned a watercolor page toward her and shown Edmund her own sigil inked in a margin older than either of them, and running had stopped being a plan and become just another word for drowning slowly.

She untied the knot.

* * *

Her mother's hands had been quick and brown and always faintly stained with walnut hull or madder root, depending on the season, because in their house magic and dyeing shared the same kitchen and neither pretended to be more respectable than the other.

"Grammar first," her mother would say, tapping the page with a fingernail worn blunt from work. "Anyone can shout a spell. A shout gets you hanged. But grammar—" and here she'd smile, and Edmund, watching her own small hands in memory, would lean in the way children lean toward honey, "—grammar lets you whisper."

The book smelled of tannin and beeswax and something underneath both, something green, like a forest floor pressed flat between covers. Her mother's handwriting marched down the pages in two columns: the left column in plain script any constable could read without alarm—recipes, mostly, dyes and salves and the curing of leather—and the right column in the looping cipher they had never named aloud, because naming things aloud was how things got burned.

Edmund turned the pages by lamplight and read her childhood in the margins.

*E., age six, salted the bread twice. Laughed so hard she cried. Told her the second salting was a charm for sweetness and she believed me, the little gull.* Beside it, a tiny sketch of a girl with hair drawn as a scribbled storm cloud.

Edmund stopped.

Six. She knew herself to be twenty-six. She knew the arithmetic of her own life the way you know the stairs of your own house in the dark—and yet the entry sat in her chest like a coin found in a coat pocket from someone else's winter. She could not see herself at six. She reached for it and found—the way you reach for a step that isn't there—a smooth blankness where a year should be. Not darkness. Blankness. The difference mattered, and the difference frightened her. Darkness might hide something. Blankness had been swept.

She turned back a page. Another entry: *E., age seven.* She reached for seven.

Nothing.

Age eight. Nothing. Nine. Ten. Eleven. She turned faster, lamplight shivering across the paper, entries marching backward through a childhood that grew less hers with every page—until somewhere around twelve or thirteen the blanks began to fill, thin and watery at first, then solidifying into memories she could actually stand inside: learning the mordant-bath charm at fourteen, the first luck-knot she ever sold at fifteen, the smell of the river town where they'd lived before—

Before Vell. Before five years ago. Before the blur.

Because that was the other gap, wasn't it. The one she had spent five years walking around the way you walk around a rotten board. Five years ago was a smear. She had arrived in Vell—she knew this the way she knew the edict existed, as a fact without texture—with a purse of coins whose origin she did not question, a new name, and no mother.

No body. No grave. No letter. Just an absence shaped exactly like a woman, which she had carried quietly ever since and called grief, because calling it anything else required questions, and questions were dangerous, and she had been so tired, and Vell was full of patrols, and there had always been a reason not to look.

She set the book down on her knees and made herself say it aloud to the empty room, quietly, the way you confess to a priest you don't believe in:

"I don't remember my mother dying."

The room said nothing. Somewhere below, the dye-shop's vats ticked as they cooled. Rain had started again against the window, soft as an apology.

And then, because the night had already taken everything else from her, she made herself ask the next question, the one underneath:

"Do I remember my mother at all?"

She closed her eyes and reached.

A face came—warm, brown-eyed, laugh-lined—but it came wrong. It came the way a portrait comes, composed, frontal, held at a fixed distance like a painting hung too high on a wall. She could not see her mother turn her head. She could not see her mother asleep. Every image of her was posed, and every pose was identical, and when Edmund tried to move around behind the memory—to catch the face in profile, mid-sentence, unguarded—it slid like wet paint.

She knew hundreds of people who could tell you what their mothers looked like while scolding them. While crying. While sleeping in a chair with their mouths open. Edmund had one image, perfect and dead as a coin, and she had carried it for years believing it was a face.

It was a mask of a face. And she had seen a mask like it only once before—last night, in a scrying bowl, flickering over the bones of a king.

* * *

Five years ago—or rather, in the country of five-years-ago, which she could survey only from its borders—there was a kitchen. That much survived. Steam on windows. Bread. Her mother's voice saying something about thorns.

"The thorn remembers the rose," her mother said, or had said, in a tone Edmund couldn't recover, warm or warning or both. "That's why they cut it out."

"Mama." Her own voice, younger. "Why do I have to forget?"

"Because remembering is how they find you." Hands in her hair. Or were they? Was that even—? "Sweetheart. Look at me. Whatever happens, if you wake up one day and I'm a stranger—"

The memory tore there, cleanly, like cloth on a nail, and Edmund surfaced in her rented room with her heart slamming and her hands shaking and rain loud on the glass, and understood two things with terrible clarity.

Someone had taken the memory deliberately. And whoever did it had left the tear ragged on purpose—or carelessly—or had left *that fragment*, that specific fragment, the way a thief leaves a door unlocked he means to come back through.

*If you wake up one day and I'm a stranger.*

She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until sparks bloomed. The Regent wore memory like clothing; the harvest ledgers—no. She hadn't seen the ledgers. That was ahead of her, a door not yet opened. What she had was a scrying glimpse and a prophecy and a hollow thing on a throne that fed on exactly what was missing from her head. Coincidence was a luxury she could no longer afford. In Vell, coincidences were just patterns that hadn't confessed yet.

* * *

She worked until dawn, because hands that are working cannot shake, and because her mother had taught her that fear is just energy with nowhere honest to go.

She laid the spellbook open on the floor and built the working the way her mother had taught her to build everything: grammar first, whisper always. A divination of gaps—not a finding-spell, which shouted, but a listening-spell, which waited. She ground dried mugwort and river-clay and a pinch of her own hair, mixed them with rainwater in a chipped blue bowl, and spoke the right-column words in a voice barely louder than breath.

"Show me the shape of what was cut. Not the content. Only the cut."

Clever, that distinction. Her mother's distinction. A spell that searched for *memories* might snag on wards, might alert whatever had placed them. But a spell that searched for *wounds*—for the negative space, the scar tissue of extraction—asked nothing of what was hidden. It asked only about the surgery.

The surface of the water stilled. Then, slowly, lines rose in it—not images but contours, a map drawn in shadow: the territory of her own skull rendered as a coastline, bays where whole years should have been. She counted them without wanting to.

Eleven major extractions. Eleven smooth-edged absences, each one neat as a spoon-scrape, arranged along her childhood in a sequence almost rhythmic. Almost scheduled.

And every single one of them terminated in the same place. All eleven wounds converged, deep beneath everything, on a single point behind her eyes—a node, a knot, a taproot—and from that point the shadows ran outward in her mind's map like threads from a spider's body, thin and fine, running out of the bowl entirely, running east.

Toward the palace hill. Toward the crown.

Edmund sat back on her heels. The rain had stopped. The window was going grey with the false dawn that made Vell look, for ten minutes each morning, like a city that had never burned anyone.

"They've been drinking from me for twenty years," she said softly. "Since I was a child."

Not five years ago. Not one terrible night. Since she was small enough to sit on a kitchen counter—except she couldn't remember the counter, could she, couldn't remember any counter, only the idea of one. Her mother had known. Her mother had carved holes in her own daughter's memory one year at a time, feeding something, starving it, buying time in the only currency the thing accepted—and the price of every payment had been a piece of Edmund, and the final payment had been her mother herself, or her mother's freedom, or whatever it was that five-years-ago had cost.

*I'm sorry,* Edmund told the blank spaces, absurdly, uselessly, the words falling into them like stones into wells that never hit bottom. *I didn't know I was missing. I thought this was all there was of me.*

Then she wiped her face, and the grief in her did what her mother had always promised grief could do if you gave it grammar instead of shouting: it cooled, and set, and became something with edges.

Resolve. The ugliest, most durable form love takes.

She fetched paper and ink and wrote, in plain readable script, the list of what she knew: the sigil in the century-old margin. The vanished mages. The flickering wrongness beneath the king's face. Eleven cuts in her own head, all draining east. And beneath it, underlined twice, the conclusion that turned fear into direction:

*Whatever sits on the throne has been harvesting me since childhood. My past is not lost. It is stored. And stored things can be stolen back.*

There would be records. Institutions that fed for a century did not feed neatly; they kept accounts. Ledgers, quotas, schedules—bureaucracy was the skeleton of every monster, and skeletons could be read. And there was exactly one person in Vell who knew the palace archives from the inside, who had already chosen to stop trusting the institution that employed her, who had ink-stained fingers and a stubborn chin and had looked at Edmund last night across a table like Edmund was a person rather than a risk.

Sera would know where the sealed shelves were. Sera owed her nothing, and would help anyway—that was the kind of impossible person she was turning out to be.

Edmund folded the list into her coat, wrapped her mother's book back in its oilcloth—then paused, and instead tucked it into her satchel, against her spine, where she could feel it. Some things you stop hiding. Some things you start carrying.

Outside, the bells of Vell rang the hour, and under their sound, faint but unmistakable, came another bell: the single slow toll of the palace, which meant a royal writ had gone out bearing names.

She was three steps down the stair before she let herself think the obvious thought, cold in her stomach:

They had started counting again. And somewhere in a ledger, a line with her name on it was waiting to be read aloud.

She went to find Sera before the city finished waking.
