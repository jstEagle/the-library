## 7. The Archive Below

The sub-archive had no door on any map I had ever catalogued.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last thing I had left that night. I had spent eleven years in the palace archive—eleven years of dust and vellum and the particular silence of rooms where the truth is kept but never told—and I knew every corridor above ground the way a blind woman knows her own kitchen. But the sealed levels existed only as rumors passed between clerks who were careful even when they thought no one was listening. A stair behind the cartography room, bricked over in my grandmother's time. A level where the ledgers went that no one was permitted to read. A cold that came up through the stone from somewhere deeper than geology.

Edmund stood beside me at the mouth of the old service passage beneath the east wing, holding a stub of candle she had charmed to burn without heat. The flame gave off light but no warmth, which felt like a small blasphemy against everything fire was supposed to mean.

"You're sure about this," she said. It wasn't a question so much as a hand held out over a drop, waiting for me to take it or step back.

"No," I said. "But I've been sure about nothing else all week, so it balances."

She almost smiled. In the candle's colorless glow her face looked younger and more frightened than she probably wanted it to look, and I understood then that we were both doing the same arithmetic: whatever waited below was worse than what waited if we turned around. Fear is just a ledger too. You total the columns and choose your debt.

We went down.

* * *

The passage was older than the palace above it, which should have been impossible and yet wasn't. Vell was built the way cities are built, one era stacked on another like sediment, and the lowest layers always remember things the upper ones have agreed to forget. The stones under our feet were fitted without mortar, each one carved with a sigil worn nearly smooth. I recognized the grammar of them before I could name why—hedge-witch marks, warding script of the kind the edict had made punishable by burning, pressed into the very bones of the building that now outlawed it.

"Your people built this," I whispered.

"My mother's people." Edmund trailed her fingers along the wall without touching, close enough that I saw the candlelight shiver across her knuckles. "Wards don't burn out. They just get ignored until someone needs them." She paused at a junction where three passages met and closed her eyes, and I watched her do something I had no word for—listening with her whole skin, the way you listen for a sound you half-remember from childhood. "This way. The air's wrong here. Stale in a way stone shouldn't be."

The wrongness deepened with every step. It wasn't smell exactly, though there was a smell underneath everything—dry paper, yes, but also something sweeter and more clinical, like the inside of a locked room where flowers have been kept past their dying. And it wasn't temperature either, though the cold had a texture to it, an intentionality, as though the dark itself had been instructed to hold its breath.

The stairs ended at a wall.

It looked like any other wall: dressed stone, mortar, a century of dust settled into the joints. But Edmund ran her palm across it and frowned, and when I put my own hand flat against the surface I felt it—the faintest vibration, like a struck bell long after the ringing has stopped being audible. Not stone at all. Stone-shaped.

"Glamour," she breathed. "A good one. Old." She rolled up her sleeve, and I saw for the first time the fine white scarring along her forearm, deliberate lines in patterns I didn't understand, inked or burned there years ago. "Stand back. If this goes badly, run and don't be noble about it."

"I'm not capable of nobility," I said. "Ask anyone in the catalogue division."

That earned me half a laugh, tight and brief, and then she set both hands against the false wall and spoke a word that wasn't a word—or rather, it was a word in the language her mother had taught her, the grammar she'd been raised inside, and the stone forgot itself. It didn't shatter or crumble. It simply stopped believing it was a wall, and became a shimmer, and then became nothing, and beyond it lay a corridor lined with iron shelves receding into dark on both sides, longer than any cellar had a right to be.

The cold came out to meet us like a host greeting guests it had been expecting.

* * *

I have tried, since that night, to explain to myself why I did not feel afraid when I first saw the ledgers. I think it is because recognition arrived before understanding. I am an archivist; shelves of bound volumes are my native country. For perhaps thirty seconds, standing in that impossible corridor, I felt something adjacent to peace. Here, finally, was work. Cataloguing is how I pray, though I would never have phrased it that way before Edmund taught me that other people have gods at all.

Then I opened the first book.

The binding was plain—grey calf-skin, unmarked spine, the kind of anonymous cover the palace uses for tax assessments and grain inventories. Inside, the hand was beautiful. That was the first obscenity: whoever kept these records wrote with genuine elegance, each entry composed like a sonnet, dates rendered in flourished numerals.

*Harvest 4,112. Subject: female, aged nineteen, gifted (divination). Extraction duration: six hours. Yield: exceptional. Retained: memory of father's funeral, memory of first snowfall, memory of her own name spoken by her mother. Subject released to Lower Ward. No complications.*

No complications.

I read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder. *Subject released.* As if a person were a net hauled in, emptied, and thrown back. As if six hours of having the self scooped out of you like the flesh of a fruit could be summarized in two syllables and a full stop.

"Edmund." My voice came out wrong, flattened. She was three shelves down, already holding a volume of her own, and when she looked up at me her face had gone the grey-white of old tallow. She had read further than I had. She had read enough to know the shape of the thing we'd found.

"How many?" she asked, and it took me a moment to realize she wasn't asking me—she was asking the shelves, the room, the century of patient elegant handwriting.

I counted instead. Four hundred and twelve harvests in the first ledger alone. There were eleven ledgers visible from where I stood, and the shelving continued past the reach of our light into a darkness that might have been more shelves or might have been forever.

* * *

We worked in silence after that. What else is there to do, when horror arrives wearing the clothes of your own profession? We catalogued. Entry by entry, name by name, I copied the details into my notebook while Edmund moved through the stacks pulling volumes and reading them with a stillness that frightened me more than screaming would have. She read the way soldiers walk through a field they know is mined—one careful footfall at a time, eyes forward, breath shallow.

I found the pattern first, because patterns are my trade. The harvests clustered. They came in waves—dozens in a single season, then years of nothing, then dozens again. And the waves aligned, near enough, with moments in the historical record I knew by heart: the famine year, the border war, the plague scare, the great procession where the king had appeared at the balcony looking, according to every account, "remarkably restored."

Remarkably restored. We had all written that phrase, or read it, or nodded at it, for decades. The king looks well. The king looks young. The king looks rested. And underneath, in grey calfskin books below the floor of the kingdom, the cost of his restfulness was being entered in a fine italic hand.

*Yield: exceptional.* Sixty-two harvests the winter of the famine alone. People in the Lower Ward had called it the forgetting sickness. I remembered my grandmother mentioning it once—a bad winter, she said, when half her street woke up not knowing their children's names, and the physicians blamed bad bread. Bad bread. We fed ourselves explanations the way we fed ourselves everything else: cheaply, and without asking where it came from.

"Sera." Edmund's voice, very quiet. "Come look at this one."

There was something in the way she said it. Not discovery. Dread with the specific gravity of recognition.

The volume she held open was older than most, its pages foxed and soft at the edges. She turned it toward me and pointed, and I read:

*Harvest 1,903. Subject: E. Almeida, female, aged five. Gifted (memory-craft, maternal line — see also Almeida, M., Harvest 1,877, terminated). Extraction authorized by writ of the Regency. Note: subject young; yield partial. Core integration pending. Recommend retention of selected material for future use. Subject released to Lower Ward with standard overlay applied. No complications.*

Five years old.

I read it again. *E. Almeida, aged five.* And beside it, in the margin of my mind, assembled itself everything I knew of the woman standing next to me: five years hiding her craft in Vell. Five years, she had told me, since she came to the city. Five years since—

"Since what?" I asked aloud, because my mouth outran my judgment. "What happened five years ago? To you?"

And Edmund looked at me with an expression I will carry to whatever grave I'm allotted, an expression like a house with all its windows dark, and said: "I don't know. That's the whole point. I don't know. I came to Vell five years ago and I can't tell you where I came from, or why, or—I know I had a mother. I know I loved her. I cannot picture her face, Sera. I have been telling myself for five years that grief does that. That time does that." Her hand flattened against the ledger, over the entry, as if she could press the words back into the page. "They didn't take my magic. They took my *life*. They took it when I was five years old and they've been wearing pieces of it ever since."

The candle between us burned its cold light, and neither of us moved.

* * *

Here is what I understood in that moment, standing in the dark among the harvested lives of my city: I had spent eleven years serving the machine that did this. Not passively. Actively. I had catalogued requisitions whose purpose I hadn't questioned. I had shelved correspondence addressed to offices that officially did not exist. I had once, I remembered with a nausea that rose in me like floodwater, been commended for my discretion regarding a missing volume—a book that vanished from the main archive the same week a diviner in the Tanner's Quarter stopped recognizing her own reflection. I had been proud of that commendation. I had shown it to my mother.

Betrayal of self by history. That is the phrase I reached for, later, trying to explain it to Edmund. You live your life assuming it means something—that your work matters, that your institution stands for something, that the roof over your head shelters rather than conceals—and then one night you find the ledgers, and you learn that the entire architecture of your respectable existence was a lid placed over a pit. Every year of diligent service reorganizes itself around the new fact. Eleven years of me, re-shelved under a heading I never agreed to.

I did not cry. I want that recorded, because what I did instead frightens me more in retrospect. I got angry in the way that archivists get angry, which is to say: systematically. I turned to the front of the oldest ledger, where a keeper's name would be inscribed, because keepers always sign their work—they cannot help it; pride is the one confession no institution has ever suppressed—and there it was, in the same fine hand that had recorded five-year-old Edmund's mutilation:

*Keeper of Harvests, by appointment: T. Vessin, Sub-Archivist.*

Vessin. Master Tomas Vessin, Keeper of Restricted Holdings, my superior of nine years, the man who had taught me conservation technique and given me extra coal rations in the cold months and told me, when I was promoted, that the archive's true purpose was "protecting the kingdom from its own memory." I had taken it for poetry.

He was feeding names to the palace. He had to be. Someone compiled these lists; someone marked which citizens were gifted; someone signed the writs. And the man who signed himself *Keeper of Harvests* drank tea three desks away from mine every morning of my working life.

"Edmund," I said, and heard something in my own voice that I didn't recognize—something with edges. "I know whose hand this is."

* * *

We took what we could carry. That was the practical decision, made in the space of ten seconds by two women who understood that the practical decisions are always made in the space of ten seconds or not at all: copies of key entries, the ledger bearing Edmund's name, the earliest volume, whose first pages predated the Regency and hinted—in the careful euphemism of bureaucratic prose—at the *establishment of the sustaining office*, as if the thing on the throne were a department.

Edmund sealed the false wall behind us with a gesture and a word, restoring the stone's belief in itself, and we climbed back up through the sigil-carved dark toward the ordinary palace, toward the world of lamplight and paperwork that sat atop the pit like a lid.

At the top of the stairs, she stopped. Candlelight caught the side of her face, and I saw that the grey terror had burned off her expression entirely, leaving something harder underneath—not courage exactly, and not rage, but the ore from which both are smelted.

"They're going to come for me," she said. "You understand that. I'm not a name in those books anymore. I'm unfinished business. 'Retention of selected material for future use.'" She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "They've had my mother's memories archived down there for twenty-five years. They've had mine since I was a child. And now the prophecy book surfaces, and suddenly I matter again." She looked at me, and the hardness cracked, just briefly, around her eyes. "Sera. If they come for me, they'll come for anyone who helped me. You should think about that tonight. Properly. Not bravely."

"I've thought about it," I said.

"And?"

"And I've decided to lie to everyone, starting with you, about how afraid I am." I meant it lightly. It landed heavily. She reached out and took my hand—ink-stained fingers closing around ink-stained fingers, the two of us marked by the same trade—and held it for exactly as long as the sentence deserved.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we find out what the Regent wants with a five-year-old's stolen childhood. Tonight—" she glanced back down the dark stair, where the cold still breathed upward like a sleeping animal, "—tonight I need to not be underground."

We walked out through the sleeping palace, past the portraits of kings who may never have existed, and the last thing I saw before we stepped into the honest dark of the streets was Edmund looking up at the royal wing, at the lit window above the throne room, with an expression I had seen once before in these pages of my own memory: the look of someone reading a ledger and finding, in its fine italic hand, her own name.

Neither of us knew yet that the summons was already written.
