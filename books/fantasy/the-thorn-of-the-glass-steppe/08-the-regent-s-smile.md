## 8. The Regent's Smile

The writ came at dawn, and it came wrong.

Edmund knew this because she had spent five years learning how official things looked from the outside—how they moved through the city like weather fronts, visible in the way people straightened their spines and lowered their voices. A royal summons did not arrive folded inside a fishmonger's wrapping paper, tucked beneath a half-pound of smoked herring, carried by a boy who wouldn't meet her eyes and was gone before she'd finished unfolding it.

But there it was. The palace seal pressed deep into grey wax, the double-crown sigil that every citizen of Vell learned to recognize the way they learned to recognize smoke before fire: *that shape means run.* And beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand, a single line:

*Edmund Almeida is commanded to attend His Majesty at the ninth bell. Compliance is expected. Refusal will be noted.*

No crime named. No charge given. Just her name, spelled correctly, which frightened her more than any accusation could have. The edict had made her a ghost for five years, and ghosts were safe because no one wrote their names down.

She read the writ four times while the herring went cold on her table and the morning light crept across her floorboards like something cautious.

They knew. That was the only reading that made sense. The archive below, the ledgers, the harvests—they had found the fingerprints she and Sera had left in the dark, or they had found *Sera*, or they had simply followed the thread backward through five years of small charms and quiet kindnesses until they arrived at a woman who sold luck-knots in a night market and trusted no one.

*Refusal will be noted.*

Not punished. Noted. As though she were an entry in a ledger herself, a line item to be balanced.

Edmund stood at her window and watched the city wake—the gulls arguing over the fish-quay, the lamplighters snuffing their flames one by one down Cordwainer Street, a woman three doors over beating a rug with the rhythm of someone who had done it ten thousand mornings. All of them ordinary. All of them invisible in the way that only people who have never had to hide can be invisible.

She thought about running. She had a go-bag packed under loose floorboards, had kept it packed for five years the way other women kept hope chests: dried figs, a change of clothes, her mother's spellbook wrapped in oilcloth, forty crowns sewn into the hem of a second coat. She could be on a barge downriver by noon. She could be someone else's name by nightfall.

But the writ had said *refusal will be noted*, and Edmund had spent enough time among the erased to understand what noting meant. They would come for her anyway. They would come slower, quieter, and they would not send paper first next time.

And underneath the fear, in the place where she kept the things she didn't say aloud—not to Sera, not to anyone—there was another feeling entirely. One she hated.

*They know my name.*

Five years of being no one, and something in her chest lifted at being seen. She despised herself for it. She got dressed anyway.

* * *

The palace of Vell did not look like a place where people were taken apart.

It looked like what it was built to look like: pale stone and green copper, terraces falling toward the harbor in graceful steps, windows tall enough to let the light pour in like a benediction. Tourists came to stare at the Glass Steppe—the vast mirrored terrace on the eastern face where the king held his public rites—and they saw beauty. They did not see that every polished surface reflected you back to yourself slightly wrong, slightly delayed, as if the glass were considering whether to keep you.

The guards at the gate took her name without surprise. Of course they weren't surprised. Somewhere in the palace there was a page with her name already written on it, and probably more than a name—a description, a date of birth, a list of known associations. *Known associations.* Her mind snagged on Sera and refused to move past it. If they had Sera—

"They'll want your outer coat," said the guard at the second door, a woman with a bored voice and eyes that missed nothing. "And anything in your pockets. You'll get them back."

"You won't," said Edmund, because the coat's lining held three charms she'd rather die than surrender, and because a ghost should at least be honest about being a ghost.

Something flickered at the corner of the guard's mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe—the look of one person who hides things acknowledging another. "Then just the pockets."

They gave her a receipt. An actual paper receipt, itemized: *one iron nail, worn; one length of red thread; one small stone, warm.* The warmth was the charm, and whoever catalogued it had felt it and written it down without comment, and Edmund spent the whole walk to the audience chamber wondering if that clerk was brave or dead.

A steward met her at the doors—grey-haired, soft-spoken, with the particular stillness of a man who has learned that rooms full of power punish movement. "His Majesty will see you alone," he said.

"Alone," Edmund repeated.

"The writ was specific." He opened the door. "You may refuse, Mistress Almeida. Refusal has been noted before."

*Before.* She turned the word over as she stepped through, and the door closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The chamber was smaller than she'd imagined. Smaller, warmer, older. Tapestries on the walls so dark with age that their hunting scenes had faded into suggestion—hounds, perhaps, or shadows chasing shadows. A fire burned low in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. And before the fire, in a plain wooden chair with his hands folded in his lap, sat King Aldous.

Or the thing wearing King Aldous.

In the scrying glass, days ago, the wrongness had flickered at the edges—visible only when the king moved, a shimmer like heat off summer stone, a hunger looking out through royal eyes. Here, with nothing between them but twenty feet of worn carpet, Edmund could see it plainly, and what struck her was not the horror of it but the *weariness*. The Regent sat the way an old actor sits between performances: posture perfect, face arranged, everything behind it gone quiet and tired.

"Mistress Almeida." The voice was the king's voice, warm and resonant, the voice of a thousand proclamations. "Thank you for coming. I know you considered otherwise."

"Your Majesty honors me." She had rehearsed the bow on the walk over. It came out stiff, but stiff was plausible; everyone was stiff before kings.

"Do sit. You'll make me feel like a headmaster." He gestured to a matching chair across the hearth, and after a moment's calculation—running would accomplish nothing, and her knees were less steady than her face pretended—she sat. Firelight moved across both of them. The Regent watched her with an expression of such gentle, patient interest that her skin crawled.

"You're wondering why you're here," he said.

"I assumed the crown takes an interest in hedge-witchery these days."

"The crown takes an interest in many things." He smiled, and it was a good smile, a practiced smile, the kind that made portraits beloved. "But you are wondering why *you*, specifically. Why now. Whether your friend in the archives has been questioned. Whether the raid on the sub-archive left traces. Whether I know about the ledger entry with your initials, dated thirty years ago." He tilted his head. "Shall I go on? I can. I find that fear is most efficiently administered all at once, like a bitter draught. Sipping it out over hours only prolongs the unpleasantness."

Every instinct Edmund had screamed at her to run, to lie, to reach for magic she no longer had in her pockets. Instead she made herself breathe, made herself meet those eyes—eyes that were blue and human and utterly vacant, windows lit to look inhabited from the street.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"Ah. Directness. Your mother was direct too."

The floor did not drop away, though it felt as if it should have. Edmund kept her hands flat on her knees and her voice level, and it cost her everything. "You never met my mother."

"No. But I have her memories. Most of them." The Regent rose, unhurried, and crossed to the window where the morning laid itself out over Vell—rooftops and rigging and the far glitter of the sea. "Do you know how memory-work functions, Mistress Almeida? Truly? Not the hedge version—threads and knots and sympathetic tokens. The real discipline. The grammar beneath the grammar."

"Enlighten me."

"A memory is not a record. It is a *structure*. Take it apart carefully and you learn how a person is built—what they lean on, what they avoid, what they love so hard it warps everything around it." He turned back to her, firelight catching the hollows under the king's eyes. "I have been reading your mother for thirty years. I read you thirty years ago too. You were five. Do you remember it?"

"No," said Edmund, and her voice cracked on the single syllable despite every effort.

"No," the Regent agreed softly. "That was the point. We took the harvest clean—we were very good, in those days. But structures leave foundations. And when you began digging in my archives, Mistress Almeida, I recognized the foundation. I recognized the way you ask questions—your mother asked questions the same way, always sideways, always three steps from the real one. You are *her*, reassembled from what we left behind. And that—" something moved behind the king's face then, something almost tender, and it was worse than any menace "—that makes you mine in a way no edict ever could."

The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the walls, a bell rang the half-hour, indifferent and bright.

"You've brought me here to threaten me," Edmund said.

"I've brought you here to make you an offer." The Regent returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands again, a merchant opening his best case. "There is a vault beneath this palace. In it are stored the extracted memories of every gifted citizen harvested since my—since the king's—accession. Including yours. Thirty years of you, Mistress Almeida. Your mother's face. Your mother's voice teaching you knots. The smell of her kitchen. The name of the village you were born in. The reason you left. The reason you hide." He leaned forward. "All of it intact. All of it *yours*. I can return it. Whole, uncorrupted, in an afternoon."

Edmund's heart was pounding so hard she could hear it in her ears, and she understood, suddenly and completely, that this was the true weapon. Not the guards, not the edict, not the fires. This. The offer. Because she had spent five years walking around the hole in herself the way you walk around a missing stair in the dark, and here was a stranger offering to rebuild the staircase, brick by brick, and asking so little in return.

"And the price," she managed.

"So small you'll laugh." The Regent smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to the empty eyes. "Stop digging. Leave the archives closed. Leave the vanished vanished. Go back to your market, sell your little charms, live your life—with a *whole* life inside you instead of a hole. That's all. One woman stops looking, and gets everything back. Where is the cruelty in that?"

*Where indeed.* Edmund looked at the fire and let the silence stretch, because silence was the only weapon left on her side of the room, and because she needed the time—needed it desperately—to untangle the knot of longing in her chest from the cold thread of thought beneath it.

Because here was what the Regent had not counted on, or had counted on too lightly: she had spent five years learning what holes could be lived around. She had built a life in the gap. Small, careful, lonely—but hers, fitted to its own absence like a key filed down to turn a broken lock. And she had spent two weeks knowing Sera. Knowing ink-stained fingers and a laugh she'd heard exactly four times and wanted to hear a fourth hundred. Knowing what it was to want to be *known*—and understanding, finally, that the Regent was offering her the one thing that would make that impossible.

If her memories came back whole, she would be the woman she had been at five years old, completed, restored—and the woman she had become in the gap would close over like water. She could not be both. The vault offered wholeness the way the Glass Steppe offered reflections: faithfully, beautifully, and reversed.

"You keep my mother's memories too," Edmund said quietly.

"I keep everything."

"Then even if I took your bargain—even if I stopped digging—you'd still hold her. Every day, for as long as you sit on that throne, some piece of her would be in you, wearing you, remembering with you." She raised her eyes to his. "You haven't offered me a trade. You've offered me permission to abandon her twice."

For a moment—just a moment—the weariness showed through, raw and bottomless, and Edmund glimpsed what lay beneath the king's face: not a monster, exactly. Something older and sadder and hungrier, something that had been eating lives for a century because it could not bear the emptiness of its own. Then the mask settled again, smooth as the Steppe's glass.

"You will refuse, then."

"Yes."

"And continue."

"Yes."

The Regent sighed, and it was almost human, almost kind. "Mistress Almeida. Edmund. I want you to understand that this conversation was a courtesy. I do not extend courtesies often, and I never extend them twice. What follows will not be a courtesy." He rang a small silver bell on the table beside him, once. "You may go. Your coat and pockets will be returned at the gate. Consider the walk home a period of grace. People sometimes reconsider, in periods of grace."

Edmund rose. At the door she paused, hand on the handle, and made herself say the thing she'd carried since the scrying glass—the question she hadn't let herself ask until now, facing the thing directly.

"The real king. Is he alive?"

The Regent regarded her for a long moment. And then, horribly, he laughed—a warm, kingly, charming laugh, the laugh from the proclamations, the laugh on the coins.

"Everyone is alive here, Mistress Almeida," he said. "That is rather the problem."

The door opened onto the grey-haired steward, and Edmund walked out with her spine straight and her vision blurred and the taste of the bargain still sweet on her tongue, sweeter than anything she'd ever refused.

At the gate they gave her back her nail, her thread, her warm stone, and the receipt. Outside, the morning had turned to rain, fine and cold, silvering the streets of Vell. She pulled up her hood and began the long walk home, and she did not see the figure waiting under the arcade across the square—an archivist's cloak, ink-stained fingers twisting together, a face that had been crying or was about to be.

Sera had news of her own. And the clock it started ticking had already begun to run.
