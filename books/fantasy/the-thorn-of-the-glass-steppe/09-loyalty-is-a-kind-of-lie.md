## 9. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

The archive smelled of dust and tallow and the particular melancholy of paper that no one would ever read again. Sera had loved that smell once. She had loved the hush of the stacks, the way lamplight pooled gold on the catalogue desks, the sense—naive, she understood now—that a place devoted to recording everything could not also be a place devoted to unmaking things.

Five years she had worked beneath the Palace of Vell, three levels down where the windows were memory and the air tasted of stone. Five years of cataloguing, cross-referencing, restoring water-damaged ledgers with hands as careful as a surgeon's. She had thought herself invisible. She had thought, in the way that quiet people comfort themselves, that no one watched the watcher of dead documents.

She should have known better. In Vell, everything was watched. That was the whole architecture of the edict.

* * *

Master Corvane sent for her at the end of the day shift, when the lamps were being trimmed and the junior clerks were filing out with the shuffling gait of the tired. His office sat at the archive's deepest corner, behind a door of blackened oak, and Sera had entered it perhaps six times in five years—always for reprimands dressed as kindnesses, always with the sensation of stepping into a mouth.

"Vashti." He pronounced her surname the way he did everything: precisely, without warmth, as though the syllables might spoil if handled carelessly. He did not look up from the ledger before him. "Sit."

She sat. The chair was hard. Everything in the archive was hard—the chairs, the floors, the rules. She had mistaken hardness for integrity once. She was learning, slowly and at terrible cost, that hardness was only ever hardness.

Corvane was not an old man, but he had the texture of one: grey at the temples, grey in the voice, grey in the way his hands moved across the page. He had been the archive's second-in-command since before Sera was born, they said, and there were clerks who swore he had not aged a day in thirty years. Sera had always filed that observation under *gossip*. Lately she had begun filing such things under *evidence*.

"You've been requesting materials outside your remit," he said.

Her pulse did not quicken. She had trained it not to, years ago, in a childhood spent learning which of her mother's questions were questions and which were traps. "Restoration work requires context, Master Corvane. A ledger's meaning lives in its neighbors."

"Mm." He turned the ledger around so she could see it. Her own requisition slips, copied out in his neat grey hand. *Prophecy collections, pre-Edict. Sealed sub-archive access requests. Census cross-references, 1109 through the present.* "Context," he said, "is a generous word for it."

"Is there a question, sir?"

"There is always a question, Vashti. The question is who is asking it, and for whom." He folded his hands. The lamplight caught the ring on his finger—the archive's seal, a quill crossed with a key—and Sera noticed, for the first time in five years, that the ring sat on the wrong hand. Clerks wore the seal on the right. Corvane wore his on the left, where a man wore a wedding band, or a debt-marker, or a leash.

"You've been seen," he continued, "in the company of a hedge-charmer from the night market. A luck-knot seller. Almeida, Edmund."

The name landed in Sera's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She listened for the splash. It took a long time coming.

"I've been seen taking supper," she said. "Vell is full of supper."

"Vell is full of supper," Corvane agreed mildly. "And full of patrols, and full of informants, and full of clerks who know precisely what a luck-knot costs at wholesale and what a hedge-charmer's margin must be to survive on dockworker coin. You are not a stupid woman. I have never thought you stupid. Stupid people are safe. You are something rarer and considerably more dangerous." He leaned back. "You are curious."

The well in Sera's chest filled with cold water. *They know.* Of course they know. Of course the palace that erased names from ledgers had noticed the clerk who went looking for the erasures.

"Curiosity is not a crime," she said.

"No. It is a *symptom*." He opened a drawer. "As is this."

He set a paper on the desk between them and turned it to face her. It was a writ—not the crude patrol writs that went down to the night market, but a palace writ, heavy stock, the royal seal pressed deep enough to shadow. *By order of the Crown: inventory and assessment of restricted collections, sub-level four.* Dated three days ago. Signed with a name Sera did not recognize and a flourish she did: the same looping hand that had signed off on the census erasures she and Edmund had found traces of in the public records.

"An inventory," Sera said carefully.

"A harvest," Corvane said, "if you prefer the older word. We are an old institution, Vashti. We keep the old words alive." He watched her with grey patience. "You have been pulling threads. I am telling you, as a courtesy I am not obligated to extend, that the threads are attached to something with teeth."

"Then why tell me at all?"

* * *

Because, it turned out, Corvane had a daughter.

He did not tell Sera this immediately. He rose, first, and poured wine from a carafe into two cups—actual cups, glazed ceramic, not the tin the clerks used—and the gesture was so domestic, so human, that it frightened Sera more than the writ had. Monsters, she was learning, did not present themselves as monsters. They presented themselves as men who poured wine.

"My wife died eleven years ago," he said, standing at the window that was not a window—it faced an interior shaft, and the light in it was borrowed from somewhere Sera could not see. "Childbed fever. The physicians did what physicians do, which is nothing, and the priests did what priests do, which is worse. I was left with a girl of four." He turned the cup in his hands. "Her name is Ilsette."

Sera said nothing. The silence was a courtesy and a weapon, and she held it in both hands.

"Ilsette is fifteen now," Corvane said. "And Ilsette, as it happens, is gifted."

The word dropped into the room like a coin into deep water. Sera kept her face still. Somewhere in the walls, the archive breathed—the slow exhalation of a hundred thousand pages, the settling of stone, the tick of the great water-clock in the reading hall three levels up.

"You understand what that means," Corvane said. "Under the edict. You understand what the patrols do with gifted children, or perhaps you don't; the night market sees only the adults who burn. Children go elsewhere. There is a place. I have seen the requisitions." His voice remained level, grey, precise. "There is a place, and the requisitions for it are signed by the same hand that signs the harvest writs, and I have been the archive's second-in-command for thirty years and I could not tell you what happens in that place because the records, Vashti, are *excellent*. That is the one thing I will say for this palace. It writes everything down."

He drank. Sera did not.

"Three years ago," he said, "I was approached. Not by the patrols. By the palace itself—the part of the palace that does not appear on any floor plan. They knew about Ilsette. Of course they knew; they know everything; that is the first lesson of working here and I had somehow, in thirty years, failed to learn it. They made me an offer. Not a threat. An offer. They are very civilized." His mouth did something that was not a smile. "They said: give us names. Quiet ones. Gifted ones. Names that will not be missed, names whose absence can be arranged in the ledgers so smoothly no one weeps. And in exchange, your daughter's name never enters our collection. She grows up. She burns nothing, she charms nothing, she lives."

"And you gave them names," Sera said. Her voice came out flat as a ledger line.

"I gave them names." He did not flinch. She would have respected it, in another life, in a world where respect still had meaning. "Dockworkers. Midwives. A lamplighter in the Copper Quarter who could make his flame hold color. A girl who sang to bees. Eleven names in three years. I could recite them for you, if you want to hear them. I have never once said them aloud. I write them and I do not say them, as though the sound is what kills." He set down the cup. "Perhaps it is."

"The erasures," Sera said. "The missing mages Edmund and I—" She stopped. Too late. The name was out, sitting on the desk between them like a spilled inkpot.

Corvane's grey eyes did not gleam in triumph. That was the worst of it. He looked only tired, and old, and human, in the way that ruins are human.

"Almeida," he said softly. "Yes. She is on a list, you know. She has been on a list for five years. Longer, in fact—the older lists. I found her name in a harvest requisition dated twenty-two years ago, when she was a child. It was marked *deferred*. Do you know what deferral means, in the old vocabulary?" He did not wait for Sera's answer. "It means *ripening*."

The room tilted. Sera gripped the arm of the chair. *Twenty-two years ago.* Edmund was five. Edmund, who had no memories of her childhood, who went quiet and strange whenever Sera asked about her mother, who had looked at the watercolor in the prophecy book with an expression Sera would carry to her grave—

*They've had her name since she was five years old.*

"They let her grow," Sera said. "All this time. Why?"

"Because she is not an ordinary vintage." Corvane said the word *vintage* without irony, which was somehow more obscene than irony would have been. "The deferred ones are held back. I don't know for what. I know only that the requisitions for them come from higher than the harvest office—higher, I suspect, than the harvest office knows exists." He straightened, and something in his posture shifted, the tired man folding away like a map, and what remained was the archivist: precise, grey, terrible. "Which brings me to the reason for this conversation."

"You want me to give them names."

"I want you to do what I did." He slid a second paper across the desk. A writ of appointment—*Special Assessor, Restricted Collections*—with her name already inked in the signature line, waiting only for her hand. "Access to everything. The sealed sub-archive. The harvest ledgers. Protection for you and—" a fractional pause, the grey eyes flicking up, "—whomever you choose to protect. All it costs is what it cost me. A name now and then. A name that won't be missed."

* * *

Sera walked out of the office and did not remember the corridor.

She must have walked it—her body was capable of that much, of carrying her up the stairs and past the water-clock and through the reading hall where a junior clerk nodded to her and she nodded back and her face did the correct things, her face had always done the correct things, her face was the finest piece of archival equipment she owned. Inside, behind the correct face, she was screaming, or she was very quiet, she could not tell which; the two had begun, in Corvane's office, to sound identical.

*She has been on a list for twenty-two years. Ripening.*

She thought of Edmund in the lamplight of the night market, laughing at something a dockworker said, her hands quick and clever with the knot-work, her whole life folded small and hidden like a charm in a pocket. She thought of Edmund's face when the prophecy book fell open to that impossible watercolor. She thought of the way Edmund had said, *I don't remember my mother's face, isn't that the strangest thing, I remember her hands, I remember her voice, but the face is just—gone, like a page torn out*—and had laughed, because Edmund laughed at the holes in herself, and Sera had laughed too, because she had not yet known that the holes had been cut by other people's hands.

*They let her grow. Ripening.*

And Corvane. Corvane with his dead wife and his gifted daughter and his eleven names written and never spoken. Corvane, who had looked at Sera across the desk and seen not a clerk but a mirror—another quiet person, another person who loved someone the edict could burn, another person who could be made to understand that love was not a refuge but a *lever*.

That was the true architecture of Vell, Sera thought, standing in the grey light of the reading hall while the water-clock ticked. Not the patrols, not the pyres, not even the harvest. The architecture was this: the palace never asked you to stop loving. It asked you to love *selectively*. It handed you one person to save and required, in payment, your silence about everyone else. Eleven names. A lamplighter who made his flame hold color. A girl who sang to bees. All of them missed, all of them wept for, all of them erased so smoothly that no one wept *in the ledgers*, and the ledgers were the only country Corvane believed in anymore.

And the writ was still in her pocket. *Special Assessor.* Her name inked, waiting. Access to everything—the sealed sub-archive, the harvest ledgers, the truth she and Edmund had been scraping at for weeks from the outside. Everything she needed to protect Edmund, handed to her by the thing that wanted to consume Edmund, on the single condition that she become, in some small and deniable way, its hands.

*They will ask for a name,* she thought. *Not today. Not this week. But soon, and they will ask gently, and they will make it small, and I will tell myself it is one name, one name that won't be missed, and then it will be two, and then I will be Corvane, and Ilsette will be my excuse, and Edmund will be my—*

She could not finish the sentence. She stood in the reading hall and could not finish the sentence, and the water-clock ticked, and somewhere above her the palace went on breathing, patient as stone.

* * *

She found Edmund at the night market after midnight, at the luck-knot stall, arguing cheerfully with a fishwife about whether a knot for safe seas could also serve for safe childbirth or whether that was, as the fishwife contended, "knot-poaching." Edmund was laughing. The lamplight made her face gold. She had ink on her fingers from the morning's sigil-work and she gestured with them as though her hands could hold the whole argument, and Sera stood at the edge of the stall's glow and thought: *I could lose this. I could lose this tomorrow, or in a year, and the losing will have my own handwriting on it.*

Edmund saw her and her whole face changed—the way it always did, the way that had undone Sera from the first supper, the way that said *you* before any word was spoken. "There you are. I was about to send a search party. Bad ones. They'd never find you, you're too good at archives." She was already tying off the fishwife's knot, already pressing it into the woman's palm with some murmured instruction, already turning back to Sera with that gold-lit face. "You're late. You're never late. What happened?"

And here it was. Here was the fork in the page.

Sera could tell her. *Corvane knows about us. There's a writ. They've had your name since you were five years old, they call you ripening, and the man who told me trades names for his daughter's life and he has asked me to do the same, and I took the writ, Edmund, I took it, I haven't signed it but I took it, because it leads to the sub-archive and the harvest ledgers and maybe the truth about your mother, and I told myself I could hold it without it holding me, and I don't know if that's a lie, and I don't know which of us I'm protecting anymore—*

She could say all of that. The words were right there, assembled, catalogued, cross-referenced, ready for retrieval.

What she said was: "Corvane questioned me. About the restricted collections. I think he suspects we've been reading things we shouldn't."

Edmund's smile flickered—fear moving under warmth like a fish under ice. "Does he know about—"

"He suspects. I deflected. He bought it." A lie, laid down smooth as a ledger line, and Sera felt it seal itself into the record of her, permanent, unerasable. "But we should be careful. We should work faster. I can get us into the sub-archive—I have a way now. A real way."

"A real way?" Edmund's eyes lit, fear forgotten, or set aside, or perhaps simply out-ranked by hope, and that was the cruelest cut of all: that Edmund trusted her hope to Sera's keeping. "Sera, that's—that's everything. The harvest ledgers, the erasure orders, maybe even—" She caught herself, glanced at the fishwife lingering nearby, and lowered her voice to the register she saved for sigil-work. "Maybe even what they did to me. To my mother."

"Maybe," Sera said. "I'll get us in. I promise."

Another lie, or a promise—she genuinely could no longer tell the difference, and that, she understood as she watched Edmund's face open like a window, was the whole of Corvane's architecture, the whole of the palace's architecture: not to break people but to make them unable to tell their promises from their betrayals until the two were written in the same hand.

"I should go," she said. "Before the patrol rounds. Be careful. Burn nothing tonight."

"Burn nothing," Edmund echoed, smiling. "You sound like my mother's spellbook."

And Sera walked away into the dark of the market with the writ heavy in her pocket and the fishwife's laughter fading behind her, and she did not look back, because she was afraid of what her face would do if it had to hold Edmund's gold-lit smile and the truth at the same time.

Behind her, in the lamplight she refused to turn around and see, Edmund was already packing up her knots, already humming, already trusting.

And three levels beneath the palace, in a drawer of blackened oak, a grey-eyed man waited with a blank list and a pen, patient as the water-clock, for his new Special Assessor to bring him her first name.
