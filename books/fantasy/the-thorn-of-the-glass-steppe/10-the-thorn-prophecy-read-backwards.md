## 10. The Thorn Prophecy, Read Backwards

The safehouse above the dyer's shop smelled of mordant and wet wool, and by morning everything Edmund owned had turned faintly the color of a bruise.

It was the dye-vats below. The fumes seeped up through the floorboards and stained whatever hung too long in the air—linen, paper, breath. Sera had claimed the corner by the window and fallen asleep sitting up, her cheek against the glass, ink still on her fingers even in sleep, as though she'd been born mid-sentence. Edmund sat across from her at a table scarred by decades of cutting, with the book open between them and a candle burning low enough that its light pooled in the watercolor portrait like something drowned.

She had not slept.

She had tried, for form's sake. She had lain down on the pallet they'd given her—straw tick, wool blanket smelling of someone else's life—and listened to the house settle around strangers who had fed her stew and asked nothing but her first name, and she had felt the strange, unbearable weight of being among people like herself, and it had kept her awake the way terror keeps children awake: not with noise but with abundance.

So she worked instead. It was what she knew. Thread, knot, tension, pattern.

*Read it backwards,* her mother's voice said in the old grammar of her hands, though she could not remember her mother's face to hang the voice on. *Nothing woven is only one direction. Pull the thread from the far end and you'll learn what the weaver was afraid of.*

The prophecy book—the *Almanac of Tomorrow*, the catalogue slip called it, though no one had ever catalogued it—lay open to the page with Sera's face. That face, younger by a century, painted in washes of grey and rose, looking out of the paper with an expression Sera wore exactly when she was trying not to be seen noticing something. Beneath it ran the verse in the spidery hand of some long-dead scribe:

*When the crown sits hollow on a borrowed brow,
the thorn shall come, unlooked-for, unbowed,
and unmake what was made to endure.
Blessed the steppe; blessed the hour;
blessed the hand that opens the door.*

Edmund had read it forty times forwards. It read like every state-commissioned prophecy she'd ever heard tell of: triumphant, vague, arranged so that whatever happened afterward could be called foretold. *Unmake the crown.* Fine. Wonderful. She'd spent five years unmaking things—unmaking her accent when patrols passed, unmaking the shimmer off her charms so they read as mere luck, unmaking herself nightly into someone too dull to burn. If destiny wanted a demolisher, it had come to the right woman.

But her mother had taught her better than to trust any text that only faced one way.

She turned the page. And then, carefully, because the paper had gone the brown and brittleness of autumn leaves, she lifted it toward the candle.

Watermarked paper. She'd noticed it the first night and thought nothing of it—a maker's mark, a mill's signature, common enough. But holding the leaf up to the flame, seeing the light come through, she saw that the watermark wasn't a mill's mark at all.

It was writing. Reversed.

Her heart did something slow and enormous, a single deep stroke like a bell in an empty church.

"Sera," she said.

Sera woke the way archivists wake—all at once, already apologizing for sleeping. "Is it—I found another reference, I was going to cross-check the—" She stopped, saw the page held up to the candle, saw Edmund's face, and went quiet in the particular way quiet people can go *very* quiet, the silence of someone who has decided to be fully present.

"Mirror-writing," Edmund said. "On the reverse of the leaf. Pressed through from a second text written on the back and scraped away." She set the page down flat again. "Someone wrote the rest of the prophecy and then removed it. But paper remembers."

"Paper remembers," Sera repeated softly, like a catechism. She was already reaching for the table's single lamp, adjusting the wick, angling the light raking-low across the leaf so the pressure-grooves of the lost ink would catch shadow. "We used to do this with palimpsests in the archive. Hold it nearly sideways. Breathe on it if you have to—the moisture raises the fibers." She glanced up. "May I?"

"It's your archive."

"Not anymore." Something moved behind her eyes—grief, or relief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "I stole this, remember? I'm just a girl with stolen property and a criminal. Do it."

Edmund breathed on the page.

And the second half of the prophecy came up out of the paper like a body surfacing after a long drowning.

* * *

They copied it first—Sera insisted, in her neat archivist's hand, before anything else, because *things that surface can sink*, and she was right. Edmund read the reversed lines aloud, turning them right way round in her mouth while Sera transcribed, and the room grew colder with every sentence, though the dye-fumes stayed warm and the candle burned on.

*Know, reader, that the verse above is a lie by omission.*

That was the first line. Edmund's voice caught on it, and she had to begin again.

*The thorn does not unmake the crown by striking it. No blade reaches the Regent; no spell cracks it; it wears its wards like skin. The unmaking is done from within, and the door is a living mage, bound willingly to the thing's core, carrying the unraveling inward as a spark is carried in a lantern. The anchor must go in whole and consent entire, for the Regent cannot take what is given freely—it can only harvest what is hidden. Thus the crown made itself a hunger for volunteers.*

Edmund stopped reading.

Sera's pen had also stopped. Ink gathered at the nib in a small black bead, trembling, about to fall.

"The lantern," Edmund said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; inside she felt like a house whose foundations had quietly shifted in the night. "Carrying the spark inward. That's—"

"That's how you kill it by becoming part of it." Sera set the pen down with exaggerated care, the way people handle objects when their hands want to shake. "Keep going."

There were more lines. A list.

*Of those who have carried the spark: Marisel of the Ford, in the year the river froze. She reached the core and her will broke there, and the Regent took her gift and her name, and she walked out of the palace smiling and did not know her own daughter, and died within the season.*

*Aldric Vane, hedge-caller, who bound himself laughing, believing his joy too large to unmake. What came back out was not laughing.*

*The sisters Ondrel, who went together so that neither would be alone in the dark. The core drank them both and was strengthened, and the harvest that winter took four hundred souls from the river towns, and the books record none of them.*

*And the last, unnamed here, who got farther than all the rest—who touched the seam of the thing and heard it think—and whose scream was heard in three streets, though the palace said the noise was cart-wheels on stone.*

Edmund laid the page down. Her hands were steady. She noted their steadiness the way you note a strange sound in the night—with a distant, clinical alarm, as if the steadiness belonged to someone else and might be dangerous.

"Every candidate," she said, "died screaming. Or worse than screaming. Walking out smiling, not knowing their own daughters." She looked at the watercolor portrait of Sera, serene under a century of dust, and felt something in her chest close like a fist around a coin. "This isn't a destiny, Sera. I keep waiting for it to be a destiny. A map with my name on it. Something that tells me what I'm *for.*"

"And it's not?"

"No." She heard herself say it and marveled at how plain it sounded. "It's a warning label. Read it forwards and it's a coronation ballad—hail the thorn, blessed the hour. Read it backwards, the way whoever wrote it must have known someday someone would—" She stopped. Started over. "Whoever scraped this text off the back wanted it hidden from the palace and findable by the thorn. They couldn't stop the prophecy from circulating. So they hid the cost where only the person paying it would ever look."

Sera was staring at the transcription. "Marisel of the Ford. Aldric Vane. The sisters Ondrel." She tapped each name. "These aren't in any chronicle I've ever shelved. I've read everything, Edmund. Everything in the civil records, everything in the ecclesiastic rolls. There are no harvests recorded in the year the river froze. There's no Aldric Vane anywhere."

"Because the Regent erases them. We know that now. Names off ledgers, faces out of memories." Edmund pulled the book toward her and looked at the margins—the little hedge-sigil she'd recognized on that first terrible afternoon, the looping mark her own mother used to sign her charms, stamped faintly beside the verse like a printer's device. Except printers' devices didn't hide third layers. "Look at the margin marks. They're not decoration. My mother used these same glyphs to bind a charm's intention—to fix what a spell was *for*. Someone with our grammar annotated this book. Maybe more than annotated."

She tilted the page again, hunting the margins the way she'd hunted the watermark, and there—faint, pressed through from the scraped verso, tucked into the flourish of the thorn-illustration—was one more line, smaller than the rest, written fast, by a hand that had been shaking:

*If you are reading this, you are the thorn. I am sorry. I could not finish the work. Do not go in alone, do not go in proud, and do not believe the core when it offers to give you back yourself. It gives only hooks dressed as gifts.*

The bead of ink on the edge of Sera's pen finally fell, a small black star on the wood.

"Do not believe the core when it offers to give you back yourself," Sera read aloud, very quietly. Then: "Edmund. Five years ago. Your missing years. You told me the gaps started after—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. The night market. The fire. The thing Edmund had never been able to remember doing, the trial she had no memory of surviving, the mother who existed now only as a voice and a grammar and a hole shaped exactly like a face.

"You think it already tried," Edmund said. "You think I already went in."

"I think," Sera said, with the terrible precision she brought to cataloguing, to truth, to everything, "that the entry in the harvest ledger had your name on it. E. Almeida, aged five. And I think whatever the Regent took from you, it kept. And I think when it offered you your memories back in the throne room, it wasn't improvising. It was running the oldest play in its book. The same offer Marisel must have heard. The same offer they all heard." She looked up, and her ink-stained fingers were white-knuckled around the pen. "The core doesn't fight the thorn, Edmund. It *recruits* her. Every time. That's why they died screaming—not because it crushed them. Because they believed it."

The candle guttered. Outside the window, Vell was waking—the milk carts, the gulls over the fish-stalls, the ordinary machinery of a city that didn't know it was being drunk slowly. Somewhere below them, the dyer's household was stirring, and the smell of dye gave way to the smell of bread, and Edmund sat very still and let the cold clarity arrive, because fighting it would only delay it.

Here was the shape of it, plain as a knotted cord pulled taut:

The prophecy was real. The unmaking was possible. But it required a living anchor—a mage, bound willingly, carried inward like a spark in a lantern—and the Regent had spent a hundred years perfecting the art of making anchors believe they were victors. Four names. Four deaths dressed as triumphs. And a fifth name waiting in the margin, unsigned, unfinished, a warning from someone who had gotten close enough to hear the thing think.

*I am sorry. I could not finish the work.*

Whose hand was that?

Edmund knew whose hand it was. She knew it the way you know a song from three notes. She had spent five years unable to remember her mother's face, but her hands remembered her mother's hands, and the handwriting on that scraped page—fast, slanting, the descenders bitten short—was the hand that had written *hold the thread, little eel, feel the tension* across a thousand practice scraps. Her mother had found this book. Her mother had read it backwards. Her mother had known.

And then her mother had gone into the palace, or been taken to it, and had come out of the world entirely, leaving behind a daughter with holes where her childhood should be and one intact memory—just one—shining like a lamp at the bottom of a well: a face, a smile, a hand on her hair. The last thing the harvest hadn't gotten.

*Do not believe the core when it offers to give you back yourself. It gives only hooks dressed as gifts.*

"So we don't go in," Sera said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either; there was a thread of something else in it, and Edmund was learning to hear Sera's threads—this one was fear braided with stubbornness. "There has to be another way. A way to reach the core from outside. Siege it. Starve it. Cut the harvest lines one by one until it's too weak to resist a blunter instrument."

"Four hundred souls in one winter," Edmund said. "From the river towns. When two sisters failed." She shook her head slowly. "Every failure makes it stronger, Sera. Waiting makes it stronger. It's harvesting right now—it took the fishwife's brother last month, it took the chandler, it'll take the next name on that grey-eyed man's list, and the list gets longer every week we spend being careful." She looked down at her own hands, the charm-burns, the thread-calluses, the hands of a woman who had made a religion out of caution and called it survival. "The prophecy isn't telling me what to do. It's telling me what it costs. Those are different things. Destinies push. Warnings just... stand there. Holding up the price tag. Letting you decide."

"And what are you deciding?"

Edmund didn't answer immediately. She looked at the watercolor Sera, and at the living Sera, and at the margin-handwriting of a dead woman who had loved her enough to leave a map through hell and a warning label nailed to the door of it.

"I'm deciding that whoever wrote *do not go in alone* knew what they were talking about," she said. "And that the network downstairs—the hedge-mages, the dyer's people—they've survived five years of purges somehow. They know things. Routes, wards, who in the palace can be bought and who can't. And I'm deciding," and here her voice roughened, because the next part was true and truths still cost her, "that I've spent five years trusting no one, and the one time I let someone in, she turned out to be the face in a century-old book, which means either the universe has a sense of humor or I was never actually hiding from the right things."

Sera's mouth did something complicated—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "That's almost a compliment."

"Don't get used to it." Edmund closed the book gently, both halves of the prophecy folded together at last, the lie and the cost, the coronation and the scream. "We need to meet these people properly. Not as guests hiding in their attic—as allies. If there's a way in that isn't me, they'll know it. And if there isn't..."

She left it hanging. Some prices you don't say aloud until you've decided to pay them, and she hadn't decided. Not yet. The clarity was cold but it wasn't frozen; there was still movement in it, still choice.

They went downstairs together into the smell of bread and mordant, into the low voices of people who had learned to speak softly for five long years. The leader of them—a broad, weathered woman with silver threading her black hair and a dyer's stains permanently blueing her knuckles—looked up from the long table where maps of Vell's understreets lay weighted with teacups, and her eyes went from Edmund to the book under her arm with an expression Edmund couldn't yet read.

"You've been up all night with it," the woman said. Not a question.

"We read it backwards," Edmund said.

The woman nodded slowly, as if Edmund had passed some test she hadn't announced. "Then you know what it asks." She pushed back her chair and stood, and the room went quiet around her the way rooms go quiet around people others have chosen to follow. "Good. Because we've been reading it backwards for twenty years, thorn. And there's something you need to see before you decide anything—something we found in the storm-drains under the palace, and something we need *you* for."

She rolled open the topmost map. And Edmund leaned in, and the cold clarity in her chest sharpened one degree further—because the thing the resistance needed her for was not a way to avoid the prophecy's price.

It was a way to make her the bait.
