## 11. Safehouse

The safehouse was a dyer's workshop in Tanners' Row, which meant the whole street smelled of vinegar and mordant and no one asked questions about stains. Edmund learned that on her first night: the best place to hide a craft was inside another one. The vats of indigo and weld-yellow steamed all day, and by evening the air was so thick with chemical sweetness that any trace of spellwork dissolved into it like salt into broth.

Sera had brought her here three days after the archive, after the prophecy book, after the night Edmund had sat on the floor of Sera's narrow room with her hands shaking around a cup of tea she never drank. *You can't stay at mine,* Sera had said, gentle and practical as always. *If they're watching records, they'll watch me next.* And then she had said the word *safehouse* as though it were ordinary, as though she had been carrying its address in her pocket for years.

She had. That was the thing about Sera — quiet people kept the deepest cellars.

Now Edmund stood at the long worktable while ten strangers became, meal by meal, something she had no clean word for. There was Brannick, the dyer himself, whose hedge-craft was woven into every bolt of cloth he sold — charms against mildew, against rot, against the tax assessor's eye. There was old Maren, who could still a child's fever with a knotted thread and had buried two of her own students since the edict. There were twins from the river quarter who spoke their magic only in unison, and a boy of maybe seventeen called Pell whose gift was so small — he could make candles remember they'd been lit — that he'd never once been afraid until the disappearances started, and then he couldn't stop being afraid.

They ate together every night at the long table under the drying racks. Stew, mostly, or bread and hard cheese, because Brannick spent his coin on charcoal and mordants instead of meat. And every night, before they ate, Maren would take Edmund's hand — or someone's hand; it rotated, but somehow it always seemed to be Edmund's — and say grace in the old way:

*What we hid, we keep. What we keep, we share. What we share, no fire can take.*

Edmund had not held hands over food since she was small enough to need a booster cushion. Her mother used to say something like it — not the same words, but the same grammar, the grammar of things passed palm to palm instead of page to page. She had thought that grammar died with her mother. Here it was, alive, spoken over turnip stew by a woman with dye-stained knuckles.

"You're doing it wrong," Pell told her on the fourth night, watching her fold a luck-knot for one of the dockworkers who came to the back door. "The third crossing. You're tensioning it like a market charm."

"It *is* a market charm."

"It's your market charm." He leaned across the table, earnest, ink-and-wax smudged to his elbows. "Brannick says everyone's knots have a signature. Yours has this little pull at the center, like it wants to hold on. You should lean into that. Make them the way you'd make them if you weren't scared."

Edmund stared at him. *Make them the way you'd make them if you weren't scared.* Five years she had made her charms smaller, quieter, more deniable — luck-knots that did almost nothing, wards folded thin enough to pass for decoration. She had taught herself to be a whisper of a witch, and here was a boy with a candle-trick telling her to be loud.

She finished the knot his way. The third crossing pulled taut, and the little charm woke in her hand with a warmth she hadn't felt from her own work since — since before Vell, since before the edict, since her mother's kitchen smelled of rosemary and hot iron.

"Oh," she said, very quietly.

"Yeah," Pell said, grinning. "That."

After dinner there were lessons. Not lessons like the ones the edict had burned out of existence — no lecture halls, no tuition, nothing written down. Maren taught fever-knots to anyone who wanted them, murmuring over a basin of water. The twins demonstrated how a ward could be split between two casters so neither name could be pinned to it. And Brannick, one night, showed Edmund the workshop's great working: a concealment laid over the whole building, not hidden but *disguised*, made of the same principles as dye itself. "You don't hide the color," he said, stirring his vat. "You change what it sits on. Anyone scrying this street sees a dying trade, a failing business, nothing worth a second look. Failure is the best camouflage there is. Nobody envies failure."

Edmund stayed up late those nights, lying on a straw pallet in the loft above the drying racks, listening to the building settle, and felt something in her chest she didn't trust yet. It was too easy. That was the trouble. She had spent five years perfecting solitude the way other people perfected instruments, and now here was warmth handed to her nightly, freely, with stew.

She caught herself humming while she worked. She caught herself saying *we*. Both frightened her more than any patrol ever had.

And through it all, Sera came when she could — twice, then three times a week, slipping down Tanners' Row after her shift with her collar up and her satchel full of things copied in her small precise hand: shipping manifests, guard rosters, lists of names. They spread them on the worktable after hours and looked for patterns in the vanishing.

"Twelve this season," Sera said one night, tapping the page. "All gifted, all erased — not just taken, *erased*. Their landlords don't remember them. Their own mothers don't remember them, mostly. Whatever pulls memories out of these people also files them somewhere." She looked up. "It's organized. It's *archived*. That's palace thinking, Edmund. That's my world's crime."

Edmund traced the list of names with one finger and felt the dread from the prophecy book stir again, patient as sediment. *The thorn will unmake the crown.* Twelve stolen mages. A king who flickered. Somewhere underneath all of it, an entry in a ledger with her own name in it — she hadn't shown Sera the dream she kept having, the ledger opening itself, the ink still wet.

"We need more than patterns," Edmund said. "We need to see it happening."

Brannick, who had come in silently for his coat and stopped in the doorway, cleared his throat.

"Then it's fortunate," he said, "that we've already discussed how that might be arranged."

* * *

He waited until Sunday, when the workshop was closed and the vats cooled, to gather them — all of them, the whole network, eleven faces in the lamplight around the cold hearth. Edmund sat beside Sera and noticed that Brannick had placed the chairs deliberately, hers nearest the fire, and understood only later why.

"You've felt it," Brannick began, without preamble. "The harvests are getting faster. Two in the last fortnight. Whatever sits on that throne is hungry, and hunger accelerates. At this rate, Tanners' Row is emptied by midwinter." Murmurs ran around the circle. Maren's hands tightened in her lap. "We cannot run. There are forty-one of us across six houses, and half are children or elders who can't walk out of Vell unseen. We cannot fight. I shouldn't think I need to explain what eleven hedge-mages do against the Royal Guard." He let the silence answer. "So we do the third thing. We learn."

"Learn what?" Pell asked.

"How it finds us." Brannick turned, slowly, until his gaze settled — gently, apologetically, but unmistakably — on Edmund. "Every victim so far has been found. Not stumbled upon — *found*, in attics and cellar rooms and warded shops, places no informer could have known. Which means the Regent has a way of sensing gift directly. A way none of our concealments fool." He paused. "Except perhaps one kind."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "Say it plainly," she said. "You've earned plainness."

"Your mother," Brannick said, "was Alva Almeida. Memory-worker. The finest the steppe ever produced, before the first edict. And memory-work is the Regent's own discipline — the harvests are memory-work, crude and industrial, but the same root-grammar. If anyone alive can feel the harvest coming, read its approach, stand where it will strike—" He spread his hands. "It's you. You're the only bait in Vell that can sense the hook."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass. *Bait.* No one else flinched, and that was worse — they had talked about this, she realized. Around her. Before her.

"Bait," Edmund repeated. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "You want me to stand in a field and let the thing that eats magicians notice me."

"I want us to *see it coming*," Brannick said. "With you at the center, we could map its reach. Learn its rhythm. Maybe — maybe — learn how to break a harvest in progress. Save the next twelve instead of mourning them." His voice softened. "I won't pretend it isn't risk. But you've seen the lists. Doing nothing is also a choice, and it has a body count."

"She just got here," Pell burst out. "She's been here nine days—"

"Pell." Maren's hand on his arm.

"And if it takes her?" Pell demanded, looking around the circle, young and furious and the only honest person in the room. "If your trap needs bait, that means the bait gets *bitten*. What then?"

No one answered. Brannick looked at his hands. And Edmund understood, sitting in the warm chair by the dead fire, that this was the shape of belonging she had been offered: not the shared meals, not the hand-holding grace, but this — a room full of frightened people deciding, kindly, reasonably, with the best of intentions, what she was worth as an instrument.

She looked at Sera. Sera's face was pale and set, and her hand found Edmund's under the table and gripped it hard, and said nothing, and that silence said everything: *I knew. I should have told you. I'm gripping your hand because I have nothing else.*

"I'll do it," Edmund heard herself say.

"Edmund—" Sera started.

"On conditions." She stood, and the chair scraped, and every face tilted up toward her, and she thought absurdly of her mother's voice saying *stand straight when you speak your terms, little thorn; a witch who mumbles her price gets cheated.* "First: nobody decides my exposure but me. I choose where I stand, when, and how far. Second: if I say the word *ash*, everyone runs — no arguments, no heroics, including me. Third—" and here her voice did finally shake, and she let it, "third, whatever we learn, whatever we catch, Sera sees it first. She's the only one in this room who's read the ledgers, and I don't trust anyone's memory but hers and mine."

Brannick studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow, and there was something in his face — respect, or shame, or both braided together. "Agreed. All three."

"Good." She sat back down. The warmth of the room returned around her, but it had changed temperature forever; she would always know now what the warmth cost and who was keeping the books.

Later, much later, when the others had gone to their pallets and the lamps were down to embers, Sera found her in the loft. She didn't ask if Edmund was angry. She climbed onto the pallet beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and stared up at the rafters where bundles of dyed wool hung like sleeping animals.

"He's not wrong," Sera said quietly. "That's the worst part. It's a good plan. Good plans are how good people use each other."

"I know."

"My superior used to say the archive serves the truth." Sera laughed, short and bitter. "The truth doesn't need servants. People need servants. Everything else is furniture."

Edmund turned her head. In the near-dark, Sera's profile was all edges and shadow, ink-stained fingers laced over her stomach, and Edmund felt the terrifying wanting rise again — the want to be known by this specific person, fully, even knowing what being known had just bought her tonight.

"If it comes for me," Edmund said, "and I say ash — you run. Promise me. Not Brannick's orders. Mine."

"I promise," Sera said. Then, after a moment: "I'm a terrible liar. Ask anyone."

"I know," Edmund said. "That's why I'm asking you."

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell rang the hour — and then, strangely, a second bell, unscheduled, cracked and urgent, from a tower that hadn't rung in years. Edmund sat up. Across the loft, Pell was already awake, candle in hand, and the little flame trembled though there was no draft.

Down the row, a dog began to howl. Then another. And beneath the bells and the dogs, faint as a needle drawn along silk, Edmund felt it for the first time with her mother's senses wide open: a pulling, patient and vast, turning over the city like a hand turning over stones.

Looking for something. Looking, she understood with absolute clarity, for her.

"Ash?" Sera whispered.

"No," Edmund said, reaching for her boots. "Not yet. But get your coat."
