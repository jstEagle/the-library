## 12. What Sera Gave Away

The bread was still warm when the sky broke.

Edmund had learned to measure safety in small domesticities: the weight of a loaf passed hand to hand, the smell of rosemary and woodsmoke in the safehouse kitchen, Maren's voice lifted over the clatter of bowls as she argued with old Piet about the correct way to salt a stew. Five years of careful invisibility had never once included this—standing shoulder to shoulder with nine other mages while the rain came down on the Glass Steppe like a benediction, everyone too busy and too warm to hide anything.

"More," said the child beside her, holding up her bowl. Her name was Tansy; she was seven, and she could make moths land on her finger by asking nicely. Nobody here flinched at that.

"You'll burst," Edmund told her, ladling anyway.

"Maren says you can't burst from soup."

"Maren," said Maren from across the room, "says many things before dinner and regrets none of them." She was a broad woman with grey shot through her black braid and hands like dock rope, and when she laughed, the whole house laughed with her, the way a bell makes everything near it hum. She had taken Edmund's wrist the first night, read the luck-knot scars there without asking permission, and said: *Hedge-witch. Almeida line. You'll do.* No one had ever assessed her so quickly, or accepted her so completely after.

Edmund should have known warmth like that always has a price tag somewhere.

The first wrongness came as a taste. She'd been eating, laughing at something Piet said about a tax collector and a very confused goat, when copper flooded her mouth—clean, cold, wrong—and her charms, every ward she'd woven into the doorframe of her own life for five years, screamed at once.

She was on her feet before she understood why. The others felt it a heartbeat later: Tansy's moths scattering off the windowsill like thrown confetti, Piet's spoon stopping halfway to his mouth, Maren setting down her bowl with a click that silenced the whole room.

"Lamps out," Maren said. Not loudly. She didn't need to be loud; the house obeyed her the way water obeys a channel. "Back way. Tansy with me. Everyone else—"

Boots on gravel outside. Many boots. And beneath them, a sound Edmund knew from nightmares she hadn't let herself have since Vell's edict went up: the low, resonant hum of null-iron being carried through rain.

"They found us," someone whispered. "How did they *find* us? We don't exist."

Maren was already moving, herding bodies toward the cellar hatch with the calm of a woman who had done this in other cities under other names. "Doesn't matter now. Move."

It mattered. It would matter for the rest of Edmund's life. But she moved.

* * *

The cellar was an old cistern, half-collapsed, breathing damp and stone-dust. Nine people crammed into its dark while above them the safehouse was opened like a shellfish—doors splintering, furniture dragged, Maren's beautiful kitchen ransacked by men who would never know what it had held. Through the grate overhead, lamplight swung in stripes. Edmund pressed her back to wet stone and counted boots. Twelve. Fourteen. Null-iron humming at the edges of her hearing until her teeth ached.

Then Maren's voice, above, clear and unhurried: "Gentlemen. You're dripping on my floor."

A man answered, official and flat. "Maren Ostwick. By writ of the Regent's court, you're detained for conspiracy, unlawful craftwork, and harboring unregistered practitioners."

"Harboring," Maren repeated, tasting the word. "You make it sound like I keep rabbits."

"You keep something better than rabbits. Where are they?"

Silence. Rain on the roof. Edmund's heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs.

"You know what I find funny," Maren said, conversational, almost gentle, "is that you needed fourteen of you to come for one old woman. That's not courage, love. That's arithmetic."

Something struck flesh. A grunt. Then the flat man again, closer now, colder: "Take her. Search the property. If anyone surfaces from below—" he didn't finish it, didn't need to.

Edmund's hands were shaking. Tansy had her face buried in Maren's absent place, which is to say in Edmund's coat, and Edmund wrapped both arms around the girl and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I know that voice. I've heard that voice reading charges at burnings.*

They took Maren away through the rain, and the sound of her—she did not cry out, did not plead, only said something low that the rain ate—was the last thing the safehouse ever said.

* * *

They came out of the cistern two hours past midnight, when the boots were gone and the rain had thinned to a fine grey whisper. The safehouse stood gutted and open to the weather, its windows staring, Maren's stew pot overturned in the doorway like a dead thing. Someone had scrawled the Regent's seal in ash on the doorpost—not as warning but as receipt. *Paid in full. One leader, collected.*

"We scatter," Piet said hoarsely. He was older than Edmund had realized until tonight; his hands wouldn't stop moving, folding and unfolding a scrap of cloth. "Different quarters, no contact for a month, then—"

"No." Edmund heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "We don't scatter. Scattering is what they want. Scatter and we're twelve separate little fires they stamp out one at a time." She looked around at their faces—Tansy clutching Piet's sleeve, a glassworker named Oren whose fingers still smoked faintly with doused heat, the twins who spoke to bees. "We need to know how they found us. This place was clean. Maren made sure of it. Wards layered three deep, no paper trail, no names. So either someone talked—"

"—or someone wrote," said Oren quietly.

Nobody wanted to look at anyone else. That was the poison of it, Edmund thought. In a community built entirely on chosen trust, suspicion was worse than any raid. The raid took your home. Suspicion took the thing the home was *for*.

"There was a report," said one of the twins—Wren, the quieter one. She had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear. "I saw it. At the chancery, before we came here. A clerk's report, sealed, routed to the palace archive. Routine-looking. Street observations, market movements. It listed hedge-signs seen in the night quarter." She swallowed. "It was signed with an archivist's mark."

The rain filled the silence.

"Whose mark?" Edmund asked. Her voice came out very steady, which surprised her, because inside her chest something had begun, very slowly, to tear along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"I don't know the name. But I know the hand. Neat. Small letters. Ink always slightly blue because she mixes her own—" Wren stopped.

Everyone was looking at Edmund now.

*Sera.* Blue-black ink, ground herself, because commercial ink was "a crime against the alphabet." The neat hand Edmund had watched move across café napkins, sketching little marginal doodles during their second date, explaining the filing system of a century of dead clerks with such shy enthusiasm. *Sera, who sought me out. Sera, who showed me a book of prophecies and watched my face while I read it. Sera, who knew exactly where a frightened hedge-witch would run when her life caught fire—who knew where all of us would go, because she'd been mapping us.*

No. That wasn't—that wasn't what it—

"She asked me," Edmund said aloud, to no one, to the rain. "She asked me about the night market. About where I sold things. I thought she was just—curious. I thought she cared."

Piet put a hand on her arm. "Girl. Reports get written under duress. Half the chancery writes them or their daughters vanish next. You don't know—"

"I'm going to find out."

* * *

She found Sera where she knew she would: the covered arcade behind the chancery, where the clerks cut through on their way home, where the rain fell silver through the broken glass roof and pooled in the cracks between the flagstones. Sera stood at the far end with no lantern and no umbrella, soaked through, her arms wrapped around herself—as if she'd been standing there a long time. As if she'd known Edmund would come, and had chosen to wait in the open, where nothing could be hidden.

Good, some cold voice in Edmund said. Let her stand in it.

"Sera."

Sera turned. Her face in the grey light was wrecked—eyes swollen, mouth bitten pale—and Edmund hated how much she wanted to cross the distance and fix it, how five years of armor meant nothing against one miserable archivist standing in the rain.

"Wren saw the report," Edmund said. No preamble. She couldn't afford one; if she softened, she'd shatter. "Sealed clerk's report. Hedge-signs, night quarter. Your hand."

Sera didn't deny it. Edmund watched her try—the breath drawn, the lie assembled—and watched her set it down unfinished. "Yes."

The word went through Edmund like a splinter finding bone. "You reported us. You reported *me*. I trusted you with—" Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "Maren took you in. Tansy made you a moth charm. You sat at that table and ate our bread, and you walked out and wrote down where we slept."

"Yes." Sera's voice was small and did not waver, and somehow that was worse than denial. "I did."

"Why?" It came out as almost a scream; Edmund strangled it down to a whisper that the rain nearly stole. "*Why?*"

And then Sera told her, standing in the water running off the arcade roof, in the flattest voice Edmund had ever heard her use—the voice of someone reciting something they'd rehearsed so they could say it at all.

Her brother. Not a daughter—a younger brother, Lenn, nineteen, apprenticed to a bookbinder, gifted with a small, sweet talent for making glue hold forever and pages never yellow. Useless magic. Harmless magic. The kind the edict wasn't even supposed to notice. Three weeks ago he'd been summoned for "registry review" and had not come home, and the summons had been followed by a letter with no signature, only the Regent's seal and a single line: *Reports receive consideration. Silence receives visits.* And a list of things to watch for. Hedge-signs. Night markets. Names.

"They already had half of it," Sera whispered. "My superior—he's been feeding them for a year, protecting his daughter, and his lists were getting better, and mine were supposed to fill in the gaps. I wrote four reports. Four. Each one I told myself I'd write nothing real, and each time I sat down with the pen I could see Lenn's face and I—I gave them the night market first, thinking it was empty enough, thinking they'd take the report and lose interest. Then the meeting houses. Then—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Then Maren's. I delayed it. Six days I delayed it, rewriting, making it vague, and I thought maybe vagueness would save you, and instead it just gave them time to verify everything else I'd given them. If I'd written nothing at all they'd have taken Lenn regardless—they told me that after, when I begged. They showed me his cell. They keep him alive as long as I'm useful. That's the whole architecture of it, Edmund. That's how it works. They don't threaten you. They just hold someone you love in a room and wait for you to pick up the pen yourself."

The rain came down between them, patient as centuries.

Edmund stood very still and did the terrible arithmetic. Because she believed her. That was the horror of it—every instinct she'd sharpened for five years, every ward and tell and trick for detecting falsehood, all of it lay quiet. Sera stood in the open with nothing hidden and lied about nothing, and the story fit the shape of the city perfectly, because it was the shape the city had been forcing on everyone: love turned into leverage, loyalty into a blade held at your own throat.

She believed her. And hating herself for it was the easiest thing she'd done all night, easier than grieving, easier than fear, because self-hatred required no decisions. It just arrived, like weather.

"If I'd been there," Edmund said finally, "if you'd told me—if you'd come to *us*, we'd have gotten him out. Maren had routes. Piet knows the river gates. You didn't even ask."

"I know." Sera's eyes closed. "I told myself it was to protect you. That was a lie I told myself so I wouldn't have to hear myself beg. The truth is I didn't believe anyone would help. I've worked in that archive for six years, Edmund. I've read the ledgers. I know exactly what this city does to people who try, and I couldn't bear to watch it happen twice, so I chose the version where I lost nothing except—" her breath hitched— "except the only thing I've wanted in six years, which was standing in front of me right now, looking at me like I'm one of those blank-eyed people on the street."

"You're not blank-eyed," Edmund said, and her own voice betrayed her, thick and unsteady. "That's the problem. I want to hate you. It would be so much simpler. You made it simple for five minutes and now you've ruined it by being *human* about it."

A sound escaped Sera that was half laugh, half sob. "I've spent three nights trying to decide whether to tell you. I wrote you a letter. I burned it. I wrote another one. I burned that too. Then I realized you'd figure it out, because you're the sharpest person I've ever met, and I decided if I was going to lose you I'd rather lose you to your face than to your deductions."

"So you stood in the rain waiting for me."

"The rain seemed appropriate."

Despite everything—despite Maren's cell, despite Tansy crying into her coat, despite the ash seal on the doorpost—something in Edmund's chest cracked in a way that was not entirely breaking. She took one step forward, close enough to see that Sera's lashes were wet and it wasn't only rain.

"I believe you," Edmund said. "God help me, I believe you, and I don't know yet whether that's wisdom or just—wanting it to be true. So here's what happens now. You're going to write one more report. Tonight. To your handler. And it's going to say that the safehouse network scattered west across the Steppe, toward the ferry towns, and that you've lost contact with your sources. And then you're going to use whatever goodwill that buys you to find out where they're keeping Maren Ostwick and a boy called Lenn. Because I am going to get them back—all of them—and you are going to spend whatever's left of your usefulness helping me do it. That's not forgiveness. Forgiveness comes later, if it comes. That's terms."

Sera looked at her for a long moment, rain sliding down her face, and something in her expression shifted from ruin to resolve—the same transformation Edmund had seen in her own mirror the night she'd decided to stop running.

"All right," Sera said. "Terms."

"And Sera." Edmund hesitated. There was one more thing, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the cellar, cold as null-iron. "Your handler's reports. The ones your superior forwards. Do they go anywhere strange? Anywhere below the archive?"

Sera frowned. "There's a routing seal I've never been able to trace. Black wax, no sigil—or a sigil so faint it might as well be none. Why?"

"Because the raid tonight wasn't sloppy," Edmund said slowly, the thought assembling itself with dreadful precision. "Fourteen soldiers, exact timing, exact target, in a house that didn't officially exist. Your report was the key, yes. But keys don't walk to doors by themselves. Somebody read what you wrote and acted on it within hours—someone with authority over the palace guard and a hunger for hedge-mages specifically. Someone collecting."

The rain thinned. Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled the hour, and Edmund thought of a hollow king on a throne, flickering, hungry, sustained by harvested memory—and of a prophecy book with her own sigil inked in its margins, waiting.

"Go home," she said. "Write your report. And tomorrow, Sera, I need you to get me into the archive's sealed correspondence. Whatever's eating this city—it's reading its mail through you. I want to know what it tastes like."

Sera nodded once, turned into the rain—and paused, half-gone into the grey.

"Edmund. For what it's worth. The bread that night—" Her voice caught. "It was the first meal in six years I ate without counting exits."

Then she was gone, and Edmund stood alone under the broken glass roof, believing her, hating herself for believing her, loving her for giving her reason to—and knowing that tomorrow they would walk together straight into the teeth of the thing that collected people like her mother's face.
