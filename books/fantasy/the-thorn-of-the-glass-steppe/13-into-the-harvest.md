## 13. Into the Harvest

The queue for extraction moved like a funeral procession that hadn't been told it was a funeral.

Sera stood in it on a grey morning in the palace's outer courtyard, her wrists bare, her hair pinned plainly, dressed in the drab wool of a minor clerk with nothing to hide. Around her, perhaps forty citizens waited beneath the eyes of the Greywardens—men and women in slate-colored coats whose faces never changed, whose hands never trembled, who held long brass instruments shaped something like tuning forks and something like needles.

She had told herself she would not be afraid. She had rehearsed it in front of her cracked mirror: *You are a volunteer. You are a clerk with nothing left to lose. You are no one.* But fear, she discovered, did not care what she had rehearsed. It rose in her like water in a cellar, slow and cold and certain of its own level, and by the time she reached the third step from the courtyard door, her mouth had gone dry as old paper.

*You are doing this because you owe her,* said the voice in her head that sounded like her conscience and looked, when she let it, like Edmund Almeida standing in the rain with her face wet and her heart broken open. *And because if you don't, no one will.*

"Name." The Greywarden at the table did not look up.

"Sera Voss. Archive division."

"Purpose of visit."

"Voluntary tithe." She recited the phrase they'd drilled into every clerk since the edict: *I give freely to the stability of the realm.* "I've been experiencing irregularities. Dreams. I'd prefer to have them... tidied."

That was the word the proclamations used. *Tidied.* As though memory were a linen closet. As though the things they took were napkins gone yellow at the edges.

The Greywarden stamped her ledger line without reading it. In the palace archive, Sera had watched this same stamp applied ten thousand times to ten thousand documents, and she had once believed that paper was the most honest thing in the world—that ink could not lie unless a person made it lie. Five months of digging had cured her of that. Ink lied constantly now. Whole people were being erased between one ledger line and the next, their names struck through in the neat administrative hand of men who went home to suppers and daughters and sleep.

*Men like Master Corvane,* she thought, and the guilt turned over in her chest like a stone in a stream bed. *Men who feed names to the palace to keep their own safe. Men like me, now.*

Because that was the truth she carried into the courtyard like a second heartbeat: three nights ago, she had signed her own name to a report. Not Edmund's name—she had protected that, had buried it under misfilings and false dates—but enough. Enough that when the raid came to the safehouse on Tanner's Row, it came *informed*. Enough that Maren, the hedge-mage leader with flour still on her hands from baking bread for fugitives, was now in a cell somewhere below Sera's feet, and the others scattered, and Edmund—

Edmund had stood in the rain and chosen to believe her anyway. Had looked at her with those gray-green eyes full of grief and no hatred at all, which was infinitely worse than hatred, and had said, *Go home, Sera. Be careful.*

So here she was. Going home, in a sense. Down into the place where the stolen things lived.

"Next," said the Greywarden.

Sera walked through the door.

* * *

They kept the extraction rooms warm. That surprised her; she had imagined them cold, surgical, all iron and frost. Instead the chamber was almost cozy—low lamplight, a padded chair, a screen embroidered with wheat sheaves in the old royal style. Someone had tried very hard to make the room feel like a grandmother's parlor, and the effort of it was somehow more horrifying than cruelty would have been. Cruelty admitted what it was. This room lied the way the whole palace lied: gently, thoroughly, with wheat sheaves.

"Lie back, mistress clerk." The extractor was a soft-voiced woman with spectacles and ink-stained cuffs, and she might have been any archivist in any division, except for the instrument cradled in her palms—a forked brass wand threaded with fine silver wire, humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has supposedly ended. "This is only a surface harvest. Dreams, recent anxieties, small redundancies. Most clients report nothing worse than a mild headache and better sleep."

*I am not most clients,* Sera thought. *I have spent two weeks memorizing the vault's intake ledgers. I know there is a corridor behind the east wall that does not appear on any plan. I know the harvests are not stored in the annexe but carried deeper, down stairs older than the edict itself. And I know that whatever you take from me, I intend to follow it.*

Aloud she said, "Will it hurt?"

"Only if you fight it." The woman smiled. "Don't fight it."

The prongs touched her temples, cool as coins left overnight in a garden.

What followed was not pain. That was the trap of it. What followed was *ease*—a loosening, like fingers uncurling after clutching too long. Sera felt her fears lift out of her one by one and be examined, weighed, catalogued. Her terror of the Greywardens' faces. Her guilt over Corvane's daughter, a girl of nine who would grow up fed and whole because her father sold strangers to the fire. Her shame about the report. Her—

*Careful,* she thought desperately, and buried Edmund deep, wrapped her in the dullest thing she owned. She thought of tax schedules. She thought of the smell of binding glue. She thought of the catalogue numbers of agricultural surveys from sixty years ago, row upon row upon row, until her mind was a warehouse of the world's most tedious paper.

"Hm," said the extractor, somewhere far above. "A tidy mind. Rare, these days."

Then the silver wire warmed against Sera's skin, and something began to *pull*, and Sera did the hardest thing she had ever done in her life.

She stopped fighting.

She let herself go slack. She let the instrument find the loose threads of her—her dread of the dark, her grief for her mother's rosary beads lost in a house fire twenty years ago, her loneliness, so vast and ordinary it embarrassed her even as it left her—and she rode the pull downward instead of resisting it, the way a diver rides the current, keeping her consciousness folded small and quiet inside the thing being taken.

Because that was the secret she had worked out alone at her desk at three in the morning, staring at the vault ledgers until the letters swam: the harvested memories didn't vanish. They were *carried*. Every extraction was a delivery. And if she could stay conscious inside what was taken—if she could cling to the thread of herself the way Edmund clung to her charms and sigils—then she would be carried exactly where she needed to go.

Down. Into the vault. Into the core.

The lamplight thinned. The wheat-sheaf screen dissolved. Sera fell through her own unraveling like a leaf through autumn air, and did not scream, because screaming would have meant fighting, and fighting would have meant dying here on this padded chair while a kind-eyed woman with ink-stained cuffs murmured *there now, nearly done* and stamped another ledger line.

* * *

She woke—or the part of her that remained woke—in a place that was not a place.

It was vast and dim and lit from within, the way deep water is lit when something enormous glows below it. There was no floor, exactly, but there was resistance underfoot, a yielding surface like packed snow or memory foam, and everywhere around her, suspended in the gloom, hung *lights*.

Not lights, precisely. Threads. Filaments of color drifting in slow currents, each one humming faintly with a life that was not its host's anymore. Some were bright and taut—a child's first snowfall, Sera guessed, or a wedding, or the taste of a particular bread. Some were frayed, dimming, going grey at the tips. And some were simply *gone*, leaving behind little halos of absence, negative spaces shaped like the memories that had once filled them.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. A century of harvests, hung in the dark like lanterns in a festival no one attended.

Sera drifted among them, and her borrowed body—or whatever it was the Regent's machinery had given her to wear down here—felt none of the cold. Only awe, and beneath the awe, a fury so clean it frightened her. *This is where they are,* she thought. *All the vanished ones. All the names Corvane fed to his masters. They're not dead. They're* ***filed.***

She had come to map. That was the mission she and Edmund had built together across three whispered meetings in the days after the raid, huddled in a boatshed that smelled of tar and rainwater, Edmund's hands shaking as she drew the ritual diagrams, Sera's hands steady as she copied the vault plans from memory. *Get in,* Edmund had said. *Find the core. Find where it keeps what it stole from me. Mark the way. Then get out.* Simple words. Impossible words. Words that had cost Sera everything she was not yet sure she wanted to survive losing.

But now, adrift in the glowing dark, she found she could not go straight to the core. Because the filaments around her were *labeled*. Not with words—with resonances. Each thread carried the flavor of its owner, and Sera, who had spent eleven years in an archive, read collections the way other people read faces. She moved through the dark reading griefs. A dockworker's terror of deep water. An herbalist's knowledge of forty poisons. A boy's entire childhood, taken whole, hanging in a bundle like laundry.

And then she felt it. A resonance she knew.

It came to her before she understood it—the way you smell rain before you see the clouds. Something warm and green and sharp-edged, threaded through with woodsmoke and crushed herbs and a low singing voice. She followed it without deciding to, drifting past banks of dimmer threads toward a cluster set slightly apart, bound more carefully than the rest, wrapped in silver wire the way precious manuscripts are wrapped in vellum.

Fragments. Dozens of them, torn from some larger whole. Sera reached out—her thought touched them, and they opened to her like books.

*A kitchen with copper pots. A woman's hands chopping fennel, quick and sure, rings on two fingers.*

*A circle chalked on a floorboard, a small girl copying it wrong, laughing, a voice saying* no, sweetheart, the thorn goes *here—*

*Night. A window. The woman holding the girl up to see a comet, saying* remember this, Edie. Whatever else they tell you, remember *this.*

Sera hung in the dark of the vault with tears she had no eyes to shed.

These were Edmund's. These were the pieces of Edmund Almeida's childhood, five years old and younger, harvested before the girl could even spell her own name—torn out of her so early and so cleanly that Edmund herself did not know what was missing. She had spent five years believing her childhood was simply *gone*, worn away like a coin passed through too many hands. She did not know it had been *taken*. She did not know her mother's voice still existed, still sang somewhere in this dark, filed and preserved and *kept*—

Kept. Sera went cold. Why kept? Surface harvests were spent—burned as fuel, she'd seen the ledgers say. But these were curated. Bound. Maintained. As if someone had been saving them. As if someone had known, a quarter century ago, that a girl named Edie would matter someday, and had set aside the raw material like a merchant laying in stock.

*The prophecy,* Sera thought. *The book. The thorn. The Regent knew her name before she ever walked into my archive.*

The filaments pulsed softly in their wire wrappings, and in their glow Sera saw something she had missed: a thread leading away from the cluster, fine as spider silk, running down through the dark toward the vast glow at the center of everything. The core. And along that thread, faint but unmistakable, traveled a rhythm—a pulse, steady as a heartbeat, and the pulse was *drawing on these fragments*, sipping them the way a man sips wine he means to make last.

Feeding. The Regent was feeding on Edmund's childhood. Had been feeding on it, slowly, for years.

And then the pulse stuttered.

The great glow at the center flickered—once, twice—and across the whole vault, every filament shivered at once, ten thousand stolen lives flaring and dimming like candles in a draft. Sera felt the change in the pressure of the dark, the way animals feel weather coming. Something had noticed her. Something down in the light had turned its attention upward through the stacks of the harvested dead, and was listening.

A voice arrived. It did not use sound. It arrived the way understanding arrives, fully formed, wearing the warmth of a mother's kitchen and the cadence of a lullaby Sera had never heard but recognized instantly, because she had just seen its source hanging in silver wire ten feet away:

*You are not supposed to be awake in here, little clerk.*

Sera did the only thing left to do. She grabbed the nearest filament—Edmund's comet, Edmund's night window, Edmund's mother's voice saying *remember this*—and she *held on*, folding it into herself, marking it, anchoring her own fading consciousness to the brightest thing in the dark.

*Run if you like,* said the voice, gentle as wheat sheaves. *But you are carrying something that belongs to me. And I always collect.*

Far below, the core began to brighten—not with alarm, Sera realized, but with appetite.

And in her mind's eye, in the last clear corner of herself, Sera began, desperately, to draw the map.
