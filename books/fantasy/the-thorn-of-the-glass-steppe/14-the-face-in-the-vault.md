## 14. The Face in the Vault

The map Sera had smuggled out was drawn on the back of a laundry requisition, which was the kind of joke that only someone who had spent years inside the palace's bowels would find funny. Edmund found it funny the way a person finds a knife funny—recognizing its purpose, respecting it, and hoping never to feel it.

*Down the cistern stair past the third landing. The door with no handle opens for salt. The vault breathes cold; do not trust your own warmth.*

Sera's hand, Edmund thought, studying the cramped, precise letters. Even her handwriting was careful, as though ink were something that could be made to behave if you asked politely enough. She wondered what Sera was doing at this moment—whether she was still walking the corridors of the memory-vault with her borrowed extraction-sigil burning on her wrist, mapping the Regent's core from the inside, one wrong breath away from being emptied into an archive shelf. Whether she was afraid. Whether she thought of Edmund when the dark came down, or whether fear had burned everything else away.

She hoped it was the first one. She was afraid to hope it was the first one.

* * *

The cistern stair was older than the edict, older than the Regent's reign, maybe older than the crown itself. The stones were slick with condensation and something sweeter underneath—a mineral smell like old coins left in rain. Edmund went down with a hooded lamp and no light but what leaked through its shutter, because down here, in the place where the city kept its stolen things, brightness felt like shouting.

Her mother's spellbook rode against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth. She had not opened it since the safehouse. She did not need to. She had spent three sleepless nights with it, learning again the grammar of memory-work—the tense structure of it, the way her mother had organized magic like language, because that was how she had taught it: *a memory is a sentence someone said about the world, and sentences can be unspoken, but never unwritten.* Edmund had hated those lessons at nine years old. She would have given anything, five days ago, to have them back.

Now she was about to walk into a room where they might literally be kept on a shelf.

The door with no handle stood where the map promised: a slab of grey stone set into the cistern wall, so seamlessly fitted that she would have taken it for the wall itself if Sera hadn't told her to look for the seam that hummed. It did hum—a low, subcutaneous note she felt in her molars more than heard. Edmund took out the twist of salt she'd brought, coarse sea salt bought from the same night-market stall where she used to sell luck-knots, and pressed it flat against the stone.

The seam drank it. That was the only word. The grains sank inward like rain into parched earth, and somewhere behind the stone, machinery that predated machinery turned over with a sound like a swallowed word, and the slab swung open on darkness that exhaled cold against her face.

*Do not trust your own warmth,* Sera had written. Edmund stepped through, and understood. The vault's cold wasn't temperature. It was absence—the specific chill of a room full of things that had been taken out of people, and the people-shaped holes left behind.

* * *

Inside, the vault was beautiful, and she hated it.

Shelves rose in ranks into a gloom her lamp couldn't finish, and each shelf held vessels: glass spheres the size of plums, stoppered with wax and silver, arranged in rows so orderly they looked like fruit in a merchant's stall. And they glowed. Not all of them—not evenly—but enough of them, enough that the whole vast space shimmered with a light that had no source, a light that moved the way light moves under water, slow and swaying.

Memories. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each sphere a life someone else had lived, harvested out of their skulls by the discipline her mother had taught her, refined by whatever hollow thing sat on the throne above.

Edmund walked the aisles and made herself look. A sphere here pulsed amber and she caught, unbidden, a flash of someone else's wedding day—a bride's laugh, rain on canvas, the taste of spiced wine. Another flickered green: a boy running down a hill with his arms out, flying-adjacent, the pure animal joy of being eight and fast. They weren't dead, these memories. That was the horror of it. They were *alive*, archived, luminous, going on living their small bright moments over and over in their glass prisons while the people they'd been torn out of walked the streets of Vell with soft gaps in their eyes and no idea what they'd lost.

She passed a shelf labeled in faded gilt: *HARVEST 47 — QUARTERLY*. Below it, smaller: *SURPLUS — QUALITY B.*

Quality B. Someone had graded these. Someone had held a woman's wedding day up to the light and decided it was adequate.

Edmund's hands were shaking. She let them. Fear, her mother's spellbook said, was just the body reading a spell written too large to see all at once. She kept walking.

Sera's map said the E-alphabetized personal holdings were deep in the eastern stacks, past the harvest ledgers' overflow, in the section reserved for *core-relevant material*—memories the Regent had woven into itself rather than merely consumed. Edmund followed the aisle markers, counting turns, and the light changed around her. The spheres here were brighter. Denser. Older wax on their seals, silver fittings gone black with handling. Whatever lived upstairs came down here often, or had once, and had touched these.

And then she saw the label, and stopped breathing.

*ALMEIDA, E. — ACQUISITION: YEAR 5 OF THE QUIET REIGN — SOURCE: JUVENILE — STATUS: CORE-INTEGRATED.*

Below it, in different ink, added later in that same precise hand she knew from the laundry requisition: *There are eleven. I'm sorry. Take them all.*

Eleven spheres. Eleven glass plums on a single shelf, glowing with a light warmer than any others in the vault—gold shot through with rose, the exact color of lamplight through kitchen curtains. Edmund reached out and touched the first one, and the vault fell away.

* * *

A kitchen. Her mother's kitchen, the one above the shop in Harrow Lane, before Harrow Lane burned out of her memory like a name scratched off a list. Steam on the windows. The smell of rosemary and hot iron. A woman standing at the stove with her sleeves rolled, singing something low and off-key, and Edmund—small Edmund, four years old maybe, five—sitting on the counter swinging her feet, and the whole scene so saturated, so *present*, that Edmund's adult body in the vault wept without deciding to.

Because she could see her mother's hands. The scar on the left thumb from a carving knife. The way she tapped the spoon twice against the pot's rim, always twice. And when the woman in the memory turned toward the child and laughed—

Edmund knew the face.

Of course she knew the face. It had been gone for five years, scraped out of her along with everything else, leaving a smooth blank where a person should be—and now here it was, alive, luminous, archived, laughing at her across a steam-fogged kitchen, and it was the most familiar thing she had ever seen and the most unbearable. It was like having a bone set after years of walking on it broken: the relief and the pain arrived in the same instant and refused to be separated.

"Mother," she said aloud, and the vault swallowed the word, and the sphere glowed a little brighter under her palm, the way a cat leans into a hand.

Eleven of them. Eleven moments. Her mother teaching her the first sigil, drawing it in spilled flour. Her mother braiding her hair before some festival, muttering the charm against headaches into the braid's root the way she did every time, superstition and spellcraft indistinguishable. Her mother's face, again and again, from every angle a childhood could offer—looking down, looking away, looking at her with that expression Edmund had spent five years unable to picture and had told herself she didn't miss.

She could take them. That was the thing her fingers already understood, curled around the first sphere's wax seal. Restore them. Crack the seals, speak the return-grammar her mother had written in the book against her ribs—*what was spoken may be unspoken*—and pour eleven stolen years back into the hole in herself. She would walk out of this vault whole. She would remember Harrow Lane. She would remember why she ran, and who she ran from, and what she'd lost besides. She would have her mother's face back, not as a weapon or a wound but as a *possession*, hers, carried forward into whatever came next.

And the Regent would feel it. That was the other half of the sentence, the clause Sera's map had underlined twice. Core-integrated meant woven in. Meant load-bearing. These eleven memories weren't stored in the Regent's hoard—they were *part of it*, threads in the fabric of the thing, and pulling them free wouldn't weaken it gently. It would tear. It would announce. Every ward between here and the throne room would come down on her head before she finished the second seal, and the harvest—the forced harvest the hedge-mages' network feared was coming—would begin tonight instead of on the Regent's chosen schedule, with the mages scattered and unbanded and the real king still sleeping his enchanted sleep.

Or.

Or she could leave them. Leave them exactly where they were, luminous and alive in their glass, until the moment the ritual demanded otherwise—because she had read the prophecy backwards now, all of it, mirrored and plain, and she knew what the unbinding required. A living anchor. A mage bound willingly to the core. And she knew, with the cold clarity the last week had beaten into her, that the anchor did not survive intact. That the ritual fed on what the mage held dearest, drew it in like a wick draws flame, and burned it to collapse the thing it sustained.

Every previous candidate had died screaming. She would not die. She had read that part too, the part that was somehow worse: she would walk away, emptied of exactly one thing, chosen by herself.

Her mother's face.

Restore them now and live whole for perhaps a day, and lose them anyway—lose them *and* lose the city, *and* lose Sera, *and* lose the hundred soft-gapped strangers walking Vell's streets who deserved their wedding days and their hillsides back. Or leave them, carry the blank space a little longer, and spend them at the end, deliberately, the only way they could buy anything worth buying.

It was not a choice. She had known it was not a choice from the moment she read the label. But knowing a thing and holding it are different crafts, and Edmund stood in the cold with her thumb on the wax seal and let herself grieve in advance, thoroughly, the way her mother had taught her to practice a difficult spell—slowly, honestly, all the way through, so that the real casting would find no resistance in her.

"I'm sorry," she whispered to the spheres. "I'm sorry it has to be one or the other. I'm sorry it was ever either."

The nearest sphere flickered. In it, her mother tapped the spoon twice against the pot and laughed at something small Edmund couldn't hear anymore, and Edmund memorized the laugh's shape if not its sound, because a thief should know what she is going to spend.

* * *

She was wrapping the spellbook tighter, turning to go, when the vault changed.

Not a sound. A *pressure*—the particular drop in the air that preceded either lightning or attention, and down here there was no weather but the second kind. The spheres dimmed in a wave, aisle by aisle, like lamps being shuttered down a long street, and in the sudden half-dark Edmund felt it arrive: the Regent's regard, pouring down through the palace's stone roots, finding her among its hoard the way a spider finds the tremor in its web.

It didn't speak. It didn't need to. The pressure simply *adjusted*, and meaning bloomed in her mind like ink in water, patient and enormous:

*You have seen the price. You will pay it anyway. That is what makes you useful.*

Then, softer—an intimacy so vile precisely because it was gentle:

*Ask the archivist what she wrote in her report. Ask her whose name was on it.*

The pressure lifted. The spheres relit, one by one, warm and innocent, her mother's kitchen glowing at the end of the row like a lit window in a dark country.

Edmund stood very still in the humming cold, eleven stolen years at her shoulder and doubt settling into her chest like snow, and began, for the first time since the raid, to run.
