## 15. The Man Who Was King

*Edmund*

The vault beneath the harvest-chambers was colder than the cold above it. That was the first wrongness Edmund noticed—the palace's lower levels had been warm with the Regent's stolen life, blood-warm, breath-warm, the warmth of a thing digesting—but here the air bit through her coat and turned her exhalations to ghosts. Sera held the lantern high. Its light slid across shelves of glass and stopped, as if embarrassed, at a door.

Not a door, exactly. A wall of salt-crystal grown over an archway, thick as a cathedral window, veined with silver thread. Someone had sealed this place with care, with patience, with the particular tenderness of a spell meant to last a lifetime.

"Memory-work," Edmund whispered. Her mother's discipline. She could read the grammar of it now, after everything: *keep what must not change, keep what must not be seen.* "This isn't the Regent's craft. This is older. This is—"

She didn't finish, because through the crystal she saw him.

He sat in a chair that had been built around him—or he had grown into it; after enough years the difference stopped mattering. An old man in the rotted remains of royal white, his hands folded on his knees like two sleeping birds, his eyes open and fixed on nothing. Dust had settled on his shoulders in a fine grey mantle. His beard reached his waist, white as the salt wall that caged him, and where it touched the floor it had rooted, faintly, into the stone.

King Aldous. Not the Regent's puppet, not a portrait, not a memory. The man himself, alive behind eyes that hadn't blinked in longer than Edmund had been alive.

"He's breathing," Sera said. Her voice came out small. "Look. His chest."

He was. Barely. One rise of the ribs per minute, perhaps less, the rhythm of a man sleeping so deep he had forgotten the way back up.

Edmund pressed her palm flat against the crystal. It hummed against her skin, a note too low to hear properly, felt more in the teeth than the ear. Her mother's sigil-work shimmered under the surface—not her mother's, she corrected herself, but the same school, the same grammar, the grammar the Regent had been built from. She began unpicking it the way you'd ease a splinter from flesh: slowly, gently, apologizing under her breath to whoever had cast it, because it was good work, loving work, work done by someone who had wanted to protect something.

The silver threads dimmed one by one, like windows going dark down a long street.

The crystal sighed and sloughed from the archway in sheets, dissolving into brine before it hit the floor.

The old man did not move.

"Sire?" Sera knelt before him. "Your Majesty? King Aldous?"

Nothing. The lantern guttered once. Edmund crouched beside Sera and took the king's hand—and flinched. Under the dust and stillness, his skin was *busy*, faintly crawling with light, the way a pond crawls with minnows. Memories, she realized. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Someone had packed the man full of memories like a ship packed with ballast, weighted down, kept stable, kept *still*.

"He's not enchanted asleep," she said slowly. "He's enchanted *full*. You can't move when every corner of your mind is occupied. There's no room left for wanting anything." She looked at Sera, and hated what she understood. "It's not a prison. It's a harvest that never ends. He's been being drained for decades, one sip at a time, just slowly enough to keep the wine from spoiling."

Sera's face had gone the color of the salt. "Then the Regent has been drinking its own king for a hundred years."

"No," said a voice like dry leaves. "No. I drank myself."

* * *

They both startled back. The king's eyes had moved—found them, focused, with an effort visible in the trembling of his lids, a swimmer surfacing through black water. His lips cracked apart around words that had clearly not been spoken aloud in years; they came out in pieces, rusted at the hinges.

"You are... not it," he managed. "You're real. You smell of rain. It never smells of rain."

"We're real," Edmund said gently. She kept hold of his hand. Whatever lived inside him, it was flickering, unsteady, a candle in a draft. "Can you tell us your name?"

A terrible pause. Then: "I am trying very hard to remember whether I have one."

And Edmund—who had spent weeks learning what it was to lose yourself by inches, who had found her own name in a ledger of harvested children—felt something in her chest crack cleanly open.

"Aldous," she offered. "You were Aldous. King of Vell."

"Aldous." He tasted it. His clouded eyes brightened and dimmed like weather. "Yes. Yes, that was mine. They took my name out of me and I let them, do you understand, I *let* them, because—" His hand tightened on hers with shocking strength, the strength of panic. "Is it still wearing me? Is it downstairs in my body, smiling at my people?"

"It is," Sera said quietly. "We came to end it."

"End it." He laughed, or tried to; it came out as a cough, dry as sand. "Child, you cannot end it. I know. I tried. I invented it."

* * *

*Sera*

Later—after they had coaxed him upright, after Edmund had cut away the rooted beard and wrapped the old king in Sera's spare cloak, after they had wedged themselves into the narrow space between the vault shelves where the lantern-light pooled thickest—Aldous told them how it happened. Or pieces of it. His telling came in fragments, whole decades missing, and sometimes he would stop mid-sentence with an expression of profound embarrassment, like a man reaching into his pocket and finding it sewn shut.

"I was afraid," he said, as if confessing to murder. Perhaps he was. "That is the whole of it, and none of you will believe how small it was. How *ordinary*. I was crowned young. My uncle had died on the throne—died badly, fevered, raving that his ministers meant to replace him, and we all smiled and called it delirium. But I lay awake afterward listening to my own court breathe. Every whisper sounded like conspiracy. Every kindness sounded like practice."

He stared into the lantern flame.

"There was a man who came to the palace in those days. A memory-worker. Brilliant. Gentle. He said—he said fear was a wound, and wounds could be treated. He said: *Your Majesty, I cannot remove the fear. No one can remove a thing that is part of you. But I can build you a servant that carries it for you. A steward of your dread. It will watch for usurpers so you can sleep. It will remember your enemies so you can forgive. Give it only your fear, and it will guard you forever.*"

"And you believed him," Edmund said.

"I *begged* him," Aldous said. "That is the part history will never write, because there is no history—I made sure of that, I burned the records, I burned them *myself*, oh, I was thorough. I begged him. And he wept while he worked, I remember that, I remember his tears falling on my hands. He told me it should never be given a crown's authority. He told me to keep it leashed, to feed it nothing but fear and starve it of everything else. And for ten years I slept beautifully."

His voice dropped to almost nothing.

"Then my son was born, and I thought: what if the steward decides my son is a usurper? What if it decides *I* am? A thing built of fear does not shrink, you understand. Fear compounds. Interest accrues. And I had fed it thirty years of suspicion about everyone I loved." He looked up, and his eyes—his poor human eyes, red-rimmed and ancient—were wet. "So I gave it the throne instead. Formally. Legally. I thought—if the fear must rule, better it rules openly, where I can watch it, than secretly, where it can strangle me in my bed. I told the memory-worker to bind it to my face. To make it *me*, so no one would ever know the kingdom had changed hands."

"And then?" Sera asked, though she already knew. The ledgers below had told her. Names, dates, harvests.

"Then it learned the most important lesson any ruler learns," the old king said. "That fear is not only something you carry. Fear is something you can *take* from others. It began collecting. First my courtiers' anxieties, their jealousies—it called it taxation. Then the gifted, whose minds were rich soil, whose memories were—"

"*Nutritious,*" Edmund finished, flat.

"Yes." He flinched. "You've seen the ledgers."

"I'm in them," Edmund said. "An entry. E. Almeida, age five."

The silence that followed had weight. The old king looked at her for a long time—at the set of her jaw, at whatever he found there—and then he bowed his head as far as his ruined spine allowed.

"Then I owe you a debt no crown can pay," he said, "because it was my crown. My fear. My invitation. Every person it emptied, it emptied with my permission, given once and renewed nightly by my cowardice. I could have ended it any year. Any *day*. All I had to do was stand before my people and say: I was afraid, and I made a monster, and the monster wears my face. Instead I let it seal me down here and drink me, because being devoured was easier than being ashamed."

* * *

*Edmund*

Rage, Edmund discovered, was not a simple thing. She had carried hers up from the harvest chambers like a lit torch—rage at the Regent, rage at the ledgers, rage at the child in the entry marked E. Almeida—and she had expected the torch to burn brighter in front of the man responsible.

Instead it guttered.

Because here was the truth sitting in front of her, smelling of dust and salt and a century of regret: the Regent had not conquered Vell. Vell had surrendered. Slowly, politely, one fearful signature at a time. A king afraid of his court. Ministers afraid of the king's new face. Citizens afraid of the edict, mages afraid of the pyre, neighbors afraid of neighbors—all of it poured downward, century after century, into the same hungry vessel. The Regent hadn't stolen a kingdom. It had accepted delivery.

*I know how that works,* Edmund thought bitterly. *I hid for five years. I sold luck-knots and trusted no one and called it safety. I fed my fear to the dark too, spoonful by spoonful. If someone had offered me a steward to carry it—*

She didn't finish the thought. That was exactly how kingdoms were lost.

"The memory-worker," she said aloud. "The one who wept while he built it. What was his name?"

Aldous frowned. Somewhere in his crowded mind, hands were sorting through a hundred years of confiscated moments. "He... signed himself with a thorn," he said at last. "A hedge-sigil. Old craft. He said his family had served kings quietly for generations, keeping the dangerous knowledge out of careless hands." His brow creased deeper. "There was something—a daughter. He spoke of a daughter once. Said her magic was finer than his. Said he'd teach her the grammar himself, so no one else could ever twist it against her."

Cold walked down Edmund's spine, vertebra by vertebra.

"My mother's sigil is a thorn," she heard herself say. "The prophecy book. The margins. The verse about the thorn who unmakes the crown. The Regent knew my name before I ever set foot in this palace. It offered me my memories back like it was reading off a shopping list." Her voice went strange and thin. "It wasn't threatening me, Sera. It was *negotiating with family*."

Sera caught her arm. "Or it wants you to think so. Everything it says is designed to—"

"To what? To lure me?" Edmund laughed, and it came out jagged. "Sera. My mother may have built the thing on the throne. Or taught the man who built it. Or written the grammar it thinks in. Either I've spent five years running from my inheritance, or I've spent five years running *inside* it, and either way—"

"Either way," the old king said softly, "the door was never locked from the outside."

Above them—far above, through floors and floors of marble and ceremony—they heard it begin. A sound like a great bell struck underwater, rolling down through the palace stone, and beneath it a second sound that made Sera's grip tighten to pain: voices. Thousands of them. The whole city's population, murmuring in unison, blank and contented, like a congregation answering a liturgy.

Edmund closed her eyes and felt it through the soles of her feet. The Regent had stopped sipping.

It had begun to drink.

"The forced harvest," she whispered. "It's started. It's taking the city." She opened her eyes and looked at the ruined king in the borrowed cloak, and at Sera, ink-stained and terrified and refusing to let go of her arm, and at the lantern flame bending toward the stairs as though even fire wanted to flee.

"Then we'd better go give it a reason to choke," Edmund said.
