## 16. Terms of Surrender

They came for her at dawn, but politely, which was somehow worse.

Edmund had expected chains, or at least the rough hands of the palace guard. Instead there was a carriage of black lacquered wood waiting in the fog outside Sera's borrowed room above the chandler's shop, its door held open by a footman whose face was smooth and slightly too symmetrical, like a portrait of a face rather than a face itself. A writ lay on the seat inside, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. It bore no accusation. Only an invitation, in a hand so elegant it seemed to apologize for itself:

*The King requests the company of Edmund Almeida. Come alone. Come willing.*

*Come willing.* As though willingness were something that could be commanded into being, like weather or grief.

She should have run. Five years of running had made her body fluent in the language of escape—she knew the weight of her own boots on cobblestone, the taste of alley air, the precise moment when a patrol's lantern-light would sweep a corner and when to be elsewhere. Every instinct she had honed since the edict screamed at her to vanish into Vell's grey morning like smoke through a keyhole.

But Sera's name was written nowhere on the writ, and that absence was louder than any threat could have been. The Regent did not need to write threats. The Regent simply left blank spaces where things you loved might go.

So Edmund sat in the carriage, and watched Vell slide past the window—bakers' chimneys just waking, gulls arguing over the fish-market refuse, a child chasing a hoop along the quay—and let herself memorize all of it. Not because she believed she would never see it again. But because she had learned, in the archive below the palace, that memory was currency here, and she refused to arrive at any table without counting what was hers.

* * *

The throne room had changed since she'd last seen it from the crowd, during the Rite of Remembrance, when she and Sera had scryed the wrongness beneath the king's smile. Then, it had been grandeur at a distance. Now, up close, she could see the rot in the beauty.

The glass windows were older than the kingdom itself, panes of steppe-glass quarried from the plains east of Vell, each one faintly green, each one singing a note too low for human ears. Her mother had told her once—they thought she hadn't been listening—that glass remembered pressure. That every pane held the shape of every storm it had ever survived. Edmund felt those storms now, pressing against her from all sides, a century of them, and understood that the room itself was a witness that could not testify.

The courtiers had been dismissed. The guards stood at the far doors, close enough to see, too far to hear. And on the throne, in the grey morning light, sat the thing that wore King Aldous.

Up close, the mask slipped more often. Edmund watched the face flicker—not visibly, not in any way a camera or a crowd would catch, but in the way that her hedge-craft caught it: the king's shadow lagged half a heartbeat behind his gestures. His reflection in the steppe-glass blinked at the wrong moments. When he moved his hand, the rings on his fingers caught light that wasn't there yet.

"Edmund Almeida." The voice was warm. It was *designed* to be warm, she realized—the way a stove is designed, the way a snare is designed. "Daughter of Ilse Almeida. Hedge-witch of the Saltmarsh line. You've been very difficult to find."

"Not difficult," Edmund said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Careful. There's a difference."

Something moved behind the king's eyes—amusement, or the memory of amusement, worn like a coat that had belonged to someone else. "Yes. Your mother said much the same thing, once."

The words landed like a hook behind her sternum. She let herself feel it—one breath of white-hot grief—then set it down, deliberately, the way she set down a blade before negotiations. *He is using your mother's voice as a door. Do not walk through it.*

"You know why I've asked you here." The Regent rose from the throne, and the movement was wrong in a way Edmund couldn't name—too fluid, as if the king's body had forgotten how joints worked and was improvising. He descended the dais steps slowly. "You've seen my ledgers. You've walked my vaults. You know what I am, or near enough. So let us dispense with the theater of monarchy. No crown. No court. Just two old practitioners of the same craft, discussing terms."

"Memory-work isn't my craft," Edmund said. "It's my inheritance. There's a difference."

"There is indeed." He stopped three steps away, close enough that she could smell him—or rather, smell what was underneath the royal perfumes: cold stone, and ozone, and something faintly sweet, like fruit going over. "And that difference is precisely why you are standing here instead of lying in one of my harvest-chairs."

* * *

He spoke of terms the way a jeweler speaks of stones, laying each one out with care, letting it catch the light.

"I will return your memories," he said. "All of them. Every hour the ledgers took from you—your childhood entire. Your mother's face, Edmund. You've spent five years unable to recall it. I have it here." He touched his own temple, a gesture almost tender. "I have her laughing. I have her teaching you the grammar of binding at six years old, your small hands clumsy with chalk. I have the day she pressed her sigil-ring into wax and told you what it meant. You could have all of it back by nightfall. Whole. Intact. As though nothing had ever been taken."

Edmund kept her breathing even. Somewhere below them, deep in the palace's roots, she knew, the vault hummed with everything it had ever swallowed. Somewhere down there, her mother existed as an archived light, catalogued and shelved like a specimen.

"And Sera?" she asked, because asking was safer than hoping.

"Spared. Unlisted. Her file—" he gestured vaguely toward the floor, the archives beneath, "—will be burned in front of you. And not only hers." He began to walk then, slowly, circling her, and she turned with him because she refused to let him stand behind her. "Every mage on my harvest lists. The hedge-witches in their safehouses. The dockworkers who buy your luck-knots. All of them, struck from the rolls. The edict remains—law must look like law—but enforcement ends. They will live out their lives practicing small magics in small rooms, unnoticed, unharvested. The city forgets. The city is very good at forgetting; I have fed on that appetite for a century."

The offer hung in the air, enormous and gleaming. Edmund made herself examine it honestly, because she owed herself that much honesty: her mother's face. Her whole childhood, returned. Sera safe. Every frightened person she'd ever sold a charm to safe. Everything she had bled for these past months, handed to her on a silver salver, requiring only one thing in return.

"And what do you want," she said quietly, "in exchange for the world."

"Willingness." The Regent smiled, and this time the smile fit the face perfectly, which was the most frightening thing she'd seen yet. "The prophecy you've been reading calls you *the thorn*. It does not call you the sword or the storm. A thorn, Edmund, does not destroy. A thorn anchors. A thorn is what holds the rose to the earth, what lets the stem climb. I am old. I am sustained by harvested fragments, thousands of them, stitched into something that must be maintained daily, expensively, precariously. I need a core. A living anchor. One mind, freely given, around which the rest can organize and endure." He spread his hands, the king's ringed fingers glinting. "Bind yourself to me. Willingly. In exchange, everyone you love lives, and you remember why you love them."

* * *

There was a silence then, long enough that Edmund could hear the steppe-glass humming its subterranean note, could hear her own heart counting out the seconds like a miser counting coins.

*Willingly.* She turned the word over and found the trap inside it, smooth as a stone found inside a loaf of bread.

Every previous anchor had died screaming—the mirrored second half of the prophecy had been clear about that. Bound candidates who fought the binding from within, tearing themselves apart against the Regent's hunger. The Regent needed willingness not as courtesy but as engineering: a resisting mind was a flawed vessel, cracked clay. Only a mind that *chose* the binding could hold. Which meant the trap was not that he would betray her after the binding.

The trap was that he wouldn't have to.

A willing anchor would hold forever. Would feel, forever. Would sit at the center of that vast stitched-together hunger, conscious, aware, providing the organizing principle around which a century of stolen selves could cohere—her mother's laugh filed next to a dockworker's wedding day, next to a hedge-witch's dying thought, all of it given shape and permanence by Edmund's own living consent. She would not be destroyed. She would be *furnished*. The finest piece in the collection, the keystone of the arch, aware of her own architecture until the sun burned out.

And the memories he offered back? She saw it now with terrible clarity. He would return them—of course he would. Into the binding. What better way to make the anchor content? What sweeter way to season the meat? He would give her back her mother's face so that she could spend eternity remembering it from inside his core, her love for Sera polished and displayed like a jewel in a case she could never open from the outside. He wasn't offering to return what was stolen. He was offering to stock the cage with everything she'd ever treasured, and lock the door from within her own consent.

"That's the bargain," Edmund said. Her voice sounded strange to her—very calm, very far away. "My whole self, for everyone else's safety."

"For everything you've wanted since you were five years old," the Regent corrected gently. "For wholeness. For her face. Think, Edmund. You have spent five years hollow, and you blame me for the hollowness, but consider: you were always going to be missing something. Everyone is. I am merely the first entity in a century to offer you the missing pieces back." He tilted the king's head, and the gesture was achingly human, and that was its own kind of violence. "Refuse me, and we proceed as we have been proceeding. I harvest. They hide. Some die badly. Perhaps the girl dies badly—you know I keep no lists I don't intend to work through. Accept me, and no one else need ever scream in my chairs again. It is not a difficult sum."

It wasn't. That was the horror of it. Edmund ran the arithmetic three times and it came out the same: one life against hundreds, one mind against a city, her endless awareness against everyone else's ordinary, precious, oblivious mornings. Any honest person would sign.

That was how she knew not to.

Because she had read the ledgers. She had seen the names, columns of them, dates going back decades—harvests conducted *after* previous bargains, previous arrangements, previous mages who had come to this room and heard some version of these terms. The Regent's promises had a shelf life exactly as long as his convenience. He had told her himself, in his way: *the city forgets. I have fed on that appetite.* Promises were just another form of memory, and he was the thing that ate memory.

And deeper than that—simpler, and harder—was the truth her mother had stitched into the grammar of her craft, the lesson under all the lessons: *a binding freely given can only be undone by the giver.* If Edmund bound herself willingly, then no one could unmake the Regent without unmaking her. She would become not the thorn that anchors the rose, but the lock welded shut. Sera would spend her life outside a door Edmund had chosen, forever trying picks that could never turn.

"No," Edmund said.

The word was small. It fell into the great humming room and vanished, and for a heartbeat she thought it hadn't landed at all.

"No?" The Regent's head cocked, birdlike, and the shadow on the wall behind him lagged, catching up late.

"No." She made herself look at the face that was not a face, at the eyes where something hungry wore a dead man's patience. "You're offering me my mother back so I'll hand you the only part of me that's still mine. My *yes*. You've taken everything else—my childhood, her face, five years of my life. The yes is the last thing in the vault, and you want me to gift-wrap it." She drew a breath that shook only a little. "I won't. Not for Sera. Not for the city. Not even for her face. Because you don't keep bargains—you keep *ingredients*. And I will not spend eternity as the shelf you arrange yourself on."

The stillness that followed was absolute. Even the steppe-glass seemed to stop singing.

Then the Regent laughed—a soft, genuine sound, horribly warm—and the mask settled back over the wrongness like water closing over a dropped stone. "Your mother refused me too, at first. Did you know that? She stood roughly where you're standing and used nearly the same words. It didn't save her. But it did make the harvest interesting." He turned, ascending the dais, settling back onto the throne with the ease of long possession. "Take her to the gate," he said to the guards, conversationally. "No escort beyond the walls. Let her run home to the archivist. Let her tell her little network that the terms were offered and refused." The king's face arranged itself into something almost kind. "Let her believe courage is a plan."

The guards opened the doors. Edmund walked between them with her spine straight and her hands steady, and only when the fog of the outer courtyard closed around her did she allow herself to shake.

She had refused the bargain. She had kept the yes. And now, she understood, looking up at a sky gone bruise-dark over the Glass Steppe, the harvest would begin in earnest—no more quiet disappearances, no more polite writs. She had shown the Regent her refusal, and he had shown her his patience, and somewhere beneath the city the machines of memory were already waking, hungry, patient, vast.

She ran for Sera. Behind her, the palace windows caught the darkening light and held it, the way glass remembers everything, the way the Regent remembered everything, the way Edmund now knew she would have to give up the last thing she loved to end it—

—and ahead, across the rooftops of Vell, the first streetlamps guttered blue, all at once, as though something enormous had drawn breath.
