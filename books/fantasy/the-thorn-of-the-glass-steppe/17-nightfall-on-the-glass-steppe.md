## 17. Nightfall on the Glass Steppe

**Edmund**

The refusal was still warm in her mouth when the sky went wrong.

Edmund stood at the window of the safehouse, watching the sun sink behind Vell's western towers, and the light did something light should never do. It thinned. Not like dusk—dusk had texture, had amber and rose and the soft bruising of evening. This was subtraction. The color drained out of the world as though someone were wringing it from the fabric of the air itself, and in its place came a faint silver shimmer, the sheen of glass under frost.

The Glass Steppe beyond the city walls caught that shimmer first. Then the rooftops. Then the river.

"It's starting," Sera said from the doorway. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not; they gripped the frame hard enough to blanch.

Below them, the night market was lighting its lamps. Edmund watched a woman hang lanterns over a stall of dried fish, watched a boy chase a dog between the tables, watched an old man argue cheerfully about the price of chestnuts—and then the shimmer reached the street, sliding along the cobbles like water finding its level, and the woman stopped mid-motion, lantern still in her raised hand. The boy sat down where he stood, in the middle of the alley, dog forgotten. The old man's argument ended in the same breath it would have continued, his mouth closing around a word he would never say.

They didn't fall. That was the worst part. They simply *stopped*, standing in the lamplight with their eyes gone smooth and pale as river stones, and from each of them—Edmund could see it now, could see it the way you see your own heartbeat in a dark room—a fine thread of light lifted, thin as spider silk, and drifted east toward the palace.

Toward the Spire, where the Regent wore a dead man's crown.

"He said he'd take the city," Edmund whispered. "I thought it was a threat. It was a schedule."

Sera came to stand beside her. Their shoulders touched. "How long?"

Edmund did the arithmetic of horror. Hundreds of threads rising from the lower districts alone, drifting in a slow convergent spiral. "If he pulls from everyone at once—not just mages, everyone—he doesn't need us anymore. We stop being leverage. We stop being anything." She turned away from the window before the sight of it could hollow her out. "He's not harvesting the city, Sera. He's *emptying* it. A hundred thousand vessels, scraped clean, and him wearing all of it."

The hedge-mages were gathered in the main room below: eleven of them, maybe twelve if you counted Tam, who was fourteen and stubborn about being counted. Old Wren with her knotwork fingers. The Ferro twins, who spoke in finishes of each other's sentences. Marisol, whose charms could make a door forget it was a door. They had spent three days arguing about whether Edmund should be used as bait, whether the network should run or fight, whether any of it mattered against a thing that ate memory for bread.

But now they were all looking at the window, at the silver light crawling up the street, at the blank-eyed neighbors they'd bought bread from and hidden behind and loved in the small cowardly ways the fearful love.

Tam spoke first. "My mum's out there."

No one answered, because there was nothing to answer with. Only the sound of the shimmer passing through the walls, faint as a struck wineglass, and somewhere beneath it—the Regent's voice, not heard but *felt*, a pressure behind the eyes, patient and vast:

***All of it is mine to gather. You were only ever late arrivals.***

Edmund looked around the room. At frightened faces. At people who had spent their whole lives perfecting invisibility, folding themselves small, teaching their children to flinch.

"We can't fight him," she said. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. But we can be loud." She held up her hands, and let the luck-knot sigil flare green above her palms—for the first time in five years, deliberately, in front of witnesses. "Every edict, every burning, every year we hid—we hid because we thought being seen meant dying. He's betting everything on that. On us staying invisible while he drinks the city." The green light reflected in twelve pairs of eyes, and something shifted in the room, some ancient weight tilting. "So tonight we are the loudest thing in Vell. Tonight we walk up the Glass Steppe with our magic *showing*, and every soul he hasn't taken yet sees exactly what a witch looks like."

Wren laughed—a short, cracked sound, half terror. "They'll burn us."

"The Regent's already doing that," Edmund said. "At least this way, they'll remember why."

**Sera**

The palace road was called the Processional Way because kings had once ridden up it in glory, and tonight it carried a different procession entirely: thirteen hedge-mages walking openly through streets full of the hollowed, their spells lit like torches, their names spoken aloud by one another so that no one could later claim they had been strangers.

Sera walked beside Edmund and kept count.

It was her way of being useful—counting. She had been an archivist too long to do otherwise. Thirteen walkers. Forty-one blank citizens passed on the way to the Gate of Bells, standing in doorways and gutters, threads of silver light streaming from their brows. Three patrols of the palace guard, motionless at their posts, their own harvests already underway—the Regent consumed his servants first, tidily, from the inside out. And overhead, converging on the Spire, a river of stolen light so dense it cast shadows.

She had given these people's names away once. Or nearly. She had written the report that made the raid possible, and the raid had taken Halvane the leader, and Edmund had forgiven her in the rain in a way that felt less like absolution than like a debt Sera intended to spend her whole life repaying. So she counted. She counted because numbers were honest, and because if she stopped counting she would start screaming.

"Gate's warded," Marisol murmured at the Gate of Bells. She pressed her palm flat against the iron and listened with her whole body. "Old work. Memory-wrought. It remembers being closed."

"Then remind it of something else," Edmund said.

Marisol smiled—thin, fierce—and began to sing. It wasn't a song Sera recognized; it was older than songs, the grammar Edmund had once tried to describe, vowels that bent meaning the way heat bends light. And the gate, which had remembered being closed for two hundred years, forgot. The iron sighed open like an old man changing his mind, and the thirteen walked through into the palace grounds, and no alarm sounded, because the Regent had already eaten everyone who might have raised one.

The gardens were full of courtiers standing among the roses, faces slack, threads rising. One of them was a young woman holding a lute she'd been playing moments ago; the last notes seemed trapped inside the instrument, trembling. Sera made herself look at each face as they passed. She made herself count them. *This is what your report fed. Look at what your fear grew.*

"Left here," Edmund whispered. "There's a servant's stair behind the ivy—it goes down to the vaults, then up through the old kitchens to the throne floor. Sera—" She stopped. In the moonless silver light her face looked carved. "You don't have to come past the vaults. What's down there—"

"I know what's down there." Sera had drawn the map from inside the harvest-vault with her mind bleeding and her name half-scraped off her; she knew better than anyone alive except Edmund. "I'm coming."

Edmund's hand found hers. Brief, hard, gone again—because they both knew that holding on now would be how they died, clinging to each other instead of moving.

They descended.

The stair was narrow and cold, and the deeper they went, the louder the pressure of the Regent's attention became, a tide pressing against the walls of Sera's skull. *You refused,* it seemed to murmur. *Refusal is a flavor too.* She gritted her teeth and recited catalogue numbers to herself, the oldest ones, the dry ones, the ones with no feeling in them at all. *Codex Aldous 4 through 19. Sealed folio, provenance unknown. Watercolor portrait, dated one hundred twelve years prior.*

The vault doors stood open. Of course they did. The Regent wanted them to come; the trap had been set since the writ first summoned Edmund across a marble floor. But wanting a thing and getting it cleanly were different arts, and Sera had learned in the archives that even tyrants misfile things.

Inside, the harvest-racks glowed with their cargo of stolen lives, thousands of luminous vials of memory arranged in rows like a library of souls—and Sera saw Edmund go white, saw her reach toward one shelf where a particular light burned warm gold, the light of a mother laughing in a kitchen, and saw Edmund close her fist and turn away from it with the cost of that turning written across her whole body.

"Not yet," Edmund said, to the shelf, to herself. "After. If I touch that now, I'll never do what has to be done."

They climbed again, toward the throne, toward the thing wearing the king.

And at the top of the final stair, Sera counted one last thing, the only number that mattered: *two*. Two women, stepping out of the dark into the silver-drowned throne room, carrying nothing but a dead language and each other's names.

Behind them, far below, the gate Marisol had unmade stayed open—and through it, up the Processional Way, came the flicker of green and blue and gold. The hedge-mages who had stayed behind at the walls were not running. They were lighting beacons. Small magics, forbidden magics, blazing openly in the streets of Vell for the first time in a century—so that anyone still awake, anyone the Regent hadn't reached, would look up from their hiding places and see the sky full of witches, and know which side of history the darkness had chosen.

The invisible, made visible at last.

Sera took Edmund's hand again, properly this time, and they walked into the light.

The throne room doors opened before they touched them, and the Regent smiled with King Aldous's mouth, and said, in a voice like a ledger closing:

*"Ah. My thorn. My archivist. Come to be filed."*

Above the Spire, the harvest-sky brightened toward dawn that no one alive would recognize, and somewhere in the deep vaults a golden vial waited, patient as grief, holding the face of Edmund's mother—and Edmund stepped forward into the dark, and began to speak the first word of the unbinding.
