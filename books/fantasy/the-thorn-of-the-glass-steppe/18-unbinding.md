## 18. Unbinding

The throne room had no doors anymore.

Edmund felt that before she saw it — the way the air changed at the threshold, thickening like water gone stale in a sealed jar. Behind her, the great bronze doors hung from their hinges, torn open by hedge-mages who had spent their whole lives learning how to hide and had discovered, tonight, what their hands could do when they stopped hiding. The corridor beyond was full of shouting, of light, of Sera.

Sera stood with her back to a toppled statue of some dead king, ink-stained fingers wrapped around the hilt of a ceremonial sword she held like a fire poker because she had never once been taught to hold it any other way. Around her, eleven hedge-mages formed a ragged crescent — Old Maren with her hands full of stormlight, the twins Ivet and Dossa breathing smoke-runes into the air, a dockworker named Pell whose luck-knots Edmund had sold him for three coppers years ago now glowing green at his wrists like embers wrapped in wool.

"You don't have to say anything," Sera said. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "Just come back down when it's done."

"I always come back down," Edmund said. It wasn't quite a lie. She came back down; she simply never stayed.

She crossed the threshold alone.

* * *

The throne room was larger than memory allowed for, though Edmund suspected memory had little jurisdiction here. The ceiling had been raised by magic or by hunger until it vanished into a dark that swallowed torchlight whole. The glass floor — the famous Glass Steppe, imported shard by shard from some conquered province a century ago — stretched before her like a frozen lake, and beneath her feet, deep in the ice-colored depths, something moved.

Not reflected. *Contained.*

The Regent sat on the throne of Vell wearing King Aldous's face the way a glove wears a hand. He did not rise. His eyes found her across forty yards of glass and light, and he smiled, and the smile was warm, and that was the worst thing in the room.

"Edmund." Her name in her mother's cadence. Not the words — those were his — but the *music*, lifted whole from five-year-old harvests, played back to her like a lullaby recorded by a thief. "You've grown so tall."

"Don't." The word cracked out of her before she could armor it.

He tilted his head, and behind the king's gray eyes something vast shifted its weight, the way a whale shifts beneath a boat — felt more than seen, enormous and patient. "Don't what? Don't speak to you in the voice that sang you to sleep? It isn't mine, I grant you. But then—" the smile widened by one tooth— "*nothing* in this room is mine. That has always been my condition. I am made entirely of what other people kept."

The sigil burned on Edmund's palm where she'd cut it an hour ago in the safehouse's last quiet minute: her mother's grammar, the opening line of the unbinding, drawn in her own blood because blood remembered better than ink. She walked toward the throne. Each step rang faintly on the glass, and each ring traveled downward, and each time it did, the thing below stirred.

"You should know," the Regent said conversationally, "that the girl downstairs will not hold. She is brave the way candles are brave. But I have eaten braver."

"Then eat me first."

"Oh, Edmund." He sounded genuinely sorry. "I intend to."

* * *

She stopped ten paces from the throne, on the spot the map Sera had smuggled out of the vault marked with a single inked star. The anchor point. The place where the Regent's roots met the world.

Her mother's spellbook was strapped against her ribs under her coat, but Edmund didn't need it. She'd spent three sleepless nights copying the unbinding onto the inside of her own eyelids. She knelt on the glass. It was cold enough to bite through cloth, cold as the night market in deep winter, cold as the alley where she'd hidden from her first patrol with her heart slamming and a half-finished luck-knot dissolving in her sweat-damp fist. She pressed her bleeding palm flat against the floor.

"*Roots above ground,*" she began, and her mother's language poured out of her mouth in shapes that predated Vell, predated the crown, predated the edict that had made this crime punishable by fire. "*Thorn without rose. King without kingdom. What was taken and woven, let it be unwoven. What was stolen and worn, let it be returned.*"

Beneath the glass, the vastness convulsed.

The floor lit from underneath — not white but a hundred colors at once, memories surfacing like drowned lanterns rising through lake water. Edmund saw fragments that were not hers: a baker's wedding morning, a soldier's last letter home, a child's first snow. The harvest of a century, all of it stored here, all of it *alive*, and all of it beginning to scream.

The Regent stood.

King Aldous's body unfolded from the throne with the smooth wrongness of a puppet whose strings had too many hands on them, and when he spoke again, the borrowed warmth was gone from his voice, and what remained was older than the face he wore.

"You do not understand what you are unbinding," he said. "So allow me to show you."

He raised one hand, and Edmund's childhood fell on her like weather.

* * *

It came as sound first. A kitchen. Rain on a tin roof. A woman humming off-key while something simmered, and the humming was the most beautiful thing Edmund had ever heard because it was *hers*, it was the original, not the echo she'd carried for twenty-five years and polished smooth with handling.

"Edmund, love, mind the kettle—"

*Mother.* The word detonated in her chest. Her palm slipped on the glass. The unbinding stuttered.

"No," Edmund gasped. "No, that's not—"

"Isn't it?" The Regent circled her slowly, boots ringing on the glass, and everywhere he stepped the memory sharpened. The kitchen assembled itself around them both — the chipped blue cups, the herb bundles drying from the rafters, steam ghosting up a window that looked out on a garden Edmund had forgotten existed until this exact moment and would now never be able to forget again. "I keep everything intact, Edmund. That is the entire art of it. Your mother taught me that, in a sense. Or rather — her memories did. She was among my first. Did you know that? Before the ledgers, before the sub-archive, there was only a frightened king and a woman who came to court asking questions about memory-work, and I was so very hungry, and she was so very *full*."

"She's alive," Edmund whispered. The kitchen flickered. "In the vault. Sera found—"

"Archived, yes. Preserved beautifully. You could visit her whenever you liked, once we reach our arrangement." He crouched beside her kneeling form, and the king's face arranged itself into an expression of infinite gentleness. "But first, you must understand what you're trying to destroy. Look at her. Really look."

And Edmund looked, because she couldn't help it, because five years of hiding and a lifetime of gaps had left a hole in her shaped exactly like this kitchen, this rain, this humming woman turning from the stove—

Her mother looked up from the pot and smiled directly at her.

Not past her. Not the blind warmth of a replayed scene. *At her.* And said, in the tone she'd used for scraped knees and broken charms and every small catastrophe of a small life: "Oh, sweetheart. What have they done to you?"

The unbinding collapsed.

Edmund's hand came off the glass as if the floor had turned to flame, and she stumbled backward into a kitchen that wasn't there, into arms that weren't there, and the sigil on her palm guttered like a candle in a draft, and the Regent watched her with the patience of something that had waited a hundred years and could wait out another hour.

"There it is," he murmured. "There's the crack in you. Do you know why every candidate before you died screaming? Not because the binding fought them. Because *they* fought themselves. They came here armored in certainty and I never once had to break through it. I only had to knock." He rose. The kitchen dissolved around them, rain fading last, the humming lingering like perfume. "Love is the finest blade ever forged, Edmund. It keeps its edge forever, because the wielder never thinks to check whether someone else is holding the hilt."

* * *

Below — she felt it rather than heard it, through the soles of her boots, through the glass, through the soles of her soul — the line was bending.

Sera's presence had become familiar to Edmund over these strange weeks the way a heartbeat becomes familiar: unnoticed until it falters. Now it faltered. A spike of fear, sharp as a needle through the floor. Then Maren's stormlight flaring white through the seams of the far doors, then darkness where a mage had stood and then didn't. The Regent's harvest-things — the blank-eyed servants of the palace, citizens emptied just hours ago in the streets — pressing the crescent of hedge-mages back, back, back.

*Hold the line,* Edmund told herself, *and get up.*

She got up.

Her knees shook. Her palm bled freely now, dripping onto the glass, and each drop hissed where it struck, and beneath the floor the vastness recoiled from her blood the way deep water recoils from lightning. Of course. Her mother's grammar ran in her veins undiluted; the harvested memories of E. Almeida, age five, were woven into the core itself, which meant her blood was the one solvent in the world the core feared.

The Regent saw it too. The mask of Aldous tightened.

"You would burn your own history to spite me."

"I would spend it," Edmund said. Her voice came out low and even, and she marveled distantly at how much it sounded like her mother's. "That's the difference between us. You hoard. I've learned to let things go — five years of practice. Every trinket I sold, every stranger I helped and never saw again. I got good at letting go, you hollow thing. It's the one craft the edict couldn't touch."

She pressed her hand down.

"*Roots above ground,*" she said, louder, and the words tore the air open. "*Thorn without rose!*" The sigil blazed. Cracks spiderwebbed outward from her palm across forty yards of glass, and through every crack light poured up — not the Regent's hoarded gold but a clean silver-green, hedge-light, night-market light, the color of small magics done quietly for people who needed them. "*King without kingdom! What was taken and woven, LET IT BE UNWOVEN!*"

The throne room screamed.

Every archived memory screamed at once, a thousand voices in a thousand registers, and the Regent threw back the king's head and screamed with them, and for one blazing instant Edmund saw through the mask to what wore it: a lattice, a web, a cathedral of stolen moments with a single black seed at its center — the original fear, the king's fear, grown monstrous in the dark for a century.

The cracks reached the seed.

The Regent moved faster than anything that size should move. One moment he stood screaming on the dais; the next his hand closed around Edmund's throat, and the king's grip was iron, and the vastness poured through it, flooding into her, searching for the flaw it had found before.

It found the kitchen. It always would find the kitchen.

"*Your mother would be ashamed of you,*" it said in her mother's voice, directly into her skull, tender and merciless. "*Look at what you're doing. Look who you're killing. Every person in this city sleeps safely because of me. Every memory I keep is a person who doesn't die twice. And you — you'd unravel them all to fill one hole in yourself. Sweetheart. What have they done to you?*"

Edmund's vision blurred. Her hands clawed at the king's wrist and found flesh that gave like wax. Somewhere far above, through the ruined doors, someone shouted her name in Sera's voice — raw, furious, afraid — and the sound reached her the way dawn reaches the bottom of a well.

*Love used as a blade,* she thought, black spots blooming across her sight. *Fine. Then I'll use it back.*

Because she finally understood the trap. The Regent needed her to believe the memories it showed her were the treasure. But she'd read the ledger. She'd seen the entry in her own handwriting of grief: *E. Almeida — partial harvest — core fragment unstable.* The memories in the vault were real, yes, luminous, alive. But the ones the Regent wore like weapons — the kitchen, the voice, the humming — those were *copies*. Beautiful, perfect, weaponized copies, rebuilt from fragments and polished by a century of practice.

The real ones sat in jars below her feet.

And her mother had taught her the difference between a charm and the thing it imitated. Had drilled it into her before the gaps began: *A copy wants you to stop looking. The true thing wants you to look closer.*

"Stop," Edmund rasped — and the Regent leaned in to savor her surrender —

"—calling her *sweetheart*," Edmund finished. "She never once called me that."

The grip faltered. A fraction of an inch. A fraction was a universe.

"*She called me 'love,'*" Edmund said, and drove her bleeding sigil-hand up through the king's wax-soft chest, into the light, into the seed. "*When she was angry and when she wasn't. 'Mind the kettle, love.' 'Come inside, love.' You took her voice and you couldn't be bothered to learn her heart. You've been wearing her for twenty years and you never listened to a single word.*"

The seed cracked beneath her palm like river ice in spring.

The scream that followed was not a thousand voices. It was one — old, male, terrified, human — the original fear of a man who had loved his crown more than his soul, and Edmund held on through all of it, held the crack open, held the unbinding's final line on her tongue:

"*What was stolen and worn—*"

The throne room went white. The glass floor shattered upward in slow motion, a blizzard of frozen light, and through the whiteness Edmund saw the lattice tearing, jar after jar afterjar cracking open somewhere far below, a hundred years of stolen selves pouring home—

And then the whiteness found the center of her, the last intact room in the house of her memory, the one chamber she had guarded through fire and edict and five lonely years, and a voice that was neither the Regent nor her mother but the ritual itself, ancient and impartial, asked her the question the thorn prophecy had promised all along:

*What will you give?*

Through the white, faintly, Edmund heard footsteps on broken glass. Someone running toward her through the ruin, calling her name, getting closer.

Edmund opened her eyes.

She knew the answer. She had known it since the vault, since the map, since the moment she'd understood what the ritual demanded and had walked up here anyway.

She turned toward the running footsteps — toward Sera, close now, hand outstretched — and began to speak the price aloud.
