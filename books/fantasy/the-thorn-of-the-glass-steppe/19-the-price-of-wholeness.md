## 19. The Price of Wholeness

The core of the Regent was not what Edmund had imagined. She had expected something mechanical, perhaps—clockwork and cruelty—or a heart of black glass beating in a chamber of bone. What she found instead was a garden.

It grew in the deep vault beneath the throne room, where the palace's oldest foundations met the living rock of the Glass Steppe itself. Pale stalks rose from a floor of fused memory-glass, each one threaded with light that moved like thought: a woman laughing at a market stall, a boy's first word, an old man's last summer. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every stolen life the Regent had ever drunk, planted here and tended like roses.

And at the center, rooted deepest, a single stalk that burned brighter than all the rest. She knew it before she touched it. She knew it the way you know your own name.

*E. Almeida. Five years old.*

Her mother's memories. The harvest that had begun everything.

"It's beautiful, isn't it."

The Regent's voice came from everywhere—from the walls, from the light, from her own skull. It wore King Aldous's timbre the way a thief wears a stolen coat, and beneath it she could hear the other voices now, the ones it had shown her in the throne room: her mother singing, her mother scolding, her mother saying *Edmund, love, come inside before dark.*

"You could tend it," the Regent said. "Instead of burning it down."

"I'm not here to tend anything." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Behind her, down the long stair, Sera was holding the line—the hedge-mages' wards flaring blue-white against the Regent's harvest-tide, buying minutes that cost everything to buy. "I'm here to unmake you."

"You're here because I let you be." The light in the garden dimmed, gathered, shaped itself into a figure standing between the stalks. Not Aldous this time. A woman. Dark hair going grey at the temples, hands stained with hedge-charm ink, a smile Edmund had spent five years failing to remember and one hour failing to forget.

"No," Edmund said.

The figure tilted its head, and it was her mother's gesture, exactly her mother's gesture, the little quirk of curiosity she'd seen in the vault only yesterday and already couldn't bear. "You haven't even looked at me properly. Five years, Edmund. You've been carrying a hole shaped like me through every day of your life. Don't you want to fill it?"

"I *did* look." Her throat was tight. "In the vault. I saw your face. I saw all of it."

"And then you ran. You always run. You ran from Vell when the edict came. You ran from every friend who got too close. You'll run from her too, eventually—that archivist girl with ink on her fingers. Because running is what I taught you." The mother-thing smiled sadly. "I can give you back the years. All of them. The whole harvest, returned intact. Your childhood. Your mother. Yourself. And all it costs is—"

"Don't say it."

"—nothing you weren't already prepared to lose."

The words landed like a blade finding the gap in armor. Because it was true. That was the obscenity of it. Somewhere on the stair behind her, Sera was bleeding for her; somewhere above, the city's streets were filling with blank-eyed people walking toward the palace like sleepwalkers toward the sea; and Edmund stood in a garden of stolen souls knowing—with terrible clarity—that the ritual in her mother's grammar required exactly what the Regent was offering to spare her from giving freely.

That was the trap inside the gift. She had seen it in the throne room and she saw it now, sharper. If she took the bargain, the anchor-binding would not be a sacrifice. It would be a purchase. And a purchased thing belonged to the seller, root and branch, forever. The prophecy's mirrored verse turned over in her mind: *the thorn gives what cannot be bought.* Freely given, or not given at all. The Regent hadn't offered her mercy. It had offered her a cage with velvet lining, and it was betting—had always been betting—that she loved herself too much to refuse.

"You learned my mother's voice," Edmund said quietly. "But you never learned her grammar."

She knelt at the base of the central stalk.

The floor was warm under her palms, faintly pulsing, like the flank of a sleeping animal. Up close she could see what the memory-light actually was: threads. Millions of fine silver threads woven through the glass, each one anchored to a harvested mind, each one humming with a life someone else had lived. The Regent's whole architecture laid bare—a web with no spider at the center, only hunger wearing a king's face.

She began the unbinding.

Her mother's spellbook lay open beside her, propped against a pale stalk, pages soft as cloth from a century of handling. She had copied the final rite onto her own forearm in charm-ink that morning, because she had known—even then, refusing to know—that she might lose the book, or her nerve, or both. The sigils glowed faintly now as she spoke them aloud, and the garden shuddered.

*Root to crown. Crown to thorn. What was taken whole must be unmade by what remains whole.*

"What are you doing?" The Regent's voice cracked—not Aldous anymore, not her mother, just the hollow thing underneath, vast and suddenly afraid. "Stop. *Stop.*"

"The unbinding needs an anchor," Edmund said. She kept her eyes on the sigils, tracing each one with two fingers as she woke it. "A mage bound willingly to the core. Every candidate before me died screaming because they were dragged here. Because they fought. Because being taken isn't the same as giving." She looked up, finally, at the mother-shaped light. "You should have left me my fear. Fear would have made me careful. Hope is what makes me stupid enough to do this."

"Edmund—" The Regent's voice splintered into a chorus now, dozens of voices, hundreds—the harvested dead and dreaming, all speaking through one mouth. "*You will die with us.*"

"Probably." She smiled, and it hurt her face. "But I won't die *yours.*"

She pressed both palms flat against the central stalk.

Light flooded up her arms.

It wasn't pain, at first. It was memory—the stalk reading her, tasting her, cataloguing what she carried. And what she carried was so small. Five years of careful nothing: luck-knots sold in the night market, alleys memorized like prayers, a hundred small kindnesses done invisibly. And beneath all of it, wrapped in silk at the very center of her, one single unbroken thing.

Her mother's face.

Not the Regent's copy—the true one, restored from the vault, held whole these two days like a coal cupped in bare hands. Rain-warm skin. Laugh lines deep as riverbeds. Eyes the color of strong tea, crinkling when she smiled, and the way that smile arrived slowly, like dawn, like it had all the time in the world—

"Oh," the Regent whispered, and for one moment the chorus went silent and only the hollow thing remained, alone with its hunger. "*There it is.*"

"Yes," Edmund said. "Here it is."

She began to feed it in.

Not all at once. Memory by memory, the way you'd feed a fire twig by twig, because the ritual demanded it—demanded that she *know* what she was giving while she gave it. Her mother teaching her the first knot-charm, hands over hands. Her mother's kitchen smelling of burnt honey and ironweed tea. Her mother's voice saying her name the special way, the two-syllable way, *Ed-mund*, like it was a spell of its own.

Each one went out of her like a lamp going dark.

She wept without deciding to. It wasn't graceful grief—it was ugly, gasping, doubled-over grief, tears cutting tracks through the charm-ink on her forearms, sobs that echoed strangely off the glass stalks until the whole garden seemed to be crying with her. And still her hands stayed steady. Still the sigils burned. Because grief, she understood now—grief was just love with nowhere left to go, and she was giving hers somewhere to go, pouring five years of guarded loneliness and two days of recovered joy into the wound at the center of the Regent, and watching it burn.

The stalk began to scream.

Not her mother's voice. Nothing so kind. It screamed in a hundred stolen voices at once, and around it the garden caught—pale stalks igniting thread by thread as the unbinding spread outward from the anchor-point, each harvested memory tearing free of the web. Freed, not destroyed. She could feel the difference in her bones: the threads snapping taut and then *flying*, streaming upward through rock and stone and soil, racing home to minds across the city—to blank-eyed sleepwalkers freezing mid-step on the boulevards, blinking, weeping, remembering their own names.

The Regent came apart loudly. It tried everything. It threw Aldous's face at her, then Sera's, then her own at fifteen, at ten, at five—all the mirrors it had collected—and she walked through them, hand over hand along the dying stalk, feeding the fire what remained. Her mother's laugh. Her mother's anger. Her mother, older than Edmund would ever see her, sitting on a porch step saying *you'll understand when you're grown, love,* which she never had and never would.

The last of it—the very last—was simply the face itself. Whole. Warm. Looking at her the way she'd looked in the vault, like Edmund was the best thing she'd ever made.

"I'm sorry," Edmund whispered. "I have to choose true things over comfortable ones. You taught me that too. I just—I forgot."

And her mother's face smiled—her real smile, slow as dawn—and went out.

* * *

Silence.

The garden was gone. In its place: a crater of ordinary rock, cracked and smoking, dust settling like grey snow. The threads were all fled home. The air tasted of rain and burnt sugar and, faintly, of nothing at all.

Edmund knelt in the wreckage of the thing that had eaten a century, and reached for her mother's face, and found—smoothness. A clean-edged absence, like a portrait cut from its frame. She knew a woman had lived who loved her. She knew the woman's hands had been ink-stained and her tea oversweet and her voice had said *come inside before dark.* She knew all of it as knowledge, hard and dry as ledger entries.

She could not picture her.

The hole where the face had been didn't ache the way wounds ache. It ached the way missing does—like a word on the tip of the tongue, like a room you know was furnished but can't furnish again. She knelt there in the dust and cried for it anyway, cried for the comfort she'd chosen against, and discovered something strange in the middle of the crying: it didn't hollow her out. Choosing it had filled the shape of the loss with something else. Something with edges. Hers.

Footsteps on the stair. Fast ones.

Sera burst through the shattered doorway with wardlight still guttering off her fingers and blood on her temple and terror all over her face—and stopped. Looked at the crater. Looked at Edmund, kneeling in the ruin, alive, weeping, smiling, all three at once.

"Edmund—"

"He's waking up," Edmund said. Her voice was wrecked and steady. She nodded upward, toward the throne room, where something ancient had just shifted in an enchanted sleep a hundred years deep—as if a great weight had been lifted off a chest, as if a man were drawing his first true breath since before either of them was born. "The real king. He's waking up."

Above them, through a hundred feet of stone, the whole palace shuddered once—and went quiet, like a held breath finally let go.
