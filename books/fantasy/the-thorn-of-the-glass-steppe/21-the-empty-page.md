## 21. The Empty Page

The archive smelled of dust and candle smoke and the faint green scent of old ink, and Edmund had come to hate it a little less every day.

She sat at the long table in the reading room of the palace's restored library—restored was a generous word; half the shelves still stood empty, their contents either burned by edict or eaten by the Regent's hunger for records—and she watched Sera work. Sera always worked the same way: sleeves pushed to her elbows, ink-stained fingers moving down a ledger with the patience of someone counting raindrops, lips moving faintly as she cross-referenced one name against another. The morning light came through the high windows in long amber columns, and it caught the loose strands of hair at Sera's temple and turned them to copper.

Three days since the Regent fell. Three days since the city woke from its walking sleep with tears on its faces and no memory of why they were crying. Three days since Edmund had stood in the wreckage of the vault with a hole in her chest shaped like her mother's face, and Sera had found her there, and neither of them had said anything at all, because there was nothing to say that wasn't already written into the way Sera took her hand.

The book lay between them on the table.

*The Thorn of the Glass Steppe*, the catalogue called it, though no catalogue had ever successfully claimed it. A century-old codex bound in cracked green leather, its pages thick as felt, its watercolors faded to the colors of things seen through fog. It had been the beginning of everything—the portrait of Sera dated 112 years before her birth, the verse about the thorn who would unmake the crown, Edmund's own hedge-witch sigil hidden in the margins like a signature on a contract she'd never read. She had hated the book for a while. She had feared it longer than that. Now it simply sat there, an old animal that had finally stopped growling, and she found she could touch it without her skin crawling.

"Turn to the end," Sera said, not looking up from her ledger. "You keep circling it. Just look."

Edmund turned to the final page.

She had seen it empty before—or she had thought it empty, back when the book first fell into their hands, back when prophecy was still a thing that happened to other people. Blank parchment, or so it seemed. Now she understood that the page had never been blank. It had only been waiting, the way a field waits under snow.

The ink was still drying.

Two figures stood in the watercolor, rendered in that strange antique palette the rest of the book used—as if the painter had mixed all their colors from ash and honey. The taller figure had dark skin and close-cropped hair and wore the patched coat of the night market, and even in miniature, even in faded pigment, Edmund knew her own hands: drawn mid-gesture, fingers spread in the opening shape of a binding spell. The other figure stood just behind her shoulder, slighter, holding a lantern, and the lantern's light was painted in gold so fine it seemed to tremble off the page. Ink-stained fingers. A smudge of soot on one cheek. Sera, unmistakably Sera, looking at the painted Edmund with an expression the artist had somehow made legible across a hundred years: *I am here. I am staying.*

They were standing, Edmund realized, on the Glass Steppe itself—the pale terraced hill above the palace where the king's garden had gone wild during the century of the Regent's rule, where the hedges grew through the cracks in the glassy stone like green fire. She knew because she had stood there yesterday, in the wind, watching the real Aldous walk slowly through his own garden like a man learning his house again, touching each hedge as if greeting an old friend who might not remember him.

Below the painting, in the same spidery hand as the verses, a single line:

*The thorn and the lamp, at the crown's unmaking, when the harvest is ended.*

"That's us," Edmund said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "That's three days ago. That's the night we—" She stopped. The night they climbed the Steppe together after the unbinding, too exhausted to speak, and watched dawn come up over Vell like a slow forgiveness. The night Sera had said, *you don't have to decide what you are yet. You can just be.* "It painted it. Before we ever did it. Before either of us was born."

"Before the paint was mixed," Sera said quietly. She set down her pen. "That's what I can't stop thinking about. Someone ground these pigments a hundred years ago. Someone chose this blue." She reached across the table—not touching the page, never touching the page; she had learned that much caution too well—and traced the air above the lantern-light. "And now it's finished. Like a letter that took a century to arrive."

Edmund looked at the two small painted women and felt something she couldn't name. Not dread, exactly. Not relief. Something closer to what she'd felt as a child, finding her mother's handwriting on a shopping list years after her mother's voice had begun to fade from memory: grief and tenderness tangled up with a kind of vertigo, the sense of being held by someone who could no longer hold you.

Then the ink began to move.

"Sera," she said. "Sera, look—"

At first she thought it was the light shifting, a cloud crossing the windows. But the cloud was inside the page. The colors were draining—not fading the way old watercolor fades, gradually, gracefully, but *unwriting*, the pigment pulling backward out of the parchment like breath drawn into lungs. The painted Edmund's hands closed. The lantern dimmed. Sera's painted face lost its expression first, then its features, then its outline, until the second figure was only a suggestion, a smudge of warm color against the pale terraces of the Steppe.

"No," Edmund said, and put her hand flat on the page, as if she could hold the image in place by pressure alone. "No, wait—"

The parchment drank its own ink.

In the space of ten heartbeats, the final page went white. Not blank the way it had been blank before—waiting, pregnant, patient—but white the way a page is white after it has said everything it intends to say. Clean. Finished. The paper itself seemed different, somehow, the fibers settled, the surface smooth as a frozen pond. When Edmund tilted it toward the window, the light slid across it without catching on anything at all.

The margins, where her mother's hedge-witch sigil had lived hidden among a hundred others, were bare.

"It's done," Sera whispered. She had come around the table without Edmund noticing; her hand was warm on Edmund's shoulder. "The whole book?"

Edmund flipped backward, page after page, faster than care warranted. The portrait of Sera—gone. The verse about the thorn—blank vellum. The scrying diagrams, the mirrored second half, the warnings written backwards in a dead clerk's hand—all of it unwritten, all of it returned to nothing, the book exhaling a century of tomorrow in a single long sigh. Only the covers remained, cracked green leather scarred with age, holding a sheaf of clean white pages like a bird holding nothing in its beak.

Only the title survived, stamped into the leather in gilt gone dull: *The Thorn of the Glass Steppe*. And even that, Edmund noticed, had changed. The letters were shallower than she remembered. Worn. As if a hundred years of meaning had been pressed into them and then slowly released.

"Prophecy fulfilled is prophecy ended," she said. She'd heard the phrase somewhere—in the safehouse, maybe, from Marisol the hedge-healer, who talked about fate the way farmers talked about weather. She hadn't believed it then. "It didn't just predict us. It was *holding* us. All this time. Every page was a hand around our throats, and now—"

"Now it let go," Sera said.

* * *

They carried the book out of the archive because Edmund could not bear to leave it shelved among the ledgers of harvests and the edicts of burnings, among all the palace's instruments of ownership. Some books owned you. This one, at least, had had the decency to release its claim.

The Glass Steppe was the only place she wanted to be. The guards at the garden gate—they were new guards, confused ones, men and women who had served the Regent without knowing what they served and were now trying to remember how to serve a frightened, aging, human king—waved them through without question. Word had come down from Aldous himself: the hedge-witch and the archivist were to be admitted anywhere, anytime. *They gave me back my kingdom,* he'd told Edmund the day before, gripping her hands with fingers thin as winter twigs, his eyes wet and ancient and unbearably kind. *Whatever you need of Vell, it is yours. Whatever you mourn, the kingdom mourns with you.* He had not known, could not know, precisely what she had paid. But he had held her hands a moment longer than courtesy required, and that had been almost enough.

The wind on the Steppe smelled of cut grass and river water. Below, Vell sprawled in the afternoon haze—rooflines and chimney smoke, the cranes of the harbor, the bright scatter of the night market being set up early, stalls going up in daylight now because no one needed darkness to sell charms anymore. Edmund could pick out the market's heart from here, the square where she had spent five years selling luck-knots to dockworkers with her craft folded small and hidden inside mundane things. Someone had strung bunting between two lampposts. Red and yellow. Festive. Absurd. Wonderful.

She sat down on the lowest terrace, where the glassy stone gave way to soil, and set the book on her knees.

"Why do I feel like it died?" she asked. "It tried to own my whole life. It drew me before I was born and made me walk toward its picture. I should be dancing on its grave."

Sera lowered herself beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Because it's the last thing your mother touched," she said.

Edmund went still.

"The sigils in the margins. Your mother's grammar—I've been comparing them to the spellbook you showed me. Same hand, Edmund. Same abbreviations, same habit of turning her *t*s into little knives. She annotated this book. Maybe she inherited it, maybe she found it, maybe she was meant to find it. But she read it. She wrote in it. She hid herself in its margins the way you hid your craft inside luck-knots." Sera's voice was gentle and precise, the voice she used for fragile catalogues. "When the ink unwrote itself, it unwrote her too. The last place her handwriting existed outside your memory."

"My memory has holes in it," Edmund said. Flatly. Not accusing Sera—accusing the world.

"I know."

"You can't know. Nobody can know what it's like to reach for your own mother's face and find a room with the furniture missing. I have her *hands*. I have the shape of her standing in a doorway. I have the sound of her voice saying my name, and I have the grammar she taught me, every rule of her magic worn into me like grooves in a stair. But her face—" Edmund's throat closed. She made herself go on, because Sera deserved the truth spoken plainly, and because speaking it plainly was the only way she'd learned to survive it. "I fed her face into that thing's core. I chose it. I would choose it again. And some mornings I wake up and I've forgotten that I chose it, and I think *she's coming back, she's alive somewhere, I'll see her at the market*—and then I remember, and it's like losing her twice."

The wind moved through the hedges. Somewhere below, a child laughed, high and bright, the sound carrying up the terraces the way sound carries on hills.

"I'm not telling you it gets smaller," Sera said. "I'm telling you it's yours. The grief. Even the hole. They took everything else from you—your childhood, your past, five years of your life poured into that thing's core like wine into a cask. But the hole is the part they couldn't take, because the hole is what *choosing* left behind. Nobody else gets to interpret it. No book, no prophecy, no king." She nodded at the white page on Edmund's knees. "That's why it let go, I think. Not because it was finished using you. Because you finished *it*. You walked out the far side of a story someone else wrote, and stories can't hold people who aren't inside them anymore."

Edmund looked down at the empty page. The sun moved a little; the light slid off the clean parchment like water off glass.

"Show me," she said suddenly.

"Show you what?"

"When you were a girl. Before the archive. Before any of this." Edmund turned to face her fully. "You know everything about me that can be known. You've read my harvest ledger. You've seen the worst of what was taken. And I realize I don't know how you got ink on your fingers. I don't know what you wanted to be, before wanting felt dangerous."

Something moved through Sera's expression—surprise, then a softness so unguarded it hurt to witness, like a door opening onto a lit room. "You want my history? My boring, unharrowed, completely un-prophesied history?"

"I want the parts nobody wrote down."

So Sera told her. About growing up above a bindery in the Tanner's Quarter, falling asleep to the smell of glue. About copying her first document at seven years old and deciding, with the absolute certainty available only to children, that letters were a kind of magic and she would spend her life among them. About the year her father drowned and she copied out his shipping manifests one final time, every line, just to hear his transactions in her head a little longer. About how she'd applied to the palace archive because it was the largest collection of words in the kingdom, and how she'd spent eleven years believing that a person could love a place built on rot if they only stayed in the clean rooms.

"And then a hedge-witch walked in asking about a book that wouldn't catalogue," Sera said, "and I thought, *oh no,* the way you think it when a story starts being about you." She smiled, and the soot-smudge ghost of her painted self seemed suddenly very near. "Best mistake I ever made."

Edmund laughed—a real laugh, surprising her, rising out of her chest like a bird startled from hedgerow. It echoed off the terraces. Below, someone in the market looked up.

* * *

The sun was low and the shadows of the hedges stretched long across the Steppe when Edmund opened the book one more time.

She hadn't planned to. But the light was doing something to the empty page—gilding it, warming it—and she found she wanted to look at the whiteness once more before she decided what to do with the poor gutted thing. Bury it, probably. Or give it to the real Aldous, a relic of the century he'd slept through. Or burn it properly this time, on her own terms, in her own fire, with her mother's grammar spoken over the flames—not as an ending but as a funeral, which is a different thing entirely.

But when she opened the cover, she stopped.

The first page was not quite empty.

There—faint as breath on a mirror, so light she might have imagined it—a mark. In the upper margin, where a hundred hidden sigils had once crowded: a single small sign, drawn in ink that had not yet dried, in a hand she knew better than her own.

Her mother's knife-bladed *t*s. One word.

*Begin.*

Edmund sat very still. The wind combed the hedges. Down in Vell, the lamps of the night market were being lit one by one, a constellation assembling itself street by street, and the smell of frying dough and woodsmoke drifted up the hill like an invitation.

Not a prophecy. Prophecies told you who to be. This was older and smaller and kinder—an instruction passed hand to hand down a line of women who hid their craft inside ordinary things. A grammar lesson. The first word of a sentence with no predicted end.

"Well," Edmund said to the empty page, and her voice did not shake. "All right."

She took out the stub of charcoal she carried for marking prices, and beneath her mother's single word, in careful letters, she began to write—not a future, not a fate, only a record: *On the third day after the harvest ended, I sat on the Steppe with Sera, and the wind smelled of grass, and I was afraid of nothing in this book.*

Behind her, Sera leaned her chin on Edmund's shoulder to read, ink-stained fingers curled around a lantern she hadn't needed to light, and asked, "What comes next?"

"Anything," Edmund said, and wrote it down.
