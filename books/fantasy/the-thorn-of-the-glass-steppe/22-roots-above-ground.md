## 22. Roots Above Ground

The night market came back the way gardens do after a long frost—not all at once, and not everywhere, and not without scars.

Edmund noticed it first in the smells. For five years she had taught herself to read Vell by scent: tallow and wet stone meant safety, iron and lamp-oil meant patrol, and anything sweet meant someone nearby was careless enough to burn candles they couldn't afford. Now the air over Lantern Row carried cinnamon again, and frying dough, and under all of it—faint as a signature on an old letter—the green smell of living charmwork, out in the open, unhidden.

She stood at her stall with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, both hands visible, both hands working. That was the other thing that had changed. She had spent five years learning to keep her fingers still around strangers. Now she braided luck-knots in full view of anyone who cared to watch, murmuring her mother's grammar into the cord like a woman humming while she sewed.

*Wind at the wrist,* the old spell went, in a language older than the edict, older than the crown it had been written to protect against. *Root below. What is knotted holds.*

A dockworker's daughter stood across the counter from her, maybe nine years old, gap-toothed, watching Edmund's hands with the ferocious attention of a child who has already been told twice that what she does is wrong.

"You don't have to whisper," Edmund told her. "That's the whole point now."

"But you're still quiet."

"I'm still quiet because I like it." She tied off the knot and held it up between them, letting it catch the lantern light. The charm turned inside the braid, a slow pulse like a sleeping heartbeat. "Quiet is a choice now. It used to be a cage. Learn the difference early and you'll save yourself some years."

The girl took the knot the way children take holy things—carefully, with both hands, slightly afraid it might object. "My mum says you were there. When the Regent fell. She says the sky over the Steppe went white as milk."

"Your mum exaggerates."

"She says you cried."

Edmund smiled, and found, to her mild surprise, that it didn't cost anything. "She's right about that one."

* *

The decree had come down in spring, written in the new king's careful hand—or rather, in the hand he was relearning to write, since sixty years of enchantment had left Aldous with the penmanship of a man perpetually startled awake. *No person shall be burned for the working of harmless craft.* Eleven words. Edmund had read them eleven times the morning they were posted, standing in the square where she had once watched a hedge-witch named Maren Cotter die when Edmund was nineteen and too frightened to look away.

Eleven words for five years of ash. It seemed obscene. It also seemed like rain after drought—insufficient, late, and still the only thing that could make anything grow.

The rebuilding had taken longer than the decree. The Glass Steppe itself had never fully recovered; travelers said the plain outside Vell still shimmered strangely at dusk, and that grass grew there in patterns too regular to be accidental, the way skin grows back over a burn. The Regent's harvest had reached deep into the ground before it collapsed. Some nights, Edmund dreamed of roots—white, fibrous, drinking light—and woke with her hands curled as if around a rope.

But the market had come back. That was the miracle nobody wrote songs about: not the fall of tyrants but the stubborn reappearance of cinnamon smoke and haggling voices and a girl buying a luck-knot with coins counted out one copper at a time.

* *

Her school—if you could call a converted chandlery with fourteen mismatched chairs a school—met three evenings a week. On Thursdays she taught the grammar.

Not spellcraft first. Her mother had always insisted the words came second. *You don't teach a child letters so she can sign her name, Edmund. You teach her letters so she can read what's already written on the world.* So the first hour of every Thursday was listening: students pressed their palms to the floorboards and learned to feel the building's old warmth, the memory of every fire that had ever burned in its hearth. They learned that wood remembers heat the way cloth remembers bodies. They learned that nothing in this city had ever truly forgotten anything—which was exactly why something had once tried to make forgetting into an industry.

Tonight the class was small. Rain kept half of Vell indoors. But the dockworker's daughter was there, front row, ink-stained notebook open, and beside her sat a former lamplighter whose gift was finding lost things, and a widow who could coax beans to sprout in dead soil, and a boy of sixteen who had walked forty miles from the coast because his grandmother had heard there was a place where a hedge-witch would tell him he wasn't damned.

He wasn't. None of them were. Edmund said so at the start of every class, plainly, the way you'd state the weather, because she remembered what it was to need the sentence spoken aloud and to have no one willing to say it.

"Now," she said, tying a practice-cord around her own wrist, "wind at the wrist."

"*Wind at the wrist*," fourteen voices answered, ragged, uncertain, alive.

"Root below."

"*Root below.*"

"And what is knotted—"

"Holds," said the boy from the coast, alone, too loud, and then blushed furiously when the others caught up a beat later.

"That's it exactly," Edmund said. "Holds. Write that down if you remember nothing else. Everything we do is just that sentence wearing different clothes."

After class she stood in the doorway a long moment, watching them scatter into the rain with their little knots and their little notebooks, and let herself feel the thing she still wasn't fully practiced at feeling: pride without fear underneath it. For five years, every student she might have imagined teaching had been a liability, a name on a list, a future entry in a ledger. Now they were just people, walking home wet and happy, carrying her mother's grammar in their mouths like seeds.

*What is knotted holds.* She hadn't known, when she was small, that the spell was also a promise. Or an instruction. Or a description of what she was slowly becoming.

* *

Sera was waiting at the stall when Edmund closed up, sitting on the counter with a paper of roasted chestnuts balanced on her knee and a book open in her lap—one of the archive's rescued volumes, its spine cracked, its margins full of her cramped annotations. Ink on her fingers. There was always ink on her fingers now; she'd stopped pretending otherwise months ago.

"You're late," Sera said, without looking up.

"The boy from the coast asked me what my mother looked like."

Sera did look up then. She had learned, over these months, when to stop talking and when Edmund needed her to. "What did you tell him?"

"That she smelled like rosemary and hot iron. That she hummed when she worked spells and swore when she worked everything else." Edmund began stacking her unsold charms into their box, hands busy so her voice wouldn't have to be. "He wanted to know her face. I told him I didn't carry it anymore."

"How did he take it?"

"He said—" Edmund paused. The chestnut paper crackled as Sera shifted. Rain ticked softly on the awning above them. "He said that must be like losing a window. You still know the sun's there. You just can't see through anymore."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then: "He's going to be better at this than you are."

"He absolutely is." And Edmund laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and the grief that lived permanently now behind her sternum shifted, the way a scar shifts when you stretch, painful and proof-of-life both.

The hole where her mother's face had been was still there. It would always be there. In the vault beneath the palace, Edmund had held those luminous archived memories in her hands—her mother laughing, her mother angry, her mother turning toward the camera of a remembered moment with her whole face open—and understood in one sickening instant that she could pour them back into herself, or she could spend them tearing the Regent apart from within, and the core would accept only one payment. She had spent them. She would spend them again. But some mornings she woke reaching for a face the way an amputee reaches for a missing hand, certain for one merciful half-second that it's there, before the truth arrives like cold water.

What remained was everything else. Rosemary and hot iron. The humming. The grammar. A voice she could still hear, though she could no longer see the mouth that made it. It was, she had decided, enough—but she had decided it honestly, which meant deciding it again most weeks.

Sera slid off the counter and came to stand beside her, shoulder to shoulder, looking out at the market the way Edmund knew she loved to look at it: as a cataloguer. Naming things. Keeping them.

"Lantern Row's fully relit," Sera said. "Third time's the charm, apparently."

"Don't say charm like that in front of my students."

"They've gone home."

"They linger."

"They linger," Sera conceded, and put a chestnut in Edmund's palm, still warm, split open like a small brown secret.

They ate them together in the doorway, watching the crowd. The fishmonger two stalls down had hung a wind-charm over his scales—an ugly, lopsided thing Edmund suspected one of her Thursday students had made—and it turned lazily in the evening air, catching light, protecting absolutely nothing, meaning absolutely everything.

* *

Later—much later, when the lanterns had burned down to embers and even the fishmonger had gone home—Edmund walked the Row alone, as she sometimes did, doing the rounds of a neighborhood she had once navigated by fear and now moved through by memory.

Here was the alley she had fled down the night the patrol nearly caught her, five years ago, luck-knots burning in her pocket like coals. The wall at its end had been rebuilt; a child had chalked a sun on it, lopsided and enormous, and no one had washed it off. Here was the corner where she had first seen Sera waiting for their date, holding a book against her chest like a shield, looking so earnestly terrified of being late that Edmund had almost laughed before she'd even said hello. Here, here, here—the whole street a palimpsest of who she had been, every layer still legible if she cared to read it.

She had spent five years believing that survival meant staying unreadable. Keep your sigil out of books. Keep your face out of memories. Keep your name off ledgers, lists, lips. Be a rumor to everyone, including yourself. She had gotten very good at it, and it had nearly killed her in the slowest possible way—not by the pyre, but by erosion, by the daily sanding-down of a self that no one, least of all Edmund, was permitted to see whole.

And then a book with her sigil in the margins. A girl with ink-stained fingers. A hollow thing on a throne wearing her childhood like a coat. And at the end of all of it, a choice between wholeness and truth that had turned out not to be a choice at all, because a self built on restored memories would have been a furnished house she merely lived in, while the self that chose—bleeding, grieving, choosing anyway—that one was hers down to the foundations.

Belonging was never something she had lost. That was the lesson, and it had taken a war to learn it. She had lost plenty—years, names, a face—but belonging had never been among them, because belonging was never something the Regent or the edict or the fear could take. It was something you built, plank by plank, knot by knot, in the tedious unglamorous work of showing up. Selling charms. Teaching Thursdays. Eating chestnuts in doorways. Staying, when staying was the hardest spell of all.

*What is knotted holds.* Not because the knot is strong. Because you keep tying it.

She reached the end of the Row, where the market opened onto the dark sweep of the Glass Steppe, and stopped. Far out, past the last lamps, the plain shimmered faintly under the rising moon—the scar tissue of the land, the place where the Regent had drunk deepest. Grass grew there now in strange, regular patterns. Travelers called it haunted. Children called it magic. Edmund called it healing, because she knew what healing looked like: not smooth, not silent, but patterned. Legible. Proof that something had been broken and had insisted on growing back anyway, differently, on its own terms.

Behind her, footsteps—unhurried, familiar, accompanied by the soft rustle of pages being closed. She didn't turn. She didn't need to.

"You'll catch cold," Sera said, coming to stand beside her at the edge of the lamplight.

"I run hot. Hedge-witch constitution."

"Mm." Sera slipped her hand into Edmund's, ink-stained fingers lacing through charm-calloused ones, and together they looked out at the dark plain and the bright market and the city between them, breathing.

Somewhere down the Row, the fishmonger's lopsided wind-charm turned in the breeze. Somewhere across the city, fourteen notebooks lay open on fourteen tables, full of a dead woman's living grammar. Somewhere in the palace, an old man was writing letters in a startled, careful hand, apologizing in ink to people he would never finish apologizing to. And somewhere in Edmund's chest, behind the scar, the hole where her mother's face had been held its shape—not empty, exactly. Shaped. The way a knot is shaped. The way a window frame is shaped, holding the outline of a sun it will never show, so that everyone who passes knows exactly where the light comes from.

"Thursday," Sera said. "The coast boy wants to learn the scrying verse. He asked me to ask you."

"Did he."

"He's afraid to ask you himself."

"Good," Edmund said. "Fear means he understands what he's asking for." She squeezed Sera's hand. "Tell him yes. Tell him all of them, yes. Every one who asks."

The moon cleared the last of the clouds and laid a road of silver across the Steppe, out toward the shimmering grass, out toward whatever grew next. Edmund stood in the doorway of the life she had built—with her mother's words on her tongue and her mother's absence at her heart and Sera's warm hand in hers—and felt the knot hold.

It would hold tomorrow, too. She would see to that herself.
