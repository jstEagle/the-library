## 2. A Map Pressed Into Unwilling Hands

The courier was still breathing when Wren reached him.

Barely. The breath came in shallow pulls, each one a small argument between the body and whatever had gutted it. Three arrows' worth of argument, by the look of the wounds—one through the meat of his left shoulder, one buried somewhere beneath the ribs on the right, and a third that had torn through the thigh and kept going, taking most of the man's blood with it into the cobblestones. The stones drank greedily. They always did. Nine years in the counting-house district had taught Wren exactly how much blood a street could swallow before it stopped looking like rain-wash and started looking like murder.

He knelt. He did not know why he knelt. That was the first lie he told himself that night—that there was no reason, that any decent person would have done the same, that it meant nothing, that he was nobody doing nothing.

The courier's eyes found him anyway.

They were gray eyes, the color of weather, and they were not confused the way dying men's eyes usually were. They were *aimed*. Whatever had brought this man bleeding to the door of the Ledger Guild of Impossible Trades at the hour when even the lamps grew tired, he had come here on purpose, and now he looked up at the first face that leaned over him with the particular desperation of someone who has run out of road and knows it.

"Guild," the man said. It cost him. Wren watched the word drag itself out of his throat like a hook from deep water. "You're... Guild."

"I'm no one," Wren said, and it was true, and it was the whole architecture of his life balanced on three words. "A clerk. You want the night steward—"

"You're *Guild*." The gray eyes did something terrible then: they focused. All the way focused, past the plain coat, past the ink stains on Wren's fingers, past the careful nothing that Wren Voss had spent nine years sewing over himself like a tarp over furniture. "I know what Guild looks like."

Wren's hands went cold. Not fear, exactly—or not only fear. It was the older feeling, the one with roots under the fear, the feeling of being *seen*, which for a swordspeaker was less an experience than a wound. There had been a time when being seen was the most dangerous thing that could happen to a man in this kingdom. There had been a time when men who could speak and make the world obey were hunted down the way hunters run wolves, not because the wolves had done anything, but because of what they might do, what they had done once, what the stories said about them. Wren had learned invisibility the way other men learned letters. He was very good at it. He had thought he was untouchable at it.

The courier disagreed.

"Help me up," the man said. "Stairs. Can't—" A wet sound interrupted him, and Wren understood with a lurch that it was laughter, or the ruin of laughter. "Nine years I've carried this thing. Nine years running. And I die on the doorstep of the one guild in the world that trades in impossible things. There's a joke in that. Someone should write it down."

"Someone will," Wren heard himself say. "It's what we do."

*Fool,* he thought. *Talkative fool.* But the courier's ruined mouth curved, and something in the set of his shoulders eased by a degree, as if a joke—even a bad one, even a dying one—was a currency the man had been short of for nine years and recognized instantly when offered.

Wren got his hands under the courier's arms and lifted. The man weighed less than he should have; the blood loss had already begun the work of unmaking him, hollowing him out the way a tide hollows a dune. His boots scraped the step. His head lolled against Wren's shoulder, and Wren felt the heat coming off him—not fever-heat, but the strange furnace-heat of a body burning its last fuel all at once, spending everything because there would be no tomorrow in which to be frugal.

Inside, the counting-house corridor stretched away in its familiar gloom, all dark oak and darker ledgers, the smell of ink and tallow and old paper pressed flat by time. Wren had walked this corridor ten thousand times without anyone once turning to look at him twice. He laid the courier down on the floor of the entry alcove, where the shadow was deepest, and knelt again, and this time he knew why he knelt, and he hated knowing.

"Don't die here," he said quietly. "Whatever you're carrying—the Guild will weigh it, price it, file it. If you die on our stone, it becomes ours by salvage law, and you don't want that. Believe me. I've read the ledgers."

"The Guild can't have it." The words came slow, spaced out like coins counted by a miser who knows the purse is nearly empty. "That's why... here. Why I ran here. No one watches a counting-house door. Everyone thinks..." Another pull of breath, thinner than the last. "Everyone thinks the impossible trades are a story."

"They are a story," Wren said. "I balance them every night. Bottled storms. Widow's griefs. Years of a life traded for a single honest answer. Tonight it was a storm for grief, if you must know. Neither party was satisfied. That's how you know the trade was fair."

Something flickered in the gray eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or the memory of recognition—a face seen once, long ago, in a crowd, in a court, in a place where a young man had stood and spoken and kings had listened.

"You were there," the courier said. "At the Ninefold Court. Before."

Wren went very still.

There is a specific quality to terror that arrives slowly, that takes its time, that walks up your spine stair by stair instead of leaping. This was that. Not the flash-fear of a blade at the throat. The slow kind. The kind that gives you time to understand exactly how much trouble you are in, itemized, with interest.

"I'm a clerk," Wren said.

"You were a *voice*." The courier's hand moved—an inch, no more, a dead man's gesture—and caught the front of Wren's coat and held it, held *him*, with the grip of someone hauling a rope that means his life. "I saw you. I was seventeen. I carried messages in the outer court. You stood below the throne steps and you spoke, and the bells rang wrong afterward, they rang *nine and one*, and everyone pretended not to notice, and I never forgot. Nobody forgets. That's why they killed you all."

"Nobody killed anybody," Wren said. "There was a purge of rumors. Rumors are cheap. Voices are—"

"Gone." The courier said it flatly, and it landed like a hammer on a nail. "All gone but one. And the one went quiet and hid among debts." The gray eyes were dimming now, the lamp behind them turned low, but they held Wren's face with the last of their light. "Smart. Gods, it was smart. Who searches a ledger for a legend?"

*Everyone,* Wren thought. *Eventually everyone. That's the problem with hiding in a story. Stories get told.*

Then the courier's hand left his coat and went to his own chest, to the satchel strap that crossed it, and began—with the terrible patience of the dying—to work the buckle.

* * *

The satchel was oiled leather, scarred, patched twice, and heavier than its size explained. Wren had seen a thousand satchels cross the counting-house floor. Couriers brought them constantly: contracts, writs, sealed grievances, the ordinary paper traffic of a kingdom that ran on obligations the way a mill runs on water. This one was different. He knew it before the buckle gave. He knew it by the way the courier handled it—not like cargo, but like a child. Like something alive.

The flap opened. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen gone soft with age and handling, lay a shape that Wren's fingers understood before his mind did.

It was warm.

Not body-warm. Not animal-warm. It was warm the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies—warm with a heat that remembered being much greater, warm with a patience measured in centuries. Through the linen he felt fine scales, small as fingernails, overlapping in the ancient pattern that no craftsman had ever successfully counterfeited, because the pattern wasn't a design. It was a language. It was the oldest language there was, older than speech, older than oath, and every scale was a letter of it.

Dragon-skin.

Wren unwrapped it with hands that shook, and hated that they shook, and could not stop them.

It was a map. Of course it was a map—what else does a dragon-skin become in human hands but a map, since dragons do not draw, they *remember*, and a skin that remembers becomes a picture of the remembering. By the thin light of the alcove lamp Wren made out the lines: the capital, rendered not in cartographer's squares but in flowing curves, as if the city had been poured rather than built. The Ninefold Court at its heart, drawn as nine concentric rings around a central mark. And beneath the Court—this was the part that made the cold climb fully into Wren's chest and sit down there—beneath the Court, in strokes of faded gold that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at them, the map showed what lay underneath. Roots. Galleries. A descent drawn in descending spirals, ring within ring within ring, going down and down toward a single point marked at the very bottom of the skin, at the very bottom of the world the map admitted to.

And at that point, written in a script that moved—actually moved, crawling and reforming like insects disturbed under bark—two words.

Wren could not read them. The shifting script was dragon-script, the tongue of scales, and though some part of him—the part he kept locked, the part that had once spoken and been obeyed—leaned toward those two words with an ache like hunger, the words themselves stayed closed to him. Sealed. He had the sudden, vertiginous sense that they were sealed *for* him specifically, that the map knew what he was and was waiting, the way a lock waits for a key.

"There's a name," the courier whispered.

Wren looked up. The man's face had gone the color of candle-tallow. The blood beneath him had stopped spreading, which was worse than if it had kept going; it meant there was not enough left to spread.

"The king's name," the courier said. "His *true* name. Struck from every record nine years ago. Burned from the rolls. Chiseled off the foundation stones. Mothers were paid to forget it." Each phrase cost him visibly, a coin from a dwindling purse, and he spent them anyway, deliberately, like a man buying something more important than more minutes. "But a name can't be struck from everywhere. Names live in things. In stone. In skin." His failing hand twitched toward the map. "In her skin. She remembers him. She remembers *everything*. That's why they chained her. That's why they take her fire."

"*Her*—" Wren started, and stopped, because the shape of the sentence assembled itself in his mind all at once, horribly complete. The last dragon. The fire taken. The king who never aged. The bells that rang nine times, always nine times, year after year, while the king on the coinage stayed young as the day he was crowned. Wren had balanced the accounts of that court for nine years without ever letting himself add the columns. Now the columns added themselves, and the sum was monstrous.

The king was dead. Had been dead for nine years. Something else wore the crown—something sustained, kept animate, kept *king-shaped*, by fire stolen from the last living dragon, chained beneath the Court and bled of her flame one measure at a time, a wick trimmed nightly to keep a corpse lit.

"That's not possible," Wren said aloud, because saying it aloud was the only way to keep it from being true. "The Court would—someone would have—"

"Someone did." The ghost of the smile again. "Many someones. They're all dead, clerk. Or scattered. Or bought. I carried warnings for six years. Then I carried proof. Then I just carried this, and ran, and every friend who helped me is a grave." The gray eyes were nearly out now. "Veyre runs it. The Chancellor. She holds the binding. She holds the *name*—that's the leash. As long as she keeps the king's true name locked away, the hollow thing stays bound to her will, and the Court stays hers, and the dragon stays a lamp."

Wren sat back on his heels. His hands, still resting on the warm skin of the map, trembled against it, and the trembling felt almost like the skin answering him, almost like a conversation beginning in a language he had sworn off forever.

"Why me?" he asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Why bring this to a clerk? There are lords. Rebels. Armies in the east that hate the Court. Any of them—"

"Because armies can't read it." The courier's voice was failing by syllables now. "Because lords will sell it. Because rebels will use it wrong and get ten thousand people killed and the dragon will still be chained and the corpse will still be king." A pause, a breath gathered like a drowning man gathering water. "Because it needs a voice. The unbinding needs a swordspeaker's true voice at the root-stone, and there's exactly one left in the world, and he's kneeling in front of me pretending to be a clerk."

The silence that followed lasted four heartbeats. Wren counted them, because counting was what he did instead of feeling, and in those four heartbeats he watched his entire constructed life—the plain coats, the ink-stained fingers, the careful nothing, the nine years of practiced invisibility—stand in his mind's doorway and begin, quietly, to burn.

"I can't," he said.

"You can." The courier's hand rose again, shaking, and pressed—not the map this time, but Wren's wrist, right over the pulse, right over the old scar that circled it like a shackle, the oath-scar, the binding-mark of the Ledger Guild that every swordspeaker wore who had sworn his voice into the Guild's keeping. The courier's thumb found the scar and rested on it. "Oath-bound. Of course. They always collect the dangerous ones somehow." His eyes, dim as coals now, held Wren's. "Then hear me. I'm not asking you to break it tonight. I'm asking you to carry. Carrying isn't speaking. Carry it until you find the reason. There's always a reason. There was one for me."

"What was yours?"

The question came out of Wren before he could stop it, and the answer came back with the courier's last real strength behind it:

"Aldous."

One word. One name, gasped like a secret pried from a vault, and Wren felt the floor of the world tilt beneath him, because he knew that name—not as history, not as rumor, but as absence. Every record in the kingdom had a hole in it shaped like that name. The ninth bell rang for a king whose name no one said. And here was a dying man spending his final coin to say it, to press it into Wren's hands along with the skin that remembered it, to make Wren—clerk, nobody, silent thing—the keeper of the one fact the entire Court had been engineered to erase.

*Aldous.*

The courier's breath stuttered. Slowed. Wren watched the light go out of the gray eyes not suddenly but gradually, the way a lamp goes out when the oil runs low, guttering, steadying, guttering, and then—

Gone.

His hand slid from Wren's wrist. The warmth left him in a long final exhale, and the street outside, which had been holding its breath, resumed its noise, indifferent, endless.

Wren knelt beside a dead man in the entry alcove of the Ledger Guild of Impossible Trades, holding a dragon-skin map and a name that could unmake a kingdom, and he had never in his life felt more visible.

* * *

He had perhaps forty minutes. He knew this the way he knew the weight of paper and the temper of ink—by long practice, by the accumulated instinct of nine years of nights. Forty minutes was the interval between a death noticed and a death reported, between a body on the stone and enforcers on the stairs. The Guild did not tolerate messes on its doorstep. Messes attracted questions, and questions were the one commodity the Guild never traded in.

He should have burned the map. He knew that too. He stood in the alcove with the courier's cooling body and the warm skin in his hands and he conducted the argument with himself that he had conducted every night for nine years, the quiet ledger-debate between survival and obligation, and for nine years survival had won every column. Survival was rational. Survival was arithmetic. The dead could not be helped by joining them.

But the skin was warm. That was the unreasonable thing. That was the column that refused to balance. He kept touching it without deciding to touch it, and each time, the faint remembered heat of it pressed against his palms like a hand pressing back, like something patient beyond all human patience waiting in the dark under the world for someone—anyone—to come down the long spiral stairs and turn the key.

*Carry it until you find the reason,* the courier had said. *There's always a reason.*

Wren wrapped the map in its linen, tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where its warmth sat against his heartbeat like a second pulse. He moved the courier's body—arranged it, straightened the limbs, folded the hands, the small dignities that cost nothing and meant everything—and composed in his mind the report he would give: found at the door, wounds consistent with arrows, no effects recovered, likely a smuggler fallen out with his partners, regrettable, filed, closed. He had lied to the Guild before, in small denominations. Tonight he would lie in larger ones. The exchange rate worried him.

At thirty-one minutes, the knock came.

Two enforcers. Wren knew both by sight and disliked both by principle: Hask, broad and humorless, with a chain-sigil stamped on his badge and a talent for finding the seam in any story; and Merrow, younger, sharper-eyed, the kind of clever that made clerks nervous because cleverness in the Guild was never idle—it was always shopping.

"Clerk Voss." Hask's voice filled the alcove the way cold fills a room. "Body on Guild stone. Yours was the nearest watch. Walk us through it."

Wren walked them through it. Found at the door shortly after the eleventh hour. No sounds of struggle—he had been balancing the storm-for-grief trade, he said, and had heard only the fall. Arrows, three, professional spacing. No satchel, no pouch, no seal, no token—nothing to name him. A runner, probably. Smugglers ran messages between the river gangs and the eastern fences; the routes passed close to the counting-house district; disputes were settled with arrows; bodies were dumped wherever the stones were convenient. Regrettable. Filed. Closed.

Merrow listened with her chin down and her eyes up, which was her way, and which Wren had learned to dread, because it meant she was reading him and not his words.

"Nothing on him at all," Merrow said. "No token. No seal. No coin."

"Stripped before dumping. Standard."

"By the men who shot him." Her eyes narrowed pleasantly. "Archer crews don't strip bodies. It's beneath them. They leave the evidence; evidence is a message. So either this man stripped himself—" she paused, and the pause had teeth— "or someone stripped him *after*, and that someone was standing right here."

Wren kept his face the exact temperature of the room. This, too, was craft. Nine years of craft. "Or he was robbed between the alley and the door. The district is full of night-pickers. You know the incidence reports better than I do; I only file them."

Hask crouched by the body. He was thorough in the manner of a man who enjoys thoroughness the way other men enjoy drink. He checked the boots. He checked the belt-line. He checked the collar, the seams, the lining of the coat, running his fingers along every stitch with the patience of a customs officer. And then he straightened, and looked at Wren, and said the thing that Wren had been dreading since the moment the courier's gray eyes had first focused on him across the bloody stones.

"He came here," Hask said. "Dead men choose doors. Out of every door in this district—taverns, temples, the watch-house, a hundred private stoops—he chose the Guild of Impossible Trades. Dying men don't wander, Clerk Voss. They aim. So the question isn't what he carried." Hask's gaze settled on Wren's chest, on the plain coat, on the nothing-shaped space where a satchel's outline would be if a satchel had recently been pressed against it. "The question is who he came to see."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, in the counting-hall, a page turned; the Guild never entirely slept; the ledgers were always listening. Wren felt the map's warmth against his sternum like a coal wrapped in wool, and he felt the oath-scar circling his wrist, and he understood with perfect clarity that he was standing at the edge of the rest of his life, and that the next three seconds would decide which side of it he fell.

"I couldn't say," Wren said. "Perhaps he was mad. Pain does that. Perhaps he wanted to die owing a debt to somebody, and we're the only ones who keep accounts." He let the smallest tired smile touch his mouth. "People do stranger things at our door. Last month a widow tried to sell us her dreams. We declined. She cried. It was very sad. Death at our threshold is sad, Enforcer Hask, but it is not mysterious. Take the body. File the report. I'll enter the incident in the night ledger—unclaimed loss, zero credit, zero debit—and we can all get back to work."

Merrow's eyes lingered on him a moment longer. Reading. Always reading. Then she nodded, slowly, the nod of a woman filing something away in a ledger of her own, one that no one else could audit.

"For now," she said.

They took the body. Their footsteps receded up the corridor, unhurried, confident, the footsteps of people who believed they had gotten the whole truth, and Wren stood alone in the alcove with the lamp burning low and the map warm against his chest and the name sitting in his mouth like a coal he could neither swallow nor spit out.

*Aldous.*

He said it once, silently, lips barely moving, no breath behind it, no power, just the shape of it—and the map against his chest warmed by a single degree, so slight he might have imagined it, so deliberate he knew he hadn't.

Somewhere far beneath the Ninefold Court, in the deepest gallery of the deepest ring, something chained and ancient had felt a swordspeaker's lips move around a king's true name for the first time in nine years.

And had begun, very slowly, to hope.

Wren snuffed the lamp and stood in the dark, holding the impossible against his heart, listening to the Guild sleep around him—and in the morning, he knew, there would be a second knock, and it would not be enforcers asking about a body. It would be the Court. It would be the Chancellor's people. Because Merrow had filed something away, and files in the Ledger Guild had a way of traveling, and somewhere across the sleeping city a woman named Sera Quill was being handed a warrant with a description on it that matched, exactly, a clerk who lied well.

He had until dawn. Perhaps less. Wren Voss, invisible for nine years, picked up the map and began, for the first time since the purge, to plan how to run.
