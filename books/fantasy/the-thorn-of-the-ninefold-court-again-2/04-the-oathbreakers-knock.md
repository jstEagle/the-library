## 4. The Oathbreakers' Knock

The summons came at the hour when candles gutter and honest people sleep, which was how Sera Quill knew it would be ugly.

She had been an inquisitor of the Ninefold Court for eleven years, and in that time she had learned to read orders the way sailors read weather. A polite summons—*attend the Chancery at your convenience*—meant paperwork. A formal writ meant a trial. But an order delivered by a breathless clerk at the third bell of morning, on cheap paper with no seal but the Chancellor's private mark pressed in wax still warm enough to smear, meant that whatever waited for her would never survive daylight or record.

The clerk had not met her eyes. They never did, when the orders were like this one.

Now she sat in the back of a hired cab, watching the Guild district slide past her window, and read the order for the fourth time.

*Retrieve the map. The holder is to be silenced. No report is to be filed until the matter is closed.*

*Silenced.* Not killed. Never *killed*, not in writing. The Court had grown squeamish about its vocabulary even as it grew careless about everything else. Eleven years ago, when Sera had first pinned the inquisitor's badge to her coat, the old hunters had spoken plainly: *kill*, *burn*, *bury*. Now they said *silenced*, *closed*, *resolved*, as if the dead were merely overdue accounts.

She folded the order away and looked at her own hands. They were steady. They were always steady; that was the thing people misunderstood about oathbreakers' work. It was not done by men and women who trembled. It was done by the calm ones.

The cab turned onto Ledger Street, where the counting-houses stood shoulder to shoulder like fat merchants at a funeral, each trying to look more respectable than the last. The Guild of Impossible Trades kept its offices at the street's end, behind a door of black oak carved with scales whose pans never quite balanced—a joke, Sera suspected, that only guildsmen found funny.

Somewhere in one of those buildings was a courier's map. Somewhere in one of them was a courier who no longer needed it, because Sera's partner had been sent ahead of her two nights ago, and partners did not come back from that kind of errand unless they chose to run.

Doran had not run. Doran had never run from anything in his life, which was precisely why he was dead, though she would not learn that for certain until later. What she knew now was the silence from his end of the network—two days of nothing where there should have been coded confirmation—and the Chancellor's sudden appetite for a dragon-skin map that no one in the Court's archives had known existed forty-eight hours ago.

Someone had talked. Someone always talked. And the talking had reached Veyre before it reached the throne, which told Sera everything she needed to know about where this order truly originated.

*No report until the matter is closed.*

She got out at the corner, paid the driver too much, and walked the last stretch alone.

* * *

The counting-house district slept badly, the way rich places do—shutters drawn tight, lamps burning in windows out of habit rather than need, watchmen posted at corners where nothing had happened in thirty years. Sera moved through it the way water moves through cloth, without disturbing anything, because that was the craft of it. Anyone could kick down a door. The work was in arriving at a man's bedside so quietly that he woke believing he had dreamed you.

But tonight the craft felt heavy in her hands, like a tool left out in the rain.

She stopped across the street from the Guild's residence wing—a narrow building squeezed between two grander ones, its windows dark except for one on the third floor, where a candle burned behind horn panes. A silhouette crossed the light once. Tall. Thin. Moving slowly, the way people move when they are pacing out their worry.

The holder of the map.

Her briefing had been thin, deliberately thin. A Guild scrivener, mid-thirties, unremarkable. Wren Voss. Nine years in the counting-house, no debts of note, no vices of note, no friends of note. The kind of file that made her itch, because files like that were never empty—they were *cleaned*. Someone had gone through this man's history and sanded off every rough edge until what remained was smooth enough to pass through the Court's attention without catching.

And there was one more thing in the file, the thing that had made her ask for this assignment herself when she might have refused it. One line, buried in the ninth year of his record:

*Suspected remnant. Unconfirmed.*

Remnant. That was what the Court called them now, when it called them anything at all—the last few swordspeakers, scattered after the purges, hunted quiet one by one over the years until the word itself had become half-forgotten. Sera had been a girl when the swordspeakers still walked openly, when a woman could stand in the Court square and speak a debt forgiven in a voice that made the very stones remember it. She had wanted, desperately and foolishly, to be one of them. She had practiced the old cadences into a hairbrush until her sister laughed at her. She had wept actual tears the day she learned her voice carried no such weight—that the gift had passed her by entirely.

Then the purge came, and the gift became a death sentence, and Sera had done what practical people do: she had chosen the winning side, taken the badge, and told herself that hunting oathbreakers was honorable work, that most of them deserved it, that the ones who didn't were the cost of order.

Eleven years of telling herself that. And every year the telling took a little longer.

She crossed the street.

* * *

The residence door was locked, but locks were conversation, and Sera was fluent. She was through it in less time than it takes to draw breath, and then she stood in the dim entryway listening to the building breathe around her.

Old buildings have voices. This one spoke of beeswax and cold stone and the particular hush of rooms where money is counted—money makes its own silence, a thick, woolly quiet that presses against the ears. She climbed the stairs avoiding the third step and the seventh, which complained, and emerged onto the third-floor landing with her knife already loose in its sheath and her breathing slowed to the rhythm of sleep.

One door at the corridor's end. Candlelight under it. And beneath the light, faint but unmistakable to anyone trained as she had been trained, the smell of blood.

Not fresh. Hours old, maybe more, soaked into something and drying. Her stomach tightened. Doran's blood? Had he reached this room at all, or had he died in some alley with his message undelivered?

*Retrieve the map,* the order said. *Silence the holder.*

She put her hand to the door.

And stopped.

It was not hesitation—not exactly. Hesitation was doubt, and Sera Quill did not permit herself doubt during a working; doubt got inquisitors killed. What stopped her was something else, something she had felt only three times in eleven years, and each time it had preceded a night she did not care to remember.

An oath-scar leaves a residue. Not magic—she had no talent for reading magic—but a texture, a wrongness in the air of a place where a binding has been laid on flesh. Hunters learned to sense it the way sailors learn to smell a coming storm: not knowledge, exactly, but a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper coins at the back of the throat.

This room tasted of copper.

Not the blood. The blood was old and ordinary. This was older and stranger—a binding wound through the very walls of the man's life, sunk so deep into him that it had colored the air of every room he'd slept in for nine years. She had felt scars like this twice before. Once on a smuggler in the fen country who had wept when she found him, begging her to understand that he'd sworn what he swore to protect his daughter. Once on a woman in the hill towns who had spat in Sera's face and died reaching for a locket.

Both times, the scar had been small. A single broken promise, a minor oath to a minor power.

What pressed against her senses now was not small. This was a great chain of an oath, links upon links upon links, the kind of binding laid only by institutions—by guilds, by courts, by powers that bound their people the way farmers bind livestock. Nine years deep, at least. Maybe more.

A remnant. The file had said *unconfirmed*. The file was wrong, or lying, or both.

Sera stood with her hand on the door of Wren Voss's room, and behind that door a candle burned and a man paced, and between her hand and that man lay nine years of silence and a binding strong enough to taste.

*He's one of them,* she thought. *One of the last. Maybe the last.*

The thought arrived with no loyalty attached to either side of her life. It simply arrived, the way truth does, indifferent to what it costs.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape: the Court had spent a decade making certain the swordspeakers were extinct, and the Chancellor—who signed her orders, who held her warrant, who knew things about the throne that Sera was only beginning to guess at—had sent her to extinguish the final ember personally, quietly, with no report filed. Which meant Veyre feared him. Which meant Veyre had reason to fear him. Which meant the dying courier and the dragon-skin map and this tired scrivener with his candlelit pacing were all threads of a knot far larger than any order written in smearing wax.

Or it meant nothing at all, and she was an inquisitor standing at a door doing what inquisitors do, and the only thing wrong with the world was her own softness, her own girlish grief for a dream of speaking words that moved stones.

*Duty,* she told herself. *You are the law's hand. If the hand refuses to close, what is the arm for?*

Her hand did not close.

* * *

She tried the handle instead. Unlocked. Of course unlocked—a man hiding behind an ordinary life keeps no bolts, because bolts are a confession.

The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound, oiled recently and well, and Sera stepped into the room with her knife low and her weight on the balls of her feet.

He stood at the window with his back to her, and for a moment she simply looked at him. He was taller than the file suggested, thinner too, with the stooped shoulders of a man accustomed to making himself smaller. His coat hung on him like something borrowed. On the desk beside him lay a leather case, open, holding a sheet of material that caught the candlelight and threw it back in colors Sera had no names for—deep greens shot through with gold, shifting as she watched, like light moving under water.

Dragon-skin. She had seen it once, as evidence, in a case closed before her time. There was very little of it left in the world.

"You're standing in my doorway," the man said, without turning, "and you haven't knocked."

His voice was quiet. Ordinary. The voice of a man who had spent years training himself to be unremarkable, worn smooth as river stone. But underneath it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, Sera felt something else—a depth, a resonance, the ghost of a register that could make stones remember debts.

"I don't knock," she said, "when I'm working."

"Ah." He still did not turn. "Working. Is that what they call it now?"

"What would you call it?"

"A trade." He turned then, and she saw his face properly for the first time: tired, watchful, younger than his eyes. "Everything in this city is a trade. You've come to collect on something. So did another courier, earlier tonight." He glanced toward the desk, toward the open case, and Sera followed his glance and saw what she had missed—the dark stain on the floorboards beside it, carefully scrubbed but not carefully enough, and a folded cloak that had been someone's, recently, and would be no one's again.

Doran had made it here. Doran had died here, or near here, and this quiet man had held him while he went.

Something in Sera's chest shifted, some load-bearing certainty giving way.

"The man who brought you that," she said, nodding at the map. "Did he speak? Before the end?"

Wren Voss studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to surprise him; his eyes narrowed slightly, recalculating.

"He asked me for water," he said. "Then he asked me to remember a name. Then he asked me to burn the map, and I told him I would, and we both knew I was lying, because a man doesn't crawl across half the city bleeding out to deliver a thing he wants burned."

"What name?" she asked—and hated herself for asking, because it was the question the order forbade her to want answered.

He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile—grief and irony and nine years of silence all pressing up beneath it at once.

"You know I can't tell you that."

"And you know why I'm here."

"Yes," Wren Voss said. "I think I've known since the courier knocked. I've been standing at this window deciding whether to run." He looked down at his own hands, flexed them, as if testing their honesty. "I decided running never worked for anyone worth the trouble."

Sera's knife stayed low. Her duty hung above her like a blade of its own, and she stood in the narrow space between them, feeling the oath-scar's copper taste thickening in her throat, feeling the old girlhood longing rise in her like floodwater against a door.

*Silence the holder. No report until the matter is closed.*

"Give me the map," she said quietly. "Walk out of the city tonight, and by the time anyone asks, you'll be a rumor I couldn't confirm. That's the trade I'm offering. Everything in this city is a trade—isn't that what you said?"

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I do what I was sent to do." Her voice did not waver. Eleven years had bought her that much, at least. "I'd rather not. But I will."

He looked at her then, fully, and she understood that he was weighing her—not her skill, which he could presumably feel in the set of her shoulders, but her truth. Whether the mercy in her offer was real, or bait.

"You hesitated at my door," he said. "Before you opened it. I heard your breathing change."

"Inquisitors breathe."

"Not like that. Not like someone arguing with themselves." He stepped aside from the window, and moonlight fell across the open case, across the impossible colors of the dragon-skin. "Take it, then. If that's your answer. Take it and go, and may you carry it lighter than I would."

And Sera Quill, who had closed a hundred doors on desperate men, who had been the law's hand for eleven unblinking years, reached toward the map—

—and froze, because somewhere below them, in the sleeping bones of the counting-house, a second door opened softly, and boots that were not hers began to climb the stairs.

Wren heard it too. His face went pale and still.

"You came alone," he whispered.

"I came alone," Sera breathed back, hand finding her knife, eyes on the door. "Which means whoever that is—"
