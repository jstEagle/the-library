## 5. Nine Years of Silence

The salt road out of Corvess was a gray thread stitched through gray country, and Wren walked it with the map burning against his chest like a coal sewn into his coat.

He had left the counting-house before dawn, before the ledgers were unlocked, before anyone could ask why the quiet clerk of the Impossible Trades was leaving through the window instead of the door. He had taken only what he could carry and what he could not leave: three days' bread, a knife he had never once needed to use as a knife, and the dragon-skin map that the dying courier had pressed into his hands with fingers gone cold as river stones.

*Find it,* the courier had said—or had meant to say, or had died trying to say. The name had come out broken, half-swallowed by blood. But the map did not need words.

It lay now against his sternum, warm where skin touched skin, and when he closed his eyes he could feel it *reading him*—the way an open ledger reads a debtor, patiently, without judgment, adding up everything he owed. Dragon-skin, Brannoc would have called it, if Brannoc had been there. Wren didn't know Brannoc yet. He knew nothing yet except that the map wanted to go somewhere, and that every mile he walked toward wherever it wanted made the ache in his chest a little worse, or a little better—he hadn't decided which.

Behind him, Corvess smoked gently against the pale morning, a city of chimneys and debts. Somewhere in its heart, Guild enforcers were asking questions about a dead man at a clerk's door. Questions Wren could not answer safely. Questions whose answers would get him killed, or worse—*recognized.*

Nine years, he thought. Nine years of being no one, and it had lasted four hours past sunrise.

* * *

By midday the road climbed into hills where the pines grew crooked from wind, and Wren allowed himself the first rest he'd permitted since the courier's body went cold on his doorstep. He sat beneath a leaning pine, ate half his bread, and tried not to look at the map.

He looked at the map.

Unrolled across his knees it was beautiful in the way that terrible things are beautiful: dragon-hide tanned to a soft bronze, veined with gold where the script ran, and the script *moved*. He'd watched letters crawl like insects over each other, rearranging themselves into place names that did not exist on any honest chart—the Court of Ninefold Bells rendered not as the capital above but as the roots below, the deep galleries under the throne, a chamber marked with a single character that shifted between two shapes: *crown*, and *wound*.

And always, at the center, the name.

Not the king's name. Not the name sung from nine bell-towers every festival day, the name stamped on coin and seal and proclamation. A different name. An older one. The courier had gasped it with his last breath, and Wren had felt it land in him like a hook set behind the breastbone.

He knew the name because he had heard it once before, nine years ago, in a voice that had been screaming.

Wren folded the map away before it could read more of him than it already had.

*Trade it,* he told himself, the way he'd been telling himself since the first mile. *That's all this is. You're a man of the Ledger. Everything has a price. Find the buyer, sell the secret, buy your freedom. Walk away clean.* It was a good plan. It was the kind of plan the old Wren—the real Wren, the swordspeaker Wren—would have laughed at, but the old Wren was dead along with everyone else who'd ever heard his voice.

Except the lie had begun to develop cracks, and through the cracks he kept seeing the courier's face. Not the blood. The *trust*. A dying man had crossed a burning quarter of Corvess, pursued by things Wren didn't want to imagine, to put that map into *his* hands specifically. Out of everyone in a city of ninety thousand, the courier had known exactly whose door to die at.

Which meant someone still remembered what Wren Voss was.

Which meant the hiding was already over, whether he liked it or not.

* * *

He slept badly and dreamed of fire, which was normal. What was not normal—and what woke him sweating in the blue hour before dawn, hand closed around the hilt of a knife he never used—was that in the dream, the fire spoke.

In swordspeech, everything spoken is true, and everything true can be spoken. Fire, commanded properly, does not burn what it loves. That was the first lesson. The second lesson was that fire remembers what you make it do.

"Get up," Wren said aloud, to himself, in his ordinary cracked morning voice, and got up.

The hills gave way to moorland, and the moorland to a valley where an old shrine stood roofless by the road—a roadside chapel to some minor saint of travelers, its walls furred with moss, its altar long since looted. There was a dry corner beneath the fallen lintel where the wind couldn't reach, and Wren sat there through the afternoon because his legs had decided for him, and because the map had grown warmer the farther he came from the Guild, and warmth was a thing he distrusted.

It was there, in the ruined shrine, with rain beginning to tick against the stones, that the memory finally caught him. He had outrun it for nine years by never stopping, never sleeping twice in the same bed, keeping his hands full of other people's arithmetic. But the rain shut the world down to a small gray room, and his hands were empty, and the memory came in the way debts come: quietly, then all at once.

* * *

The Hall of Voices had stood on the cliffs above Tarnholt, white stone and sea wind, and it had held forty-one swordspeakers the winter Wren turned nineteen.

He could still recite their names if he let himself, so he didn't let himself, except that night in the shrine he let himself. Master Aldous Vane, who had taught him that a swordspeaker's silence was not cowardice but discipline—that the voice was a drawn blade and you did not walk around unsheathed. Old Maren, who could speak a storm calm and did it twice a year for the fishing fleet, gratis, because some trades were not trades. The twins, Ferro and Ferin, who finished each other's sentences and once, drunk, had spoken a bridge back into existence after the flood took it, laughing the whole time. And Kestrel. Kestrel, who was twenty-two and could make fire sit up and beg, and who had kissed Wren on the last warm evening of that last autumn and told him that when she was Grandmaster she was going to outlaw war outright, by decree of voice, and see who dared argue.

Forty-one names. He recited them all, lying in the dark of the ruined shrine, the way he did perhaps twice a year, a debt collector calling on accounts that could never be paid.

They had died in one night.

It had not even been a battle. That was the part that broke something in him and had never entirely mended. Battles had shape; battles could be fought. This had been *administrative*. The Guild of Impossible Trades—which had sheltered the swordspeakers for six hundred years, which kept its oaths carved in iron above its doors—had simply opened its ledgers one night and read out the cancellation. Every bond, every protection, every debt of honor owed to the Hall of Voices: struck. Struck in a hand Wren had known instantly, because he'd spent ten years learning ledger-script under it.

His master's hand. Aldous Vane's hand.

Later—much later, too late—he had understood that the entries weren't a betrayal written in haste. They were neat. Careful. The work of a man doing a terrible thing *correctly*, which was somehow worse. And Wren had spent nine years telling himself a story about it: that his master had been coerced, that the Guild's high factors had held something over him, that the good man Wren had loved was buried somewhere under the ink. He had built that story brick by brick because the alternative was unbearable.

But the courier's map had changed the arithmetic. Because now Wren knew what the erased entries had been hiding—not just the purge itself but the *reason* for it, the great secret sold up-chain until it reached whatever power wore the king's face. The swordspeakers had been killed not because they were dangerous but because they were *dangerous to a lie*. A voice that speaks only truth cannot be permitted near a throne built on falsehood. Forty-one of them, and the Court had bought the Guild's silence with whatever it was that chancellors have to offer, and the Guild had sold, and Aldous Vane had written the cancellations in his careful hand.

And Wren—who had been away that night, sent on a routine trade to the coast, the only apprentice unaccounted for—had come home to ash and forty-one graves and had done the only thing left that made sense. He had knelt in the ruins of the Hall of Voices, alone, with no witness but gulls and God, and sworn an oath in the oldest form of swordspeech, the binding kind, the kind that takes:

*I will not speak again. Not to command, not to compel, not to shape. I will keep this voice locked until the debt is paid or I am dust.*

Oaths in swordspeech are true the moment they are spoken, and they stay true. His voice had gone silent in the way that mattered—not mute, never mute, but *bound*, caged, a blade sealed in its scabbard with the scabbard welded shut. He could talk like any man. He could haggle over storm-bottles and widow's griefs and never once touch the power underneath. For nine years it had worked. For nine years he had believed that silence was safety, that invisibility was craft, that a man who made himself small enough could slip through history without being noticed by it.

Lying in the shrine, listening to the rain, Wren finally admitted what he had known since the courier fell against his door:

The oath wasn't discipline. It had never been discipline. It was fear wearing discipline's clothes. He had hidden not because the world was dangerous—though it was—but because if he never spoke, he could never fail them again. Never be the one swordspeaker who lived. Never have to explain, in that voice, why he'd been the one sent away that night, the one spared, the one whose survival felt less like luck and more like an entry someone had failed to cancel.

The rain ticked. The map lay warm against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

*You're going to follow it,* he thought. *Say it honestly, at least. Say it the way you'd enter it in a ledger.*

So he did, silently, in the careful columns of his own mind.

*Item: one map, dragon-skin, stolen from a dead man's hands by sentiment.*
*Item: one journey, destination the Court of Ninefold Bells, purpose—*

Purpose. He tried to write *trade*. Tried to write *sale of secret, buyer unknown, profit: freedom*. The words wouldn't hold their shape. They crawled apart the way the map's script crawled, rearranged themselves into something truer: *purpose—to find the wound beneath the crown and see it opened. Purpose—to finish what the courier carried. Purpose—to pay, at last, forty-one outstanding debts.*

"No," Wren said aloud, to the empty shrine, in his plain rough human voice. "I'm trading it. That's all."

The shrine did not answer, shrines being honest in their silence in a way he was not.

* * *

He walked on at first light, and the country changed around him the way countries do when you stop watching them: moor giving way to hedged farmland, farmland to the first thin traffic of the salt road—carters, pilgrims, a tinker with a mule that hated him. Ordinary people living ordinary lives under a king whose bells rang nine times for a man who never aged.

Wren kept his hood up and his eyes down, a clerk's habit worn smooth as river stone. But somewhere past the second milestone he realized he was no longer walking away from the Guild. He was walking toward the capital. Toward the bells. Toward the thing the map pointed at with its shifting character that was either *crown* or *wound*, and the difference between those two words, he suspected, was the whole of the story he'd wandered into.

At a crossroads shrine—these little gods of travelers were everywhere in the lowlands, and he found himself pausing at each one, though he'd never been devout—he stopped and took stock like an honest merchant.

If he sold the map, whoever bought it would kill the messenger and bury the secret deeper. He knew how the Guild worked; he knew how power worked. The buyer might be Veyre's people, might be foreign crowns, might be the Guild itself coming to reclaim what had walked out its window. Any sale ended with the lie intact and Wren paid off and running again, nine more years of silence stretching ahead of him like a sentence.

And if he followed it—

If he followed it, he would need help. He could not do this alone; he was a clerk with a knife he'd never used and a voice he'd sworn away. He would need a dragon-keeper, if any still lived, to read the vaults. A forger, to get him inside the Court's walls. Someone small and quick and light-fingered. He listed them in his head the way he'd list inventory, and noticed—with something that was not quite annoyance and not quite hope—that he was not listing goods. He was listing *people*. People who had also been struck from ledgers. Dispossessed. His own kind, though he'd have bitten his tongue bloody before saying so.

"You're collecting them," he said to the crossroads shrine. "Like debts. Like strays."

The shrine, being a box of weathered wood with a chipped saint inside, said nothing. But the map, against his chest, warmed another degree, and Wren chose—deliberately, knowingly, the way a man signs his name to a contract he hasn't read—to believe the warmth meant nothing.

Self-deception, he was learning, is a trade like any other. You give away the truth and receive something you can live with. The trouble with the exchange rate is that it worsens daily, and eventually the truth comes due with interest.

He could feel it coming due already. Every mile east, the courier's face surfaced more often—blood-masked, certain, pressing the map into his hands as if Wren were not a hiding man but a chosen one. *Find it.* Or whatever the broken word had been. Perhaps it hadn't been *find* at all. Perhaps it had been a name after all, a name that began the same way, and the dead courier had been trying to tell him who waited at the end of the map: not a treasure, not a buyer.

A king. A real one, buried alive beneath a crown, with his true name torn out of every record in the kingdom—except one, except the skin of the last dragon, except the memory of a bound voice that had heard that name screamed once, nine years ago, by a woman with fire in her hands.

Kestrel had known the false king's secret. Wren had never understood how, or why she'd been screaming that particular name when they burned her—only that she had, only that the sound of it had followed him through nine years of silence like a debt he couldn't locate in any ledger.

Now he knew where it was kept.

The road bent east, toward the distant smudge of the capital, toward nine bells he could almost hear ringing already. Wren pulled his hood lower, settled the map against his heart, and lied to himself one final time, cleanly, professionally, the way his master had taught him to close a column:

*I'm trading this. Nothing more.*

Then he walked on, and the lie came with him, growing heavier with every step, and somewhere behind them both—far to the west, in Corvess—a rider in Court colors had already picked up his trail, and was riding with a warrant and a conscience that had not yet decided which one to obey.
