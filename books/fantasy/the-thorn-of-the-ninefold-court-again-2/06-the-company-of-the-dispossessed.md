## 6. The Company of the Dispossessed

The salt road ran west from the Guild's territory like a scar that had decided, after long consideration, to become a river instead. Wren walked it with his hood up and the map pressed flat against his ribs beneath his coat, where it lay warm as a sleeping animal. Three days out of the counting-house, and still he caught himself listening for footsteps behind him—the measured tread of Guild enforcers, the soft jingle of Sera Quill's oathbreaker chains. The road gave him nothing but wind and gulls and the smell of brine off the flats, and somehow that was worse.

He had told himself, in the grey hours before dawn when he'd slipped past the Guild's wardens, that this was a trade. The map for his freedom. A simple exchange, entered cleanly into whatever invisible ledger governed men who no longer believed in ledgers. He would find whoever the courier had meant him to find, sell the dragon-skin at a fair price, and vanish into some coastal town where nobody knew what a swordspeaker looked like when he was trying not to be one.

That was the story. He was growing suspicious of it the way a merchant grows suspicious of coin that never tarnishes—too clean, too bright, too good.

The salt village of Harrowmere announced itself before he saw it: a haze of woodsmoke over the dunes, then the crooked roofs, then the drying racks hung with fish that glittered like armor in the afternoon light. It was the kind of place the Court forgot on purpose. No bell tower. No tax assessor worth the name. The kind of place where the dispossessed washed up and learned, slowly, to call the tide their landlord.

Wren went looking for a disgraced man.

Brannoc Hale kept a shed at the edge of the village, down where the sand went black and greasy with old tar. Wren knew of him the way everyone in certain circles knew of him—a dragon-keeper of the old vaults, dismissed nine years ago for drunkenness, or heresy, or grief; the stories disagreed. What the stories agreed on was that Brannoc had been the last keeper of Emberveil, and that he had wept openly at his dismissal, and that he now mended nets and drank and said nothing to anyone about anything.

The shed door was open. Inside, a mountain of a man sat mending net with hands far too fine for the work, fingers moving through the mesh with the delicacy of someone accustomed to handling living things.

"Brannoc Hale."

The big man didn't look up. "Don't buy. Don't beg. Don't preach."

"I'm none of those." Wren stepped inside. The shed smelled of tar and fish and something older underneath—something mineral and warm, like a hearthstone that remembered fire. "I need to know if the vaults under the Ninefold Court still stand."

The net-mending stopped. Brannoc looked up then, and Wren saw the eyes that had wept at a dismissal: pale grey, rimmed red with years of salt wind and salt grief, and underneath everything, banked low but not extinguished, a fury like coals raked over and left to smolder.

"Nobody asks that," Brannoc said.

"Somebody has to."

"You've got the look of a man asking questions he already knows answers to." Brannoc set down the net. "Who are you?"

Wren considered lying. It was his craft, nearly—silence shaped into whatever shape people needed. But there was something about the shed, about the old warmth in its bones, that made lying feel like spitting in a well.

"Wren Voss," he said quietly. "Ledger Guild. Or I was, three days ago."

Brannoc's face did something complicated. Recognition, disbelief, and then a strange, terrible hope, like a man hearing a knock at a door he'd boarded shut. "They say the swordspeakers are dead."

"They say correctly. All but me, and I'm mostly silence." Wren pulled open his coat and drew out the map—not all the way, just enough that the firelight from the little stove caught the edge of it, that impossible iridescence, skin that had once covered something vast and alive. Brannoc went white. Then he went still, in the way of a man who has trained his whole body never to tremble.

"That's—" His voice cracked. He tried again. "Where did you get that?"

"A dying courier. In the snow outside my door." Wren let the map disappear back into his coat. "It shows the roots of the Court. It shows the vaults. And I think—I think it shows what they did to your dragon, Brannoc. If she's even alive."

For a long moment the only sound was the sea, patient beyond the dunes, and the tick of the cooling stove. Brannoc stood. He was huge, half a head taller than Wren, but when he spoke his voice had gone small and careful, the voice of a man carrying water across ice.

"She's alive." Not a question. A fact he had carried nine years like a stone in his chest. "I felt it. Every year on midwinter night, one ember in my dreams, burning steady. They told me I was mad. Drunk. Grieving wrong." His fists closed. "She's *alive*, and they chained her, and you're standing in my shed holding her skin in your coat."

"The vaults run beneath the capital. The map shows ways in—but the script moves. Shifts. I can read perhaps a third of it, and less each day, as though it knows I'm guessing."

"Then you'll need someone who reads things that don't hold still." Brannoc was already pulling a canvas bag from under his cot, already stuffing it with the efficiency of a man who had packed to leave a hundred times in his dreams. "And you'll need papers for the capital, because the gates have teeth now. Teeth and clerks."

"I know a forger," Wren said, surprising himself. He hadn't known until he said it—he'd heard the name in the counting-house gossip for years, a rumor with excellent penmanship. "Isolde Marrick. Last heard of in Corvane, two days east of here."

"Then we walk east first." Brannoc shouldered the bag and paused at the shed door, looking back at Wren with those banked-fire eyes. "I'm not doing this for you, swordspeaker. Understand that. I'm doing it for her."

"I wouldn't want it any other way," Wren said, and found, to his mild alarm, that he meant it—that walking beside this grieving giant felt less like recruitment than like being forgiven for something.

* * *

Isolde Marrick worked out of the back of a chandler's shop in Corvane, and received them sitting behind a table so covered with wax seals, blank writs, and half-finished commissions that she seemed to be growing out of it like a beautiful fungus. She was small and sharp-faced, with ink permanently shadowing the creases of her knuckles, and she took one look at Wren's hooded figure and Brannoc's unmistakable bulk and said:

"No."

"We haven't asked anything yet," Wren said.

"You were going to ask for royal credentials. Court-grade. Possibly with a chancellor's countersignature, because you have the look of men who attract chancellors' attention the way open wounds attract flies." She capped her pen with a decisive click. "No. I spent four years building a quiet life of honest fraud, and I will not have it undone by two large tragic men with secrets."

"How do you know we have secrets?" Brannoc rumbled.

"You both sit like men hiding documents." She leaned back. "Everyone hides documents. Most people hide boring ones. You two hide ones that whisper."

Wren weighed his options. Lies, which she would taste instantly. Truth, which was becoming an expensive habit. He chose truth, and put the map on her table between a jar of sealing wax and a forged pardon for a smuggler named Denny.

Isolde went silent in a way that made the whole room seem to lean in. Her hand hovered above the dragon-skin without touching, the way the devout hover over relics. When she finally spoke, her voice had lost its shopkeeper's snap.

"This is real. This is actually—" She swallowed. "Do you know how many forgers have been ruined chasing rumors of this? The Court burns every copy, every description, every rumor. There are men hanged for drawing a picture of a thing like this from memory." Her eyes came up, and Wren saw the same terrible hope he'd seen in Brannoc, wearing different clothes. "What is it showing?"

"Roots of the Court. Vaults. Ways in." Wren hesitated. "Script that moves."

"Show me."

He unfolded it fully for the first time since Harrowmere. The map spread across her cluttered table like a living thing disturbed from sleep, and the lines began to crawl—roads rearranging themselves, the great root-systems of the palace flexing like the fingers of a buried hand, letters surfacing and sinking in the old high script of the founding age.

Isolde read it the way she did everything: fast, greedy, aloud. "'Beneath the ninth bell... the crown's root... the name that was struck—'" She frowned. "It keeps sliding. The key phrases keep sliding away from me, like—like text written by someone who keeps changing their mind."

"It does that," Wren said. "Can you read it?"

"Slowly. With effort. With tools I don't have." She bit her lip, and then, abruptly, she was business again, sweeping forgery supplies into a leather case with terrifying speed. "There's a pickpocket works the Corvane market. Calls herself Pip. Half-feral, quick as a debt collector, and—this is the part that matters—she reads anything. Anything. Street cant, guild cipher, dead languages off tombstones for a copper a word. I've used her twice to verify scripts I wasn't sure of." She cinched the case shut. "If anyone can pin that shifting script down, it's her. Assuming she doesn't rob us first."

"Charming company you keep," Brannoc muttered.

"The best people are found in bad places," Isolde said. "It's only the worst ones who advertise elsewhere."

* * *

They found Pip exactly where Isolde said they would: failing spectacularly to be inconspicuous near the spice stalls, a small figure in a boy's cap with sharp dark eyes tracking the crowd like a hawk pretending to be a sparrow. Wren watched her lift a purse from a wool merchant with a touch so light the man never broke stride, and thought, absurdly, of himself at fourteen, learning to make words do theft.

Isolde hailed her with two fingers. Pip sauntered over, all swagger and suspicion, and froze halfway when she got close enough to really look at them.

"You're trouble," she announced. "All three of you. You've got the walk. Trouble-walk. I know trouble-walk better'n anybody in this market, and you three are a procession of it."

"We'll pay," Isolde said.

"Everybody pays. Question's how much, and for what, and whether I live through it." Pip's eyes flicked between them, quick as her hands. "Big one looks like he's carrying a funeral. Ink-fingers looks like she's about to commit several crimes. And you—" her gaze landed on Wren and stayed there, uncomfortably long, "—you look like somebody turned you off and forgot to tell you."

Wren held out the map.

Pip looked at it. Looked at him. Looked at it again, and this time she didn't reach for it—she went still, utterly still, in a way that made her seem suddenly much older than her small frame suggested.

"That's dragon-skin," she whispered. "My gran used to tell about it. Said the old script runs *in* it, not on it. Said it's not writing, it's remembering." Her eyes came up, blazing now. "You want me to read a remembering?"

"Can you?" Brannoc asked.

"Can I—" Pip laughed, a wild, delighted, terrified sound. "Mister, I've been reading things I shouldn't since I could walk. Give me a corner and an hour and I'll give you a road into the palace that the Chancellor herself has forgotten." She snatched the map's edge before Wren could react—and then yelped, snatching her hand back and sucking her fingertips.

"What happened?" Wren demanded.

"It bit me!" Pip stared at her own fingers, then—Wren watched it happen—began to grin. "Warm. It went warm right as I touched it. Like it knew me." She looked up at Wren, and there was something naked and hopeful under all that street armor, something that recognized something in him. "Right. Fine. Yes. I'm in. But I'm warning you all now: I steal things. Habit. Don't leave anything you love lying around where I might get attached."

"Welcome aboard," Isolde said dryly. "We're all thieves here. I forge truth. Brannoc steals hope back from the powerful. And our friend—" she glanced at Wren, "—well. Our friend stole nine years of silence from himself. Perhaps we'll help him steal it back."

Wren said nothing. But that evening, camped in the ruin of a tollhouse with a fire going and Brannoc's stew in his bowl and Pip arguing with Isolde about whether the map counted as stolen property, Wren sat a little apart and listened to them, and felt something in his chest he had not felt since before the purge.

Belonging. Reluctant, unasked-for, arriving like weather. These three strangers with their griefs and crimes and stubborn fires had simply folded him in, the way travelers fold a stranger against the cold, and he had not known how starved he'd been until the warmth hit.

He hated how much he wanted to keep it. Everything he loved had been taken once already; the smart move, the survivor's move, was to hold this at arm's length, to remain the man who traded the map and vanished. That was the plan. That had always been the plan.

But then Pip crawled over and thrust the map's corner at him, her eyes shining. "Look. Look what it did when we all touched it. It's showing more. It's—it's like it was waiting for enough of us."

And there, crawling across the dragon-skin in letters of buried fire, new lines surfaced that had not been there in Harrowmere or Corvane:

*Four come seeking. Four are seen.*

Wren felt the warmth of the fire on his face and the cold walk of dread up his spine, and understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own ledger entry in advance, that the road ahead had already noticed them.

Far off to the north, faint on the wind, a bell began to ring.

Nine strokes.

Someone in the Court knew they were coming.
