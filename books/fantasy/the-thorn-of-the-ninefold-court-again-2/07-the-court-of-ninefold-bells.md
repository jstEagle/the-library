## 7. The Court of Ninefold Bells

The capital announced itself first by sound.

Isolde Vane heard it from the crest of the last hill, while the others were still arguing about Pip's third attempt to pick her lockpicks out of her own pocket. A deep, bronze resonance rolling up out of the valley like a tide, nine strokes, unhurried and vast, each one settling into her chest and staying there.

"There," she said. "Nine bells. Right on time. Kings who never age do love a schedule."

Brannoc shaded his eyes against the afternoon glare and said nothing, which was how she knew he was moved. The big man wore his silences like other men wore cloaks—pulled close when the weather inside turned bad. He had been born in that valley, in the dragon-keeper's quarter below the palace walls, before the quarter burned. She had read his face for three days now on the salt road, and she knew what the bells did to him.

"Nine times a day?" Pip asked, hopping up onto the milestone to see better. The boy's hair was cropped short for the disguise, which had not stopped him from looking exactly like a pickpocket in his new merchant's coat. "That's a lot of bells."

"Three at dawn, three at noon, three at dusk." Isolde adjusted the gray wool hood that made her, supposedly, a widow traveling to settle an estate. "One bell for each fold of the Court. The ninth is the king's own. It hasn't missed since—" She caught herself. Since the king died, she almost said, and didn't. That was the trouble with the truth: it got into your mouth the way grit gets into ink, and then everything you wrote with it came out wrong.

"Since ever," she finished smoothly.

Wren stood apart from them, the way he always did, watching the city the way a man watches a door he once fled through. He hadn't spoken much in three days. He rarely spoke at all, actually—which she'd found unnerving until she understood it was discipline rather than emptiness. A man keeping his hands off a weapon he carried behind his teeth.

"Well," Isolde said briskly, clapping dust from her skirts. "Shall we go lie to a city?"

* * *

She had forged documents before, but she had never forged her way *into* a living city, and the difference was the difference between painting a portrait and painting over a window while someone watched you through the glass.

The Salt Gate took an hour. Isolde presented herself as Mistress Corvane, executrix of the estate of a minor salt-factor deceased in the western provinces, traveling with household staff—a keeper of beasts (Brannoc, whose papers claimed him as a licensed handler of exotic livestock), a clerk (Wren, gray-cloaked, ink-stained fingers she had manufactured with walnut dye), and an apprentice (Pip, who kept forgetting not to slouch). The seal she'd cut for the western assizes was good work; she was quietly proud of the double-struck C, which most examiners would take weeks to question.

The gate-sergeant barely looked at it. He looked at their faces instead, one by one, with the flat patience of a man counting coins he already knew the sum of.

"Purpose in the capital?"

"Grief and paperwork," Isolde said, letting her voice crack just enough. "My late husband's accounts are held by a Guild house here. I mean to close them."

Something flickered across the sergeant's face—not suspicion. Recognition. He waved them through with a small, tired gesture, the way a man waves away a fly he cannot be bothered to kill.

It was only after they'd passed under the arch, into the smell of tar and hot bread and too many bodies, that Isolde understood what she'd seen in him. Not doubt about her papers.

Relief. He was relieved they were nobody. The gate of the Ninefold Court had become a place where being somebody was the dangerous thing.

She filed that away. Forgers filed things; it was the whole craft. Every city was a document, and every document had its erasures, its overwritten lines, its watermarks of fear showing through the vellum where you held it up to the light.

This city's watermark was silence.

* * *

They lodged at the Sign of the Leaning Scale, a Guild-affiliated inn near the counting-houses, chosen because anonymity lived best in plain sight. Isolde took the corner room with a view of the bell-tower because she liked to know where the loud things were.

From her window she catalogued the capital the way she'd catalogue a suspected forgery.

First, the bells themselves. Nine towers around the palace crown, each bell cast with one of the Court's sigils—the scales, the quill, the sword, the rose, and five more she could name but had never seen honored. At dawn, noon, and dusk they rang in sequence, and when the ninth struck, every soul in the streets stopped.

Not bowed. Stopped. Mid-stride, mid-sentence, mid-bargain. A fishwife with her arms full of herring went still as her own stall. A pair of City Watchmen froze with a dice cup suspended between them. Even the pigeons seemed to hold their places on the eaves. For the length of the ninth bell, the whole capital held its breath—and then exhaled all together and went on, and no one remarked on it, because no one alive remembered doing anything else.

Second, the chalk marks. On doorposts, on lintels, low down where children could reach them: small white circles, some fresh, some weathered nearly to nothing. Isolde counted eleven on her street alone. She asked the innkeeper's daughter what they meant and got a smile so practiced it might have been one of her own seals.

"The king's blessing, mistress. For the household."

"And the ones half worn away?"

The girl's smile stayed perfectly fixed. "Old blessings last longest," she said, and curtsied, and was gone up the stairs before Isolde could ask why some households had stopped being blessed.

Third, the prices. Everything in the capital was expensive, but fear had its own tariff. A room with a window cost triple. A word with a Court official cost more than the room. And the cheapest thing in the whole city, the thing sellers practically pressed on you for free, was agreement. Agree with anyone, nod at anything, praise the king's eternal health, and the world opened its doors. Disagreement was the one luxury priced beyond anyone's means.

By the second evening, Isolde had filled six pages of her working book with observations in cipher. By the third, Wren sat across from her in the inn's back parlor, turning her pages with those careful ink-stained fingers, and said the first full sentence she'd heard from him in two days:

"They're grieving without knowing it."

"That's the best kind of grief to rule over," Isolde said. "It never asks questions."

He looked at her then, really looked, with those ledger-keeper's eyes that weighed everything and entered nothing, and she felt briefly, absurdly, *seen*—a sensation she usually paid good money to avoid.

"You enjoy this," Wren said. It wasn't quite an accusation.

"I enjoy competence," Isolde corrected. "The lying is incidental. Now stop admiring me and tell me what the credentials need to accomplish, because getting us through a gate is a farthing's work. Getting us into the palace archives is a different animal entirely, and I'll need to know what we're hunting before I can hunt it."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Outside, dusk was coming down, and somewhere above the rooftops the first of the evening bells gathered itself.

"The root of the crown," he said at last. "There's a place beneath the palace where the Court began. Old records, older than the ledgers. If there's proof of what happened to the king—if there's anything left of the true succession—it will be buried there."

"Beneath," Isolde repeated. "You want me to forge our way *down*."

"I want you to forge our way anywhere," Wren said, "and I'm told you're very good at pretending that frightens you less than it does."

The ninth bell struck. Through the parlor window Isolde saw the street below empty of motion, saw Brannoc standing in the yard with his head bowed and his big hands open at his sides, saw Pip frozen halfway over the fence with a stolen apple in his fist, and felt the great bronze voice roll through her ribs and say, in its wordless way, *all is well, all is well, the king lives forever.*

All is well, she thought, when the sound faded and the city exhaled. That's the finest forgery I've ever encountered. Nine hundred years old, still wet, and signed by no one.

"Yes," she told Wren. "I can do it. But credentials for the undercourt will need a name with weight attached. A real name, recently dead or conveniently absent, with real authority. And names like that have families, secretaries, and enemies, and I'll need four days and a great deal of luck."

"Four days," Wren said. "We may have two."

"Then I'll need even more luck," Isolde said, "and I suggest you start praying to whatever gods a man swears oaths to."

Something crossed his face at that—quick, involuntary, gone before she could read it. He rose, murmured something about the stables and Brannoc, and left her alone with her cipher pages and the cooling tea.

Isolde looked down at her notes and, beneath her catalogue of the city's fears, added one line in plain script, for no reader but herself:

*Nobody stops for the ninth bell because stopping is easier than asking why.*

Then she blew out the candle and got to work.

* * *

The forgery consumed her for two days and most of two nights.

She built it in layers, the way a mason builds a wall that must bear weight. The foundation was a genuine writ of access to the Court's lower registries—genuine because Pip lifted it from a sleeping archivist's satchel, along with the archivist's lunch, which Isolde made him return. Upon that true stone she laid false courses: an amendment granting passage to the undercourt galleries, a countersignature from a deputy chancellor whose hand she studied for six hours via three separate samples, and the crowning piece—a warrant sealed with the rose sigil of the fifth fold, which she cut herself from a coin-melted blank over fourteen hours of the steadiest work of her life.

When it was done, she held it to the candlelight and looked for the watermark of fear in her own work, the flaw that would betray her.

There was none. That frightened her more than anything else she'd seen in the capital.

On the third morning, she laid the finished credential on the parlor table and let the company gather around it: Wren with his silent weighing eyes, Brannoc ducking under the low beam, Pip radiating the specific pride of a boy who had contributed a lunch.

"One use," Isolde said. "Perhaps two if the examiner is drunk or devout. After that, the seal's signature flaws get remembered, and every oathbreaker in the Court will have our description. So whatever we do down there, we do it once, and we do it right."

"And if we're caught?" Pip asked.

"Then I'm a forger, you're an accomplice, the big man's an unlicensed beast-handler, and our quiet friend here—" she nodded at Wren, "—is whatever the Court does to people who used to matter."

"We won't be caught," Wren said.

Everyone looked at him. It was the most certain thing he'd said since the salt road, and certainty sat strangely on him, like fine clothes borrowed from a larger man.

"How can you know that?" Isolde asked.

"Because I've been caught before," Wren said quietly, "and I know exactly what it costs. I don't intend to pay it twice."

Before anyone could answer, the noon bells began—and this time, something was wrong.

Isolde heard it before she understood it: the eighth bell rang clean and golden, and then the ninth—

The ninth bell cracked.

Not broke. *Cracked*—one stroke that came out bent in the middle, a bronze voice catching on something, a sound like a king clearing his throat and failing. Every head in the inn went up. In the street below, the frozen crowd stayed frozen a heartbeat longer than usual, and Isolde saw, written plainly across forty faces, the thing the whole city spent its days not saying.

Hope. Barely a spark of it, quickly smothered—but hope, flaring at a broken note like grass through pavement.

Then the bell-towers fell silent, the crowd exhaled, and the fishwife went back to her herring, and the moment closed over like water.

Pip spoke first, softly, for once not performing anything at all. "Bells don't crack by accident."

"No," Brannoc rumbled. From the doorway of the parlor, the dragon-keeper's eyes were wet and fierce. "And dragons don't dim by accident. Something down there is running out."

Isolde gathered her forged credential off the table and slid it into the oilcloth wallet against her breastbone, where it lay over her heart like a second, braver heart of its own.

"Two days ago I told our quiet friend he should pray," she said. "Consider this answered. Whatever's failing under that palace is failing *now*, and we move tonight, before whoever holds the leash decides to mend the bell instead of the beast."

She stood, squared her widow's hood, and gave them all the wry grin that had carried her through three kingdoms' worth of lies.

"Well then," said Isolde Vane, forger, widow, and the finest liar in the Ninefold Court. "Let's go meet the thing wearing his crown."
