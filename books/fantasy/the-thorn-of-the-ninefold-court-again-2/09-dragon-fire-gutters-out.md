## 9. Dragon-Fire, Gutters Out

The way down began behind a wall of stacked salt-barrels in the tanner's quarter, where the stink was thick enough to make Pip retch and the shadows old enough to have opinions. Brannoc pressed his palm flat against the third barrel from the left and felt for the seam that had not existed when he was last here—nine years ago, with different companions and a lighter heart.

"There," he said. "Feel it?"

Wren crouched beside him, fingers tracing the mortar. "I feel cold."

"Cold is right. The vaults were built to keep her *in*, but stone remembers what it holds. Cold like this means something on the other side is still breathing."

Isolde lifted her lantern higher. In its glow her face was all sharp angles and professional skepticism. "You're telling me we're about to break into a royal dungeon through a wall that only opens for people who pet barrels."

"I'm telling you I was the keeper of the ninth vault for eleven years," Brannoc said. "And the door knows my hand, even if the men who took my keys don't know my face anymore." He worked his thumb into the seam. The stone gave with a sound like a long-held sigh, grinding inward along a track greased by decades of dust. Air rolled out—cold, mineral, and underneath it something else. Something Brannoc had spent nine years trying to forget and had never once succeeded.

Sulfur and cinnamon. Old fire, banked low.

Pip made a small noise at the back of her throat. "That's what dragons smell like?"

"That's what *she* smells like," Brannoc said, before he could stop himself.

He went through first. It was his grief; he would walk into it ahead of anyone.

* * *

The stair descended in long spirals cut from the living rock beneath the Court, each step worn smooth at the center by generations of keeper's boots. Brannoc counted them without meaning to—one hundred and forty to the first landing, ninety more to the second—and the counting hurt worse than any wound he'd ever taken, because he had once counted these steps joyfully, humming, carrying buckets of rendered fat and honeycomb and the strange black coal called heartstone that dragons ate when their own fires ran thin.

Behind him, the others followed in silence. Even Pip, who had not stopped talking since the salt road, had gone quiet. Wren walked closest, his hand never far from the map wrapped against his chest, his eyes moving over the walls with a swordspeaker's attention—as though the stones themselves might be read like ledger lines.

"They built the city on top of her," Isolde said at last, voice hushed despite herself. "All those bells. All those towers."

"The Court was always hers first," Brannoc said. "The Ninefold Bells were cast from her shed scales. Did you know that? Every bell in the palace rings with dragon-metal in it. That's why they carry so far. That's why no one can bear to hear them fall silent." He laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "We chained her under her own music."

At the second landing, the corridor opened into the gallery proper, and Brannoc raised his hand for stillness.

The dragon-vaults were not one cavern but nine, arranged in a ring around a central well-shaft that rose toward the distant gray suggestion of the palace foundations above. Once, all nine vaults had held dragons—the pride of the Court, the living engines of its power and its wonder. Brannoc had tended Emberveil in the ninth vault, southernmost, where the rock veined with quartz and caught whatever light there was and scattered it in soft rainbow ghosts.

Now he stood at the mouth of the first vault and looked out across the ring, and the darkness answered him with emptiness. Eight vaults, open and hollow, their gates hanging crooked or torn away entirely. Eight absences. He knew the history—he had helped write the petitions, in the early years, begging the Court to explain why the dragons were being moved, relocated, reassigned—but knowing the shape of a thing did nothing to blunt standing inside its wound.

"Gone?" Wren asked quietly.

"Dead or sold," Brannoc said. "Mostly dead. The Court told the people they'd been sent east, beyond the Saltreach mountains, to breed new clutches in wild country." He shook his head slowly. "People wanted to believe it. You can build a whole kingdom on a lie if the lie is beautiful enough."

"And her?" Pip whispered. "Emberveil? She's really still here? After all this time?"

Brannoc didn't answer. He turned south and walked.

* * *

The gate of the ninth vault was closed, and it was closed wrong.

It had been forged anew since his day—thicker, uglier, banded in iron that gleamed dully with an oily sheen he recognized and hated: oath-iron, the same metal the Guild used for binding contracts, the same metal that had gone into the collar of every swordspeaker's oath. Chains as thick as his waist ran from the gate back into the rock on either side, and set into the chains at intervals of three feet were medallions of pale metal that drank the lantern-light rather than reflecting it.

"Siphons," Brannoc said. His voice came out rough. "Fire-siphons. They're drawing off her flame. Feeding it up through the shafts into the palace."

"Feeding it to *what*?" Isolde asked. But Brannoc saw the moment the answer landed on her too—saw her glance at Wren, at the map, at the whole terrible architecture of the thing they'd come to unmake.

"The king doesn't age," Brannoc said. "Nine years, and the bells ring for a man whose portrait hasn't changed. Coincidence makes poor fuel for a miracle. Dragon-fire, on the other hand—" He put his hand on the gate, and the iron was warm. Not hot. Warm, like the flank of a sleeping animal, like a hearth gone low in the night. "Dragon-fire will keep anything alive. Even a thing that should have died."

He shouldered the gate.

It groaned, resisted, and then—whether because his hand remembered the old wards or because the vault itself had grown tired of holding—swung inward, and the smell of banked fire rolled out to meet them, and Brannoc Vane, keeper of the ninth vault, disgraced, exiled, stripped of name and office, stepped across the threshold of his own grief.

* * *

She was smaller than he remembered.

That was the first cruelty. Not the chains—though the chains were horror enough, great linked lengths of oath-iron running from collar to floor-bolt to ceiling-anchor, pinning her wings folded against her flanks, weighting her neck until her head rested nearly on the stone. Not the siphon-crowns driven into the soft places between her scales, eight of them, nine of them, each one drinking. Not the darkened scales themselves, once the color of hammered bronze shot through with veins of living gold, now dulled to the brown of old blood, cracked in places, flaking.

The first cruelty was simply that she was *smaller*. Dragons did not shrink. Dragons grew for centuries, slow and vast, their bodies keeping pace with their years. For Emberveil to be smaller meant her body had been consumed—burned as fuel, drawn out through the siphons, fed piece by piece into whatever hollow thing sat wearing a crown above them.

"Oh," said Pip, very small. And then again, "oh," and then she turned her face into Brannoc's sleeve and didn't look.

Isolde had gone white. Even Wren stood very still, and Brannoc thought he saw the swordspeaker's jaw working, thought he saw something in Wren's eyes that might, in another man, have been tears.

Brannoc walked forward alone.

His boots sounded loud on the stone. Ten paces from her head, he stopped, knelt, and laid both palms flat against the floor—an old gesture, the keeper's greeting, *earth-to-earth, breath-to-breath, servant-to-sovereign.* The posture of a man who served a god and knew it.

"Emberveil," he said. His voice broke on the second syllable, and he let it. "It's Brannoc. It's your Brannoc. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me nine years. I'm sorry I believed them when they said you'd been moved. I'm sorry I signed the petitions and then stopped signing them, and I'm sorry I lived comfortable while you—"

The bond stirred.

It was faint—fainter than breath, fainter than memory. When Brannoc had been keeper, the bond between them had been a river: her thoughts flowing into his mind warm and vast and slow, images more than words, the taste of high wind and the geometry of thermals and a patience so deep it made granite look hurried. Now there was barely a thread. A hairline crack of light in a sealed room. But it found him, the way it always had, and through it came—

Not words. Not yet. A feeling first: recognition, ancient and exhausted, like an old dog lifting its head when the door opens. Then pain, vast and banked. Then a question, shaped not in language but in the image of the vault gate, opening, opening, opening, asked and re-asked across nine empty years: *why did no one come.*

Brannoc bowed his head until his forehead touched stone. "I know," he whispered. "I know. I'm here now. I'm here."

From behind him, Wren spoke softly. "Can she understand us? Can she speak?"

"She speaks when she wants to. Through the bond, to a keeper. Words are... effortful for them. Like us writing letters in a language of pictures." He lifted his head. "But she'll want to know why we've come. She'll want to know if we've come to free her or to use her. Everyone who's come near her in nine years has been the second kind."

He turned back to her, and he opened the bond fully—laid himself bare across it, as a keeper must, offering everything: the nine years, the exile, the salt road, the ragged company behind him, the map against Wren's chest, the false king, the name buried at the crown's root. He offered it as truth, because dragons tasted lies the way men tasted rot, and he would not have her last clear memory of him be flavored with deception.

For a long time, nothing.

Then her eye moved.

It was the size of a dinner plate, lid heavy-lidded, filmed half over with a gray nictitating membrane that the siphons kept her too weak to clear. But it moved. Rolled in its socket with a grinding slowness, until the slit-pupil found Brannoc kneeling in the dark, and fixed on him, and within it—deep, deep down, at the bottom like a coin at the bottom of a well—a spark of ember-gold light flickered.

Still burning. Gods help them all, after nine years of being drained, some part of her was *still burning.*

Her chest expanded—a slow, labored bellows-drag, the sound of it filling the vault like wind in a chimney—and when she exhaled, no flame came, only heat, a wash of dry warmth that stirred Brannoc's hair and smelled of sulfur and cinnamon and home.

And through the thread-thin bond, in a voice like the last coals of a burned-down house settling in the night, she spent her hoarded strength on a single word:

***Soon.***

Not *help me*. Not *free me*. Not even *who are you*. 

*Soon.*

Brannoc didn't understand it. He felt the weight of it settle into him through the bond, felt it land somewhere below thought, in the place where certainty lives, and he understood only that it was not despair. She had lain here nine years, drained to feed a corpse's pantomime of life, and the word she chose to spend her almost-nothing on was a word of *timing.*

"Soon," he repeated aloud, hoarse. "What's soon, Em? What do you see?"

The pupil drifted, slow, past him—to Wren. To the map-shaped bulge against the swordspeaker's chest. And the ember in her eye brightened, just slightly, just for a heartbeat, and Brannoc felt through the bond something that was almost amusement and almost hope and almost hunger, all braided together.

She knew. Somehow, in whatever dim country her mind had retreated to, she knew what Wren carried, and she knew what it meant, and she was telling them—insofar as a dying god tells anything—*hurry.*

"We have to get her out," Pip said. Her voice wobbled. She'd crept forward despite herself, and now stood at the edge of the lantern light staring up at the ruined majesty of the last dragon in the world, and her chin was trembling, and she was glaring at the chains as though she could pickpocket them into oblivion. "Those siphons—we break them, right? We pull them out and she gets strong and we fly out of here and burn the palace flat. Right?"

"No," Brannoc said gently.

"Why not?"

"Because the siphons aren't just draining her. They're *anchoring* her. Pull one out wrong and it takes the fire with it—all of it, everything she has left. And the fire goes..." He looked up the central shaft, toward the distant palace, toward the hollow king. "...wherever it's been going. Into him. We'd be topping up the thing we came to kill."

"Then how?" Isolde asked. Practical as ever, already sketching the problem in the air with one finger, already looking for the forgery angle, the workaround, the clever false seal. "There's always a how. What's the mechanism?"

"The unbinding," Wren said quietly.

They all turned. He hadn't moved from the gate, but his hand had come up to rest over the map, and his face in the lantern light was terrible and calm, the calm of a man reading a bill he cannot pay.

"The map shows the rite. The true name, spoken in swordspeech, at the crown's root—that's what breaks the binding. Not just the king's binding." His eyes went to Emberveil, to the chains, to the siphon-crowns drinking her life. "*All* the bindings laid in that season. Hers included. One word, spoken truly, and every chain forged from the same oath comes loose at once."

"Then say it," Pip said. "Say the word, chains pop off, dragon barbecue, done."

"It's not that simple," Wren said, and his voice had gone flat and careful in a way Brannoc had learned to recognize on the salt road—the voice of a man stepping carefully around his own wound. "Swordspeech isn't a trick. It's an oath. And mine—"

He stopped. Closed his mouth. Looked away.

Brannoc rose from his knees. He crossed the distance between them and put one broad hand on Wren's shoulder, and through the fading warmth of the bond he felt Emberveil watching them both, patient as geology.

"You heard her," Brannoc said quietly. "*Soon.* She's been saving that word for nine years, son. Saving it like a coin in a boot. Whatever it costs you—" he squeezed, gentle as a man handling a bird—"she's already decided it's worth it. Question is whether you have."

Wren said nothing. But he didn't shrug the hand away, either.

Above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of rock, the Ninefold Bells began to ring. Nine strokes, slow and ceremonial—the noon marking, or perhaps a summons, or perhaps only the daily ritual of a court worshipping a king who was not a king. Each stroke shivered down through the stone, and each stroke, Brannoc realized with fresh horror, traveled straight down the siphon-chains into Emberveil's body. She flinched at the first. At the third, a low sound escaped her—not a roar, she hadn't the strength for a roar—something smaller and infinitely worse. A whimper, dragged out of a creature the size of a cathedral.

By the ninth stroke, the ember in her eye had guttered low again, and her head had sunk back onto the stone.

The bells faded. The vault settled into its long dark. And Brannoc stood between the last dragon and the last swordspeaker, in the deepest room beneath a lying kingdom, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever came next would begin here—or nowhere.

"We can't stay," Isolde murmured. "Guards rotate through the upper galleries. We've maybe an hour before someone checks the gate."

"Then we go up," Brannoc said. "And we come back. With everything we need. And soon—" he looked at Emberveil's dimmed eye, at the single golden spark still refusing to die at the bottom of it— "*soon* means what she says it means."

As they withdrew, Wren lingered at the threshold, and Brannoc saw him press his hand once against the warm iron of the gate—a touch as light as an apology—before letting it swing shut.

Behind them, in the dark, the last dragon breathed.

And somewhere in the palace above, in rooms full of candlelight and ceremony, something that wore a king's face paused mid-gesture, mid-smile, mid-lie—and for the first time in nine years, felt the chains on its own soul shift, and knew that someone had come asking questions in the deep places.

It turned its hollow eyes toward the south.

Toward the ninth vault.

And it began, softly, to hum the dragons' mourning-song—the one song it should not have known.
