## 10. A Debt Called Justice

The map did not want to be read at night.

Wren had learned that in the three days since they'd made camp in the ruined waystation above the dragon-vaults' old service road—a squat stone shell with a collapsed roof and a hearth that Brannoc had coaxed back to life out of pure stubbornness. By daylight, the dragon-skin lay flat and cooperative on the trestle table, its lines shifting like slow water: roads becoming rivers becoming the veins of the Court itself, all of them converging on a point beneath the capital marked by a symbol Wren had not seen since his master's death. The crown's root-stone.

By night, though, the skin grew restless. It drank the firelight and gave back shadows, and the script Pip swore it wrote—the shifting, sliding letters only the boy seemed able to hold in his eyes—crawled faster, as if the thing were dreaming. Wren kept it wrapped in oilcloth after sundown now, weighted under the whetstone he no longer used, because there was nothing left to sharpen. Swordspeakers did not fight with edges. They fought with words, and he had buried his words nine years ago in a grave alongside four friends.

He was sitting closest to the hearth, pretending to sleep the way he'd perfected over nearly a decade—eyes shut, breath even, attention wide awake—when Pip finally cracked.

"All right." The boy's voice came thin from his corner near the door. "All right, I can't sleep either, so stop lying about it, all of you. I know you're not sleeping. Nobody breathes that evenly."

Brannoc grunted from where he sat whittling a stick into something that might eventually become a spoon or might simply remain a ruined stick. Isolde looked up from her seal-work, the little brass matrices scattered around her like fallen teeth. Sera Quill stood apart by the broken window, arms crossed against the draft, watching the dark hills for riders that hadn't come yet but would.

"Fine," Wren said, and opened his eyes.

The fire popped. Somewhere outside an owl called once and thought better of it.

"Read it again," Isolde said quietly. Not a request. She'd been circling this for two days, the way she circled any document she intended to own. "Read us the part you've been reading alone."

Wren considered lying. He was good at lying; it was the second craft he'd learned after silence. But the map's weight was through his pack and into the floorboards and into his spine, and the company had bled enough small truths for him these last weeks that withholding one more felt like theft.

So he rose, unwrapped the dragon-skin, and spread it across the table in the firelight, where it sighed and settled like something glad to be uncovered.

"The rite," he said. "The unbinding. I've read it eleven times. It doesn't change." His finger traced the converging lines down, down, beneath the city's ninefold rings of bells and walls, to the root. "It needs three things. Emberveil's flame, still living—that we have, barely, guttering in her chains below the Court. It needs the king's true name—and we have that too, thanks to a dead courier who paid for it with everything." He paused. His mouth had gone dry around what came next, the way it always did, every time, all eleven times.

"And it needs," he said, "a voice."

"A voice," Sera repeated from the window. Flat. Careful. She already knew. He could see she'd known since the second reading; she was a hunter, and hunters recognize quarry behavior.

"Not just any voice." Wren kept his eyes on the script, because looking at them would be worse. "A swordspeaker's true voice. Spoken true. Not whispered, not written, not signed. The binding on the king was laid in the high speech, so it can only be unwound in the high speech, at full voice, at the root-stone, by someone bound to the words themselves." He laughed, and hated the sound of it, short and ugly in the quiet room. "Do you understand? The oath that keeps me silent isn't a habit I could break if I got brave one morning. It's a Guild binding. Sworn blood and ledger-law and curse, all three. Nine years ago I swore never to speak it again, and the Ledger holds oaths the way a river holds stones."

"Meaning?" Pip asked, though his face said he understood perfectly and wanted to be wrong.

"Meaning if I speak the unbinding, the binding shatters—" Wren tapped the root-stone mark, "—and so do I. The curse comes due. Every clause. That's how the Guild writes them. You don't break an impossible trade; the trade breaks *you*." He straightened, folded his hands behind his back like a man presenting a bill of sale, because if he didn't make it a transaction he would make it a confession, and he wasn't ready for that. "I've spent three days trying to find a loophole. There isn't one. The map is very clear, and dragon-skin doesn't lie. It costs exactly what it says it costs."

Silence held the room for a long moment. The fire worked through a knot in the wood and spat.

Then Brannoc set down his ruined stick.

"How much?" he said.

"Brannoc—"

"No, lad, let's have it plain, the way you'd write it in one of your ledgers." The old keeper's face was seamed like bark, and his eyes were wet and furious and steady. "What does 'the curse comes due' mean when it lands on a man? Say it aloud. All of it. Because we've all been doing arithmetic in the dark for three days and I'll not have it done in whispers anymore."

Wren closed his eyes. When he opened them, he made himself look at each of them in turn, because they deserved that much.

"When I swore the silence-oath, the Guild's scriveners wrote my forfeit into the bond. It's standard for a binding of that grade. If I speak in the high speech—in *any* true swordspeech—the debt collects." His voice stayed level through sheer craftsmanship. "My hands first. The Guild takes the sword-hand's memory; a swordspeaker who speaks falsely loses the ability to hold anything true in his grip. Then the tongue goes numb to the high speech forever—which is fair, honestly, that part's just physics of the art. And then—" and here his craftsmanship failed him, and the levelness cracked, and he heard himself say the rest in the ordinary human voice he used for ordinary human things, "—and then the bond calls in the years. All of them. Nine years of borrowed quiet, collected at once. The scrivener who wrote my oath told me what that looks like. She'd seen it done to a man in Vail who broke a smaller bond. He aged forty years between one word and the next, and then he forgot every person he'd ever loved, in reverse order, newest first, until there was nothing left to forget." 

Pip had gone white. Even Sera had turned fully from the window.

"So," Wren finished, into the ringing quiet. "That's the price of the ticket. One kingdom, unbound and free. One hollow king restored to being a man. One dragon freed from nine years of being a candle. Cost: one ex-swordspeaker, spent entire. No refunds. No exchanges. The Guild does not negotiate with dead men."

"That's not funny," Pip said hoarsely.

"No," Wren agreed. "It's not. But if I don't laugh at it, I'll start screaming, and I promised myself I'd retired from that too."

Isolde moved first. She always did—she was a woman who believed any problem could be solved with paper, patience, and sufficient audacity. She swept the seal-matrices aside and planted both palms on the table.

"There are other ways," she said. "There are *always* other ways. A forged dispensation from the Guild—an instrument releasing your oath—"

"You'd need the First Scrivener's living hand and seal, and the bond would smell the forgery before the ink dried. Bonds aren't documents, Izzy. They're wounds. You can't counterfeit a wound."

"Then we cut the bond loose another way. Kill the Guildmaster—"

"The bond doesn't die with him. It dies with the *Guild*, and the Guild has chapters in nine cities and ten thousand clerks." He shook his head slowly. "I've run this list, Isolde. Every entry. For three days. There is no honest trade here and no dishonest one either. There is only the price on the page."

"Then we don't pay it," Brannoc growled. "We go home. We take Emberveil's word—she said *soon*, and dragons mean what they say—and we find some other road to her chains. We pry the vault open with iron and sweat and we burn the bindings off her hide without touching the king's, and we leave the Court its rot. Let them keep their hollow king. Let them choke on him."

"And Veyre?" Wren asked softly. "Veyre, who plans to let the last dragon die and crown herself over a court of ash? We walk away, and in a year there's no Court to save. Just a regent and a graveyard with bells." He looked down at the map, at the lines converging like a wound closing. "I've spent nine years telling myself the world's rot wasn't mine to sweep. It bought me nothing except practice at being small."

Sera spoke from the window, and her voice was strange—not the inquisitor's blade-voice he'd first heard on the salt road, but something older underneath it, worn smooth.

"I knew a swordspeaker once," she said. "When I was a girl in the provinces, before the purge. He settled a grain-feud between two villages that had been killing each other for a generation. He spoke maybe thirty words. Half of them were apologies." Her jaw worked. "They hanged him anyway, two years later, with the rest of them. But I never forgot what thirty true words could do. I became an inquisitor because I thought law was the next best thing to that." She crossed to the table and put her hand flat on the dragon-skin, beside Wren's, not quite touching it. "Don't you dare tell me the cost is too high. Don't you dare make this decision *for* us by deciding it's simple. It isn't simple. It's the least simple thing I've ever heard. But don't stand there pricing your own soul like it's nobody's business but yours."

"It isn't yours either," Wren said.

"Isn't it?" Sera's eyes were bright and hard. "You pulled me out of a river in Chapter—out of the flood at Marrow Ford, when you could have let me drown and saved yourself a day's walking and an enemy's company. You fed Pip when he tried to rob you. You vouched for Isolde to a man with a crossbow. You've been collecting us for weeks, Wren Voss, whether you meant to or not, and people who get collected get a vote. That's how belonging works. I know you don't remember. That's rather the point."

The words landed harder than any blade he'd ever faced, because they were true, and truth was the one weapon he had no training against.

"She's right," Pip said quietly. He was sitting with his knees drawn up, and for once he looked less like a street-thief and more like what he probably was underneath: a kid who'd learned early that the world priced everything. "You want to do the noble lonely thing where you sneak off at dawn and spend yourself without asking anybody. I've seen it. People like you always do it." His chin came up. "But here's what I know about debts, because I grew up inside them. A debt you pay without telling the people it protects—that's not payment. That's just another kind of stealing. You'd be stealing our chance to say goodbye."

"Or our chance," Isolde said, and her voice wobbled and steadied, "to say we'd rather you lived and the kingdom rotted. Which is what I vote, by the way. For the record. For whatever my record's worth—I'm a forger, my records are famously unreliable." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Wren had ever seen, brave and cracking at the corners. "One man's soul is worth more than a kingdom, Wren. Kingdoms come back. They're weeds. Souls don't."

"They do if they're spent well," Brannoc rumbled. "I kept dragons thirty years, girl. I watched Emberveil give her fire to warm a valley through the Long Frost, knowing it would take her a century to bank it again. Nobody called that waste. Some fires are *for* spending." He fixed Wren with eyes gone amber in the firelight, old keeper's eyes, eyes that had watched wonder die by inches for nine years. "But hear me: it must be *your* choice, freely spoken, same as the unbinding. Not duty. Not guilt. Not Sera's speeches or the boy's tears. If you go to that root-stone dragging a chain of oughts, the words will taste of it, and the high speech knows the difference. It always has."

Wren looked at them—all of them, this ragged, impossible company he had absolutely collected, God help him, without ever meaning to—and felt the self-deception he'd been carrying since the courier died finally split along its seam.

He had told himself he followed the map to trade it for his freedom. He had told himself it was obligation, leverage, an exit. Lies, all of them, and he saw that now with terrible clarity: he had come because for the first time in nine years, people had looked at him and seen not a legend and not a ghost but a man, and men, it turned out, owed things. Not to kingdoms. To *people*. To a boy who read shifting scripts. To a forger who burned her life's work as a warning. To a keeper who grieved a dragon like a daughter. To an oathbreaker choosing truth over law.

To a king he'd never met, hollowed out by other men's ambitions—which was, when he was honest, the story of Wren Voss's whole adult life.

"Here is my answer tonight," he said at last. "Not the final one. I'll not swear it here by firelight where it's easy; I'll swear it at the root-stone where it costs. But tonight I'll tell you the shape of it." He drew a breath. "I am going to speak the unbinding. And I am going to do it knowing full well what it costs, and I am going to let you all hate me for deciding, and then I am going to ask one favor."

"Name it," Sera said.

"When the curse comes for me—if it comes, if there's no loophole even I haven't found—" his voice caught, and he let it, because they'd earned his cracks along with his resolve, "—don't let the last thing I hear be arguing. Let it be something worth having heard. Sing, if anyone sings. Brannoc, tell me a story about Emberveil young. Pip—" his throat tightened, "—Pip, steal something of mine first, so I die a victim of crime like a respectable man."

Pip laughed, wet and startled. Isolde was crying openly and pretending it was the smoke. Brannoc bowed his head.

And it was Sera who went rigid first, her hand snapping up for silence, her eyes on the dark window.

"Wren," she breathed. "How long has the map been glowing?"

He turned. On the table, the dragon-skin had begun to shine—faintly, fitfully, a pulse of ember-light moving along the converging lines like a heartbeat in a vein, brighter with each pulse, all of it flowing toward the root-stone mark. Toward the deep places. Toward the vaults.

Toward Emberveil, whose one word through Brannoc's old bond had been *soon*.

The pulses quickened. Outside, far below the hills, so faint it might have been thunder or might have been grief, the earth itself seemed to shudder—and somewhere in the dark, the first bell of the distant capital began to ring.

Not midnight. Not noon.

Nine times.
