## 11. Midnight in the Seal-Forge

The seal-forge beneath the Tanners' Row tenement had not been used in three years, and it smelled of it—cold tallow, old smoke, and the ghost of a hundred lies pressed into wax. Isolde Vane lit the lamp with hands that did not shake, because they never did. Shaking was for amateurs and honest women.

She hung her coat on a nail that had held better coats, rolled her sleeves to the elbow, and looked at the workbench the way other women looked at altars.

"Right," she said to the empty room. "Let's commit treason beautifully."

* * *

The summons had to be perfect. That was the whole cruelty of it.

A forgery good enough to fool a clerk would get them all killed. A forgery good enough to fool a clerk and a captain would get them killed slightly later. What Wren needed—the company needed, the whole ragged, doomed enterprise needed—was a document that would survive the scrutiny of Chancellor Veyre herself, should Veyre's agents think to check. Which they would. Veyre checked everything. It was said she read her own correspondence twice, once for content and once for the possibility of betrayal in the margins.

Isolde had been forging seals since she was eleven years old, when she'd copied her father's merchant's mark to sign for bread during the famine winter and discovered that authority was mostly ink. Twenty-two years since. She had faked the writs of two archdukes, a papal indulgence, a marriage contract between houses so ancient their names were legally weapons, and—for one glorious fortnight in Marrowgate—a pardon from a king who turned out not to exist.

That last one still stung. She'd been proud of it.

But this. This was different. This was the Great Seal of the Ninefold Court itself: nine interlocking rings, each one cut with a phrase in the old court-hand, each phrase a bell-note rendered in metal. The true matrix sat on a chain around the Lord Chamberlain's neck and had not left his body since the king's coronation—or what everyone believed was his coronation, nine years ago, before everything under the crown went hollow.

Isolde couldn't copy it. No one could copy it; the rings were cut by a die-sinker who'd taken his method to his grave, and every attempt at reproduction came out wrong in ways that experts could smell.

So she wouldn't reproduce it.

She would *become* it, for one night, the way a forger becomes the hand she imitates—living inside the strokes until the strokes lived in her. She had spent four days in the palace archives, posing as an archivist's widow come to settle her late husband's accounts, memorizing the seal from the wax impressions in the ledger books. Four days of pressing her thumb against cooled wax while guards weren't looking, tracing ring after ring with her eyes closed until she could feel the phrases in her fingertips like braille. Nine rings. Nine bell-notes. And underneath them all, almost too fine to see, the sinker's secret signature: a thorn, worked into the eighth ring's inner curve, small as a grain of rice.

Everyone missed the thorn. Everyone always missed the thorn. That was why Isolde put thorns in her own work too—invisible ones, private jokes between her and the future. *I was here,* they said. *You just don't know it yet.*

Tonight the thorn would save their lives.

* * *

She began with the wax, because everything began with wax.

Not candle-wax—never that, it remembered its shape too eagerly—but the lac compound she mixed herself: damar resin, beeswax aged seven years in a sealed crock, vermilion ground fine enough to bleed light. The Court used a scarlet so deep it was nearly black, a red that seemed to have weight. She'd stolen a broken impression of it off a discarded writ two weeks ago and had been chewing on the problem ever since, in the way she chewed on everything, quietly and completely.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, through floorboards and decades of packed earth, the city of Ninefold Bells settled into its nightly ritual of pretending nothing was wrong. Isolde had catalogued that pretense with an archivist's precision over the past month. The bells rang nine times at dusk for a king who never aged, and no one in the wine-shops asked why the same toast was made every year to a reign that had stopped having anniversaries. The baker on Cordwainer Street gave the palace guard free rolls and never met their eyes. A woman beat a rug on her balcony each morning with the rhythm of someone trying not to listen to her own thoughts.

Fear, Isolde had learned, didn't look like fear. It looked like routine done very carefully.

"Careful," she murmured to the wax as it softened, "is my entire personality."

She poured. The lac spread across the warmed slate in a disc the size of her palm, and she let it cool to the precise moment—not measured by any clock but by the drag of the air against it, a feel she could not have taught and could not have unlearned—and then she took up her stylus and began.

Ring one. *By grace enduring.* The old court-hand was a brutal mistress; every letter leaned as though walking into wind, every terminal stroke tapered to a point fine as regret. Her stylus moved without hurry. Haste was how forgers died. She let the letters come the way she'd practiced them in the archive, in her head, in the steam on windows: *by grace, by grace, by grace enduring.*

Ring two. Ring three. The lamp flame bent and righted itself in some draft from the stairs. Sweat gathered at her hairline and she did not wipe it, because wiping meant pausing, and pausing meant thinking about what waited upstairs and outside and everywhere.

Because here was the truth she kept folded away like a letter she refused to open: if this failed—if the summons was detected, if the timing slipped, if any single one of forty moving pieces jammed—she would hang. Not the company. Her. Forgers were the Court's particular hatred. Thieves got fined, smugglers got the lash, even murderers sometimes got mercy if the victim had deserved it. But a forger touched the one thing the powerful truly worshipped, which was the appearance of legitimacy, and the Court repaid that blasphemy with rope and spectacle.

And yet.

And yet there was Brannoc down in the dark with a dying dragon chained to the roots of the world, saying *soon* in a voice gone to rust. There was Pip, sixteen and sharp-eyed, reading a map that rewrote itself, wearing a man's coat three sizes too big and a courage three sizes too big for that. There was Sera Quill, walking the palace halls in her inquisitor's gray with a war going on behind her face. There was Wren—

Wren, who had spent nine years being no one, and who was so obviously dying to be someone again that Isolde wanted to shake him, or feed him soup, or possibly both.

They had sat up two nights ago over cold tea while Pip snored in the corner, and Wren had said, in his careful, voice-starved way, "You could walk away. You've done nothing yet that can't be undone."

"And you have?" she'd said.

He hadn't answered. His silence had answered.

"I forged a pardon for a king who didn't exist," she told the cooling wax now, conversationally, the way she talked to her work when no one else would bear it. "Turns out I was early. This time I'm forging for a king who does exist, he's just been buried alive inside his own name." She set the first ring complete and admired it. "If that's not worth a rope, nothing is."

* * *

The summons itself was the easy part.

Court protocol was a language, and Isolde was fluent. The writ she composed—by lamplight, in the hand of Under-Secretary Maren Cotch, whose penmanship she had studied through a keyhole for an afternoon—ordered the Third and Seventh Watch companies to report to the Salt Gate at midnight tomorrow, there to receive sealed orders regarding "the matter of the western tolls." Deliberately dull. Deliberately bureaucratic. The genius of it, if it worked, lay in what it did rather than what it said: the Third and Seventh Watches were the companies garrisoned nearest the palace vaults. Pull them to the Salt Gate on a pretext, and the descent route Brannoc had mapped stood open for exactly the length of time a bored sergeant could be persuaded to hold his post awaiting orders that would never come.

She sanded the ink. She blotted it with linen, not paper—Cotch blotted with linen, she'd seen the cloth on his desk, monogrammed, the vain little man—and she set the writ aside to dry flat beneath a board.

Then she returned to the seal.

Rings four through six took an hour. Her wrist ached in the specific way it ached when the work was going *well*, when the line was coming through her rather than from her. Ring seven she ruined—a tremor, tiny, inexcusable—and she scraped the wax clean with a surgeon's patience and started the whole impression again from nothing, because a partial seal was a confession.

On the second attempt, ring eight arrived with its thorn.

There it was. Grain-of-rice small, tucked into the inner curve where only a magnifier and a guilty conscience would find it. Isolde allowed herself three seconds of pure professional joy, grinning at the lamp like a madwoman, before ring nine demanded her face back.

*By grace enduring, the nine bells speak as one.*

She pressed the final ring home and sat back.

It was beautiful. It was, she thought with no false modesty whatsoever, the finest work of her life—finer than the indulgence, finer than the marriage contract, finer even than the pardon for the king who hadn't existed. The wax caught the lamplight and threw it back deep red, like something arterial, like something alive. If Veyre held this in her clever white hands, she would believe it. Isolde was certain of it, and certainty in her trade was worth more than gold and rarer than honesty.

Which was, of course, when she heard the footsteps on the stairs.

* * *

Four sets. Heavy, unhurried, spaced like professionals who had done this before. They had doused the lamps in the tenement above an hour ago; the building slept, or pretended to. Nobody climbed to a disused cellar at midnight by accident.

Isolde moved.

Years of practice turned her body into a machine for disappearing work: the writ into the false bottom of her sewing basket, the styluses into their roll, the wax compounds into crocks that had held preserves in a former life. But the impression itself—she hesitated over it, half a heartbeat, the red disc gleaming on the slate like a wound.

She couldn't hide it in time. And she couldn't destroy it, either, because the whole plan died with it—the summons was useless without the seal to close it, and the seal took six hours, and dawn gave her perhaps five before the writ had to be in the Salt Gate courier's saddlebag.

The footsteps reached the landing. A pause. The soft metallic sound of a key being tried, finding the door already unlocked—because of course it was; they'd have picked it or bought the landlord, and resistance at the threshold only told them what to search for.

Isolde made her decision the way she made all her decisions: quickly, irreversibly, and with style.

She swept the seal-impression off the slate, crossed to the forge itself—the great iron hooded mouth that had eaten charcoal for a century—and fed it the shavings of pitchwood kindling she kept for exactly this contingency. Flint struck spark. Spark found pitch. Pitch found the old dry soot lining the forge's throat, and fire went up it with a roar like something waking.

The door opened as the flames peaked.

Three men and a woman filled the frame, dressed in the undyed wool and plain steel of Veyre's agents—the chancellor's quiet people, the ones who weren't inquisitors because inquisitors left records. Their leader was a lean man with a face like a closed ledger. Behind him, the woman's eyes went straight past Isolde to the burning forge, and narrowed.

"Madam," the leader said pleasantly, "you're working late."

"Deadlines," Isolde said.

"May we see what you're sealing?"

"Certainly." She smiled the smile that had gotten her out of nine arrests in four cities. "As soon as you show me your warrant. This is a licensed workshop under the Waxwrights' Charter, and the Charter is quite specific about unlawful entry. Article twelve. There are fines."

"There are," the leader agreed, stepping inside anyway, "and then there are chancellors."

Behind him, the woman had gone to the forge. She stood looking into the flames, and Isolde watched her watch them, and understood one second too late.

"The draft's strong," the woman said. "For a cold forge." She turned. In her gloved hand, lifted delicately from the ash lip of the firebox, was a curl of wax—vermilion-red, half-melted, but unmistakably bearing the ghost of a curved stroke. A scrap Isolde had trimmed from the ruined first impression hours ago and tossed, carelessly, into the cold ash instead of the slop pail.

One scrap. One stupid, tired, human mistake.

"Search her," the leader said.

They found the styluses. They found the wax crocks. They found the false bottom of the sewing basket, because the woman—thorough, patient, cursed thorough—ran her fingers along seams the way Isolde ran hers along seals, and the writ came out into the lamplight and lay there naked, Cotch's handwriting glowing with borrowed authority.

The leader read it twice. Once for content, Isolde thought hysterically. Once for the possibility of betrayal in the margins.

"Well," he said. "Chancellor Veyre will want to meet you."

"Will she?" Isolde said. "Tell her I'm busy. Tell her I have a deadline."

The blow, when it came, was almost respectful—quick, professional, placed to stun rather than break. The floor tilted. The lamp swam. The last thing Isolde saw clearly was her own forge-fire guttering in its iron throat, eating the last curls of wasted wax, and the last thing she thought, as the dark came up to meet her like a closing ledger, was:

*Nine rings. Six hours. They'll never make one in time.*

*Thorn and all.*

* * *

She woke to the smell of river water and the creak of a boat.

Her wrists were bound with cord—good cord, waxed, expensive; Veyre did not skimp—and a sack covered her head, but through its weave she could see lantern-glow and hear oars, many oars, rowing in the disciplined rhythm of the palace barge. Someone sat across from her. She felt the attention like heat.

"You burned your work," the woman's voice said. Almost admiring. "The impression in the forge. Why? We already had you. Burning it gained you nothing."

Isolde smiled into the sack, and heard the smile in her own voice, wry and unhurt and lying through its teeth.

"Habit," she said. "I never leave finished work where anyone can find it."

What she did not say—what she would carry out of this boat, out of whatever cell they built for her, out of anything short of the rope itself—was the arithmetic she'd done in the half-second before the door opened. The seal took six hours. They would never risk letting her near wax again. Therefore the seal was dead. Therefore the plan was dead—unless.

Unless the warning she'd sent by firelight, the forge roaring up through the chimney of the Tanners' Row tenement like a red banner against the night sky, had been read by the one person in the company who watched chimneys the way other people watched doors. Unless Pip, street-rat, map-reader, keeper of the company's escape routes, had seen the flame and understood the only message Isolde had time to send:

*Taken. Burn everything. Go without me.*

Unless, somewhere above the black water, the smallest member of her strange new family was already doing exactly that.

Isolde settled against the gunwale, wrists stinging, grin intact, and began—in the old habit of prisoners everywhere, and forgers most of all—to count.

One: they had the writ, which meant they knew about the Salt Gate, which meant Wren would change the route, which meant she'd cost them time but not blood. Two: Veyre would question her, and Isolde had spent twenty-two years learning how to answer questions, and answering questions badly was still answering questions. Three: the Court did not execute forgers quickly. It liked its spectacles. It liked announcements, and dates, and public squares.

Which meant, she thought as the barge slid toward the pale bulk of the palace, there would be a trial. And a sentence. And a day.

And on that day, in front of whatever crowd the Court could gather, Isolde Vane intended to be the most entertaining criminal the Ninefold Bells had hanged in a hundred years—and to use every word of her confession to tell the truth so loudly that even a hollow court would have to hear it.

The oars dipped. The palace grew. Somewhere far below it, in the dark, a dragon whispered *soon.*

"Soon," Isolde agreed softly, to no one, into the sack over her head. "Mind the shop till I'm back."
