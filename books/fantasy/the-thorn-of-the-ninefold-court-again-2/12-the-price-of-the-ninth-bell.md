## 12. The Price of the Ninth Bell

The news came to them the way bad news always came—carried by someone who did not want to be carrying it.

Wren found Pip in the cellar beneath the tallow-seller's shop, wedged between a cask of rancid grease and a wall of damp brick, turning the dragon-skin map over in her small hands as though she could bully it into honesty. The lamplight made her look younger than her years, and older than her size, both at once. She had that particular stillness she wore when she'd been reading the shifting script for too long—the squint of a girl arguing with ink.

"It's changed again," she said without looking up. "It does that when you're getting close, Brannoc says. Like it's nervous."

"Maps don't get nervous."

"This one sweats." She tilted it toward the lamp, and Wren saw what she meant: along the western margin, where three days ago there had been nothing but the faint topography of the vault-galleries, thin lines now wept across the skin like condensation on cold glass. They pooled. They gathered. And where they gathered, they wrote.

Pip read them aloud, slowly, the way she read everything—half pronunciation, half invention, trusting her instincts more than any grammar. "'The ninth bell tolls for no one. Let the old fire gutter. Regency endures.' " She frowned. "That's not very nice poetry."

"No," Wren said quietly. "It isn't."

He took the map from her—she surrendered it with a scowl but no real resistance; they had an understanding, he and Pip, built on the mutual recognition of people who survived by knowing when to hold on and when to let go—and held it closer to the flame. The words shimmered. Beneath them, fainter, in a different hand entirely, another line surfaced like something rising through dark water:

*Veyre knows the beast dies within the season. Veyre means to let it.*

He had suspected. He would have been a fool not to suspect; Chancellors did not spend nine years feeding a king's hollow shell with stolen fire out of devotion. But suspicion was a fog and this was a blade. He read the line three times, feeling each pass cut a little deeper, and then he set the map down on the cask with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to do something less careful.

"She's going to let Emberveil die," he said. "And when the fire fails, the false king fails with it. And when the king fails—" He heard his own voice go flat, the counting-house voice, the one that priced griefs and bottled storms. "She steps into the silence wearing mourning white, and the Court crowns her regent before the bells finish ringing. Ninefold bells for a dead king. Then silence. Then her."

Pip was quiet for a long moment. In the cellar's gloom, her face went through a sequence of expressions Wren recognized from his own mirror, years ago: horror first, then arithmetic, then a kind of furious clarity.

"The bells," she said suddenly. "The ninth bell. Everyone thinks it rings for the king, right? Nine times, nine courts, all that." She jabbed a finger at the map. "But it says *tolls for no one*. What if it doesn't ring for him? What if it rings for the dragon?"

Wren stared at her.

"I used to sleep in the bell-tower," Pip said, defensively, as though this explained everything. It rather did. "Before Isolde fished me out of that. You hear things up there. The ninth bell's got a crack in it—old crack, nobody repairs it because the ninth hour is mostly ceremonial. When the wind's wrong, it sounds like something crying." Her jaw set. "Somebody knew. Somebody put that crack there on purpose, or somebody heard the dragon through it. Either way, the bells have been telling the truth for nine years and everyone just calls it weather."

For a moment—for one unguarded moment—Wren felt something dangerous move in his chest. Not hope exactly. Something adjacent to hope: the realization that he was standing in a damp cellar arguing about bell-cracks with a pickpocket, and that he cared, fiercely, stupidly, about what happened next to all of them. Belonging, he had learned, arrived exactly when you weren't guarding against it.

"We take this to the others," he said.

* * *

Brannoc took the news the way stone takes rain—he absorbed it without visible change, and only afterward did you understand how much had soaked in. He sat in the loft above the cooper's yard with his huge hands flat on his knees, staring at nothing, and when he finally spoke his voice was rough gravel.

"She'll be weaker now than she's ever been. Siphoned near dry, chained in the dark, and now they mean to simply... stop feeding the siphon. Let her burn out like a lamp." His hands curled slowly into fists. "I kept dragons twenty-two years. Do you know what a dragon's death is like, when it's starved instead of fought? It's slow. It's *quiet*. She'll feel herself going out for weeks, and she won't be able to even make a light to see it by."

Isolde, at the worktable with her gravers and her powders, did not look up from the seal blank she was cutting. That was how Wren knew she was angriest: Isolde worked faster when she was angry, her tools moving with a small vicious precision.

"So we accelerate," she said. "If Veyre's schedule is *let the fire die*, then our schedule is *get there first*. The root-stone. The unbinding. We stop asking whether we're ready and we go."

"And if we go too soon?" Sera stood apart, as she always stood, half in shadow, her inquisitor's cloak folded away but its absence somehow still visible on her. "Veyre has the whole Court behind her. If we strike before we've drawn the guards off, we die in the galleries, and Emberveil dies anyway, and Wren spends his voice for nothing." She said it plainly, without cruelty, which was worse. Sera never wasted cruelty on true things.

"Then we draw them off properly," Isolde said. Still not looking up. "I can forge anything, gentlemen and lady. A summons. A writ. Something with enough royal weight on it to pull every oathbreaker in the palace down into the administrative warrens chasing their own tails. Give me two days and access to a courier's satchel, and I'll give you an empty palace."

"You forged a summons once before," Sera said. "You were caught."

Isolde's graver paused—one heartbeat—then resumed. "I was *young*," she said, "and I signed my own flourishes. I've had eleven years to practice humility." A pause. "And better wax."

Wren watched them circle it—the plan taking shape between them like a fire being built stick by stick—and felt again that unfamiliar weight, the weight of being trusted with other people's lives by people who had chosen him. Brannoc's grief, Isolde's fury, Sera's grim duty, Pip's cracked-bell epiphanies. None of it was his to command. All of it was his to answer for.

"Pip," he said.

She looked up.

"You know the palace better than any of us. Better than Sera, even—she served the law; you served the rooftops." He kept his voice level, though everything in him objected to the sentence he was building. "I need eyes inside. Where Veyre keeps her private correspondence. Whether the regency paperwork already exists—I'd bet the Guild's entire ledger it does, sealed and waiting, dated ahead. If we can find it, we can prove what she intends. We can break the Court's belief before we ever break the binding."

"That's scouting," Pip said. "That's my favorite word."

"It's also the most dangerous thing anyone in this company has done yet."

"I've been caught before," Pip said, with the breezy confidence of someone describing weather in a country she'd left. "Twice. I got away twice."

"And the third time?" Sera asked quietly.

Pip grinned at her, all elbows and bravado. "There's never been a third time."

Wren looked at her—that small, wiry, impossible girl, thirteen years old and armored in nerve—and every rational part of him said *no*, and every part of him that had spent nine years alone said *she'll do it whether you ask or not, better she goes knowing you believe her*.

"Third bell tomorrow night," he said. "In and out before the watch changes. If you're not back by dawn, we assume the worst and move without you."

"If I'm not back by dawn," Pip said, "you'd better move fast."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin over the capital, and Pip did not come back.

They waited in the loft while the city woke beneath them—the bells ringing their morning eight, the ninth silent until its hour, the streets filling with smoke and carts and the great unseeing traffic of ordinary life. Wren stood at the window with the map against his chest and counted minutes the way he had once counted debts, and the arithmetic kept coming out wrong. Brannoc paced. Isolde sharpened a graver she did not intend to use as a weapon. Sera stood motionless by the door with her hand resting, very lightly, on the hilt she no longer carried.

At the ninth hour, far off, the cracked bell tolled—nine strokes, the last one wavering, bending downward like a sound in pain. Nobody spoke through it. When it finished, Wren turned from the window.

"She's in Veyre's custody or Veyre's cells. Those are the only two places in that palace that keep people overnight."

"Then we get her out," Brannoc said.

"With what? Four of us against a garrison?" Isolde snapped—and then stopped, because anger at the situation was not anger at each other, and they had all learned the difference, barely, recently.

Sera spoke from the doorway. "We wait. Veyre will want something from her—a name, a confession, a thread leading back to us. Interrogation takes time. Pip has..." A hesitation; even now, Sera weighed her words like coin. "Pip is good at giving people nothing. I've seen her do it."

"She's thirteen," Wren said.

"Yes," Sera said. "And she chose this. Which means what she gave up, she gave up freely, and what we owe her, we owe completely." Her eyes met Wren's, and for a moment the inquisitor and the swordspeaker looked at each other across the gulf of their separate silences. "Get me into the palace tonight, and I'll find out where they're holding her. My face still opens doors. Some doors."

It was Isolde who brought the news, hours later, breathless, a street-child's coin pressed into her palm as payment for information she hadn't even had to buy—the child had been bursting to tell someone. Word ran through the lower city the way word always ran: the Court had announced an execution.

"Public," Isolde said. "Day after tomorrow. Bell-tower square. A thief, they're calling her. Caught spying in the Chancellor's wing. They're making an example." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, Wren could see her hating it. "They're making an example of a *child*, to flush out whoever sent her."

The loft went very still.

"They think she's ours," Sera said at last. "They're right."

"She didn't give us up," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question, but Isolde shook her head anyway.

"The child I paid says the Chancellor's men questioned her a full day. Whatever they asked, whatever they threatened—" Isolde swallowed. "The charge is theft. Just theft. No conspiracy. No accomplices. They have *nothing* from her."

Wren closed his eyes. He saw it plainly: the small figure in some stone room, knees hugged to chest, facing down grown men with instruments and authority, handing them a name—some invented urchin, some ghost with a fake history, *Tally, m'lord, everyone calls me Tally*—and holding that lie steady under pressure that would have broken soldiers. Giving up nothing but a false name. Spending her fear like currency, buying their secrets with her own terror, and paying, apparently, without flinching.

When he opened his eyes, the fear in him had changed. It had not gone away—fear never went away, it only transformed, and he had learned to recognize its alchemy. It had sharpened. It had become the thing he had sworn nine years ago he would never let himself become again: a man with something worth burning for.

"Change of plan," he said. "No summons, no forgery, no drawing guards off to the warrens. Veyre wants an audience in that square. She wants us watching. So we give her an audience—but not the one she expects." He looked around the loft, at the dragon-keeper with his ruined hands, the forger with her gravers, the oathbreaker with her borrowed conscience. "Two days. Isolde, I need you to forge the boldest thing you've ever made. Sera, I need your face and your rank. Brannoc, I need you below, ready to open the vaults the moment the square empties."

"And you?" Isolde asked.

Wren touched the map against his chest, and thought of the ninth bell tolling for no one, and of a girl in a cell giving away nothing but a lie, and of a voice he had buried alive for nine years waiting in his throat like a debt come due.

"Me," he said, "I'm going to stand in that square and remember why I broke my silence once already. Because the next time I speak, I'm speaking for her."

Far below, in the tower, the cracked ninth bell hung silent in the gray air, waiting—as they all were—for the hour that would test what it was truly ringing for.
