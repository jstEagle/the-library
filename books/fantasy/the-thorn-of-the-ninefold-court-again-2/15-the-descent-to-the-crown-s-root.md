## 15. The Descent to the Crown's Root

Brannoc had kept dragons for thirty years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: fire was not a weapon. It was a promise. Every hearth he had ever lit in the vaults of Emberveil's keeping had been a small covenant—*you are warm; you are watched; you are not alone.* The Guild had taken his rank and his name and his honor, but they had never managed to take that out of him. He still thought in covenants.

So when Wren said *we go down*, Brannoc did not argue. He went to the old keeper's door at the edge of the dragon-vaults—the one sealed with wax stamped by a guildmaster three chancellors dead—and he broke it with a crowbar and an apology, and he lit no lamp, because he did not need one.

"You've walked these galleries before," Isolde said behind him, her voice pitched low as though the stone itself might inform on them.

"Walked them? Girl, I *slept* here." Brannoc set his shoulder against the rusted gate and pushed. It groaned open onto a throat of descending stairs, cut wide enough for a dragon's shoulders, worn smooth by centuries of scale and claw. "Emberveil would curl along the third gallery of a winter night, and I'd sleep in the crook of her foreleg like a fool boy. Warmest bed in the Ninefold Court." His voice caught on the last word. He cleared his throat and went on down. "Coldest now, I'd wager."

The stairs swallowed them.

Behind came Pip, quick-footed and quiet, and behind Pip came Sera Quill in her gray traveling leathers, oathbreakers' insignia scraped off but the habits of a hunter still written into every step—weight on the balls of her feet, eyes moving in sweeps, hand never far from her hip. And last, Wren, with the dragon-skin map folded against his chest like a second heart.

The dark closed over them the way water closes over a dropped stone.

* * *

By the fifth turning of the galleries, even Sera had stopped pretending she could see. They moved by touch and by Brannoc's memory: left at the pillar carved with the nine bells, right where the wall sweated cold iron, down again through a corridor whose ceiling lowered until Isolde had to walk bent double, cursing in four languages, two of them invented.

"The map wants us lower," Wren said. In the black, his voice sounded closer than it was, intimate as a confession. "It's pulling toward the root-stone. I can feel it through my coat."

"It can feel *you*, more like," Brannoc muttered. Dragon-skin was not parchment. It remembered. It had been alive once, and some stubborn ember of that life remained, and Brannoc had seen it turn toward heat the way a flower turns toward sun—and Wren Voss, whether he knew it or not, carried heat of his own. Not flame. Something older. A voice, banked and buried, burning in its own sealed room.

They were deep enough now that the air changed. Brannoc felt it before he named it: the particular dry warmth that rose from the vaults' lowest reaches, carrying a scent like struck flint and old copper. Emberveil's scent, thinned almost to nothing, but present. Alive.

"She's breathing," he said, and stopped so suddenly that Isolde walked into his back. "Do you feel it? The air's moving. Down there—she's still breathing."

Pip's small voice came out of the darkness, thin with awe or fear or both. "How far?"

"Not far. Two galleries past the sump, maybe three. But—" Brannoc hesitated. This was the part he had not said aloud, because saying it made it real. "But the sump is where the chains run. Where they tap her. There'll be watchers. Or there'll be worse."

"Worse," Sera repeated flatly.

"The tappers don't like being seen." He resumed walking, hand on the wall. "Veyre keeps a few of your old colleagues down here, Quill. Oathbreakers who'd rather guard a dying dragon than answer questions upstairs. Men who've been paid too long in silence to ever want daylight again."

"Then we go around," Sera said.

"There is no around. There's only through, or there's only back."

Silence followed, the heavy kind. Then Wren's voice, quiet and certain: "Through."

* * *

They heard the sump before they saw it—a vast wet echoing space where water dripped in slow metronome from some unseen height, and beneath the dripping, a sound Brannoc had prayed he would never hear again: the rhythmic, grinding pull of a chain-tap, drawing fire out of a living creature one mouthful at a time.

He found the last stub of a torch in an alcove where keepers had once stored spares, and he did not light it from flint. He crouched, put his palm flat against the stone floor, and did the thing he had sworn off doing nine years ago when they stripped his office and his name.

He listened for her.

Not with ears. With the bond—the old, broken, forbidden thread that had been burned into him the day he first laid hands on a hatchling's flank and been *accepted*. The Guild had declared such bonds illegal a generation ago; they called them slavery now, which was a fine word for men who chained what they could not bond. Brannoc's thread was frayed to a single fiber. But a single fiber, pulled taut in the dark, will sing.

*Emberveil,* he thought, not words so much as warmth shaped into meaning. *Old girl. Old girl, it's Brannoc.*

Nothing. Then—not nothing. A tremor along the fiber, faint as heat shimmer. Pain, vast and slow, like a mountain becoming aware of an itch. And beneath the pain, coiled so deep he nearly missed it, a single coal of attention turned toward him, the way a dying fire turns its last light toward the face above it.

*I'm going to need a light,* he told her. *Just a thread. Just enough to see our way. I know what they take from you every day, and I know I have no right to ask, and I'm asking anyway.*

The fiber sang.

Heat bloomed under his palm—not much, a coin-sized warmth, then a palmful, then a thread of true orange light rising out of the stone seam like water out of a spring, climbing his wrist, wrapping his hand, settling into the old torch-stub until it caught with a sound like a sigh. Flame stood up off the pitch, gold-edged and steady, and it was not ordinary fire. Ordinary fire flickered. This *breathed*.

Isolde made a small strangled noise. Pip stared. Even Sera, who had hunted monsters for the Court and claimed to be past wonder, took half a step backward.

"That's—" Wren began.

"That's her fire," Brannoc said. His voice cracked and he let it. "A thread of it. She gave it. Do you understand? Chained in the dark, tapped hollow for nine years, and when an old fool asks for a light, she *gives*." He lifted the torch, and the flame leaned toward him like a living thing leaning into a familiar hand. "That's not a weapon, any of you who think it is. That's a promise. She promised me we'd get there."

Wren looked at the flame for a long moment, and something moved across his face that Brannoc couldn't read—recognition, perhaps, of another kind of gift given against all sense. "Then we don't waste it," Wren said. "Forward."

* * *

The sump opened before them like the inside of a bell.

Brannoc raised the torch and wished he hadn't. The chamber was a cathedral of scaffolds and pipes, brass conduits running in braided rivers from the chamber's center to shafts ascending toward the palace above—toward the throne, toward the thing wearing the king's crown, feeding it mouthful after stolen mouthful. And at the center, half-drowned in shadow and shallow black water—

Chains. Dozens of them, thick as masts, running to collars the size of wagon wheels. And within the collars, dim as a banked hearth, the shape of a dragon.

She was smaller than Brannoc remembered. That was the horror of it—not death, which would have been clean, but *shrinkage*, the slow tax of nine years' theft. Her scales, once the color of sunset hammered into armor, had gone the grey-brown of old ash. Her ribs showed beneath the dull hide like the staves of a barrel. But her eye—one eye, catching the thread of borrowed flame—opened.

And looked at him.

Thirty years fell away. Brannoc's knees nearly went.

"Hold," Sera breathed, hand out. "There—movement. Left gallery. Torches."

And there they were: four lights swinging down the far stair, and the clink of mail, and voices—oathbreaker voices, bored and hard, coming to check the taps as they must do on some schedule known only to themselves. Coming straight toward the one gallery passage that offered cover, which meant coming straight toward five fugitives standing in open floor holding a torch that burned like a piece of sunrise.

"Snuff it?" Isolde whispered.

"If I snuff it, she gave it for nothing," Brannoc said. His grip tightened on the haft. "And I don't waste her promises."

"Then we don't fight in the open," Sera said, already moving, hunter's mind laying the ground out loud. "Pillars, two rows. We let them pass the first row, we take the last man quiet, and we're in the south passage before they know—"

"No."

The voice was Wren's. Brannoc turned. Wren was staring at the torch—at the flame—with an expression Brannoc had last seen on the faces of men reading their own death warrants. But he wasn't looking at the flame, Brannoc realized. He was looking *through* it, down the thread of fire to the dragon herself, and Emberveil's ancient eye was looking back.

"She's telling me something," Wren said softly. "Through the skin. Through the map. She's been trying to tell me since the courier died." His hand went to his chest, to the folded dragon-skin. "The root-stone isn't just buried under the Court. It's buried under *her*. The king's name, the binding, all of it—it's anchored through her chains. She isn't just their prisoner. She's the lock."

Above them, faint through miles of stone, the bells began to ring.

Once. Twice. Three times.

Four.

Five.

Six.

Seven.

Eight.

Nine.

And in the water at the center of the sump, in the grey ruin of the last dragon of the Ninefold Court, something answered the ninth bell—a pulse of ember-light that ran the length of her body like lightning running a wire, and every chain in the chamber shivered, and every pipe sang a low moaning note, and the oathbreakers' torches stopped moving.

Stopped. And began, slowly, to turn. Toward the south passage. Toward the dark. Toward them.

"They heard it," Sera whispered. "Saints help us, they heard it too—and they're coming to see."
