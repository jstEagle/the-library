## 16. Betrayal in the Deep Galleries

Pip counted exits the way other people counted coins—compulsively, constantly, and with a running total in his head that never quite balanced to zero.

There were four in this stretch of gallery, by his reckoning: the way they'd come, a crawl-space behind a fallen pillar no grown person could fit through but he could, a fissure in the eastern wall that breathed warm air like something sleeping, and the dark mouth ahead where the torchlight simply gave up. Four exits and seven of them alive, if you counted the guide.

Pip had been counting Hask since the salt road. Not because Hask had done anything wrong. Because Hask had done everything *right*, which was worse. A man who knew the deep galleries too well, who never stumbled on the uneven steps, who always walked three paces back from the front and two paces from the rear—the exact middle of any marching order, the spot where you were hardest to grab first and easiest to slip away from last. Pip knew that walk. He'd worn it himself in three cities and two border towns, back when his legs were shorter and his name was different.

So when the ambush came, Pip was not surprised. He was only early.

* * *

It began, as most bad things did, with Brannoc's fire-thread guttering.

The old dragon-keeper carried a splinter of Emberveil's flame cupped in an iron lantern-cage, and it lit the galleries not like a torch but like a memory of sunlight—gold shot through with red, throwing shadows that moved even when the holders didn't. It had kept them company for two days now, down past the flooded cisterns, past the ossuary stairs where the bones of the Court's founders lay sorted into neat centuries, past carvings so old that dragons stood upright in them and men knelt.

"Close now," Brannoc murmured, for perhaps the hundredth time. His big hand rested on the cage like a man touching a sleeping child. "She's deeper than the maps say. They dug *down* to hide her."

"They dug down to hide everything," Sera said. She walked at the front with her oathbreaker's blade drawn—not because she expected enemies, Pip thought, but because she couldn't stand the feeling of walking unarmed in a place where she'd once hunted men like herself. "Every sin this Court ever committed is under our feet. The capital isn't built on stone. It's built on shame."

Wren said nothing. Wren had been saying nothing since the root-stone rite was revealed, nothing since Isolde was taken, nothing since the word *soon* had come up out of Emberveil's prison through Brannoc's old bond and lodged in all of them like a fishhook. But his silence had changed quality lately. Before, it had been the silence of a man hiding. Now it was the silence of a whetstone.

Hask walked in the middle. Hask always walked in the middle.

They came to the Gallery of Nine Arches—a long vaulted hall where the pillars had been carved as pillars only at the top; below, they widened into the bodies of kneeling figures, faces worn smooth by time or deliberately defaced. The fire-light made their blank faces seem to watch. Pip hated this hall. He hated how sound behaved in it: every footstep arriving twice, once from your boot and once from somewhere behind you, half a heartbeat late.

He was counting the arches—one, two, three, four—when the fifth arch exhaled.

Not wind. Not water. A *whistle*, thin and high, pitched below hearing for anyone whose ears hadn't been tuned by ten years of listening for watchmen's signals over rooftops. Pip knew that whistle. Three rising notes and a fall. *Position taken. Come ahead.*

His blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the vaults' chill.

"Hask," he said. Just the name. Loud enough.

And then the dark mouths of the arches filled with men.

They came down on ropes, some of them—oathbreakers in grey lacquered armor, dropping from the gallery's upper works where they must have waited hours in the cold—and others simply stepped out of side passages that Pip would have sworn an oath on his own stolen silver didn't exist. Eight of them. Ten. Twelve. More figures in the dark beyond the torchreach, holding still, holding the exits. Holding the four exits Pip had counted, plus a fifth he'd missed, which stung almost more than the blades did.

"Ambush" was too clean a word. This wasn't an ambush. This was a harvest.

"*Down!*" Sera roared, and then the world became noise.

Pip did what small people do when large violence happens around them: he got low, and he got gone. He rolled beneath the sweep of the first blade—he felt its wind part his hair, smelled the oil on it—and came up sprinting along the base of the third pillar, into the shadow between the kneeling figure's carved knees. Above him the fight opened like a storm: Brannoc roaring and swinging the iron cage itself as a weapon, fire-sparks scattering; Sera meeting two of her former brothers-in-oath blade to blade, shouting words Pip couldn't hear over the clangor; Wren—Wren moving like something out of the old stories, silent and terrible, turning aside a sword with a bare hand and putting its owner down without ever seeming to hurry.

But there were too many. There had always been going to be too many; that was what the whistle meant. *Come ahead*—come ahead to the killing ground, come ahead to the place chosen for you before you chose it yourself.

Pip saw Hask in that chaos. Saw him pressed against the fourth pillar with his hands raised, screaming—actually screaming, eyes wide and wet—"I told you the middle route! I told you, I gave you exactly what was paid for, this isn't—" until an oathbreaker cuffed him to silence and dragged him toward the shadows, not gently but not lethally either. A paid guide kept alive for questioning. A bought knife kept sharp.

*I told you,* Pip thought, wedged in the dark under a dead king's carved elbow. *You told them everything.*

Because that was the thing about being small and quick and generally assumed to be worthless: people planned around you, not for you. Veyre's agents had bought their route from Hask, and Hask had sold the whole shape of the company—numbers, strengths, the fire-lantern, the descent order—but nobody selling a route bothers to sell the pickpocket. The pickpocket was scenery. The pickpocket was a joke Brannoc told about rations going missing. The pickpocket was *presumed*.

Good, Pip thought, with a savagery that surprised him. Let them presume.

* * *

The company broke the way a dropped plate breaks—in pieces, all at once, none of them where you wanted.

Sera went down fighting at the sixth arch, not defeated but *buried*, three blades forcing hers wide while a fourth wrapped a chain-net over her shoulders. Pip heard her snarl "the root-stone, go, GO" before they gagged her, and heard it answered by the worst sound of the night: Wren's voice, raw and human and furious, cut off mid-word as they took him from behind. Not killed. Taken. Everything about the ambush said *taken*—Veyre wanted them breathing, at least the ones who mattered. A dead swordspeaker was a martyr. A live one was leverage.

Brannoc refused to be taken. That much Pip witnessed clearly, from his hiding place: the old keeper set the fire-cage down on the gallery floor, kissed his own scarred knuckles like a farmer blessing a field, and swung the lantern high so the flame-splinter flared—and in that flare of borrowed daylight, gold and red and impossibly warm, six oathbreakers recoiled as if struck. In the confusion Brannoc waded into the eastern dark, toward the fissure, toward Emberveil's depth, buying escape with exposure. They chased him. Most of them chased him.

Which left the gallery nearly empty, and left the keys unguarded.

Nearly.

Two men remained at the western passage—the way the company had come, now the way *out*—one holding a ring of iron keys heavy enough that Pip could hear them clink from twenty feet away, the other standing watch over the prisoners' gear, hastily piled: Wren's sword-belt, Sera's badge of office torn off and tossed down like trash, the map-case. The map-case. Pip's stomach turned over. If the map stayed here, everything—Isolde's sacrifice, the courier's death, all of it—ended in this gallery, in the dark, unpaid.

The two guards talked in low voices. Pip crept close enough to listen, moving the way he'd learned to move across warehouse roofs: weight on the outside edges of his feet, breath timed to theirs, hands ready.

"—says bring the map up personal. Chancellor's orders."

"And the small one?"

"What small one?"

"The kid. The thief."

A laugh, ugly and relaxed. "What about him? Hask never mentioned a kid worth keys. Probably ran at the first flash of steel. They always run." The man nudged the map-case with his boot. "Kid's somebody else's problem. Or nobody's. Down here, doesn't matter much either way."

Pip sat down in the dark between the kneeling king's carved feet, hugged his knees, and let himself shake for exactly ten heartbeats. He counted them. Ten was the rule; after ten, the shaking stopped being fear and started being permission to feel sorry for yourself, and there was no time for that. At eleven he wiped his face on his sleeve, and at twelve he was already planning.

The keys hung on the talker's belt. The map-case sat between the watcher's boots. Two men, one exit each of them facing away from the other, both of them looking forward into the gallery because who comes *back*? Cowards flee. Cowards don't double back into the killing ground alone.

Cowards don't, Pip agreed silently. Good thing I'm not one. Good thing nobody's ever been able to figure out what I am.

He needed light out and noise elsewhere. He had neither a lamp nor a distraction—or rather, he had only what a pickpocket carries: three knives, a coil of waxed cord, a bent nail, someone's seal-ring he'd lifted off Isolde's workbench weeks ago as a keepsake and had never given back, and a strip of dried mutton he'd been saving against hunger. He looked at the mutton. He looked at the dark fissure in the eastern wall, breathing its slow warm animal breath.

"Sorry," he whispered—to the mutton, or to whatever lived in the wall; he honestly wasn't sure which.

* * *

He went up. That was the trick nobody watched for, because guides don't climb and soldiers don't look up in underground halls with no visible ceiling access—but Pip had spent nine years of his short life learning that every room has a second floor if you're willing to be uncomfortable about reaching it. The kneeling figures' shoulders made ledges. Their defaced heads made handholds. He climbed the third pillar like a spider, wedge-nail out, cord looped twice around his wrist, and flattened himself into the joinery above the fifth arch where ancient masons had left a gap between vault-ribs wide enough for a boy, a cat, or a very determined regret.

Below, the warm-breathing fissure delivered what he'd gambled on.

Something lived in the walls of the deep galleries—something small and blind and hungry, pale as a maggot and the size of a dog, a vault-worm of the kind Brannoc had described around the campfire with the fond disgust of a man who'd fed them in another life. It smelled the mutton. It came out of the fissure in a slithering rush, took the meat, and—startled by the sudden clatter of the oilcloth wrapping—panicked. A panicked vault-worm does one thing: it flees toward warmth and noise, and the nearest warmth and noise was two bored guards at the western passage.

The scream that followed was extremely satisfying.

"Worm! Worm, it's a worm, kill it, KILL IT—" The talker drew his blade and hacked at a creature the size of a hound that was, to be fair, trying to burrow up his trouser leg. The watcher spun to help, boots scuffing, and for three full seconds both men were occupied with pale thrashing horror and their own dignity.

Pip dropped.

He came off the pillar in a fall he'd rehearsed against haycarts and awnings and once, memorably, a bishop's canopy bed, landing in a roll that put him directly beside the map-case. Snatch. Cord. Roll. He was up and moving before the worm-scream finished echoing, and he took the key-ring on the way past—not lifted, *taken*, a hard yank that snapped the belt-loop and left the talker holding half his belt and no trousers' dignity at all.

"THE KID!" someone roared behind him. "IT'S THE KID!"

Well, Pip thought, sprinting into the western passage with the case hugged to his chest and forty keys jangling a song of betrayal against his ribs. *Now* they remember me.

Boots thundered. A crossbow bolt sparked off stone a finger's width from his ear. Pip ran the way he'd learned to run from watchmen in Cordova's Warrens—never straight, always turning, using his size to take corners that bigger pursuers had to slow for. Left at the cracked pillar. Right through the gap between fallen stones that only he could fit through. Behind him, curses, the crash of a man trying to follow and getting stuck, the flat barked orders of someone organizing a search pattern—which meant officers, which meant Veyre's own, which meant he couldn't stop, not ever, not until—

Until he heard it.

Through the stone, faint, rising from far below: a sound like a forge exhaling. Like a banked fire remembering it had once been a sun. And threaded through it, so quiet Pip might have invented it, a vibration in the rock that his bones understood before his ears did—a single syllable repeated, patient as geology:

*Soon. Soon. Soon.*

Brannoc had reached her. Or she had reached for Brannoc. Either way, the deepest thing in the world was waking, and every oathbreaker in these galleries had just felt it too. Pip heard the pursuit falter behind him, heard fear reorganize the search, heard men who had hunted a boy begin instead to argue about whether to run upward or downward.

In that unraveling, in that crack of doubt running through the ambush like a fault-line, Pip slipped sideways into a forgotten stair—a maintenance spiral, choked with dust, marked with the old sigil of the dragon-keepers that Brannoc had taught them to watch for—and he stopped. He caught his breath. He held the map-case and the key-ring against his chest, and he laughed, once, a small broken sound that was mostly terror and mostly triumph and entirely his.

Isolde would have grinned. Sera would have said something dry. Brannoc would have clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to bruise. Wren would have looked at him—really looked, the way almost nobody ever bothered to—and nodded.

"Presumed," Pip told the darkness, wiping his nose. "You all presumed."

Then he turned his face upward, toward the distant gray suggestion of the surface, toward the capital where his friends were chained and a false regent was consolidating her theft—and began to climb, keys whispering against his chest like a promise.

Somewhere below, the dragon said *soon* again.

"Sooner than you think," Pip answered, and climbed faster.
