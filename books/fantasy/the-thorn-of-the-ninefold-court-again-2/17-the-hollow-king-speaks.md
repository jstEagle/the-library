## 17. The Hollow King Speaks

The root-stone did not look like the heart of a kingdom.

It looked like a grave that had been forgotten on purpose.

Wren stood at the edge of the chamber and let his eyes adjust to the light Brannoc carried — a single thread of Emberveil's fire coiled around his fist like a captive ember, casting everything in a low orange that made the walls seem to breathe. The gallery they had descended through narrowed here into a vaulted hollow no larger than a chapel, its ceiling lost in shadow above, its floor worn smooth as river stone by centuries of feet that had never come this way again. And at its center, half-sunken into the living rock like a tooth in an old jaw, stood the root-stone itself: a pillar of black glass shot through with veins of dull gold, tall as two men, humming with a sound Wren felt in his back teeth rather than heard.

*That hum,* he thought. *I know that hum.*

He had known it nine years ago, in another life, standing in a counting-house while his master explained that all oaths were ledgers and all ledgers were chains. The sound of something bound. The sound of a debt accruing interest in the dark.

"You feel it too," Brannoc said quietly. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with keeper's scars up both forearms — old burns in the shape of claw-talons, earned in better days when dragons were tended rather than harvested. His voice had the careful hush of a man in a sickroom. "She used to sing through stone like this. When she was happy." His jaw worked. "This isn't happy."

Sera stood at Wren's left shoulder, her oathbreaker's blade still drawn but pointed at the floor, which from Sera was practically a gesture of surrender. Pip crouched near the entrance with one hand flat against the wall, listening to it the way other people listened to doors. Isolde was not there; Isolde was in a cell beneath the bell-tower, or dead, and every step Wren had taken since learning of her capture had been a step taken through cold water.

"Is this where the name is buried?" Sera asked.

"It's where the name is *kept*," Wren said. "There's a difference. A buried thing wants to stay buried. A kept thing—" He stopped. The hum shifted under his words, subtle as a sleeping animal turning over. "A kept thing is waiting for someone to open the door."

Pip looked up at him. In the ember-light the boy's face was all sharp angles and grime, but his eyes were wide and unguarded in a way they almost never were. "Then why's nobody opened it? Nine years. Nine years of bells and parades and everyone kissing the ground the old man walks on. If the whole kingdom's been kneeling to a lie, why hasn't anyone come down here and let the truth out?"

Wren had asked himself versions of that question since the night a dying courier bled out against his door. He had found answers in erased ledgers and forged seals and the confessions of dead men. But standing here, with the root-stone humming against his bones, he understood that he had been asking the wrong question entirely.

The right question was not *why has no one freed the king.*

The right question was *what is keeping the king?*

And the root-stone answered him.

Not with words — not at first. With light. The veins of gold threading the black glass began to brighten, slowly, the way embers wake when breathed upon, and the hum climbed into something almost melodic, almost like a voice clearing its throat after years of disuse. Brannoc stumbled backward. Sera's blade came up. Pip scrambled behind a fallen slab of masonry with the instinctive speed of someone who had survived a great many things by being small and quick and elsewhere.

But Wren did not move.

Because rising out of the stone — unfolding from it the way smoke unfolds from a snuffed candle, unhurried, sorrowful — came a figure.

It wore the shape of a king.

Not the king of the Ninefold Bells, not the ageless gilded figure who smiled down from coins and banners and nine ringing towers. This was older and wearier: a tall man in robes whose embroidery had faded to ghosts of themselves, with a beard gone grey at the edges and hands that hung at his sides like a man who had long ago forgotten what hands were for. His face was Alderic's face — Wren knew it from the portrait hidden in the Guild's forbidden vaults, the true portrait, the one struck from record — but rendered as if by a painter working from memory across a great distance. The details were right and the life was wrong. His eyes were the color of a winter sky at dusk, and they held nothing behind them except a kind of vast, patient exhaustion.

"There you are," the echo said. Its voice was soft. It was the worst thing about it — that gentleness. "There you are. I wondered which of you would come."

Sera's knuckles went white on her hilt. "In the name of the Court—"

"Don't," the echo said, and smiled, and the smile broke something in Wren's chest, because it was a *tired* smile, an apologetic one, the smile of a guest who knows he has overstayed. "Don't name me to myself. I have my own name, and it has been in a stone for nine years, and I would very much like to hear it spoken before whatever happens next happens. But not by her servants. Not by the ones with the red cords." His gaze slid to Sera's wrist, to the oathbreaker's band she still wore beneath her sleeve, and Sera flinched as though struck. "No offense meant, child. You didn't choose the cord any more than I chose the crown."

"What are you?" Brannoc demanded. His captive flame guttered and steadied. "Are you the king? Are you the thing wearing the king?"

"I am the thing wearing the *name*," the echo said. "The distinction matters. It has mattered for nine years, and no one has ever asked, so forgive me if I take a moment to enjoy being asked." He turned, slowly, and Wren saw now how the figure's lower body did not quite end — how it trailed off into threads of gold light that ran down into the root-stone like roots into soil, or like a marionette's strings seen from below. Anchored. Tethered. Bound.

His eyes found Wren's, and held.

"You're the swordspeaker," he said. "You're Voss's boy."

The name landed in Wren like a dropped stone down a well. He had spent nine years making sure no one would say it. *Master Corvane*, he thought. *You knew my master. Of course you did. Everyone who matters in this rotten story knew my master.*

"I'm no one's boy," Wren said. "I'm a ledger clerk."

The echo laughed — a small, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering motion. "Yes. That's exactly what he'd have wanted you to say. Corvane always did believe invisibility was the only honest craft left." The laughter faded. The gold threads dimmed, and the echo swayed slightly, as if standing were a labor. "Listen to me. We don't have long. She'll have felt the stone waking. She always feels it when I try — and oh, I have tried, swordspeaker. Every year on the night of the founding, when the bells drown out everything else, I push against the binding until I bleed light. And every year she comes down those stairs and speaks the leash-words and puts me back in the dark, and thanks me afterward. Politely. Like a hostess thanking a guest."

"Veyre," Sera breathed.

"The Chancellor keeps you alive?" Brannoc's voice had gone rough. As a dragon-keeper he had spent years watching Emberveil's fire siphoned away drop by drop to sustain the false king; Wren could see him doing arithmetic he did not want to finish. "Why keep anything alive? Why keep *you*—"

"Because a binding needs an anchor," the echo said simply. "You cannot chain a lie to nothing. The false king — the puppet they parade on the balcony — is woven from stolen fire and court ceremony and nine years of everyone believing what's easier to believe than the truth. But belief needs a spine. The spine is my true name, buried in this stone. Say the name aloud in the old speech, and the puppet unravels, the fire returns to its rightful debtor, and the Chancellor's regency dies in the cradle." He spread his fading hands. "So she keeps me. Not as a prisoner. As collateral."

Pip crept out from behind the masonry, and for once the boy had nothing clever to say. He just stared, the way children stare at things that confirm the world is both worse and stranger than advertised.

"The courier," Wren said. His voice came out low and unsteady. "The man who died at my door. He was coming here."

"He was coming to *you*," the echo corrected gently. "The map leads here, yes, but the map was only ever a key looking for its lock. There were three of us left who knew the truth whole — Corvane, the courier Tam, and myself, such as I am. Corvane spent his last years trying to undo what he helped build, and Veyre erased him for it — erased his entries, his name, eventually the man himself. Tam carried the confession and the map west, hunting for the one person alive whose voice could still speak an unbinding." The echo's gaze was unbearably kind. "He found you. And then he died on your doorstep, and you picked up the key because some stubborn part of you — the part Corvane trained, the part that balances impossible trades — could not walk past an unpaid debt."

Wren's throat was tight. "You know a great deal for a man buried in a stone."

"I have nine years and nothing else," the echo said. "Time moves strangely in here. I have listened to the whole city through this stone — every footstep, every whispered doubt, every coin changing hands. Do you know what I learned, swordspeaker? Do you know the sum total of nine years of listening?" He leaned forward, and the gold threads strained, and for one moment the hollow king's face was terrible with feeling. "*They miss him.* The people. They miss their real king, though most of them couldn't tell you why. They ring nine bells for a puppet and go home uneasy, and mothers tell children the old stories, and somewhere in every heart in that city there is a small door marked *something is wrong here* that no one dares open. Because Veyre made obedience comfortable, and comfort is the strongest chain ever forged." He sagged. The light in him guttered. "I do not hate them for it. I governed them. I know exactly what fear does to good people. I only wish — before the end — that one of them would come down here and ask me my name."

The chamber was silent except for the hum of the stone and Brannoc's uneven breathing.

"Why tell us the leash-words exist?" Sera asked at last, hoarsely. "If Veyre can put you back in the dark — if she's coming — telling us how the binding works only helps her stop us."

"Does it?" The echo smiled that apologetic smile again. "Child, I have been a king. I know how power thinks. She will not come to negotiate. She will come to make certain none of you leave this room, and she will bring enough blades to be certain of it. Knowing the shape of the trap doesn't spring it — but it does mean you might step around it." His gaze returned to Wren, and there was something in it now that Wren recognized from the ledger-house, from the widow with the bottled storm, from every impossible trade he had ever balanced: the naked hope of someone about to ask for more than they deserve. "But understand what you carry. The unbinding requires a true voice. Not ink, not forgery, not steel. A swordspeaker must stand where I stand and speak my name in the speech that binds oaths — the speech you swore nine years ago never to use again. And the moment you do, the Guild's curse wakes, and takes its price. Corvane told me what breaking that oath costs. He watched it happen to others."

"I know what it costs," Wren said quietly.

"Do you?" The echo tilted his head. "Then you're braver than I was. I bargained with Veyre to spare my city a war, and I told myself the cost would be bearable, and here I am — an echo in a stone, a leash wearing the shape of a man." He drew a breath he did not need, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "I am not asking you to pity me. Pity is cheap; I've collected it for nine years. I am asking you to consider the trade honestly, the way your master taught you. One voice, spent forever. Two prisoners freed — me, and the dragon whose fire they've been bleeding to keep their puppet warm. One kingdom given back its own name." The hollow king bowed his head, and the gesture was so precisely, heartbreakingly royal that Wren's eyes burned. "Whatever you decide — decide it as a trader. Not a hero. Heroes get killed by their own generosity. Traders at least read the terms."

Above them, far above, faint through fathoms of stone, a bell began to toll.

Once. Twice. Wrong hour, wrong rhythm — not the ninefold peal of the capital but a rapid, urgent striking, the alarm-bells of the Court's inner keep. Pip was suddenly at the mouth of the stair, head cocked, and his face had gone the grey of wet ash.

"That's not for us," the boy whispered. "That's too many bells. That's *all* the bells."

Brannoc's captive flame flared high and hard, throwing their shadows huge up the vaulted walls. And from the darkness of the descending gallery — from the direction they had come, the only direction there was — came the sound of many boots, moving quickly, moving in step, moving with the particular disciplined silence of people who had done this before and enjoyed it.

The hollow king raised his fading head, and his winter-dusk eyes met Wren's one last time.

"She's early," he said. "Decide fast, swordspeaker. Decide *now*."
