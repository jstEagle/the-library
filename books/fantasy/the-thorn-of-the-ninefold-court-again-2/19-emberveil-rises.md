## 19. Emberveil Rises

Brannoc had spent forty years learning the grammar of dragons, and in all that time he had never once heard silence like this.

The root-stone chamber still rang with the shattering of Veyre's binding. Wren's voice—the swordspeech, the voice that had commanded kings and unmade oaths—hung in the air like a struck bell that refused to finish its note. Brannoc felt it in his teeth, in the old scar tissue along his forearms where dragon-chain had bitten him a lifetime ago. The binding was broken. He knew it the way he knew weather coming, the way he knew a fire about to catch: something vast had turned over in its sleep beneath the world, and opened one eye.

Then the eye opened all the way.

It began as heat. Not the heat of torches or forge-fires or any honest flame Brannoc had ever tended, but a heat that came up through the stone itself, through the soles of his boots, through the marrow of him, as if the mountain's own blood had remembered it was supposed to be warm. The galleries around them—the deep places where Emberveil had been chained for nine years, siphoned drop by drop to keep a hollow man sitting upright on a hollow throne—began to glow. Not metaphorically. The veins of ore in the walls kindled gold, then orange, then white, and Brannoc understood with a keeper's certainty that every stolen ember of her fire was coming home at once, and there was nowhere in the world built to hold it.

"Down!" he roared, and hauled Pip flat against the root-stone's base. Sera had already thrown herself over Isolde, who was coughing, half-conscious, her wrists raw from the cells above. Wren did not move. He stood where the speaking had left him, swaying, his lips still parted around a word no longer in them, and Brannoc saw the curse take him—a gray thread of light coiling out of the shattered oath-scar on his throat, wrapping him like smoke made solid—and there was nothing anyone could do about it, not now, not in this flood of returning fire, and Brannoc's heart broke a little even as the mountain began to sing.

Because it *was* singing.

The chains went first. Brannoc heard them before he saw them—a sound like nine cathedral bells being pulled apart by their own tongues—and then the great links, each one thick as his torso, each forged from cold-iron and regret by smiths who had sold their craft to the Court, simply came apart. Not melted. *Unmade.* The fire took back what had been used to bind her, and the metal forgot how to be metal, crumbling into rust-red sand that drifted down through the glowing dark like snow falling upward.

And Emberveil rose.

Brannoc had seen her young, sixty years ago, when he'd been a boy apprentice in the vaults and she had been the pride of the Ninefold Court—the last dragon, the living hearth of the kingdom, the reason the bells rang nine times instead of eight. She had been bronze and copper then, winged like a cathedral given flight, and when she breathed the whole court had smelled of woodsmoke and cinnamon and home.

She was more than that now.

Nine years of theft should have left her a husk. Instead, with every stolen spark flooding back into her at once, she blazed. Her scales ran molten—bronze gone to white-gold, white-gold gone to colors Brannoc had no names for, light that hurt to look at and hurt worse to look away from. Her wings unfurled through three galleries at once, filling the caverns the way a tide fills a cave, and her eyes—

Her eyes found Brannoc across the burning dark, and they were old, and they were kind, and they were furious, all three at once, and Brannoc fell to his knees without deciding to.

*Little keeper,* she said—not in words, not in any tongue, but in the old bond, the one the Guild said had died with the last dragon-keepers, the one Brannoc had carried alone and half-mad for four decades, mocked for claiming he still heard her. *You stayed.*

"I never—" His voice cracked. "I tried to come back. They took my name. They took my—"

*I know what they took.* The bond wrapped around him like a wing. *I counted every year beside you. You think I did not know your footsteps on the stones above?*

Above. Above them, the palace. Above them, Veyre.

The thought arrived and Emberveil arrived with it. She moved—not flew, not yet; there was no room—but poured herself upward through the galleries like fire climbing a chimney, and the company scrambled after her because there was nowhere else to go, because following a rising dragon is the oldest instinct there is, older than fear.

* * *

They came up into the throne room through the floor.

Not through a door. Through the *floor*—the ancient flagstones splitting along seams laid when the Court was founded, seams Brannoc realized only later had been designed for exactly this, for the day the last dragon would need to stand in the hall of kings, though no founder alive had believed the day would come. Stone bloomed outward like a flower of rubble, and Emberveil's head rose through the wreckage into the throne room of the Ninefold Court, and Brannoc, hauling himself up behind her neck, got his first look at what nine years of quiet terror had made of the greatest hall in the world.

It was full of people. Courtiers, guards, clerks, servants pressed against the far walls—all of them frozen mid-flight toward the doors, because the whole palace had been shaking since midnight, because word had run through the corridors like spilled water: the regent has gone down to the roots, the regent has taken the Court's might with her, something is happening below, something is *rising*—

And on the dais, alone, sat the king.

Or what was left of the echo of him. With the binding broken, the false king—the man's echo, the hollow thing Wren had pitied at the root-stone—was failing. Brannoc could see it plainly: the shape of him flickering like a candle in a draft, the crown sliding sideways off a head that was becoming less head than suggestion. But the face, in the last moments it had, was peaceful. Grateful, almost. Looking at Emberveil the way a tired man looks at his own bed.

*Thank you,* the echo seemed to say, though no sound crossed the hall. And then it came apart—gently, the way ash comes apart when you breathe near it—and the crown rang on the marble steps and rolled, and rolled, and stopped at the foot of the dais, and nobody moved to pick it up.

In the ringing silence after, Brannoc became aware of a second figure standing at the base of the throne.

Chancellor Veyre had come back up from the deep galleries ahead of them—or been spat out by the same collapsing tunnels, Brannoc never learned which—and she stood amid the ruin of everything she had built with her hands folded and her chin high, as if the molten dragon filling her throne room were merely an unexpected guest. She was already talking. Brannoc caught fragments: *treason, disorder, the succession, I have governed in the king's name these nine years, the law is clear—*

Emberveil turned her head and looked at her.

Just looked. And Veyre's voice died in her throat, because there are arguments you can make to a mob and arguments you can make to a judge, but there is no argument you can make to a creature who has watched your whole species lie for nine years and remembers every one.

*You fed me drops,* the dragon said, and the whole hall heard it this time, the old bond carrying her voice into every skull at once, the way it had carried it to Brannoc alone for forty years. *A thimble of my fire, stretched thin, to warm a corpse-shaped lie. You called it governance. I call it the smallest theft in history, and the cruelest.*

"I preserved the realm," Veyre said, and her voice held, Brannoc granted her that. It held the way a rope holds under a falling stone. "There was war. There would have been collapse. Someone had to—"

*Someone had to be paid,* said Emberveil. *Someone had to sign the ledgers. Do not dress it in duty now. I was awake, Chancellor. Every hour of nine years, awake inside your machine, feeling myself poured out coin by coin while you told the world the king was well.* A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils, and where it touched the banners of the regency hanging from the rafters, they blackened and dropped. *I know exactly what you are owed. Nothing.*

Veyre reached for something at her belt—a seal, Brannoc thought, or a knife, or some last instrument of the impossible trade she'd made of a kingdom—and Emberveil exhaled.

Not full fire. Not yet. Just breath—the first free breath of a freed creature, nine years of held smoke leaving her all at once—and the breath alone crossed the throne room like a hot wind off a forge and took Veyre's claim apart. Her robes of office grayed and crisped at the edges. The chain of regency, the heavy golden thing she'd worn since the third year of the lie, tarnished to black in the space of a heartbeat and snapped, its medallion hitting the marble with a sound like a guilty conscience. She staggered, suddenly small, suddenly just an old woman in a burning room, and the guards along the walls—one by one, in ones and twos, then all together—laid down their weapons.

Not because a dragon told them to. Because they had all known, Brannoc realized. Some part of every soul in that hall had known for years that the king's shadow moved wrong, that his eyes held no one home, that the bells rang for a man who wasn't there. The knowledge had lived in them like a splinter, too terrible to name, and now the dragon had named it, and the relief on their faces looked almost like joy.

Justice, Brannoc thought, watching Veyre sink to her knees amid the ash of her own regency. It hadn't come through law. Law had been bought. It hadn't come through armies. The armies had been hers. It had come up through the roots of the world, through a dying courier and a silent swordspeaker and a disgraced keeper and a thief and a forger and an oathbreaker who chose truth—it had come the way fire comes, which is to say: it had come because someone finally let it.

* * *

It was Isolde, of all people, who said it first.

"The crown," she croaked from where Sera still half-supported her, nodding at the circlet lying on the steps. "Someone should—there has to be a restoration, there's procedure, the true king—"

"There is no true king," Sera said quietly. "There's a man. He was bound into that thing for nine years. He didn't age, Isolde. Whatever's left of him—"

What was left of him was standing at the edge of the ruined floor.

None of them had seen him arrive. Later, Brannoc would piece it together: when the binding shattered, whatever remained of the man beneath the echo had woken in the deep places, in the dark, and walked up through the galleries behind them, past the dead chains, past the empty root-stone, drawn by the one thing in the world that still owed him anything. He was gaunt. His hair had gone white at the temples though his face was young—young the way a portrait is young, nine missing years painted on and peeling. He wore a servant's smock someone must have wrapped around him in the vaults, and he was shaking so hard Brannoc could hear his teeth.

He looked at the crown on the steps for a long time.

Then he looked at Emberveil.

"You," the man said. His voice was wrecked, rusted from disuse, but it worked, and hearing a real human voice come out of the shape the whole court had worshipped made half the hall weep aloud. "You're the one she stole from. All those years. I could feel it—I could feel *you*, underneath, keeping me lit, and I couldn't stop it, I couldn't even—"

*Hush,* said Emberveil, gentler than Brannoc had ever heard the old bond. *You were chained as surely as I. We were both furniture in her house.*

"I'm the king." He said it like a man testing whether a leg would bear weight. "I was. Aldric. My name is Aldric, and I—" He stopped. Looked at the crown again. At the courtiers, hundreds of them, staring. At the guards kneeling. At the small ragged company by the dragon's neck—Wren among them, gray-faced, mute now of magic but alive, propped between Pip and Sera—and something in the man's face steadied.

He came down the dais steps. Past the crown. He did not pick it up.

He walked to Emberveil, and the whole hall held its breath, because everyone knew what was supposed to happen next, it was in every ballad and every mural in the city: the king stands above, the dragon bows her head, the realm ordered rightly, beast beneath throne.

Aldric, mortal again, aged nine lost years in a single night, knelt.

He knelt on the scorched marble at the foot of a dragon, in front of his entire court, and bowed his head, and said, in a voice that cracked and shook and did not stop:

"You kept me burning when I couldn't burn for myself. You were robbed for my sake, and I wasn't even in there to refuse it. Whatever I am now—whatever's left—I owe it to you. So I'm asking. Not commanding. Asking. Will you stay? Will you let me try to build a court worth your fire?"

The silence went on so long Brannoc's heart began to pound.

Then Emberveil lowered her great head, until her brow nearly touched the kneeling man's, until the whole throne room glowed with the light of her, and she spoke—not to Aldric alone, but to every soul in the hall, to the city beyond, to the hills and harbors and salt roads where word would fly by morning:

*Rise, Aldric. Not as king above me. As steward beside me. Let the bells ring nine times for both of us—and let the ninth bell mean:* remember.

And the bells—every bell in the Ninefold Court, untouched by any hand—began to ring.

* * *

They rang until dawn.

Brannoc stood in the shattered throne room as the sun came up over a city pouring into the streets, and watched his life rearrange itself. Guards were carrying Veyre away—not to a cell, the crowd below was shouting, to a *trial*, a real one, with a restored king and a dragon as witness, justice arriving outside any law and then, at last, being handed over to law to finish. Sera stood at the center of it, giving orders in a voice that had finally stopped apologizing for itself. Isolde was already sketching new seals on the back of a guard's dispatch—honest ones, she kept insisting, provisional, purely administrative. Pip had fallen asleep sitting up against Emberveil's foreleg, and the dragon had curled her tail around the child with the unconscious tenderness of a creature who had waited nine years to protect something again.

And Wren Voss sat alone on the broken steps of the dais, holding his throat, saying nothing.

Brannoc went to him. Sat down beside him on stone still warm from a dragon's rising. For a long time neither of them spoke, which suited them both; it was, Brannoc reflected, the most fluent conversation either of them had ever had.

"He'll want to reward us," Wren said at last. His ordinary voice. Just a man's voice, rough with exhaustion. "The king. There'll be titles. Estates. A chapter in the histories."

"And?"

"And I don't—" Wren laughed, short and unsteady. "I spent nine years learning to be no one, Brannoc. I got very good at it. I don't know how to be someone the histories mention."

Brannoc looked out at the hall—at the dawn, the bells, the dragon, the sleeping thief, the weeping clerks, the whole improbable wreckage of a kingdom putting itself back together—and felt something he had not felt since he was a boy in the vaults: hope with no asterisk after it.

"Then don't decide tonight," he said. "Nobody balances a ledger in the middle of the fire, lad. You wait till the ashes cool. Then you count what's actually yours."

Wren nodded slowly. And then his gaze drifted, and fixed, and Brannoc followed it to the far end of the hall—to a clerk's table shoved against the wall, where a harried official was sorting through the documents Veyre's fall had scattered, and had paused, frowning, over a single sealed letter bearing a crest Brannoc knew and Wren certainly did: the Ledger Guild of Impossible Trades.

The courier had come at last. And it was addressed, in a hand Brannoc did not recognize, to *the swordspeaker who speaks no more*.
