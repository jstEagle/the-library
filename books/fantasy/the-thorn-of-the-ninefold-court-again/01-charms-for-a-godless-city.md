## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The trouble with selling luck in a city that outlawed magic is that your customers are all liars, and so are you, and everyone knows it, and nobody says it out loud because saying things out loud is how the Wardens find you.

I'd been at my stall by the Tanners' Canal for three years when Tam Ossler came shuffling up that gray morning with his cap wrung between his hands like he was trying to drown it. I knew what he wanted before he opened his mouth. They always had the same face — that particular Vell expression, guilt and desperation fighting to a draw.

"August." He glanced over his shoulder at the fishmonger's awning, the lamplighter still making his rounds, the ordinary machinery of a city pretending nothing was wrong with itself. "You got anything for... you know."

"Charms," I said. "I sell charms. Little knotted things made of string and river clay and wishful thinking. Souvenirs of superstition. Perfectly legal, if slightly embarrassing."

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean." I kept my voice flat and bored, the voice of a woman discussing the price of onions. "Which is why we're going to have this conversation about souvenirs."

He put his hands on the counter and leaned in, and I could smell the dock on him — tar and brine and something underneath, fear-sweat, sharp as vinegar. "Ship came in last night. Out of Sorrel Bay. Three days late and half her crew won't say why, and the ones who will say—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They're saying there's something wrong with the water past the shoals. Something *old* wrong."

"Old wrong," I repeated. "Is that worse than new wrong?"

"August. Please."

Here's the thing about me: I have rules. Three of them, carved somewhere below my ribs where I can feel them every time I breathe too deep. Never big magic. Never attachments. And never, ever let them see you care whether they live or die. The rules had kept me alive seven years in a city where the penalty for practice was a Warden's hood and a long walk into a building people didn't come back from. The rules were all I had left of a life I didn't talk about, from before, when I'd carried different things than knotted string.

So naturally I reached under the counter and pulled out the luck charm.

It was a good piece of work, even if I said so myself, and I usually did, silently, at two in the morning when I couldn't sleep. Braided horsehair and canal clay, a knot tied nine times — eight knots for show, one knot for real, and no, I'm not telling you which. Inside the clay was a sliver of iron and a word I'd written in a language the Wardens' censors wouldn't recognize because it wasn't from anywhere anymore.

"That's four silver," I said. "And it's a souvenir."

"It's four silver," Tam agreed, and counted out coins with shaking fingers, and our eyes met for exactly as long as it took to agree on a lie we both needed.

He tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, the way they always did, and some small traitorous part of me unclenched. There. One dockworker, marginally less likely to drown. It wasn't much. It was never much. A life shrunk down to fit survival looks exactly like this: a stall, a canal, a frightened man, four silver, and a lie shaped like a knot.

* * *

After he went, I sat on my stool and did inventory of things that weren't charms.

My stock: forty-one souvenir knots, six "warding" bells that rang prettily and did nothing, a tray of river-stones I sold to young lovers as tokens of constancy, and one knife under the counter with enough notches on the grip that I'd stopped counting years ago. My history: mercenary, once. Soldier of fortune, if you liked it fancy; sellsword, if you liked it honest; dead woman walking, if you asked the people I used to run with, which was why I'd come to Vell, where nobody asks questions because questions are dangerous here too.

My god: silent.

That's the inventory item I never wrote down, the one that lived in the space where an inventory should be. On the inside of my left wrist, old ink gone brown with age, sat a sigil — a circle broken by a single descending line, like a door seen edge-on, or a blade falling, or a road running out. The mark of a patron whose name I still knew but who had not answered me in seven years. Seven years of prayers dropped down a well with no splash. Every practitioner in Vell had a story about their god. Mine had become very simple: *he spoke once, then he didn't.*

The priests of the Concordance — the tidy official religion, the one with the Wardens attached like teeth — said the old gods were gone or never were, that magic was a disease the world had wisely legislated against. Maybe they were right. Maybe my god was dead and the sigil was just a scar, and every night when I touched it and felt nothing, that nothing was the truest prayer I'd ever made.

I was rubbing the sigil absently with my thumb — a habit, like biting a nail — when the bell over the market square began to ring.

Not the hour bell. The other one.

Everyone in the Tanners' Market did the same thing, all at once, without looking at each other: they became busy. The fishmonger suddenly needed to rearrange his entire display. The ribbon-seller developed an urgent interest in her own shoes. A woman buying apples examined each one with the concentration of a scholar deciphering scripture. And I folded my hands on my counter and became exactly what I appeared to be: a charm-seller, harmless, bored, a woman whose whole life could fit in a wheelbarrow.

They came up the canal road in pairs, the way they always walked. Wardens.

Tall, gray-cloaked, faces hidden behind smooth masks the color of weathered bone. Nobody knew what was under the masks. Nobody wanted to know. They moved through the market without hurry, because hurry was for prey, and paused now and then beside a stall or a doorway, tilting their heads in that slow listening way, like herons watching water. They were hunting practitioners — anyone carrying a working charm, anyone humming a cantrip under their breath, anyone whose shadow fell wrong. In Vell, that was a crime with no defense and no appeal.

One of them stopped. Four stalls down. Beside the ribbon-seller.

I watched the heron-tilt of its head. Watched it lean close to a bundle of dyed silks, close enough that its mask nearly touched the fabric. The market held its breath around us, forty people pretending very hard to be objects.

Then the Warden straightened, moved on, and the market exhaled in one ragged communal breath, and the ribbon-seller sat down abruptly on her stool like her strings had been cut.

Routine. That was the horror of it — it was routine. This was Tuesday in Vell. This was the shape of an ordinary day: sell a lie, watch a hunt, touch a dead god's scar, keep breathing. I had shrunk my life to fit inside these hours the way you shrink a foot into a shoe one size too small, until the pinch felt normal, until you forgot feet came any bigger.

I told myself I was content. I told myself that most mornings. Some mornings I almost believed it.

* * *

Midday brought the rain, thin and cold off the canal, and drove away the few customers I might have had, which suited me fine. I ate bread and hard cheese under my awning and watched the water slide by, green-gray and patient.

A boy darted up in the downpour — Maret, the tanner's son, ten years old and missing two teeth, the only person in Vell I tolerated within arm's reach, which was as close to an attachment as I permitted myself and therefore exactly as doomed as you'd expect.

"Mistress August." He huddled under the awning, dripping. "Mam wants to know if you've got anything for the baby's cough. A *souvenir,*" he added, with the heavy wink of a child who has learned which words are load-bearing.

"Tell your mam a souvenir costs two coppers and involves honey, boiled with thyme, and absolutely no knotwork whatsoever." I fished the coppers out of his fist and replaced them with a twist of dried herbs from my basket. "And tell her if she boils it strong and keeps the window shut, the souvenir works better."

"Did you hear?" he said, pocketing it, already halfway back into the rain because children are incapable of sheltering in place. "About the ship? From the bay?"

"What about it?"

"Some of the sailors are saying the water was singing." He grinned, delighted, the way children are delighted by exactly the wrong things. "Singing under the hull, all night, like a lady. And old Corvin the mate says his granddad told him stories about that, about singing water, but nobody remembers the stories anymore, just that there *were* stories. Isn't that funny?"

"Funny," I agreed. "Go home, Maret. Stay out of the canal fog."

He splashed away, and I sat with the rain coming down and the word *singing* sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here's another inventory item I don't write down: seven years ago, the last night my god spoke to me, his voice had sounded — and I know how this sounds, and I don't care — his voice had sounded like water. Deep water. Old water. Water moving somewhere beneath the world, carrying a song too large for a human throat.

I hadn't thought about that in years. I worked hard at not thinking about it. Not-thinking was my second profession, and I was better at it than charms.

* * *

The rain cleared toward evening, leaving the market washed and emptying, vendors packing their goods into carts, gulls arguing over scraps by the canal. I was folding my awning when I noticed the man.

He was across the lane, leaning against the shuttered wall of the chandler's shop, and he'd been there too long. Dockworkers passed him without a glance, which meant either he was invisible or he was paying them to be blind, and neither option comforted me. He wore a traveler's oilskin gone stiff with salt, and he stood like a man holding himself upright by argument rather than strength.

Watching me. Or watching my stall. Hard to say which was worse.

I finished closing up with the unhurried movements of a woman with nothing to hide, which took considerable acting, since my hand wanted to go straight to the knife. Then I loaded my barrow — knots, bells, stones, knife — and pushed it into the lanes toward home, taking the long way, doubling back twice, losing him or being allowed to lose him, I couldn't tell which.

Home was a rented room above a dye-shop, one bed, one table, one window overlooking a courtyard where the dye-vats breathed chemical colors into the dark. I barred the door, lit no lamp, and sat on the bed with the knife across my knees, doing the arithmetic I did every night.

Assets: one knife, one dead god, one talent for small magics disguised as craft, and a face nobody remembered, which is a kind of wealth in Vell.

Liabilities: everything else.

Somewhere out in the wet streets, a ship's crew wasn't talking, a boy was repeating a story nobody remembered the body of, and a salt-stiff stranger had spent the afternoon learning the layout of my little life. Any one of those things alone would be weather. Together they had the shape of something arriving.

I lay down in the dark and pressed my thumb to the sigil on my wrist, the way I did every night, the way I had done for seven years of silence.

*Nothing,* said the well.

But that night I dreamed of water, and in the dream it was singing, and I woke just before dawn with my heart slamming and the taste of brine in my mouth and the absolute certain knowledge, arriving from nowhere, from everywhere, from seven years down a dry well:

Something was coming for me, and it was going to start today.

Below my window, in the courtyard, the first cart of the morning rattled past toward the market. Ordinary. Gray. Tuesday.

I got up anyway. I dressed, hid the knife, counted my coins, and went down to open my stall, because whatever was coming would find me there or find me here, and at least at the stall I'd see it coming.

The fog off the canal was thicker than usual that morning, lying in the lanes like something spilled. And out of it, while I was still arranging my knots, I heard footsteps — uneven, dragging, wrong — and turned to see a man stagger out of the white and into the mouth of my alley, holding his side, leaving a trail behind him that the fog drank too fast.

He looked at me. He looked at my stall, my knots, my hands.

"Oh thank the nine," he whispered — words that meant nothing, words from a country that didn't exist — and fell forward into my arms, bleeding, as the first light found the canal.

He was clutching something folded tight against his chest, and his blood was all over my hands, and the morning had barely begun.
