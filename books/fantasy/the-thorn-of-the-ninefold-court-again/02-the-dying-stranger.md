## 2. The Dying Stranger

The trouble with living small is that the world eventually notices you've left room for it to fall on you.

I'd built my life in Vell the way you build a wall out of river stones — carefully, one at a time, choosing each piece so it fit against its neighbors without gaps. A stall in Saltmarket Row that I rented from a woman who asked no questions because I paid no questions' worth of rent. A stock of charms that were, individually and legally, worthless: knotted cords, dried herbs, bits of glass polished until they caught the light like something holy. A routine that never varied, because variation was where the Wardens found you.

And underneath all of it, folded up small enough to hide behind my ribs, a dead god's sigil and seven years of pretending I didn't miss her.

That morning had been ordinary in every way that mattered. Fog off the harbor thick enough to chew. The fishmonger two stalls down arguing with his wife about eels. A child stealing an apple and getting away with it, which was the closest thing to justice Vell offered most days.

My first customer came at midmorning, and she was afraid.

I could tell before she said a word. Fear has a smell — sweat gone sour under wool, breath held too long — and this woman wore it like a second coat over her dockworker's canvas. She was maybe forty, hands scarred from rope work, eyes doing that thing frightened people's eyes do in Vell: checking corners, checking the sky, checking me, deciding in real time whether I was salvation or a trap.

"You're the charm seller," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I sell knots," I said. "Pretty ones. For luck."

"My brother's ship went down." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Three weeks ago. And now there's something in his house. Something that moves things when we're not looking. My mother says it's grief. My mother says a lot of things." She swallowed. "They say you can fix things like that."

*They say.* In a city that outlawed magic forty years ago, *they say* was the most dangerous phrase in the language. Half of what *they said* was nonsense. The other half was true, and either kind could get you taken by the Wardens.

"Ma'am," I said gently, "if your mother says it's grief, your mother might be right. Grief does move things. It moved me for a good two years."

Something flickered across her face — contempt, maybe, or disappointment. She'd wanted a witch. Witches were easier than grief; witches took payment.

But then she leaned in, and her hand came down flat on my counter, and I saw what was wrapped around her wrist: a cord, cheap-braided, with a knot in it that I recognized because I'd tied it myself. Three years ago. To another dockworker. Her brother, presumably, back when he was alive to buy luck he'd never use.

"That knot," she whispered. "It burned. The night after the funeral. Burned right through without scorching his skin, and my mother threw it in the sea, and I fished it out, because—" Her voice cracked. "Because it was the last thing he bought."

I looked at the knot. The knot, being dead cord and nothing else, did not look back. But I felt the shape of the working in it anyway, the little thread of intent I'd spun into it, and I knew — the way you know rain is coming in your knees — that whatever was in her brother's house had noticed that knot. Had noticed *me*, maybe, through it, the way a fisherman notices the tug on his line.

There are rules for surviving as a practitioner in Vell. I had three. Never big magic. Never attachments. Never, ever let a working lead anything back to your door.

Every rule I had told me to say: *burn it, bury it, go home, grieve your brother.*

What I said instead was: "Two silver. And you tell anyone who asks that you bought a pretty knot."

She paid. I gave her a new cord with a knot tied to settle restless things — small magic, quiet magic, the magical equivalent of turning down a lamp rather than lighting one — and instructions to hang it above the door and stop fishing charms out of the sea. She left lighter. I sat back down behind my counter feeling like I'd just done something enormous and stupid, which was ridiculous, because it had been a knot. A knot and a prayer I didn't let myself finish.

That was the trouble with a silent god. You got out of the habit of talking to her, but you never quite got out of the habit of *wanting* to. Every working I did was half craft and half a letter sent to an address that had been empty for seven years.

I should have closed the stall early. I'm telling you now, with the benefit of hindsight, which is just regret wearing a nicer coat: if I'd closed early, none of what followed would have found me. Or it would have found someone else, which is not the same as nothing, but it isn't mine either.

Instead I stayed open through the gray afternoon, selling nothing, watching the fog burn off into a sky the color of old pewter, and thinking about the knot that had burned. Thinking about what kind of thing burns a charm without touching the wrist beneath it. Thinking, despite myself, about sigils.

Mine sits over my heart — or sat, once, when it was anything more than a scar. A nine-pointed star with one point broken off, inked into me the day I swore myself to her, back when gods still answered in Vell, back before the Edict, back before the silence. Seven years of nothing. Seven years of praying into a well and hearing only my own voice come back, thinner each time.

Around dusk I packed up. That's when I heard the sound from the alley behind my stall.

You learn the sounds of Saltmarket Row. Drunks have a rhythm. Fights have a rhythm. Rats, cats, cart wheels, all of them have rhythms, and after six years you can hear a wrong note in the music the way a musician can.

This was a wrong note. A body going down against wet stone, but slowly, controlled, the way someone falls who is trying very hard not to fall.

I want to be honest here, since honesty costs me nothing anymore: my first instinct was to stay inside. Lock the shutter. Let the alley keep its own problems. That was Rule Two, Rule One, and a rule I'd never needed to write down because it was just the shape of my whole life — *never attachments, never big magic, never get involved.*

I went out anyway.

He was slumped against the wall between my stall and the cooper's, a man somewhere past middle age, dressed in traveling clothes that had once been fine and were now ruined in the specific way that comes from running. Mud to the knee. A coat torn at the shoulder. And blood — a lot of it, spreading under him with the terrible patience that blood has, soaking into the cracks between the cobbles like it was trying to write something.

His eyes found me before I'd decided whether to run. Gray eyes, fever-bright, and focused on me with an intensity that made my skin prickle, because men dying in alleys don't look at strangers like that. Men dying in alleys look at the sky, or at nothing.

"You," he said. "The charm-seller. On the Row."

"How—" I stopped. There was no good way to finish that sentence. *How do you know me* invited answers I didn't want. "You're hurt. I'll fetch a physicker—"

"No." His hand shot out and caught my wrist, and his grip was astonishing, iron in a failing body, and cold — cold the way deep water is cold, cold like something that had been underground a long time. "No physickers. No Wardens. Listen to me. There's no time. They're coming, and they can't find it on me."

"Find what? Who's coming?"

"The Wardens." He laughed, and it turned into a cough, and the cough brought up blood he didn't bother wiping away. "Always the Wardens. Forty years and they never stop walking." He pulled at my wrist, dragging my hand toward him. "Take it. You have to take it. I chose you. Do you understand? Out of everyone on this rotten street, I watched you for three days, and I chose *you*."

"That's flattering and also insane," I said. "Let go of me."

"They erased it." His voice dropped, urgent, ragged. "The whole country. Every road, every name, every memory. Nine gods built it and eight of them agreed to unmake it, and everyone forgot, everyone, even the people who lived there, even—" He shuddered. "Even me. I was born there. I don't remember its name. *Nobody* remembers its name. But the ninth refused. The ninth refused, and they silenced her, and they couldn't close her door."

I should have run then. I want that on the record too. Everything in me that had survived seven years in Vell was screaming *run*, because this was exactly the kind of talk that ended with gray-robed figures asking questions in voices like empty rooms, and the people they asked tended to stop answering anything, ever.

But his hand was on my wrist, and under the cold of his grip I felt something else. Something warm. Something I hadn't felt in seven years.

My sigil was warming.

Not burning. Not speaking. Just warming, faint and far away, like a fire in the next room of a house you thought was empty. Like a hand resting briefly on your shoulder from behind.

I stopped trying to pull away. God help me, I stopped.

"What's your name?" I heard myself ask.

"It won't help you." He pressed something into my palm — folded paper, oilcloth-wrapped, heavier than paper had any right to be. "Nothing will help you. That's the truth of it. But it might help *everyone*, and I'm past helping myself." His eyes bored into mine. "The ninth door is still open."

"The ninth—"

"Find it. Before they seal it. Before they finish the job." Blood ran from the corner of his mouth and he talked through it, faster, spending words like a man spending his last coin. "The map knows the way. It won't show you everything — it doesn't show anyone everything — but it knows. And when you get there, when you understand what it costs—" His grip tightened. "Don't pay it blind. Promise me. Whatever they tell you the price is, don't pay it blind."

"I'm not promising a dying stranger anything," I said, which was a lie, because my fingers had already closed around the packet, and we both knew it.

Light bloomed at the mouth of the alley.

I'd seen Warden-light twice before in my life, both times from a safe distance, both times wishing I hadn't. It isn't light, really. It's the absence of shadow — a white that falls on everything equally, that erases depth, that makes the world look like a drawing of itself. Under Warden-light, faces go blank. Voices go flat. And practitioners feel it in their teeth, in their bones, in the place where their god used to live: a pressure like the whole sky leaning on you, asking, *what are you, what are you, what are you.*

The man in my arms went rigid.

"No," he breathed. "Too fast. Too fast, they've been closer than I thought—" He shoved the packet hard against my chest, hard enough to bruise. "Hide it. Don't read it where they can see. Don't let them take it from your body, August Blackwood."

My name. My whole name, the mercenary name, the name I'd buried along with the career, the name nobody on Saltmarket Row knew.

"How do you know my—"

The light swung toward us.

Everything happened fast after that, the way bad things do. I heard boots — many boots, moving in perfect unison, which is a sound that will follow you into your sleep for years. The man pushed himself off the wall with the last of whatever was holding him upright, put himself between me and the alley mouth, and said, clearly, in a voice pitched to carry:

"Here! I'm here! It ends here!"

And the light took him.

I don't have better words for it. The white fell over him and he stood in it, arms spread, and the light *drank* him — I watched it happen, watched the color go out of him from the edges inward, watched his outline soften like chalk in rain. He wasn't screaming. That was the worst part. He was smiling. At the end, as he came apart into that featureless brightness, he turned his head and found me in the dark behind a rain barrel where I'd flattened myself, and he smiled like a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far, and his lips shaped two words I couldn't read.

Then there was no man. There was a stain of blood on the stones, already fading, already drying gray, and five figures in gray robes standing in the alley mouth, and me behind a barrel with my heart trying to exit through my ears.

The Wardens stood very still. Warden-still, the stillness of things that don't need to breathe. One of them tilted its head — I could see the smooth blankness of its mask from my angle, featureless as an egg — and I pressed myself flatter against the cold stone and did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than any fight I'd ever fought as a mercenary:

I did nothing. No working. No shield, no veil, no trick of shadow. Nothing for the pressure in my teeth to find. Just a woman hiding behind a barrel in a dirty alley, terrified, ordinary, invisible in the way that only genuinely mundane things are invisible.

The pressure swept over me like a searchlight made of questions. *What are you.* Nothing. *What are you.* A charm-seller. Tired. Cold. Afraid. *What are you.*

It passed.

The Wardens stood a moment longer over the place where the man had been. Then, in unison, without a word, they turned and walked away, and the light went with them, and the alley was just an alley again, smelling of bilge and rotting cabbage, utterly indifferent to what had happened in it.

I stayed behind that barrel until my legs stopped shaking enough to stand. Then longer. The fog came back in off the harbor, as if the evening were embarrassed and wanted to cover everything up.

When I finally got to my feet, my hand was still closed around the packet. I hadn't let go of it once. Some part of me had understood, before the rest of me did, that letting go was not among my options anymore.

* * *

I didn't open it in the alley. I didn't open it at the stall. I carried it home clutched against my sternum like a stolen infant, walked the long way twice to make sure I wasn't followed, checked my window latches and my floorboards and the space under my bed where I keep a knife I've never had to use, and only then, by candlelight, with the door barred and salt poured across the threshold — old habits, older superstitions — did I unwrap the oilcloth.

Inside was a map. Folded, worn soft at the creases, drawn on paper that seemed older than paper had any business being. Roads. Mountains. A coastline I didn't recognize, and I know coastlines; I'd fought along half of them. In the center, where a capital city goes on maps, there was a symbol: a nine-pointed star with one point broken off.

My god's sigil. Exactly my god's sigil, down to the way the broken point curved, down to the specific wrongness of it that every one of her sworn carried somewhere on their skin.

The candle guttered. The sigil over my heart warmed again, faint and far, like a coal remembered rather than felt.

And the map — I swear this, and I know how it sounds — the map shifted under my eyes. Just slightly. A road that had run north now bent east, patient as a growing vine, rearranging itself the way a sleeping person rearranges the blanket.

I set it down. I backed away from it. I made tea I didn't drink and told myself, out loud, in the empty room, the sensible things: *You don't know that man. You owe him nothing. Whatever he was running from, it's finished, it took him, it's done. Burn the map. Go to sleep. Open the stall tomorrow.*

I picked it up again.

Because here is the thing I couldn't argue with, the crack in the wall of my careful small life, the flaw in the stone: the warmth over my heart hadn't faded. For the first time in seven years, my silent god was *warm*, and she was warm in time with the paper in my hands, and I had spent seven years learning to live with silence the way you learn to live with a limp — and I would have crawled across Vell on my knees for one more hour of that warmth.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a white light swept the fog, slow and thorough, like something searching.

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark, holding a map to a country that had never existed, listening to my heart beat against a sigil that had finally, impossibly, begun to answer.
