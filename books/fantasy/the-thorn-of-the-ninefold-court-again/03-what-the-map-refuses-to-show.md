## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

I did not sleep.

That's not a boast. Sleep is a skill like any other, and I've always been mediocre at it, but this was something else — a full and deliberate abstention, the way a man might decline to walk off a cliff. Every time I closed my eyes I saw the white light sweeping the fog, and the dying man's mouth shaping words around blood: *the ninth door is still open.*

Doors open onto things. That was the whole trouble with the sentence.

So I sat at my kitchen table with one candle lit — defiance, or stupidity, hard to say which — and unrolled the map for the third time, pinning its corners down with my ink pot, a stone, a spoon, and my own left hand.

Here is what I knew about maps: they are arguments. Somebody draws them to convince you that the world is a certain shape, and you believe them because paper is confident and roads are expensive to lie about. A good map is a promise. A bad map is a swindle. This map, I decided somewhere around the second hour of staring at it, was neither.

It was a dare.

The parchment itself was ordinary enough — soft with handling, stained at one corner where blood had dried to the color of old rust. The edges were worn in the way of things carried against a body for years, not days. Whoever had owned it had loved it the way you love a habit: without thinking, without choosing, until it's part of your skin.

But the ink. The ink was wrong.

Not faded-wrong, not forged-wrong. Wrong the way a word is wrong when you've stared at it too long — familiar letters arranged into something your eye refuses to accept. I traced the coastline along the top of the map and it ran true for a while, honest Vell harbor, honest salt marsh, honest hills I'd marched over in another life. Then, past the marshes, the coast kept going. And going. Into country that has never been on any chart of any kingdom I've served, traded with, or bled in.

There were cities there. Little careful towers drawn with a draftsman's patience, each one labeled in a script I didn't know — not the trade-tongue, not the old imperial hand they taught us officers so we could read requisitions in conquered towns, nothing from the archive-syllabaries I'd once skimmed in a patron's library out of boredom. The letters looked almost like our alphabet the way a heron looks almost like a man standing in shallow water. Related, distantly. Long ago.

And the names under them — here was the thing that made me set down the candle and press my palms flat against the table until the shaking stopped.

The names changed.

I'm not being poetic. I looked away, looked back, and the city that had been called — I'll call it *Sereth*, because that's what it said first, in letters like frost on a window — now read something else, softer, rounder, gone before I could hold it. The road leading to it had shifted too. It used to bend east around a range of mountains rendered in fine hatched strokes; now the mountains stood west of the city, and the road ran straight through where the peaks had been, as if it had always done so, as if I were the one misremembering.

Maps don't do that. Maps are the most honest liars in the world: they lie all at once, up front, in full view, and then they hold still and let you catch them.

This one moved when I wasn't looking. Like something alive that didn't want to be caught watching.

"All right," I told it. My voice sounded wrong in the little room, too loud, scraped raw from disuse. Talking to myself was another thing I'd mostly trained out of my life — attachments, I'd learned, included conversations with empty chairs. "You're either cursed, enchanted, or haunted. In Vell those are three flavors of the same death sentence."

The map lay still under the spoon and the stone and did not dignify that.

* * *

Morning came gray through the shutters, and with it the practical problem of what to do with a day. This is the thing about a small life: it doesn't pause for miracles. My charms wouldn't sell themselves, and rent doesn't care whose sigil is warming under your shirt.

The sigil.

Let me tell you about the sigil, since apparently I have to start telling people about it eventually anyway.

Seven years ago — eight, nearly, but seven is the number I use, because that's how long it's been silent — I had a god. Not the way most people in Vell have gods, which is to say not at all, or the way merchants have them, which is to say on festival days and in lawsuits. Mine was a working god. A soldier's god, if soldiers ever admitted to having them, which they don't, officially, because gods make armies sentimental and sentimental armies lose. There were nine of them once, the old stories said, nine who walked together before the kingdoms were kingdoms, and each took their followers where they found them. Mine never gave me a name I could pronounce. It gave me a sigil instead, inked over my heart in a rite I've never been able to describe to anyone without lying about half of it: a mark like a thorn, a single hooked line, simple as a fishhook, sharp as one too.

For six years it answered. Small things. Warmths. Warnings that arrived as pressure rather than words. Enough to keep me alive through contracts that should have killed me three times over.

Then, seven years ago, on no particular day, in the middle of no particular job — silence. Not the silence of death. I know what divine death feels like; it's cold and final and it smells like a snuffed candle. This was different. This was the silence of someone holding their breath behind a wall, listening to footsteps pass.

I waited a year. Two. I stopped waiting at five and started pretending I'd never waited at all. That's what the charm stall was, really — not a living. A way of staying close to the work without praying. A way of keeping one hand in the water in case the river ever came back.

And last night, for the first time in seven years, the sigil had warmed.

Not much. Not words. A heat like a coin held in the sun, sitting over my sternum while I sat in the dark holding a dead man's map. Coin-in-the-sun heat, at midnight, indoors, in autumn.

I'd spent the night telling myself it was fever. Indigestion. Fear doing funny things to the body. Fear does do funny things to the body — I've vomited behind barricades, gone deaf from terror, once fought an entire skirmish believing my left arm was somebody else's. But fear doesn't warm in patterns. And when I'd finally blown out the candle, the warmth had pulsed — twice, slow, patient — exactly when my eyes had passed over the ninth name on the map. The one label written not in that frost-heron script but in plain trade-tongue, plain as a threat:

*The Ninth Door.*

Every other place on the map shifted and slid and refused to be known. That one name sat still. Patient. Like the door it named.

* * *

I tried to burn it that afternoon, between customers.

I want to be clear that this was a considered decision, reached after a full morning of rational argument with myself, and not panic. Panic came later, when the map won.

My reasoning went as follows. One: the map was evidence, and evidence gets people hanged in Vell, especially evidence that glows faintly at its borders when you're not looking directly at it. Two: the dying man had given it to *me*, specifically, which meant whoever wanted it would come looking for me specifically, and I had spent seven years constructing a life whose entire architectural principle was *nobody comes looking*. Three: whatever was on it — roads, cities, a door — belonged to a story that had ended badly for everyone involved, and I had survived this long by refusing to be a character in anyone's story but my own small, dull, profitable one.

Four: my god was dead, and dead gods don't warm, and if I burned the map I could stop having to think about the fact that mine apparently wasn't.

That last reason I didn't write down anywhere, even mentally, even in the ledger I keep in the back of my skull where I record inconvenient truths and mark them *paid*.

Mid-afternoon, the stall was empty. Rain had come in off the harbor, thin and needling, driving everyone indoors, and rain is good for two kinds of business: umbrella-brokers and crimes. I took the map into the back room where I do my ink-work, set it in the iron brazier I use for sealing wax and singeing charm-cords, and lit the coals beneath it.

Parchment burns beautifully. Anyone who's destroyed correspondence knows this — it curls, it blooms orange, it goes to ash with a sound like a sigh. I've burned letters, orders, one entire contract that would have made me a party to a massacre. I know the ritual. You watch it all the way down. You don't look away, because looking away is how the burning becomes real and the burning needs witnesses, even if the witness is only you.

The map curled at the edges. It browned. It caught —

— and the fire went out.

Not died down. *Out.* Coals black, flame vanished, the whole brazier suddenly cold enough that when I touched the rim — stupidly, automatically — it felt like river stone in November.

In my apron pocket, something rustled.

I want to describe accurately what happened to my body in the next several seconds, because I think it matters. My hands did not shake. My training held; it always holds; it's the only inheritance I've never squandered. What happened instead was slower and worse: everything went very quiet and very precise, the way the world does right before a blade comes out. Colors sharpened. Sounds dropped away except my own breathing. Somewhere underneath all of it, a voice I hadn't heard from in seven years of soldiering — the voice of the person I'd been before I got careful — said: *run.*

I reached into the pocket anyway. Of course I did. Curiosity has killed more mercenaries than swords have; we're just usually curious about the wrong things, like whether the chest is trapped, and never about the right ones, like why we needed the chest at all.

Folded paper. Soft with handling. Stained at one corner, dark as old rust.

I unfolded it with hands that did not shake, and the coastline ran true past the harbor, past the marshes, into country that has never existed, and the city that had been Sereth was Sereth again, and the ninth name sat at the top of it, still and patient, in letters that looked freshly inked.

"You don't get to refuse," I told it. My voice cracked on *refuse*, which was embarrassing, and I covered it by laughing, which was worse.

The sigil over my heart pulsed. Twice. Slow. Patient.

Like something saying: *yes, I do.*

* * *

I closed the stall early. Bolted the back door, shuttered the windows, did the small paranoid arithmetic of a woman who has been hunted before and recognizes the weather. Then I sat on the floor of my back room with my back against the wall and the map on my knees, because the table felt too formal and the bed felt too far, and I had a conversation with a piece of parchment and a silent god, and I'm going to report it faithfully even though it makes me sound mad, because I've decided that ship sailed the moment fire forgot how to burn.

"Here's what I know," I said. "Someone killed a man in an alley over you. Wardens are sweeping the fog for him — for whoever he handed you to. Which is me. So you're not a curiosity anymore, you're a debt, and I pay my debts fast and clean, and the fastest way is ash." I tapped the brazier. "Except you don't burn. Fine. There are other ways. The harbor's deep. Acid works. Or I give you to the Wardens myself, let them have their own property back."

The map did nothing, which was somehow more eloquent than anything it could have done.

"And if I keep you," I went on, and here my voice did something I didn't authorize, dropping low, going rough, "if I follow you — what then? Roads that aren't there. Cities with no names that hold still. A door. And on the other side of that door, what? An answer?" I pressed my palm flat against my sternum, against the thorn-shaped line of the sigil, feeling the faint banked heat of it like a hand pressed to the other side of a wall. "Seven years. Seven years of nothing, and now you're warm, and it happens to be the exact hour some stranger dies handing me a map. You'll forgive me if I don't find that comforting. Gods that come back after seven years of silence come back wanting something. I've been wanted before. It's never gentle."

Twice. Slow. Patient. The pulse again, and this time — I swear this, and I know how it sounds — this time it wasn't just warmth. It was rhythm. A heartbeat's rhythm, but slower than a heartbeat, vast and unhurried, like something enormous breathing far underground.

Or like something exhausted. Holding a weight up. For a very long time.

I thought about the dying man's face. Not the blood, not the light — the face. He hadn't looked afraid of me. He'd looked *relieved*, the specific relief of a courier who has finally gotten the package out of his hands, and under the relief something else, something I've only seen on the faces of men I've pulled out of lost causes: gratitude mixed with apology. *Sorry it's you. Thank you. Sorry it's you.*

He'd known what it would cost me. He'd handed it over anyway, because the alternative was letting it die with him, and whatever was written on that parchment mattered more than my consent, more than my small life, maybe more than his own.

"The ninth door is still open," I quoted, to the room, to the map, to the warm thorn under my hand. "Open onto what?"

The sigil pulsed.

And this time — faintly, at the very edge of hearing, the way you sometimes hear your own name across a crowded market and can't swear to it — I felt something that was almost, *almost*, a word. Not in the ear. Under the skin. One syllable of pressure and grief and terrible, ancient patience, and if I'd had to bet my life on translating it, I'd have put money on:

*Nineteen years. Hold on. Nineteen—*

No. That's not right. That's the shape of it but not the sense, the way a dream keeps its emotion after losing its plot. Whatever it said, it said *wait*, and it said *come*, and both at once, which is a cruel thing to ask anyone, and cruelest of all to ask a woman who built her whole life out of never waiting for anything again.

I sat on that cold floor until the rain stopped and the light went copper and then blue, and I did not burn the map again. I did not throw it in the harbor. I wrapped it in oilcloth and hid it inside the false bottom of my charm-case, under the luck-charms and the fever-charms and the little braided cords against nightmares, and I told myself I was keeping it close in order to destroy it properly later, with method, with time, with acid.

I am a very good liar. It's half of what kept me alive as a mercenary. But I've never managed to lie to myself about the important things, only the logistics.

Because here's the truth underneath, the truth I marked *paid* in the ledger at the back of my skull and then quietly failed to collect: my god was warm. After seven years in the grave, something of mine was alive in the dark and asking me to come find it. And hope, real hope, the kind you'd cut out of your own chest with a belt knife if you could — hope is a worse infection than any curse. Curses you can break.

A knock at the front shutter made me come up off the floor with a knife already in my hand.

Through the gap: lamplight, fog, and the pale oval of a face I didn't know — a thin man, soaked through, with the particular desperate composure of someone who has walked a long way to be turned away.

"August Blackwood?" he said, through the shutter slats. His voice was hoarse, educated, ruined at the edges. "They told me at the dockside you sell charms. I need — " he swallowed, glanced over his shoulder at the fog behind him, and when he turned back his eyes were the eyes of a man reading his own sentence, "— I need someone who can read what I've spent eleven years memorizing. Before they take the memory of it out of me too."

The sigil over my heart pulsed. Twice. Slow. Patient.

I unbolted the shutter.
