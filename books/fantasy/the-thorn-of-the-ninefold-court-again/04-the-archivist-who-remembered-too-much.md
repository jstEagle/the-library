## 4. The Archivist Who Remembered Too Much

There are three ways to lose an argument with August Blackwood, and I had discovered all of them within the first quarter hour of knowing her, which I believe is some kind of record.

But I am getting ahead of myself. Prison first. A man should establish his credentials.

* * *

The cells beneath the Vell Royal Archive were not, strictly speaking, cells. The Archive's founders had been of the opinion that knowledge was a sacred trust and that those who mishandled it deserved something worse than a dungeon — they deserved paperwork. So they built the Punishment Vaults: windowless rooms lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, each one catalogued, cross-referenced, and utterly without interest. My sentence was to read them. All of them. In order. Including — and here even my jailers had winced — the complete minutes of the Guild of Lamplighters, 1841 to 1863.

Twenty-two years of lamp-trimming disputes. I have read them twice. There was a man named Ostrey who felt strongly about wick length. I could tell you things about wicks that would make you weep.

They thought this was torture. They had not understood what they were dealing with. Ilen Vasque does not get bored. Ilen Vasque gets *interested*, which is far more dangerous, and by my fourth year below ground I had read every ledger in my cell and begun petitioning — politely, in triplicate, because pettiness is a discipline — for access to the Restricted Stacks. My crime, you see, had never been theft or heresy. It had been *cataloguing*. I had found a cache of pre-Unification documents in the Archive's deepest vault, documents written in a script no living scholar recognized, and instead of burning them as the law required, I had made an index. A beautiful one. Cross-referenced, annotated, with conjectural phonetics.

For this they took my title, my rooms, my stipend, and my future, and gave me wicks.

So when the wall of my cell exploded inward on a grey Tuesday morning, scattering four hundred pounds of masonry across Ostrey's lamplighter minutes, my first feeling was not fear.

It was indignation at the dust.

* * *

She stood in the hole she'd made — and it was a hole, not a door; she had not troubled herself with doors — holding a sledgehammer like it owed her money. Grey coat, travel-worn. Dark hair tied back with the kind of efficiency that suggested she'd stopped bothering with beauty years ago and hadn't missed it. And eyes that did the thing I have since learned is characteristic: they assessed everything, assigned it a value, and then pretended they hadn't looked at all.

"You're the archivist," she said.

"Depends who's asking, and whether they're holding a hammer."

"I'm asking."

"Then yes. Formerly. Currently I'm a resident scholar of the Guild of Lamplighters." I brushed plaster off the manuscript pages scattered at my feet — my index, my beautiful forbidden index, which I had copied out three times from memory just to keep my hand in. "You're robbing a royal prison. May I ask why me? I'm flattered, but there are criminals in Vell with far better resumes."

She reached into her coat and pulled out a folded piece of paper. Unfolded it. Held it up.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because people lie about moments like this. They say their heart leapt. Mine did not leap. It *stopped* — the specific, terrible stillness of a man who has spent six years staring at fragments of a dead language and suddenly sees it whole, breathing, alive, held up in the hand of a stranger who broke through his wall with a hammer.

"That's—" My voice cracked. I hated that. "Where did you get that?"

"A dying man bled on it in my alley. Can you read it?"

"Can I—" I laughed, and it came out slightly unhinged, which under the circumstances I'll defend. "Madam, I have committed professional suicide over less than half a page of that script. Do you know what this is? This isn't a dialect. It isn't a cipher. The orthography doesn't correspond to any language family in the canon. I catalogued forty-one characters before they arrested me, and I've reconstructed maybe twelve sounds, and you are standing in a prison cell holding what appears to be a *complete document*—"

"It's a map," she said flatly.

"It's a map," I agreed, "to nowhere. Look — these place-names. They don't decline. Every noun in every language we know declines, conjugates, inflects, but these just *sit there*, like the words refuse to be used. That's not ignorance. That's design. Someone didn't want these places found."

Something crossed her face then — quick, quickly buried. She folded the map away.

"So you can't read it."

"I can read *some* of it. Given time. Given materials." I stepped over the rubble toward her, and she did not step back, which I noted. Most people step back from a convict emerging from a hole in the wall. "Given, ideally, freedom."

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"There's no offer. You're a job, not a partner. I break you out, you look at a piece of paper, you tell me what it says, we're done."

"And where exactly do you propose I do this looking? Here?" I gestured at the cell, the ledgers, the fine archival dust. "In the company of the Lamplighters' Guild? Because I warn you, once you've read twenty-two years of wick disputes, your standards for intellectual discourse become either very low or very high, and mine went high."

"They're coming," she said, which I gathered was her way of conceding a point. Somewhere above us, boots. Many boots, moving in the organized rhythm of men who had been told to hurry. "Wardens. They swept the archive district an hour ago asking after someone. If they find you talking to me—"

"They'll assume I orchestrated my own rescue via exploding wall, which frankly does me credit." I grabbed my index copies off the shelf, rolled them, and shoved them inside my shirt. Six years of work. I was not leaving them for the Wardens, whoever and whatever Wardens were. "Well? Are you breaking me out or admiring the architecture?"

For a moment she just looked at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic behind her eyes — the cost-benefit of attaching herself to a stranger, which I would later learn violated one of her personal commandments. Then boots again, closer, and arithmetic lost to necessity.

"This way," she said. "Try to keep up, and if you scream, I'll leave you."

"A gentleman never screams. He objects, loudly, in footnotes."

* * *

I will summarize the escape, because escape narratives are tedious and mine involved an amount of crawling that my dignity still refuses to discuss. Sewer, grate, courtyard, laundry line — hers, pre-strung, which told me she'd planned this — and then rooftops, where I learned two facts about August Blackwood: she moved like water finding its level, and she had absolutely no interest in slowing down for archivists.

We ended up in the bell tower of a deconsecrated chapel in the tanner's quarter, breathing hard, the city spread out below us grey and smoky. Through the windows I could see them: Wardens. A dozen, maybe more, moving through the streets in that unnerving synchronized way, unhurried, thorough, stopping pedestrians with a gesture. Their masks caught the afternoon light.

"What are they?" I asked. "Genuinely. I've read every account of the city watch going back four centuries and nothing moves like that."

"You've seen them before?"

"I saw them arrest me. Well — I saw *masks* arrest me. The magistrates said it was the Crown's new inquisitorial division." I sat down against the bell housing, legs finally admitting to trembling. "It wasn't, though, was it? Magistrates don't send masked things after a man for making an index."

"No," she said. She was watching the street below, and her hand kept closing around something in her pocket. "They don't."

"August Blackwood," I said. "That's your name? You knew mine, so it seems only fair."

"How did you—"

"The prison registry is posted in the guardroom. I walk past it daily. Also you called me 'the archivist' like it was a diagnosis." I leaned my head back. "August Blackwood, charm-seller, sometime mercenary — yes, I read the incident reports too, the ones they think are sealed — currently committing approximately nine felonies to acquire my services. Why? You said yourself you only need me to read a paper. People don't commit nine felonies for a translation. What did the dying man tell you?"

Silence. Long enough that I started composing a footnote about it.

"He said the ninth door is still open," she said finally. "And then he died. And the map—" Her jaw worked. "The map won't burn. I tried. It comes back. In my pocket. Whole."

I should have laughed. A sane man would have laughed, or prayed, or both. Instead I felt that terrible familiar pull, the one that had gotten me imprisoned in the first place — the hunger of a mind meeting a question worth its whole weight.

"Show me," I said.

And she did. And there in the bell tower, with Wardens patrolling the streets below and the light going amber through dirty glass, I read her map properly for the first time — not the characters alone but the structure underneath them, the grammar of refusal, and I understood enough to be afraid.

"It's not hiding the destination," I told her. "Hiding implies someone might stumble on it. These place-names aren't concealed. They're *prohibited*. There's a difference. Concealment says: you may not know. Prohibition says: it has been decided that you will never know, and the deciding is still ongoing." I traced one line of script. "This word here — I'd rendered it 'road,' in my old notes. But it's closer to *obligation*. A road that is also a duty. Whoever wrote this wasn't giving directions, August. They were assigning a task."

"To who?"

"To whoever can read it." I looked up at her. "Which, as far as I can determine, is me. Congratulations. You've broken a man out of prison to volunteer him for something."

Her expression didn't change, but her hand went to her pocket again, and this time I saw what she was holding: a small lead sigil, worn smooth, shaped like nothing I recognized. She was rubbing her thumb across it absently, the way people touch a wound.

"That means something to you," I said.

"It doesn't."

"It clearly—"

"It doesn't," she said, with the particular finality of a woman closing a door on a room she intends never to enter, and I let it go, because I was learning already how she worked. You didn't push August Blackwood. You left doors open near her and waited.

* * *

We argued about the plan, by which I mean she announced a plan and I objected to it in detail until dark.

She wanted to leave Vell that night, north through the salt marshes, following the map's first road. I pointed out that the map prohibited rather than directed, that we had no supplies, that the Wardens had doubled their patrols, and that I had been underground for six years and my legs had opinions about marshes. She pointed out that staying meant the Wardens eventually connecting the escaped convict to the woman selling illegal charms, and that my legs' opinions were noted and overruled.

"You understand," I said, as we gathered our things in the blue last light, "that I owe you nothing beyond the reading. One document, translated, and we part ways. I'm not an adventurer. I'm a scholar. Scholars annotate; they don't *die*."

"Naturally."

"I mean it. I have a life to rebuild. A pardon to appeal. There's a whole career of rehabilitation ahead of me, assuming I can convince the magistrates that the exploding wall was your initiative."

"Wasn't it?"

"...Largely."

She almost smiled. I want that on the record: August Blackwood almost smiled, and I counted it, the way a miser counts a coin, without knowing yet what I'd pay for more of them.

"One condition of my own," I said. "When this is done — whatever this is — you tell me everything you're not telling me now. The dying man. The sigil. Why the Wardens make you flinch."

"They don't make me flinch."

"You checked the window four times in the last hour and your thumb hasn't left that lead since sundown."

A long pause. Below us, a Warden paused in the street, head tilted, listening — to us, perhaps, or to something only masks hear.

"All right," she said at last. "Deal."

And that was the moment, though neither of us knew it — the handshake over a dying city, two liars agreeing to trade truths later. Trust between guarded people is never given. It's *escrowed*.

She went down the ladder first. I followed, my forbidden index against my heart, her impossible map waiting in her pocket, and somewhere below us the Wardens turned as one, all their blank faces tilting toward the chapel at once —

— and the lead sigil in her fist began, faintly, impossibly, to glow.
