## 5. Wardens at the Gate

*August*

The salt marshes south of Vell smelled like the inside of a fishmonger's boot, and I loved them immediately.

Anything that stank this thoroughly was beneath the Wardens' dignity. That was my theory, anyway, and I had built my entire adult life on theories like it: stay low, stay foul, stay forgettable. Seven years of practice. Seven years of shrinking myself down until I fit neatly into a market stall and a rented room and a silence where a god used to live.

"You're certain this is the way?" Ilen picked his way along a hummock of sedge, arms out for balance, looking like a scholar who'd been dropped into a duck pond by enemies. His boots — good boots, archive-issue, probably worth more than everything I owned — were already ruined.

"No," I said. "I'm certain the other ways have Wardens on them."

"That's not certainty."

"It's the only kind I sell."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Behind us, Vell sat on its hill with its lamps coming on one by one, warm and golden and full of people who would turn us in for the reward. Somewhere in those lamplit streets was a dead man's blood drying on the cobbles of my alley, and somewhere above him, three Wardens with hollow eyes were asking questions of anyone who'd ever bought a charm from the woman with the crooked awning.

I'd left the stall behind. The awning, the table, the little brass scale — gone, abandoned mid-morning like a snake leaving skin. It hurt less than I expected, which told me something about how much of me had ever actually lived there.

The map rode in my coat pocket, folded small, refusing to be still. Twice since dawn I'd felt it shift against my ribs like a sleeping animal turning over. Once I'd caught Ilen watching me touch it, and he'd looked away too fast, which meant he felt it too.

"Left," he said suddenly.

"Left is water."

"Left is *less* water." He squinted at the flat grey horizon where sky and marsh dissolved into each other. "There's a channel marker. Old one. And channels mean boats, and boats mean—"

"Boats mean fishermen who report strangers to the garrison for coin."

"—boats mean a route around the checkpoint at Sallow Bridge, which you would know if you'd ever read a survey chart instead of just fencing trinkets under it."

I stopped walking. He took two more steps before he noticed, and turned, and stood there in the gathering dusk with mud to his ankles and his hair plastered flat by the sea wind, waiting for me to say whatever I was going to say.

I didn't know what I was going to say. That was the problem. I'd known Ilen for exactly two days — one of which I'd spent breaking him out of an archivist's cell through a laundry chute, an experience neither of us had enjoyed — and already he'd learned how to needle me with the accuracy of someone who'd been doing it for years.

"The bridge," I said finally. "Fine. But we go wide, and if I say run, you run, and you don't ask what from. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Then, because he couldn't help himself: "You do realize 'run without asking' is how people end up following mercenaries into ditches."

"And yet here you are. Following a mercenary into a ditch."

We went left, toward the old channel marker, and the light died out of the sky, and that was when the bells started.

***

*Ilen*

I want it recorded — somewhere, in some archive that will never exist now — that I knew the bells were wrong before August did.

Vell rang its alarm bells in a pattern. Three strokes, pause, three strokes: fire. Five and five: flood or riot. What came rolling across the marshes that evening was nothing so orderly. It was a long, sustained tone, one note held past the point where any bell should have rung out of breath, as though someone had laid their whole palm against the bronze and simply *held* it.

"That's not a bell pattern," I said.

August had gone very still beside me. In the last light I could see her face doing something complicated — fear layered over calculation layered over something older and rawer underneath. "No," she said. "That's a summons. They ring it like that when they don't need volunteers."

"Wardens can't be summoned. Wardens are always—" I stopped, because even saying it felt like opening a door you couldn't close. Everyone knew the stories. The masked ones who walked out of the palace district on no schedule anyone could predict, who found practitioners in cellars and attics and crowded festivals, who never ran because they never needed to.

"They're always already deployed," August finished quietly. "Yes. So either the rules changed today, or—"

The note cut off. And in the silence after it, out on the black water of the channel, a light kindled.

Not a lantern. Lanterns flicker; this burned steady and white and *patient*, and it began to move along the waterline toward the Sallow Bridge, unhurried, the way you walk when you already know where the thing you're hunting is going to be.

"Run now?" I asked.

"Now," she agreed, and we ran.

***

*August*

Here is what nobody tells you about running through a salt marsh at night: it is mostly falling, interrupted.

The ground gave way in treacherous places — firm sedge one step, hip-deep brine the next. My lungs burned with the reek of rot and iodine. Behind us, the white light swept the channel, and ahead of us the Sallow Bridge glimmered faintly, and between us and it lay half a mile of drowning land, and I understood with perfect clarity that we were going to lose.

They don't chase you, the stories say about Wardens. They just arrive where you're going to be.

My foot went through a crust of salt-hardened mud and I went down hard, palm-first, and the pain shot up my arm — and with it, unbidden, unasked-for, rose the working.

I hadn't touched it in seven years. I'd buried it so deep I'd almost convinced myself I'd forgotten it: a small thing, a smuggler's trick, a working of unnoticing. You take the attention of everyone looking for you and pour it into the nearest empty thing — a stone, a stump, a dead man's shoe — and for a few minutes, maybe ten, they look at *that* instead. It wasn't battle-magic. It wouldn't stop a blade. It was the magic of being overlooked, which had been my whole profession even before I knew the word for it.

It was also, unmistakably, illegal. Big enough to hang for. Big enough that the last time I'd used anything near its weight, my god had still spoken to me.

And my hand was already moving. Mud-smeared fingers pressing into the wet earth, the old words rising in a language I'd sworn off like drink — and underneath the fear, underneath the guilt, something in my chest *turned over*. Like a sleeper shifting. Like an ember breathing when you finally blow on it.

Seven years of silence. And it stirred.

"*No,*" I whispered — to the god, to myself, I honestly don't know — and then I did it anyway.

The working left me like blood leaving a wound. I felt it go out across the marsh, feelers of quiet sliding over the water, over the sedge, seeking the Warden's light — and the light *paused*. Hung there over the channel, wavering. Considering a clump of dead reeds instead of two fleeing figures.

And in the space where my god had lived, that deep cellar-place behind my sternum, warmth spread. Faint. Flickering. The ghost of a voice clearing its throat after years of disuse. Not words. Not yet. Just — *attention*. Being noticed by something vast and gentle and terribly far away.

I knelt in the freezing mud with my heart slamming and my eyes burning, and I could not tell you, even now, whether what I felt was joy or grief. It was both. It was betrayal and homecoming in the same breath, the taste of stolen bread at a funeral feast. I had kept that silence like a vow, like a scar, like the only honest thing about me — *my god is dead, my god is gone, my god left* — and one forbidden working and it had rolled over in its sleep like a dog hearing its name.

"August." Ilen's hand on my shoulder. "August, it worked, whatever you did — it's stopped — but the other light, the second one, it's on the bridge itself, we need to move—"

I got up. I wiped my hands on my coat and told my face to be a mercenary's face again, and we moved.

But all the while, wading and stumbling through the dark, I kept one hand closed over the sigil hidden under my collarbone, and it stayed warm, and I hated it, and I pressed it closer.

***

*Ilen*

She cast. I saw it happen and I am trained, professionally, to notice things, and I nearly missed it — that's how fast she was, how practiced, how completely her body remembered what her mouth denied.

One moment she was kneeling in the muck like a woman praying; the next, the Warden-light had lost interest in us entirely. And August's face — I watched it in profile, lit by that pale searching glow — went through something I have only seen once before, in the purges, on the faces of people dragged from the archives who heard their forbidden names read aloud. Recognition and ruin together.

She had told me, on the laundry-chute day, in the flat tone of a woman reciting inventory: *no patron, no sigil, no craft beyond charms. My god went silent years ago. Whatever you think I am, I'm not.*

Liar. Beautiful, careful liar. And the lie had cost her something visible to pay for its breaking, because when she stood up she was crying without appearing to know it, and she wiped her face with a movement so automatic it broke my heart slightly, the way you'd brush away rain.

I didn't say anything. There are things an archivist knows better than to record aloud.

We reached the bridge by cutting through a stand of blackthorn that shredded my coat and, I suspect, August's entire remaining patience. The second light stood at the span's midpoint — tall, narrow, robed in a shapelessness that drank the lantern-glow rather than reflecting it. A Warden. Facing away from us, head tilted, listening to the marsh the way I imagined it listened to everything: for the sound of memory it could eat.

"There," August breathed. "Under the span. There's a maintenance ledge — fishermen use it. We cross beneath."

"Beneath a Warden."

"You have a better idea?"

"I have several worse ones, but none better."

We went down the near abutment hand over hand, salt slime slick under my palms, the channel water lapping black a body-length below. Above us, the boards of the bridge groaned. Above us, the Warden shifted its weight, and the sound it made was like a page turning — dry, papery, endless.

Halfway across, my grip failed. Ten years in an archive does nothing for the forearms. I slid, caught the ledge edge with two burning fingers, and hung there over the dark water with my boots kicking nothing, and I had time to think, absurdly, *the letters, the letters in my coat will be soaked* — and then August's hand locked around my wrist and hauled, and she pulled me up onto the ledge with a strength that had nothing to do with charm-stall arms, and we lay there side by side, gasping, inches apart, our breath loud as treason.

Above us, the Warden stopped moving.

The silence stretched. I could hear my own pulse. I could hear August's. I could hear, faintly, from beneath the Warden's mask — I will swear to this on any relic you name — the sound of whispering. One word, over and over, in a voice worn smooth as river stone.

A name. Someone's name. Its own, perhaps, back when it had been a person who owned one.

Then the light moved on, north along the bridge, patient as arithmetic, and August tapped my arm twice — *move* — and we crawled.

***

*August*

We made the far bank as the moon rose, fat and orange through the marsh haze, and put a mile of black country between us and the bridge before either of us spoke.

Ilen spoke first, because of course he did. "That working. The one at the channel."

"What about it?"

"It was old. Older than outlawed — the phrasing, the structure, I've seen fragments in pre-Court glosses. Where does a charm-seller learn a working like that?"

"Same place she learns everything." I kept walking. "Bad teachers and worse necessity."

"August."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than I meant, and I made myself soften it, made myself shrug. "Look. You read your books, I do my tricks, tomorrow we follow your impossible map, and nobody asks anybody questions until breakfast. Deal?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he'd let it go. He had that archivist's patience, I was learning — the patience of a man who'd spent a decade waiting for documents, who knew that everything surfaces eventually if you just keep the file open.

"Deal," he said at last. Then: "For what it's worth — whatever answered you tonight. It didn't answer like something dead."

My foot found a rut and I stumbled, and used the stumble to hide the fact that my hand had flown to my collarbone.

"It answered like something *waiting*," he said, gently, infuriatingly, and walked on ahead of me into the dark, and I let him, because if he'd been facing me he would have seen my expression, and my expression would have told him everything:

That the warmth under my collarbone was spreading. That for the first time in seven years, when I listened inward, the silence had texture — and the texture was the texture of a held breath. Of a door, somewhere very deep, propped open by something that had spent everything it had to keep it that way.

Of a god, hoarding its last strength, waiting for me to come home.

Ahead, on the moonlit road, Ilen paused and unfolded the map against his knee, and even from twenty paces I saw the ink crawl and rearrange, drawing us a line southward, toward hills that had no business existing.

"Roads that were never built," he murmured, reading a label I couldn't see. "Well. At least it's honest."

Behind us, far off across the marshes, a single white light kindled — and then another, and another — a constellation of patient eyes, all pointed the same direction.

Ours.

"Walk faster," I said.
