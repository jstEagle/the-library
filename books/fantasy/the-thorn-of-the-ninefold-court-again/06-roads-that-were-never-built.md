## 6. Roads That Were Never Built

I have spent my entire adult life in the company of dead people.

Not literally — although after three years in the royal dungeons, one develops opinions on that distinction too. I mean that my profession, before it was criminalized, was conversation with the dead: archivists speak to the vanished through their marginalia, their ledgers, the wine stains on tax records. The living lie constantly. Paper is more honest. A scribe who grumbles in the margin of a grain ledger in 214 After Founding tells you more truth than any magistrate standing in front of you wearing his best face.

So believe me when I say I know what old country looks like. I have read its soil the way other men read love letters.

And the country we walked into on the third night out of Vell was older than paper. Older than the idea of paper.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is a habit August has already scolded out of me twice. She scolds efficiently. It's one of her better qualities, right after her talent for breaking into prisons and just ahead of her habit of glaring at me like I'm a ledger she suspects of fraud.

* * *

The map had been behaving strangely all day, which for this map was roughly equivalent to a well-behaved hound suddenly refusing to heel. All morning it had lain in August's palm like a dead moth — ink faded, roads vague, the whole thing radiating the smug blankness of documents that know something you don't. Then the sun went down, and it woke up.

I saw it happen over her shoulder as we made camp in a hollow beneath a hedgerow so ancient it had probably been old when the hedges were invented. The lines darkened. Not freshened — *darkened*, as though they'd been there all along under a wash of milk, and the sunset had peeled it back. Roads unspooled across the parchment where there had been nothing but stain and guesswork. And every one of them bent, gently but unmistakably, toward the moon.

"Tell me you're seeing this," August said.

"I'm seeing it."

"Is it supposed to do that?"

"August, I have catalogued eleven thousand documents and not one of them has ever done anything except gather mildew and disappoint me. Nothing about this is *supposed*." I leaned closer. The roads were drawn in a hand I almost recognized — that looping, impatient script, the letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line. "Although. If I had to guess, I'd say the map isn't showing us where to go. It's showing us when."

She gave me the look. You'll come to know the look, if this account survives; it's the look of a woman deciding whether the strange man she broke out of prison is an asset or an obligation.

"The moon comes up," she said slowly, "and the road appears."

"And goes down, and it disappears. Yes."

"So we walk at night."

"We walk at night," I agreed, "like sensible criminals."

* * *

Here is what I will say for the road: it did not appear with trumpets. There was no shiver of enchantment, no shimmer, none of the theater that hedge-witches use to justify their fees. One moment we were picking our way through bracken by moonlight, August in front with her knife loose in its sheath and her shoulders up around her ears like a woman walking into weather. The next moment, the ground under our boots was cobbled.

Not gravel. Cobbles. Fitted stone, worn into long shallow dips by centuries — no, let me be precise, because precision was my crime and I may as well be proud of it — worn by *millennia* of feet. The stones were lichen-spotted and frost-cracked and utterly real, running straight through bramble and birch as if the forest had grown up around them politely, the way water parts around a prow.

August stopped. I stopped behind her, because following a woman who walks like that seems safer than leading.

"Well," I said. "That's inconvenient."

"Inconvenient." She crouched and put two fingers on the stone, the way you'd check a corpse for warmth. "There's a road here, Ilen. Under us. Under the *moss*. This road has been here the whole time."

"No," I said. "Worse than that. Look at the wear pattern. Cobbles don't erode from the edges inward unless they carry traffic for — " I did the arithmetic and disliked the answer. "This road was old when Vell was a fishing camp. This road may have been old when anyone was keeping records at all."

"You're telling me nobody noticed a road."

"I'm telling you nobody could notice a road. There's a difference, and the difference is going to matter." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it does when the archivist in me takes over from the man, and I hated it, because the man wanted very much to sit down and refuse to process any of this. "August. What nations have stood in this region?"

"What? None — Vell, obviously, and before that the Salt Kings, and before that — " She stopped. Her face did something complicated. "Before that, tribes. Before that, nothing. Everyone knows that. It's in the schoolbooks."

"It's in the schoolbooks because schoolbooks are written by governments, and governments are written by whoever survives. But look at this road and tell me it was built by tribes." I swept my arm at the countryside around us — the hills, the hedgerows, the distant shape of a bridge over a stream that no map of Vell's hinterlands had ever marked. "Look at the *bridge*. Tribes don't cut ashlar stone. Somebody built all of this, and then somebody made everyone forget somebody built all of this, and then the forgetting got written down as history."

The moon slid out from behind a cloud, and the road ran on ahead of us, pale and patient, vanishing into hills that I realized — with a cold settling feeling, like silt finding the bottom of a glass — appeared on no survey I had ever catalogued. And I had catalogued *all* of them. It was rather my life's work, until it became my life sentence.

August stood up. She looked down the road the way you look down a throat.

"Then we'd better see how far it goes," she said.

* * *

We walked until the small hours, and I will confess — since this narrative appears to be a confessional whether I like it or not — that part of me was happy.

There. I've said it. In the middle of all the dread, some starved little clerk-creature inside my chest sat up and wagged its tail. Because I have spent my life reading about wonders secondhand, dust on my fingers, other men's adventures pressed flat between pages, and here was a road that no book described, humming under my boots with an age that made the Salt Kings look like last week's laundry. The ditches alongside it were cut in a pattern I'd seen once, only once, in a fragment so forbidden that my predecessor had sewn it into his coat lining, and he had still died badly for it. The milestones were square-cut pillars carved with nine spiraling marks apiece. Nine. I counted them three times. Nine.

"Do you feel that?" August asked, around midnight.

"If you mean the sensation of walking through someone else's memory, yes."

"I was going to say the quiet."

That too. The quiet was wrong in a specific direction. Not empty — full. The silence of a room where many people have just left. Twice I caught myself turning to say something to a companion who wasn't there, and both times the shape of the absent remark was oddly specific, as if the road itself expected me to be traveling with someone I'd forgotten.

I didn't tell August that. I'm telling you instead, which is either cowardice or scholarship, depending on how generous you're feeling.

It was near dawn — the hour when the map's ink began to fade and the road, I assumed, would dissolve under us like a dream — that she finally asked the question I'd been waiting for. She asked it casually, watching the horizon, which is how August asks things that aren't casual at all.

"You said the Court erased itself. Back in the prison yard. You said it like it was a known fact. Known by whom?"

"Known by me," I said, "and now by you, which makes us the two most dangerous people alive, so thank you for that. Walk with me a while and I'll explain, because if I'm going to ruin your understanding of the world, I'd rather do it properly."

"That's very considerate."

"I'm a professional."

* * *

So I told her. Or I told her the shape of it, because the details would take a year and get us killed faster.

Every archive keeps its ghosts, and the royal archive of Vell kept one particular ghost: a shelf, sealed by decree older than the decree's own explanation, holding fragments of a country that official histories insisted had never existed. The Ninefold Court. Every nation's oldest records mention it the way you might mention a missing tooth — a gap in the smile of the world. Trade ledgers with tariffs paid *to whom?* Border treaties with a neighbor's name scraped off. A hymnal with nine verses and eight gods. The Court hadn't fallen. Falling leaves rubble, and there was no rubble. The Court had been *removed*, root and branch, from memory and record and — I came to believe — from the world itself. Deliberately. By its own hands.

"Why?" August said. "Who does that? Who erases themselves?"

"A dying man burns his own letters," I said. "A mother scrubs a dead child's name off the doorframe because she can't bear to read it. Erasure isn't madness, August. It's grief with power behind it. Something was wrong with the Court — wrong enough that its own gods agreed to unmake every trace of it. Eight of them, anyway."

She was quiet for a while. The road carried us along through the grey beginning of morning, and the milestones' nine spirals kept sliding past, and I watched her turn something over in her head the way she turned charms over in her fingers.

"And the erased things," she said at last. "Where do they go? People don't just stop being remembered cleanly. Grief doesn't lift. It *scars*."

I stopped walking.

Of course she'd ask that. Of course she, of all people, would put her finger directly on the wound in the middle of the whole affair, the thing I had spent years circling in the sealed stacks without daring to touch. That's the trouble with mercenaries. They think like surgeons.

"They leave thorns," I said.

"Thorns."

"That's the word in the fragments. *Spinae memoriae* in the old liturgical script — thorns of memory. When something is erased, the removal doesn't heal clean. It leaves a scar in everything that touched it. A hole shaped like what was lost. Villages built on land the Court held can't say why they feel haunted. Songs lose their final verse and no composer knows why. People wake grieving and can't name the beloved. The erasure takes the thing, but the love that pointed at the thing — the love has nowhere to go. So it stays. Hooked in. Like a thorn under the skin, working deeper, and no one remembers there was ever anything to pull out."

August had gone very still. In the growing light I could see her right hand, the one she kept half-closed most of the time, and I could see that she was pressing her thumb hard against her palm. Against something. I thought at the time it was just a habit, a gambler's tic, a mercenary keeping her sword-hand honest.

I am a professional, as I said. I am also, occasionally, an idiot.

"Say the rest," she said quietly.

"The rest is worse. The thorns don't fade. They accumulate. Seven hundred years of accumulated grief for a country nobody remembers, soaked into the land like rain into chalk. And the fragments are clear on one more point, which is the point I've been avoiding saying out loud since Vell." I made myself look at her. "Erasure can be reversed. The thorns remember. Everything the Court took is still *in* them — filed, hooked, waiting. Which means somewhere, someone or something is going to try to pull them. And I do not know what happens to the world when seven hundred years of buried love comes up all at once. Neither does anyone else. That's rather the problem."

The sun crested the hills behind us, and under our feet, I felt — felt, not heard — the road sigh. Like a sleeper rolling over. Like a patient whose fever has broken, or hasn't, and is dreaming hard.

August opened her right hand.

Across her palm, old and white and precisely healed, ran a scar I had noticed the first day and filed, professionally, as knife damage. Up close, in good light, it was not knife damage. It was the shape of a thorn. A long, curved barb, exactly the kind the black brambles along this road wore on their stems, printed into her flesh like a seal in wax.

"Ilen," she said, and her voice was steady in the way that ice over deep water is steady, "when were you going to mention that the erasure scars *people*?"

I looked at her scarred hand. I looked at the ninth spiral on the nearest milestone, worn nearly smooth by thumbs rubbing it, over centuries, the way a saint's foot gets kissed away.

"About the time you showed me that," I said honestly. "Which is now. So: new information for both of us. The road ends, according to the map, at a settlement half a day on. A village."

"A village."

"A village that appears on no survey, no census, no tithe roll, in any archive, in any century. A village that shouldn't exist, sitting in the middle of a country that forgot it. Given what we've just established about thorns and the people they hook into—" I gestured down the pale length of the road, toward the hills, toward whatever was waiting there with its polite, hollowed-out face. "I suggest we find out whether the people who live there still remember how to answer questions. Or whether we're walking into seven hundred years of grief wearing human skin."

August closed her hand over the thorn-scar, slow, like shutting a book she wasn't done reading, and started walking.

"Keep up, archivist," she said. "You're the one who wanted to ruin my understanding of the world."

And the road, patient as a held breath, carried us toward the village without a name.
