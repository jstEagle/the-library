## 7. The Village Without a Name

The rain found us an hour before the village did.

I want to say I saw the rooftops first, that some mercenary instinct in me picked out chimney smoke against the gray like it used to pick out ambushes. The truth is less flattering: I was so wet and so tired that when the houses appeared between the birches, my first thought wasn't *threat* or *opportunity*. It was *roof*.

"Tell me you see that," Ilen said behind me.

"I see it."

"Good. For a moment I worried we'd graduated from roads that don't exist to villages that don't exist, and honestly, August, I'm not sure my dignity survives that."

"Your dignity died somewhere around the prison cell," I said. "I've been carrying it for both of us."

We came down into the village the way rain comes down — slowly, without anyone deciding it. It sat in a shallow bowl of land with its back to a hill, maybe forty houses, slate roofs gone soft with moss, a well in the center square wearing a little stone roof like a hat. Chickens. A dog that looked up at us with the profound indifference of an animal that has never once been surprised. Smoke rising from chimneys in thin, unhurried threads.

Everything about it was ordinary except for one thing, and it took me a while to find that thing, because it was shaped like an absence.

There were no signs. No carved names over doors, no painted marks on the wells, no shrine-post at the crossroads with the village's name on it — which is unusual, because even the poorest hamlet in Vell's hinterlands has *some* marker, some claim staked into the earth saying *this place is called this, these people are from here.* This village had nothing. It was as if someone had built a town and then carefully peeled the label off.

"They'll have bread," Ilen said, with the reverent tone of a man who had eaten nothing but hardtack and regret for six days. "Villages have bread. It's practically their defining feature."

"Villages also have people who ask questions," I said. "And we're two strangers walking out of a wood that isn't on any map, following directions given by a dying man, one of us visibly a foreigner and both of us visibly lying about something. Try not to be memorable."

"I'm always memorable. It's a curse of the face."

He did have a face worth remembering — sharp-featured, ink-stained fingers, hair going gray at the temples in a way that made him look distinguished instead of tired, which I suspected was a trick he'd practiced. But I didn't tell him that. Six days on the road had taught me that Ilen collected compliments the way other men collected debts: quietly, patiently, waiting for them to come due.

The first house we knocked on belonged to a woman named — well. That was the problem.

She opened the door before I finished knocking, a broad comfortable woman with flour on her forearms, and she smiled at us like we were expected. "Come in out of the wet," she said. "You'll want soup."

"We will," Ilen said instantly, betraying every principle of caution I'd tried to instill. "Thank you. We're travelers, and we seem to have lost our way—"

"Oh, everyone does," she said, stepping aside to let us in. "Everyone does, sooner or later. Sit, sit."

Her house was warm and low-beamed and smelled of onions and woodsmoke, and it should have been the safest-feeling room I'd stood in since Vell burned behind us. Instead something at the base of my skull kept whispering *wrong*, the way it whispers when a floor is pitched half a degree off true. I couldn't name it yet. I've learned since that I should have trusted it immediately. That's the thing about instincts: they're always right, and I'm always late.

The woman set out bowls. Her name, when Ilen asked it, took her a moment too long to arrive.

"Maren," she said finally, like she was reading it off the inside of her own eyelid. "I think. Yes. Maren."

"You *think*?"

"It's been a long time since anyone asked." She laughed, and the laugh was easy, but her eyes went somewhere else for a heartbeat — somewhere with no walls and no furniture — and came back. "People don't ask here. You know how it is in small places. Everyone knows everything already, so nobody says any of it."

That was the wrongness, I realized. Not the missing signs. The missing *saying*.

* * *

We stayed the night, because the rain got worse and because Maren's soup was genuinely excellent and because I told myself we were gathering information, which is what mercenaries say instead of admitting they're tired. Ilen charmed the table. He does this — not with magic, which would have gotten us killed twice over, but with talk, questions dressed up as small courtesies. How many families live here? What do they farm? Where does the road go when it leaves the valley?

And every answer came back the same shape.

"How long have you lived here?" he asked the miller, a lean man with hands like tree roots.

"All my life," the miller said comfortably.

"And your father before you?"

"Yes."

"And his father?"

The miller smiled and poured more beer. "Yes."

"And how long is that? What year was the mill built? Who built it?"

"Well." The miller considered this with the good humor of a man being asked a riddle he knows has no answer. "Someone must have. It's a good mill."

It went like that all evening, question after question sliding off these people like rain off oiled cloth. They knew *how* to do everything — bake, mill, mend roofs, birth calves — and knew *nothing* about anything else. Not their history. Not their neighbors' histories. Not where the road south went (it went away, they agreed; it certainly went away). Not what lay beyond the hill. One old man told us, cheerfully, that he had lived in the village for either thirty years or sixty, and that both figures felt true, and that it didn't matter much either way, did it?

"No," I said, when Ilen glanced at me. "I suppose it doesn't."

But it mattered. It mattered enormously. I'd grown up around soldiers who'd taken head wounds, and there's a particular quality to their forgetting — not grief, not confusion, just a smooth polite surface where the memory used to be, like water closing over a stone. These people were whole villages of that. Generations deep.

They weren't sad about it. That was the worst part. There was no mourning in Maren's house, no sense of loss pressing at the windows. Just a vast, gentle, courteous emptiness, papered over with soup and chickens and the pleasant conviction that someone must have built the mill, because it was a good mill.

A people who had lost themselves *politely*. Who apologized for not knowing, and offered you bread instead, and meant it kindly, and never once noticed that the hole where their past should be was big enough to drive a cart through.

Ilen figured it out before I did, sitting by their fire with his bowl cradled in his ink-stained hands. I watched him get there — watched his eyes move from the miller to the rafters to me, doing whatever archivist arithmetic he did, counting something invisible.

"August," he said quietly, in trade-tongue, the language none of the villagers seemed troubled by. "Look at the joinery."

"What about it?"

"The roof beams. Look at how they're cut." His voice stayed light, conversational, a man making small talk. "Mortise joints without pegs. Interlocking geometry. That's not peasant carpentry, that's — that's *engineered*. That's a tradition of building that takes centuries to develop, and it doesn't exist anywhere in any archive I have ever catalogued. And the millstone — the grain of the stone, August. I've seen that stone before."

"Where?"

"In a fragment. In a book that was banned before I was born, describing a country that officially never existed." He turned his bowl slowly in his hands. "These people aren't hiding from the Court. These people are what's left of it."

* * *

I slept badly in the loft above Maren's kitchen, which was normal; I sleep badly everywhere, it's one of my talents. Sometime past midnight I gave up and climbed down for water, and that's when I found the child.

She was perhaps seven, sitting alone in the main room with a stub of charcoal and a sheet of paper — real paper, good paper, the kind that costs money — drawing. She didn't look up when I came down. Children on the road learn to ignore adults; children in houses forget to, mostly. This one ignored me with a strange total absorption, bent over her work like the charcoal was the only thing keeping the world attached.

"Can't sleep?" I asked.

She shook her head without looking up.

"What are you drawing?"

"A door," she said.

And she was. Over and over, across the whole sheet, in the quick confident strokes of a child drawing something she has seen ten thousand times: a door. Tall, narrow, framed in what looked like carved stone, with a lintel worked in patterns I couldn't quite parse at that angle — geometric, interlocking, the same grammar as those impossible roof beams. She drew it again. And again. Each one identical down to the smallest flourish, each one placed on the page with the same careful spacing, rows and rows of the same door marching across the paper like a prayer written by someone who didn't know the words anymore.

"Do you draw anything else?" I asked. "Horses? Houses? Your family?"

"Horses sometimes," she said. "But the door wants drawing." She said it the way another child might say *the song wants singing* — no mystery in it, no awareness that *wants* was a strange word to use. "Every night it wants drawing, and then I can sleep."

My mouth had gone dry. "Where is this door? Have you seen it somewhere?"

She frowned at the paper, considering, and the frown had the same polite blankness as the miller's smile. "No," she said. "But it's mine. I can tell it's mine." She drew another door. "Do you know its name?"

"The door's name?"

"The door's name," she confirmed seriously. "Nobody knows its name. I thought maybe a traveler would."

Somewhere upstairs, Ilen shifted in his sleep, and a beam settled with a soft wooden knock, and I stood in a stranger's kitchen at midnight holding a cup of water I no longer wanted, looking at a child filling a page with the front door of a vanished country.

"It doesn't have a name," I heard myself say. "Not anymore."

"That's sad," the child said, without sadness, and drew another one.

* * *

I woke Ilen. He grumbled, then saw my face and stopped grumbling, which is the fastest way I know to sober that man up. I showed him the paper — the child had surrendered it without ceremony, asking only if I wanted charcoal too — and watched him hold it under the banked fire's coals until his expression did something complicated.

"This is the ninth gate," he whispered. "Or — it matches the fragment. The descriptions of the Court's gates. The lintel pattern is — August, this is a *record*. This child is drawing a document she doesn't know she's copying. The erasure took the histories, took the archives, took the names, and it missed—" he tapped the paper, gently, like it might bruise, "—it missed whatever lives below memory. Habit. The hand. The thing that draws when the mind isn't watching."

"So they're not hollowed out," I said. "They're sealed."

"Is there a difference?"

"There's a difference." My voice came out harder than I intended. "A hollowed-out thing is empty. A sealed thing has something inside it that somebody went to a great deal of trouble to keep quiet. Somebody did this to them, Ilen. Eight gods and a working so large it ate a country, and what's left is a village full of kind people who can't remember their own grandmother's name but whose *children* still draw the door every night because the truth got so far under their skin that forgetting couldn't reach it."

Ilen was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on his face.

"That's either hopeful or horrifying," he said.

"Both. It's usually both, with this map."

He set the paper down between us. Outside, the rain had stopped, and through the shutter-cracks I could see moonlight beginning to pick out the wet slate roofs, the well with its little stone hat, the sleeping village that had no name because it had given its name away — or had it taken, I still don't know, and I suspect the distinction matters more than I want it to.

"We should leave at dawn," Ilen said. "Before we learn why the map brought us here."

"The map didn't bring us here," I said. "The map shows roads. Something else decides what's on them."

"Then *something else* should explain itself."

But in the morning, before we'd finished saddling our borrowed mules, Maren pressed a loaf into my hands and held my wrist a moment longer than courtesy required. Her eyes had that faraway look again, the one with no walls in it.

"You'll find it, won't you," she said. It wasn't quite a question. "Whatever it is that everyone's looking for without knowing. You have the look of someone who's going to open something."

"I don't—"

"My mother used to stand at the window on rainy nights," Maren said, "and watch the road. Except we haven't had a mother's-name-day festival in — in as long as — " She stopped. Frowned, politely, at the hole where the rest of the sentence should have been. Then she smiled, warm and apologetic, and patted my wrist, and let go.

"Safe travels," she said. "Come again someday, if you remember the way."

Behind her, in the doorway of the kitchen, the child was watching us go, charcoal-smudged fingers curled around a fresh sheet of paper.

She wasn't drawing horses.
