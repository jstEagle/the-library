## 9. The First Thorn

We ran.

I want to say we ran heroically — cloaks snapping, dawn breaking behind us like a promise — but the truth is that we ran the way two people run when they've had four hours of sleep and no breakfast: badly, in the wrong direction twice, once through somebody's turnip patch, with me swearing in three languages and Ilen apologizing to the turnips.

The bell behind us kept its rhythm. Long strokes, evenly spaced. It didn't speed up when we sped up, which was somehow worse. A bell that chases you is a bell that doesn't need to hurry.

"The Wardens don't come at night," Ilen panted beside me. "That's documented. They're bound to—"

"Your documents are from a country that deleted itself, Ilen."

"Point taken."

We crested the ridge as the sky went grey, and I looked back once. The village sat below us in its little bowl of fog, lamps still lit against nothing, and beyond it the road was empty. No riders. No pale masks. Just the bell, ringing on, patient as arithmetic.

"They're not following," I said.

"That's worse," said Ilen.

"Yes."

Because if the Wardens weren't chasing us, it meant they didn't need to chase us. It meant the land itself was doing their work — and the map agreed, because when I pulled it out, shivering in the wind, the ink had rearranged itself overnight. Our road no longer ran east along the river. It cut north now, straight across a stretch of country that the map rendered not as lines and hills but as a single dense black smudge, hatched over like a scar, with one word in that script Ilen half-knew:

He read it aloud, sounding it out the way you'd sound out a word you hoped was harmless. "*Bramblefield.* Or possibly 'graveyard of hedges.' The grammar's ambiguous."

"Wonderful. Which do you vote for?"

"I vote we go around."

I turned the map sideways. Around was a hundred miles of nothing. Through was maybe five. My god's sigil — the dead little knot of silver at my sternum, silent seven years, warm now in a way that frightened me more than any bell — pulsed once toward the smudge. Toward the thorns.

"It wants us to go through," I said.

"It's a piece of jewelry, August."

"It's my *god*, and it wants us to go through." I heard how that sounded and grimaced. "Look, everything it has nudged us toward so far has been horrible, but it hasn't been wrong. The shrine. The village. The ninth niche. If it's steering us into a field of brambles, there's something in those brambles it needs me to see."

"And if it's steering you into a trap?"

"Then it's the trap I was always going to walk into anyway." I folded the map. "Might as well arrive on schedule."

Ilen looked at me for a long moment. He does this thing where he opens his mouth to argue and then visibly reorganizes his argument into something he can live with losing. "You know," he said finally, "in the archive we had a term for people who took theological direction from their accessories."

"What term?"

"*Early retirements*."

***

The thorn-field announced itself before we saw it.

First the birds stopped. Not flew away — stopped, mid-morning chorus, all at once, like someone lifting a needle off a record. Then the light changed. It came down yellowish and thick, the way it comes through honey or old glass, and the air smelled of green things rotting gently, sweetly, the smell of a hedge left a hundred years uncut.

Then we saw it.

It wasn't a field. Fields have edges. This was a sea — a rolling, black-green sea of bramble that stretched from the tree line to the horizon, thicketed higher than our heads, arched over into tunnels and caverns of thorn. Every stem was thick as my wrist and armored in hooks the length of my finger. And it was all *moving* — slowly, barely, the way a sleeping animal breathes — whole acres of it shifting with a wind I couldn't feel on my face.

Ilen made a small sound I'd never heard from an archivist before. Something between reverence and nausea.

"This is a thorn," he whispered. "This is what he meant. What the villagers' stories meant. Erased things leave thorns — scars in the world, scars in memory. But I thought—" He swallowed. "I thought it was a figure of speech."

"Archivists." I said it kindly. "Everything's a figure of speech until it eats you."

The path, when we found it, was almost insultingly neat: a corridor cut through the briar, just wide enough for two, floored with packed dark earth. As if the field wanted visitors. As if it had set the table.

My sigil burned. Not the faint warmth of the last weeks — burned, a coal laid flat against my breastbone, and beneath the burn I felt something else, something I hadn't felt since before Vell outlawed magic, since before my god went quiet: attention. Being *watched*. Being, gods help me, *accompanied*.

"You feel that too?" I asked it silently. "Good. Then whatever happens in here, you get to explain it to me after."

The sigil flared, which I chose to interpret as agreement and Ilen would later insist was indigestion of the divine.

We walked in.

***

Inside, the world was green-black and close. The briar arched overhead so completely that daylight came through in coins and slivers. The breathing movement of the field surrounded us — that vast slow swell, in and out, in and out — and after a while I realized the rhythm matched my own breath. When I breathed shallow, the tunnels tightened. When I breathed deep, the way widened.

"It's reading us," Ilen murmured. "August. The field is *reading* us."

"Then let's be boring."

But I should have known better. I've spent seven years being boring, being careful, being a woman shrunk down to fit survival, and none of it had ever protected me from the thing that actually lived inside me: the fact that I am, at bottom, full of holes. Gaps where a childhood should be. Blank stretches where other people keep faces and names. I'd always assumed the gaps were mine — damage I'd done myself, a mercenary's habit of burning her own past for fuel. Walking under those arching thorns, I began to wonder whose damage I'd been carrying, and on whose behalf.

An hour in, the sigil began pulling. Left. Off the path.

"No," I told it.

It pulled harder. The burn sharpened into language, almost — the way a dream almost makes sense right before you wake. *Left. Now. Her.*

Her?

I stepped off the path.

"August—" Ilen started.

"One minute."

The brambles parted for me. I want to lie about that, make it sound like a struggle, but the truth is the thorns drew aside like courtiers, like something making room, and I walked three steps off the safe packed earth into the living heart of the field, and there grew a single bramble different from all the rest.

It stood alone in a small clearing, no taller than my knee. Its thorns were white instead of black. Its leaves were the green of new spring, absurdly young amid all that ancient dark. And it was trembling — actually trembling, the way a dog trembles, the way a person trembles when they've been waiting a long time and are afraid the waiting will end badly.

I knelt.

Don't ask me why. Ask my hands; they knew. I reached out and laid my palm flat against the white thorns, the way you'd touch the forehead of someone feverish, someone you loved—

—and my sister came home to me.

Not all at once. In pieces, the way water finds cracks. Her name — *Sera* — arriving like a blow. Her face, rounder than mine, freckled across the nose, a gap in her teeth she never let close because she whistled through it and thought it was a talent. Her hand in mine on a road I suddenly remembered: dust, evening, a city wall behind us with nine banners coming down. She was laughing. Why was she laughing? Because I'd told her — I could hear myself saying it, seven years ago or seventy, my voice young and terrified and certain — I'd told her, *they can't erase what we won't put down*, and she'd laughed and squeezed my hand and said, *then hold on tight, August.*

Hold on tight.

She had been real. She had been *mine*. Seven years I'd walked around with a Sera-shaped hole in my chest and called it loneliness, called it the price of the trade, called it anything except what it was, because the truth was too big to fit through the door I'd built my life out of. I had a sister. I had held her hand while the world forgot itself. I had—

The thorn drank.

I felt it happen. That's the part I'll never manage to say properly, the part that wakes me up with my heart going like a war drum. It wasn't pain. Pain would have been honest. It was *drainage* — a cool, gentle pull, almost soothing, like fever breaking, like a knot unknotting — and the memory went with it. Not faded. Faded would imply the memory still existed somewhere, dimmed, recoverable. This was *removal*. The name went first. Then the gap in the teeth. Then the hand, then the laugh, then the road, then the feeling itself — that fierce specific love, scooped out of me clean as marrow from a bone — and last of all the knowledge that anything had ever been there at all.

I knelt in the clearing with my palm bleeding lightly against a small white bramble, and I felt grief with no object. Mourning for a funeral I couldn't remember attending. Tears on my face and absolutely nothing in me that knew why.

"August!" Ilen crashed through the briar behind me, wild-eyed, and caught my shoulders. "Gods — your face — what happened? What did you see?"

And here is the horror, the true horror, better than any Warden: I opened my mouth and said, "Nothing. I don't know. I just — needed to touch it."

I believed it. That's the worst part. I *believed* it, wholly, even as the tears kept coming, even as some animal cellar-deep part of me screamed that something enormous had just been taken, that I had just lost — *her* — that I had held her hand and promised to hold on—

The scream got exactly as far as *her* and hit the hole where she used to be and fell into it, and I stood up, and wiped my face, and said, "We should keep moving," in a perfectly reasonable voice.

Ilen was staring at the bramble. At the white thorns, which were no longer white. They'd gone black as all the rest, sated, and the little plant stood still now, no longer trembling. Sleeping. Full.

"August." His voice had gone very quiet, the way it did in the shrine, the way men speak in places where loudness feels like sacrilege. "What did it take?"

"Nothing. I told you, I don't—"

"It was white, August." He crouched, not touching it, reading it like a page. "Every account I've ever found of the Court's purges — every fragment, every banned marginal note — describes thorns that drink. Memory-thorns. They start white. They feed on—" He stopped. Started again, gentler. "They feed on what you love. That's the currency. Not fear, not pain, not years of your life. Love. Love is what the Court runs on. Love is what it *ate*."

The words went into me and found the hole and echoed.

I looked down at my bleeding palm. And underneath the reasonable voice, underneath the nothing-I-don't-know, something in me rose up with its hackles up and its teeth bared — something older than the forgetting, something the thorn hadn't reached because I'd never spoken it aloud and therefore never given it a handle to drink from:

*Never again.*

Whatever else this country wanted from me, whatever tolls waited at whatever doors — it would not get another thing I loved. I would walk the rest of this road with my heart shut like a strongbox, like a grave, like Vell's city gates at curfew, and if the ninth door itself asked nicely I would tell it what I told the dockworkers who tried to haggle: fixed price, no credit, no exceptions.

"August?" Ilen was still watching me. He's infuriatingly good at watching me. "You went somewhere just now. Where did you go?"

"Nowhere," I said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it. "Just — deciding something."

"About what?"

"About how much of me is for sale." I turned back toward the path. "Answer: less than I thought."

***

We made the far edge of the field by late afternoon, and I won't pretend the crossing after the clearing was anything but silent. Ilen kept glancing at me the way you glance at a cracked foundation — worried, calculating load. I kept my eyes forward and my hand closed around the ache in my chest like a fist around a coin I refused to spend.

At the field's edge the briar thinned, and the last tunnel opened onto ordinary country: brown grass, real wind, a sky going copper with evening. Behind us the thorn-sea breathed on, vast and patient, full of everything it had ever drunk.

Ilen paused at the border. "One question," he said. "And you don't have to answer."

"Noted."

"When your sigil pulled you off the path — toward that bramble specifically — did it pull you toward danger? Or did it pull you toward *her*?" His voice was careful. "Did your god want you to know you'd lost something? Even knowing the knowing wouldn't survive?"

I opened my mouth to say I don't know what — *don't be absurd*, probably, some deflection with good structural bones —

— and my sigil burned, once, hard, and for a quarter of a second I was on my knees in the clearing again, and a hand was squeezing mine, and a voice was saying *hold on tight, Aug*, and the name attached to the voice was a shape with nothing inside it, a doorway with the house demolished behind it, and then it was gone again, gone, gone, and I was standing in copper light with wet eyes and no idea, no idea at all, why.

"August?"

"I don't know," I said. And then, because lying to him had started to cost more than the truth: "Ilen — whatever's ahead of us, whatever the door asks — promise me you'll watch me. If I start giving things away without noticing. If parts of me start going missing and I smile about it." I held up my bleeding palm between us, the blood already browning, already looking like a mark rather than a wound. "Someone has to keep count of what I lose. Because I'm starting to suspect I won't."

He looked at my hand for a long time. Then he took off his coat — the heavy archive coat, the one he guarded like a second skin, the one he slept in and complained about and never once let me carry — and draped it over my shoulders against the evening cold.

"Counting's what I'm for," he said.

And I noticed — filed away, in the strongbox, behind the lock — that he flinched, just slightly, when the coat left his body. As if there was something in it heavier than wool. As if he was carrying something he hadn't told me about, and had just moved it further from my reach.

I decided, in the spirit of the day, not to ask.

Yet.
