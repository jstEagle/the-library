## 10. What Ilen Keeps in His Coat

There are three things every disgraced archivist learns before the ink dries on his sentence: how to sleep sitting up, how to eat without appearing to enjoy it (enjoyment invites conversation, and conversation invites questions about why a man of your standing is eating prison rations), and how to keep secrets so deep inside yourself that even you begin to forget where you buried them.

I had been practicing the third skill for eleven years. That night, by the fire, I finally failed at it.

We had made camp in the lee of a ruined wall — not ruined the way ruins usually are, with rubble and history and a plaque some civic-minded committee nailed up before the committee itself was forgotten, but ruined the way things are ruined when they are *unmade*. The stones were whole but wrong. They fit together in patterns that hurt to look at directly, like arithmetic done by someone who had learned numbers from a dream. August said she thought the wall was beautiful. August also ate pickled eels straight from the jar, so her aesthetic judgments were, in my professional opinion, unreliable.

The map lay between us on my coat, folded into its usual sulk. It did that now — sulked — whenever we stopped moving. Roads crawled across its surface like insects under cloth, rearranging themselves, refusing to commit to geography the way I have never committed to a haircut.

"You're staring at it again," August said.

"I'm studying it. Staring implies affection."

"You've been studying it for an hour. Your eyes went glassy around minute forty."

"That was contemplation."

"That was you falling asleep upright and catching yourself. You do it every night. It's very dignified. Like watching a horse decide against lying down."

I chose to let that pass, because she was right, and because arguing with August Blackwood was a sport where she held both the sword and the rulebook. Instead I began gathering the map, and that was when it happened — the corner of the fold caught on something inside my coat, and a sheaf of papers slid halfway out, tied with a ribbon gone grey from handling.

August saw them. Of course she did. She had the reflexes of a cat and the tact of a collapsing bookshelf.

"What's that?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing," she repeated, "that you keep sewn into your inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, tied like a relic."

"It's archival material."

"You slept with it through a prison break. You slept with it while we were wading through a salt marsh. You slept with it during that entire day in the nameless village, which — for the record — you spent snoring in a hayloft while children stared at us like we were weather."

"They were staring at *you*," I said. "You gave one of them a luck charm. In a village full of hollowed-out descendants of a vanished empire, you handed a child contraband enchantment. I've been meaning to raise this formally."

"And I've been meaning to ignore it formally." She nodded at the papers. "Show me."

"August—"

"Ilen." My name in her mouth had a particular weight. She didn't use it often. When she did, it wasn't a request; it was a door being opened whether or not you were dressed to receive visitors.

I should tell you what I was afraid of, since apparently we're being honest tonight. It wasn't that she would mock the letters. August was many things — reckless, profane under pressure, constitutionally incapable of walking past a locked thing without trying the handle — but she was not cruel. No. What I was afraid of was simpler and worse: that she would understand them. That she would read what I carried and see, laid out plain as a catalog entry, exactly how much of me had died eleven years ago and never bothered to file a report.

But she was looking at me across the fire with those grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less, and I thought: *you asked her to trust you with her god's silence. You watched her touch a thorn and lose a sister she never knew she had, and you held her arm while she shook.* Fairness is a debt. Even archivists pay their debts.

I untied the ribbon.

* * *

"There were nine hundred and forty-one documents in the restricted vault of the Royal Archive," I told her. "Concerning the Ninefold Court. Histories, treaties, tax rolls — yes, tax rolls, the Court kept meticulous records right up until the moment it decided to stop existing, which tells you everything about bureaucracies everywhere. Maps. Correspondence. A child's primer, teaching letters alongside the names of nine gods."

"Your gods," August said quietly. Her hand had drifted to her palm, where the sigil lived under the skin like a splinter of old light. "Our gods. If yours is the ninth."

"If mine is the ninth," I agreed. "The point is: nine hundred and forty-one documents. And an order came down — sealed, royal seal, wax still warm from the signet — instructing me to burn all of them. Not catalog them. Not move them to deeper storage. Burn. With witnesses. With a written attestation afterward confirming that no trace remained, signed in triplicate." I smiled the smile I'd perfected for tribunal audiences. "They wanted the erasure *documented*, August. They wanted proof of forgetting, filed alphabetically. I have never decided whether that's the funniest or saddest thing I know."

"Which did you choose?"

"Both. Alternately. It depends on how recently I've eaten." I turned the bundle over in my hands. "I burned eight hundred and ninety of them. I want that understood. I am not a hero. Heroes burn nothing; martyrs burn everything. I was a tired man in his thirties who understood that if he refused outright, they'd simply replace him with someone who'd light the match faster, and then there would be *nothing left*. So I burned, and I lied in my attestation, and I hid fifty-one documents inside the bindings of agricultural ledgers nobody had opened since the reign of a king whose name means, roughly, 'damp.'"

"Damp."

"The historians of that era were not poets."

"And they caught you anyway."

"They caught me because love makes people stupid, and I was already stupid, and I compounded it." I drew out the top letter. The paper was soft as moth wing. "Her name was Sefa Vane. Under-librarian, west annex. Sharp tongue. Better memory than mine, which she considered her single greatest virtue and my single greatest failing. We used to argue about whether an archive was a building or a promise. She said a promise. I said a building with good drainage. She was right. She was always right, which I told her constantly, and which she somehow never tired of hearing."

August said nothing. The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere out in the dark, the un-country breathed — that low, patient sound the land made here, like a sleeper deciding whether to wake.

"She found out what I'd hidden. Found the ledger trick within a week — she knew my handwriting better than I did, could spot when I'd faked a margin note. And instead of turning me in, she started helping. Copying. Memorizing. Sefa had the kind of memory that didn't store facts so much as *become* them. She could recite the Court's founding treaty backward. She learned the primer by heart — the children's primer, with the nine gods' names in it." My thumb moved over the faded ink. "When the audit came, they searched the annexes. She took the blame. Said she'd stolen the documents herself, that I'd known nothing, and she recited — in front of the tribunal, as evidence of her guilt — passages from the founding treaty that officially did not exist."

"Oh, Sefa," August whispered. It was the wrong pronoun and the right one, and I felt it land somewhere beneath my ribs.

"Purges need examples. A librarian who remembers forbidden things is a better example than a soldier who kills people, because soldiers are replaceable and librarians are supposed to be obedient. She was executed in the Archive courtyard. I was made to attend. Attendance was mandatory." I heard my own voice go flat and distant, the way it does when I describe catalog systems. Flat was safer. "These are her letters. Forty-one of them. She wrote most of them in the last year, knowing what was coming — she'd known for months, I think, before I did. She wrote them and hid them inside the bindings too, interleaved with the documents, so that when I eventually found what I'd saved, I'd find her as well. She planned her own afterlife, August. Filed and cross-referenced. She'd have wanted a finding aid."

I passed him — passed *her*, passed the bundle — across the fire. August took it the way you take something breakable, which was ironic, because the letters were past breaking. Paper that has survived executioners doesn't scare easily.

She unfolded the top one and read by firelight. I watched her face do what faces do with Sefa's writing: start composed, then flicker, then go carefully, deliberately still, the way you hold still when something large walks past in the dark.

"'Tell Ilen,'" August read aloud, softly, "'that a building can burn and a promise can't. Tell him he owes me one argument, and I'm calling it in wherever I am. Tell him the drainage in the west annex is a scandal and always was.'" She looked up. Her eyes were bright and furious in the way that August's eyes got — grief processed immediately into anger at whatever had caused it, in this case an empire that had erased itself and taken a woman with perfect recall along as ballast. "She sounds like she'd have liked me."

"She'd have found you exhausting," I said. "Then liked you enormously. It was her order of operations for everyone."

August read two more letters in silence. Then, instead of handing them back, she sat holding them, and I watched her arrive at a decision — you could practically hear the gears, she was so transparent sometimes, a mercenary with the poker face of an open drawer.

"My turn, then," she said.

"Your turn for what?"

"Fair's fair. You showed me the corpse in your coat. I show you the corpse in mine." She set the letters down gently, which I noted, and filed, and knew I'd be rereading the gesture later at 3 a.m. like the sentimental idiot I was. "You know my god went silent seven years ago. Everyone assumes I did something wrong. Prayed badly. Offended it. Practitioners talk — the ones still alive — and they look at me the way you look at a house where the family died. Cursed-by-association." She pulled her sleeve back. The sigil sat on her forearm, a closed knot of lines like a thorn drawn in ink that had decided to become flesh. "It hasn't spoken since. Hasn't warmed. Hasn't *anything*. Seven years of talking to something and getting weather back." She flexed her hand. "Here's the part I don't tell anyone. I don't remember what it sounded like."

"You don't—"

"Not the words. Those I remember, mostly, the last ones. I mean the voice. The *sound* of it. I know I knew it. I know it was the most familiar sound in the world, more than rain, more than my own heartbeat. And there's just — a hole. Shaped exactly like it. Smooth edges. Like the silence was cut rather than fallen." She met my eyes across the fire. "Archivist. Does that sound like a god abandoning someone? Because it sounds to me like surgery."

I thought about the primer. About the ninth niche in a village shrine, empty in a way that differed from the other eight empties — I hadn't been able to articulate how, yet, but the difference itched in me the way an uncatalogued document itches, present and misfiled. About the thorn-field, and the way forgetting here didn't fade but *excised*.

"No," I said slowly. "It sounds like erasure. It sounds exactly like what the Court did to itself — except the Court consented. All nine of them, supposedly. But if eight consented and the ninth refused—" I stopped. The thought finished itself in the silence, and I watched August arrive at the same place, and neither of us said it aloud, because saying it aloud would make her silence not a wound but a *crime scene*, and there is a kind of knowledge you cannot give back.

"We'll get there," August said at last. Her voice was rough. "To the answer. Whatever it is." Then, because she was August, she deflected with the grace of a thrown elbow: "If your Sefa could memorize the founding treaty, could she have memorized the map's script? The parts you can't read?"

"Parts I can't read *yet*," I corrected. "And yes. She'd have taken one look and started reciting it back to me within the hour, probably with commentary on the penmanship." The ache arrived on schedule, right on time, eleven years punctual. "That's why I carry them. Not sentiment. Utility. She was the finest mind I ever—"

"Ilen."

"—and because if I forget the sound of her—"

"*Ilen.*"

I stopped. The fire popped. August leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't catalog, which for a man of my profession is essentially a religious experience.

"You don't have to make her useful," she said. "She's allowed to just be hers. That's — God. That's the whole point of any of this, isn't it? The Court forgot its people politely, generation by generation, until nobody could say where they were. The thorns drink memories of love because love is the only thing worth drinking." She tapped her own scarred palm against her knee. "You keep forty-one letters in your coat so a woman who was erased stays *written*. Don't you dare dress that up as utility. I'll take the letters back and read them all out loud."

"That's blackmail."

"That's charm work," she said. "Small magic. Never big magic. House rules."

And I laughed — actually laughed, a real one, the kind that hurts on the way out because it's been stored too long. She smiled like she'd won something, because she had, and for a while neither of us spoke. The map lay sulking. The un-wall stood its wrong, beautiful watch. The fire burned down to a language of coals.

Later — much later, when her breathing had gone long and even and she slept the light, armed sleep of a woman who expected the world to misbehave — I sat with the letters open on my knees and read the last page of the last one again. *A building can burn and a promise can't.*

Then I looked at August, asleep with her scarred palm curled open like something offering itself, and I understood — with the terrible clarity available only to men who should be sleeping — that I had begun making the same promise twice. That somewhere between a salt marsh and a nameless village, without filing a single form, I had entered her into the collection. Permanent holdings. No deaccession clause.

Sefa would have found that exhausting. Then she'd have approved enormously.

I was folding the letters away when I saw it: at the edge of the firelight, at the exact border where dark becomes a decision rather than an absence, a figure stood among the un-stones. Tall. Still. Masked — a smooth blank face that caught the coal-light and gave none of it back.

A Warden. Watching us. Not approaching.

Watching the letters in my hands.

And tilting its head, very slightly, like a reader who has found a misfiled page.
