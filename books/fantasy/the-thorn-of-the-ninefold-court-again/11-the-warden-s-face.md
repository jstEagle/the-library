## 11. The Warden's Face

The Warden caught me at dawn, in the hour when the mist lies thick over the thorn-field and even the crows haven't decided to be awake yet.

I'd like to say I fought. I'd like to tell you I went down swinging, all teeth and charm-smoke and the kind of desperate cleverness that gets mercenary heroes out of trouble in the better class of ballad. The truth is less dignified. I stepped on a bramble, swore, looked up, and there it was — seven feet of grey cloak and featureless mask, standing between two hawthorns like it had been waiting for me since before the hawthorns were saplings.

"August Blackwood," it said. Its voice was wrong in a way I couldn't immediately name, the way a room is wrong when someone has just left it. Flat. Echoing slightly. A voice played back through something that had never had a throat.

My hand went to my knife out of pure reflex, and the Warden tilted its head, almost politely, as if to say: *go ahead, that was charming.*

"Ilen," I said instead, low, not turning my head. "Run."

He didn't run. Of course he didn't run. He came crashing through the bracken with his coat flapping and his spectacles fogged and the expression of a man who has read about combat extensively and found it wanting. "You can't fight it alone—"

"I'm not fighting it," I said. "I'm being arrested by it. There's a difference."

"There is *not*—" Ilen started, and then the Warden raised one grey hand, and the world folded.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that morning. It didn't strike me. It didn't bind me. It did something worse: it made the space around me agree that I was already caught. The mist thickened into walls. My knife arm simply declined to move, the way your leg declines to move when your mind has decided you're still asleep. And Ilen — Ilen hit an edge in the air that wasn't there a moment before, hard enough to sit him down in the wet grass, glasses askew, swearing words he'd clearly learned from a book and pronounced beautifully.

"AUGUST!" he shouted through whatever the Warden had built between us. His fists beat on nothing. It made a sound like knocking on the lid of a coffin from the inside.

"Don't break anything," I called back. "It's older than you."

Then the Warden took hold of my collar with fingers like cold candlewax, and pulled me forward, and I fell — not to the ground but *through*, down a short dark drop that smelled of dust and old paper and, beneath that, faintly, of thorns.

* * *

The place where I woke was small and round and lit by no lamp I could see. Grey light came from everywhere and nowhere, the way light behaves in dreams or in temples. The floor was smooth stone worn into a shallow bowl, as if generations of people had stood exactly where I stood, wearing it down one despair at a time.

The Warden stood across from me, motionless.

"All right," I said, because my mouth has never met a silence it liked. "I've been kidnapped by furniture. Is there a form I fill out? Do you take bribes? Because I know a man who'd pay a truly stupid amount for a luck charm right now, and I happen to—"

"Be still."

That voice again. Wrong-echoed. And now, in the quiet after it, I heard what I should have heard at once: under the flatness, another sound. A whispering. Thin and continuous, like wind through a keyhole, like someone reciting something they'd forgotten how to stop reciting.

The Warden's mask was porcelain-pale and blank — no eyes, no mouth, just the suggestion of a face the way a thumbprint suggests a person. But as I watched, the mask began to change.

It didn't come off. It *thinned*. Like frost on a window melting from the center outward, the blank surface went translucent, and behind it —

Behind it was a face.

A woman's face. Ordinary. Mid-forties, maybe, with laugh lines that had stopped laughing some time ago and eyes that were — I keep reaching for the word and coming back empty. *Unfinished.* That's the closest I can get. Her face looked unfinished, like a portrait someone abandoned halfway, the edges of her softening into smudge, her jawline dissolving and reforming as though the artist kept changing their mind about whether she existed at all.

Her lips were moving.

I leaned close without meaning to. The whisper resolved into words, one name, over and over, worn nearly smooth by repetition:

"*Tamsin. Tamsin. Tamsin.*"

"She doesn't see you," said the Warden's flat voice, from somewhere that wasn't the woman's mouth. "She sees very little now. She is mostly used up."

"What is she?" I asked. My voice came out smaller than I wanted it to.

"The Court does not make its Wardens." The mask rippled; the woman's face swam beneath it, dimming, brightening. "The Court *finds* them. People who forgot too much. People whose names were taken, whose loves were taken, who have nothing left to hold themselves together with. The erasure leaves them... hollow. We give hollow things purpose."

"You wear them," I said. The horror arrived slowly, the way cold water arrives — ankle-deep first, so you don't notice until you're in past your waist. "You put masks on people and walk them around until they're gone."

"We *house* them." Something in the tone might have been offense, if furniture could be offended. "Would you rather she wandered? Would you rather she dissolved in a ditch somewhere, whispering a name no one remembers anymore? At least here she is useful. At least here the whisper has work to do."

"Tamsin," I said. "Who is Tamsin?"

The woman's face flickered. For a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — her eyes focused. Found mine through the thinning mask. And I saw her look at me, actually *look*, the way a drowning woman looks at a rope, and her whisper changed rhythm, urgent now, climbing:

"*Tamsin — Tamsin — do you know Tamsin — please — I had a — I had a —*"

She reached for the word and it wasn't there. I watched her reach for it. I watched the sentence collapse in her mouth like a bridge losing its middle span. "*I had a — Tamsin — I had — Tamsin —*" and then the rhythm smoothed again, and the urgency drained away, and her eyes went soft and unfocused and she was whispering the name into the gray light as if into a well, forever, with no expectation of an echo.

Something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was load-bearing.

Because I knew that look. Not from her. From myself. Seven years of talking to a god who didn't answer, seven years of pressing my forehead against that silence and saying *are you there, are you there, are you there* — I knew exactly what it cost to keep speaking into a quiet that never speaks back. She'd been doing it long enough to wear a bowl in the stone floor.

"How long?" I whispered.

"Longer than your nation has existed," said the Warden.

"No. *Her.* How long has she been in there?"

"The question assumes there was a 'before' worth measuring." The mask began to thicken again, frost creeping back over the window. "This interview is concluded. You will be released. Your companion will be released. This is not mercy. This is policy. You are more useful walking toward the door than rotting in a cell."

"The door," I said. "So you know about the ninth door."

For the first time, the Warden hesitated. It was a small thing — a pause the length of a held breath — but I've spent my life reading silences, and I know one that's hiding something when I hear it.

And in that hesitation, the woman's face surged up against the inside of the mask like a swimmer breaking surface, and her hand — I saw it now, a grey-gloved hand trembling at her side — twitched upward, one finger pointing past me, toward the wall of grey light behind me.

*Toward the way out.*

"*Tamsin,*" she whispered, and the whisper had edges on it now, desperation ground fine as grit. "*Tamsin — run — girl — run—*"

She was warning me. Whatever was left of her, buried under however many years of mask and purpose and forgetting — it was spending itself, burning whatever fuel remained, to warn a stranger.

I moved. I'll grant myself that much. While the Warden's attention was split between holding me and holding *her* down, I threw myself backward into the grey light, and the light parted like a curtain, and I dropped onto cold grass with the taste of thorns in my mouth and the sky wheeling overhead.

Ilen was three feet away, mid-argument with a patch of empty air, his hands still raised as if the invisible wall might apologize. When I landed beside him he made a noise I will charitably describe as a battle cry and uncharitably as a yelp.

"August! What happened, where were you, there was a *fold*, there was a fold in the air, I've read about spatial workings but never—"

"Later." I sat up. The mist was thinning. The Warden was gone — or rather, the space where it had been was merely empty now, ordinary, guilty of nothing. "We need to walk. Now. Fast."

He scrambled up, brushing mud off his coat, peering at me with those too-careful archivist's eyes. "What did it show you?"

I opened my mouth to say *nothing*, which was my first instinct and my oldest rule. Never attachments. Never truths you can't take back.

But her face was still behind my eyelids. *Do you know Tamsin. Please. I had a—*

"It showed me a woman," I heard myself say. "Under the mask. Someone the Court erased so thoroughly she doesn't remember her own shape anymore. All she has left is one name, and she spends it warning strangers to run." I flexed my hand. The thorn-scar on my palm — the old one, the one I've had since before any of this — gave one slow, deep pulse, like a second heart deciding whether to bother beating.

"And August," Ilen said quietly, "what are we running *from*?"

"That's the thing." I got my feet under me and started up the slope, away from the thorn-field, toward the grey line of hills where the map insisted the borderland waited. "We're not. We're running *toward*. That's what they can't figure out what to do with us. They don't want to kill us, Ilen. They want to *keep* us. Killers chase you. Keepers wait."

He hurried after me, coat-tails flying. "That's cryptic even by your standards."

"Good. Cryptic keeps people sharp." I glanced back once, at the field, at the mist closing over it like water over a stone. Somewhere under that porcelain and grey wool was a woman whispering a daughter's name — or a sister's, or a lover's; the word had worn away, only the name remained — into a floor that other forgotten women had worn smooth before her.

Every Warden we'd dodged, every patrol we'd fled, every featureless face in every village square: not soldiers. Survivors. The Court hadn't built an army against us.

It had built an argument. *Look what forgetting makes. Look what's left when everything you love is taken. Come join the quiet.*

"They're victims," I said aloud, mostly to myself, and hated how much it changed. Fear is simple. Fear you can fight. But pity — pity walks beside you the whole rest of the road and asks nothing except that you carry it.

"Sorry?" said Ilen.

"Nothing. Keep walking."

Behind us, faint as wind through a keyhole, I would have sworn I heard a single word follow us up the hill. Not *Tamsin* this time.

Something else. Two syllables, worn smooth, spoken like a prayer into a well:

"*Hurry.*"

And far ahead, at the top of the ridge, the morning mist parted just long enough to show me the glint of water — a river, wide and slow, running silver in the new sun.

Running, I noticed, the wrong way.
