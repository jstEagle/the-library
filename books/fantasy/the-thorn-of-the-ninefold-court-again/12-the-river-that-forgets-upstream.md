## 12. The River That Forgets Upstream

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a woman who has decided you are an idiot but is too tired to say so. I had become fluent in it over the past week. August wore it the way other travelers wore cloaks — close, practical, adjusted for weather.

"It's flowing backward," she said.

I looked up from my notes. We had come down out of the hills at dusk onto a wide grey valley, and across it ran a river — broad, unhurried, silvered with the last light. It looked like any river. Rivers are not known for their novelty. Water goes downhill, finds the sea, complains about it in the language of erosion.

"Rivers flow toward the sea," I said. "That one is flowing away from it."

"Then it's a very committed liar."

I crouched at the bank and did the thing archivists do, which is to be insufferably delighted by evidence. The water moved against the current of the land. Where every stream and seep around us drained down toward the coast we'd left behind, this river climbed — gently, without drama, as if it had simply never agreed to the arrangement. Leaves on its surface traveled upstream. A stick I dropped bobbed politely back to me like a dog returning a ball.

"Time-touched ground," I said. "The map warned us."

"The map doesn't warn. The map smirks."

"That's what warning looks like when it's drawn by a country that erased itself."

August said nothing to that. She had developed a habit of going quiet whenever I mentioned the erasure directly — not avoidance exactly, more like a person touching a bruise to check whether it still hurt. It always did. I'd stopped pretending not to notice.

We made camp above the bank rather than cross at night, which was the first sensible decision either of us had made in days, and then ruined it completely by making camp within sight of the water. In fairness, there was little choice; the valley walls funneled everything toward the river, and the thorn-fields we'd crossed two days ago still itched in my memory like a word on the tip of the tongue. You do not go back through thorn-fields. You go forward, even when forward smells like cold iron and old forgetting.

I should explain about the river before I explain about the rest, because the rest only makes sense if you understand what that water was.

* * *

I woke before dawn to the sound of August arguing with someone.

Given that we were alone in a valley that predated every nation whose histories I had ever catalogued, this was concerning. I sat up. She was standing at the riverbank, arms crossed, speaking in a low, flat voice to — as far as I could tell — nothing.

"August."

"He's talking," she said, without turning. "My god. First full sentences in seven years, and he wants to discuss the water." A pause. Her jaw tightened. "He says don't drink it."

"Your god," I said carefully, "who has been silent since before we met, picked this morning to become chatty?"

"Apparently the river loosens his tongue. Or frightens him. He's not being clear. He's not been clear in seven years, why start now." But she wasn't sarcastic, really. Underneath the dryness there was something raw and lit, like a coal someone had breathed on. Seven years of praying into a well and hearing your own echo, and now the echo answered. I understood better than she knew how much that cost a person to admit.

"What does he say about the water?"

She turned. In the grey pre-light her face was pale and set. "It flows backward. Not just downhill-backward. Backward." She held up a hand before I could speak. "Yes. I know how it sounds. He says things go into it and come out earlier than they went in. He says the Court used it for — " she grimaced, hunting the words — "corrections. When someone remembered too much. They'd walk them into the water and walk them out again, and the walking-out version hadn't remembered yet. Clean as laundry."

The wind came down the valley and touched the river, and the river ignored it, moving its own way, patient as a debt.

"So we don't drink," I said.

"We don't drink. We ford fast, we stay shallow, and we don't get it in our mouths. Splash it in your eyes if a Warden gets close, maybe. Otherwise pretend it's not there."

"Pretend it's not there. Yes. That has worked so well for everyone in this story so far."

Her mouth twitched — almost a smile, quickly filed away. August filed most of her smiles somewhere I couldn't reach them. "Eat. We cross at first light."

* * *

First light in that valley came up the color of old pewter, and the river was wider than it had been the evening before. I noted this and chose not to mention it, because some observations are gifts best kept to oneself.

The crossing should have taken ten minutes. The water ran shin-deep at the near edge and knee-deep mid-channel over smooth grey stones, and we went in boots slung around our necks, packs high, the way you cross any river — except this river pushed against us from behind, nudging us forward, hurrying us along like a servant eager to see the last of us. Strange, to be rushed by water. Stranger still when I realized the spray on my face felt warm where morning air should have chilled it. Warm like skin. Like breath.

Halfway across, August stumbled.

It was nothing — a rolling stone, a bad step — and she caught herself, cursed, kept moving. But her foot had gone into a deeper pocket between rocks, and when she pulled it free the water swirled up and a single bright line of it broke across her lips.

She stopped dead in the middle of the channel.

"August."

"I'm fine." Her voice was strange. Flat, then searching. "I'm — fine. Keep moving."

But she didn't keep moving. She stood in the middle of that backwards river with the water combing around her calves and her eyes gone distant, and I watched something happen to her face that I have no clean words for. It was like watching a house with all its lamps lit go dark room by room. Not grief. Grief needs a shape. This was the shape leaving.

"August." I grabbed her arm. The river shoved at my back, urgent. "Walk."

"I was — " She blinked. "Why am I crying?"

"You're not."

"My face is wet."

"That's the river. Walk."

She walked. Whatever the water had taken or shown or unmade, she carried it out of the channel on her own feet, and on the far bank she sat down hard on a stone and scrubbed her mouth with her sleeve and would not look at me, and I had the sense — strong as a hand on my chest — that whatever had been in her head for those three seconds, she had chosen to lose it rather than examine it. Some people burn letters unread. My companion drowned hers in a river that flowed toward yesterday.

"Don't ask," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"Liar. You're an archivist. Asking is your whole personality."

"And you're a mercenary whose whole personality is deflection, so we're evenly matched. Can you stand?"

She could. We walked. And I want to record, because it matters, that I was careful. I kept my mouth shut against the spray. I laughed at her joke about my personality from behind sealed lips. I was so careful, right up until the moment I wasn't, because careful is a thing people do until they're tired, and I had been tired for eleven years, ever since they took Sera's name off the archive rolls and put it on the execution lists instead.

* * *

The mistake happened in the late afternoon, and it happened because of a fish.

I want that on the record. History will blame me for many things — the archive, the escape, the map — but let it also note that the fall of Ilen Vess, disgraced archivist of the Royal Collegium, began with a trout.

We had stopped to rest on the far side of the valley, where the land began to rise toward mountains that were not on any map I had ever catalogued and that the map in August's coat insisted existed anyway. There was a pool below us, fed by a stream that came down from the heights — clean snowmelt, or what looked like clean snowmelt. And in the pool, trout. Actual fish, fat and silver, rising to flies in the ordinary manner of fish everywhere. After six days of dried meat and August's cooking, which is dried meat prepared with contempt, the sight of fresh food undid something in my judgment.

"They're upstream of the river," I said. "Separate water. Snowmelt. Look — it comes down off the rock."

August looked dubious. August always looked dubious; it was her resting expression, the way doubt sits on some faces the way serenity sits on others. "You want to catch a fish."

"I want to *not eat jerky*. Those are different desires with the same solution."

"We don't have hooks."

"I have a knife, patience, and a scholarly tolerance for humiliation."

What followed was twenty minutes of the least dignified scholarship of my career, and I will spare us both the details except to say that I did eventually corner a trout against the stones with my cupped hands, and that triumph makes people stupid, and that in the moment of lifting it — flapping, silver, gloriously real — I opened my mouth to shout for August and got a mouthful of splash-water for my trouble.

Cold. Clean. Tasting faintly of stone and snow.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened. That's the trap of it. Nothing happened, and I laughed, and I stood up holding my fish like a trophy, and I turned to call to her —

— and the world went white at the edges, soft as paper held to a flame.

Here is what forgetting feels like from the inside: it feels like reaching for a book you know is on the shelf, and finding the shelf bare, and knowing — knowing with total, screaming certainty — that there was a book, that you have read it, that you loved it — and being unable to say what it was called. Not the title slipping your tongue. The entire existence of the title, gone, leaving only the hole where it lived. The hole hurts worse than the loss. The hole is shaped exactly like her.

"*Ser—*" I said, and the name broke apart in my mouth like wet paper.

The fish hit the stones. I was on my knees. I didn't remember getting there. My hands were shaking and I pressed them flat against the ground as if the valley might tilt and pour me backward down the slope, back into the water, back and back and back, and the terror that went through me was not the fear of losing something. It was the fear of having already lost it and being unable to mourn, because mourning requires a name, requires a face, requires —

A face. Brown eyes. Ink-stained fingers. Laughing at me across a reading desk while I pretended to work. A door in the archives, locked, her hand warm in mine. Letters. I had letters. I had letters in my coat, sewn into the lining, eleven of them, and I read them — when? Why? Whose handwriting —

"No," I heard myself say. Out loud. To a valley. "*No.*"

Because here is the thing about holes: the mind rushes to fill them. Already I could feel the lie growing in the empty place, smooth and reasonable — *you never loved anyone, you're an archivist, archivists love paper, that's all, that's enough* — a whole life rewriting itself around the absence, tidy and painless and obscene. In a year I would believe it. In a month. By tonight, maybe, if I let the river's gift finish its work.

"Hey. Hey — Ilen."

Hands on my shoulders. August, kneeling in front of me, her grip hard enough to bruise, her face very close and very fierce. Behind her the sky had gone orange and the mountains stood black against it.

"She had brown eyes," I said. Desperately. Testing. "Tell me she had brown eyes."

August went still. Then, slowly, deliberately, she lied to me: "Brown eyes. Ink on her fingers. She thought you were funny, which proves she was brilliant." She must have read it in my face — the letters, the story I'd given her by firelight weeks ago, traded truth for truth. She held my gaze. "Say it back."

"Brown eyes. Ink. She thought I was funny." The words landed in the hole and held its edges open, like tent-pegs in torn cloth. "Her name. August, her *name*, I can't — "

"Carve it."

I stared at her.

"Your knife," she said, calm in the way of people standing in a burning building who have already decided which window they're going through. "Your arm. Now, before the smoothness takes it. Skin holds better than heads."

It was insane advice. It was the sanest advice anyone has ever given me. I fumbled the knife out one-handed and she took my forearm and braced it across her knee, and I set the blade against my own skin, and my hand shook so badly I couldn't start.

"Do it," I said.

"You sure?"

"It's four letters. Cut."

She cut. Four letters, shallow and crooked, red welling up along them in the failing light, and each stroke of the blade was a nail driven through the fleeing name, pinning it to me: **M-A-R-E-N**.

And the moment the last letter closed, the name came *back* — not gradually but all at once, flooding home like water finding a broken channel. Maren. Maren. Maren Ilvane, who annotated her lovers' marginalia and signed her letters *yours against protocol*, who had brown eyes and ink on her fingers even in my memory of her dying, because ink was what she'd been arrested for, ink was the crime, pages and pages of the Court's true history in her neat archival hand.

"Maren," I said aloud, and started to laugh, and the laugh collapsed halfway through into something else entirely.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to fall apart. I only know that at some point I was sitting on the cold stone with my bleeding arm cradled against my chest and my face buried in my good hand, shaking, and August was beside me — not touching me, exactly, but present the way ballast is present, keeping the boat upright by refusing to leave it.

"I forgot her," I managed. "Eight seconds. Eight seconds and the river ate eleven years."

"No. It tried." Her voice was low and even. "You spat it back up."

"I didn't even taste it. I was so careful all day, I kept my mouth shut like a — like an idiot with a secret, and one fish, one stupid fish — " The laugh again, awful and thin. "Do you understand what this means? It's not a border we can hold. It's not Wardens at a gate. It's *water*. It got in through a splash. Through *laughter*. What happens at the Court itself? What happens at the door?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved on her face; she'd gotten a blaze going while I came apart, practical to the marrow, and the trout — she had retrieved my fallen trout — turned on sticks above the flames, filling the dark with a smell like mercy.

"Then we write everything down," she said finally.

"What?"

"Everything that matters. Both of us. Tonight, before sleep, every night. Names, faces, why they matter. If the river takes something, the page gives it back." She tapped her temple. "Memory's just a record no one's archived properly. So we archive. That's your whole profession, isn't it? Use it."

I looked at her — this guarded, scarred, impossible woman who had spent a week pretending our partnership was a business arrangement — offering to build a levee out of paper against a flood of forgetting.

"That won't stop the door," I said quietly. "Whatever the toll is at the end of this road, a notebook won't pay it."

"No," she agreed. "But it'll make sure we know what we paid." She tore two leaves from the back of my field journal, handed me one, and took up the charcoal stub herself. "Start with the name on your arm."

So I wrote: *Maren Ilvane. Brown eyes. Ink-stained fingers. She thought I was funny, which proves she was brilliant. Eleven letters, sewn into my coat lining. Read them monthly. Do not let the smoothness take them.*

Across the fire, August wrote without showing me, her shoulders curved around the page like a wall. I caught only the first line, upside-down, in her blunt soldier's script:

*Things I refuse to lose:*

The list under it was longer than I expected. Longer, I noticed, than seven years of self-containment should have allowed. She caught me looking and covered the page with her palm, and for once neither of us made a joke, because we had both seen the same truth in the firelight: the river had shown us the price tag. Everything ahead of us — the mountains, the map's next cruel instruction, the door itself — would be priced in exactly this coin. Names. Faces. The specific weight of another person in your memory.

We ate the trout. It tasted like survival.

And it was while we were packing the ashes that August went still, her head tilting toward the black shoulder of the nearest mountain, and said, in the voice of someone reporting weather:

"The cairn's up there. My god says something's waiting for us at the top. Something old." She paused. "He says it remembers being a god. And he says — " her jaw worked — "he says it's lonely."

Above us, under the first stars, the eighth god's grave waited on the mountain like a held breath.
