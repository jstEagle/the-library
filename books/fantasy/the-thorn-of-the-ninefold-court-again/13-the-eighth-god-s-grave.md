## 13. The Eighth God's Grave

The mountain had been waiting for us, I think. That's the only way I can describe it. We crested a ridge at dawn and there it was, sudden as an accusation — a grey shoulder of stone rising out of the mist with no foothills leading up to it, no sensible approach, just rock going up and up until it vanished into cloud. As if the land had simply decided to be a mountain that morning and hadn't bothered with the usual preliminaries.

"Maps don't do this," Ilen said. He was holding our map open against his knee, though calling it a map was generous. It had settled overnight into something almost cooperative, the ink lying still for once, showing a single line climbing toward a mark shaped like a small pile of stones.

"A cairn," I said.

"A grave marker," he corrected, because he was Ilen. "Cairns mark paths. Graves mark endings." He folded the map away and looked up at all that impossible stone. "This one's been waiting a long time."

I didn't ask how he knew. Seven days of walking roads that shouldn't exist had taught me that Ilen's knowledge came in two varieties: the kind he'd earned in archives, and the kind he'd absorbed from forbidden books like damp into plaster. Both arrived without warning and neither came with sources.

We climbed.

I want to say the climb was hard, but the truth is stranger: the mountain was *easy*, in the way a held breath is easy. The path appeared under our feet exactly when we needed it, switchbacking up through scree that should have slid, past ledges that should have crumbled. The air thinned. My lungs burned. But the mountain carried us, patient as a hand under an elbow, and that patience frightened me more than any Warden patrol ever had.

Because things on this side of the erasure were supposed to be broken. Hollowed. The village had been hollowed. The river had been hungry. Everything we'd met since Vell had been a wound wearing scenery.

The mountain wasn't wounded. The mountain was *attentive*.

* * *

Near midday, above the cloud line, we found the cairn.

It stood on a shelf of flat stone no wider than a threshing floor, and it was enormous — not the modest finger of rocks you'd raise over a dead shepherd, but a tower of stacked boulders taller than Ilen, each stone fitted to the next with a precision that made my teeth ache. No mortar. No tool marks. Just stones holding each other up through what looked suspiciously like mutual agreement.

And it was warm.

I felt it before I touched it — a heat radiating off the stones like a hearth left banked, like bread fresh from an oven, like skin. After seven years of my god's silence, after weeks of thorns and forgetting and rivers that ate names, warmth was the most alarming thing I'd encountered yet.

"Don't," Ilen said.

"I haven't done anything."

"You were about to."

He knew me too well already, which was its own problem. I crouched by the cairn instead, close enough to feel the heat on my face, and studied the lowest stone. There was writing on it. Not the sliding script of the map, which never stayed still long enough to read twice — this was carved deep and old, worn smooth at the edges, and it did not move. It sat in the stone like a scar sits in skin.

Ilen knelt beside me. He read for a long moment, lips moving, and then went very still in the way he did when something terrible fit together in his head.

"It's a name," he said finally. "Or part of one. The rest has been... worn away. Deliberately, I think. Someone chiseled it out." He traced the empty space with one finger, not touching. "You don't deface a god's grave unless you're very angry or very frightened."

"Which god?"

"The eighth." He said it quietly, the way you'd speak near a sickbed. "August. This isn't a memorial. Look at the heat. Look at how the mountain carried us up. Something is still *in there*. Something is still—"

The cairn breathed.

There's no better word for it. Every stone shifted at once, a fraction, an inhale, and the heat rolled off it in a wave that smelled of woodsmoke and rain and something older underneath, something like the inside of the earth before anyone named it. I sat down hard on the cold rock. Ilen grabbed my arm, whether to steady me or himself I couldn't tell.

Then a voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stones and the wind and the space behind my own teeth, and it was tired. Gods, it was tired. It sounded like a fire burned down to embers, like a man who has been awake so long that sleep has become a country he can no longer find on any map.

"*Little thief,*" it said.

My whole body went rigid. "I'm not a thief."

"*You carry stolen goods.*" A pause, long enough for my heart to beat three times. "*A sigil. A silence. A piece of my brother that he gave away before they took his voice.*"

Ilen made a sound beside me, small and involuntary.

"My brother," the voice said again, softer now, and the weariness in it cracked open into something worse. Grief. Old grief, the kind that has stopped being an event and become a landscape. "*The ninth. The one who refused. You wear his mark, girl. Did you know that? Did you know what warms your palm?*"

I looked down at my hand. Through the glove, faint as a coal under ash, the dead sigil glowed.

"No," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I know it's his. I've known since the shrine. What I don't know is why he's silent, or what you people did to him, or why every step of this road costs more than the last. So if you're going to talk, talk. And don't call me little thief."

The silence stretched. Then, unbelievably, the voice laughed — a dry sound, like wind through a chimney.

"*Oh, he would have liked you. Stubborn as a fencepost. Very well, August-who-sells-charms-in-Vell. Ask.*"

* * *

We sat on the cold stone with our backs against the warm cairn, which felt like leaning against a sleeping animal — comfort and danger in equal measure — and the eighth god told us how the world ended.

Not ended. *Was ended.* There's a difference, and the difference is everything.

"We were nine," it said. "Nine gods, and a Court built on us like a house built on pillars. And the magic we gave them — you have to understand, it wasn't power the way your hedge-witches understand power. It was *depth*. Every working reached down into the world and pulled something up. And the Court reached deeper and deeper, generation after generation, because that is what courts do. They compete. They climb. They mistake altitude for wisdom."

Ilen was listening the way he listened to nothing else — completely, hungrily, his whole body leaned toward the voice. "What did they pull up?"

"*Things that should have stayed down. Unravelings. The magic began to eat the ground it stood on. Our worshippers' memories first — small ones, then large. Then their shadows. Then their children's faces. The Court was dying of its own depth, and the dying was spreading. Another century and it wouldn't have been a country. It would have been a hole.*"

"So you erased it," I said.

"*We healed it. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*" The heat pulsed once, like a heartbeat. "*Forgetting was the only cure. A wound that remembers itself never closes. So eight of us consented. We unmade the Court from the inside out — its roads, its records, its name. And we took the memory of it from every mind that held it. Kindly. Gently. We wrapped the forgetting in mercy like a pill in honey.*"

"And the ninth?"

The pause went on so long I thought the god had gone out entirely.

"*The ninth refused.*"

"Why?"

"*Because he loved them.*" The words came out raw. "*Not the Court. The people. He loved their small lives, their charms and their quarrels, their stubborn fencepost hearts. And he said — he stood in our council and said — you cannot cure a people by taking from them everything they are. A person is their memories. Take the memories and you have cured the wound by killing the patient. And we—*" The voice caught, and the cairn shuddered, and somewhere far below us a sheet of scree slid loose and rattled down the mountainside. "*We voted. Eight to one. And because he would not consent, his refusal had to be silenced for the erasure to hold. We took his voice. We took his name from every mouth. We left him only enough of himself to hold the last door — because even in our mercy, we could not quite bring ourselves to murder our brother outright. So we made him the lock. And he has been holding it open for seven years. Silent. Alone. Spending himself like a candle burned at both ends.*"

Seven years. The number landed in my chest like a dropped stone.

Seven years since my god went quiet. Seven years since the sigil on my palm went cold and I told myself he'd abandoned me, that I'd failed some final test, that gods were fair-weather creatures and I'd been a fool to serve one. Seven years of building a small life with small rules because I thought I'd been left.

He hadn't left. He'd been *held down*.

Something hot ran down my cheek and I realized I was crying, which was infuriating, and I wiped my face with the back of my wrist and said, "Take me to him."

"*That,*" said the eighth god, "*is why I called you here.*"

* * *

Ilen stirred beside me. "Called her? We followed a map."

"*The map follows the faithful. It always has. But listen to me now, both of you, because I am old and nearly spent and I will only say this once.*" The heat drew in, gathered, concentrated, and I had the sense of something vast turning its attention from the wide world down to two small figures on a ledge. "*Little thief. August. Child of my brother's silence. Hear the truth of it, and then choose.*

"*The door my brother holds is not a door out. It is the last knot of the erasure — the place where all the taken memories were bound, the anchor-stone of the cure. If it opens fully, the erasure unravels. Every forgotten thing comes back. Every memory returns to every mind that lost it. Including the sickness. Including the depth-magic that was eating the Court alive. You would not be freeing a people. You would be re-opening a wound that killed them once already.*

"*But the door cannot stay half-open forever either. My brother is nearly spent. When he fails — and he will fail, soon, within the year — the knot rots on its own, and the unraveling happens anyway, uncontrolled, all at once, everywhere. There is no path where nothing is lost. There is only a path where the loss is chosen, and gentle, and paid by someone willing.*

"*So I offer you what I offered no one else, because you carry his mark and I owe his mark a debt.*"

The heat rose off the cairn like breath in winter.

"*Walk away, August. Turn south. Go back to Vell and your charms and your small careful life, and live it. The door will fail on its own in time, and the world will burn, but you will not see it — you will be dust by then, and dust owes nothing. Or—*" and here the voice gentled, became almost tender, and that tenderness was the most dangerous thing I'd heard in weeks, "*—or come to me now, before you go on, and I will give you a clean death. No pain. No toll. No choosing between the people you love and the world they live in. Sleep, and wake in whatever comes after, owing nothing to anyone. My brother spent himself for strangers. You owe him less than nothing. Choose the mercy, child. It is the only mercy left on this mountain.*"

And oh, it was tempting.

I want to be honest about that, because everyone writes about heroes refusing temptation as if temptation were ugly. It isn't. It was the softest voice I'd heard since before my god went silent. It smelled of woodsmoke and rest. Every ache I carried — the fear, the guilt over the first thorn, the sister-shaped hole I couldn't stop probing with my tongue like a missing tooth — every one of those aches went quiet at the edge of that offered sleep, the way pain goes quiet when you're finally allowed to put down something heavy.

I could stop carrying it. That was the offer. Not victory, not rescue — just *permission to stop*.

I looked at Ilen. He was watching me, and his face was doing something complicated — hope and dread fighting for the same ground. Because if I took the death, he'd go on alone. And if I refused it, he'd have to keep walking beside someone the road was actively trying to spend.

"August," he said carefully. "Whatever you—"

"Shut up a minute."

He shut up. One of his many virtues.

I pressed my palm flat against the warm stone of the cairn, right over the worn-away name, and I let myself feel the whole weight of the offer. Rest. Surrender. A god's permission to be small.

Then I thought about my brother-god — my *god* — hanging silent in the dark for seven years, holding a door ajar with hands that had been tied behind him, spending himself for people who didn't remember he existed. Nobody had offered him a clean death. Nobody had asked his consent. Eight gods voted and he paid, and the payment was still running, and it would run until he was ash.

If I walked away, he'd fail alone. If I died clean, he'd fail alone. Either way the knot rotted and the world burned and the last thing the ninth god ever did was hold a door for people who forgot to say thank you.

"No," I said.

The word came out small. I said it again, louder.

"No. I'm not taking your mercy, and I'm not walking away. You erased him because he loved people too much to let you cure them by hollowing them out. Well, congratulations — I've got the same disease. I'm going to that door, and I'm going to find another way through it, one where the price gets paid by whoever deserves to pay it, which is none of us and all of us, and I don't care how impossible that is. Impossible is just expensive. I've been poor my whole life."

Silence. Long silence. The wind moved across the ledge.

Then the eighth god laughed again — and this time the laugh had warmth in it, real warmth, grief and love tangled up together like roots in soil.

"*Stubborn as a fencepost,*" it murmured. "*He said the same thing, you know. Standing in our council. 'Impossible is just expensive.' Word for word.*" The heat flared, and beneath my palm the stone seemed to lean into my hand, the way a tired animal leans into the last friendly touch it expects to get. "*Then take the hard road, daughter-of-my-brother. But take this with you: the door opens for payment freely given. Remember that phrase. Freely given. It matters more than you know yet. And when you reach my brother — tell him—"*

The voice frayed. The heat guttered like a candle in a draft.

"*Tell him we were wrong,*" it whispered, and the whisper was almost gone, almost human, almost small. "*Tell him his brother is sorry. Tell him—*"

And it went out.

Not dramatically. No collapse, no avalanche. The warmth simply drained out of the stones between one breath and the next, and the cairn became just rocks, and the mountain became just a mountain, cold and indifferent and high, and the wind picked up like a landlord changing the locks.

Ilen got to his feet slowly. He was looking at me with an expression I hadn't seen on him before — something raw and unguarded, quickly buried under the archivist's composure but not fast enough.

"'Freely given,'" he repeated. "Payment freely given. That's the key to the door?"

"That's what it said."

"Then we need to understand what counts as free." He was already pulling out the map, already talking faster, retreating into knowledge the way I retreated into sarcasm. "Consent given under coercion isn't free. Consent given in ignorance isn't free. Which means the toll has to be paid by someone who understands exactly what they're losing and chooses it anyway, and August—" He stopped. He'd heard himself. I watched him hear himself, watch the arithmetic finish behind his eyes, and watched him decide not to say the rest of it out loud.

"Say it," I said.

"...Nothing." He folded the map. "Come on. Weather's turning."

We started down the mountain, into the cloud, along a path that was suddenly just a path — ordinary, crumbling, requiring actual care. Behind us the cairn stood cold and silent, a grave at last, truly a grave.

I flexed my bare hand as I walked. Under the fading glow, the sigil sat quiet in my palm — but not dead-quiet anymore. Warm-quiet. Listening-quiet. Somewhere below the world, my god had heard his brother's last words, or he hadn't, and either way he was still holding the door, still spending himself, still waiting.

*I'm coming,* I told him, in the privacy of my own skull, where prayers are free. *And I'm bringing the bill.*

Behind us, far down in the valley, something answered.

Not a voice. A light — a thin, wrong, silver gleam moving through the dusk at the base of the mountain, in a line, many abreast. Moving uphill.

Toward us.

Ilen saw it a moment after I did. His jaw tightened.

"Wardens," he said. "They've found the grave. They'll have felt it die." He glanced at me, and for once there was no sarcasm in him at all. "How fast can you run?"
