## 14. Doors Within Doors

We ran all night, which is the sort of sentence that sounds romantic until you're the one doing the running.

The Wardens did not chase us so much as *advance* — that was the horror of it. No shouting, no hounds, no drumbeat of pursuit. Just that silver line moving uphill at a pace no living thing could sustain, patient as arithmetic. We went down the mountain's far side through scree and pine, and when dawn came grey and thin we were in country I had never seen on any map, and I have seen most maps, including several I was imprisoned for seeing.

"This is it," August said, stopping so suddenly I nearly walked into her back. She had the map out — the bloodstained, impossible, self-reassembling thing she'd carried since Vell — and it had finally, after weeks of sulking and rearrangement, gone still.

I looked past her shoulder and felt my archivist's heart do something undignified in my chest.

There was a door in the middle of the meadow.

Not attached to anything. Not set into a wall or a hillside or even, strictly speaking, standing on the ground — though its frame cast a shadow, and the shadow fell the wrong way, toward the sun instead of away from it. It was old ironwood, black with age, carved over every inch with script I knew intimately: the Court's hand, the letters I had given up a career, a reputation, and three years of my freedom to catalogue.

"Borderland," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The map's been walking us here since the river."

"I know." She said it flatly, the way she said everything now, but her scarred palm was pressed against her thigh, and I'd learned what that meant. It ached. The thorn-scar always ached near Court-work. "You ready?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I've been unready for this my whole life, so at least I'm practiced."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the light behind us crested the ridge — silver, wrong, many abreast — and we stopped being philosophical and started being fast.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the borderland, from fragments, from burned books I'd memorized before burning them, from a lover who used to whisper Court-lore to me in bed like other people whisper endearments:

The Ninefold Court did not simply vanish. Vanishing is for rabbits and thieves. A country built by nine gods erased itself the way a surgeon removes a limb — carefully, at the joint, leaving nothing that would fester. But a wound needs a border, a place where the cut happened, and so the edge of the erasure remained: a maze of thresholds where memory pooled against the seal like water against a dam. Each door held a life. Walk in, and you walked through your own remembering.

The texts called them *the honest rooms*. Because a door can't lie about what you loved.

"Stay close," August said, as we came through into the first corridor. "Whatever you see—"

"You first," I said, because she was already frozen mid-step, staring at something ahead of us, and her face had gone the color of old tallow.

I followed her gaze and understood immediately why the archivists' manuals had failed to prepare anyone for this. The corridor stretched ahead of us — stone, torchlit, ordinary except that it existed inside a meadow's worth of nowhere — and set along both walls, dozens deep, receding into dark, were doors. Hundreds. Thousands. And each one stood slightly open, spilling light and sound, and each one was showing someone something.

An old woman stood before one, laughing, reaching toward a kitchen full of steam and voices. A boy sat cross-legged in front of another, watching it the way children watch fires. None of them looked at us. None of them looked at anything but their doors.

"Are they—" August began.

"Villagers," I said quietly. "From the nameless place. Or their cousins. They come here the way the drowned come to water." I didn't say the rest: *and they don't leave.* The manuals had been clear on that point, in the way manuals are clear about things nobody survives to confirm.

"Then we don't look," August said. "We walk straight. Eyes down. Count your steps if you have to."

"I'm an archivist," I told her. "Counting is the only prayer I know."

* * *

It worked for eleven doors.

I counted them because counting kept me sane: eleven doors, eyes down, boots on stone, August's shoulder a fixed point ahead of me like a star you navigate by. At the twelfth door the sound changed.

Every other doorway murmured — other lives, other loves, static from strangers' hearts. This one was quiet. And then, from inside the twelfth door, a voice said:

"You're late, Ilen."

I stopped counting.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only integrity I have left, and because I suspect — I *suspect* — that someday someone will ask me how it felt, and I will want to answer accurately rather than prettily.

It felt like being named.

Not my name. Something older than names, the word for the shape of a person that exists before language gets its hands on it. I turned my head, and the door was open wider than it had been, and the room beyond it was a small archive office with a crooked window and a chair with a broken left arm that I had repaired twice with string, and sitting on the desk — sitting *on* the desk, feet swinging, because she never once in her life sat in a chair like a person — was Sera.

Sera. Sera with ink on her fingers and her collar half-unbuttoned in defiance of three separate dress codes, Sera who had been executed on a Tuesday in autumn for refusing to burn a shelf of forbidden histories, Sera whose last letter lived folded against my heart under six layers of coat and guilt. Sera alive, annoyed, waiting.

"You're late," she said again, and grinned, and it was her grin exactly, the one that meant trouble or revelation, usually both. "They're going to start the hearing without you. You know how the Provost feels about punctuality."

"Sera." Her name came out of me like something coughed up from underwater. "You're dead."

"Am I?" She looked down at herself, hands, ink-stained fingers, the little burn scar on her wrist from the lamp she knocked over our second week working together. "Doesn't feel like it. Here, feel." She held out her hand. "Come check. That's what you do, isn't it? Check everything twice. Catalogue everything. You never trusted anything you hadn't verified." Her grin softened into something more dangerous. "So verify me."

And gods forgive me — gods, or the eight who consented, whichever ones are taking attendance — I stepped toward the door.

Here is the terrible thing, the thing I need written down somewhere outside my own skull: I *knew*. Some cold clerk in the cellar of my mind stamped the document *forgery* and filed it correctly. The room was perfect, but perfect is precisely how lies are built; truth has loose threads, and this room had none. The Provost had died two years before Sera's arrest. There would be no hearing. The date was wrong. The light through the crooked window was the light of a morning that had never existed.

I knew all of it, and I stepped forward anyway, because knowing is a small cold thing and love is a large warm one, and I had spent four years learning which of them wins.

A hand closed on my arm.

"Don't," August said.

"It's her."

"It's not."

"She's right there—"

"Ilen." August's grip tightened, and she turned me by main force, and her face was very close to mine and very hard, harder than I'd ever seen it, and underneath the hardness something moved like water under ice. "Listen to me. Whatever's in there, whatever it's wearing, whoever it's showing you — I don't care. I need you."

"You don't," I said. It wasn't even an accusation. It was just true, and I was too far gone to lie gracefully. "You've crossed half a country with me because I can read a map. You don't need me. You need a dictionary, and dictionaries don't step through doors after dead women."

"Yes, I do." And she said it fast, too fast, the way people say things they've decided to say before deciding whether they're true, and her hand on my arm shook — actually shook, this woman who had stared down Wardens and gods and the eighth god's grave without blinking — and she said, "I need *you*, specifically. Not a dictionary. Not a map-reader. You. So get away from that door and walk, because I am asking, and I have never asked anyone for anything in seven years, and I hate it, and I'm doing it anyway. Please."

Please.

August Nakamura does not say please. I had known her for — how long? Weeks? It felt like years and like an afternoon simultaneously, which is, I later learned, the standard sensation of traveling with someone you were always going to love. In all that time she had bartered, demanded, joked, and gone silent, but she had not once begged. She kept her debts the way misers keep coins, and here she was spending one on me, buying me back from a door, paying in a currency she clearly believed she didn't have enough of.

The clerk in the cellar of my mind stamped one final document. *Withdrawn.*

I let her pull me back. The door's light reached for me — I swear it reached, the glow bending off the floor like a hand — and then the corridor shifted, the way the map used to shift, and the twelfth door was simply elsewhere, and Sera's voice, fainter, amused, terribly kind, said:

"Clever girl. But he'll come back. They always come back."

Then there were only doors again, dozens of them, murmuring their stranger-lives, and my own heartbeat loud enough to embarrass us both.

* * *

We walked in silence for a long time. The corridor narrowed, branched, narrowed again; the doors grew fewer, and the ones that remained were shut, which somehow was worse. Behind us, faintly, came the sound of the villagers arriving — soft footsteps, soft laughter, people coming home to rooms they'd never leave. Ahead of us the air changed taste, going metallic, going *old*, the way the air tastes in places where something enormous happened a very long time ago.

Finally August spoke, eyes forward. "Who was she?"

"You saw the door."

"I saw a door." A beat. "I saw your face."

I considered lying. It would have been easy; she offered me a hundred exits and only watched me not take them. But she had just paid a beggar's coin for me, and I found I couldn't counterfeit anything for her. It felt like forging scripture.

"Her name was Sera," I said. "She worked in the royal archives with me. Junior cataloguer. Better than me, honestly — she read Old Court script fluently, which I never managed, which is ironic, considering." My voice did something strange around the next part, and I let it. "When the purges started, the Crown ordered the Court archive destroyed. All of it. Every text. She refused. Formally, in writing, with citations — she cited the destruction order's own legal flaws at them, footnotes and all. They executed her for it." I touched my coat, over the letters. "Her last letter says, 'If they erase the Court, they'll erase why it mattered, and then no one will ever know what was lost. Someone has to remember badly rather than forget well.' Badly," I repeated. "That's me. The bad rememberer. I've been doing her job for four years and calling it disgrace."

August walked three steps in silence. Then: "What did the door show you?"

"A morning that never happened." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "A hearing that couldn't occur, presided over by a man already dead. It didn't even bother to be plausible. It just needed to be *her*. Plausibility was extra."

"That's how they work, then." Flat, clinical — her armor voice, but I'd gotten good at hearing what was underneath it. "Not lies. Offers. They find the shape of what you want and pour themselves into it." Her scarred hand flexed at her side. "The thorns drink love-memories. The doors offer them back. The whole borderland is one machine, and the machine runs on wanting."

"And you?" I asked, before I could stop myself. "What did yours offer? Back there. Before I caught up to you. You froze first."

For a moment I thought she wouldn't answer. The corridor bent around a corner that hadn't existed a moment before, and the air went colder, and far ahead — I felt it before I saw it, a pressure behind the sternum, a warmth in a scarred palm — far ahead something vast waited at the end of the passage, and I understood we were close to the center of the borderland, close to whatever the map had been dragging us toward since Vell.

"What did yours offer?" she countered.

"Touche," I admitted. "We'll trade, then. On the far side. When we're through."

"When we're through," she echoed, and it wasn't quite a promise, but it was the closest thing to one either of us had made yet.

Then the last door appeared.

It stood alone at the corridor's end, larger than the others, ironwood black as a drowned ship, and carved across its lintel in Old Court script — the script Sera read better than I ever would, the script I had spent a lifetime failing to master — were nine words. I read them aloud, because reading aloud is how I pray:

"Ninth door. Still open. Toll unpaid. Come home."

Behind us, down the long corridor, the soft footsteps of the villagers had stopped. All together. All at once.

And every door along the walls, hundreds of them, opened wide — and began, in one voice, in a hundred borrowed voices, some of them familiar, one of them hers —

—to call our names.
