## 15. The Ninth Door Is Still Open

The map stopped moving on the seventh day of the ninth month, which Ilen said was either a coincidence or the Court's idea of a joke, and I have learned not to bet against the Court's sense of humor.

We stood at the edge of a valley that the map had refused to show us for three hundred miles. It simply hadn't been there — not hidden, not obscured, just *absent*, the way a word is absent when it sits on the tip of your tongue. And then the moon came over the ridge, and the valley filled in like ink spreading through wet paper: black grass that moved against the wind, and at its center, a door.

I want to tell you it was a grand door. I want to describe gold leaf and titanic hinges and nine kinds of precious wood, something worthy of a country full of gods. But it wasn't. It was a plain door, oak gone gray with age, standing alone in the middle of nowhere with no wall around it, no house, no frame — just a door, upright in the grass, as if the building around it had been erased and the door had been forgotten in the cleanup.

And thorns.

Thorns grew over it the way ivy grows over a tomb. Living thorn, black-stemmed, the canes thick as my wrist and studded with spines long as my smallest finger. They'd woven themselves through the keyhole, around the handle, across the panels, a bramble cage built by something patient. The flowers were white and wrong — they bloomed facing away from us, every one of them, turned toward the door as if keeping watch.

"That's it," Ilen said. He didn't make it a question. After six weeks of walking roads that weren't there, you stop asking questions and start narrating. "That's the ninth door."

"You're very brave, pointing from back here."

"I'm providing tactical observation."

"You're standing behind me."

"Tactically," he said.

But he came forward anyway, because that was the thing about Ilen — for all his flinching and his foot-dragging and his talent for finding the exact worst thing to say at the exact best moment, when it mattered he moved toward the frightening thing. I never told him I'd noticed. It seemed like the kind of admission that would ruin both of us.

The grass parted for us as we walked. Not politely — reluctantly, the way a crowd parts for something it doesn't want to touch. The stalks were warm. I kept my hands in my coat pockets and felt the sigil under my left cuff, the dead god's mark I'd carried for seven years like a coin from a country that no longer existed. It had been warming steadily since the village. Since the shrine with the eight empty niches and the one niche that wasn't empty at all, only sealed, and the seal shaped exactly like the scar of ink on my skin.

*Say something,* I thought at it, the way I'd thought at it ten thousand times over seven silent years. *You owe me an explanation. You owe me seven years.*

It didn't answer. But it warmed again, and this time the warmth had a rhythm to it, and it took me until we were twenty paces from the door to recognize the rhythm as breathing.

Gods aren't supposed to breathe. Gods are supposed to be weather. That's what I'd been taught as a mercenary's daughter with a patron sigil inked at thirteen: your god is the storm you stand inside, vast and indifferent and occasionally, if you're very lucky or very useful, pointed in a helpful direction. My god had been silence for seven years. Silence is also weather. I had built my whole life inside it — the stall, the rules, the small gray days. Never big magic. Never attachments. Never anything that might make noise loud enough to notice the quiet.

Now the quiet was breathing, and it sounded tired.

"August." Ilen had stopped at the edge of the thorn cage. "Look at the flowers."

I looked. Up close, the white blooms weren't facing the door. They were facing *through* it, straining against their own stems, bent toward whatever lay on the other side like prisoners at a window. And each bloom had a faint discoloration at its center, a darkening like a bruise, and when I leaned close — carefully, because I had survived this long by never leaning carelessly into things — I heard them.

Whispering.

Not words. Names, maybe. Fragments of names. A sound like someone saying a word in their sleep, over and over, losing more of it each time. Hundreds of flowers whispering hundreds of half-names, and underneath all of it, so deep I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it, a voice I knew.

Seven years ago I had lain in a ditch outside Vell with three broken ribs and a spear wound and a sigil that had suddenly, catastrophically stopped answering, and I had screamed at my god until my throat tore, and the silence had been the loudest thing I'd ever heard. I knew that silence the way you know a childhood home. And beneath the flowers' whispering, worn thin as old cloth but unmistakable, was the other side of that silence. Not absence. Restraint. The sound of someone holding a door closed from the inside with their whole body while everything they love pounds on it.

"Oh," I said. It came out very small. "Oh, you *idiot*."

"Excuse me?" Ilen said.

"Not you." I pulled back my sleeve. The sigil had gone from warmth to heat, and the ink-lines were moving — slowly, laboriously, the way a man moves when he's been carrying something too heavy for too long and has forgotten any other way to walk. "Not you."

The voice, when it finally came, arrived the way light comes through a cracked shutter. In pieces. In pain.

***small*** — it said, and I went to my knees in the black grass, because seven years is a long time and I had imagined this moment in a thousand forms and never once imagined it would sound *broken* — ***small one. you came.***

"You're here," I said. "You've been *here*. The whole time. Seven years, and you were here, holding this—" I gestured at the thorned door and my hand shook. "Why? Why did you leave me sitting in that ditch? Why did you let me think—"

***had to hold it,*** the voice said. ***the door. the others spent themselves closing the country. i spent myself holding one way out. one crack. it cost...*** A pause, long enough that I thought the voice had guttered out. ***it cost everything i had left to spend. i could not speak and keep the door. i chose the door.***

"You chose the door over me."

***i chose everyone's future over one woman's comfort.*** Something moved through the sigil that might have been, in a healthier god, wry humor. ***you would have made the same choice. it is why i marked you.***

"Don't flatter yourself. I'd have picked me."

***no,*** it said gently, ***you wouldn't have. you sell charms to frightened dockworkers for coppers. you broke a disgraced archivist out of a royal prison because he was sad in the right way. you are the least mercenary mercenary i have ever marked, and you have spent seven years pretending otherwise because pretending is easier than hoping.***

Ilen had crouched down beside me. I was distantly aware of him saying my name, twice, and then deciding not to intrude on whatever was happening to me, which was wise of him, because what was happening to me was seven years of grief collapsing all at once into seven years of fury.

"You could have died," I whispered. "In that ditch. I nearly did. I sold *charms*, I lived in a room above a fishmonger, I—"

***yes.***

"I hated you."

***i know. i listened. it was the only conversation i got.***

And despite everything — despite the ruined voice and the thorned door and the valley that shouldn't exist — I laughed. It came out wet and ragged, but it was a laugh, and somewhere in the sigil something laughed too, a flicker of warmth like a banked fire remembering it used to be a blaze.

Then the warmth dimmed, and the voice came again, and this time it was careful, the way you're careful when you know what you're about to say will change the shape of someone's life.

***small one. ask me how the door opens.***

I looked up at the door. At the thorns. At the flowers straining toward the other side, whispering their dying names. Behind me Ilen had risen to his feet, and he was very still, the particular stillness of an archivist who has just realized he knows the answer to a question he desperately doesn't want asked.

"How does the door open?" I asked, because some questions you cannot refuse to ask, the same way some doors you cannot refuse to reach.

***a door between a dying country and the living world must be paid for in the world's own currency,*** the god said. ***and the only currency this door accepts is memory. not any memory. the toll is this: all that you remember of everyone you have ever loved. give it, and the door opens, and you walk through into the world, and you live.***

The black grass had gone still. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath.

"And if no one pays?" Ilen's voice, steady, archivist-flat, the voice he used when the truth was too large for his actual voice.

***then the door closes forever, and the court finishes dying behind it, and everything it swallowed — the roads, the villages, the people who forgot themselves politely — stays forgotten, and the wardens finish becoming what they are becoming, and eventually they spill out into your world, because erasure is hungry and it has been dieting for two hundred years.*** The sigil pulsed once, slow and weary. ***the others believed no price was too high for mercy. i believed someone should at least be told the price. i held the door open seven years so that someone could choose with open eyes.***

"Someone," I said. "One someone. One payer."

***one. the toll takes everything they love from their memory. not their capacity to love — that remains, a scar with no history, an ache without a name — but every face, every name, every moment attached to it. gone. they walk through alive and free and hollow in exactly one place, and the hollow never fills.***

I sat in the cold grass and did arithmetic. It was simple arithmetic. It was the simplest and cruelest sum I had ever done.

My mother, dead eleven years — her face, her voice, the smell of her bread, gone. The men I'd fought beside, the ones who lived and the ones who hadn't. The dockworker Tomas and his frightened daughter. Seven years of small kindnesses, small attachments, the tiny loves I'd allowed myself against my own rules because a person can't survive on nothing, even a person who's sworn to try.

And Ilen. Six weeks of sarcasm and shared fires and the letters he carried in his coat for a dead man whose name he'd carved into his own arm at a forgetting river. Six weeks of a guarded, bitter, brilliant man choosing, every single morning, to walk beside me.

Gone. All of it. A clean sweep, and I'd wake on the far side knowing nothing of why my palm ached.

"No," Ilen said.

I looked up. He was pale. He'd done the same arithmetic; I could see the answer on his face.

"It won't be you," he said.

"It hasn't been decided who—"

"It won't be you," he said again, and there was something under his voice now, something that scared me more than the Wardens ever had, because it was certain. "August, listen to me. You've already lost a god to this place. You've already lost seven years. You don't get to lose *yourself*—"

"And you do?" I stood. My knees were unsteady and I stood anyway. "You carry a dead man's letters in your coat. Your lover's name is carved into your arm because a river tried to drink it. Ilen, the toll would take *him*, and you'd be free of it — don't you see, part of you wants—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. His hands were fists at his sides. "Don't you dare. Don't you *dare* tell me what part of me wants. I have thought about forgetting him every single day since the river, and I will tell you what I've learned: grief is not the price. Grief is the receipt. You pay it because what you loved was worth more than what the losing costs. And you—" his voice failed and restarted, quieter, worse, "—you are worth more than what the losing costs. So it will not be you."

Behind us, in the sigil, my god breathed one long, exhausted breath, and I understood — with the terrible clarity the assignment of this moment demanded — that the silence had never been abandonment. Every day at my stall, every night above the fishmonger, every time I'd cursed a faithless god, it had been here, bleeding out slowly so that a door would stay cracked open. Sacrifice doesn't announce itself. It just goes quiet and lets you misread it.

***there is time,*** the god murmured. ***not much. the wardens feel the door waking. but some. decide what you love. decide what it costs. then decide who pays.***

And far off across the valley, where the dark grass met the ridge, lights kindled. Not torchlight. Warden-light — white and sourceless and patient, one lantern after another, a slow constellation assembling itself around the only exit from a dying country.

They had felt it too. The door was waking, and everything the Court had forgotten was coming home to collect.

Ilen took my hand. The one with the scar. Neither of us said anything, because there was nothing left to say that the lights in the distance weren't already saying better:

Decide quickly.
