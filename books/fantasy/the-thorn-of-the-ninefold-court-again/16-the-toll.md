## 16. The Toll

### ILEN

There is an art to stealing from yourself that no archivist ever taught me.

I learned it in the archive, actually — the way you slip a folio into your coat and walk past the desk clerk with your heart beating so loudly you're certain he can hear it. The trick is to act like nothing has changed. To be boring. Boring men are invisible. I had been boring for eleven years before they arrested me, and it nearly worked for another eleven after.

So when August finally slept — truly slept, her back against the thorned door's roots, her hand curled around the scarred palm as if she could strangle the ache out of it — I began the work of robbing myself.

The door wanted memories of love. That much the ninth god had made clear, in its broken whisper through the bark: *a toll paid in those held dear, freely given, wholly surrendered.* Not a memory here or there. All of them. Every face, every voice, every moment in which someone had mattered enough to leave a mark.

For August, that would be everything. Her sister, half-erased and howling somewhere behind us in the dark. Me, if I counted — and I was vain enough, or hopeful enough, to believe I did.

For me?

I sat with my notebook on my knees and made the list by moonlight leaking through the thorn canopy. It was a short list. That is what disgrace does; it prunes you. My mother, dead twenty years, remembered mostly as a smell of ink and bread. My mentor Vessal, who disowned me publicly and would have enjoyed being forgotten, which is its own small cruelty. And Sera.

Sera.

Eleven letters in my coat. Eleven letters from a woman executed in the purges for teaching children to read histories that had been declared unreadable. I have read them so many times that the paper has gone soft as cloth, that I know the water stain on the fourth letter is shaped exactly like the delta of some unnamed river, that I could recite her closing line — *come home when it's safe, or don't come home at all, but either way, live like a man worth missing* — in her exact cadence, including the little pause where she'd have smirked.

She had been dead nine years. I had done nothing with those nine years except carry her.

Here is the bargain I struck with myself, in the dark, like a coward negotiating with his own reflection: if I paid the toll, I lost the letters' meaning. But August lost *everything* — her sister found again only to be lost forever, whatever fragile thing was growing between us, seven years of silence and the god who caused it. One grief against a whole self. It wasn't even arithmetic. It was charity.

And besides — and this is the part I will not pretend was noble — there was a version of the loss that felt almost merciful. To forget Sera was to stop carrying her. Nine years of guilt, folded up and handed over like a fare. Some traitorous part of me had been waiting for permission to put her down.

I told myself that part didn't exist. I was very good at telling myself things. It's why they let me near the archives for so long.

I took out the letters. I read them one last time by feel, mostly, because the moon kept hiding behind the thorns like it knew what was coming and didn't want to witness. Then I did the archivist's final kindness to a document: I memorized the physical facts. Weight of paper. Smell of lavender gone to dust. The way her handwriting leaned left when she was angry and right when she was lying about being fine.

Facts survive even when meaning doesn't. I know this professionally. A man can remember that a document existed without remembering what it said. Perhaps some hollow future-Ilen would find these letters in his coat and understand, dimly, that he had once been loved, the way you understand rain happened by the mud it left.

That would have to be enough.

I folded the letters away. I practiced saying goodbye in my head — not to Sera, but to the *having* of her — and found, to my disgust, that my eyes were wet.

"You're doing it wrong," said a voice above me.

August stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms crossed, and her expression was the one she wore when she'd caught a pickpocket working her stall: not surprised. Almost disappointed in herself for expecting better.

"How long have you been awake?"

"Since you started narrating your own martyrdom under your breath." She came closer. "You breathe like a man reading aloud when you're scheming. Did you know that? Archivists."

"August—"

"Don't." She crouched, took my wrist, and turned my hand over. On the palm, in ink still faintly glistening — I hadn't even noticed myself writing it, gods help me, I'd written it like a man taking dictation — were the words: *SERAPHINE VAEL. THE LETTERS. THE RIVER STAIN.*

"You wrote down what you're planning to forget," she said quietly. "So you'd keep a copy."

"I—"

"That's not sacrifice, Ilen. That's a man trying to smuggle contraband past the border guards inside his own skull." She released my hand. "The door doesn't take payment in trivia. It takes the love out of it. You can keep the words 'river stain' until you die and it won't mean one damned thing."

"I know how erasure works," I said, and hated how much my voice sounded like a lecture hall. "Better than you do, probably. I've spent my life studying what's left behind. Thorns, gaps, the negative space where a country used to be. I know you can't smuggle anything through."

"Then what is this?" She flicked the ink on my palm.

"It's arithmetic," I said. "One grief against a whole person. You have a sister who just got given back to you. You have a god who spent seven years holding a door open on the chance you'd come. You have—" I stopped, because the next item on the list was me, and saying it aloud felt like claiming a prize I hadn't won.

"I have what?"

"More to lose than I do. That's all. Sera's dead. She's been dead nine years. The door would be collecting a debt already settled. For you it would be robbery."

August looked at me for a long time. Firelight did something complicated to her face — anger and fear fighting for the same ground — and then she laughed, one short, terrible note.

"A debt already settled," she repeated. "Gods. Do you hear yourself? You've rehearsed this. You have *talking points.*"

"Archivists prepare."

"You prepared a eulogy for your own heart and you're calling it bookkeeping." She dropped down beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because the thorned door chose that moment to shudder, deep in its roots, like something breathing in its sleep.

When she spoke again her voice had gone low and dangerous, the voice she used on Wardens.

"Tell me what you'd actually lose. Not the summary. Tell me."

"You know what I'd lose."

"The summary isn't good enough. If you're going to hand yourself over to that thing, I want to watch you count the cost out loud, coin by coin, so you can't pretend afterward it was cheap."

I should have refused. Instead I heard myself say, "Her name was Seraphine Vael. She corrected my citations on our second meeting, which was also our first date, technically, though neither of us admitted it for a year. She smelled of lavender that had given up pretending to be fresh. When she lied about being fine her handwriting leaned right. She was executed on the ninth of Frostmonth for the crime of teaching children that their country had once been called something else." My voice cracked on the last word and I pushed through it like a man wading a cold river. "There. Itemized."

"And you'll never feel any of that again," August said softly. "Not the irritation. Not the wanting to correct her right back. Not the nine years of missing her. Gone, gone, gone — and the worst part is you won't miss it, because missing requires remembering that something's absent." She shook her head slowly. "You think that's a fair trade for my sister? For my god? For—" 

"For you," I said. It escaped before I could stop it, and once it was out there was no calling it back, so I finished it like a man stepping off a ledge: "Yes. For you. Obviously for you."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then August said, "Absolutely not," and the negotiation collapsed into war.

* * *

### AUGUST

He tried to argue like an archivist. I fought dirty.

"You don't get to decide your life is worth less than mine," I told him. "That's not math, that's vanity wearing sackcloth."

"My life isn't worth less. My *grief* is worth less. There's a difference—"

"There is no difference! That's the entire point!" I was on my feet now, pacing, because sitting still felt like agreeing. "The door doesn't weigh griefs, Ilen. It doesn't have a scale back there behind the thorns with 'dead lover' in one pan and 'living sister' in the other. It takes all of it or none. You give it Sera, and what comes out the other side is a man with eleven letters in his coat and no idea why he kept them. That's not mercy. That's amputation and calling the stump clean."

"And what comes out the other side for you?" He stood too, and we were shouting now, quietly, the way people shout when there are things listening in the dark. "A woman who doesn't know why she crossed a country. Who doesn't know her sister's name. Who walks past me in some market someday and feels sick and doesn't know why. You call that a better outcome?"

"Yes!"

"You hesitated."

"Because you're infuriating, not because you're right!"

The door groaned. We both flinched. Somewhere in the thorn-mass, sap moved with a sound like a swallowed word.

We lowered our voices. We circled each other. This was, I realized distantly, the most honest conversation we'd ever had, which was grim, because it consisted entirely of two people competing to see who could die first.

"Let's be precise," Ilen said, in his infuriating lecturing tone, "about what happens if neither of us pays. The door stays shut. The Court finishes waking. The Wardens converge — they're already converging, you felt it in the ground an hour ago same as I did — and every living thing within a hundred leagues gets erased to feed a dying country. Your sister included. Permanently, this time. No thorn to touch, no stolen minute of memory. Just gone, along with everyone else."

"I know the stakes."

"Then you know one of us pays, or everyone pays. And between the two of us—"

"—there's no 'between,'" I snapped. "Because if you pay, I spend the rest of my life as a woman with a hole in her shaped exactly like you, and I will *hate* that, Ilen, I will hate a stranger I can't even remember resenting. Whereas if I pay, you—" I faltered, because I'd walked straight into his argument without noticing.

"If I pay," he said gently, "I won't even know I lost anything. That's the cruelty of it. That's the *gift* of it. You'll suffer a wound you can't see. I'll suffer nothing at all."

"That's not true."

"August—"

"It's not true because you're lying to yourself, you great dusty fraud." I grabbed his coat — the coat, the one with the letters — and pulled him close enough that I could see the fire reflected twice in his eyes. "You think forgetting Sera costs you nothing because she's dead. But she's not dead *in you.* In you she's the reason you wouldn't burn the archive. She's the reason you flinch when anyone says 'for the good of the realm.' She's the reason you're here, following a madwoman across a cursed country instead of hiding in some library. You cut her out and you don't get a man with one fewer grief. You get a man with no idea why he does anything he does. You get *hollow*, Ilen. I've seen hollow. I grew up next door to it. Hollow is what the Wardens are made of."

His jaw worked. For a moment I thought I'd won.

Then he said, "And you think keeping your memories makes you less hollow than giving them up? You've spent seven years alone in a city that hates you, selling luck to dockworkers, talking to a god that stopped answering. You call that full?" He caught my wrists — gently, always gently, damn him — and held on. "August. Whatever's growing between us, it started three weeks ago. Your sister came back from the dead four days ago. Your god spoke two nights ago. You are standing at the beginning of everything, and I am standing at the end of mine, and you're telling me the scales balance?"

"They don't balance," I whispered. "They never balance. That's what love is. Somebody always owes more."

Something went through his face then — surprise, and under it, terror, because I'd said *love* out loud, in the open, where the thorns could hear it, and we both knew I hadn't meant it abstractly.

"That's cheating," he said hoarsely.

"Everything's cheating. This is a fight over who gets to be the martyr. There are no rules."

"There's one rule." His grip tightened on my wrists. "Neither of us decides alone. That was the rule since the marshes, since you pulled me out of that cell — we don't make the big choices solo anymore. So here's my proposal, and you're going to hate it: neither of us pays tonight. We sleep. We think. And in the morning, if the door still needs feeding, we decide *together*, with clear heads, and whichever of us wins the argument—"

"Loses," I said.

"—loses," he agreed, "fairly. In daylight. With witnesses."

"The only witnesses are thorns and a dying god."

"They count. They've seen worse arguments."

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to hit him. I did both, roughly in order, and he absorbed the blow to his shoulder with the patience of a man who had catalogued worse.

But underneath the exhaustion and fury, something in me understood what he was really proposing. Not a truce. A postponement of the inevitable, bought so that we could have one more night — one more night of both of us being entirely ourselves, memories intact, grievances intact, the whole ridiculous inventory of who we were still on the shelf. He was bargaining for time the way I bargained for luck charms: badly, desperately, knowing the price was fixed and haggling anyway.

"All right," I said. "Together. Daylight. Clear heads."

"Thank you."

"But I'm sleeping between you and the door."

"You can't guard a door with your body, August."

"I can guard it with my *intentions,* which is more than I trust yours with right now." I settled down against the roots, deliberately, between him and the pale gleam of the sealed gate. "If you try to sneak off and pay the toll in the night, I'll wake up. I'm a light sleeper. Occupational hazard of selling illegal charms."

"And if I try to pay the toll openly, in front of you, at dawn?"

"Then I'll fight you," I said simply. "Right there. In front of the god. And it can watch us wrestle over who loves whom more, and maybe it'll be so embarrassed it opens the door for free."

That got a real laugh out of him — short, wrecked, but real — and for a while we just breathed, shoulder to shoulder, watching the thorns move in a wind we couldn't feel.

It was Ilen who broke the quiet, very late, when I was almost asleep.

"August. The ground's shaking again."

I was awake instantly. He was right — a tremor ran up through the roots and into my spine, rhythmic, deliberate. Footsteps. Many of them. Coming from every direction at once, the way water finds a drain.

Far off in the dark, something began to whisper. Hundreds of voices, all whispering the same wordless syllables, the sound of names being eaten.

The Wardens had found the door.

And we had spent our last night arguing about who got to save the world, instead of deciding how.
