## 17. The Wardens Come Home

The door told us it was open before we did.

I felt it the way you feel a tooth before the ache arrives — a low, wrong hum in my back teeth, in the scar on my palm, in the place behind my sternum where a god had spent seven years being quiet and then, lately, wouldn't stop murmuring like a man talking in his sleep. The thorn-seal that covered the ninth door had begun to move. Not die — I want to be clear about that, because I would have preferred dead. Moving. Unfurling, petal by black petal, with the slow patience of something that had been waiting longer than language.

Ilen stood beside me with his coat collar up against the wind and his hands buried in his pockets, which was where he kept everything he couldn't say out loud. Letters. Names. A whole archive of grief folded into wool.

"It knows we're here," he said.

"Of course it knows we're here. We walked three hundred miles to knock."

"That's not what I mean." He turned to look at me, and in the light bleeding off the thorn-door his face had gone the color of old paper. "Doors like this don't just sit there being openable, August. Opening one is loud. Not sound-loud. *Loud* the way a scream is loud through water. Everything the Court ever erased is going to feel that scream, and everything the Court ever erased has legs now, and masks, and no reason at all to let us finish."

I looked back down the valley we'd climbed out of. The river that forgot upstream glinted somewhere below, doing its backward business, keeping its own counsel. Behind us, further than any map admitted, lay a village of polite hollow people, a shrine with eight empty niches and one that wasn't, a field of brambles that drank memories like a drunk drinks gin — fast, and without remembering the morning after.

And somewhere behind all of that, following us the whole way with the tireless patience of weather: the Wardens.

"They've been herding us," I said. It wasn't a new thought. I'd been carrying it for days like a stone in my boot. "Every time we nearly died, there was an exit. Every time we got lost, the map found a road. You don't chase something for three hundred miles and keep letting it live unless you want it to arrive somewhere."

"You think they wanted us to open the door?"

"I think," I said, "that whatever's left of the Court wants this door opened almost as much as we do. And I think that's the worst thing I've learned all year, and I once watched a man get eaten by his own shadow in Vell."

Ilen made a noise that was half laugh, half prayer. He'd gotten good at those. So had I.

We set about the work of preparing, because there is always work, and work is how you keep from thinking. Ilen unpacked his kit — chalk, ink, a knife sharp enough to shave with and too honest to lie about what it was for — and began marking the ground around the door with the script he'd been deciphering since the marshes, the old Court-tongue that lived in his head like a squatter who'd redecorated. I built wards. Small ones. Then less small ones, because small ones were going to matter about as much as good manners in a landslide.

My god helped. That was the strange part. Seven years of silence, seven years of selling luck charms to frightened dockworkers while my patron sat inside me like a cold hearth — and now, every time I reached for magic, it leaned into my hand like a dog that had finally heard its name. It cost me something every time. I could feel the shape of what it had spent holding this door ajar, the way you can feel the shape of a missing tooth with your tongue. But it gave anyway. Gods, it turns out, are not so different from soldiers. They give until there's nothing, and then they give the nothing too.

"Ilen," I said, while he worked. "If this goes badly—"

"Don't."

"If this goes badly, I want you to run. Not fight. Run. You're the only archivist alive who's read what we've read. If I go down, the knowledge goes down with me unless it's in your head, walking very fast in the other direction."

He didn't look up from his chalk. "You're assuming I'd honor that request."

"I'm assuming you're smart enough to."

"I've been reliably informed," he said, "that I am the stupidest smart person either of us has ever met. You said so. In the marshes. With feeling."

"I say things with feeling when people are about to do something idiotic. It's a character flaw."

"And yet." He finished a line of script and sat back on his heels, surveying it. His mouth did the thing it did when he was pleased with his own work and pretending not to be — a small crooked tug, like a picture hung slightly off true. "Here we are. Two idiots at the end of the world."

The wind changed.

There's no better way to describe it. The wind had been coming down off the mountain all evening, smelling of snow and stone, and then it stopped coming from anywhere at all, and the valley went still in the particular way a room goes still when someone walks into it. The thorn-door's petals shivered. My scar lit up like a struck match.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, they're early."

* * *

They came out of the dark the way fog comes off water — no marching column, no horns. One moment the slope below us was empty; the next it had texture. Shapes in grey, moving without hurry, dozens of them, maybe more, their masks catching the door-light and throwing it back wrong, so their faces seemed lit from inside. Hollow-eyed. Silent. They didn't need to hurry. They knew, the way weather knows, that we weren't going anywhere worth going.

"August." Ilen's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for cataloguing disasters. "How many?"

"All of them," I said. "It looks like all of them."

I counted anyway, because counting is something to do with fear. Thirty. Forty. More pooling out of the treeline like spilled ink finding its level. And at their center — this took me a moment, because the mind protects itself, the mind draws curtains — at their center walked one Warden whose mask was different. Cracked. A long fracture running from crown to jaw, and through the fracture, skin.

I knew the skin.

You have to understand what that was like. In the thorn-field, weeks ago, I'd touched a bramble and remembered a sister I never knew I had — her name (Maren, her name was Maren, it had come to me whole and warm, a gift I didn't know I was borrowing), her laugh, the way she used to braid grass crowns and put them on my head and call me Her Grace of the Mud Puddle. And then the thorn drank it. All of it. In the space of a breath she went from existing to never having existed, and what was left was a hole shaped exactly like love, and I have been carrying that hole ever since, learning its weight, learning to walk with it, telling myself at least she was free of them, at least wherever she was now, she wasn't wearing a mask.

Through the crack in the mask, Maren's face looked up at me across forty yards of frozen grass.

Half-erased. That's the only honest way to say it. Half of her face was hers — the set of the jaw, the small scar over her eyebrow from a fall she'd taken when she was nine, a fall I now knew about because a thorn had told me and then taken the knowing away, except apparently not all of it, apparently some of it had soaked in deeper than the drinking. The other half was smoothed over, featureless, the way wax looks after a seal is pressed. And her mouth — her mouth was moving. Even at this distance I could see it moving. Saying something, over and over, the way the villagers said names they'd forgotten the owners of.

She was saying mine. I was almost sure she was saying mine.

"August." Ilen's hand closed on my arm. "August, look at me. What is it?"

"My sister," I said. The word came out of me sideways, ugly, like a splinter working its way out of flesh. "The one from the thorn-field. She's — Ilen, they *made her a Warden.*"

His grip tightened. "That's not possible. The Wardens are constructs. Erasure-stuff given legs. They're not—"

"People," I said. "Chapter eleven of our horrible journey, remember? Under the mask, it's always someone. They're built out of the forgotten. Of course they could build one out of her. She was *there*, Ilen. She was in the field. I touched the bramble and the thorn took her memory and fed it into the Court and the Court—" My voice cracked and I hated it and I kept talking anyway, because if I stopped I was going to scream, and screaming attracts Wardens. "The Court doesn't waste anything. That's the whole point of it. Nothing gets thrown away. It just gets *used.*"

Below us, the line of Wardens had stopped. They stood in the frost in perfect silence, forty-some figures arranged with the terrible neatness of a formal garden, and at the center of them Maren tilted her head up toward the door, toward us, and her half-face wore an expression I recognized with a lurch because I'd worn it myself for seven years. Waiting. Listening for a voice that wasn't coming.

"She's in there," I whispered. "Some piece of her is in there, and it's calling, and the rest of her is standing in a mask answering doors for the thing that ate her."

Ilen said nothing for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was very gentle, which was worse than sarcasm. Sarcasm meant he thought I could afford to lose.

"August. Listen to me. Whatever is left of her in that thing — we cannot reach it. You know that. I know you know that. The Wardens aren't prisoners waiting for rescue. They're what's left *after* the rescue failed. There's no one under there to save. There's just the shape saving leaves behind."

"You don't know that."

"Ilen the archivist knows that," he said. "Ilen your friend wishes to God he didn't."

* * *

They advanced at moonrise.

No signal. No order given or needed. The line simply began to walk uphill, unhurried, boots or boot-shaped absences pressing the frost, and the thorn-door behind us unfurled another petal, and my god stirred in its broken way and said — faintly, in a voice like a bell heard through a wall — *they come because it opens. Stop the opening and they stop.*

"Yes, thank you," I muttered. "Tremendous tactical insight. Any chance you mentioned this seven years ago?"

Silence. Guilt, from a god, feels exactly like guilt from anyone else. Just heavier.

Ilen and I fell into the plan we'd built in the daylight hours, back when the enemy had been hypothetical and my heart had been merely heavy instead of actively tearing itself along a seam I hadn't known was there. He went to the door — the script needed finishing, the last ring of it, and it needed a living hand and a willing toll, but the preparation was his, hours of it, chalk and blood and the old tongue spoken backward into the hinges of the world. I stood between him and the hillside, and I raised wards, and the first rank of Wardens hit them like a tide hitting a seawall.

Wards hold. That's the point of wards. Mine held for eleven seconds.

Then they didn't, and I was fighting, and fighting is a mercy in its way because it doesn't leave room for anything else. I'll spare you the choreography. Magic in Vell was illegal, but nobody legislates against a knife, and I've been a mercenary since before I was careful, and it turns out seven dormant years sit in the body like money in a mattress. I burned two of them down with workings I hadn't dared touch since Vell — big magic, my oldest rule, broken twice in ten seconds — and felt my god pour itself into the burn each time, generous, spendthrift, a soldier spending its last coin. Wardens came apart strangely when they died. Not blood. Unraveling. Threads of grey lifting off them like smoke deciding to become mist, and in the unraveling, for half a heartbeat, each one showed a face.

A dockworker. A child. An old woman with flour on her hands. Faces of the forgotten, surfacing for one breath on their way to nowhere, and I killed them anyway, and I will carry that too, add it to the collection, the museum of August's sins, admission always free.

I held the line. That's the phrase, isn't it. Held the line. For minutes or hours, frost and firelight and the wet-dust smell of unraveling, Ilen's chalk-script glowing behind me brighter with every ring he closed, and the Wardens came and came and came, patient as arithmetic.

And then Maren came up the hill, and the battle became something else entirely.

She moved differently from the others. The others fought like weather, like forces. She fought like a person — guarding her left side, favoring her right leg, using a short blade with a grip I recognized because I'd taught it to her, except I hadn't, because I'd never taught her anything, because I hadn't known she existed until a bramble ate the knowing. Muscle memory of a life the Court had erased from both of us, living on in a stolen body. Her cracked mask caught the doorlight, and through the fracture her eye found mine, and her mouth was still moving, still saying the same thing, and this close I could finally read it.

Not my name.

"*Go home,*" she was saying. "*Go home, go home, go home.*"

Whether it was Maren speaking or the erasure wearing her mouth, I genuinely could not tell you. That's the cruelty of it. That's the whole design. The Court builds its soldiers out of love and lets the love leak through just enough to break you, because a broken defender defends nothing.

Our blades met. Hers slid past my guard and opened my sleeve and the skin under it, shallow, a signature, and through the contact I felt her — really felt her, one flicker, the way you feel a stranger's grief through a wall at night. Cold. Tired. And underneath the cold, so deep down it was barely a temperature at all: a grass crown. Mud puddles. *Her Grace.*

I had a choice.

Understand that. I want it written down somewhere besides my own head, because my own head is not a reliable archive anymore. I had a choice, right there, blade to blade on a frozen hillside: I could let the line break. Fall back, stumble, yield the ground — and the Wardens would pour past me to the door and tear Ilen and his script apart, and the ninth door would stay sealed, and the Court would stay erased, and the erasure would keep spreading outward forever, village by village, country by country, until it reached Vell, until it reached everywhere, until the whole world was a polite hollow people who couldn't say where they lived. Or I could hold. Hold, and win, and win meant Maren stayed a Warden. Win meant the thing wearing my sister served the machine that unmade her until the end of its unnatural service, and the piece of her whispering *go home* went home never.

Save her, and damn everyone. Save everyone, and abandon her.

Seven years ago I'd have called it easy. Duty over love, the mercenary's arithmetic, one life against the world. But seven years ago I hadn't stood in a shrine with nine empty niches. I hadn't held a shaking archivist while a river tried to eat the name off his tongue. I hadn't touched a bramble and felt a whole human being pass through me like light through glass and vanish. The Court had spent centuries teaching the world that forgetting is mercy. And I stood there with my sister's blade against mine and understood, finally, with total clarity, that the Court was wrong — that forgetting isn't mercy, it's just death with better manners — and that I would rather carve the truth of her out of my own chest with a spoon than let one more piece of Maren be fed into that smiling machine.

So I chose the world.

God forgive me, I chose the world, and I did it the only way I could bear to: I stopped defending against her and defended *through* her, caught her blade in a ward that should have shattered and somehow didn't because my god threw everything it had left into it, and I turned her strength aside and put her down — gently, as gently as you can put down something that won't stay down — and stepped over her unraveling body while her half-face looked up at me and her mouth kept its rhythm. *Go home. Go home.*

"I'm trying," I told her. My voice came out in pieces. "I'm trying to make one for everybody."

Behind me, the last ring of script closed with a sound like a lock the size of the sky, and the ninth door stood bare, its thorns drawn back, its threshold glowing — ready. Ready for a toll. Ready for a price. And the Wardens regrouped on the hillside, more of them than I'd beaten, all of them, probably, the whole grey population of the erased, and they started uphill again without haste, without hatred, without anything at all except the terrible patience of the inevitable.

Ilen called my name from the door. There was something in his voice I didn't like. Something resolved.

I turned to face the hill, spat blood, and rolled up my sleeves.

"Hurry," I told him. "Whatever you're doing back there — *hurry.*"
