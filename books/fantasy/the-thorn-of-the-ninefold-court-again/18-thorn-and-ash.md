## 18. Thorn and Ash

### August

The Wardens came out of the dark like a tide that had been waiting politely for an invitation.

I counted them the way you count anything when you're about to die — badly, quickly, and with a strange detachment, as though the numbers belonged to someone else. Eleven at the tree line. More behind them, their lantern-light smearing through the thorn-haze of the borderland like oil through water. Their masks caught the glow of the ninth door and gave nothing back.

Behind me, Ilen had his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his hands buried wrist-deep in living bramble, working the door's lock the way he'd once worked archives — patiently, obsessively, muttering fragments of a script only he could read. Thorn had already opened three lines across his forearms. He hadn't noticed. Or he'd noticed and decided his blood was a resource he could spare.

"How long?" I called over my shoulder.

"However long it takes you to keep them off me," he said, not looking up. "So: forever, ideally."

"Wonderful. I've always wanted forever."

The first Warden reached me before I finished being flippant, which is usually how it goes.

I'd spent seven years selling luck charms the size of a thumbnail because big magic got people erased. I'd built a whole life on the principle that power was a debt and silence was solvency. And now I stood in front of eleven hollow-eyed things wearing my countrymen's faces, and I reached down into the place where my god lived — that faint, guttering warmth, that voice worn thin as old paper — and I pulled.

The working came out of me like a scream I'd been holding since Vell.

Light went up from the ground in a ring, white and thorn-shaped, and the nearest three Wardens simply stopped — not dead, never dead, they didn't have death in them anymore, just absence wearing boots — but stopped, swaying, their lanterns guttering. My god made a sound in the back of my skull that might have been approval or might have been pain. With him, lately, those were the same word.

"August!" Ilen's voice, cracking on the second syllable. "It's asking for confirmation — the door wants to know who's opening it—"

"Tell it whoever's paying!"

"That's not—" He swore in archive-cant, which is mostly dead verbs. "August, there's a toll. There was always going to be a toll."

Of course there was. There had been a toll on everything since the day the dying man put a map in my hands: tolls of blood, tolls of sleep, tolls of villages full of people who couldn't remember their own names. I parried a Warden's blade with a bar of raw light and thought, distantly, that I should have asked more questions. That was the story of my life. I should have asked more questions.

Two Wardens flanked me while I was philosophizing. One caught my shoulder and spun me; under its mask I saw a woman's face, mid-erasure, lips moving around a name that wasn't hers anymore. I drove light into its chest and it came apart into ash and moths. The moths were worse somehow. Everything here ended in something soft.

* * *

### Ilen

The door was a wound in the world dressed up as architecture.

Thorn grew across it in whorls, dense as script, and every strand of it was legible if you'd spent ten years cataloguing forbidden histories and one year in a cell learning to read by feel. It wanted a payment. It was very clear about this, in the way contracts are clear — technically, legally, cruelly. *One soul's love-memories,* said the thorn, in the language of the Ninefold Court, which had always been fluent in price.

I understood what it meant. I understood it the way you understand a diagnosis: completely, and then not at all, and then all at once in the middle of the night.

My lover had been executed eleven years ago. Her name was carved into my left arm in letters I'd re-inked every morning since the river, because forgetting her even briefly had been the worst thing that had ever happened to me, and I had died twice, and been imprisoned, and watched my life's work burn. So the arithmetic seemed simple. The door wanted love-memories. Mine were mostly grief. Grief I had carried so long it had become furniture — load-bearing, familiar, mine, but furniture nonetheless. I would pay in memories of a woman already gone, and August would walk out of this collapsing country whole, and the cost would be a scar where a wound used to be.

I began composing the payment. You compose it in images; the door reads images. Her hands. The letters in my coat. The last thing she said to me, which I will not write down here because some things stay private even inside a memory being sold.

That was when I heard August stop fighting.

* * *

### August

She was at the edge of the clearing, half-turned toward the door, mask cracked down one side like a dropped plate.

I knew her. That was the thing no amount of preparation could have blunted. Seven years I hadn't known she existed; one bramble in a thorn-field had given her back to me for the length of a heartbeat, and now here she was again — my sister, or what the Court had left of her, ash to the elbows and a name whispering itself out of her mouth like smoke escaping a crack in a wall. She wasn't saying *August*. She was saying whatever name had come before, the one the erasure ate first.

The other Wardens were converging on Ilen. I could stop them. I could spend myself doing it, and maybe fail anyway, and lose both of them plus the world.

Or.

The door's toll hung in the air between us, patient as a creditor. *Love-memories.* And I stood there in the burning grass doing the cruelest mathematics of my life.

Ilen had a lover. Dead, mourned, kept folded in letters against his ribs. If he paid, he'd lose grief. I would lose—

No. I wouldn't let him do it. That was the first thought, hot and total. The second thought arrived right behind it, quieter, and it was worse: *and if he doesn't, I will.*

Because here's what nobody tells you about love: it's the only currency you can't counterfeit. I could feed the door my mercenary years, my childhood, the taste of Vell rain, and it would spit them back as insufficient. But Ilen laughing at his own jokes before he finished them. Ilen carving a name into his arm because forgetting it once terrified him more than death. Ilen asleep sitting up against a shrine wall, trusting me enough to be unguarded. The way he said *August* like a man testing whether a word still worked.

I loved him. I had been carefully not saying it for weeks, saving it, hoarding it like a charm too valuable to sell. And the door knew. Doors like this always know.

A Warden's blade opened my arm and I barely felt it. My sister was twenty feet away, whispering her eaten name. Ilen was shouting my name from the door. Between those two facts I chose, and the choosing took less time than it takes to tell.

I threw the last of my god's warmth outward — not to kill, never to kill, but to knock my sister backward into the deep thorn-shadow where the door's collapse would pass over her, where the freeing of the Court might reach her before the erasure did. She looked at me as she fell. For half a second, something behind the mask's crack focused. Something that might have been recognition.

Then I turned and ran at the door.

* * *

### Ilen

She hit the bramble beside me at a dead sprint, and I saw her face and knew.

There's a look people get when they've decided something irreversible — I'd seen it on condemned archivists, on lovers saying goodbye at prison gates. August wore it like she wore everything else: defiantly, daring you to comment.

"No," I said. "Whatever you're doing — no."

"The door needs a toll," she said, and pressed her palm flat to the thorn, and the thorn drank.

I grabbed her wrist. She was already going glassy at the edges — not visibly, nothing so kind as visibly, but I knew her well enough by then to see the absence starting, the way a room changes when someone has quietly decided to leave it.

"It's me, isn't it," I said. "You're paying with—"

"You have letters," she said. "You have a whole coat full of her. You were going to pay with grief and call it sacrifice. Don't look at me like that. This is the only selfless thing I've ever done and I'd hate to ruin it with witnesses."

"August." My voice did something humiliating. "August, don't—"

"There it is," she said softly. And she smiled — that wry, closed-door smile I'd catalogued across a hundred campfires without ever letting myself file it under what it actually was — and she said, "For the record, since apparently I'm the sentimental one after all: I lo—"

The door took the rest.

* * *

### August

—the rest of the sentence was gone. There had been a sentence. There had been someone standing very close to me, someone whose face made my chest hurt in a way I couldn't source, and I had been telling him something important.

The door was open. Light poured through it like water through a broken dam, and behind us the Court was coming down — I could feel it in my teeth, nine gods' worth of country folding up like a letter being put away. Wardens were falling apart into ash and moths all across the clearing, freed or unmade, and somewhere in the drift of them a woman's face looked at me without a mask and mouthed something before she scattered.

I was running toward the light. Someone was screaming behind me — my name, I thought, screamed like it was the last copy of a book burning — and part of me wanted to turn around, needed to turn around, except turning around required knowing why, and the why was already gone, slipping away between one heartbeat and the next like water through fingers.

"Don't—" the voice said. "August—"

August. That was me. Good. I still had that.

I stepped through the door with a scar forming on my palm and an ache opening in my chest shaped exactly like a man, and I did not remember his name, and I did not remember making the choice, and some small stubborn coal at the bottom of me — the last unspent ember of a god who had held a door open seven years so I could stand here — whispered:

*Run.*
