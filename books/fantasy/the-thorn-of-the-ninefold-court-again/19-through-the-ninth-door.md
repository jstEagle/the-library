## 19. Through the Ninth Door

The door takes my blood the way a drunk takes the last coin in your pocket — gratefully, and without any intention of giving it back.

I don't remember deciding to cut my palm. I think I did decide, somewhere between Ilen's screaming and the Wardens closing like a fist, but the memory of it is already going soft at the edges, like bread left out in the rain. There is a thorn-shaped wound on my hand and the door is drinking from it and I am standing in front of the thing I have walked half a country to find, and all I can think — absurdly, wretchedly — is that I never got around to telling him.

Telling who.

No. No, I know. I know his name. It's right there. It's a short name, an archivist's name, ink-stained letters, he keeps letters in his coat, he carved a name into his own arm once by a river that runs backward, and I held him steady while he did it, and his name is —

His name is gone.

I stand very still while the world rearranges itself around the absence. The thorn-sealed door shudders open one inch, then another, light spilling through the gap like water through a cracked hull. Behind me, the Wardens are still coming. Somewhere among them is a face I was fighting not to look at, a face that made me pay this toll instead of another, and I can't — the shape of why won't hold. There was someone. There was someone behind me, or beside me, or ahead of me, and there is now a hole where they were, and the hole has edges as sharp as anything growing on this door.

"Go," says a voice. Faint. Broken. A voice I have been listening to for seven years of silence without knowing that's what silence sounds like when it's trying. My god. The ninth. The one who spent itself holding this door ajar so that I would have somewhere to run when everything ended.

"I'm not leaving you here," I say, because apparently even with a hole in my head I'm still stubborn enough to argue with gods.

*You already are,* the voice says, gentle as ash falling. *You left the moment you paid. Go through. Please. I am so tired, little thorn.*

And because I have never in my life done the sensible thing when someone asked nicely, I go.

* * *

Stepping through the ninth door feels like stepping through a memory of cold water — you feel the idea of the shock without the shock itself. One moment the Court is collapsing around me, stone folding into stone, thorn blooming and burning in the same breath, and the next I am in a corridor made of nothing at all, walking on a floor that remembers being a road.

Behind me, muffled through whatever the door is made of, the Court comes down.

I want to say I felt triumphant. I want to tell you I stood tall and watched nine gods' worth of country crumble into the erasure it chose for itself, and felt like a hero out of one of those ballads the dockworkers hum in Vell when they're drunk enough to forget singing them is illegal too. But mostly what I felt was hungry. Not for food — for the missing pieces. My mind kept reaching for things that weren't there anymore, the way a tongue goes back and back to the socket of a lost tooth.

There was a man. There was definitely a man. He had — hands. Ink on his hands, probably, or maybe I'm inventing that. He said something when I turned away from him. He screamed something. My name, probably. August. That much survived: my own name, sitting in my skull like the last chair left standing after a fire.

August. Good. One name. I can work with one name.

I keep walking. The corridor doesn't end, exactly; it just keeps being approximately as long as I need it to be, which is either a mercy or a trap. Light comes from nowhere. My boots make no sound. Somewhere far behind me — or far ahead, time is negotiable in here — a voice is fading. The god's voice. It's saying something. It's been saying something for a while and I've been nodding along like a woman half-asleep at her stall while a customer talks about the weather.

*— proud of you,* it might be saying. *— sorry. So sorry it had to be —*

Sorry it had to be what? I turn to ask, and the corridor is empty, and the voice is gone, folded away into ordinary quiet, the kind of quiet Vell has every morning before the harbor wakes up. Just quiet. Just air. The specific, unremarkable silence of a city that has never contained a god and never will again.

That's fine. I didn't need a god. I ran a charm stall for seven years under that silence and made rent most months.

Seven years. Why seven? What happened seven years ago?

Nothing. Nothing happened. Nothing has ever happened to me. I sell luck charms to frightened dockworkers and I have rules — never big magic, never attachments — and I have a scar on my palm shaped like a thorn, and that is the whole of me, top to bottom.

Except the scar is fresh. The scar is bleeding. The scar is hours old, and I got it opening a —

A what?

My hand knows the word even if my head doesn't. It curls around the absence, fingers spread, tracing the outline of something enormous that isn't there anymore. I got it doing something. I came from somewhere. There was a country — no. There wasn't a country. Countries don't just stop existing. Countries get conquered, absorbed, renamed. They leave ruins and tax records and bitter expatriates writing memoirs nobody reads.

This one left nothing. This one left me.

* * *

Somewhere in the walking — I've stopped counting steps, the floor eats numbers — I realize I'm running.

Not the controlled jog of a mercenary covering ground. Running. Arms pumping, breath sawing, the flat-out animal sprint of someone fleeing something directly behind them. Except when I glance over my shoulder there's nothing. Corridor. Light. Quiet.

So why am I running?

There's a reason. I can feel the reason the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. There was a danger, and I escaped it, and the escape cost — it cost —

It cost a man with ink on his hands.

No. Stop. That's not — I don't know any man with ink on his hands. I don't know any men at all. That's rather the point of me. Never big magic, never attachments. Rules keep you alive in a city that hangs practitioners from the harbor crane. Rules and a locked door and charms sold quick and cheap to people too scared to look at your face twice.

But my legs won't stop running, and my eyes are wet, and I am grieving something with no shape and no name and no evidence it ever existed except that my body knows. My body carries the grief the way it carries the scar: written into it, load-bearing, impossible to set down.

I try to reconstruct him. It seems important that I try. There was a person — I'd stake my life on it, and I have staked considerably more than my life on it, apparently — and he deserves better than to vanish unwitnessed. So: he existed. He had a voice. Was it deep? I think it was dry. Dry and sardonic, the voice of someone who'd been hurt enough times to develop a sense of humor about it, someone who traded truths across a fire because truths were the only currency he had left. He kept something precious in his coat. Letters? Yes — letters. From someone he loved who was taken from him, and he carried the letters against his chest like armor that only worked if it hurt.

He was brave. He was brave in the specific way of men who don't believe they are, who volunteer for the dangerous thing while insisting it's merely the necessary thing. He worked a — a mechanism, a lock, a spell, something, at the end, with his sleeves rolled up and his jaw set, while I fought. I fought for him. I bled for him. And then I fed the price myself instead of letting him pay it, and he screamed, and I walked away, and now he is a man-shaped hole in the middle of me and I cannot even give him the dignity of his name.

Was it — it started with a vowel. Didn't it? Or a consonant. It was a name. People have names. His was a good one, I'm almost sure it was a good one, the kind you say slow.

It's gone. It's gone and the corridor keeps going and I keep running from nothing toward nothing, crying for a stranger I died a little to save, and the worst part — the truly obscene part — is that some small practical corner of me is already noting that the grief is lighter than it should be. That forgetting is easier than remembering. That the hole is starting to feel like a feature instead of a wound. Some traitor architect in my mind is already drafting plans to build over the vacant lot.

I hate that corner of me. I tell it so, out loud, to no one, and my voice sounds wrong in the endless light, too loud and too alone, and I keep running anyway.

* * *

Things start leaving mid-sentence after that.

I notice it first with the sister. I don't — wait. Sister? I had a sister. Did I have a sister? There was a face, half-erased, whispering a name, wearing a Warden's hollow eyes, and I chose — I chose her over — no, I chose the world over her, or I chose her over the mission, I spared her, I fed the difference myself, and she's free now, the god said she'd be free, the door collapsing would free all of them, every hollow-eyed stolen face released back into —

Back into what? Who was she? Whose face? I'm holding onto the fact of a sister the way a drowning man holds a plank, and the plank is dissolving, and I can hear myself narrating it — *she was my sister, she wore a mask, I saved her, I saved her, I —* — and somewhere in that sentence the *she* stops pointing at anything. The sentence finishes and the referent is dead. I saved her. Saved who? Saved whom. Grammar without a soul. The sentence stands there like a coat on a hook, waiting for shoulders that will never come back.

*I saved her,* I say again, testing it, and feel nothing, and the nothing is so total that for a moment I mistake it for peace.

Then the word *door* goes.

I don't hear it leave. Words don't announce their departures; they just aren't there when you reach for them, like coins from a pocket picked by someone patient. I come around a bend in the corridor — corridors shouldn't have bends, this one is inventing architecture as it dies along with everything else — and I think: I came through the —

Through the. Through the what. Entrance? Gate? There's a gap in my language, smooth-edged, healed over already, and when I try to think about the thing I used to enter this place my mind slides off it the way water slides off oilcloth. There was a passage. Passages exist. But the specific word, the word everyone uses, the word children learn before they can tie their shoes — it's simply not in me anymore. I reach for it and touch smooth blankness, and worse, I catch myself thinking *well, doors are such a minor category, hardly essential vocabulary,* and the traitor architect nods approvingly and pencils in another revision.

What else is it taking? I inventory frantically, a merchant counting stock after a break-in. My name: present. Vell: present. Charms: present. Magic: present, though it feels distant, theoretical, a language I studied once. Fire: yes. Mother: — mother. Do I have a mother? I search and find a warm gray blur, affection with no face attached, and I decide that's normal. Everyone's mother is probably a blur if you interrogate it hard enough in a lightless corridor between worlds.

Mercenary. I was a mercenary. Before the stall. Blades, contracts, a sigil on my wrist that my patron god touched like a lamp being lit — and the sigil is gone now, the skin smooth, and I understand with distant clarity that the god spent itself holding something open for me, and I cannot remember feeling grateful, and I cannot remember feeling anything about it at all, and the grief that should be there is a room with the furniture removed.

Keep running. Keep the list. Name, city, trade, scar. If I can hold four things I am still a person. Four things and a direction.

Why am I running?

The question arrives politely this time, without panic, the way a clerk asks for paperwork. I'm running because — there was danger. Because the place I came from was falling. Because someone told me to go. Someone with a tired, broken voice, someone who called me *little thorn*, someone who begged, and I went, and the reason I'm running is —

Is —

I'm running because my legs are moving. That's all I have. Momentum without origin. A story with its first chapters torn out, running on the strength of its ending alone, and even the ending is going soft now, the corridor brightening at its far end into something that looks horribly like an ordinary morning, gulls, harbor-smell, the grey particular light of Vell at dawn.

Vell. Home. I own a charm stall in Vell. I've always owned a charm stall in Vell. Haven't I? Seven years of it. Seven years of what?

The last thing to go is not a word. It's smaller than a word. It's the ache — the low, persistent, nameless ache I've been carrying since I woke up on this side of everything, the ache that was the ghost of the man with ink on his hands, the body's last honest record of what I paid. I feel it lift out of me like a splinter drawn clean, and for one heartbeat I am whole, seamless, a woman with a scar and a stall and no debts, and I nearly weep at how much it hurts to stop hurting.

Then the light takes me, ordinary and grey and smelling of salt, and I am falling forward into a morning I do not remember earning, and the last thought I manage to finish is:

Whatever I forgot, I hope it was worth it. I hope whoever paid alongside me knows that I —

I —

The gulls are loud this morning. I sit up on cold cobbles in a city I half-recognize, palm stinging, heart pounding from a dream of running, and I think: I should open the stall early today. Something tells me someone might come.

Someone always does.
