## 20. A City That Outlawed Magic

The first thing I noticed was the light.

It came through a window I didn't recognize, in a room I didn't recognize, over a bed that was — I checked with my hands before I opened my eyes all the way — mine. The blanket smelled of lavender and salt. The ceiling had a water stain shaped vaguely like a fish, and someone had drawn an eye on the fish in charcoal so that it seemed to be watching me back. I did not remember drawing that eye. But it was drawn in the particular impatient scribble I use for everything, the way a signature is a signature even when you can't remember signing.

I lay still and took inventory, because that is what you do when you wake up somewhere wrong. You count what you have and you decide whether to panic about any of it.

One body. Mine, as far as I could tell. Sore like I'd walked a very long way — hips, calves, the bottoms of my feet complaining in a chorus — but unbroken.

One palm. This deserves its own line item. My right palm carried a scar I had never seen before: thin, dark, curved at one end like a barbed point. A thorn. Not the ragged kind you get from a hedge row; this was clean, deliberate, the way a brand is deliberate or a promise is deliberate. It didn't hurt. It *hummed*, faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound is technically over. When I pressed it with my thumb, something behind my ribs pressed back.

One city, audible through the window. Gulls. Cart wheels. Someone arguing about the price of eels with the specific passion Vell reserved for the price of eels. Home sounds. My sounds.

And one absence.

This is the part I have trouble explaining, even to myself, and I am the only audience I've got. There was a hole in me. Not a wound — wounds have edges, and edges tell you the shape of what made them. This was more like reaching into your pocket for a coin and finding the pocket sewn shut, and not being able to remember ever keeping coins there, and yet your hand keeps going to the pocket anyway, every few minutes, all day long. Something had been in me that wasn't anymore. Something I must have loved carrying, because the space where it used to be was worn smooth, like a stair worn by feet.

I got up. I dressed. I went downstairs, because whoever owned the water-stained fish presumably also owned whatever was below it, and I wanted to know what I'd woken up owning besides my own confusion.

It was a charm stall.

Not even a stall, really — a proper little shop wedged between a chandler's and a place that sold nothing but rope, with a counter, a stool, and shelves of small hung things swaying gently in the draft from the door. Luck charms. Salt charms against drowning. A whole rack of the little knotted threads dockworkers buy when they want to feel like the sea has agreed to be reasonable. Every piece was strung and priced in my handwriting. Some of them were good work. Two or three were better than good — one knot near the window held a warmth I recognized without being able to name where I'd learned it, a warmth like a hand briefly closed around yours.

I stood in my own shop, in my own city, holding a charm I apparently made, and felt the smooth-worn hole in me ache like a tooth.

"Morning," said the rope-seller, leaning across the gap between buildings with the ease of long acquaintance. "You're up late."

"How late?"

"Past noon. Big night?" He grinned. Middle-aged, sunburnt, missing half a finger to some nautical accident he'd probably told me about. He knew me. That much was clear. He knew me and I knew nothing about him except that his grin had a gold tooth on the left side and he weighed his rope honestly, which put him ahead of most merchants in Vell.

"Something like that," I said. "Long trip."

"That right? Where'd you get to?"

I opened my mouth and found nothing there. Not silence exactly — silence implies a shape where the answer should be. This was just empty, a door opening onto a wall. "Around," I said.

He laughed. "Always are, you. Brought anything back?"

"Just myself."

"Worth the fare, then." And he went back to his rope, satisfied, and left me standing there with my heart going strange against my ribs, because he had asked me a question that mattered — where have you been, what happened to you — and I couldn't answer it, and worse, nobody in the world seemed to think that was odd. Nobody looked at me like a woman who'd been away. They looked at me like a woman who'd slept in.

* * *

Vell was different. It took me most of the afternoon walking its streets to put my finger on how, which embarrassed me, because a mercenary learns to read a city the way a sailor reads weather, and I was reading mine like a stranger.

Here is what I finally saw: nobody was hiding.

I don't know how else to say it. Vell, the city I knew — the city stitched into my bones somewhere deeper than memory, apparently, because I knew it even with the holes in me — was a city of lowered voices and cupped hands. In the Vell I knew, you sold luck out of alleys. You never let anyone see your hands do the actual working. You learned which constables took bribes and which took fingers, and you learned the color of Warden light, and when that pale silver slid along a street everyone found urgent business elsewhere.

In the Vell I woke up in, a woman two streets over was selling charms off an open cart with a painted sign. Painted. *Maren Truek, Charms & Blessings, Fair Prices.* She was haggling cheerfully with a grandmother while a city watchman waited his turn, patient as a man in a bread queue. Down by the harbor, a boy no older than twelve was doing a cantrip for coins — sparks off his fingertips, the oldest trick there is — and the crowd around him was tossing coppers and laughing, and not one face wore the flat gray fear I would have staked my life on seeing.

I grabbed a man by the sleeve. A clerk type, ink-stained, harried. "The magic," I said, too loudly. "Since when is it legal?"

He looked at me the way you look at a dog that asks you to explain rain. "Since always?" he said. "Madam, are you well?"

"It was banned. There were Wardens. They—"

"Wardens?" Now he frowned, but it was the frown of a man trying to recall a word from a language he'd studied badly in school, not the flinch of a man who'd heard boots in the night. "Old story, isn't it? Some kingdom way back outlawed magic and then, what, vanished. Everyone says it differently. My gran used to say they erased themselves out of spite." He shrugged. "Anyway. Not here. Magic's been fine in Vell forever. Are you sure you're well?"

*Erased themselves.* The words went into me and found the hole and sat down in it, and for a moment I couldn't breathe for the ache of them, an ache with no address, no reason, just pure undirected grief like mourning a funeral you never attended for a person whose name you never knew.

"I'm fine," I said, and let go of his sleeve, and watched him hurry off thinking Vell had acquired a madwoman overnight.

That was the shape of the day. I walked until my sore legs gave out, and everywhere it was the same. The laws hadn't changed so much as the fear had been lifted out from under them, quietly, the way you'd lift a stone out of a garden — and the grass had grown back over the spot so completely that even the old-timers, when I pressed them, could only offer folklore. The Ninefold Court. A country that hated magic so much it erased itself rather than live with it. Or loved magic so much it hid itself away with it, depending who told it. Nobody agreed. Nobody cared. The story had gone soft at the edges, worn smooth, the way —

The way a pocket wears smooth where something used to sit.

I stopped in the middle of the fish market with that thought and stood very still while eel-arguments crashed around me. Because I had thought *pocket* a moment ago, upstairs, before any of this, and now the market was full of pockets, metaphorical ones, worn ones, and I could not shake the feeling that the whole world was doing what I was doing: reaching into itself every few minutes for a thing that was no longer there, finding the space sewn shut, and going about its day.

You don't miss what you can't remember. That's the mercy of it, everyone says. But I'm telling you there is another kind of missing, the kind that lives in the body instead of the mind. The kind that knows the weight of what it lost without knowing its name.

* * *

I spent three days relearning my own life before I trusted it enough to sleep in it properly.

The room above the shop had my clothes, my knife roll — a mercenary's kit, oiled and maintained, though I couldn't have told you my last contract — and a journal that turned out to be the strangest document I have ever read, and I include in that category the fevered confessions of men I once guarded for a week. The journal was mine. The handwriting was mine. But it kept stopping. Whole pages ended mid-sentence, sometimes mid-word, the ink trailing off as if the hand writing it had simply forgotten why it was moving. One entry read: *The toll is paid with* — and then nothing, and then, several lines later, in the same ink, continuing a different sentence entirely: *— and the knots hold better if you soak the thread in brine first.*

Another entry, near the end, just said: *Don't look for the shape of it. You'll only cut yourself.*

I read that one a dozen times. I decided, eventually, to follow it, mostly because following advice from my past self is the closest thing I have to trusting anyone, and partly because when I tried to look for the shape of it — the shape of the hole, the missing thing — the scar on my palm warmed until I had to stop.

The scar did other things, too. On the second night, sitting over cold soup, I caught myself talking to it. Not aloud. Just — addressing it, inside, the way you address a god you're not sure is listening. *Well?* I thought at it. *Anyone home?*

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. Gods don't answer; that's practically their defining feature, if the folklore I'd grown up on and apparently never questioned is anything to go by. But the humming under my skin shifted key, very slightly, and I got the distinct impression — ridiculous, unprovable, true — of something vast turning over in its sleep. Comfortable. Almost finished resting.

"You could have left a note," I told my palm.

The scar stayed warm a moment longer, and then settled, and I swear it settled the way a dog settles when it decides you're staying.

* * *

On the fourth day I opened the shop.

It felt absurd — a woman selling luck she can't account for, in a city that can't remember why it was ever afraid — but the rent (I found the ledger; I owed it) does not care what you can account for. And the work came back to my hands faster than I expected. Knots, sigils, the little folded paper prayers dockworkers tuck into their boots. My hands knew things my head didn't. Halfway through the first morning I tied a binding I had no conscious knowledge of, an old pattern, intricate, and when I looked at it finished I felt proud and homesick at once, which makes no sense unless you understand that I was homesick for my own hands.

Business was good. Fear sells better than hope, everyone knows that, but hope sells steadier, and that was the other change I couldn't stop noticing: people bought charms here the way they bought bread. Not desperately. Casually. A young couple bought a blessing for their boat and argued playfully over the color of the thread. An old man bought a charm against forgetting — the irony of that one sat in my throat like a fishbone — and when I asked what he wanted to remember, he smiled and said, "Everything, girl. Everything. It all goes so fast."

I charged him half price and didn't know why, and he didn't ask, and we were both kind to each other across a gap neither of us could see the edges of. That was the day I understood what this new Vell actually was. Not a city that had won something. A city that had been *paid for*. Somebody had bought this peace, this easy morning light, these children doing spark-tricks for coppers. Somebody had paid the whole bill, and the world had eaten the meal without tasting it, and the only receipt in existence was a scar on my palm and a smooth-worn hollow behind my ribs.

I don't know how I know that. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is all I have left of whatever I traded away. I don't know it the way you know a fact. I know it the way you know a room is empty before you open the door — by the quality of the silence coming through the cracks.

* * *

He came in at closing time on the sixth day.

I noticed him the way you notice weather changing — a pressure, a shift, before any actual detail. The bell over my door rang and a man stepped in out of the evening, tall, rumpled, with the stooped shoulders of someone who has spent his life bent over desks and the careful eyes of someone who spent it bent over desks *he shouldn't have been reading.* His coat was good wool gone soft at the elbows. He had ink on his fingers, old and layered, the stain of a profession rather than a Tuesday.

"Closing," I said.

"I'll be quick." His voice did something to me. I want to write that plainly because everything else about that moment was anything but plain: his voice did something to the hole in me. It didn't fill it — nothing fills it — but it fit against the edge of it, the way a broken pot shard fits the break it came from, and for one lurching heartbeat I nearly said something insane, something like *have we met*, and had to bite it down hard.

"What can I do for you?" I asked instead, professional, steady, palms flat on the counter — and then realized my scarred palm was flat on the counter, facing him, and pulled it back too fast.

His eyes followed the motion. Something moved through his face, quick and swallowed, like a fish turning under ice.

"A charm," he said. "For protection."

"For whom?"

"Me."

Against what?

He was quiet long enough that the question hung there between us, and then he smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen on a living man — warm and wrecked at once, a smile built to carry a weight I couldn't see, carried daily, carried *cheerfully*, God help him. "Against forgetting," he said lightly. "Everyone needs one. Don't you find?"

"Everyone needs one," I agreed, because I had sold one such charm six days ago and meant it then too.

I made it while he watched. Simple work, a salt-and-iron knot, the kind that costs two bits and does almost nothing and means everything. My hands were steady. Mostly. When I handed it across the counter our fingers touched, brief, accidental — and the scar on my palm sang. Not hummed. *Sang*, one clear note, silent, inside the bone, and the hole behind my ribs tore wide open for a single terrible instant, and I understood absolutely nothing except that I was about to cry in front of a customer.

I didn't. I am a professional.

"Two bits," I said, voice level as a shelf.

He paid with four and told me to keep the change. He does this — I learned later, I learned within the week, I learned the way you learn the tide — he comes in every day, near closing, buys something small he doesn't need, pays too much, says something gentle and slightly strange, and leaves. Every day. The rope-seller thinks he's sweet on me. Maren Truek down the street thinks he's lonely. The old man who fears forgetting thinks he's a scholar with too many books in his head.

They're all wrong, I think. I think he's grieving. I think he's grieving at my counter, politely, daily, for something he can see and I cannot, and I think — and this is the part I only admit at night, lying under the watching fish, with my scarred hand curled against my chest like a held breath — I think whatever he's grieving, *I'm the one who caused it.*

I don't know how. I don't know what. The knowledge isn't in me; that's the entire problem, the whole architecture of my situation: the knowledge isn't in me, it was taken out, or given away, or *spent*, and what remains is a shape, an edge, a pot-shard fit.

But tonight he paused at the door on his way out, charm in hand, evening light on his ruined kind face, and he looked back at me and said:

"Goodnight, August."

My name. Which I had not told him.

The bell rang. The door swung shut. And I stood alone in my little shop in a peaceful city that doesn't remember its own fear, pressing my thumb into the thorn-shaped scar, feeling it hum its one unfinished note, wondering who I was before I was the woman who owns this stall —

and who he was, that he knows my name like a prayer he's not allowed to say twice.
