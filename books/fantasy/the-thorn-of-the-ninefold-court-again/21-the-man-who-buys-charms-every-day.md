## 21. The Man Who Buys Charms Every Day

I have purchased forty-one charms from August Blackwood, and every single one of them is useless.

This is not an insult to her craft. The charms are excellent — well-woven, honestly priced, each one humming faintly with the kind of small, careful magic that used to get people hanged in Vell and now gets them a nod and a smile. No, the charms are useless because I buy them for no purpose at all. A charm against nightmares, and I sleep like a stone. A charm for safe travel, and I haven't left the city in four months. A charm to keep bread fresh, though I live above a bakery. Forty-one little woven knots of intention, sitting in a drawer in my room like the world's most pathetic reliquary.

The archivist in me would call it a collection. The man in me knows better. It's a ritual. I go to her stall every morning at the ninth bell because at the ninth bell she is there, rolling up the awning with one hand, thorn-scarred palm turned away from the street as if she's still hiding it out of habit — and she is not, because the law changed and nobody remembers why it ever needed changing, least of all her — and I buy something I don't need so that I can stand in front of her for ninety seconds and hear her say my name.

She says it like it's new every time. *Ilen.* Two syllables, slightly amused, the way you'd say the name of a man who has just bought his third anti-nightmare charm. She doesn't know what she gave me when she said it the first time. She doesn't know she said it once while bleeding, once while laughing over a fire made of wet gorse, once through a door that was closing on everything either of us loved.

She says it now, this morning, and I hand over two coppers for a charm against bad weather, and Vell's sky is the color of a rinsed dish, cloudless and mild, and we both know it.

"Third one this week," she says, squinting at me. "You expecting a flood?"

"Optimist," I say. "Always prepare for the worst."

"You're the gloomiest customer I have, and I sell to dockworkers." She ties the charm off with a bit of red thread, and her fingers move with a fluency that makes something in my chest turn over, because I remember when those fingers were shaking, when they were pressed flat against a dying god's door, when they let go of mine. "There. May your skies disappoint you."

"I'll treasure it."

"You'll put it in the drawer," she says. "With the others."

And there it is. She noticed. Of course she noticed; she notices everything except the one thing that would crack her open, and I have spent four months making sure of that, guarding her ignorance like a border garrison guards a pass. I feel my face do something complicated, and she laughs — short, surprised at herself — and looks away first.

*Forty-two,* I think, pocketing the charm. *Only fifty-eight to a hundred, and then I'll have to invent a new ritual.*

The truth about rituals, though, is that they aren't really for counting. They're for keeping. Every morning I walk down to the salt-stained end of Copperlane where her stall leans against a wall that still bears, if you know where to look, the ghost of an outlawed sigil scraped off by Wardens' irons — and I am the only person in Vell who knows what was there, or who scraped it, or why. I carry the whole erased country in my head like water cupped in two hands, and every day some of it sloshes out, and I let it. That was the bargain I made with myself at her stall on the first morning after: I will remember enough to honor it, and forget enough to survive it, and I will never, ever make her pay again for what she already paid.

It is a strange arithmetic, grief. You'd think losing someone would be the hard part. Losing someone who is standing three feet away from you, haggling cheerfully, alive and sunlit and utterly free of you — that is a discipline. That is a monastic order of one, with daily obligations and no hope of advancement.

* * *

Tuesdays are market-heavy, so I linger. This is permitted under the rules I've established for myself: linger only when there's a crowd, because crowds mean she can't study me too closely, and I cannot afford to be studied. August Blackwood reads people the way I used to read marginalia — quickly, hungrily, catching what others missed. Before, she read me like a confiscated manuscript, cover to cover, and then she walked into a wall of thorns for me. Now she reads me as *regular*, which is a mercy and a wound in roughly equal measure.

Today a child is at the stall, maybe seven, holding out a knucklebone and asking whether August can charm it to always land sixes.

"That's cheating," August says gravely.

"It's *luck*," the child says.

"Luck is just cheating the gods politely." And she takes the knucklebone, breathes on it, mutters something — I catch the shape of it, an old working, Court-cadenced, and my hands curl involuntarily around the edge of the stall — and hands it back. "There. Now it lands sixes when you deserve it and ones when you're being a menace. Which is better than sixes."

The child runs off delighted. August watches him go with an expression I've catalogued carefully over four months: not happiness exactly. Something adjacent. The look of a woman standing outside a house she owns but can't find the door to.

"You're good with children," I say, and immediately want to bite my own tongue off, because I know why she's good with children, I know about the sister, the half-erased girl with the Warden's hollow eyes, and August does not, will not, must not—

"They're less frightened than adults," she says, shrugging. "Adults flinch when I do the working. Kids just watch." She glances at me sidelong. "You don't flinch either."

"I'm an archivist. We're professionally unflappable. It's in the oath."

"What oath?"

"*Silentium, custodia, ignis* — silence, custody, fire. In that order." I say it lightly. I do not tell her that I recited the third word while feeding royal edicts into a brazier rather than burn the archive beneath them, that the fire I chose was my own career, my name, my freedom. I do not tell her that the archive I refused to burn was full of her country, her god, her ninefold court, and that every page I saved is now ash-logic in my skull, a library with one reader.

"Huh," she says. "You never told me what got you disgraced."

"No," I agree. "I didn't."

She lets it go, the way she lets everything go these days — easily, almost gratefully, as if curiosity is a muscle she's decided not to use. Four months ago she would have pressed. Four months ago she was a woman who pressed on everything, doors included. Now she is a woman with a scar on her palm and a drawer full of my useless charms and an ache she has told me about exactly once, drunk on festival cider, three weeks in: *"It's like missing a step in the dark. Every night. Same stair. Same missing step."*

I had held her elbow until she stopped swaying, and I had said nothing, and it was the hardest sentence I have never said.

* * *

In the afternoons I work. This is the other half of the discipline. A man who remembers a vanished country cannot simply sit in it; he has to build something out of the remembering or drown. So I have become, quietly, unofficially, the keeper of what the city forgot. Magic is legal again — the edicts rescinded themselves the night the Court came down, ink un-writing itself in every ledger in Vell, and no clerk questions it because there is nothing to question *with*. But the old practices are gone. The god-names are gone. There is a shrine on Lantern Row with nine empty niches that everyone admires as "charming old stonework" and no one prays at, because prayers require names and the names went into the thorns.

So I write. Under a false name, in a crabbed antiquarian hand, I am producing what future scholars will someday call *The Vell Fragments*: a reconstruction of nine gods, one of whom I describe only as "the Silent One, who held the last door." I describe the erasure. I describe the toll. I describe, in language so veiled even I can barely follow it, a woman who paid it, though I give her no name and no face, because some things should exist in records and some things should exist only in one stubborn memory.

Sometimes, writing it, I test myself. I close my eyes and recite the inventory: her voice, low and dry as a struck match. Her laugh, which happened rarely and always looked like it surprised her. The way she said *Ilen* when she was furious versus when she was afraid (identical, as it turns out). The weight of her hand on my arm at the backward river. The exact words she used when she lied to me in the maze of doors — *I need you* — and how we both knew it was a lie, and how we both let it stand, because some lies are load-bearing.

I remember all of it. That is my punishment and my post. Someone has to be the archive now. The royal college stripped me of the title, but titles are just names for things you'd do anyway.

* * *

Thursdays, she closes early. I know because I have mapped her week the way I once mapped a cataloguing system — thoroughly, obsessively, and with more tenderness than any system requires. Thursdays she closes early and walks to the harbor and stands looking at the water, and I have never once followed her there, because even a monk gets one hour of abstention from his own surveillance.

But today is Wednesday, and Wednesday means the stall, and today something is different. I see it from halfway down Copperlane: she isn't selling. She's sitting on her stool with a piece of charcoal in her hand and paper on her knee, and she's drawing, and her face has that look — the outside-the-house look, intensified.

I approach the way I approach all fragile things now: slowly, sideways, announcing myself.

"Charms against bad drawing?" I offer. "I'm a repeat customer. Volume discount."

She doesn't laugh. She turns the paper toward me, and my heart stops, restarts badly, and stops again.

It's a face. My face. Slightly wrong — the jaw too soft, the nose too dignified — but unmistakably, catastrophically mine. Drawn from memory by a woman who has no memory to draw it from.

"I keep doing this," she says. Her voice is steady but her thumb is pressing hard against the thorn-scar on her palm, worrying it like a rosary bead. "Little sketches. I throw them out, and the next day there's another one. Always the same face. Always a stranger." She looks up at me, and her eyes are doing the thing I dread most, the thing I have spent four months preventing: searching. "You have his jaw," she says. "Isn't that strange? Everyone's got a double somewhere, I suppose."

"Common as rain," I say, and my voice comes out level, and I hate myself for how good at this I've gotten.

"Do you—" She hesitates. Charcoal dust on her fingers. The scar flexing. "Do you ever meet someone and feel like you've already paid for something with them? Like there's a bill outstanding and you can't find the receipt?"

Every day. Every morning at the ninth bell. Forty-two times and counting.

"Once," I say. "A long time ago. Someone I owed a great deal."

"What happened to them?"

"They walked somewhere I couldn't follow," I say. "But they left me a job to do. So I do it."

She nods slowly, and for one terrible, luminous second I think she's going to ask the question, the real question, the one that sits under all our conversations like a keel under a boat — and then a customer calls out about warding-charms for a fishing boat, and the moment passes over us like weather, and I buy a charm against drowning even though I don't swim, and she ties it with red thread and says *may your seas disappoint you*, and I walk home with my fist closed around useless magic like it's the only thing keeping my ribs together.

That night I add the forty-second charm to the drawer. Then I take out paper, because tomorrow is Thursday, and Thursday she goes to the harbor, and I have kept every rule for four months and I am tired, tired, tired of rules written by a coward.

Tomorrow I am going to break one.

Not the big one. Not yet. But she asked me a question today — *do you ever feel like there's a bill outstanding?* — and I answered it like an archivist, and she deserved better than an index entry. Tomorrow I will stand next to her at the rail and I will tell her about a debt. I won't tell her whose. I won't say the word *door*, or *thorn*, or *toll*, or any of the words that would make the wall between us visible, because walls you can see are walls you try to climb.

But I will tell her that someone once bought a charm every day from a woman he loved, and that he considered it, all things considered, a fair price.

And if she asks his name —

Somewhere across the sleeping city, in a room above a bakery, a drawer holds forty-two small useless miracles. Somewhere far closer than that, a woman sleeps missing a step in the dark. And somewhere farther than both, past the edge of every map that admits to existing, a silence that used to be a god keeps its own kind of vigil, and I could swear — though I am an archivist, and sworn to evidence — that tonight, for the first time in four months, it sounded less like silence and more like someone clearing their throat.

Preparing, perhaps, to speak.

Preparing, perhaps, to tell her what she paid.

And God help me — help whichever of the eight remain, help the silent ninth, help anyone — I don't know yet whether I'll be brave enough to stop it.
