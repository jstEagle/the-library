## 22. The Thorn of the Ninefold Court

**August**

The dream comes again, and like always, it's a door.

Not a grand one. Not the kind you'd paint in a story, all gilt and hinges singing. Just a door, wooden, thorn-wrapped, standing alone in a field that smells like rain that fell somewhere else. In the dream I'm walking toward it. Someone is calling my name from behind me — not my name exactly, more like the shape of my name spoken by someone who had earned the right to wear it down to two syllables.

*Aug.*

And then I wake up in Vell, in my room above the charm stall, with my palm aching the way it does every morning, and no idea who was calling me.

Seven years ago — or so I'm told; my own memory before that is a hallway with the lamps out — I woke up owning this stall. Deed in my name, stock on the shelves, a scar shaped like a thorn blooming across my left palm. The magistrate's clerk swore up and down I'd been registered for years. The landlady said I paid early and tipped badly, which she seemed to consider proof of good character. Everyone agreed I'd always been here.

Everyone except the part of me that wakes at dawn reaching for someone who isn't there, hand already half-curled around a word I can't find.

I've stopped telling people about that part. Vell has enough madwomen.

This morning the ache is worse than usual, which by now I know means something. My charms work better on the bad days. The luck knots tie themselves tighter. The fever-sigils burn steadier. It's as if some part of me, the part doing all the remembering I can't, gets angry on those days and pours the anger into the work.

So: a bad day. Good for business. I went downstairs and opened the shutters and let the morning in.

* * *

**Ilen**

She doesn't know my name.

I want to say that plainly, once, here at the end of things, because everything else about this arrangement is built on indirection and I owe the truth at least one clean sentence: August Nakamura looks at me every day and sees a stranger. A slightly shabby stranger who buys charms he doesn't need and lingers too long and asks questions a stranger shouldn't ask.

She doesn't know my name, and I have decided — after three hundred and six mornings of deciding it — that she never will.

It wasn't an easy decision. It was made for me, mostly, the night I stood outside her stall in the rain and watched her laugh at something a customer's daughter said, and understood that if I told her, if I said *Ilen, you knew me, you chose me, you gave me up so the world could keep turning*, then I would be taking from her a second time. She paid the toll once. Nobody gets charged twice for the same miracle. That's not how gods work. That's barely how merchants work.

So instead I come every day. I buy a charm against headaches I don't get. A charm for safe travel, though I haven't left the city since. A charm for restful sleep, which is funny, because I sleep fine. Guilt makes an excellent mattress once you stop fighting it.

Today I bought the travel charm. Again.

"Third time this month," she said, wrapping it in brown paper with hands that moved like they'd done everything twice before learning to do it right. "You planning a trip?"

"Around the harbor," I said. "Very dangerous waters."

"You're a strange man, you know that?"

"I've been told." I have been told by archivists, by magistrates, by a woman with thorns in her palm and my whole heart in her keeping without knowing it. "Stranger than you'd believe."

Her eyes did the thing they do sometimes — caught on me like a fishhook snagging cloth, held for a heartbeat, let go. Somewhere under the forgetting, something remembers. I live on those heartbeats. They are a thin diet but I've learned to make them stretch.

"Safe travels," she said, and pressed the paper packet into my hand, and her fingers brushed mine, and she flinched — just barely, just enough that only a man who'd spent a year cataloguing his own grief would notice — and looked down at her scarred palm like it had spoken.

"Sorry," she muttered. "Old scar. Does that, sometimes."

"What happened to it?" I asked, though I knew, though I had been there when it happened, though I had screamed her name loud enough to crack a door made of living thorn.

She frowned. The frown lasted four seconds. I counted.

"Honestly?" she said. "No idea. Been there as long as I remember."

*As long as I remember.* Seven years of remembering, and nothing behind it. She says it lightly. She has practiced saying it lightly the way I have practiced not saying anything at all. Two disciplines, one wall, and we stand on opposite sides of it every morning at her stall, trading coins for charms neither of us needs.

"Careful out there," she called as I left. "Harbor's treacherous."

"It is," I said. "Believe me. I know."

* * *

**August**

The strange man came back that afternoon.

He does that — leaves, then returns the same day, like he forgot something, though it's never anything you could put in a pocket. Today he came back and stood at the edge of my awning while I served a dockworker who wanted a charm against drowning, and I felt him watching the way you feel weather change.

"Forgot my change," he said, when the dockworker moved off.

"You didn't pay yet."

"Then I definitely need to stay."

Wry. He's always wry, this one, in a way that sits oddly on him, like a coat cut for a bigger man. Like humor is a language he learned second and speaks with an accent. I should find it tiresome. Instead I find myself saving up small jokes through the day like coins, so I'll have something ready when he comes.

That should worry me. It doesn't, quite. There's a room in my chest where worry about him should live, and the room is empty, and the emptiness aches like a tooth.

"I have something for you," I heard myself say.

I hadn't known I was going to say it. But my hands were already moving, digging under the counter, past the luck knots and the fever sigils, into the drawer where I keep the charms I don't sell — the ones that come out wrong, too warm, humming faintly, made on the bad days when my palm aches and something in me works magic like it's grieving.

This one I'd made last week. A protection charm. Old pattern, older than any pattern I know, worked in silver wire and a thorn — an actual thorn, blackened, that I'd found embedded in my workbench wood one morning and couldn't pry out and eventually decided to use rather than argue with.

"For you," I said. "Free."

He looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread he suspects is poisoned.

"I can't take free charms," he said. "Bad practice. Undermines the economy."

"It's not charity. It's payment."

"For what?"

And here is the thing I cannot explain, the thing I have decided to stop trying to explain even to myself: I don't know. For seven years of buying charms he didn't need. For standing at my awning in the rain. For a voice in a dream calling my name down to two syllables. For a door in a field that I walk toward every night and never reach.

"For coming back," I said.

He took the charm. His hands weren't steady. Neither were mine, and when our fingers touched over the little bundle of wire and thorn, the ache in my palm flared so bright I nearly dropped it — and then settled, sudden and total, like a fever breaking. Like something setting down a weight it had carried a very long way.

"So you don't forget me," I said.

I meant it as a joke. Mostly. The kind of thing charm-sellers say. But it came out quiet, and it came out true, and it came out sounding like a prayer I'd been saying for years in a language I'd lost.

He went very still.

"Never," he said.

Just that. One word, and he said it the way other men say vows, the way soldiers say *hold*, the way you say a thing when you mean it twice — once for the person hearing it and once for the person who isn't there anymore to hear it.

Then he smiled, that crooked second-language smile, and paid me double for the charm I'd given him free, and walked out into the street like a man carrying something breakable through a crowd.

I looked down at my palm. The scar sat there, thorn-shaped, old, ordinary.

Except it wasn't aching anymore.

For the first time in seven years, it was warm.

* * *

**Ilen**

I stood three streets away before I opened my hand.

Inside: the charm. Silver wire, worked in a knot-pattern I recognized with a full-body jolt — the border-script of the Ninefold Court, the script Ilen the archivist had gone to prison for cataloguing, worn smooth by her hands into something new, something hers. And at its center, black and hard and unmistakable, a thorn from the ninth door. From the door she closed with herself. From the door I walked away from, screaming, holding everything she'd paid to save.

She'd kept it. All these years, some splinter of it had been in her workshop, in her wood, under her hands — and she'd worked it into a charm and given it away to the stranger she buys company from, and said *so you don't forget me*.

Gods don't weep. I know this professionally; it's in the archive, nine entries, nine confirmations. Gods don't weep.

I am not a god, and I stood in a Vell alleyway with a charm in my fist and wept like the archive had burned all over again.

Because here is what she doesn't know, what she will never know, what I have guarded for seven years like the last coal of a fire: the toll took her memories of me. Every one. The map, the marshes, the village without a name, the river running backward, the doors within doors, the argument at the threshold — hers and mine, the bitter glorious fight over who got to be the martyr — all of it, scoured clean. That was the price. That was what she fed the door while I begged her not to.

But the toll took memories. It could not touch what made them.

She doesn't remember loving me. She still loves me. I see it every morning in the space between her jokes, in the empty room where her worry lives, in the fact that after three hundred and six days she still saves up small kindnesses for a stranger who buys charms against journeys he never takes. The erasure took the story and left the truth, the way a fire takes the house and leaves the hearthstone warm.

She gave up everything she remembered of me to save the world, and the world, being what it is, gave her back a charm stall and a scar and an ache she can't name. And she stands behind that counter anyway, every day, and works her grieving magic, and hands a piece of the door she died behind to the ghost who haunts her without haunting her, and tells him not to forget her.

Never. I said it and I meant it doubly and I will mean it triply tomorrow, and the day after, until one of us runs out of days.

I put the charm on a cord and hung it around my neck, where letters used to live, where a lover's name used to be carved into skin that healed over but never forgot the shape of the knife. Some things you carry in your coat. Some things you carry in your ribs. This one goes over the heart, where it was always addressed.

Then I turned back toward her stall, because it was nearly evening, and evening is when she laughs at her own worst jokes, and I have decided to spend whatever remains of my life collecting them.

One more purchase. One more hour. Tomorrow, the same.

* * *

**August**

That night I dreamed the door again.

But something had changed. The field smelled like rain that had finally fallen here. The thorns on the door had gone soft, gone grey, gone to sleep. And the voice behind me — the two-syllable voice, the one that wore my name down gentle — it didn't call this time.

It just said, quietly, in a tone like someone laying down a burden at the end of a very long road:

*Thank you.*

I woke before dawn with wet eyes and no reason for them, and my palm warm as a banked coal, and I lay in the dark and laughed once, helplessly, at absolutely nothing.

Below the window, Vell was waking up. Magic-legal, quietly, nobody-remembers-why Vell. Somewhere a bell rang the hour. Somewhere a charm-seller's day was starting, same as every day, same as forever.

I got up. I lit the lamp. I sat at my workbench to sketch — because I do that now, most mornings, faces I don't know, in charcoal, quick, urgent, like taking dictation. I've a drawer full of them. Same face, mostly. Crooked smile. Tired clever eyes. A face like a coat cut for a bigger man.

I drew it again. Then, without deciding to, I wrote beneath it a single word:

*Come back.*

Which was strange, because he always comes back.

I looked at the word for a long time. Then I folded the page small and put it in the drawer with the others, and went down to open my shutters, and set out my charms, and waited — patient as a thorn, warm as a coal — for the strange man who buys charms every day to come and stand at my awning and be, without either of us knowing why, home.

Far away — farther than Vell, farther than maps go — something that had once been a god settled into ordinary quiet at last, whispering thanks into the dark.

And on my palm, the scar slept, shaped exactly like a thorn, holding its place in a story I almost remember, waiting — the way all true doors wait — to be opened again.
