## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Hours

The dying are the best customers. I mean that professionally, not cruelly—or mostly not cruelly. A man with three days left in him doesn't haggle. He wants to know his snowfall will go to someone who'll feel it properly, and he wants coin for his daughter, and those two desires make him wonderfully easy to deal with.

Master Odell Corvane, spice merchant, sixty-one years old, lungs full of wet rot, lay propped on pillows in a room that smelled of cinnamon and camphor and the particular sweetness of a body preparing to quit its lease. His wife had died eleven years ago. His daughter lived in Ashenford and hadn't written since spring. He'd sent for me through a boy who didn't know my face, which is how it's done.

"You're younger than I expected," he wheezed when I came through the window instead of the door. Old habit. Doors have witnesses; windows have shadows.

"I'm told it's comforting," I said. "Like being robbed by a child."

That got a laugh out of him, which cost him a coughing fit, which I waited out with the patience of someone paid by the memory rather than the hour. When he could breathe again, I pulled the stool up beside the bed and set my kit down where he could see it: six vials of grey glass, a stopper of cork and wax, and the little brass needle that did the actual work. The needle was the only part anyone ever looked at. People assume magic lives in instruments. It doesn't. It lives in hands, and hands can be hanged.

"First snowfall," he said. "The one I told you about. Vell, the year the bridge froze. I was seven."

"Seven," I agreed. "You've mentioned it four times tonight."

"Good. Means it's still there." He closed his eyes. "Take it gently, thief."

*Thief.* In Vell we don't say *mage*. Mage gets you a cell in the Ministry of Purity's basement and a trial conducted entirely in Latin nobody speaks anymore. We say *thief*, or we say nothing at all, and we keep our hands in our pockets, and when the purity criers come through the squares ringing their little bells we look bored, because boredom is the only expression that has never once been arrested.

Here is what the Ministry will tell you about magic: it is a corruption, a rot, an offense against the natural order of the Ninefold Court—except the Court itself hasn't existed for a century, so what they're really saying is that magic offends Aldric Vane, who sits in the Court's shadow wearing its robes three sizes too large and calling himself Lord Protector until such time as a better word occurs to him. Here is what I will tell you about magic: most of it is small. Most of it is this—a needle behind the ear, a pull like drawing thread through linen, and another person's afternoon in a bottle.

I touched the needle to the soft place behind Odell's left ear and thought, as always, of doors.

Not real ones. Memory has architecture if you're rude enough to go looking. There's a front hall where everything recent piles up—names of new neighbors, the taste of this morning's tea, whatever argument you had last Tuesday. Deeper in, there are rooms with dust sheets over them. Rooms people don't visit on purpose. And deeper than that—and this is the part I never say aloud, because it's the part that would get me hanged fastest—is a locked door at the very back, the one every mind keeps, the one marked with your own name.

I slipped past the hall. Past the dust sheets. I found the winter.

It opened like a book falling open at a well-read page: a courtyard in the merchant quarter, slate roofs going white from the eaves down, a boy of seven standing barefoot in the doorway with his breath smoking, watching snow come down on Vell for the first time in a generation. His mother calling him back inside. Him refusing. Snow landing on his eyelashes and him crossing his eyes to look at it and laughing—the laugh arriving before the cold did, wonder outrunning discomfort, which is the whole trick of childhood right there, the thing adults spend their coin trying to buy back.

I drew the thread. Gently. Gently.

Odell shivered under my hands. "Cold," he murmured, smiling. "Is it—did it work?"

"It worked." I sealed the vial, waxed the cork, held it up to the candlelight. Inside, the memory swam like a minnow made of weather: white and slow and impossibly quiet. "Do you want to see it one more time before it goes?"

"No." His eyes were wet. "If I watch it leave twice, I'll only remember losing it."

Which was wiser than anything I've said in years, and I told him so, and he laughed again, weaker, and asked me how much.

"The rate's the rate. Twelve crowns. Ten, since your boy found me fast."

"Give eight to Tam, the boy. He's quick and his mother's proud." He pressed the coins into my hand and then kept his hand over mine a moment too long. "Where will it go? My snow?"

"To someone rich, probably. Someone who'll pay thirty for what cost you nothing but a life." I shrugged, because lying beautifully requires a certain flatness. "Someone who never stood barefoot in a doorway. They'll feel it fresh, Master Corvane. That's worth something."

"Tell them—" He stopped. Started again. "No. Don't tell them anything. Let them think they dreamed it."

I went out the window with twelve crowns minus two and a bottle of somebody's first snow warming against my ribs like a second heart.

* * *

Let me explain the trade, since no one explained it to me and I nearly died of ignorance twice before I was fifteen.

Memories keep, if you bottle them right. Wax-sealed, kept dark, kept cool, a stolen hour stays good for decades—longer, some say, though nobody's tested it because testing requires opening one, and opening a charm pours it into whoever holds it, whole and unasked-for. That's the product. That's the entire industry: *someone else's past, decanted.* The buyer uncorks the vial, breathes it in—it takes like wine takes, all at once and then everywhere—and for the length of that memory they are not themselves. They are a seven-year-old in a snowy courtyard. They are a bride before her wedding. They are a soldier the night before the battle he survived, back when everything was still possible and terror hadn't yet curdled into the ordinary kind.

Who buys? Everyone, in a city where feeling things is dangerous. The grief-stricken buy joy. The joyless buy grief, because at least grief proves you were somewhere doing something that mattered. The ambitious buy other men's triumphs to study them. The lonely—well. The lonely buy everything.

And who sells? Thieves like me, lifting hours out of sleeping heads for coin, leaving the donors lighter by one afternoon and none the wiser. And sometimes, when the coin is honest and the donor is willing, we do it the way I did tonight: openly, tenderly, a deathbed transaction witnessed by no one, which is the only kind of legal contract left in Vell.

What the Ministry says, officially, is that memory-magic unmakes a person—that every stolen hour leaves the thief less human, that the trade is why Vell's streets have gone strange, why children are born forgetful, why the river smells of iron. What the Ministry does, unofficially, is buy. Oh, they buy. Inquisitors with deep purses and shallow faces come through the night market twice a year looking for charms of particular vintage. You can guess whose memories interest a usurper. I've sold to Hale's people exactly once, years ago—a soldier's memory of swearing an oath, which they wanted for reasons I declined to ask about—and I washed my hands for a week after.

Me, personally? I sell what sells. First snows. First kisses. Last looks at mothers. Sea voyages, harvest festivals, the specific glory of winning a footrace at nine years old. Ordinary hours made extraordinary by being somebody's only copy.

But here is the thing I keep in the floor under my bedroll, wrapped in oilcloth, behind a loose board, beneath the trapdoor of my own silence: I don't sell everything I take.

Some memories I keep. Not many. Maybe forty vials, grey glass catching the candlelight like a congregation of tiny ghosts. A fisherman's dawn on open water. A girl's memory of her father teaching her to whistle. An old woman's recollection of dancing at her own wedding, seventy years gone, her feet remembering steps her mind had lost. A dog's loyalty—I took it from a dying hound outside the tannery district, and yes, I felt monstrous, and no, I haven't regretted it, because on bad nights I uncork it just enough to feel something love me without conditions.

I keep them because I have none of my own worth keeping.

I'm told I had a childhood. There's evidence: I exist, which generally requires one. But somewhere between my seventh year and my fifteenth there is a corridor with no doors, a hallway in my head where memory should be stacked to the ceiling and isn't. Nothing painful there. Pain would be something. It's just—absence. Smooth absence, like the inside of a bottle. Whatever happened to me, it happened so thoroughly that even the wound is missing. Doctors of the mind, when I could afford them briefly, called it *excision* and went pale and stopped meeting my eyes. Excision is not forgetting. Forgetting is what time does. Excision is what someone does, with a needle, on purpose.

So I collect. Every charm in my hoard is a room I never lived in furnished with someone else's furniture, and I visit them the way other people visit family. On the worst nights—the anniversary nights, though of what I couldn't tell you, the calendar itself seems embarrassed on my behalf—I lie on my bedroll and uncork them one at a time and let other people's yesterdays wash through me like tide. I have been a child in the snow. I have been loved badly and well. I have whistled. I have danced at my own wedding.

None of it is mine. All of it is mine. I've stopped expecting those two facts to reconcile.

* * *

The night market runs in the flooded cellar of a burned-down chandlery off Rope Walk, which tells you most of what you need to know about commerce in Vell: it survives underground, in the bones of things that failed above. I got there past midnight, hood up, coins sewn into my coat lining the way my teacher Marlow taught me before Marlow taught me everything else, including how he ended up in the Purity cells, which was a lesson delivered entirely through his absence.

Fen was working the charm table tonight. Fen is fourteen, or claims to be, and runs messages and lookout duty for maybe half the pickpocket children in this quarter, and in exchange the older thieves pretend not to notice when Fen lifts from us, which is the closest thing to diplomacy our neighborhood produces.

"Corvane's?" Fen asked, meaning the job, not the man. Word travels faster than feet down here.

"Done. Twelve crowns, ten net."

"Snow?"

"First snow. Bridge-frost year."

Fen whistled low. "That'll fetch forty. The ash-lady's been asking after winter charms all month." The ash-lady was a widow in the Glass Quarter who burned through memories of her husband like a drunk burns through wages, buying the same anniversary dinner six times, each bottle a little dimmer than the last because age fades even stolen light. "You selling tonight?"

"Not that one."

"You never sell the good ones." Fen said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "You just stack 'em up under your floor like a dragon. One day somebody's going to rob you, Casper, and they won't even know what they've got."

"They'll have forty bottles of other people's Tuesdays," I said. "They'll be disappointed and rich."

But Fen wasn't wrong, and I knew it, and the knowing sat in me like swallowed river water. A dragon on a hoard. Except dragons sleep on gold because gold is *theirs*, and I slept on other people's lives because mine had been taken, and I genuinely could not have told you which of us the stories would judge more harshly.

I bought bread and a heel of hard cheese and a candle stub, and I climbed home through the rooftops—the long way, because the short way passed the Purity garrison and I've made a religion of not being seen by buildings with bells—and I sat in my single rented room above a dye-shop that stank of mordant even through the floor, and I lit my one candle, and I lifted the loose board.

Forty-one vials now. Forty-one borrowed hours, gleaming in their wax and grey glass. I arranged them the way I always do, newest at the back, and I held Odell Corvane's snowfall up to the flame and watched the little storm turn inside it, and I thought about the boy in the memory, seven years old, barefoot, laughing at his own breath.

Somewhere in Vell there was a seven-year-old version of me doing something equally ordinary and equally unrepeatable, and someone had stood behind me with a needle, and drawn the thread, and sealed it in glass, and carried it away. Maybe it was sold. Maybe it's sitting in some collector's cabinet between a saint's fingerbone and a jar of pickled eels. Maybe it was fed to one of the Ministry's machines—I've heard rumors, everyone's heard rumors, iron and bone and a humming that makes dogs howl three streets off. Maybe it's simply gone, poured out into air, dissipated, become weather.

Or maybe it's in a bottle somewhere, waiting. Maybe someday a stranger will uncork it and gasp and be me, briefly, and never know, and I will stand across the market from them holding their grandmother's lullaby, and neither of us will recognize the other, and we'll pass like ships carrying each other's cargo.

I put the snow with the others. I blew out the candle. And because I am a creature of habit the way drowning men are creatures of water, I took one vial down—the dancer, the old woman at her own wedding, my oldest and dearest theft—and I breathed her in, and for ninety seconds I was loved and young and spinning in a lamplit hall while a fiddle sawed away and my feet knew exactly where they were going.

Then it faded, the way it always fades, and I was Casper Whitlock again: twenty-six, alone, a dragon on a hoard of other people's Tuesdays, in a city that hangs my kind from the bridge-frost railings every solstice as a warning dressed up as a festival.

I was re-stoppering the empty vial when I heard the knock.

Three raps, soft, spaced wrong—not the knock of any client, not the pattern Fen uses, not the constables' double-bang. Someone had found my door, which should have been impossible, and was knocking politely, which was worse.

Through the wall I heard the dye-shop cat hiss at something on the roof, and below my window, unhurried footsteps crossed the yard and stopped.

"Casper Whitlock," said a voice I'd never heard before, warm as spiced wine and twice as likely to be poisoned, "I'm told you sell the sea. I'd like to buy all of it."
