## 2. A Client Named Wren

There are three rules to selling memories in Vell, and I break all of them regularly.

One: never sell to strangers. Two: never let them see where you keep the stock. Three: never, under any circumstances, fall in love with a client. The last one isn't really a rule so much as a survival instinct, but I keep it in the same drawer as the others, and that drawer has been getting crowded lately.

The man who came through my door on the third of Frostmonth broke rule one on arrival, rule two within the hour, and was making serious progress on rule three before the kettle had finished its second song.

"I'm told," he said, "that you sell the sea."

He stood in the doorway of my shop—which is to say, the front room of my flat, which is to say, a room containing one chair, one table, one shelf of innocuous tinned goods, and absolutely nothing that looked like inventory. He was tall in the way that suggests he's used to ducking through doors built by shorter, angrier people, with dark hair going silver at the temples and a coat that had been expensive about ten years ago and had decided to age gracefully rather than bitterly. His eyes were brown. I want to say they were warm. Warmth is a thing I know mostly secondhand, from bottled memories, so my calibration might be off.

"Depends who's telling," I said.

"A woman at the Lantern Market. She said a man there sold her her grandmother's laugh, and it fit like a glove."

Marta. Wonderful. Marta had also once tried to pay me in pickled eels and a live rooster, so her testimonials came with an asterisk.

"The sea's seasonal," I said. "Winter stock runs thin."

"That's a shame." He didn't leave. He did the opposite of leaving; he stepped inside and closed the door behind him with the soft click of someone who'd already decided the conversation wasn't over until he said it was. "I've been away from it a long time."

"People who've been away from the sea usually just walk to the sea. It's free. It's right there." I gestured vaguely eastward, toward the harbor, where the actual sea sat being free and right there for anyone with legs and forty minutes to spare.

"It's not the same," he said, and something in his voice made me stop reaching for the door.

Here's what nobody tells you about the charm trade: everyone assumes my clients are sadists or perverts or grieving widows, in roughly that order. And yes, I've sold all three their pleasures. But the bulk of my business—the quiet, steady bread-and-butter of it—is people who want to feel something they've lost the ability to feel on their own. The widow wants her wedding day. The gray clerk wants to be nine years old and fearless. The dying merchant wants snow, because he grew up in the southern provinces where snow is a rumor, and he's going to die in a city where it falls every year on people too busy to look up.

They don't want the thing itself. They want the version of themselves that could be astonished by it.

"I have three," I heard myself say, which surprised me, because I hadn't decided to say it. "Sea memories. All different vintages."

His smile arrived slowly, like weather. "I'll take all three."

"You haven't asked the price."

"I'm assuming it's outrageous."

"It's outrageous," I confirmed.

* * *

I made him wait while I fetched the stock, which is both good business and good theater. The charms live in a false-bottomed chest under my bed, wrapped in wool, each one a small glass bottle no bigger than a thumb, stoppered with wax and labeled in my own cramped hand: date, donor, sensation, and a word or two of flavor notes, because I am nothing if not a sommelier of other people's lives.

*Storm off the Gullet, donor: fisherman, 61 yrs. Salt spray, terror edging into joy, the specific blue-green of a wave about to kill you.*

*Dawn swim, donor: girl, 19 yrs. Cold shock becoming weightlessness. Gulls. The sun coming up over the water like an apology.*

*A harbor at night, donor: dockworker, 44 yrs. Lamplight on black water. The sound of ropes. Loneliness, but the comfortable kind, the kind that keeps you company.*

That last one was mine in every way except the living of it. I'd lifted it from a dockworker named Bren two years back, paid him fairly, and then kept a copy for myself against all professional ethics, because some nights the comfortable kind of loneliness was the only kind on offer, and I wanted to at least choose the vintage.

I laid the three bottles out on the table between us like cards.

He picked up the storm first. Turned it in his fingers. Didn't ask how it worked, which marked him as either knowledgeable or rude, and he didn't strike me as rude.

"How much for all three?"

"Forty crowns." Which was robbery. Twenty would have been fair. Thirty would have been fair with a margin for his coat.

He counted out forty crowns without blinking, and then, instead of pocketing the bottles and leaving like a sane person, he said: "Do you take tea with your customers?"

"Do your other charm-sellers take tea with you?"

"I don't have other charm-sellers." He said it lightly, but underneath the lightness was something that rang true, the way a coin rings when you bite it. "You're the first. In this city, anyway."

"In this city," I repeated.

"In any city," he amended, and smiled again, and I found myself putting the kettle on, which is how rule three starts: not with a leap, but with a kettle.

* * *

His name was Wren. Just Wren—he offered it like a man handing over a calling card he'd written himself, and I didn't press, because half of Vell operates on single names and the other half operates on lies.

He drank his tea the way he seemed to do everything: with more attention than strictly necessary. He held the cup in both hands. He asked questions—not the usual ones, not *is it safe*, not *does it hurt the donor*, not *are you licensed* (ha)—but strange, sideways ones.

"When you bottle one," he said, "do you taste it? Or does it stay theirs?"

"Taste it?" I laughed. "It's not soup. There's a—a contact, when I draw the memory out. A few seconds of it, raw, unfiltered. Like swallowing a mouthful of someone else's afternoon. Then it goes in the bottle and I'm clean again."

"And the copy you keep for yourself?"

My teacup paused halfway to my mouth. "I don't keep copies."

"Bren Ovid, dockworker, Pier Nine," Wren said mildly. "Harbor at night. You've worn that bottle's wax smooth. You re-stopper it after every use, which is careful of you, but wax takes fingerprints of pressure, if you know what to look for."

The room got very small and very quiet. My hand, entirely without my permission, drifted toward the knife under the table's lip.

"Easy," Wren said, palms open. "I'm not here to rob you. If I were, I'd have done it while you were fetching the stock, and I'd have taken the whole chest, and I'd be three streets gone by now."

"That's very reassuring. Would you like references?"

"I'd like to buy you dinner." He said it plainly, without the usual armor of irony men wear when they say such things, and that plainness disarmed me more thoroughly than charm ever could. "Or breakfast. Whichever comes first in your schedule of crime."

"Why?"

"Because you're interesting."

"I'm a criminal with nice handwriting."

"You're a criminal with excellent handwriting, a sommelier's palate for human experience, and—" he hesitated, and for the first time since he'd walked in, he looked briefly, genuinely uncertain, "—and you looked lonely when you thought I couldn't see you. When you set the bottles down. You looked at the harbor one like you were greeting it."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a Ministry crier was announcing the week's purges—*by order of the Office of Purity, all unregistered practitioners are reminded*—and we both ignored it the way everyone in Vell ignores everything, which is our national art form.

"I don't dine with clients," I said.

"I'm not a client anymore. I bought the sea. Now I'm just a man who owes you forty crowns' worth of conversation."

"You paid the forty crowns."

"Then I owe you the conversation for free." He leaned back, unhurried. "Come on, Casper Whitlock. One meal."

I hadn't told him my name.

* * *

We ate at a noodle stall under the aqueduct, where the steam hid us from casual eyes and the proprietor was deaf in the way that makes him excellent at discretion. Wren talked. That's the thing I keep circling back to, days later: he talked, and he listened, and the ratio was nearly perfect, and I have been alone so long that a balanced conversation felt like a drug.

He'd traveled. He mentioned ports the way other people mention old friends—Sarrow, the Pearl Coast, somewhere north whose name he swallowed. He'd been a sailor, maybe, or something adjacent to sailing. He knew tides. He knew which wines went with fish and, oddly, which went with grief. ("Most people think anything sweet," he said. "It's not. Grief wants something dry. Sweetness insults it.")

And he asked me things. Not interrogations—questions with soft edges, questions that gave me room to lie if I wanted, and I took that room, and he let me take it, which was somehow worse than catching me.

"Where did you learn the craft?" he asked, over the noodles.

"Apprenticeship," I lied smoothly.

"To whom?"

"A dead man."

"Convenient."

"He'd have agreed. He was very committed to being dead."

Wren laughed—a real one, startled out of him—and for a moment his whole face changed, lost its careful architecture, and I thought: *there you are.* Whoever he actually was, he lived behind that laugh, and he didn't let him out often.

But here is what I noticed, because noticing is my trade the way bottling is: he watched my hands.

Not the way people watch hands at dinner—tracking the chopsticks, the gesture, the spill risk. He watched them the way I watch a mark's pockets. Every time I moved them—reaching for the tea, turning the bottle of the dawn-swim charm over between courses (he'd asked to see it again), tearing bread—he tracked the motion with a focus that had nothing to do with the motion itself. He was reading my technique. The way I hold a bottle. The angle of my fingers on wax. Once, when I gestured while telling a story about a client who'd tried to pay me in chickens, his eyes flicked to the webbing between my thumb and forefinger, where a memory-thief's tell lives—the faint silvery scarring that drawing magic leaves, like frostbite in reverse—and stayed there a beat too long before returning politely to my face.

Charm-sellers aren't rare. Vell has perhaps a dozen operating below the Ministry's radar, and every one of them bears those marks, and none of them are worth studying like scripture.

So why was I?

"Tell me about the sea," I said, to change the subject from myself, because he'd gotten too close to it. "You bought three memories of it. Why does a man who clearly knows the real thing need bottled copies?"

He was quiet for a moment. The aqueduct groaned overhead, carrying its eternal cargo of water across the city.

"Because the last time I saw it," he said finally, "I was on a ship that was burning, and I was watching a man drown, and I couldn't reach him." He turned his cup. "I've seen the sea every day since, from piers, from windows. It doesn't help. The sea isn't the problem. The problem is the version of me that existed on it." He looked up. "You sell people back to themselves. I read that somewhere, about your work, and I thought—it sounded like something I needed."

*You sell people back to themselves.* I turned the phrase over like a coin, looking for the false metal in it. There wasn't any. There should have been. Everything in Vell has false metal in it.

"One meal," I said, standing, because staying another minute felt dangerous in a way I couldn't afford to examine. "As negotiated."

"That was the negotiation?"

"That was the entire contract. Verbal. Binding. Witnessed by a deaf noodle vendor."

He rose too, and for a moment we stood close enough that I caught whatever he smelled like—salt, faintly, though that might have been my imagination supplying props—and he said, quietly: "Same time next week? I hear winter stock runs thin, but I'm patient. I can wait for spring."

"There won't be a next week."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, "but there will be, won't there."

And the infuriating thing—the thing that kept me awake that night staring at my ceiling while the Ministry criers sang their purge-lullabies—is that he was right.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I did what I always do when a stranger gets under my skin: I worked. I inventoried the chest, re-wrapped the charms, updated the ledger in my excellent criminal handwriting. And around the fourth bell past midnight, I found myself doing something I hadn't done in six years of trade.

I checked my own tell.

Sat by the candle, turned my hand over, and looked at the silver webbing between thumb and forefinger. It had spread, I realized. Slowly, the way rot spreads, or ivy. When I started, it was a fleck no bigger than a grain of rice. Now it reached almost to the first knuckle, branching like frost on glass, like lightning deciding where to land.

Every thief pays the toll. I'd known that from the start. What I'd never asked—what I'd carefully, professionally never wondered—was what happens at the end of the road. Where the marks go when they finish spreading. Whether there's a final payment waiting for a man who has spent his whole life carrying other people's pasts in place of his own.

Because that was the part I'd never said aloud, not to Marta, not to the pickpockets, not to anyone: I don't steal memories because it pays well. It pays adequately. I steal them because my own are gone—all of them, before the age of eight, a blank so complete that even the shape of the loss has worn smooth. No face for a mother. No name for a street. Nothing. Whatever the old court's purges burned in the Year of Ash, they burned me down to the wick, and everything I am now is furniture I carried in later.

Bren's harbor. The girl's dawn swim. Forty years of borrowed hours, shelved under my bed, keeping me company.

And now a stranger with salt in his hair and a drowned man in his history, who knew my name before I gave it, who watched my hands like a student, who said *you sell people back to themselves* like he'd rehearsed it—

—who had he read? Who talks about my work in words that neat?

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to Vell breathe, and I made myself a promise shaped exactly like a bad idea: tomorrow, I was going to find out who Wren was. Even if it meant asking questions in places where questions get people disappeared.

Even if it meant the archive. Even if it meant the Ministry itself.

Rule four, which I invented that night: never go looking for a stranger's truth, because yours is usually buried underneath it, and it's been buried there for a reason.

I fell asleep at dawn, dreaming—as I always dreamed, those weeks—of water, and lamplight on black waves, and a loneliness so comfortable it felt almost like being held.
