## 4. Inquisitor's Kindness

They came for me on a Tuesday, which I have always felt was an insult. Arrests should happen in the dead of night, with boots and broken doors and at least one dramatic shout of a name. Instead, two Ministry clerks arrived at my shop in broad daylight with a warrant written in beautiful copperplate and an apology so sincere I nearly offered them tea.

"The Inquisitor will see you now," the taller one said, as though I were expected at a dinner party.

"And if I decline?"

He smiled the smile of a man who has never once been declined. "Then the Inquisitor will see you anyway, but the walk will be less pleasant."

I locked up my shop—habit, mostly, though who would steal from a memory-thief's stock I couldn't say—and went with them through streets that smelled of coal smoke and rain that hadn't fallen yet. People looked away as I passed. That's Vell for you. Nobody sees anything, nobody knows anybody, and everyone is absolutely certain which neighbors deserve watching.

I want it noted that I considered running. I had three exits from my own shop memorized, two of them involving roofs, and a purse of emergency coin sewn into my coat lining since the day I'd learned what the inside of a Ministry cell looked like from a client's memory—a smuggler named Oskar who'd sold me six hours of interrogation and charged me double because, he said, "you'll want to know what's coming someday." He'd been right. Oskar's memories had taught me how to be terrified properly, with technique.

But running confirms guilt faster than any confession, and besides, Seraphine Hale does not send clerks. If Hale wanted me, there would have been no Tuesday visit at all. There would simply have been a morning when I didn't wake up.

So I walked.

* * *

The Ministry of Purity kept its interrogation rooms on the fourth floor, above the archives, below the bell tower—the architecture of the whole building arranged, I suspected, to remind visitors that they existed somewhere between record and judgment.

The room itself was almost gentle. Two chairs, a table, a window with actual daylight coming through it. A pot of something steaming on a brazier. No chains bolted to the floor, no drains in the center of the tiles. I'd seen the other kind of room through Oskar's eyes, and this wasn't it, which meant either I was being treated as innocent or as important, and I genuinely could not decide which frightened me more.

Inquisitor Seraphine Hale sat by the window reading a ledger, and she did not look up when I entered. She let me stand there for exactly long enough to notice that she was letting me stand there, then closed the book and smiled.

"Master Whitlock. Thank you for coming."

"You make it sound like I had a choice."

"There's always a choice." She gestured to the chair across from her. "It's just that most of them are worse than the others. Sit, please. The tea is chamomile. I'm told it helps with nerves."

"I'm not nervous."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "I noticed."

I sat. Up close, Hale was smaller than her reputation suggested, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that would be called kind in a portrait and merciless in person. Her hands were folded on the ledger like a woman at prayer, or a spider at rest, and I confess I couldn't tell which image comforted me less.

"You know," she said, pouring two cups, "in ten years of this work, I've found that the ones who aren't nervous are always the interesting ones. Nervous people are merely guilty. Calm people are *practiced*."

"Or innocent."

"Or innocent," she said, and handed me a cup as though the word were a gift she was humoring me with. "Shall we begin?"

"Begin what? I haven't been told why I'm here."

"No?" She opened the ledger again, turned it toward herself, and ran one finger down a column. "Three weeks ago, a licensed charm-seller named Dov Marren died of a lung fever in the Lantern District. Before he died, he sold his shop inventory—including several charms of unlicensed provenance—to a dealer who, according to the dealer's testimony, paid in Ministry-minted coin and asked unusually specific questions about where the charms came from. That dealer describes a young man. Clever hands. Careful eyes. A habit of looking at people's temples when he thinks they won't notice." She looked up. "You're staring at my temple right now, Master Whitlock."

I made myself look at her nose instead. "I have a squint. Bad lighting."

"Mm. And three days after Marren's death, someone accessed the Ministry archive. Night of the nineteenth. The lock wasn't forced, the wards weren't tripped, and nothing was taken—except that one shelf was disturbed. Shelf nine. Restricted holdings." She let that sit between us like a stone dropped in still water. "You see my difficulty. Either we have a burglar of impossible skill wandering the city, or we have a memory-worker good enough to lift the archive key out of a clerk's head without him ever knowing it was gone."

"That's quite a story," I said. "Do you tell it to all your suspects?"

"All my suspects are in cells," Hale said. "You get the chair by the window."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Seraphine Hale, before that morning: everything the trade knew, which was to say, everything worth fearing. She had joined the Ministry of Purity eleven years ago, after the Purge Edicts made charm-work a hanging offense, and she had risen faster than anyone in its history—not because she was cruel, but because she was *patient*. Other inquisitors broke down doors. Hale knocked, smiled, remembered your mother's name, and came back six months later when you'd forgotten she'd ever visited. She had dismantled the Glasswright Ring in a single winter. She had turned the Ferryman Syndicate against itself without arresting a single member until every one of them was already hers.

And there was one more thing the trade whispered about Hale, quietly, in the way people whisper about things they don't fully believe: that she wasn't hunting charms at all. That somewhere in her ledgers there was a single name she'd been chasing for a decade, and every arrest, every raid, every polite Tuesday summons was just another stitch in a net woven around one particular knot.

I had always assumed the whispers were embellishment. Inquisitors love their legends; it does half their work for them.

But when she looked at me across that little table, with the steam rising between us and the daylight making her seem almost maternal, I thought: *oh*. *Oh, no.* It's true. It was never about the charms.

"So," I said lightly, "shelf nine. What was on it? Pornography? Heresy? The Minister's gambling debts?"

"A book."

"A book."

"Written a hundred years ago, give or take. Illuminated. Prophetic, if you believe that sort of thing." She sipped her tea and watched me over the rim. "It was removed from public circulation long ago, on the grounds that prophecy is a disease. It spreads. It makes people do foolish things—abandon families, start wars, follow children claiming to be vessels of destiny. The Ministry prefers its citizens diseased with nothing more exotic than ambition."

"And you think your impossible burglar came for this book."

"I think," Hale said, "that whoever took the key from Clerk Endrin's mind also left something behind. A small gift, as it were. A memory of a rainy evening and a warm meal, planted where the theft should have been, so that when Endrin counted his hours that week, everything balanced." She set down her cup. "That is *extraordinary* work, Master Whitlock. Do you know how many practitioners in Vell could seed a false memory clean enough to survive an audit? I can count them on fewer fingers than I have. It's artistry."

"It sounds like you admire them."

"I admire the work. The worker, I intend to find." She tilted her head. "Tell me about yourself, Master Whitlock. Where were you on the night of the nineteenth?"

"Asleep. Above my shop. Alone, if you must know, which feels like an invasion all on its own."

"Alone." She wrote something in the ledger. She wrote very small, I noticed, and very fast, and I wondered how much of this conversation was going onto that page in shorthand I'd never read. "No one to confirm it."

"My insomnia confirms it. Ask it anytime."

A flicker at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but acknowledging receipt of one. "You came to Vell... when? Your papers say eight years ago, but papers lie. Papers are the first thing people steal."

"They're also the first thing people lose," I said. "Fire, flood, bad landlords. My history is my own business, Inquisitor. I sell charms, licensed ones, from a licensed shop, and I pay my taxes, and until this morning the Ministry had no complaint against me."

"None whatsoever. You're entirely correct." She turned another page, unhurried. "Which is why this isn't an arrest. This is a conversation. Colleagues in the trade talk to me all the time—it keeps everyone honest. For instance." Another page. "Dov Marren's inventory included a charm of first snowfall, seen from a merchant's window. Unusually vivid. Unusually *deep*—the kind of extraction that takes years of practice and leaves no scar on the donor. The buyer of that charm paid triple rate and asked whether the seller had 'more from the same source.'" She looked up. "Does that sound like anyone you know?"

The snowfall charm. The dying merchant whose last winter I'd lifted while his daughter held his hand downstairs, believing I was a physician's assistant. I had bottled it, sold it within the week, and thought no more about it except sometimes at night, when the edges of other people's lives leaked into mine and I fell asleep watching snow I'd never actually stood in.

"Charm-buyers ask questions," I said. "It's how they avoid buying garbage."

"Triple rate," Hale repeated softly. "For a snowfall."

"People are sentimental about weather."

"Yes," she said, and for a moment—just a moment—something moved behind her eyes that was not the inquisitor at all. Something tired, and old, and grieving. "Yes, they are. My father used to carry me on his shoulders through the first snow every year so I could be the one to break its surface. He died when I was nineteen. I have bought four charms of snowfall since, from licensed dealers, and none of them were right, because they weren't *mine*, and I knew it, and I drank them anyway." She straightened her ledger. "So understand me, Master Whitlock. I am not a zealot. I don't hate the gift. I hate what the hungry do with it—what it makes of the people who carry it, and the people it's taken from."

"That's very moving," I said. "Am I under suspicion of sentimentality?"

"You're under suspicion," Hale said, "of being the Thorn."

* * *

The word landed in the quiet room like a dropped glass.

I did not flinch. I want that recorded too, wherever records go—I did not flinch, even though every hair on my arms stood up and the chamomile turned to ash on my tongue. Because I had seen that word only once before, in an illumination on a forbidden page, wearing my own face, and I had spent three weeks telling myself it was coincidence. Painters guess. Prophecies blur. A century-old illustration of some faceless figure could resemble anyone, the way clouds resemble ships.

Hale watched me not-flinch with the attention of a naturalist watching a moth that might be a leaf.

"The Thorn," she repeated, when I said nothing. "You've heard the term."

"It's in a nursery rhyme," I said. "'Thorn and crown and ninefold fall.' Children skip rope to it. Hardly evidence of sedition."

"It's in a great many things, if you know how to listen. A rhyme. A marginal note in a shipping manifest from sixty years ago. A fragment of a letter burned in the Purge, of which half a line survives: '*the Thorn unmade, and the ninefold court with it.*'" She recited them the way other people recite scripture—or shopping lists. Ten years of collecting, laid flat on the table like specimens pinned to cork. "Every culture leaves crumbs of it. The old court spent its last decades searching for the person the prophecies described, and the usurper Vane has spent his entire reign doing the same, and the free cities whisper about it in taverns, and through all of it, the Thorn remains—" she spread her hands, "—a rumor with excellent posture."

"And you've hunted this rumor for ten years."

"I've *listened* for ten years. Hunting is crude. Listening finds what hunting scares off." She leaned forward slightly. "The prophecies agree on very little, Master Whitlock, but they agree on this much: the Thorn is not a warrior. Not a mage of the old blood. Not anyone the courts would ever have chosen. The Thorn is a vessel—someone who carries what others have lost. Someone full to the brim with borrowed life." Her voice stayed gentle, terribly gentle, the voice you'd use on a sparrow you intended to keep. "Someone, perhaps, who hoards memories they never lived, because their own childhood was erased."

The room went very still. Somewhere below us, in the archives, pages were turning; somewhere above, the bell tower kept its silence; and I sat in the middle of it with a cup of chamomile and a face that I prayed was made of stone.

Because she couldn't know that. She *couldn't*. No record of my childhood existed—I had checked, exhaustively, over years, bribing clerks and reading dead men's minds, and every trail ended in the same blank wall, the same eight missing years, the same white space where a boy named Casper Whitlock should have been learning to walk and talk and fear the dark. I had told no one. Wren knew pieces of it, and I trusted Wren the way you trust a knife—you trust it not because it loves you but because it cuts true—

"—Master Whitlock." Hale's voice, mild as milk. "You've gone somewhere."

"Just wondering," I said, and heard my own voice come out steady, gods bless the thief's craft, "why the Ministry's chief inquisitor spends her Tuesday mornings discussing nursery rhymes with a charm-shop owner. If you suspect me of being a prophesied figure, shouldn't I be in irons? Or worshipped? I'm flexible."

"If I suspected you," Hale said, "you would already know. I don't suspect. Suspecting is for people who need warrants." She rose, smoothed her coat, and gathered her ledger, and the interview—or whatever it had been—was visibly ending, which confused me more than anything else that morning. "I'm letting you go, Master Whitlock. No charges. No file. As far as the Ministry is concerned, this conversation never happened."

"Why?"

"Because nets are not thrown at fish that might be fish. They're thrown at shapes in the water." She walked me to the door herself, hand light between my shoulder blades, courteous as a hostess. "Go home. Sell your charms. Sleep badly, alone, above your shop. And Master Whitlock—if you should happen to hear anything, anywhere, about anyone carrying what isn't theirs..." At the threshold she paused, and her kindness was the worst thing I had ever seen. "My door is always open. I'd rather protect the Thorn than deliver it to men like Vane. Whatever it turns out to be. Whoever."

* * *

They let me out through the front of the building, past clerks who never looked up, into a street gone silver with the promised rain finally arriving. I stood on the Ministry steps and let it hit my face, cold and honest, and I thought about what Oskar's memories had taught me about the moment after an interrogation ends.

Oskar had put it plainly, in the flat tone of a man remembering pain: *"The ones who scare you are nothing. The ones who are kind to you—they've already decided. Kindness means they don't need to break you. It means they're following you home."*

I didn't go straight back to the shop. I doubled twice through the market, bought onions I didn't need, watched reflections in pewter and window-glass, and saw no tail—which proved nothing, because Hale wouldn't use tails. Hale would use time. She'd shown me her ledger; she'd shown me her decade of listening; she had, with exquisite courtesy, informed me that the net was already woven and that I was standing in the middle of it being politely invited to walk deeper.

She knew about the archive. She knew about the snowfall charm. She had said the word *vessel* while looking directly at me, and she had mentioned my erased childhood—my erased childhood—as easily as commenting on the weather, which meant either she was guessing with the confidence of a prophet herself, or someone had talked.

And there was only one person alive who knew enough to talk.

I stood in the rain outside a chandler's shop, holding a bag of onions, and made myself think it through like a professional instead of like a fool in love. Wren had appeared in my life eight months ago with sea-memory money and an appetite for my company that had seemed, at the time, like the first luck of my entire existence. He watched my hands. He asked about my techniques. He lingered. And I had let him in—into the shop, into my bed, into the small guarded country of my habits—because loneliness is a door that opens from the inside.

*If he talked,* I thought, *then everything is already lost, and there's no point running.*

*And if he didn't,* I thought, *then Hale knows things no informant could tell her, which is worse.*

Either way, the net was closing, and there was one move left that made any sense at all. Burn the stock. Clear the ledgers. Get the pickpockets paid and scattered before the Ministry's courtesy curdled into something with teeth.

I turned toward the shop through the thinning rain, moving fast now, and that was when I saw him—Wren, across the street, under the chandler's awning, watching me with an expression I had never seen on him before and recognized instantly anyway, because I'd worn it myself for three weeks.

Fear.

Wren was afraid, and he was afraid *of the Ministry steps I'd just descended*, which meant he knew exactly what had happened inside, which meant either he'd been waiting for me all along—

or he'd been sent to collect me.

He raised one hand, half-wave, half-warning, and mouthed words the rain swallowed whole.

*We need to talk,* I was almost certain he'd said.

Almost certain was a currency I could no longer afford.
