## 5. Seven Years of Small Charms

The charms went into the brazier one at a time, because I couldn't bring myself to do it any other way.

Seven years of small thefts, each one stoppered in a thumb of glass and sealed with wax the color of old honey. A grandmother's kitchen, steam on a window. The smell of a harbor at dawn. Somebody's wedding day, borrowed from the bride herself, who'd wanted to forget it and then, three weeks later, came back wanting it again. I'd sold her a copy. She'd cried in my shop and paid me double and told me I was an angel, which was funny, because in Vell angels are illegal too.

I fed them to the fire and watched the little glass bubbles soften and pop, releasing their cargo into the room in bursts—cinnamon, salt air, someone's dead mother laughing—and by the time I reached the bottom of the crate my shop smelled like every good day anyone had ever had, all of them leaving at once.

"That's a waste," said Pip from the doorway.

Pip is eleven, or claims to be, or has decided to be. Pickpockets in Vell pick their own ages the way they pick pockets: whatever gets them through the door. She stood there with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and her chin up in that way she does when she's decided not to be hurt about something before anyone can hurt her about it.

"It's inventory," I said. "It's going bad."

"Charms don't go bad."

"These ones did." I held up the last bottle—a memory of first snowfall, the merchant's, the one that started everything—and turned it in the light. "Some things spoil from the inside."

She wasn't buying it. Eleven-year-olds never do. But she climbed onto my counter anyway, the way she always did, heels drumming against the wood, and watched me work, and for a while neither of us said anything, and the fire popped out somebody else's happiness into the cold.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about running a charm-stall in Vell: nobody knows you exist, and you spend your whole life making sure of it.

Magic's been outlawed since before my parents were born—since before *their* parents were born, if I'd had any, which is its own kind of joke. The Ministry of Purity burns hedge-witches in Copper Square on alternating Thursdays. They call it sanitation. So the trade that survives is the small trade, the deniable trade, the trade of things nobody can prove were ever taken. Memories don't leave bruises. A man who sells his grief doesn't report it stolen; he thanks you for taking it.

My clients came in through the back of a candlemaker's shop, down a stairs that smelled of tallow and dust, into a room where I sat behind a table like a doctor who diagnosed nothing. They gave me their worst hours and I bottled them, or their best hours, when they could afford those, and I paid in coin or took payment in silence, whichever kept us both alive. And sometimes—more often than I ever admitted—I kept copies. Not to sell. To live in. Because my own childhood was a locked room with no furniture in it, erased so thoroughly that even the shape of the erasure had been smoothed away, and a person can't walk around empty forever without starting to fill herself with other people's weather.

That's what the ledger called it, once, in a margin I probably wasn't supposed to read: *vessel*. I'd laughed at the word. It seemed dramatic. Now there was a book under the Ministry with my own face painted in it, captioned like a saint or a weapon, and the bells hadn't stopped ringing in my head since.

So: burn the stock. Burn the copies. Burn the ledgers with names in them, because Seraphine Hale reads ledgers the way priests read scripture, patiently, looking for the parts where everyone is damned.

"You're leaving," Pip said. Not a question. She'd seen me pack a go-bag before; she just hadn't seen me pack everything.

"I'm visiting family," I lied.

"In the marshes?"

"The marshes have excellent family."

"Cas." She used the name only she and the others used, the short version, the one that meant she was serious. "If the inquisitors come here asking after you—"

"They won't find anything. That's rather the point of tonight."

"And if they come asking after *us*?"

I stopped feeding the fire.

This is the part nobody warns you about, when you build a life out of invisibility: you collect people in the dark. You take in the orphans the city grinds up and spits out—the ones who learned to lift purses before they learned letters—and you teach them which rings to steal and which rings to leave alone, and you tell yourself it's business, apprenticeship, a fair exchange of skills for shelter. And then one night you're burning seven years of stolen joy in a tin brazier and a child asks you if the men with white gloves will come for *her*, and you understand that invisible lives cast visible shadows, and you've been standing in front of theirs this whole time.

"They won't," I said. "And if they do, you never met me. You stole candles from the candlemaker and got lost. You cried. You're good at crying."

"I hate you," she said, which in Pip's language means several things, none of which are hate.

* * *

They came at dusk, all four of them, because word travels fast among street children when the word is *Cas is doing something stupid*.

Sparrow arrived first, sixteen and already built like a man who'd survived being fifteen in Vell, with a knife he polished more than he used. Then the twins—not twins, but they'd claimed each other so long ago that biology had given up arguing—Nock and Ferret, fourteen and thirteen respectively, arriving together as always, moving together as always, finishing each other's sentences as always, which was insufferable and I would miss it more than I can say. And last, late as always, came Mouse, who was nine and could open any lock in the city with two hairpins and a smile, and who brought me, because he was Mouse, a stolen orange.

"For the road," he said, solemn as a funeral. "You get scurvy on roads. Probably."

"There hasn't been scurvy in Vell in fifty years."

"So it's working. Keep eating oranges."

I made tea because there was nothing left to do with my hands that wouldn't shake. The shop was stripped bare—shelves empty, floor swept, the false wall behind the candle rack open to show nothing at all where a fortune in bottled hours used to sit. The brazier still smoked. It smelled like a hundred people's best days, and none of mine.

"You could come with me," I said, and hated myself for saying it, because it was selfish, because what I actually meant was *don't make me be alone in this*, and offering that to children is the kind of thing that should get a person arrested by someone.

"Nope," said Sparrow.

"Definitely not," said Nock.

"We'd slow you down," said Ferret, which was the honest version.

"We've got routes," said Sparrow. "We've got the candle lady. We've got—" he gestured at the bare shelves, at the smoke, at me, "—we've got practice disappearing. You taught us. Whole city full of people who don't see street kids unless they're paying for something. We'll be ghosts."

"Ghosts with routes," said Nock.

"Ghosts with an orange surplus," said Mouse.

Pip didn't say anything. She sat on the counter with her knees drawn up and looked at me with an expression I recognized because I wore it myself, most days, in the privacy of my own skull: the look of someone cataloguing exactly how much a thing is going to hurt, in advance, so it can't surprise them later.

"Pip," I said.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the speech. The speech where you tell me I'm clever and I'll be fine and this isn't goodbye, it's *see you later*. Everyone gives that speech right before they die in a story."

"This isn't a story."

"Everything's a story. That's what you told me when you taught me to read. You said stories are just true things wearing costumes." She hopped off the counter and dug in her pocket and held something out to me. A charm. One of mine—or one like mine, glass and wax, badly sealed, amateur work. "I made it. Don't laugh."

"What's in it?"

"Nothing yet. Empty. I figured—you're always giving people bottles of stuff. Figured you might want one that's yours. For putting something in. Later. When you find something worth keeping."

I took it. My hands, traitors to the end, shook slightly, and she saw, and pretended not to, which is the highest form of kindness a street kid knows.

It's an empty bottle, is the thing. It's a piece of glass the size of my thumbnail, sealed with crooked wax, worth nothing, made by a child who steals because the alternative is starving. And I stood there holding it while four orphans and one almost-orphan watched me with the terrible patience of people who have already been abandoned by everyone they ever counted on and have developed a professional interest in how it's done properly.

So I did it properly.

I hugged them. All of them, individually, which they endured with varying degrees of squirming—Mouse clung, Ferret patted my back twice like we were closing a business deal, Sparrow let it happen with his arms at his sides and then, at the last second, squeezed hard enough to crack a rib, and let go before I could comment. Pip hugged me like she was angry about it, fists clenched against my spine.

"If you die," she said into my shoulder, "I'm stealing your ghost. I know a guy."

"Of course you do."

"He owes me."

* * *

They left one at a time, staggered, the way I taught them—never move as a group, groups draw eyes—and each departure took a piece of the shop with it until the room was just a room, just tallow-smell and ash and me.

Last was Pip. She stopped at the door.

"The bells," she said. "In Copper Square. When they burn someone. I used to think the sound was the worst thing in the world." She shrugged. "It's not. The worst thing is quiet after. When everyone just... goes back to whatever they were doing." She looked at me. "Don't be quiet after, Cas. Wherever you go. Make noise. Be loud. Be a problem."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with an empty bottle and a dead fire and the specific silence of a life ending.

* * *

I packed the last of it by lamplight: a knife, a change of clothes, coin sewn into the hem where coins belong, and Pip's little bottle wrapped in cloth and tucked against my ribs where it wouldn't break. I took the orange. I ate it on the stairs, standing up, because Mouse was right about roads, probably.

At the bottom of the candlemaker's stairs, in the last doorway before the street, I stopped and did the thing I'd been avoiding all night. I reached into the place where I keep the memories that aren't for sale—my private hoard, the collection of other people's lives I'd stolen not to sell but to survive on—and I took stock.

A winter morning, someone's. A mother singing, someone's. First snowfall, a dying merchant's. Seven years of borrowed hours, and not one hour my own.

The plan had been simple: run, hide, stay invisible. That was the whole strategy. It had worked beautifully for twenty-six years.

But I'd read the prophecy. I knew what was painted in that forbidden book, and I knew—in the way you know a debt is coming due—that invisibility was over. Whatever the Thorn was, whatever the Thorn *did*, the men with white gloves were going to find it whether I ran or not, and the only question left was whether I'd be found crouching in a ditch or standing somewhere with my chin up.

Make noise, Pip said. Be a problem.

There was a boat leaving on the morning tide. I'd need to be at the docks before dawn, which meant walking across half of Vell in the dark, past Copper Square, past the Ministry, through the teeth of the machine that wanted me.

I pulled my hood up and stepped out into the street.

The bells began to ring.
