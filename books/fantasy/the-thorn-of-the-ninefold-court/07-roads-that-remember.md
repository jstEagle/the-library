## 7. Roads That Remember

The marshes began where the road gave up pretending.

One moment we were on the Salt Road proper—ruts and gravel and the occasional shrine to a god nobody in Vell had worshipped since before I was born—and the next the ground went soft underfoot and swallowed the path whole, like it had been waiting years for an excuse.

"Charming," I said.

"It's called the Mirenweald," Wren said. "It's not charming. It's strategic."

"Everything you do is strategic."

He had the grace not to argue. That was new for him. Three days out of Vell, with my stock burned and my pickpockets scattered to safer hands and Seraphine Hale's face printed on every gate-post from here to the coast, he'd stopped performing. Mostly. The charm still surfaced now and then, reflexive as blinking, but underneath it there was something quieter and more watchful that I liked considerably less.

I hadn't forgiven him. Let's be clear about that. Chapter six of my life, if anyone were keeping ledgers: *the man I let into my bed turned out to have been sent.* He'd confessed at the docks with salt wind whipping his confession sideways, and I'd nearly opened him hip to ribs with the little knife I keep for clients who get grabby. Instead I took his hand. I'm still not entirely sure why. Fear, probably. The devil you've already kissed being preferable to the devil alone.

So we walked into the Mirenweald together, two liars sharing a fire, and the marsh did what marshes do: it remembered everything.

* * *

The dreams started the second night.

I should explain something about my trade, because it matters here. What I take isn't a copy. When I lift a memory from someone—a first snowfall, a mother's voice, the particular terror of a burning building—I don't leave them their original and pocket a duplicate like some street magician palming coins. I take the thing itself. It moves. It lives in me until I bottle it, and even after bottling, a residue stays behind, a watermark, the way wine stains a glass no scrubbing fully lifts.

I'd always thought of the residue as harmless. Ghost-smells. Faint music through a wall.

The marsh disagreed.

That night I dreamed of a war. Not any war I'd lived—the only war I'd lived was the slow one of surviving Vell, all elbows and ledger-books—but a war centuries old, fought in colors that haven't existed since. I stood on a ridge—I wasn't *me*, exactly; I was tall and armored and certain in a way I have never once been—and below me the Ninefold Court burned, its nine spires going down one by one like candles snuffed by a deliberate breath. And I knew, with the dreamer's terrible fluency, that this was the last stand of the old kings, and that the man beside me on the ridge wore iron worked with thorn-patterns, and that we had come to finish the job.

I woke with mud in my mouth and Wren's hand on my shoulder and my own voice hoarse from shouting words in a language I didn't know and somehow did.

"You were speaking Old Court Tongue," Wren said. His face was very carefully blank, which is what his face does instead of showing alarm.

"I don't speak Old Court Tongue."

"No," he said. "You really shouldn't."

* * *

By the fourth day I'd stopped sleeping willingly.

It wasn't just the war dream. The leaks came in fragments, unbidden, stitched to nothing. I'd be wading through reeds waist-high and suddenly taste siege-bread gone green with mold. I'd hear a woman singing a lullaby in a dialect dead four hundred years. Once, crossing a black channel on a fallen willow, I knew—absolutely, bodily *knew*—that the water below hid a drowned village, and when I said so, Wren looked at me too long and then made us detour three miles around rather than confirm or deny.

"The residue," I said that evening, over a fire built small because smoke travels. "From the charms. It's coming loose."

"That's not how the trade works."

"You're an expert now?"

"I'm an agent of the old court who spent two years learning everything there is to know about memory-thieves." He fed a stick to the flames. "Including you. Especially you."

"There's a sentence to warm a person."

"Casper." He used the tone he saved for things he meant, which was rare enough that I shut up. "The charms you lifted weren't ordinary memories. You know that. Half your stock came from people who'd touched the old magic—soldiers' descendants, archive clerks, that dying merchant whose family kept letters from the Court's fall. The residue in you isn't inert. Out here—" he gestured at the dark water, the reeds, the sky bruising toward storm, "—out here, the land remembers too. The Mirenweald was the battlefield. Memory calls to memory. Yours are bleeding."

"Bleeding where?"

He looked at me the way you look at someone standing on ice you can hear cracking.

"Out," he said.

* * *

Here is what I learned about leaking, in the nights that followed, and I record it plainly because someday—if there is a someday after all this—someone may need it:

A stolen memory doesn't leak like water. It leaks like blood into bathwater, blooming outward, taking you with it. The anchor goes first. You forget which century holds your body. Then your name goes soft at the edges. In the war-dreams I stopped watching the tall armored man on the ridge and started *being* him, and each time it got harder to claw back, and each time I surfaced gasping into the present with less certainty that the present was the real one.

The third night, I didn't come back on my own.

I was in the throne room of the Ninefold Court, before the fall, and a king with a face like winter was pressing something into my hands—no, into *his* hands, the tall man's—and saying, *keep it hidden, keep it hidden, the Thorn must sleep until the court deserves it again,* and I could feel the weight of the thing, cold and alive—

Wren slapped me. Open-palmed, across the face, hard enough to ring.

I came up out of the dream like a drowning man breaching, and found myself kneeling in cold marshwater ten feet from our fire, arms outstretched toward the dark as if reaching for something, mud to my elbows, sobbing without knowing why. Wren crouched in front of me, both hands on my jaw now, forcing my eyes to his.

"Say your name."

"What?"

"Your name. Say it."

"Casper Whitlock."

"Where are you?"

"The Mirenweald."

"What year?"

I hesitated. He saw me hesitate. Something in his face cracked a little, and he shook me—not roughly, but firmly, the way you wake someone from sleepwalking off a cliff.

"What *year*, Casper."

"Seven-oh-three of the Common Accord," I said, which was wrong by three hundred years, and we both knew it, and I watched him decide not to be afraid in front of me, and failed.

* * *

After that he taught me the anchoring. It was the first honest gift he'd given me, and I hated how much I needed it.

The technique was old court craft, he said—what the memory-wardens used when they handled relics saturated with the past. Five anchors, one for each sense, chosen and rehearsed while sane so they could be gripped while lost. A sound. A smell. A texture under the fingers. A taste. A sight.

"Pick things that are *yours*," he said. "Not borrowed. Not bought. Things that happened to Casper Whitlock, in Casper Whitlock's one short life."

"That's a short list."

"It only has to be five long."

We sat cross-legged by the fire like children at lessons, and I resented every minute and held onto every word. My anchors, in the end:

The sound: rain on my stall's awning in the Tanners' Row market, that specific tin drumroll, the sound of a day when business might come. The smell: tallow and cheap ink, my ledger-book, the smell of my own careful false accounts. The texture: the chipped edge of my belt-knife's handle, worn smooth by ten years of my thumb. The taste: burnt tea, always burnt, because I never waited for the kettle properly. And the sight—

I stalled on the fifth.

Because the truth was, most of what I'd seen that was truly mine was ugly or small, and the one beautiful thing in recent memory had a face I currently didn't trust, sitting across the fire offering me his scarf against the damp as though betrayal were a thing you could apologize for with wool.

"Wren," I said. "Give me your hand."

He gave it. Of course he did; he was trying so hard to be trustworthy it leaked out of him like my memories leaked out of me.

I pressed his palm flat between both of mine and memorized it. The callus on the middle finger from lockwork. The old burn scar near the wrist he'd never explained and I'd never asked about. The warmth of him, which was infuriating, because even traitors apparently ran warm.

"Fifth anchor," I said. "Don't let it go to your head."

Something moved through his expression—guilt and tenderness fighting for the same window—and settled into something gentler than either.

"I won't," he said, and then, because honesty seemed contagious and dangerous, added: "For what it's worth, the assignment ended a long time ago. Everything since the docks—that's just me."

"For what it's worth," I said, "it isn't worth much."

But I kept holding his hand anyway. Trust, in my experience, isn't a wall you rebuild. It's a bridge you keep walking across while inspecting every plank.

* * *

The anchoring worked. Mostly.

I won't pretend the nights became gentle. They didn't. The war came back every time I slept—it was drawn to me now, the way iron filings find a lodestone—and some nights the leaks brought things worse than battles. I saw the old kings feeding their own memories into vessels. Prisoners, mostly. Children, sometimes. I saw the founding of the practice I'd built my whole criminal livelihood upon and understood that I was not an inventor but an inheritor, that my little trade in bottled hours was the bastard grandchild of court atrocities, and I sat with that knowledge by the fire and found, to my disgust, that it changed nothing. I still had to eat. Guilt, like everything else in Vell, was a luxury good.

But when the floods came, I gripped my anchors. Rain on tin. Tallow and ink. Chipped knife-handle. Burnt tea. Wren's scarred palm.

And slowly, over days, something shifted. The dreams stopped feeling like drowning and started feeling like—reading. As if the ancient war were a book I'd been thrown into, and now I'd learned to hold my place in it. I began to notice details. The thorn-sigil on the tall man's armor recurred; so did a phrase the winter-faced king repeated, *the Thorn must sleep,* and another, spoken by a dying queen whose memory I somehow carried, *nine crowns make one chain.*

"Tell me about the Thorn," I said to Wren one gray morning, testing.

"You know what I know."

"I know what you've *told* me. Those aren't the same animal."

He smiled the tired smile of a man who'd been caught fairly. "The Thorn is the counter-seal. The old court bound its legitimacy into blood-magic—Vane uses a corrupted version of the same working, that's why his claim holds, that's why Ilsevet can't simply walk in and take it back. But the original binding had a flaw built in, a failsafe. One object, one person, capable of unmaking a claim that was never honestly made." He shrugged. "The court hid it. Then the court fell. Then everyone who knew the hiding place died badly, and for three hundred years 'Thorn' has been a word prophets use to frighten usurpers."

"And now there's a face in a prophecy book."

"Now there's a face in a prophecy book," he agreed, "and it's yours, and I'm sorry, and I'd do the finding-all-over-again anyway, which makes me either a patriot or a monster, and I've stopped being sure which."

"At least you're sure of something. It's more than I've managed."

* * *

On the ninth night, the leak showed me the marsh itself, and I finally understood why Wren wouldn't let us take the direct route.

I dreamed from inside a soldier's memory—old, old, older than the rest—and in it the Mirenweald was not marshland. It was a city. Nine districts ringing a citadel, canals where the channels ran now, bridges where the willows lay fallen, and above it all the Ninefold Court in its living glory, white stone and nine banners. And beneath the city, beneath the water table, beneath everything, a vault, and in the vault something that slept and was *waiting*, and the soldier-memory carried the same phrase like a fever: *the Thorn must sleep until the court deserves it.*

I woke calm, which frightened me more than screaming would have. I built the fire up. I drank burnt tea. I anchored myself, sense by sense, and then I shook Wren awake.

"We're not fleeing through the marsh," I said. "We're traveling above it. This whole place is the old capital, drowned and buried, and whatever the prophecy means by the Thorn—it's down there. Under us. Under the water."

Wren sat up slowly. Around us the reeds stood silver in the moonlight, and far off across the black channels something glimmered that might have been marsh-gas, might have been lantern-light, and might, if the drowned city still kept its old wards, have been the reflection of nine towers that no longer existed anywhere except in stolen memories like mine.

"How much did you see?" he asked quietly.

"All of it." I wrapped my hands around the warm cup, around the chipped handle of my other life, and told him the rest: "Wren—the vault isn't empty. Something in that dream was *aware of me*. Whatever we're running from, and whatever we're running toward—" I nodded at the dark water, patient as a held breath, "—I think it just noticed us back."
