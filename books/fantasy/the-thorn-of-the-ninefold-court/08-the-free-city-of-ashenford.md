## 8. The Free City of Ashenford

Ashenford smelled of woodsmoke and river rot and, underneath it all, faintly of hope—the way a beggar's coat smells faintly of the perfume he wore before he lost everything.

We came through the south gate at dusk, two travelers with mud to the knees and a story about a dead grandmother in Vell that we'd rehearsed until Wren could deliver it without blinking. He couldn't, as it turned out. He blinked constantly. It was his one flaw as a liar, which was ironic, considering lying was apparently his profession.

"Stop blinking," I muttered through my teeth as the gate guards waved us past with the enthusiasm of men paid to care very little.

"I'm *narrating* grief," he said. "Grief blinks."

"Grief doesn't wink at the guard's wife."

"That was sympathy."

The refugee quarter took up everything east of the tanneries—a sprawl of tents and lean-tos and houses with too many families in them, built by people who had run out of country but not out of stubbornness. Vell's purges, the border lords' wars, the salt famine up north: Ashenford collected the debris of other people's catastrophes and made a market of it. There were more accents than coins, and both changed hands quickly.

I hated how much it felt like home.

Not Vell—I'd never had a home there either, just a rented room above a charm-shop that smelled of beeswax and fear. But this: the crowd that didn't look at you twice, the children running messages between stalls, the smell of someone frying onions over a brazier because tomorrow might not allow it. Places where everyone had lost something learned not to ask what.

Wren steered us through the lanes with an ease that should have alarmed me. He'd claimed he knew a contact here. What he hadn't mentioned—and I was keeping a list, mentally, titled *Things Wren Hasn't Mentioned*, currently eleven pages long—was that the contact seemed to know him well enough to be furious.

* * *

She found us before we found her, which told me most of what I needed to know about her.

A woman stepped out of an alley between a pie stall and a shrine to some river god with a fish's face, and she was wearing robes that had once been magnificent—silver-threaded, court-cut, now hemmed with patches and stained with dye-works indigo. She was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the kind of posture that suggested she'd been lecturing people since birth and intended to continue posthumously.

"Wren Sallow," she said, "you absolute blister on the backside of the old court."

"Mage Ondile," Wren said, spreading his hands, "you look radiant."

"I look *indigo*. The dyers pay me in cloth." Her eyes were sharp and quick and they went from him to me and stayed on me with the weight of a physical touch. "And this is the one you wrote to me about. In cipher. Badly."

"You decoded it."

"Because I'm better than you. Everyone is better than you; it's your defining trait." She looked at me again, longer this time, and I felt something brush against my awareness like a fingertip against a soap bubble—mage-sight, testing for magic. I'd been tested before. Ministry examiners did it with cold iron and worse. This was gentler, but no less invasive, and I flinched.

Ondile's eyebrows rose. "Hm," she said, in the tone of a woman who has just read the first page of a very long book. "You'd better come inside. And Wren—if you've led trouble to my door, I will turn you into something small and amphibious and sell you to the tanners' children."

"He'd make a good toad," I said.

Ondile smiled for the first time. It transformed her face entirely, and briefly I understood why the histories called her the finest court mage of her generation, before whatever disgrace sent her here. "I like this one," she told Wren. "Keep them away from open windows and sharp politics."

Inside was a dyer's loft: racks of hanging cloth in every color, steam and the bite of mordant in the air, and below, through a floor hatch, a hidden room where the real business happened. Maps. Coded letters. A wall covered in names written in three different inks—black for the living, red for the taken, white for the unknown.

The resistance, such as it was. Smaller than I'd imagined. Desperate enough to matter anyway.

"The exiled heir's people?" I asked quietly, and watched Wren go still beside me.

Ondile snorted. "Ilsevet's people are scattered across nine cities and half of them don't know they're Ilsevet's people yet. We're what's left of the old court's service—those who weren't killed, converted, or clever enough to flee early." She glanced at Wren. "Some fled later than others."

"Some had assignments," Wren said.

"Some hid behind assignments like cowards behind shields." But she said it without venom, almost fondly, and I realized these two had history—not lovers' history, something older and more comfortable, the history of people who'd survived the same disaster and blamed each other for surviving it differently.

Then she turned to me, and the fondness drained out of her voice. "Before anything else. Before food, before rest, before you ask me for anything. Do you understand what you are?"

I'd been asked versions of that question my whole life. *What are you, boy? Where do you come from? How does a child with no history learn to do what you do?* I had answers ready. I reached for one.

"Don't," Ondile said. "Don't reach for the practiced lie. Just stand there a moment. Let me see you."

And because I was tired, and hungry, and because she reminded me of nobody I'd ever known—which meant she reminded me of every stranger who'd ever shown me unexpected kindness—I let her.

Whatever she saw made her close her eyes briefly, as if pained. "A vessel," she murmured. "They built a vessel and never told it what for. Sit down, child. You look like you haven't eaten since Vell."

I hadn't, actually. Word travels fast in refugee quarters.

* * *

The twins arrived at dinner, which I suspect was deliberate; they struck me as people who timed their entrances for maximum effect and minimum responsibility.

They came down the hatch arguing, mid-sentence, as if the argument had been running for years and neither intended to let it end. They were identical down to the freckles and the crooked noses—broken in the same place, probably in the same accident—and they differed only in that one wore a red scarf and one wore a green one, and even then I suspected they swapped them for fun.

"—all I'm saying is that if the cargo was *legal*, Ferro wouldn't have needed to bribe the lockkeeper—"

"If the cargo was legal, Rynne would have nothing to complain about, so really the smuggling is a service—"

"To whom?"

"To narrative structure."

"There's no *narrative* in bribing a lockkeeper—"

"Every bribe is a story where everyone agrees to pretend—"

"*Boys,*" said Ondile, and the twins stopped as one, looked at the strangers at their table, and grinned identical grins.

"Ferro," said the red scarf.

"Rynne," said the green scarf.

"They're lying," Ondile said. "They swap names weekly to confuse informants."

"We swap *scarves*," said one twin.

"The names follow the scarves," said the other.

"It's a whole system," they finished together, and sat down and started eating off our plates without apology.

They were smugglers, it emerged—runners for the resistance network, moving letters and charms and occasionally people along the river routes. They talked with their mouths full, finished each other's sentences not from telepathy but from decades of practice at being each other's audience. They asked me questions in a tag-team rhythm designed to slip past careful answers:

Where from? Vell. Trade? "Charm-work." Legal charm-work? "Define legal." Ha! Good answer. Heard about the inquisitors in Vell? "Everyone's heard." Seen any? "Define seen." Ohh, we *like* this one—

And through it all, Wren watched me with that expression I was beginning to catalog: the one where his mouth smiled and his eyes apologized in advance for something I didn't yet know he'd done.

* * *

It was Rynne—or Ferro, or whichever twin was telling the truth at that hour—who mentioned the child.

"Pip'll want to meet you," he said. "She's been asking. Word gets around; a charm-worker comes in with Wren Sallow, of course word gets around."

"What kind of word?" I asked carefully. Charm-worker could mean many things in a city like this, most of them ending in a pyre.

"The interesting kind," said the other twin. "Pip steals. Best fingers in the quarter, and she's nine."

"A pickpocket," I said. "You're proud of a nine-year-old pickpocket."

"We're proud of a nine-year-old *professional*," Ferro corrected. "There's a difference. Standards. Pip never takes from anyone who can't spare it."

"And," Rynne added, dropping his voice with the particular relish people reserve for their best stories, "she remembers everything she steals."

I put down my cup. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning she doesn't take purses. Or—" Ferro waved a hand, "—not only purses. She takes *memories*. Off people. Little touches, brush-past work, same as any cutpurse lifts a coin. Except what she lifts stays in her head. She's got four years of stolen lives rattling around in there. Sailors, soldiers, a bishop once—she won't say whose, but she says prayers in a language none of us recognize and grins about it."

The room went quiet around me. Ondile was watching me again, that first-page-of-a-long-book look.

"How," I said. My voice came out strange. "How does she do it?"

"She just does," Rynne said. "Like breathing. Why? Is that unusual?"

Everything in me wanted to laugh or cry or possibly both, and I settled on the wry mask, the one that had kept me alive in Vell for years. "In my experience," I said, "it requires equipment. Bottles. Training. A great deal of hiding."

"Well, Pip hides nothing," said Ferro. "That's her problem. That's why she wants to meet you."

"Why?"

"Because you're the first person she's ever heard of," Rynne said, "who does what she does. She thinks she's a monster, Casper Whitlock. Been thinking it for years. Thought maybe she was cursed, or marked, or going mad—stolen voices in her head that aren't hers. And then a runner brings word that there's a memory-thief in the city, a real one, grown, alive, not burned."

He shrugged, suddenly awkward, all the showman's polish gone.

"So yeah. She wants to meet you. No pressure."

No pressure. As if a child hadn't just been handed my entire biography like a knife.

* * *

I found her on the roof.

Of course she found me first—that's the truth of it, and I'll tell it true even if it costs me dignity. I went up to the loft's roof to breathe, to get away from the warmth and the maps and the weight of being *known*, and she was sitting cross-legged on the ridge tiles in the dark like a gargoyle with better posture.

"You're him," she said.

Small, thin, dark-haired, with scraped knuckles and eyes that were older than nine had any right to manufacture. She looked at me the way I imagined scholars looked at rare manuscripts: hungrily, carefully, aware the thing might crumble.

"I'm someone," I said.

"Casper. The charm-seller. From Vell." She scooted along the tiles toward me with the balance of a cat. "Ferro says you bottle them. Memories. In glass. Like fireflies."

"Something like that."

"Do they stay good? In the bottles?"

"Years. If the wax holds."

"But you could keep them in your head too. Like me." It wasn't quite a question. She was watching my face the way I watched marks in a crowd—for tells.

"Yes," I said finally. "Like you."

Something broke in her expression—something tight that had been holding for a long time—and she scrambled the last few feet and threw her arms around my neck before I could brace for it. Nine years old, light as a bird, smelling of chimney soot and stolen bishops' incense.

"I'm not a monster," she whispered into my collar. It wasn't relief. It was fury, the fury of a child who'd been lied to about herself. "Everyone acted like I was. Ondile said I was *unusual*. Ferro and Rynne said I was *special*, which is worse. The priest at the shrine said God would sort it out, which means he didn't know either."

"The priest was right, though," I said. "About the sorting. It just takes longer than a Sunday."

She pulled back to look at me. "Does it hurt? Having other people in your head?"

I thought about the dying merchant's snowfall. About the sea I'd sold to Wren, three times over. About all the hours I carried that belonged to strangers, and the ones that belonged to no one, and the hole where mine should have been.

"Sometimes," I said. "But it hurts less when you're not the only one carrying it."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "Can I steal something of yours? Just to see?"

"Pip—"

"Just a little one. A color or a smell. I'll give it back."

"You can't give them back. That's rather the whole tragedy of it."

"Oh." She frowned, processing the economics. Then, briskly, businesslike, holding out her hand: "Trade, then. One of mine for one of yours. Fair's fair. Pick anything. I've got a storm off the coast of Corvane that'll knock your socks off, and a wedding where the bride ran off with the fiddler, and—" her voice dropped, reverent, "—a man falling asleep in a sunfield who was so happy it's almost unbearable. I keep that one for bad nights."

My throat closed up. Because I recognized it—not the memory, I'd never held it—but the *keeping*. The hoarding. The desperate archive of other people's best moments, saved against a night when you needed proof that happiness existed somewhere, even if it was borrowed.

Even if it was never yours.

"The sunfield," I heard myself say. "I'll trade you the sunfield for a first snowfall."

Her eyes went wide. "Deal," she breathed, and spat in her palm, and I shook it, because some contracts are sacred regardless of the sealant.

* * *

Later—much later, after Pip had fallen asleep against my shoulder mid-story and been carried off by Ferro, after Ondile had pressed a map into my hands and said *tomorrow we plan, tonight you sleep*, after Wren had found me on the roof again and sat beside me without speaking, which was the kindest thing he'd done all week—I lay in the loft's back room and listened to the house settle around me.

Somewhere below, cloth hung drying in colors I'd never earn the right to wear. Somewhere upstairs, a child slept full of stolen sunlight. Somewhere in this city, disgraced mages and lying twins and a woman with a ledger of names were building something out of the wreckage, and for one night—one single, unrepeatable night—I was inside it instead of outside looking in.

I belonged somewhere. I tested the thought like a loose tooth, waiting for it to hurt. It didn't. That scared me more than the inquisitors ever had.

Because things you belong to can be taken away. Rooms above charm-shops had taught me that. So had childhoods, though I couldn't remember mine well enough to mourn it properly, which was its own wound.

I was still turning that over when boots hit the roof tiles overhead—running boots, many of them—and Ferro's head appeared at the hatch, green scarf askew, all the performance gone out of his face.

"Rider just came in from the salt road," he said. "Vane's legions crossed the ford at Marrow Bridge this morning. Three thousand heavy infantry and a memory-engine on wheels." He swallowed. "They're marching on Ashenford. They'll be here inside the week."

Beside me, Wren went very still. Above us, the running footsteps scattered. And in the back of my mind, unbidden, a stolen dream surfaced—the ancient war leaking through again, banners and burning—and for the first time it didn't feel like someone else's memory at all.

It felt like weather coming.
