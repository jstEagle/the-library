## 9. Seraphine's Ledger

The boy they'd brought me was thirteen at most, all elbows and bravado, and he looked at my instruments the way children look at a dentist's tray—knowing exactly what each one is for and hoping desperately that knowing will somehow prevent its use.

"Name?" I said.

"Fitch." He spat it like a seed.

"Fitch what?"

"Just Fitch. Names cost extra in Vell. You know that."

I did know that, actually. It was one of the few things I still found charming about the city—the way everyone priced themselves, haggled their own identities down to something affordable. Inquisitor Hale of the Ministry of Purity, on the other hand, came with her name attached like a brand on livestock. Seraphine Hale. Ten years in service to Aldric Vane's purity, ten years of carrying a name that had become less an identity than an accusation other people whispered behind doors.

I set my ledger on the table between us. Not the instruments. The ledger. There's an order to interrogation, and the order matters more than the tools. Fear is a spice; use too much and you ruin the dish.

"You were taken at the Saltgate," I said, "with forty-one memory charms in your coat."

"Carrying them for a friend."

"The friend being Casper Whitlock."

He said nothing, which from a thirteen-year-old pickpocket is practically a signed confession. I opened the ledger anyway, mostly so he could watch me not needing his cooperation.

Here is what nobody tells you about hunting the Thorn for a decade: eventually you learn everything about your quarry except where they are. I could recite Whitlock's charm inventory by season. I knew which tea they drank (black, bitter, no honey—someone who doesn't trust sweetness), which streets they favored, which fences they used, which orphans ran their errands. I knew they had vanished from Vell three weeks ago like smoke through a keyhole, taking nothing but a satchel and, according to one witness, "the prettiest man in the harbor district."

And I knew what His Radiance believed about them. That was the part that had begun to itch.

"Do you know what happens to carriers of unlicensed charms?" I asked Fitch.

"Burning. Or the machine." He said *the machine* the way sailors say *the deep*—with the particular reverence of people who have decided that if it's going to get them anyway, they might as well give it a proper name. "But I'm just a kid. Kids go to the workhouses."

"Usually." I turned a page. "You're going to tell me about Whitlock instead, and then you're going to be a kid who goes to a workhouse instead of a furnace, and someday you'll be old enough to appreciate how rare that bargain is."

He chewed the inside of his cheek. Outside the window, the Ministry's bells rang the third hour, and somewhere below us a clerk dropped a stack of papers and swore with impressive creativity. Interrogation rooms are never as silent as novels pretend. Real ones smell of ink and cold mutton.

"What do you want to know?"

*Everything,* said the hungry thing in my chest. But I've learned to feed that thing only what serves.

"Start with the first time you saw them lift a memory."

* * *

It took two hours, three cups of watered wine, and one carefully timed moment where I stood up as if to leave before he cracked wide open. Children always think silence is worse than pain. They're usually right.

What Fitch gave me was not intelligence, exactly. It was something better: a portrait.

Whitlock, he said, paid the street kids in food and coin and never once in favors. Whitlock remembered every child's name—not their own childhood, Fitch added, puzzled, but everyone else's, like they were keeping a library of other people's beginnings. Whitlock never took memories from the kids. Never touched them. When the little one, Pip, had been caught by a shopkeeper last winter, Whitlock had gone and lifted the shopkeeper's memory of the theft so Pip walked free with no crime ever having happened, and then Whitlock had spent two days sick as a dog, shivering under blankets while the stolen memory sat in them like swallowed glass.

"They don't sell half of what they take," Fitch said. "There's a box. Locked. We think—" He stopped, embarrassed.

"Go on."

"We think they keep the sad ones. The ones nobody would buy. A dying woman remembering her daughter. A soldier's last morning before a battle he didn't survive. Stuff like that. Why would you keep stuff like that if you were going to sell it?"

I wrote it all down because writing keeps my hands busy and my face professional, and because underneath the professionalism something uncomfortable was happening to me.

For ten years I had hunted the Thorn of the Ninefold Prophecy. I had read every commentary, memorized every disputed line, attended His Radiance's lectures on the subject with the attentive blankness of a good courtier. The Thorn, per Vane's doctrine, was a destroyer-in-waiting. An unmaking. A blade wrapped in human flesh, waiting for someone foolish enough to hand it a cause. Every analysis, every briefing, every red thread on every map in the Ministry had been built on that single assumption: *the Thorn is a weapon.*

A weapon who kept locked boxes full of dying women's memories. A weapon who made himself sick absorbing a shopkeeper's petty grievance to spare a child. A weapon who—

A weapon who hoarded grief like a magpie hoards silver, because their own beginning had been cut out of them and other people's sorrows were the closest thing to a past they could afford.

I put down my pen.

"Seraphine?" Fitch said. He'd started calling me by name somewhere around the second cup of wine, which should have annoyed me and instead felt like being seen by a stray cat. "Did I say something wrong?"

"No," I said. "You said something right. That's frequently worse."

* * *

That night I did something inadvisable, which in my experience is the only kind of thing worth doing after dark in the Ministry of Purity.

I went to the restricted vaults.

Access required His Radiance's seal, technically. But I had spent ten years learning the Ministry's rhythms the way Whitlock had learned Vell's, and I knew that Archivist Corvane took his supper at the eighth bell, that the ward-keys hung on a hook shaped like a heron because some long-dead Minister had thought that funny, and that the wards themselves responded to senior inquisitors' blood without logging the visit if you pressed the heel of your palm rather than slicing properly. Sloppy. Deliberately sloppy, I'd sometimes suspected, because ministries are built by people who want back doors for themselves.

The Ninefold Prophecy cycle lived in a case of black iron at the far end, past the confiscated grimoires and the heretical genealogies. I'd read the public transcription a hundred times. What I wanted was the marginalia—the archivists' notes, the collations, the things scholars had written in the margins over a century and that no doctrine had bothered to burn yet because nobody important read margins.

Margins are where truth goes to hide. Ask any censor.

I carried the volume to the reading alcove and worked by candlelight, and this is what I found:

The prophecy's ninth verse—the Thorn verse—exists in four manuscript traditions. Three describe an instrument of destruction. The fourth, oldest, in a hand the archivist dated to within living memory of the prophecy's writing, uses a different word entirely. Not *thorn* in the sense of the piercing barb. *Thorn* as in *the vessel that holds the rose.* A container. A keeping-place.

The margin note, in faded iron-gall ink, read: *"The Ninefold does not destroy. The Ninefold empties. That which is poured out cannot be wielded against its pourer. Note for His Radiance's edification: a vessel drained of all it holds has no contents left to threaten anyone—and no self left to refuse him."*

No self left to refuse him.

I read that line four times. My candle guttered. Somewhere in the vaults a pipe ticked as the heating cooled, the building settling into itself like an old man into a chair.

Here is the arithmetic I performed in that alcove, and I commend its elegance even now, even as it made me ill:

Vane's claim to the Ninefold Court rests on royal memories. Everyone in the Ministry knows the official version—that the old court's legitimacy died with the massacre, that Vane holds the true succession by conquest and counsel. What the Ministry officially knows is a bedtime story. What I had begun, quietly, over months, to suspect, was that Vane's claim rested on something far more literal: the actual memories of the actual royals, fed to him, worn by him, a crown made of other people's heads.

The same crime. Do you see? The exact same crime I had spent a decade punishing in gutter-thieves and hedge-witches. Stealing what belongs to another mind. Wearing another soul's history as your own.

Except Whitlock stole to eat, and to fill an emptiness someone else had carved. And Vane stole to rule.

And the Thorn—the vessel, the emptied thing—was the one object in the world that could prove it. Because a vessel that pours out everything it holds pours out proof along with the rest. Every stolen memory released is a testimony given. Every fabricated claim it touches comes apart like wet paper.

Which meant Vane didn't want the Thorn destroyed. Of course he didn't. A destroyed Thorn proves nothing. He wanted the Thorn *emptied into his machine*—drained of the prophecy's power, drained of the evidence, drained of the self that might refuse him. The margin note wasn't scholarship. It was an instruction. Someone had written it *for* him, generations ago or recently, I couldn't tell, and he had built a decade of my life around retrieving a person he intended to hollow out like a gourd.

I thought of Whitlock's locked box of sad memories. I thought of Pip walking free. I thought of a child with no childhood keeping everyone else's, and a king with no legitimate claim wearing everyone else's crowns, and the Ministry of Purity standing between them with its instruments laid out neatly, defending the larger thief against the smaller one because the larger one signed our pay.

My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the privacy of my own telling: my hands did not shake. Whatever else I am, I am not a woman who shakes when the floor gives way beneath her. I simply begin looking for the next floor.

I copied the margin note. I copied the variant verse. Then, because I am thorough and thoroughness is the only virtue I've never been accused of lacking, I went one shelf further and pulled the requisition logs for the machine.

* * *

The machine has no official name. The technicians call it the Chirurgeon, which tells you everything about technicians. It sits in a sealed wing beneath the capital, and its purpose, per the requisition logs, is "memory extraction and integration"—a phrase written by someone being paid by the word and ashamed of every one of them.

Extraction, we all knew about. Extraction was punishment. Criminals, heretics, unlicensed practitioners—stripped of their gifts, rendered safe, sent to the workhouses as pleasant blanks. I had authorized extractions. I had watched them. I had told myself the standard lies: that it was mercy compared to burning, that the condemned wouldn't miss what they couldn't remember missing.

*Integration* was the word I'd skimmed past for years. Integration meant the extracted material didn't vanish. It went somewhere. Into the Chirurgeon's lattice of iron and bone. And from the lattice, into—

Into whoever stood at the receiving throne.

The logs showed the intake schedule for the coming season. Bulk requisitions: sedatives, bone-lattice replacements, preservation salts enough for dozens of subjects. And at the bottom of the final page, in His Radiance's own hand—a hand I recognized from a hundred countersigned warrants—two words circled twice:

*"Heart's-blood."*

There was no entry explaining heart's-blood. There didn't need to be. The old texts mention it exactly once, in a treatise on the Ninefold vessels that the Ministry classifies as superstition: *the vessel surrenders all willingly given, but the final seal—the anchor-memory, the one held dearest—yields only to the heart's-blood of the vessel itself, freely spilled.*

He intended to bleed it out of them. Whatever Whitlock refused to give—and everyone refuses to give one thing, everyone keeps one anchor—he meant to open them for it like a letter.

I sat in that alcove until my candle drowned in its own wax, and I thought about the interrogations of my career. All those frightened people across the table. How many had been guilty of anything beyond hunger? How many had I stripped and shipped to workhouses, telling myself it was mercy, telling myself it was law, telling myself the story the Ministry tells itself so it can sleep?

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest: *he sent me to find the Thorn. Ten years. He chose me. Not because I'm the best hunter in the Ministry, though I am. Because I'm loyal enough to deliver the prize and unimaginative enough not to ask what happens afterward.*

Well. He was half right. Loyalty, it turns out, is also a vessel, and I had just discovered what mine contained.

I closed the ledger. My ledger—the small black one, not the official record, the one that lives inside my coat against my ribs where a weapon would sit if I carried weapons, which I don't, which has always struck my colleagues as either sentiment or arrogance. Both, probably. A ledger is a weapon. It's just one that takes longer to draw.

Fitch went to a workhouse the next morning. A real one, the decent sort upriver, with a recommendation in my hand that will keep him fed and unremarked until he's grown. He thanked me, which was the worst part. Children shouldn't have to thank their jailers for choosing the soft cage.

Then I sat at my desk and began, in my small precise script, a different kind of case file. Subject: Aldric Vane, styling himself Protector of the Ninefold Court. Charges pending collection: usurpation by memory-theft, construction of forbidden apparatus, conspiracy to commit ritual bloodletting upon a civilian. Evidence: accumulating.

It's strange, planning treason. It feels mostly like paperwork. I suppose most monstrous things do, from the inside. Empires are filed into existence and filed out again; nobody notices the moment the pen changes allegiance.

By the tenth bell I had eleven pages. By midnight, nineteen. And on the twentieth page, reviewing what I had and what I still needed, I wrote the line that changed the shape of everything:

*"The Thorn is the only living witness. Therefore: the Thorn must not be captured. The Thorn must be reached first."*

Which raised the immediate practical question that had ended my career as an inquisitor and begun whatever I was becoming: everyone was hunting Casper Whitlock—my colleagues, my agents, the legions mustering on the salt road, the informants seeded through every free city from Vell to the marshes.

Everyone except me. I was going to find them first. And I was going to have to decide, somewhere on that road, whether I was arriving as rescuer or as the most patient trap His Radiance had ever sprung.

Even I didn't know the answer yet. But I packed the ledger, burned my copies of the warrant list, and left the Ministry before dawn without leaving a note, and I can tell you this: the floor held.

For now.

Three weeks later, half-dead on the salt road with Vane's legions burning everything behind me, I would finally understand what I was running toward. But that morning, crossing the Ministry courtyard with my collar up and twenty pages of treason against my ribs, I knew only one thing with certainty:

I had spent ten years hunting a weapon. I had found a person instead. And persons, unlike weapons, can choose whose side they're on—which meant, if I moved fast enough, they might choose mine.
