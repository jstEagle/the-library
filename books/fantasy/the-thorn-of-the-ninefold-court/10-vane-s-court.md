## 10. Vane's Court

The capital of Vane's new order had been called Corvale once, when it belonged to a king who at least had the courtesy to be born to it. Now the banners over the gates were plain grey—Aldric Vane considered heraldry vulgar—and the city itself had been scrubbed so thoroughly of its history that the tour guides had taken to inventing scandals about the old royal family just to have something to say. Seraphine Hale found this almost touching. A city that lies about its past is a city that still misses it.

She arrived on the ninth day of the month of Ashes, in a coach with the Ministry seal, and was met at the palace steps by a chamberlain whose smile had the fixed quality of a man who had learned that smiling was safer than the alternatives.

"Inquisitor Hale. His Radiance is delighted you've come."

"Is he," said Seraphine. "How nice for us both."

The chamberlain blinked. People were always briefly startled by her before deciding she'd been joking. She let them decide. It saved time.

* * *

Vane's court assembled in the Hall of Nine Thrones—or what remained of it. The old king had kept nine empty seats flanking his own, one for each of the free cities, an arrangement of polite fiction that everyone had understood as polite fiction and honored accordingly. Vane had eight of them broken up for firewood during his first winter. He sat alone beneath the vaulted ceiling now, on a throne he had not replaced but *repaired*—new iron where the gilt had chipped, bolted plates over the old carving, until the thing looked less like a seat of power than like a wound dressed in armor.

He rose when she entered, which was theater; Vane always rose for his instruments. He was a handsome man in the way that well-preserved ruins are handsome, silver at his temples arranged as though it had grown there out of dignity rather than dye, hands folded, voice pitched to carry warmth exactly as far as the second row of courtiers and no further.

"Seraphine." He used her given name the way other men used titles—as a grant of favor, revocable at will. "You bring me good news from the provinces?"

"I bring you news, Your Radiance. Whether it's good depends on your appetite."

Around them, the courtiers performed their small ballet of pretending not to listen while missing nothing. Seraphine had spent ten years among these people and had catalogued them the way she catalogued everything: the Lord Marshal, loyal because disloyalty required imagination he didn't possess; the Treasurer, loyal because Vane paid him; the Lady Chancellor, loyal because she genuinely believed order was worth any price, which made her the most dangerous of the three. And scattered among them, the quiet ones—the ones whose eyes moved too quickly, whose letters home went through channels Seraphine had quietly begun to trace.

"The Thorn," Vane said. "Progress."

"It fled Vell ahead of my warrant. It has contacts I'm still mapping. It killed two of my men in the marsh country—" This was a lie, or close enough to one; the men had died of bad water, but dead was dead, and a legend needed feeding. "It is resourceful. It has help."

"From the exiled heir."

"Perhaps." She watched him. "Ilsevet's agents move freely through the free cities. If they've collected our runaway, we'll know soon enough."

Vane smiled, and Seraphine felt the particular chill she always felt when he did, because his smiles never reached anywhere near the region of his eyes. They were issued, like currency, from a central authority.

"You've served me faithfully, Seraphine. Ten years." He stepped down from the armored throne and took her hand, pressing it between both of his—a gesture calculated to be seen. "Faithful service will be rewarded. Soon there'll be no more hunting, no more running. When the Thorn is mine, all of this—" a gesture that took in the hall, the court, the grey banners, the whole scraped-clean city— "will be *settled*. Permanently."

It was the word *mine* that snagged, later, when she lay awake in her guest chambers counting the cracks in the ceiling. Not *destroyed*. Not *ended*. Mine.

At the time, she only bowed and said, "Then I'd best catch it."

* * *

The summons came three nights later, delivered not by chamberlain but by a page-child with frightened eyes: His Radiance requested the Inquisitor's presence in the Undercroft. Immediately. Alone.

The palace undercroft had been the royal wine cellars once. Vane had cleared out the casks in his first month—not, as the rumors claimed, out of temperance, but because he needed the space. What he'd built down there instead occupied three vaulted chambers connected by corridors lined with iron pipe, and it hummed. That was the first thing you noticed, descending the stairs: a hum below hearing, felt in the teeth more than the ears, like standing inside a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

The machine filled the central chamber.

She had heard of it, of course. Every senior officer of the Ministry had heard of it, in the way one hears of distant disasters—accurately, and without comprehension. Reports called it the Loom. Built from salvaged court-magic and Vane's own designs, iron and bone and glass, it resembled nothing so much as a great ribcage stood on end, vertebrae of brass ascending toward the vaulted ceiling, and at its heart a chair. An ordinary-looking chair, padded, almost kind.

A man sat in it.

He was young, maybe thirty, with the ink-stained fingers of a scribe and the shaved temples of a licensed mage—one of the few permitted to practice under Ministry charter, which meant his capture required paperwork Seraphine had apparently signed without reading, or hadn't been shown at all. His eyes were open. That was the detail she would return to afterward, in the dark, unable to stop returning to it: his eyes were open and aware and looking directly at her as she entered, and he mouthed something. She couldn't make it out. She has spent years wishing she could.

"Seraphine." Vane stood beside the machine with the relaxed air of a host showing off his garden. "Good. You're punctual. I wanted you to see this."

"Your Radiance, I wasn't aware we had a prisoner requiring—"

"We don't." He touched the mage's shoulder almost fondly. "Master Ellery here volunteered his talents to the crown. Didn't you, Master Ellery?"

The mage's throat worked. Whatever he said came out as a croak, but it might plausibly have been agreement, if you were generous, or deaf, or lying to yourself with professional skill.

"What you're about to witness," Vane continued, "is the solution to a problem that has plagued every ruler since the Ninefold War." He gestured, and two attendants in leather aprons began fitting copper circlets to the mage's temples, threading wires of drawn gold into sockets along the machine's spine. "Every kingdom in history rests on legitimacy—the belief of the governed that the governor has the *right*. Belief is fragile. Belief can be argued with. Belief can be *remembered differently* by different people, which is the true disease of nations. But what if belief weren't a matter of opinion?" His voice dropped into the register he used for sermons. "What if memory itself could be administered? Centrally. Uniformly. What if loyalty weren't demanded of the people but *installed*, the way one installs a floor?"

The hum deepened. Seraphine became aware that her heart was beating too fast, and that she had not moved, and that moving would mean admitting she wanted to leave.

"Your Radiance. The Ministry exists to suppress unlicensed magic. What you're describing is—"

"Scale, Seraphine. I am describing scale." He smiled again, warmly, at her. "You've hunted memory-thieves for a decade. Petty criminals bottling stolen hours for coin. Tell me—did you ever ask yourself why I take the trade so seriously? Why a smuggler with a bag of charms earns the scaffold while a murderer might buy pardon?"

Because you fear the prophecy, she thought. Because somewhere in a forbidden book there's a face that matches a thief's, and you lie awake over it. But she said, "Magic unchecked becomes power unchecked."

"Mm." He turned back to the machine. "Watch."

They fed the mage to the Loom slowly. That was the horror of it—it was *slow*. No screaming, no blood. The attendants adjusted valves; the hum climbed through registers; and Master Ellery began, gently, to forget. Seraphine watched his face the way she'd watched a hundred interrogations, reading it with professional detachment that lasted perhaps four minutes. First went something that made him frown, puzzled, like a man who'd walked into a room and lost his reason. Then something that made him laugh—a soft, surprised, childlike laugh, delight at some vanished wonder—and the laugh was worse than screaming would have been. Then his mother, Seraphine judged, though there was no way to be certain; then his name, visibly, the way a candle gutters; and the attendants caught what spilled out of him in glass vessels fitted to the machine's base, vials filling with a faint luminescence that Seraphine recognized with a lurch, because she had confiscated thousands of them in her career.

Memories. Bottled. The Loom didn't destroy them. It *harvested*.

And then the machine reversed, and gave something back.

She saw it happen to his face—the return of structure, of selfhood, rebuilt on a new foundation. When the attendants finally unstrapped him, Master Ellery stood, bowed to Aldric Vane with genuine, radiant devotion, tears of love standing in his eyes, and asked—voice clear and steady—whether there was anything further His Radiance required of him.

"No, Master Ellery. You may return to your work. You've served the crown well today."

The mage left humming. Seraphine stood very still and thought, with the cold clarity that had made her reputation: *that man believes he has always loved Aldric Vane. There is no argument that can reach him. There is no testimony, no ledger, no truth that can touch a mind rewritten at the root.*

"There," Vane said softly. "Now you understand the war."

She turned to him. Ten years of discipline held her face still. "The free cities?"

"The free cities are a symptom. Ilsevet is a symptom. Even your Thorn is a symptom." He laid his hand on the machine's brass vertebrae, and the hum seemed to lean into his palm like a dog. "The disease is *disagreement*, Seraphine. A nation that remembers its past identically cannot rebel, because rebellion requires imagining an alternative, and the alternative will have been removed from them. One throne. One memory. Forever." He looked at her, and for one unguarded instant she saw beneath the statesman, beneath even the fanatic—to something hungrier and older and terribly patient. "I don't want to defeat the Ninefold Court, Seraphine. I want to end the *possibility* of courts. Of choices. Of all of it."

*Later,* she would think. *Think about it later.* Because the alternative to thinking about it later was thinking about it now, in front of him, and Seraphine Hale had survived ten years in Vane's service precisely by never letting her face think faster than her mouth.

"A remarkable instrument, Your Radiance," she said.

"It will be. It isn't finished." He said it lightly, but she marked the words, filed them, pinned them like specimens. "There are components still required. Rare ones." His gaze rested on her a moment too long. "You'll have them when I need them. Dismissed."

She bowed. She walked up the stairs at a measured pace. Only in her own chambers, door barred, did she allow herself to sit down on the edge of the bed and discover that her hands were shaking.

* * *

She told herself, for two days, that she was compiling the report he expected. That was how it started—with legitimate paperwork. She was Inquisitor of the Realm; documentation was her trade.

But ledgers have a way of becoming confessions.

She began with the prisoner intake records for Master Ellery, and discovered the arrest warrant bore her own signature—forged, and competently, though not so competently that she couldn't prove it. She traced the licensing office's chain of custody and found three gaps where pages had been replaced, each gap corresponding to a mage who had gone from licensed practice to "voluntary service" over the past eighteen months. Seven mages. She found six of them in the capital's registries, reassigned, productive, devoted. The seventh had died—officially of fever—and his body had been released to no family, buried in a pauper plot she visited herself at dusk, standing in the rain over an unmarked grave with her notebook dry under her coat, writing down the gravedigger's name.

She built a second ledger alongside the first. The official one, for Vane. The true one, for—she did not yet say for whom, even in her own head. For the record. For whoever came after. For the version of events that would exist if Seraphine Hale did not.

Some entries were small. The date the Loom first hummed, deduced from coal requisitions to the undercroft. The names of the attendants—leather-aproned, silent, paid through an account she couldn't quite reach. The exact phrasing Vane had used, transcribed within the hour while it was fresh: *one throne, one memory, forever.* Some entries were large. The forged warrant. The grave. The fact that the machine was unfinished, and hungry, and that its master had spoken of *rare components* while looking at her with the particular patience of a man who already knows where he'll find them.

On the fourth night, she sat late with candle and notebook and forced herself to write the entry she had been circling for days, the one that changed everything:

*I have spent ten years believing I hunted monsters to protect the realm. Tonight I watched the realm's protector feed a living man into a machine and call it mercy, and I understood that I have not been the shield. I have been the hand that clears the road ahead of him.*

She read it back. It was true, and being true, it was useless unless it survived her. Ledgers burned. Inquisitors fell down stairs. She needed more than paper.

She needed the Thorn.

The thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome, and stayed. The runaway her whole career had bent toward capturing—the vessel, the anomaly, the face from a book she'd read once in a restricted archive and told no one about. Everyone wanted the Thorn: Vane, who feared it; the exiled heir, who presumably wanted to use it; the prophecies, whatever prophecies actually wanted. And Seraphine now understood something none of them seemed to grasp, something she wrote in the last line of her true ledger before snuffing the candle:

*If Vane completes his machine, the Thorn is the last loose thread in the world—the last memory outside his reach. He doesn't merely want it captured. He needs it fed to the Loom, and everything it carries with it.*

Which meant, she reasoned, blowing out the light, that her duty had inverted itself somewhere in the undercroft without her noticing: the most important thing she could do for the realm was to make sure a wanted criminal stayed beyond her own grasp.

The next morning, a dispatch arrived from the provincial offices in Ashenford, requesting guidance on refugee registration protocols—routine, tedious, addressed to her office as a matter of course. She read it twice. On the third pass, she noticed what a tired clerk's eye would skip: the courier's seal was correct, but the wax had been poured slightly too thickly, the way it always was from one particular sub-office, the one through which, she happened to know, Ilsevet's sympathizers routed their quiet correspondence.

She took out a clean sheet of paper, and in the careful, unremarkable hand of a bureaucrat answering a bureaucratic question, she began to compose her first message to the enemy—asking, among other routine matters, whether the provincial offices had encountered any difficulty registering a certain class of transient persons, and adding, as a personal postscript, that she would be inspecting the Ashenford facilities personally within the fortnight.

Then she sealed it with the wrong thickness of wax, and sent it, and began packing.
