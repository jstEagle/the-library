## 11. The Price of the Charm

I had always assumed the Thorn's power would announce itself. Trumpets, maybe. A burning sensation in the palms. Something biblical and inconvenient, given that I'd spent my whole adult life perfecting the art of going unnoticed.

Instead it arrived on a Tuesday, in a beet field, while I was arguing with a goose.

"The bird doesn't like you," Ferro called from the cart, with the particular relish he reserved for my suffering. He was up to his elbows in turnips, because apparently even smugglers had cover crops, and his twin sister Sable was three fields over pretending to count fence posts while actually counting Vane's patrol on the salt road.

"The bird has no taste," I said.

The goose honked at me with the confidence of a creditor.

We'd been at the twins' farmstead for eleven days—Ilsevet called it a waystation, the twins called it home, and I had learned to call it nothing at all, because naming things made them easier to lose. The routine was simple: Ferro and Sable ran goods down the old salt road for the resistance, I bottled memories for the mages' experiments, and Wren kept careful distance, which is to say he stood exactly far enough away to be near me without being accused of it.

Then Sable came over the fence line at a dead run, and the goose forgave me everything, because geese understand priorities.

"Six riders," she said, breathless. "Grey cloaks. Memory-machine on a mule cart."

Ferro went still in a way I'd never seen him go still. "They're not supposed to be this far east."

"They are now." Sable grabbed his arm. "The ledger crate's in the barn, Fero, it's got names in it—"

"Names" was doing quiet work in that sentence. Names of sympathizers. Names of safehouses. Names of every farmer between here and Ashenford who'd ever sold the resistance an egg. If Vane's inquisitors cracked that crate, they wouldn't need to torture anyone. They'd just need to read.

"We burn it," Ferro said.

"It takes ten minutes to burn properly. They'll be here in four."

And everyone looked at me.

Not all at once. That would have been kinder. But I felt it happen—the small, guilty rotation of heads, like compass needles finding north. Sable looked first, then Ferro, then, last and worst, Wren, who didn't look at me so much as look *away* from me too slowly.

"I don't know how to use it," I said. "That's not—I've never—"

"You unmade the patrol outside Ashenford," Sable said. "The one that almost took Pip. Everyone saw."

"I panicked. Panicking isn't a skill."

"Casper." Wren crossed the yard, and for once he closed the distance instead of keeping it. Up close his eyes did the thing they always did—the cataloguing thing, the agent thing—but underneath it was something rawer. "You don't have to."

Which was, of course, exactly the right thing to say, and exactly the wrong thing, because now refusing felt noble instead of sensible, and nobility has killed more fools than cowardice ever has.

"How does it work?" I asked Ilsevet's mages in my head, but they were two days away, so I asked the only teacher I had: the leak itself.

Because that was the thing about my stolen memories. They leaked. Every night since the marshes, they bled into my dreams—the ancient war, the Ninefold Court in its living years, the first Thorn standing where I now stood with a goose watching. And the dreams kept returning to one image: the original Thorn, hands outstretched, and soldiers kneeling as their oaths poured out of them like water from a broken jug.

Oaths are memories. Loyalty is just a memory you keep choosing. And I was a vessel built for carrying exactly that kind of cargo.

"Get to the tree line," I told the twins. "Both of you. Take the ledger crate if you can lift it, burn it if you can't."

"And you?" Ferro said.

"I'm going to do the stupidest thing I've ever done, and I once stole the Lord Mayor's wig off his head during a speech."

Wren didn't move toward the trees. "Casper—"

"Wren." I looked at him, really looked, and hated myself a little for what I said next: "Watch from the fence. If I forget who I am, tell me. You're good at knowing my history better than I do."

It came out sharper than I meant. It was true, though—that was the wound of it. Since the marshes, when he'd admitted the courtship began as assignment, he'd taken up a habit of speaking my past aloud to me at night, quietly, like a man reading scripture to keep a candle lit. *Your name is Casper Whitlock. You grew up in Vell, in the tannery quarter. Your own childhood was stolen before you could hold it.* As if saying it often enough might make it stick harder than the leaks could pull it loose.

He flinched. Then he nodded, and went to stand at the fence like a man at a graveside.

* *

Six riders came up the salt road at a canter, grey cloaks dusted white with road lime, and behind them a mule cart bearing the machine—a cage of iron ribs around a drum of bone-white ceramic, humming faintly, the way a hive hums. I'd seen its work in Seraphine Hale's world, secondhand through stolen memories: mages fed in whole, memories drained out, people left as pleasant vacant rooms.

The lead rider reined in at the farmyard gate and surveyed the scene: one young person standing alone in a turnip field, arms loose, expression arranged into helpful rural vacancy. I'd worn that face for twenty years in Vell. It fit like an old boot.

"Afternoon," I said.

"Where are the farmers?" He had an inquisitor's voice—polite, patient, the voice of a man who already knew your answer and was curious whether you'd insult him with it.

"Gone to market. Salt road's been twitchy lately."

"Mm." He dismounted. Behind him, his soldiers fanned out with the bored competence of men who'd done this fifty times. One drifted toward the barn. "We're looking for smugglers. Twins, actually. Boy and girl, mid-twenties, charming as sin. You seen them?"

"No one comes through here but geese."

The inquisitor smiled. And then, because inquisitors are creatures of procedure, he did the thing I'd been waiting for: he reached to his belt and unhooked a small brass instrument, a memory-sniffer, the kind they used in Vell to detect charm-work. He pointed it at me the way you'd point a lantern into a dark room.

The needle slammed against its stop.

I felt it happen before he did—the instrument reading me, and me reading back. Because here's what nobody understood about the Thorn, including me: I wasn't a thief who happened to carry stolen goods. I was a *ledger*. A hundred lives of borrowed hours sat in me, shelved and cross-indexed, and the Thorn's power wasn't some separate faculty bolted on top.

It was inventory. All I had to do was open the shelves.

"Interesting," the inquisitor said softly. "Hold him."

Two soldiers started forward. And I reached into myself the way I'd reached into a dying merchant's mind a lifetime ago, except inward, and I found the memory I wanted—not one I'd stolen, but one I could feel sitting in the structure of everything I carried: the shape of an oath. A soldier's oath. I'd lifted one once, years ago, from a veteran selling his war stories by the bottle in Vell. The pride of it. The belonging. The way a grey cloak feels like armor even before it's armor.

I held it up inside myself like a lantern, and I let it pour out.

* *

I want to describe it honestly, because everything after depends on honest description.

It didn't feel like casting a spell. It felt like opening a door I hadn't known was load-bearing. The stolen oath-memory left me in a rush I couldn't steer—it went through the six riders like wind through wheat, and it didn't travel alone. Memories cling to memories; that's the whole architecture of a self. My oath-memory caught their loyalty-memories the way a struck bell catches other bells ringing, and suddenly I was pulling, and they were pouring, and—

The soldiers stopped.

All six. Mid-stride, mid-draw, mid-sneer. Their faces went smooth as eggs. The nearest one looked at his own hand holding a drawn blade with mild interest, the way you'd look at a tool left by a previous tenant. The inquisitor swayed in the saddle, blinked twice, and asked, in a voice gone vague and friendly, "Is this the road to market?"

"Yes," I heard myself say. "Best hurry."

They turned. They rode back the way they came, unhurried, unbothered, six men who no longer knew why they wore grey or whom they served, and the mule followed the cart, because mules are loyal to nothing but forward motion.

I stood in the turnip field feeling absolutely wonderful, which should have been my first warning.

Then the cost arrived.

* *

It didn't hurt. That's the cruel part. Pain would have been information. This was subtraction—clean, silent, surgical. Like a page lifted from a book by someone very skilled, leaving the binding intact.

I was looking at the farmyard, and then there was a hole in the farmyard where a year used to be.

I remembered arriving at Ashenford. I remembered the marshes before that, the flight from Vell, Wren's confession, the hollow hill. I remembered everything up to roughly the moment we'd settled here. And then—I checked, frantically, the way you pat your pockets after a cutpurse works a crowd—and found eleven days of nothing. The twins' faces: familiar, but as illustrations, not acquaintances. The goose: an anecdote someone must have told me. The nights by the fire, Wren reading my history aloud to keep me moored—

Gone. A year, give or take. Not stolen. *Spent.* The Thorn had paid for six soldiers' freedom out of my account, and it had taken the most recent coin first, the way creditors do.

I sat down in the turnips. This seemed wise.

"Casper." Wren was there—he'd covered the distance from the fence in seconds, and his face was doing something terrible, something I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors after bad bottlings: the look of a craftsman watching beautiful work destroy itself. "Hey. Look at me. Your name is Casper Whitlock. You grew up in Vell, in the tannery quarter—"

"I know who I am." My voice came out strange, thin at the edges. "I know who I am. I just don't know what I've been doing for—" I counted backward from the marshes and hit fog. "How long was I here? Wren. How long?"

"Eleven days."

Eleven days. Eleven days of a life I had lived and could not find. Somewhere in that missing span I'd learned the twins' laugh, learned which floorboard lied about being solid, learned whatever private joke had made the goose hate me. Someone had lived those days wearing my face. They'd simply failed to leave a forwarding address.

"You saw," I said. "What it cost."

"Yes."

"One year. For six men. What does the whole prophecy want, Wren? What does emptying *everything* buy?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Ilsevet says the Thorn breaks Vane's claim. Nobody mentions the exchange rate."

Wren crouched in front of me, mud soaking his knees, and did the thing he did now instead of touching me: he held his hands open, palms up, close enough that I could take them if I chose. An offer without pressure. He'd learned that in the marshes, after the confession, after I walked alone among the reeds. It was the most honest gesture I'd ever seen from him, and I hated that it was.

"You bought six people their minds back," he said. "And the twins. And every name in that crate—every farmer, every safehouse between here and Ashenford. That's not nothing, Casper."

"It's not nothing. It's also not mine anymore. Do you understand the difference?" I pressed my fists against my eyes until the dark went orange. "In Vell I stole other people's memories and sold them. Now the power in me steals *mine*. I'm just the middleman in my own robbery. At least the clients used to pay."

From the tree line came crashing—Ferro and Sable, hauling the ledger crate between them, having watched the whole thing from cover. They stopped at the edge of the trampled turnips. Sable's face was pale under her freckles. Ferro set down his end of the crate with exaggerated care, the way you do when you don't trust your hands.

"You saved us," Sable said. "You know that, right? Whatever it took—you sent six inquisitors back up the road smiling like pilgrims."

"Did I." I looked at her earnest face and searched for the last eleven days and found a locked drawer. "Tell me something true about us. Anything. I appear to have mislaid us."

She knelt in the dirt beside me, and she talked—about the night we'd drunk plum brandy and I'd taught her to bottle a memory safely, about how Ferro cheated at cards and I let him think I didn't notice, about the goose, whose name, apparently, was Duchess, and whose grudge against me was legendary and mutual. She rebuilt my eleven days brick by brick out of her own remembering, secondhand history, a life returned to me as hearsay.

It helped. That's the terrible truth—it helped, and I understood immediately how seductive it would become, letting other people remember me. A vessel filling itself with borrowed accounts of itself. I'd seen where that road ended. I'd bottled enough hollowed-out clients to know.

* *

That night I sat on the porch steps with a bottled charm in my hand—one of my own stock, a sunset over the Vell harbor I'd lifted from a dockhand years ago and never sold—and I thought hard about arithmetic.

One year for six soldiers. Ilsevet wanted me emptied entirely, a blank key to break a usurper's lock. Vane wanted my heart's-blood for his machine. Everyone had a plan for spending me. The only question, I was beginning to see—the question the whole bloody prophecy turned on—was who would get to choose the price.

And whether, when the final bill came due, there would be anything left worth keeping.

Wren came out and sat beside me, at the exact distance he'd calibrated, and said nothing, which from Wren was a paragraph.

"There's something else," I said finally. "When it happened. When the year went. For half a second, I felt the shelves—all of them. Everything I carry. And underneath the stolen memories, at the very bottom, there was something I've never touched. Something that isn't cargo." I turned the harbor sunset charm over in my fingers. "Something that's been waiting."

Wren went very still. "Waiting for what?"

I looked east, toward the hollow hill, where a exiled heir waited with a prophecy and a plan for my emptiness. I looked west, toward Vane's capital and its humming iron-and-bone hunger. Two crowns, one vessel, and at the bottom of me, something patient as a seed.

"I don't know," I said. "But I think it knows the way to the Ninefold Court."

Duchess the goose screamed at the moon, and somewhere up the salt road, six men without oaths were riding toward a story none of them remembered joining—and neither, soon, would I.
