## 12. What Wren Never Told

There is a particular kind of cowardice reserved for people who have done nothing wrong except everything, and I had been practicing it for three days by the time Casper asked.

We were camped in the reeds where the marsh road gave up pretending to be a road, a fire small enough to hide under a cupped hand, and Casper was watching me across the flames with that expression they'd perfected over years of selling other people's feelings — the one that said *I know exactly what you're feeling because I've bottled it before, and yours is leaking.*

"You've been rehearsing something," they said.

"I have not."

"You have. You do the thing with your jaw when you're composing. Left side." They tapped their own jaw to demonstrate. "You've been composing since Ashenford. Whatever it is, it's longer than an apology and shorter than a confession, which puts it somewhere in the neighborhood of a eulogy."

I fed a stick to the fire because it seemed useful for my hands to be doing something and my hands are rarely honest on their own. "It's not a eulogy."

"No?"

"It's more of a—" I stopped. Started again. This was the problem with loving a memory-thief: they could read you like a ledger, and worse, they knew which entries were forged. "Casper."

"That's my name. You've used it before. Usually before lying to me."

"Before *telling you things you didn't want to hear,*" I corrected, "which is different in the way that a knife is different from a spoon."

"And yet both end up in your back." They leaned back against a fallen willow, arms crossed, waiting. The firelight did things to their face I had no right to find beautiful anymore, given what I was about to do to it. "Go on, then. Compose at me."

So I told them.

I told them all of it, or as much of it as I could get out in order, which is not how confessions work but is how mine came: badly, out of sequence, with digressions. That I serve the exiled heir of the Ninefold Court — that part they already knew, from the docks, from the night they'd nearly gutted me and taken my hand instead. But the rest of it, the part underneath, the part I'd been sitting on like a man warming himself on a chest full of vipers:

That Ilsevet's agents had tracked the Thorn for eleven years. That when the prophecy fragment surfaced — a stolen page, sold through three black markets before it reached us — showing a face that matched no one in the old court's records and everyone's nightmares, the heir had sent her best infiltrator into Vell to find it.

That her best infiltrator was a man who sold the sea for a living, charming, forgettable, good with tea.

That his orders were simple. *Find the Thorn. Confirm it. Deliver it to the Hollow Hill alive.*

*Or not at all.*

*Alive*, I told Casper, looking at the fire instead of at them, because the fire couldn't look back. *Or not at all. Those were the words. Not alive if possible. Not alive unless necessary. Alive or not at all, which meant my job was to bring you in breathing, and if breathing proved inconvenient—*

"To kill me," Casper said. Not a question. Their voice was very level, the levelness of someone holding something heavy with straight arms.

"To make sure you didn't fall into Vane's hands," I said, which was true and also a lie, the way a knife is different from a spoon. "The orders were framed around protecting the prophecy. A Thorn in Vane's machine ends the old bloodline forever. Dead in a ditch beats captured."

"But dead in a ditch still ends the Thorn."

"Yes."

The marsh made its noises around us — frogs, wind through the reeds, the distant argument of waterbirds — and none of it helped. I kept talking because stopping felt fatal.

"The first time I came to your shop," I said, "I'd already been watching you for two weeks. I knew your hours. I knew your suppliers. I knew about the pickpockets you feed off your own profits and pretend you don't. My brief was thorough. Your whole life was in a folder, Casper. You were a target with a bibliography."

They said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a heron made a sound like a door that needed oiling.

"The memories of the sea," I went on, quieter now, because this was the worst of it and the worst of it deserved quiet. "Three charms. First wave, deep dive, the storm. I didn't want them. I mean — I wanted them, they were lovely, but I bought them because buying them put me across a table from you for an hour while you talked about salt and light the way you talk about nothing else, with your whole face. That was the assignment. Get close. Learn the technique. Catalogue the tells. Report whether the Thorn could actually do what the prophecy claimed."

"You watched my hands," Casper said.

"I watched everything. That was the assignment too."

"And after?"

I looked up. They were still leaning against the willow, still composed, but their eyes had gone somewhere flat and far, the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't like.

"After," I said, "the assignment stopped being the reason I stayed. I know how that sounds. I know exactly how that sounds, because it's what every spy says when he's caught, and I've heard it, I've *used* it, in other lives, other cities. But it happens to be true, which is the cruelest joke of my career: I finally fell in love honestly, and the falling was the only dishonest thing about me."

"When?" they asked. "When did it stop being the assignment?"

I thought about it properly, because they deserved a proper answer and because the answer existed, precise as a date in a ledger.

"The second cup of tea," I said. "First visit. You refilled my cup without asking, and you got it wrong — too much leaf, water too hot — and you apologized for it like it mattered, like my opinion of your tea was a real thing worth protecting. And I remember thinking, *this person has spent their whole life making sure other people feel safe and seen and paid fairly for their grief, and nobody in this city has ever once done the same for them.* And I thought, *I'm going to be the one who does it.* And then, about four seconds later, I thought, *oh no.* Because I knew what I was there to do. And I did it anyway. For months. I let you—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "I let you think you were finally seen, when the seeing was surveillance. Every kindness I paid you was billable, Casper. I was the best customer you ever had and I never paid for anything."

Silence. The long kind. The kind with teeth.

Casper stood. Slowly, without hurry, the way they did everything — a thief's economy of motion, nothing wasted, nothing revealed. They looked down at me across the little fire, and their face wasn't angry. That was the terrible thing. It wasn't angry at all. It was sad in the specific way of someone confirming a suspicion they'd been carrying politely for weeks, the way you carry a package for a stranger, hoping they'll claim it so you can set it down.

"The storm memory," they said. "The third charm. Did you ever open it? After?"

"Yes."

"What did you see?"

"Casper—"

"*What did you see?*"

"A woman diving off a boat," I said. "Her sister's laugh from the deck above. Salt in her nose. The moment she decided the sea was the only honest thing left." I swallowed. "And I saw your face afterward, when you took the charm back from me and held it like it was warm. And I understood why you hoard these. Why you keep memories you never lived. Because your own were taken, and someone had to love something, even borrowed."

Something moved behind their eyes — not forgiveness, not its opposite. Recognition, maybe. Grief's dull cousin.

"You should have told me at the docks," they said. "When you confessed about Ilsevet. You chose the half-truth. You stood there and let me believe the worst version — that you were a court agent — because the worst version didn't include *alive or not at all.*"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the worst version was survivable," I said. "The truth was that I'd spent seven months deciding, every single day, whether I loved you more than I obeyed. And I couldn't answer it. And I thought if I never said the orders out loud, I'd never have to find out which one won."

"And now?"

"Now you've made me say it out loud," I said, "and I still don't know."

Casper nodded slowly. Then they crouched, took the bottle of river water from beside my pack — my pack, note, not theirs; they'd always known where I kept everything — and poured a careful measure over the fire. Steam hissed up between us like the last word in an argument neither of us wanted to finish.

"Thank you for telling me," they said.

"Don't." I stood too. "Don't thank me. Thanking me makes it worse."

"It's not thanks. It's bookkeeping." They slung their own pack over one shoulder, settled their cloak, and became, in the space of a breath, the person I'd first watched through a shop window two winters ago: complete, self-contained, needing no one. "You told the truth. That's a debt cleared. What it doesn't clear is the year I spent trusting you while you were cataloguing me. Those aren't the same debt, Wren. One was yours. The other one's mine — for trusting a client who lingered over tea. I knew better. Thieves know better."

"Where are you going?"

"For a walk."

"In the marshes. At night."

"I grew up invisible in a city that executes people like me," Casper said. "Marshes at night are a step *up*. Don't follow me. If you follow me, I'll know the obedience won, and then I'll have to decide what to do about that, and I'm tired, Wren. I'm tired of deciding things about you."

They walked out along the reed path, and the dark took them in stages — cloak, shoulders, the pale blur of a face turned briefly back toward the fire, though not toward me, never quite toward me — and then nothing but frogs and wind and the smell of cold water and rot, which is the marsh's idea of honesty.

I sat by the drowned fire for a long time.

Here is what I did not tell them, because there wasn't time, or because I'm a coward, or because some truths need a better stage than a mudflat: that I'd written to Ilsevet six weeks ago, from Ashenford, formally requesting release from the assignment. That I'd told her the Thorn was a person, not a weapon, and that persons don't get delivered. That her reply hadn't come yet, and that every morning since, I'd woken wondering whether the silence meant she was considering my request or dispatching someone faster than me.

That if her answer arrived and it was *not at all*, I would burn every bridge I had, starting with the one I was standing on, and I had told myself that proved the love won.

But tonight, watching the place where Casper had stopped existing into the dark, I understood the flaw in my arithmetic. Love versus obedience was the wrong contest. The real question was whether Casper would ever be able to look at me again without seeing the folder — their whole life reduced to a briefing document, annotated in my handwriting. I could defect a hundred times over and it wouldn't unwrite those margins.

I waited until the moon was high enough to silver the reeds, and then I broke my promise immediately, because I am a liar even when I'm trying not to be, and followed the path at a distance.

Not to persuade. Not to explain further. Just to keep them in sight until dawn, because *alive or not at all* had been my orders, and I had decided — finally, stupidly, too late to matter — that the orders were going to lose.

But the marsh had other ideas.

Half a mile along the causeway, I found Casper standing perfectly still at the edge of the black water, head tilted, listening. And out of the reeds ahead — lantern-light, many lanterns, moving in the disciplined pattern I'd drilled against for ten years — came riders wearing the grey and silver of Aldric Vane.

Inquisitors' colors.

And at their head, on a white horse incongruous as a swan in a sewer, sat a woman whose voice I'd know anywhere, gentle as a lullaby and twice as dangerous, calling across the water:

"Thorn of the Ninefold Court. And your shadow, Master Wren. How convenient. Seraphine Hale keeps excellent records, and hers say you've been a very naughty servant of a very dead king."

* * *

I want it noted, for whatever record survives this war, that my first thought was not for myself. It was: *she knows the name Wren, which means the court's rosters are compromised, which means Ilsevet's network is burning, which means the reply I've been dreading may never come at all.*

My second thought was for Casper, thirty feet ahead of me in the dark, frozen between the riders and the deep water, and whether they believed — after tonight, after everything — that I would spend myself freely to keep them breathing.

They didn't look back at me.

They looked at the water.

And I realized, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that they were weighing the marsh against the inquisitors, and finding it hard to say which side offered kinder odds — and that whichever choice they made next, I had taught them, over one long dishonest year, exactly how alone a person can feel while someone stands right behind them.

Hale dismounted. Drew no weapon. Smiled the smile that had emptied a hundred interrogation rooms.

"Walk toward me slowly," she called, gentle as ever, "or we can do this the memorable way. I understand you collect memories, Thorn. Let's see how many of yours survive the night."

Behind me, in the reeds, something else moved — something heavy, deliberate, and following the same trail I had.

Ilsevet's people had found us after all.

Whether they'd come as rescuers or executioners was, as ever with the old court, an open question.

And Casper, hearing them too, finally turned — and looked at me across twenty feet of black water and one broken trust, waiting to see which side I'd stand on, this time, in front of witnesses.
