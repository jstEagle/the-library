## 13. The Heir in the Hollow Hill

The hill swallowed us at dusk.

That's the only way I can describe it. One moment Wren and I were walking a shepherd's track through the last folds of the Carrow Fells, the marsh behind us and its leaking ghosts finally gone quiet, and the next the hillside opened like a mouth. No door, no seam, no torchlit tunnel—just a wall of gorse that rippled once, like water disturbed by a stone, and let us through.

"Charmwork," I said, because my hands had started shaking somewhere around the second hour of walking and I needed to say something that wasn't *I'm afraid*.

"Wardwork," Wren corrected. "Older. The kind they stopped teaching after Vane burned the colleges." He glanced back at me, and in the grey light his face did that thing it does—half apology, half invitation to trust him anyway. "You'll want to keep your stolen memories close in here. Some of them may... recognize their kin."

I didn't ask what he meant. I'd learned, over these weeks on the road, that asking Wren questions before he was ready to answer them only got you answers shaped like riddles, and I'd had enough riddles. I had a prophecy with my face in it. I had a year of my own childhood missing like a tooth from my jaw. I had an inquisitor's kindness haunting me worse than her cruelty would have.

Inside the hill, the dark smelled of cold stone and beeswax and, absurdly, lavender.

Torches caught one by one along the passage—not lit, exactly, but *remembering* fire, each flame arriving as if it had always been there and had merely been waiting to be noticed. The walls were worked stone, old work, the kind where every block bears a mason's mark worn nearly smooth. And set into those walls, at intervals, were niches, and in the niches stood charms.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Glass and bone and iron and river-clay, some glowing faintly, some dark as dead coals. Memories, bottled and shelved, the whole length of the passage—a library of other people's lives, arranged by hands long dust.

I stopped walking. I couldn't help it.

"Casper." Wren's voice came soft. "It's just storage."

"It's just—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "Wren, this is a *hoard*. This is what they say people like me do. What they say we dream of doing. A cave full of taken things."

"You're not a dragon, Casper."

"No," I said. "I'm worse. I'm the thing the dragon eats."

He didn't have an answer for that, which was honest of him. He put his hand between my shoulder blades—not pushing, just resting there, warm through my coat—and I let the touch carry me forward down the corridor of other people's yesterdays.

* * *

The heir received us in a hall carved from the hill's living rock, and I will grant Ilsevet this: they made no effort to look like a monarch.

No throne. No crown, though a circlet of black iron sat on a side table among teacups and maps, treated with all the reverence of a serving dish. No guards lined the walls—just three court mages with tired eyes, and a woman I took for a steward, and a brazier that smoked slightly, badly seasoned wood, because even exiled royalty apparently couldn't get good fuel this far from anywhere.

Ilsevet sat at a plain table with their boots off, which is either a gesture of humility or the confidence of someone who has never once needed to impress anyone. They were perhaps fifty, weathered, with the kind of face that had been handsome and had decided, somewhere along the way, that it had better uses than beauty. Their eyes were pale grey and they went straight past Wren as if he were furniture and settled on me with an attention so complete it felt physical, like being held up to a lamp.

"So," they said. "The Thorn."

"The thief," I said. "If we're being accurate. And if we're not being accurate, then I'd like to know who I'm talking to first."

Something moved at the corner of their mouth. Not quite a smile. Approval wearing a smile's clothing.

"Ilsevet of the Ninefold Court," they said. "Last of the line that ruled before Aldric Vane decided crowns should be won rather than inherited. You've heard of me, presumably."

"I've heard of the exile," I said carefully. "I've heard the free cities call you 'the rightful heir' when they're feeling generous and 'the pretender' when they're not. I've heard your name used to sell newspapers and recruit orphans." I nodded at Wren. "And I've heard you employ charming men to find lost property."

"Casper." Wren's voice had a warning note in it.

"No, let them speak," Ilsevet said mildly. "I'd rather be insulted accurately than flattered vaguely. Sit, thief. Drink. You've walked out of the marshes and you look like something the marshes gave back."

There was tea. There was bread that tasted of woodsmoke and honey, real honey, the first sweetness I'd had since Ashenford. I ate because refusing seemed like a strategy for fools—you don't negotiate well empty—but I kept my eyes moving. Exits. Sight lines. The mages' hands. Old habits. The habits of a person whose entire life is built on the assumption that everyone in any room might, at any moment, decide you're worth more as a commodity than as a guest.

Ilsevet watched me do it and, again, that almost-smile.

"You steal memories," they said. "Tell me how."

"Why?"

"Because everything depends on *how*, Thorn. Because the prophecy doesn't say 'the Thorn shall destroy the usurper.' Prophecies are lazy that way. It says the Thorn shall *unmake* him, and unmaking is a technical matter." They leaned forward. "So. How?"

I looked at Wren. He looked at his tea. Traitor.

"When I take a memory," I said, "I don't cut it out. People think that. They think of theft as removal, as a hole left behind. But that's clumsy work—that's what gets memory-thieves burned in Vane's purges, because a hole screams. What I do is..." I searched for the words, the ones I'd never said aloud to anyone, because saying them aloud made them real. "I persuade. A memory is a thing the mind holds onto. I offer it somewhere else to hold on. I make the remembering want to leave. And it comes across into the charm whole, rooted, still growing, still *theirs*. The person who sold me their first snowfall can't recall it anymore—but the snowfall itself never knew anything happened. It just changed gardens."

Silence in the hall. The brazier popped. Somewhere deeper in the hill, water dripped with the patience of geological time.

"That," said one of the mages, an old man with ink-stained fingers, "is not how charm-craft works."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. That's why they call it magic instead."

"And the reverse?" Ilsevet asked quietly. "Can you give back?"

"I've never tried. Giving back would mean persuading a memory to root in soil that already knows it was torn away. It would mean the person having to hold on to something that remembers leaving them." My throat tightened. "Maybe. With time. With consent. Nobody's ever asked me to try."

"Nobody," said Ilsevet, "has ever understood what you are."

"There it is." I set down my cup. "The pitch. Go on, then. Your agent spent months buying sea-memories off me and learning my hands. Your mages have been circling me since before I knew there was a circle. Say what you brought me here to say."

Ilsevet rose. Crossed to a chest against the far wall—an iron-bound thing, old, scarred—and drew from it a book.

I knew the book. Of course I knew the book. I had seen it once, by candle-stub light, in a forbidden archive beneath the Ministry of Purity, while bells rang alarm below and my heart tried to climb out through my ribs. The Ninefold Prophecy, vellum and gold leaf, a century older than me, open to a page where a painted figure stood amid thorns with my own face looking out of the illumination, patient and terrible.

They laid it on the table between the teacups. Domestic. Horrifying.

"Read it," Ilsevet said.

"I read it once. It ruined my life."

"Read it *properly*. In the Ministry you saw the censored copy—the one Vane's predecessors allowed the public archive, trimmed of everything dangerous. This is the full text. Read the ninth verse."

My hands weren't steady. I noticed that from a great distance, the way you notice weather. I turned the pages—vellum whispering, the illuminations shifting in the torchlight the way painted things shouldn't—and found the ninth verse, and read it aloud because Ilsevet waited and silence seemed worse.

"*When the Thorn stands crowned in others' hours,*" I read, "*and wears a hundred lives it never lived, then shall it choose: to keep the garden and let the false king keep his throne of borrowed blood—or to pour out every season it has stolen, till nothing remains but the bare branch, and by that emptiness break the root he feeds upon.*"

I read it twice more. The words didn't change. Words are loyal that way, right up until they ruin you.

"Borrowed blood," I said. "Vane's claim—"

"Is fabricated." Ilsevet said it flatly, without triumph, which somehow made it worse. "Aldric Vane holds the Ninefold Court because the Court's oldest binding—the blood-oath laid on the throne itself, the magic that makes rebellion impossible and obedience instinctive—recognizes royal blood. His own line has none. So he stole it. He has been feeding the throne the memories of true royals for fifteen years. Bottled charm after bottled charm, poured into the oath, until the binding believes he is what he drank." They tapped the book. "The machine you may have heard rumors of. Iron and bone. It works both directions—it can take a life's memory and it can *implant* a claim. Vane is not a king, Casper. He is a man wearing a costume stitched from murdered memories. And the blood-oath, which is older and stranger than any of us, cannot tell the difference."

"Then expose him. Show the oath—"

"The oath can't be shown. It can only be *fed* or *starved*. And here is the part you've already guessed, because you're cleverer than you let yourself be." Ilsevet met my eyes, and for the first time the almost-smile fell away entirely, and what was underneath was old and tired and sorry. "The Thorn's power runs opposite to Vane's. Where he forces memories in, you persuade memories out. If the Thorn stands before the blood-oath and pours out every stolen season—every life it carries, given freely, all at once—the oath will taste truth for the first time in fifteen years. It will feel the difference between a gift and a theft. And in knowing the difference, it will reject everything Vane has fed it. His claim doesn't need to be exposed. It needs to be *emptied against*. You wouldn't destroy him, Thorn. You'd unmake the lie he's made of himself. The throne would go silent. Crownless. And no army marching under a crownless banner keeps its nerve for long."

The hall was very quiet. Even the drip of water in the deep seemed to pause, listening.

"And the cost," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Let's not skip that part. The cost."

"You heard the verse. *Every season it has stolen.*"

"All of them." I was on my feet now—I didn't remember standing. "Every charm I've ever lifted. Every life I carry. The merchant's snowfall. The fisherman's wedding. The child in the Ashenford quarter who traded me her mother's laugh because she couldn't bear to hold it alone—all of it, gone. Poured out into a throne-room full of soldiers so they'd stop saluting the right flag."

"Yes."

"And what's left?" I heard my own voice crack and hated it. "What's left of the vessel when you empty it? Because I'll tell you what's left of the *people* Vane's machine empties. I've seen them. Hollow-eyed things that eat when you tell them to eat and sleep when you tell them to sleep and answer to whatever name you put on them. You're not asking me to pay a price. You're asking me to stop existing politely."

"Not stop," said the ink-fingered mage gently. "Empty."

"Empty *is* stopping. Empty is just stopping with better manners."

Wren spoke for the first time in an hour. "Casper—"

"Don't." I pointed at him without looking at him, because looking at him would have been a mistake, because his face was doing the thing again, the apology-thing, and I could not afford the apology-thing right now. "You knew. Both of you. You've known since the marshes, probably since the docks. You've been walking me toward this hill this whole time, this whole *road*, every kindness and every anchor and every night I taught myself to trust the sound of your breathing—was that the assignment too? Was the road the assignment?"

"My orders were to bring you here," Wren said quietly. "What happened on the road was mine. All of it was mine. I'd take an oath on it if oaths meant anything to you."

"They don't. Oaths are just memories with costumes on."

"Casper." Ilsevet's voice cut clean. "Look at me."

I looked. It was a mistake. There was no cruelty in those pale eyes, no ambition even—only a grief that had been carried so long it had become structural, load-bearing grief, the kind that holds up a person the way beams hold up a roof.

"You think I don't know what I'm asking," they said. "I am the last of a line that was hunted for its blood. I have spent fifteen years watching my name be used as a weapon by people who never met me. I have wanted my throne back with a hunger that frightened me, and I have had to sit with that hunger every single day and ask whether I was any different from the man who took everything." They pushed back from the table. "So hear this, Thorn, and hear it clearly. I am not asking you to die for my crown. I am telling you what the prophecy says must happen, and I am telling you the truth, which is more than anyone else in this war will give you. But I will not pretend the choice is mine to make. It never was. It's yours. It has only ever been yours."

"Generous," I said. "You've built yourself a lovely position. Whatever I choose, you're blameless. If I refuse, you mourned honestly. If I accept, you freed the realm." I picked up the prophecy book, felt its weight, its awful patience. "Do you know what my own mother's face looks like? I don't. Someone took those memories before I was old enough to hold them—someone emptied *me* first, years before I ever learned to steal. I have spent my whole life carrying other people's yesterdays because I wasn't allowed any of my own. And now the heir of the Ninefold Court sits me down over tea and asks me to give up the only self I've ever managed to build—stolen, patched, borrowed, *mine*—so that another crown can sit easier on another head."

I set the book down. Carefully. Gently. The way you put down something you'd like to throw through a window.

"No," I said.

The word landed in the hall like a dropped glass.

"I won't be your vessel. I won't be Vane's vessel. I'm done being the container other people pour their ambitions into. Find another Thorn. Find another way. Burn the machine, kill the man, argue with the oath—I don't care. But you don't get my hundred lives. They're not yours to spend. They're barely mine, but they're *barely mine*, and that's more than anyone's ever offered me, and I am not giving them up so the world can go back to pretending thrones are sacred."

Nobody moved. The mages looked at Ilsevet. Ilsevet looked at me for a long, long moment—and then did the last thing I expected.

They bowed. Slightly. From the neck. An heir of the Ninefold Court, bowing to a thief in a room that smelled of bad firewood.

"Then we have a problem," Ilsevet said, "because I believe you. And because I also believe this: Aldric Vane has read the same ninth verse. He has had fifteen years and an army of scholars to read it. He knows what you are, and he knows what you could do to him, and he knows you have not yet refused anyone but me." They straightened. "Which means, thief, that the most dangerous man in the Nine Realms is going to stop sending agents to bring you in alive."

The brazier guttered. And in the pause that followed, from somewhere far above us—through the gorse, through the wardwork, down through all that patient stone—came a sound I had hoped never to hear again.

Bells. Many bells. The alarm-signal of the free cities, ringing from a direction that could only mean one thing.

Vane's legions had found the hollow hill.
