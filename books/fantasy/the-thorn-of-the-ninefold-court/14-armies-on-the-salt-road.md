## 14. Armies on the Salt Road

The salt road burns for eleven miles, and I count every one of them, because counting is the last honest thing left to me.

From the ridge above Harrow's Bend you can see the whole of it: the legions moving east in three columns like a wound being drawn shut with a hot iron. The supply wagons first, then the pike blocks in their gray lacquer, then the memory-machine on its carriage of bone-white timber, swaying gently, almost tenderly, the way a nurse rocks a cradle. Behind them, torches touch every farmhouse and waystation along the salt road, because Vane does not requisition what he can make an example of.

"Beautiful, isn't it," says Captain Orla Dree beside me, her breath fogging in the dawn cold. She means it. That's the thing about Vane's officer corps that no one outside it understands—we are not monsters. We are believers. Monsters would at least be simpler to leave.

"Gorgeous," I say. "I've never felt so patriotic."

Dree laughs, because she thinks I'm joking, and this is the professional hazard of my life: I have spent ten years learning to lie with my whole face, and now even the truth sounds like wit.

She hands me the field orders. I read them twice, though once would have been enough to turn my stomach. *Advance on Ashenford. Secure the Thorn. Priority target alive; collateral expenditure authorized at Inquisitor-General's discretion.* Collateral expenditure. Somewhere between here and Ashenford there are forty thousand people who have just been reclassified as a rounding error, and the man writing the arithmetic is the same man whose claim to the Ninefold Court rests—this is the part only I know, only me and a ledger bound in black calfskin—on memories that were never his.

*Collateral expenditure authorized.* I fold the orders. I smile at Dree. I tell her I'll ride ahead to coordinate the inquisitorial vanguard, which is true, if by *vanguard* you mean a mule, a stolen map, and a woman planning to commit the largest act of treason in the history of the Ninefold Throne before lunch.

* * *

Let me explain how an Inquisitor-General defects, since presumably no one has ever done it gracefully and I see no reason to be the first.

You cannot simply walk out of Vane's army. There are loyalty charms woven into the officers' oath-bands—not the crude ones we feed to prisoners, but fine work, old work, work that whispers *the throne is eternal* into your blood when you sleep. I have worn mine for nine years. I have watched it do its quiet surgery on men I liked. And I have known for six days—I will come to that—how Vane intends to use the Thorn once he has it, and knowing that has made the charm feel less like a duty and more like a leash on something that used to be a person.

So on the night before the advance, I go to the quartermaster's tent, where the oath-band spares are kept in an iron chest, and I do the thing I have rehearsed in my head two hundred times: I take out my knife, I cut the band from my own wrist—it comes away with a ribbon of skin, and I will not describe the sound—and I drop it into the brazier where the quartermaster melts down damaged goods.

The whisper dies. What replaces it is worse and better: silence, and the full weight of everything I've done wearing that band.

Then I steal a mule. An Inquisitor-General of the Ninefold Court, reduced to stealing a mule named Buttercup. If the chroniclers ever write this up, I would prefer they omit the name. They will not. Chroniclers never spare you the humiliating details; it's the whole reason I burned so many of their archives.

* * *

Six days earlier—the night the machine ate Corvin Hale, though no relation, despite what the garrison gossiped—I stood in the command pavilion and watched Vane feed a living man's past into iron and bone.

I had seen it before. That was the horror of it: not the spectacle but the familiarity. The machine needs a mage's memories the way a furnace needs coal, and Vane has kept it fed for years, always with condemned criminals, always with paperwork in order, always with me signing the warrants. I told myself for nine years that the condemned were guilty of something. Treason, heresy, unlicensed magic. There was always a crime on the record. The record was the point. The record let me sleep.

Corvin's crime, according to the warrant I signed myself, was falsifying his licensure. His actual crime, as far as anyone could discover, was being a hedge-witch in Duncarrow who cured a tax collector's daughter of lung-rot without charging enough to seem suspicious.

Vane stood beside the machine with his hand flat against the bone housing, and he did not watch the man. He watched the readout dials. And when Corvin's childhood came up through the copper veins—first snowfall, I remember thinking, it's always first snowfall or the sea, people hoard such small weather—Vane said, softly, with real tenderness:

"There. Do you see, Seraphine? A whole self, and none of it load-bearing."

And then he did the thing that broke my faith in a single motion, the way you break a stick over your knee: he drew a phial from his coat, uncorked it, and drank.

Not the machine. Him. He took a mouthful of another man's childhood the way a man takes wine at a tasting, considering it, rolling it against the palate. And I saw his face change—saw grief move across it that was not his grief, saw love that was not his love—and then he set the phial down and became himself again, smooth as a lake closing over a stone.

"You look troubled, Inquisitor-General," he said.

"I'm wondering about the ethics, my lord."

"The ethics." He smiled. Vane has a beautiful smile; it's the most dangerous thing about him, and he knows it, and he maintains it the way other men maintain a sword. "The old court built its throne on exactly this, Seraphine. Did you think the royal line's *divine right* was anything but bookkeeping? Nine generations of kings, each one drinking the last. Each one remembering coronations that never happened, ancestors who never lived, oaths sworn by ghosts. The difference between us and them—" he tapped the machine's bone housing, affectionately, horribly, "—is that I keep honest records."

He keeps honest records. That is the sentence that has been eating me alive for six days. Because he does. Because the machine logs everything, and because Vane—arrogant as a cathedral—keeps those logs in his private archive under his own seal, certain no one would dare read them.

I dared. I am telling you I dared, and what I found in the logs is the reason there is a half-dead woman on a mule named Buttercup riding ahead of three legions toward a city full of people who have every reason to shoot her.

The logs say this: Aldric Vane's claim to the Ninefold Court rests on a memory of investiture—a crowning, witnessed by nine lords, sealed with the old rites—that never occurred. It is a purchased memory. A bottled, stolen charm, drunk by an ambitious soldier twenty-two years ago, bought from a memory-thief whose name the records redact but whose technique the records describe in detail. Bottled memories leave signatures. I have spent a decade reading them.

The signature on Vane's founding memory belongs to the same hand, the same craft, the same thief who—by every report that has crossed my desk this season—is currently running east ahead of my legions with sea-colored eyes and a lover with a face out of a century-old prophecy.

Casper Whitlock steals memories to survive. So, it turns out, does the king. The difference is that Whitlock sells snowfalls to lonely merchants, and Vane sold himself a crown, and one of them is going to answer for it.

Guess which one my army has orders to bring in alive.

* * *

Three days of hard riding, and I want it noted for posterity that Buttercup is a liar and a coward and I would die for her.

The marsh country begins south of the salt road, where the burned farmland gives way to reeds and drowned alders and water that smells of iron. I leave the road on the second night, because the road is for armies and I am no longer an army; I am a middle-aged woman with a calfskin ledger sewn into her coat lining and a wrist wound that has gone the interesting color of a bruise on a corpse. The fever started that night too. Of course it did. The body keeps its own ledger, and mine has decided to present its accounts now, at the worst possible moment, with compound interest.

I navigate by the things Wren's network taught me to look for—reed-cut stumps stacked in threes, a fish trap hung too high, a cairn of white stones that means *friendlies within a day's walk*. Yes, I know their signals. I built half of them. Ten years of hunting the resistance teaches you its grammar, and the bitter joke of my career is that I am probably the only person alive fluent in both sides of this war.

On the fourth day the fever wins for a while. I remember pieces: Buttercup standing very patiently while I vomited into the reeds. A heron watching me with the contempt herons reserve for the dying. Singing, badly, some marching song from my cadet years, and realizing mid-verse that I couldn't remember why I'd joined, only that I'd been seventeen and the throne had seemed like a religion and I had wanted so badly to believe in something larger than my father's debt.

That's the confession, then, since we're doing confessions: I didn't become an inquisitor out of cruelty or ambition. I became one because I was poor and brilliant and the throne was the only door open, and I walked through it, and behind the door was a machine made of iron and bone, and I signed the warrants anyway, because the alternative was becoming fuel for it, and I was afraid.

Fear. That's the whole empire, really. Vane doesn't rule by magic; the magic is just the lock. He rules because everyone inside is more afraid of what's outside than ashamed of what they've done.

I woke on the fifth morning with frost in my hair and a crossbow bolt embedded in the alder trunk four inches from my ear.

"Don't move," said a voice above me, young, female, shaking slightly. "Don't—Inquisitor-General Seraphine Hale, by the Ninefold, you're *her*, you're on the posters—"

"I'm aware," I said. My voice came out like gravel in a bucket. "The posters flatter my nose. Are you going to shoot me or arrest me? I'd rather the shooting. Less paperwork."

Silence. Then, from somewhere to my left: "Tamsin said don't kill her till she's talked." A pause. "Also she said if she's lying, kill her after."

"Tamsin," I croaked, "sounds like a sensible child."

"She's twelve and she remembers everything she steals, so yes." The crossbow lowered an inch. "Why should we believe anything you say?"

"Because," I said, and pulled the ledger from my coat lining, and held it up with both hands like an offering, which is precisely what it was, "I have brought you the usurper's own handwriting, his own seals, and proof that his crown is a stolen charm. Also I appear to be dying, and dying women make terrible liars. Everything hurts too much to bother."

* * *

They carried me the last day on a litter, which I found undignified until the fever explained that walking was not among my remaining talents. The resistance camp sits in a drowned quarry south of Ashenford, half-sunk in black water, reachable only by paths the twins know and I pretend I didn't help map. When they lifted me ashore, the first face I saw belonged to Casper Whitlock.

Up close, the Thorn looks disappointingly ordinary. Tired eyes, windburned cheeks, the hands of someone who works for a living—quick hands, though. I had read those hands' signature on a hundred confiscated charms, and here they were attached to a person who looked like they hadn't slept in a month, standing next to a tall man—Wren, the agent, the liar, the lover—who had moved between me and the camp's archers the moment I appeared, and had not entirely moved away since.

"So," Casper said. "The Inquisitor-General. In the flesh. Half-dead, they said."

"Half-dead, fully treasonous, and bearing gifts." I tried to sit up and discovered my ribs had opinions. "Your Grace," I added, because if you're going to commit treason, commit it with style, "I believe I've been hunting you for ten years, and I owe you an apology of approximately nine years and eleven months' duration."

Wren's hand went to his knife. Casper caught his sleeve—barely glanced, barely touched, and he stopped. I filed that away. Whatever is broken between these two, it bends around each other like light around a mass.

"Why?" Casper asked simply. Not *why defect*, not *why here*. Just: why. The question underneath all the others.

"Because I read the logs," I said. "Because your king drinks stolen childhoods with his dinner and calls it honest bookkeeping. Because the machine that ate a hedge-witch named Corvin last week is coming to Ashenford, and its final component isn't iron." I looked at Casper's hands. At the faint silver scarring where a charm had been broken off the skin, once, badly. "It's heart's-blood. Yours. He doesn't mean to execute the Thorn, Whitlock. He means to *harvest* it. Every memory you've ever taken, every power you carry—he wants it fed through the machine and poured into his claim, so that no one, ever, not in a hundred years, can prove his crown is false. You're not a fugitive to him. You're an ingredient."

The silence in that drowned quarry went on long enough for me to hear the water lapping the quarry walls, and the distant, very faint sound—so faint it might have been thunder, except that thunder doesn't come from the east in columns—of three legions burning their way up the salt road toward everything these people love.

Casper Whitlock looked at the ledger in my hands for a long moment. Then at Wren. Then, terribly, calmly, at me.

"Then we'd better make sure," they said, "there's nothing left in me worth harvesting."

And I understood, with the clarity that fever grants and mercy does not, exactly what they meant to pay—and that the proof I'd carried bleeding through the marshes was only the beginning of the price this war intended to charge.

Behind us, far to the east, the sky over the salt road glowed the color of a forge, and the glow was moving closer.
