## 15. Found Family, Found Purpose

Ashenford smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and, underneath it all, the particular sourness of a city pretending it wasn't afraid. I'd come to know that smell intimately over the years—Vell wore it like perfume. But Ashenford's version had a stubbornness to it. A *we're still here* baked into the brick.

I stood on the wall above the refugee quarter with a cup of something the twins swore was tea and I suspected was punishment, watching the salt road unspool toward the horizon. Somewhere out there, past the marshes and the burnt farms, Vane's legions were marching with the patience of men who believed history was on their side.

"Brooding again?" Pip said, materializing at my elbow the way she did—no footsteps, no warning, just a small girl with sharp eyes and a satchel of other people's pasts. She was eleven, maybe twelve, and she remembered everything she stole, which made her either the most dangerous thief in the free cities or the saddest. Possibly both.

"I'm strategizing," I said.

"You're brooding. Your face does this thing." She demonstrated, which was insulting, because she did it well. "Like you've bitten something and you're too polite to spit it out."

"That's my *thinking* face."

"It's the same face." She sipped her own cup and grimaced. "This is definitely not tea."

Below us, the courtyard of the old granary had become a training ground. Ilsevet's mages moved through their forms in the grey morning light—seven of them, ragged and underfed and glowing faintly at the fingertips, like candles someone had tried to snuff and failed. The disgraced court mage herself stood in the center, silver-haired and imperious, correcting a boy's stance with the end of her walking stick and the tone of a woman who had once corrected kings.

I was supposed to be down there. That was the arrangement. The Thorn trains, the Thorn learns control, the Thorn becomes a weapon we can aim at the usurper instead of a disaster we're all bracing around. Everyone said it politely. Nobody said *disaster* out loud except Pip, who said it constantly, because Pip had the survival instinct of a honey badger and the tact to match.

"You could just talk to him," she said.

"Don't start."

"I'm just saying. He stands over there." She pointed with her cup. Wren was at the edge of the courtyard, ostensibly sharpening a knife, actually watching me with the careful, hungry, guilty attention he'd been wearing for two weeks now—ever since the marshes, ever since he'd told me the truth and I'd walked into the fog alone and come back with frost in my lungs and a decision in my pocket. "And you stand over here. And the whole camp has to watch you two orbit each other like embarrassed moons."

"Pip."

"Yes?"

"Drink your punishment tea."

She grinned and drained it, and I went down to train.

* * *

Ilsevet taught magic the way a butcher teaches knife work: precisely, unsentimentally, and with the assumption that you would eventually cut yourself.

"The Thorn does not *push*," she said, circling me while the other mages watched from the walls of the practice yard. She'd cleared it for the lesson, which I appreciated. What I did was not something you wanted an audience for. "Everyone who has ever tried to use a power like yours has tried to push. To seize. To *take*. That is the thief's instinct, yes? But you are not a thief in this. You are—" she searched for the word, her accent thickening with her irritation, "—a door. Doors do not push. Doors *open*."

"And what happens when the door opens?"

"Whatever is on the other side walks through." She stopped in front of me, and her eyes—pale as skimmed milk—held mine. "In the marsh, you unmade a year of your own past to turn three soldiers. You felt it go. Describe it."

I could have lied. I was good at lying; it had kept me alive in Vell for a decade. But Ilsevet had a way of looking at you that made lies feel like showing up to a duel with a butter knife.

"Like water finding a hole," I said. "Like I'd been carrying a bucket with a crack in it and I finally looked down and the floor was wet. I didn't feel it leave. I just noticed what was missing. My mother's voice. I don't have—" I stopped. Adjusted. "I didn't have a mother's voice anyway. But I had *a* voice, one I'd borrowed from a fishwife in Vell, and now when I try to reach for it there's just a shape where it was."

The yard was quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, a gull screamed its opinion.

"Good," Ilsevet said. "You noticed. Most do not notice until they have lost the load-bearing walls." She tapped my sternum with the walking stick, gently. "Your self, Casper Whitlock, is a house built from stolen furniture. When you open the door, the house chooses what to pay. You do not choose. So we will practice paying *small*."

She had me stand before a barrel of rainwater and open the door a crack—just a crack—toward it. Nothing lived in the water. Nothing remembered. The theory was that I could practice the *shape* of the power without a price, like a musician fingering scales on a stringless instrument.

It didn't work that way, of course. Nothing in my life had ever worked the way it was supposed to.

The door opened, and the barrel *noticed* me. The water didn't have memories, but it had a kind of—attention. A long, patient, mineral patience, the patience of rain that had fallen on the mountains before there were names for mountains. It poured into me through the crack, cold and vast, and for a moment I was every storm that had ever crossed the Ashenford coast, and then Ilsevet's stick cracked across my knuckles and the door slammed shut and I was on my knees in the yard, gasping, my nose bleeding freely.

"Small," she said, "means *small*."

"That was small," I managed.

"That was a keyhole. You kicked the door off its hinges because the water was *lonely*." She crouched, and her voice lost some of its edge. "Everything wants to be remembered, child. Even rain. Especially rain. That is the trap of your gift. The world will pour itself into you gladly, and you will be very full, and you will be very empty, both at once. Now. Again. And this time, when it comes, you *thank it* and you *close the door*."

I tried again. Eleven times. On the twelfth, I managed it—a sip of the water's patience, held for three heartbeats, released with something like courtesy. My nose didn't even bleed.

From the edge of the yard, I felt Wren's attention like sunlight through a window. I didn't look. If I looked, I'd have to decide what my face was going to do, and I hadn't decided yet. It had been two weeks and I still hadn't decided. There was a version of me that had forgiven him in the marsh, the moment he'd said *it began as an assignment* and then, worse, quieter, *and then it didn't*. There was a version of me that was going to make him say it a hundred more times before I let him off the gallows he'd built himself.

I was mostly the second version. But the first version kept leaving notes around my head, and I kept finding them, and it was very annoying.

* * *

The war council convened that evening in the granary's map room, which was a generous name for a room containing one map, two candles, and a table that wobbled whenever anyone gestured, which was constantly.

Ilsevet stood at the map. The twins—Sable and Sorrel, identical down to their smirks—flanked the salt road with their fingers. Seraphine Hale, ex-inquisitor, still bruised yellow-green along her jaw from her escape, sat with her ledger open like a scripture she no longer believed in but couldn't stop reading.

"They'll come up the salt road," Sorrel said. "Three days, maybe four. Vane doesn't do subtle."

"He doesn't need to," Sable said. "Twenty thousand against our four. Subtle is for people who might lose."

"He *might* lose," Seraphine said quietly, and everyone looked at her, because Seraphine saying something hopeful was like a cat filing a tax return. She turned a page in her ledger. "I served him for six years. I know how he thinks. He believes the Thorn is an object. A relic to be seized. He will commit his center early and hold his reserves for the machine." Her finger touched the ledger. "He thinks he's hunting a thing. He isn't hunting a thing. He's hunting a *person who chooses*. That's the flaw in every plan he has ever made. He cannot model choice."

"Flattering," I said. "Also, four against twenty thousand."

"Five," said Pip, from the doorway, where she had absolutely been eavesdropping, because of course she had.

"Go to bed, Pip," Sable said.

"You're planning a battle in the building where I sleep. This is *my* business." She came in, all elbows and defiance, and planted herself at the table. "The sewers under the east quarter connect to the salt road culvert. I've been through them. The grate's rusted through. If they breach the walls, that's how we get people out. I know the way."

"You're not fighting," Sorrel said.

"I'm not *fighting*. I'm *routing*." She said it like the distinction was obvious and everyone else was slow. "Somebody has to know where the holes are. That's always been my job. That's the job."

There was a silence, the particular silence of adults realizing a child has out-argued them. Ilsevet broke it with a sigh that seemed to come up from her boots.

"She's right," the old mage said. "And she'll do it whether we permit it or not, which means she does it with our knowledge or without it. Put her on the sewers. Pair her with someone."

"I'll—" I started.

"You," Ilsevet said, "will be on the wall, being a door, at the moment the army arrives, and every moment after, until you have nothing left to open doors with. You will not be in a sewer." Her milk-pale eyes held mine. "You are the reason they are coming, Thorn. You are also the only reason they might leave. We do not spend the reason in a culvert."

It was decided, more or less. Pip scowled, was overruled, and extracted a compromise: she'd map the route with the twins tomorrow, daylight only, and she'd wear the whistle Sable gave her, and she'd come back before dark. She agreed to all of it with the sincerity of a professional liar, which, to be fair, she was.

I caught her at the door on the way out.

"Pip."

She turned, wary, expecting a lecture.

"When you go down there tomorrow—come back." I didn't know how to say the rest of it. I was bad at this. Ten years of charm-selling had made me fluent in everyone's feelings except my own. "There are people in this camp who'd be worse off without you. I'm one of them. Come back."

Her face did something complicated and young. "You sound like Wren," she said, and ducked out before I could decide whether that was a knife or a gift.

* * *

They came back late. After dark. Without Pip.

I knew before anyone told me. I was on the wall—I spent a lot of time on the wall those days, watching the salt road, practicing *small* on the wind, which had a memory like a gossip—and I saw the twins come through the gate at a dead run, and I saw their faces, and the bottom dropped out of my stomach with the particular speed of a floor that has been waiting all your life to betray you.

"Sewer grate," Sorrel gasped. "She went ahead. She does this, she—she said she'd only be a minute—"

"A patrol," Sable said. "Vane outriders, ahead of the column. They're using the culvert for shelter. She went in the far end and they're between her and us."

The camp was already moving—Ilsevet shouting, mages spilling into the yard, someone ringing the assembly bell—but I was already over the wall. I heard Wren shout my name behind me, and I didn't stop, and I heard him curse, and then I heard his boots behind mine, and I didn't stop for that either.

The culvert mouth was a black gash in the embankment, breathing cold air. Inside: shouting. A man's voice, hard and amused, and then Pip's voice, high and furious and *not crying*, because Pip didn't cry, Pip bit.

I went in.

It was dark as a pocket. I had a knife and no plan, which was, historically, my favorite combination. There were four of them—outriders, young, tired, one holding a lantern that threw everyone's shadows huge against the wet brick. They had Pip against the wall, and she had someone's dagger in her fist, and there was blood on her sleeve that I desperately needed to not be hers.

"Well, look at this," the lantern one said, as I stepped into the light. "Reinforcements."

Here is what I should have done: opened the door. Small. Careful. Turned their loyalty like I'd turned the soldiers' on the salt road, paid the price in private, gone home hollow but whole.

Here is what I actually did: I heard Pip snarl *let go of me* and something in my chest tore loose, and I opened the door *wide*.

The culvert filled with me. With everything I'd ever carried—the fishwife's laugh, the merchant's snow, the dying man's first love, the rain's mineral patience, a thousand borrowed hours all straining toward the light—and the four soldiers turned like weathercocks in a gale, their certainty pouring out of them, their orders dissolving into a confusion so total one of them sat down in the water. And the price came due. I felt it go in chunks. A name. A face. The taste of a specific bread from a specific morning. My own—

And then Wren was there.

I don't know how he'd kept pace. It didn't matter. What mattered was that the last two soldiers hadn't turned fast enough—one still had Pip's arm, and the other had a crossbow coming up, and Wren went through them like something out of a story his mother shouldn't have told him. Knife work, close and brutal and beautiful, and I came back to myself enough to catch the crossbow man's wrist and open the door *just* for him, a crack, a sip, and his eyes went soft and lost and he sat down next to his friend.

And then it was just the two of us, back to back in the dark, breathing hard, four soldiers sitting in the water of a sewer looking like men waking from a dream they'd pay for later, and Pip pressed against the wall with her stolen dagger still up, watching us with enormous eyes.

We didn't speak. There was no time and no need. Wren's back found mine—warm through the wet cloth, solid, *known*—and we turned together, a slow wheel, covering each other's blind sides out of a grammar we'd never studied. When he stepped left, I was already there. When I dropped low, his blade passed over my head like it had been scheduled. It was the closest thing to a conversation we'd had in two weeks, and it was more honest than any we'd had with words. *I'm sorry* was in the way he covered my flank. *I know* was in the way I covered his. *Again* was in the wheel of us, turning, turning, until the culvert was quiet.

Pip broke it, because Pip broke everything.

"You're both bleeding," she said, "and one of you is crying, and it's not me."

I touched my face. Wet. Could have been the culvert water. Wasn't.

"Names," I said, and my voice came out strange. "I lost some names."

"Mine's Pip," she said instantly, gripping my sleeve with a small, fierce hand. "Pip. Say it."

"Pip."

"And his?"

I looked at Wren. He looked back, and there was terror in his face, real terror, the terror of a man watching a price being paid on his behalf.

"His name is Wren," Pip said. "Wren. Say it."

"Wren," I said, and held on to it like a rope in floodwater. "Wren. I'm not losing that one. That one stays."

"Good," Pip said, with the finality of an eleven-year-old who has solved you. "Now let's go home, because I'm bleeding, and if you tell the twins I went ahead alone I will invent memories about both of you and sell them to the camp."

* * *

They stitched Pip's arm by lamplight—shallow cut, she announced, barely a scratch, she'd had worse falling out of trees—and the twins took turns scolding her with a ferocity that was entirely love wearing armor. Ilsevet looked at me for a long moment, taking inventory of what was missing, and whatever she saw made her jaw tighten.

"You opened it wide," she said. "In a sewer. With no anchor."

"There was a child."

"Yes." She sighed. "There usually is. That is how they get you." She pressed a bottle of something into my hands—smelling salts, or courage, or both. "Sleep. Tomorrow we count what you have left, and we plan around it. And Casper—" she paused at the door, "—the man who kept pace with you into the dark. Whatever he is to you, decide before the army comes. Not after. Battles are no place to leave a door undecided."

I found Wren on the wall, where I'd spent so many evenings alone. He didn't speak when I joined him. Below, the camp was banking its fires, and beyond it the salt road ran black toward the distant smear of torchlight that was twenty thousand men and a machine of iron and bone.

"I lost the bread," I said, after a while. "The morning bread, from the woman on Tanner's Lane. I can't—" I shook my head. "It's just a hole with a shape. I keep reaching for it."

"I'm sorry."

"I know you are. That's the worst part. You're always *actually* sorry." I turned and looked at him, really looked, for the first time since the marshes. "I'm not done being angry. I want that on the record. But today, in the dark, your back knew where mine was. And I don't know what to do with that except—" I exhaled. "Except stop standing on opposite ends of the camp. The moon thing. Pip's right, it's embarrassing."

Something in his shoulders came down an inch. Not forgiveness received—permission granted. It was enough, for now. It was a door, cracked, held open with courtesy.

"Whatever's left in you," he said quietly, "when the army comes. I'll help you hold it. All of it. I'll speak your history to you if you start to lose it. I've been—" he stopped, and laughed, a little brokenly. "I've been memorizing it. Since the marsh. In case."

"In case."

"In case."

We stood there, the thief and the spy, watching the torchlight gather at the edge of the world. And it was then that the bell in the granary tower began to ring—not the assembly bell, but the alarm, fast and frantic—and a rider came through the gate at a dead gallop, shouting a word that emptied the wall of every thought I had.

The rider was one of Seraphine's old contacts, half-dead in the saddle, and the word he shouted was her name, and the word after that was *coming*, and the word after that was the one that turned my blood to ice water:

"*Vane himself rides for Ashenford—and he brings the machine.*"
