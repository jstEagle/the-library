## 16. The Siege of Ashenford

The first thing I forgot was the taste of oranges.

I didn't know I'd forgotten it until later, when Wren told me I'd been eating one when the horns sounded—peeling it with my thumbnail, juice running down my wrist, complaining that Ashenford's citrus was always a week past ripe. He swears I laughed. I have to take his word for it. That memory is gone now, spent like a coin tossed into a dark well, and what came back up in its place was a soldier on the south wall whose loyalty to Aldric Vane had cost him his brother's farm and two of his teeth.

That's the arithmetic of the Thorn. One memory given, one man unmade from his obedience. It sounds like a fair trade until you're the one paying.

* * *

The wall shook at dawn.

Not breached yet—the masons of Ashenford built for stubbornness—but shaken, the way a tooth is shaken before it comes loose. Dust sifted down between the merlons like snow that couldn't commit. Below us, Vane's legions arranged themselves with the terrible patience of a tide: ranks of pikes, then arbalists, then the grey-cloaked memory-priests who walked behind the lines carrying censers of iron, feeding the machine's hunger even at this distance. I could feel them the way you feel a hand hovering over the back of your neck.

"They'll come at the Salt Gate," Wren said beside me. He had his bow strung and his sleeves rolled and the particular stillness he gets when he's frightened and has decided to be useful about it instead. "It's the oldest section. The mortar's rotten under three hundred years of patching."

"You've been here before."

"Twice. Smuggling." A pause. "And once to say goodbye to someone."

I didn't ask who. There wasn't time, and there was also—I'll admit it—a cowardice in me that morning. Every question about Wren's past was a door into rooms where I might find out how much of him was assignment. Better to fight a wall than open that one.

The battering ram they brought was no ordinary timber. It was sheathed in iron and bone—I recognized the bone, because I'd seen the machine Seraphine described, and someone had taught the war-engine to sing. When it struck the gate, the note it made went through stone and flesh alike, and men on either side of me put their hands over their ears and wept without knowing why. The song was made of taken memories. I could hear fragments in it: a lullaby in some dead dialect, a woman calling a name across a field. They'd fed it children's memories. Of course they had. Vane never met a cruelty he wouldn't refine.

"Casper." Ilsevet stood at my shoulder in borrowed armor that fit her badly, because exiled heirs don't grow up learning to wear plate. "You know what we're asking."

"I know."

"You can refuse."

"I know that too." I looked down at the legions, at the thousands of small lives bent toward breaking us, each one held upright by loyalties they'd probably sold cheap or had stolen outright. "Ask me again after breakfast."

I'd finished the orange. Apparently I'd complained about it. Wren tells me these things now, patiently, like a man reading entries from a ledger I can no longer keep myself.

* * *

The gate fell an hour after sunrise.

They came through in columns, shouting the usurper's name, and the defenders met them in the breach, and the whole world narrowed to iron and screaming and the specific smell of blood in cold air. And I stood on the wall above it all with my hands open like a beggar, and I began to spend.

The first soldier I turned was the one who gave me back oranges. I reached down through the noise—not physically; the Thorn doesn't work through hands, it works through whatever it is that memories are made of—and I found his loyalty the way you find a knot in wet rope, and I pulled it apart, and in exchange something of mine unraveled. I felt it go. Not the content of it, not at first—just the weight leaving, like a pack set down after a long march. Lighter. So much lighter.

That's the trap of it, if you want to know. Emptying feels wonderful. Every theft I'd ever committed sat in me like swallowed stones, and the Thorn let me pay them out one by one, and each payment was relief, sweet and total, right up until I tried to remember what I'd relieved myself of.

The second soldier cost me my grandmother's kitchen. I know this because I could feel the shape of the absence: a warmth-shaped hole, flour-dust and cardamom and a voice humming off-key. Except—I don't have a grandmother. I never did. My childhood was taken before I could hold it. So what I lost was a grandmother I'd *stolen*, some merchant's memory bought and bottled years ago, absorbed so deep into me I'd mistaken it for inheritance. I grieved her anyway. Grief is generous that way. It doesn't check receipts.

By midmorning I had turned eleven soldiers. They wandered out of the melee blinking, weapons hanging slack, looking at the usurper's banners as though seeing them for the first time—which, in every way that mattered, they were. Some knelt. Some just sat down in the mud. One began to laugh and couldn't stop, and I understood him better than I wanted to: I knew exactly what it felt like to wake inside a life you hadn't chosen.

"Casper." Wren's hand on my arm. "Casper, look at me."

I looked. His face was doing something complicated—fear and tenderness fighting for the same ground.

"What color were your boots when you came to Vell?"

"Brown," I said. "Scuffed at the left toe. You noticed them the first night and pretended you hadn't."

He exhaled. "Good. Keep that. Tell me things like that. Whenever I ask."

"Why?"

"Because—" He steadied himself. "Because I'm going to be asking you your own name soon, and I'd rather not get there yet."

* * *

Here is what I remember of the afternoon, and "remember" is a word I use loosely now, more like a landlord than an owner:

I remember the second breach, on the river side, and wading down into the smoke with Ilsevet's mages around me while I emptied myself against the tide of soldiers. I remember turning a sergeant who had led the charge, watching thirty years of discipline dissolve off him like frost off a window, and feeling a year of my own life go with it—I don't know which year. Wren says it was the year I learned to pick locks, which means somewhere in me is a thief with no idea how she became one.

I remember the twin smugglers holding the river stair, back to back, swearing in two languages simultaneously, which is apparently their love language.

I remember Sef—the child who steals memories, who carries other people's childhoods in her head like a magpie with a silver hoard—pressing something into my hand on the wall. A charm. Her own making. "If you run out," she said, "you can borrow. That's allowed. I'm lending, see? Lending isn't losing."

I haven't opened it. I'm saving her, the way she saved me. Though I confess I don't fully remember what she saved me *from*. There are gaps now. Rooms in my house where the furniture's gone and I can't recall what stood there, only the pale rectangles on the floor where it used to sit.

And I remember—for this one I have Wren's testimony and my own fading echo—I remember forgetting the twins' names. Mid-battle. Standing next to them. Opening my mouth to shout a warning and finding nothing there, just two faces and the certainty that I loved them and the horrible blank where their names should have been. I shouted "Duck!" instead, which worked, but it isn't the same.

Afterward, Wren took my face in both hands and said, very calmly, "Bettan and Ossa. The smugglers are Bettan and Ossa. The mage is Corvale, who once turned a duke's wig into wasps. The child is Sef, who bites. Your name is Casper Whitlock. You came to Vell with nothing and built a family out of thieves and orphans and you called it nothing, but it was everything. You hate cilantro. You hum when you concentrate. You once stole a memory of a wedding just because the bride looked lonely, and you kept it in your pocket for a month before you could bear to sell it."

"How do you know all that?"

"Because I was watching," he said. "I've been watching since the tea shop. Watching was the assignment." His thumb traced my cheekbone. "Loving you wasn't. I need you to believe those arrived separately."

I believed him. I think I believed him. Belief is harder to verify when you can't cross-check it against yourself anymore.

* * *

By dusk the breach was held. Barely. Bodies stacked in the gatehouse like cordwood, and the defenders too exhausted to celebrate, and the enemy withdrawn to their lines to wait for whatever came next.

What came next was visible from the wall: lights moving along the salt road. Not torches—lanterns of a specific white, ranked in procession, and behind them a shape too large and wrong to look at directly. The machine. Vane had brought the machine, and he was bringing it forward, and Seraphine's warning rang in what remained of my ears: *he comes for the Thorn, and for your heart's-blood.*

I sat down on the stones. My legs had opinions and none of them were favorable.

Wren sat beside me and put my head on his shoulder, and began, quietly, to talk. Not strategy. History. Mine.

"You were born somewhere you can't remember, and that's not your fault. You survived Vell alone at fourteen, sleeping in the bell tower of the Purity chapel because no one looks up. You taught three orphans to read using a stolen shipping manifest. You cry at other people's weddings and pretend it's dust. Your favorite thing in the world is the moment right before rain, when the air goes green and everything holds its breath—you bought that memory twice, from different people, because you liked comparing versions."

"That last one's true," I murmured. "I remember liking it."

"Then hold onto liking it." His voice cracked, just slightly, and he covered it by pressing his lips to my hair. "Because I'm running low on facts, Casper, and I refuse—do you hear me—to introduce myself to you tomorrow. I refuse it. Whatever it costs, we buy you back."

I wanted to tell him it couldn't be done. That I was a purse with a slit in the bottom, and every hour the seam widened, and eventually there would be nothing in me to anchor, no *me* left to be anchored. But my mouth had gone strange and slow, and what came out instead was:

"Wren. What's my name?"

His breath stopped. Then he said it—*Casper Whitlock*—the way you'd say it to a drowning person throwing the rope, and caught my hands, and held on.

Above us, the white lanterns came on, patient as stars, and behind them the machine began to sing.

* * *

I write this now—or rather, Wren writes as I dictate, because my letters have started swimming—by the light of a shuttered lamp in the granary cellar. We have maybe hours. Perhaps less. Ilsevet says the final stand will come at dawn, at the ruined shrine on the hill where the old court once stood, because prophecy loves symmetry and Vane loves an audience.

I am nearly empty. What remains of me fits in a thimble: Wren's face. The green hush before rain. Sef's charm, still unopened in my pocket, warm as a live coal. A scuffed left boot-toe.

Vane thinks he's coming to take what's left.

He's wrong, and I wish I could remember why I'm sure of that. But sureness is the one thing the Thorn never touched—it sits deeper than memory, down where names don't reach—and so I know only this:

At dawn, I walk out to meet the usurper of the Ninefold Court, and I will give him exactly what he came for.

All of it. On my terms. Whether or not I remember, afterward, what my terms were.

Wren says I'm smiling as I dictate this. I'll take his word. He keeps my records now—all of them—and God help him, he reads them back to me like scripture, like a man determined that no line of me go uncounted.

The lanterns are closer. The singing is louder.

Dawn is coming, and I intend to be unforgettable, even if I can't remember being anything at all.
