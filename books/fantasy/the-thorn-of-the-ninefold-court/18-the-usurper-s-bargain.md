## 18. The Usurper's Bargain

I have been drunk exactly twice in my life, and I remember both occasions with the fond, embarrassed clarity of a person who does not intend to repeat the mistake. Being captured by Aldric Vane's personal guard was like being drunk, in that the world tilted, my own limbs stopped answering to me, and I woke up somewhere I did not choose with a taste in my mouth like copper and regret.

Except that with drink, you get the hangover afterward. Here the headache came first.

They took me at the salt gate, in the hour after the sabotage, when the memory-machine was still smoking behind us and Seraphine Hale's blood was still drying on my hands—I had carried her, half of her, and she had weighed less than I expected, as though a decade of faith removed has mass. Wren fought beside me for eleven minutes. I know it was eleven because afterward he told me, in the flat voice of a man doing arithmetic on his own failure, "Eleven minutes. I held them off for eleven minutes, Casper, I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

The twelfth minute belonged to Vane's Guard-Captain, a woman named Ondrey who moved like a door closing. She put a hand on my chest—not a blow, just pressure, the way you'd steady something fragile—and said, "The Thorn comes quietly or not at all," and the *not at all* was aimed past my shoulder at Wren.

Wren was face-down in the mud with three soldiers' knees in his back. He was shouting my name. Then someone's gauntlet found his temple, and he wasn't shouting anything, and I went quiet, because there is a kind of silence that isn't surrender but negotiation, and every thief in Vell knows its grammar.

*I'll come,* I told Ondrey, without moving my lips much. *But if they kill him, I will spend everything I am unmaking your army from the inside out, soldier by soldier, until there is nothing left of the Ninefold Court but a story nobody believes.*

She looked at me for a long moment. There was no fear in her face, which was itself frightening—but there was respect, of a grim professional kind, the respect of one tradesperson acknowledging another's tools.

"He lives," she said. "That's above my pay. Come."

* * *

They did not chain me. That was the first courtesy, and courtesies from men like Aldric Vane are never free; they are invoices with the total left blank. They gave me a cell that wasn't a cell—a room, warm, with a window of real glass looking out over a city I didn't recognize, all black spires and bone-white walls, the usurper's capital wearing its wealth like armor. They gave me food I was afraid to eat until Ondrey, standing in the doorway with her arms crossed, watched me watch it and said, exasperated: "If we wanted you dead or drugged, girl, you'd already be both. Eat."

So I ate. And I slept, horribly enough, because my body was a spent wick and it took what it could get, and I dreamed of nothing—which frightened me more than any nightmare would have, because for seven years my sleep had been crowded with other people's memories leaking through, and now the leaks had slowed to a trickle, and I lay in the dark counting what was left of myself the way a miser counts coins by touch.

Morning—or what passed for morning under that bruised-colored sky—brought the summons.

They walked me through corridors of white stone veined with iron, and I understood, with the professional appreciation of someone who has spent a career noticing how people store their treasures, that this palace was built around the memory-machine the way a body is built around a heart. The veins in the walls weren't decoration. They were conduits. Somewhere below us, that thing of iron and bone that Seraphine had described—the thing that drank a mage whole while he screamed—was pulsing, feeding, and the whole city drew its strange prosperity from the tap.

Aldric Vane received me alone.

That surprised me. I had imagined ministers, inquisitors, ranks of kneeling courtiers. Instead: one tall room, one table, two chairs, and the man himself, pouring tea with the unhurried competence of someone who has never once needed to impress anyone. He looked older than his coin-portraits. Tired around the eyes. Hands like a scholar's, except for the callus on the right forefinger where, I would learn later, he still practiced the old court's memory-craft—the craft he claimed to despise.

"Casper Whitlock," he said. "Sit. The tea is from Ashenford's hills. I confiscated the shipment personally. It seemed fitting that you should drink the last of the free cities before we discuss their future."

"You talk like a ledger," I said, sitting, because my legs had opinions and they were all cowardly ones. "Everything itemized. Nothing you mean."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Recognition. "Hale says the same thing," he said. "In her reports. She has a gift for insolence she thinks I don't notice." He pushed a cup toward me. "You've read her ledger, I assume. She reached you with it. Yes—don't perform surprise, it doesn't suit you. So you believe you know me. A tyrant. A thief of memories. A man whose crown is built on stolen royal blood."

"Aren't those things true?"

"They are true," Vane said, "the way a knife's edge is true. Accurate, and useless, unless you also understand which way it points." He sat, folded his hands, and looked at me with an expression I recognized from my own mirror: the look of a person about to do something unforgivable and wishing, distantly, that it could be otherwise. "You think I hunt you because you can destroy me. That is the story Hale believed too, and I confess I let her believe it, because fear makes good soldiers. But I am not hunting you, Master Whitlock. I am *recruiting* you. And I am going to prove to you that everyone else who wants you—the heir Ilsevet, the resistance, your charming Wren—wants you for exactly the same purpose, and is simply better at dressing it up."

"I've heard recruitment speeches before," I said. "Usually they open with flattery about my technique."

"Your technique is vulgar," Vane said mildly, "and your bottling method wastes perhaps a third of each extraction. No. What I admire about you is not your craft." He leaned forward. "It is that you survived. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what happens, usually, to children whose memories are taken whole?"

The word *whole* landed in my stomach like a dropped stone.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he let it lie there between us like a coin on a table.

"Of course you don't," he said gently. "That's rather the point. You've spent your life collecting other people's childhoods because you have none of your own. First snowfall. First sea. First love, secondhand, thirdhand. You thought you were filling a hunger. You never asked who carved the hollow." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "I can show you who. That is my bargain's first installment, freely given. Not a threat. A gift."

Every instinct I had—seven years of instincts, honed in the alleys of a city that outlawed the very trade that kept me alive—screamed at me not to take it. Gifts from Aldric Vane. Invoices with the total left blank.

But I was so tired. And the hollow was so old.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

He did not use the machine. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger survives this war: whatever else Aldric Vane is, he did not force that particular horror on me. He used a charm—one of his own making, iron-cased, cold to the touch—and asked my permission with actual words, which was somehow worse than force would have been, because it made me complicit, and he knew it, and he did it anyway.

"Hold this," he said. "And understand: I cannot restore what was taken. Memory is not water you can pour back into a cracked vessel. But the vessel remembers its cracks. Even emptied, even hollowed—you carry the shape of what was done to you. This will show you the shape."

I held it. And the shape rose up around me like floodwater.

*A room with nine windows, and light coming through all of them at once, and I am small—I am four years old, maybe five, small enough that the world arrives mostly at knee-height—and a woman kneels in front of me. She wears grey and silver. Her face is kind the way surgeons are kind. Behind her stand others, robed, and one of them holds a basin of something that catches the window-light and throws it wrong, and there is a sigil on the floor beneath my bare feet, and I am crying, and the kind woman says,*

*"It must be emptied, little thorn. The prophecy requires a vessel, not a child. Forgive us. The court must survive."*

*And then the light comes down through all nine windows at once, and it does not hurt, which is the worst part, it feels like being loved, it feels like warmth pouring through me and taking everything, my mother's laugh, the smell of bread, the name I called my father, the color of our door, the song, the street, the season, the self—all of it flowing out into the basin into the sigil into the hands of the Ninefold Court, who needed a weapon against a prophecy and made one out of a child, and the last thing I feel before the light finishes is the woman's thumb wiping a tear from my cheek, and her kindness, and her theft, being the same gesture.*

I came back to myself on the floor. I don't remember falling. Vane was crouched beside me at a respectful distance, holding out a glass of water, and his face wore something I had not expected and did not want: pity.

"The Ninefold Court," he said, when I could hear again. "Ilsevet's court. The court Wren served. The court the resistance wants to restore. They took your childhood, Whitlock. Every stolen year you've mourned—they stole it themselves, a century into their reign, because their own prophecies terrified them, and they needed a Thorn they could control. They made you. And then their carelessness lost you to the gutters of Vell, where you grew up feral and clever and full of other people's memories, and they have spent twenty years looking for their missing weapon without knowing they'd misplaced it." He stood, straightened his sleeves. "Now ask yourself. When Wren found you—when he bought your sea-memories and flirted over your tea and followed you into the marshes—do you imagine he didn't know? The old court's agents keep the old court's records. He knew what you were from the beginning. He was ordered to bring the vessel home to the heir. Alive—" and here he smiled, thin and terrible, "—or not at all. He told you some of this himself, I suspect. The assignment part. Did he tell you the *vessel* part? Did he tell you that Ilsevet's plan for you differs from mine only in the destination? She needs you emptied onto a throne-room floor to break my claim. I need you emptied into my machine to build a claim no one can ever break. Either way, Whitlock—either way—you end as a tool. The only question anyone has ever asked about you is *which hand*."

The room was very quiet. Through the window, the black spires stood against the grey sky like needles waiting for thread.

"And your offer?" My voice came out scraped. "Survival for service. Say it plainly. I deal in plain."

Vane returned to his chair and sat, and folded his hands, and became once more the ledger.

"Serve willingly," he said. "Lend the Thorn's power to the machine—not your heart's-blood, that was always Hale's misunderstanding, the machine wants consent, willing service, that's the final component—and I will give you back a life. Protection. Purpose. And this: every memory the machine takes from others, I will let you draw from first. You want a childhood, Whitlock? There are ten thousand mages in my cells. Take a mother's laugh from one. Take a father from another. Build yourself a past out of the guilty, brick by brick, and live in it. No one will ever use you again. You will be the user." He spread his hands. "Or refuse, and be handed to the machine anyway, unwilling, and get nothing. Or escape, run back to Ilsevet, and be emptied on a throne-room floor for a dead dynasty's redemption. Three doors. I am offering you the only one with a person behind it instead of a purpose."

He let it sit. He was good at that; I'll grant him his craftsmanship. He let the silence do his selling.

And here is the truth I will carry into whatever comes next, the truth that cost me more than the capture, more than the chains-that-weren't: for eleven full breaths, I wanted it. I wanted the mother's laugh. I wanted the father, the door, the street, the song. Eleven breaths, standing in the usurper's tower with his poisoned gift still burning its shape behind my eyes, I stood on the edge of becoming exactly what he offered—a thief promoted, fed on prisoners, dressed in stolen childhoods forever.

Then I thought of Wren's face going slack in the mud. Of Seraphine's weight in my arms, lighter than faith should be. Of a child thief asking, *do we run, or do we ruin him?* Of a bottled charm hidden where no searcher would find it, holding one face at first snowfall.

Eleven breaths. Then I set the empty teacup down with a click, very carefully, the way you'd set down a blade you were deciding not to use.

"No," I said.

Vane sighed. It sounded almost genuine. "I did hope," he said. "Truly. It's rare, meeting someone worth hoping for." He rang a small bell on the table, and the door opened, and Ondrey filled it with soldiers. "Take the Thorn below," he said. "Gently. The machine will begin preparation at moonrise. And Master Whitlock—" he paused at the window, silhouetted against his needle-spired kingdom, and did not turn around, "—when you are on the table, and the light comes down, and it feels like being loved—remember that I told you the truth. That will matter to you, in the moment. It always does."

They took me down through the white veins of the palace, down toward the pulse of iron and bone, and I counted the turns of the stairs the way a thief counts anything—automatically, hungrily, professionally—because somewhere above me Wren was alive, because Ilsevet's armies were marching, because a child had asked whether we run or ruin him, and I had smiled.

Below me, through the floors, faint as a heartbeat, the machine was waking up.

Moonrise was four hours away.

I started planning before the third landing.
