## 19. Ninefold Breaking

The Ninefold Court had been beautiful once, they said. Now it was a jaw of broken teeth against a bruise-colored sky, and I was being marched up its spine to die in front of forty thousand people.

I want it noted that I did not trip on the stairs. Given everything—the bindings on my wrists, the iron collar humming against my throat, the fact that I hadn't eaten since the tent—tripping would have been forgivable. But I have always had my pride, and pride is the last thing they let you keep.

Vane walked ahead of me. He didn't look back. Kings who look back at their prisoners are kings who doubt themselves, and Aldric Vane had never doubted anything in his life except, perhaps, whether his mirrors flattered him enough. His cloak was the color of dried blood, which I suppose he'd have called crimson, and it caught the wind like something alive.

Behind us came the machine.

You haven't lived—really lived—until you've heard the memory-engine breathe. It was a thing of iron ribs and bone joints, tall as three men, and it made a sound like a lullaby played backwards through water. Seraphine Hale had sabotaged it in the command tent, cracked something vital, bought us hours. But Vane's engineers were clever, and hours had been enough. It rolled up the great stair on wheels of femur-white, and every step it took, the air tasted more like copper.

The assembled armies filled the plain below: Vane's legions in their black and gold, Ilsevet's free-company banners, the Ashenford militia with their mismatched armor, the Salt Road clans. Forty thousand people come to watch either a coronation or an execution, depending on how the afternoon went.

And there, at the top of the stair, on a platform of shattered marble where the old thrones had been dragged out of the ruin and set in a row of nine—nine empty chairs, one for each of the old court's houses—Wren.

They'd bound him to a post beside the thrones. Someone had worked him over; there was blood at his temple, drying in a line down his jaw like a signature he hadn't meant to sign. When he saw me, he didn't shout. He didn't struggle. He just looked at me the way he'd looked at me over tea all those years ago, when he was buying the sea in little bottles and lying about why.

*Hold on,* that look said.

*To what?* I thought back. *Be specific.*

Because here was the problem, laid out as neatly as a ledger: the Thorn's power required emptiness. Every stolen memory, released or burned or given away. Everything I had taken from dying merchants and frightened clients and soldiers whose loyalty I'd unmade in the marshes. And underneath all of that, the ones I'd never stolen at all—the ones that were mine before the old court scraped my childhood out of me like marrow from a bone. Ilsevet had told me the truth of it in the hollow hill. The prophecy didn't ask for my hoard. It asked for *me*. A vessel poured out entirely, so that the storm could pass through clean.

Vane wanted me emptied into his machine, so he could bottle legitimacy and pour it into his claim. Ilsevet wanted me emptied onto the throne, so the old blood-right would shatter and her house could rise from honest ruins. Everyone, it turned out, had a plan for my insides.

Nobody had asked what *I* wanted.

Which was, I'll admit, partly my own fault. I had spent twenty years not asking myself, because the answer was a hole shaped like a childhood I couldn't remember.

* * *

"Kneel," said the herald, and I knelt, because my knees had opinions and the collar agreed with them.

Vane ascended the nine steps to the first throne—the throne he'd claimed—and turned to face the armies. His voice carried without magic, which was somehow worse. Magic you can fight. A voice like that is just certainty, weaponized.

"People of the Ninefold," he said. "For a century you have been told the prophecy speaks of a Thorn that will unmake the false and crown the true. Here stands the Thorn." He gestured at me the way a man gestures at livestock. "And here stands the machine that will read the truth out of them—all of it, every stolen hour, every borrowed life—so that we may finally know whom the prophecy crowns."

A murmur ran through forty thousand throats. Somewhere in it, I knew, were the twins, and the child thief who remembered everything she stole, and Ilsevet with her hollow-hill patience, and Seraphine Hale standing in Vane's own ranks with a ledger full of treason pressed against her ribs. My found family, scattered through an enemy crowd, each of them hoping for a miracle.

I had a different plan. It wasn't better. But it was mine, and after everything, I had developed strong feelings about ownership.

"You're wondering," Vane continued, warming now, pacing before the thrones, "why the Thorn must be emptied at all. I will tell you. Because the Thorn is not a person. It is a vessel. It has no past worth keeping—" and here he smiled down at me, kindly, the way Hale had once been kind to me in an interrogation room, and I understood that kindness and cruelty use the same face when they're both lying "—no self worth sparing. Only capacity."

"That's where you're wrong," I said.

My voice came out smaller than I wanted. Collars do that. But the herald, professional that he was, carried it outward anyway, and forty thousand people got to hear the prisoner talk back.

Vane descended one step. "Speak, then. Entertain us."

"The machine reads memories out of a vessel and gives them to whoever holds the tap." I nodded at the engine, breathing its backwards lullaby behind us. "That's how you built your claim, isn't it? Royal memories, harvested from the dead and the captured, poured into your bloodline's oath-stone until the stone believed you were king. You didn't forge your legitimacy. You *bottled* it. You're just a thief with better equipment."

The murmur changed key. Fear does that—it harmonizes.

"I am the rightful heir of the Ninefold Concord," Vane said, and for the first time, something flickered under the certainty.

"Prove it," I said. "Not with the stone. Not with the machine. With *me*. Because here's the funny thing about vessels, Your Radiance—we don't just hold what's put in us. We hold what leaks. Twenty years of other people's memories, and some of them leaked into my dreams, and do you know what I've seen?" I let the pause stretch. Theater. Wren taught me that, or maybe I taught him; by the end we'd stopped keeping score. "I've seen a man in a black cloak signing harvest orders. I've seen the oath-stone of the Ninefold Court being fed. And I've seen whose memories went in first."

I hadn't seen any such thing, precisely. But I had seen enough leaked fragments of the old war to know the shape of the lie, and Vane's face confirmed the rest. For one heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—he looked at me the way I used to look at ledgers that refused to balance.

Then he recovered, because usurpers always recover, and said, "Empty the vessel."

* * *

The engineers unshackled my wrists and locked them instead into the machine's cradle—iron cuffs at the temples, cold as riverbed stones. The collar stayed. Through the engine's ribcage I could see the crowd, a sea of faces blurred by heat-shimmer and my own rising pulse. Wren strained against his post. I watched him mouth something. I'm fairly sure it was my name. I'm fairly sure I pretended not to see, because if this went wrong, I didn't want my last sight of him to be hope.

The machine began to drink.

It doesn't hurt, exactly. That's the horror of it. It feels like remembering in reverse—like watching a fire burn downward through a house, floor by floor, and knowing which rooms are going next. First went the stock, the trade goods: first snowfall from the dying merchant, gone. The taste of someone else's wedding cake. A fisherman's dawn. Three seas I'd sold to a charming stranger and then, later, quietly re-stolen for myself, because I was sentimental and he had nice hands.

The crowd gasped as the machine's spires lit. Vane raised his arms. "Behold! The truth of the Thorn, poured out for all to—"

"No," I said.

Here is what nobody understood, not Vane, not Ilsevet, not even Wren, who loved me and still got it wrong: a vessel chooses what it pours. The old court had made me a container. They had never once managed to make me obedient. Ask any thief in Vell—the whole craft is not taking things. It's deciding *what* leaves the room, and when, and through which window.

I reached—not with my hands, which were cuffed, but with the thing underneath my skin that the prophecy had named a thorn—and I took hold of the stream.

Every stolen memory I owned, I gave away. Not to the machine. To the *air*. To the crowd. I opened the floodgates sideways and let forty thousand people catch what fell: the merchant's snowfall landing on ten thousand upturned faces at once. The fisherman's dawn breaking over soldiers who had burned fishing villages. A child's first steps, given to a legion that had orphaned children. Grief, joy, boredom, the smell of bread, the terror of a mother in a burning free city—I spent it all, recklessly, generously, the way I had never once dared spend anything in my careful invisible life.

And the machine choked.

It was built to drink from one vessel through one tap. Instead it stood in a rainstorm of everyone's memories at once, drowning in input it couldn't parse, and somewhere deep in its iron chest something cracked—a sound like a bell failing—and the backwards lullaby became a shriek, and the shriek became silence.

The collar died against my throat.

Vane turned. I will give him this: he moved fast for a man watching his life's work die. He had a knife out and his hand around my throat before the echo settled, and he said, close enough that only I could hear, "What did you do?"

"What you should have done years ago," I rasped. "Given it away."

He raised the knife.

And the crowd—forty thousand people, suddenly holding two decades of stolen lives inside them, suddenly *knowing* what a harvested memory felt like from the inside—moved. Not all at once. Like ice going out on a river. First the Salt Road clans, who had just received a dead woman's lullaby and recognized the singer. Then the Ashenford militia. Then, horribly for him, a full third of Vane's own legions, because I had unmade soldiers' loyalty once before in the marshes, and apparently loyalty, once loosened, doesn't always grow back.

Seraphine Hale stepped out of the ranks with her ledger held high over her head like a lantern. "I have names!" she shouted, and her voice cracked across the plain. "Dates! Harvest orders signed in his hand! Read it yourselves!"

Vane's knife arm wavered. Not from mercy. From arithmetic. Even usurpers can count.

* * *

Later—they tell me it was minutes; it felt like weather—I stood at the edge of the platform while the Ninefold Court's claim burned around me. Not literally. There was no fire. The burning was quieter than that: the oath-stone, cracked open by the machine's death-scream, going grey and dumb; Vane's guards setting down their weapons one by one, like men waking from a shared dream; the man himself kneeling in the wreckage of his certainty while the free companies closed in, and no one, anywhere on that vast plain, calling him king.

I had done it. The prophecy fulfilled, the Thorn emptied, the fabricated claim unmade before the assembled armies.

And I felt—

Nothing.

That's not poetry. I mean it with a clerk's precision. The emptying had taken everything I'd ever stolen, and the release had taken the rest, and what remained of Casper Whitlock fit in a thimble. I knew language. I knew that the tall bleeding man by the post mattered to me, though I could no longer say why, the way you know a door matters when you can't remember the house. I knew the wind was cold. I knew, distantly, that I had won, and that winning felt like standing in an unfurnished room.

Wren reached me first. Of course he did. He'd torn half the post out of the ground getting loose, and he crossed the platform at a dead run and caught my face in his hands, and his eyes went through me like a man searching rubble.

"Casper."

"I know the word," I said. "It's mine, isn't it? It sounds like mine."

His face did something terrible and gentle. Behind him, Ilsevet was ascending toward the nine thrones, and the armies were roaring, and history was turning its great slow page—but Wren didn't look at any of it. He was holding a small glass charm in his shaking fingers, stoppered with wax, glowing faintly from within.

"You kept one," he whispered. "In the hill, when they explained the price—you hid one. I saw you palm it and I never told anyone, I never—" His breath broke. "Do you know what it is?"

I looked at the little bottle. Snowlight moved inside it, soft and white and patient.

"No," I said honestly. "But I think I'd very much like to find out."

He laughed, or sobbed, or both, and pressed it into my palm, and closed my fingers around it—and above us, unnoticed by either of us, Ilsevet reached the first of the nine empty thrones, and stopped, and did not sit.
