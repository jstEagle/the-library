## 20. One Memory Kept

I have lied to queens, to inquisitors, to myself, and I am, if I may say so, quite good at it. But nothing—nothing—prepared me for the particular hell of introducing yourself to the person you love when they look at you with the polite blankness of someone being accosted by a stranger at a market stall.

"Hello," I said. "My name is Wren."

Casper looked at me. Casper looked at the little bottle in their hand. Casper looked back at me.

"All right," they said carefully, in the tone one uses with a large and possibly unstable dog. "Wren."

It had been three days since the Ninefold Court came down around our ears like a chandelier dropped from heaven. Three days since Casper emptied themselves of every stolen memory—the merchant's snowfall, the smuggler twins' laughter, a hundred small bright hours that were never theirs to begin with—and poured them out as a storm that stripped Aldric Vane's fabricated claim to ash before ten thousand witnesses. Three days since Ilsevet reached the empty throne and, to the visible relief of everyone who'd spent two years fighting to put her on it, declined to sit.

Three days since Casper woke up blank as winter, holding one bottled charm they had refused, at the cost of everything else, to release.

And I had been standing at their bedside ever since like an idiot, rehearsing.

*Hello, my name is Wren, we were lovers, also I betrayed you, also you forgave me, also here is your entire personality, would you like tea?*

"You're staring," Casper said.

"I'm sorry." I sat down in the chair by the window, then stood up again, because sitting felt presumptuous. "I'm told I do that."

"Told by whom?"

And there it was: the question under all the questions. *By whom.* By me. By Sable and Finch, who kept coming by with soup and leaving it untouched because they couldn't bear to watch him eat like a stranger. By Maren, the child thief who remembered everything she ever stole, and who had taken one long look at hollow-eyed Casper and announced, with the brutal clarity of the very young, *"He's been emptied out. Like a bottle."* By Seraphine Hale, of all people, who visited once, set her ledger on the table, and said quietly, "If you need testimony of who he was, I have some. It's not flattering. It's true."

"It doesn't matter who," I said.

"It must matter somewhat." Casper turned the little bottle over in their fingers. Snowlight moved inside it, soft and white and patient. "Everyone in this house looks at me like I'm a funeral. And everyone looks at *you* like you're the gravedigger. So either you're my brother, or my enemy, or something worse, and I'd rather know which before breakfast."

The honest answer was *something worse*, or possibly *something better*, depending on how you counted betrayal against devotion, and I had spent three days failing to find the arithmetic. So instead I said:

"I'm the man who sold the sea."

Their eyebrows went up. "That sounds like a story."

"It is. It's mine. Would you like to hear it?"

They considered me for a long moment. Even hollowed out—even with every borrowed memory burned away—there was something in their face that hadn't been erased. A wariness with wit behind it. A way of holding still that wasn't emptiness but attention, total and unnerving, the way a lockpick attends to a tumbler. Whatever the old court had taken from Casper Whitlock's childhood, whatever Vane had shown them in his tent of horrors, whatever they had surrendered atop the ruined court—they had not surrendered *this*. The person underneath the memories. The thing that chose.

I know, because I fell in love with that thing before I knew anything else about them.

"Tea first," Casper said. "Then the sea."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about loving someone with no past: every conversation is a first conversation, and you can never relax, and you can never stop being grateful.

We sat in the kitchen of the safehouse in Ashenford's refugee quarter while the city outside did the noisy, miraculous work of learning to govern itself. For nine days now the free cities had been convening—delegates arguing in rented halls, Seraphine's ledger circulating like scripture, Ilsevet pointedly not sitting in any chair that resembled a throne. There was no king. There was, gods help us, a *committee*. And the committee had not yet decided what to do about the Thorn, which was currently buttering toast in my kitchen with hands that remembered the motion even if the person didn't.

"Do you want to know what you're doing?" I asked.

"No," Casper said, and ate the toast. "I want to do it wrong and find out why it's wrong. That's how you learn things. Apparently." A pause. "Did I used to say that?"

"You said something like it. About locks."

"Was I good with locks?"

"You were—" I laughed despite myself. "You were the best I ever saw. You used to say a lock was just a door that hadn't met the right question yet."

Casper chewed on this along with the toast. "Hm," they said. "Flattering. Was I insufferable about it?"

"Honestly? Yes."

"Good." They pointed the knife at me. "I'd hate to think I was humble. Humble people seem exhausting."

And that was the moment I understood I wasn't mourning anymore. That the grief I'd been carrying for three days—the grief of introducing myself over and over, of watching them meet their own reflection without recognition—had a trapdoor in it, and beneath the trapdoor was something almost unbearably light. Because Casper wasn't gone. Casper was *new*. Every day was the first day, and every day they chose—or didn't—to let me stay in it.

I had courted them once under false pretenses, sent to deliver them alive or not at all, and I had spent months paying for that lie with truth, and they had forgiven me, and then they had forgotten the forgiveness along with everything else. Which meant, by any fair accounting, I owed them the whole story again. From the beginning. With the ugly parts left in.

So I gave it to them, over tea, the way I'd promised: the sea.

"I grew up in Tarrowmouth, before Vane closed the coast," I said. "Before magic became contraband and memories became currency. My mother sold fish. My father sold lies, mostly to my mother. And when I was eleven, a charm-seller came through—a real one, from the old days—and he had a vial of the open ocean. Not the shore. The *middle*. Blue going down forever, and a whale singing somewhere underneath it, so low you felt it in your sternum more than heard it. He charged my mother a week's wages. She bought it anyway. She said she wanted me to know the world was bigger than the smell of fish."

Casper was very still. "What happened to it?"

"He drank it," I said. "The charm-seller. Years later. He said he'd sold so much of himself he needed to remember what water felt like. That's the trade, you see. That's the whole filthy trade. Somebody always pays, and it's never the buyer."

"And you became him."

"I became worse. I stole instead of bought. I told myself it was kinder—taking a memory leaves the life intact, only the *remembering* goes—and maybe it was, a little. Maybe it was just theft with better manners." I wrapped my hands around the cooling cup. "Then I came to Vell, and I found a client named Casper Whitlock, who bought three memories of the sea off me and lingered over tea and watched my hands too closely, and I thought, *there's another thief, and a better one than me,* and I was supposed to report that to my masters. Because I wasn't just selling charms. I was an agent of the exiled court, and you were the Thorn, and finding you was my assignment."

Casper's eyes narrowed—not in anger; they had no memories to be angry *with*. In assessment. "And did you report it?"

"Not at first. Then yes. Then—" I spread my hands. "Then I burned my reports and ran to warn you, and you nearly gutted me on the docks, and then you took my hand instead. I've never decided which of those frightened me more."

"The gutting or the hand?"

"The hand."

Something moved across Casper's face. Not recognition—nothing so kind—but its cousin. Interest. The same spark I'd seen across that first cup of tea, years ago, when they were whole and guarded and pretending not to be charmed by me.

"That's a good story," they said. "Is it true?"

"Every word. Including the unflattering ones."

"Hm." They looked down at the bottle in their palm. Snowlight turned inside it. "And this? Everyone keeps looking at it like it's a relic. What is it?"

And there it was. The question I had dreaded and hungered after in equal measure, because I knew exactly what it was—I had seen them take it, that night in the marshes when the leaks first started, a memory lifted from *me* while I slept, stolen from the thief by the thief, and I had woken knowing something was missing and never guessed what until the hill, until the price was named, until I saw them palm the little bottle with fingers quick as sin.

It was my face, at first snowfall, in the year we fled together. My face, as seen through their eyes. The one memory Casper had *chosen* to keep, out of everything they'd ever held—their own childhood, gone before they could fight for it; their stolen hoard, spent like coin to save the world; and from all of it, one bottle, one hour, one image of *me*.

How do you tell someone that? How do you hand a stranger their own heart and say, *here, this is yours, it has always been yours, please don't drop it?*

Carefully, I decided. The way you'd handle a live coal.

"Open it," I said, "and see."

* * *

A charm opens two ways. The seller's way—thumb to the wax seal, quick and clinical, a sip taken standing up. And the other way, the way lovers and thieves learn: held between both palms, warmed until the wax softens, and then breathed open, slow, so the memory has time to settle into you like weather instead of striking like lightning.

I showed Casper the second way. I put my own hands over theirs around the bottle, and I felt them startle slightly at the touch and then not pull away, and I said, "Breathe," and they breathed.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the snowlight rose out of the glass like breath off a winter pond, and settled into their eyes, and Casper Whitlock—who had woken blank as January, who had met their own name like a rumor, who had endured three days of strangers calling them *hero* in voices full of someone else's grief—went absolutely still.

I watched them see me.

Not the me of now, sleep-deprived and gray-faced in a refugee kitchen. The me of that memory: younger by two hard years, standing in the first snowfall outside Ashenford with flakes melting in my hair, laughing at something, off guard, unaware I was being watched. Unaware anyone would ever think that face worth keeping.

I knew the exact moment it landed, because their eyes filled.

"Oh," Casper whispered. "Oh, that's—you. You look—" They stopped. Started again. "Why would I keep *this*? Of everything. Everything I ever took, everything I ever was, and I kept a picture of *you standing in the snow*?"

"Because it was the only one you didn't steal," I said. "That one was given."

They looked at the empty bottle. At me. At the window, where Ashenford was building something unprecedented out of arguments and hope, and where the first flakes of the season's early snow had begun, in fact, to fall—small, tentative, as if the sky were testing whether the world could bear such an ordinary miracle again.

"Wren," they said. Testing my name in their mouth like a new coin.

"Yes."

"I don't remember loving you." Their voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "I don't remember anything. But I kept your face through the end of the world, and my hands apparently knew to hold onto you, and just now, when you touched my hands, my heart did something extremely undignified. So I have a proposal."

"Name it."

"Don't tell me the rest yet. Don't tell me about the gutting or the forgiveness or whatever terrible romantic nonsense we apparently survived. Just—" They turned their palm up on the table between us. Open. An offering, or an invitation, or a lock waiting for the right question. "Just be here tomorrow too. And the day after. Let me choose you again, properly, from nothing. I'm told I'm good at choosing."

Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere across the city, a bell rang—not alarm, not mourning; the assembly bell, calling delegates to argue about the shape of a world with no thrones in it. There would be summons soon. Questions about the Thorn, about the emptying, about what Casper was now and what they might yet become. Seraphine's ledger had pages that concerned them. Ilsevet had questions she hadn't asked. The world had a way of needing its miracles documented and filed.

But that was tomorrow.

I put my hand in Casper's open palm, and they closed their fingers around it, warm and certain, like they'd done it ten thousand times.

"Hello," I said. "My name is Wren. I sell the sea."

Casper smiled—new, unremembered, entirely their own—and outside the window, the snow came down on Ashenford like a blessing the sky had finally decided we'd earned.

"Well, Wren," they said. "It's snowing. Tell me what that's like."
