## 22. First Snowfall, Again

The trouble with being a blank page is that everyone wants to write on you.

I learned this in the first week after the Ninefold Breaking, when I woke in Wren's arms with nothing behind my eyes but white. People I had apparently saved came to visit and told me stories about myself the way you'd tell stories about a saint or a particularly clever dog. *You unmade an army,* they said. *You burned a false king's claim to ash.* *You held my hand when the wall came down.* They said it with such warmth and such certainty that I began to suspect they were describing someone else entirely—a stranger who happened to wear my face, which, given my history, was at least thematically appropriate.

"You're frowning," Wren said this morning. "You only frown when you've decided the story doesn't fit."

"The story never fits," I said. "That's the whole problem with stories. They have edges. I don't."

He laughed, because he is the kind of person who laughs at terrible things said bravely, which is either a virtue or a symptom. Then he set the charm on the table between us, and the laughing stopped.

It was small, as charms go. A thumb of glass, cloudy at the base where the memory pooled like sediment in old wine. I knew what it was before anyone told me. Some knowledge survived the emptying—how to read a ledger, how to hold a knife, how to lie to an inquisitor with a kind face—and apparently one of those survivors was the shape of this particular bottle under my palm. My fingers remembered it the way a river remembers its bed.

"Are you sure?" Wren asked. He had asked every day for two months, and every day for two months I had said no, and every day for two months he had put it back in his pocket like a man returning a letter unopened.

"I'm sure," I said. "I'm tired of being a rumor about myself."

* * *

Let me explain what I am now, since even I only know secondhand.

Nine months ago I stood atop the ruined Ninefold Court with a usurper's blood-magic claim coiling around the city below like a black vine, and I chose to let go. Every stolen memory—the merchant's first snowfall, the smuggler twins' mother's lullaby, the child thief's whole bright catalogue of other people's lives, the sea, three times over, from a man named Wren who bought them because he was falling in love with me and too much of a coward to say so—all of it went out of me at once, a storm of borrowed hours that tore Vane's fabricated legitimacy apart in front of ten thousand witnesses. The throne didn't just lose its king that day. It lost its argument.

What remained of me was a woman with winter where her past should be, holding one small bottle she refused to open.

Wren tells me I looked at him afterward and asked, quite politely, if he was the enemy. He tells me he said yes, mostly, historically, but that he was working on it. He tells me I decided that was good enough, and took his hand, and that was the first decision the new Casper ever made. I believe him. It sounds exactly like something I would do.

Rebuilding a self out of other people's testimony is strange work. Seraphine Hale—once my hunter, now something more embarrassing than a friend—brings me documents. Ilsevet, the heir who declined a crown and seemed almost relieved to do it, brings me the prophecy book itself, though I've asked her to stop; looking at my own illuminated face makes my skin crawl, like catching your reflection in a stranger's window. The twins bring me gossip and stolen pastries. The child, Nettle, who remembers everything she steals, sits with me sometimes and lends me memories the way you'd lend an umbrella: here, take this one back by evening, don't get it wet.

And Wren brings me everything else. He tells me about the tea shop, the three memories of the sea, the way I nearly gutted him on the docks and then, instead, took his hand. He tells it so often I've begun to feel nostalgic for events I cannot recall, which may be the strangest thing grief has ever done to me.

Today, though, we are not going to talk about it anymore. Today I'm going to live it.

* * *

There is a technique to opening a charm—I can feel it sitting in my hands like a bird's memory of flight, all instinct and no history. You cradle the glass. You breathe slow. You think, absurdly, of doors.

"Do you want me to describe it first?" Wren asked. "So you know what's coming?"

"No." I turned the bottle in my fingers. Snow-light moved inside it. "If you tell me, I'll be braced for it. I want to be ambushed by my own life for once."

"That's very you," he said.

"You keep saying that. 'That's very you.' How would you know? You've only met the replacement model."

"I'll tell you a secret," Wren said, and leaned close enough that I could see the grey coming into his temples, new since the siege, since the nights he held me upright on the walls and spoke my history aloud so I wouldn't dissolve mid-battle. "I fell in love with the replacement model first. Everything since has been confirmation."

"Wren."

"Casper."

"If I open this and discover I hated you, I want you to know I'll be very gracious about it."

"You won't," he said. "I checked."

And because he is insufferable and right, I stopped arguing and opened the door.

* * *

Snow.

Not snow as I know it now—snow as a fact of Ashenford winters, cold and inconvenient and tracked across floors. This was snow as a revelation. First snowfall, seen through eyes that had never seen it. A dying merchant's last good memory, lifted gently from him in a room that smelled of cloves and lamp oil, and bottled, and sold, and somehow—not sold. Kept. Of all the hundreds of charms I must have made in that cramped little shop above the chandlery, this is the one I hid from my own ledger.

I understand why now. Oh, I understand why.

In the memory I am standing at a window—my window, my shop, the glass warped with age—and the first flakes are coming down over Vell's crooked rooftops, and I am watching them with the merchant's wonder laid over my own careful thief's stillness, and for one moment the two feelings are the same feeling. Wonder without ownership. Joy on loan. And underneath both, quiet as a coin slipped into a pocket: *someday I will see this for myself.*

Well. Here I am.

Here I am, and outside the window of our rooms above Hale's office in free Ashenford, it is actually snowing—first snowfall of the season, arrived this morning like it had read the schedule—and the memory in my head and the weather at the glass have finally shaken hands. The merchant's wonder is mine now. Not borrowed. Not bottled. Just mine, worn smooth from nine months in a pocket, the way a river stone gets smooth.

I came back to myself crying, which was undignified, and found Wren crying too, which was worse, because he had promised me he wouldn't.

"You said—" I started.

"I lied," he said. "I'm told it runs in the trade."

"How was it?" he asked, when we had both recovered some shreds of dignity. "For you. Actually."

How was it. It was like drinking water after months of hearing it described. It was like finding a letter in your own handwriting that you don't remember writing. It was small—it was such a small thing, snow at a window—but it was *hinged*, somehow, it connected to everything: to the girl whose childhood was stolen by a court that fed on her, to the thief who built herself out of other people's leftovers, to the weapon who emptied herself to break a throne, to the blank woman who woke up and chose, as her first act, to take a stranger's hand. All of it was in that little bottle, folded up like a map. All of it pointed here.

"It fit," I said, and heard my own surprise.

* * *

We went out into it, because there was no reason on earth to stay inside.

Ashenford in its first snow is a different city than the one I supposedly bled for. The refugee quarter has become just—the quarter. There are children sledding down the steps of the old Ministry of Purity, which Seraphine says would have gotten them hanged two years ago, and which she now watches with an expression she'd deny under torture. The salt road is open again, carts creaking in with winter goods, and nobody checks papers at the gate because there are no papers anymore, just names, offered and believed. The Court That Chose meets in a converted granary and argues loudly about drainage, which Seraphine insists is the highest achievement of civilization. She may be right. I wouldn't know. I'm building my opinions from scratch and currently favoring anything that involves pastry.

Nettle found us in the square, as she always does, materializing out of a crowd like a pickpocket's idea of providence. She's taller. When did that happen?

"You opened it," she said, breathless, snowflakes caught in her lashes. "I can tell. Your face is doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like you just got given something you didn't steal." She considered me with the grave professionalism of a twelve-year-old appraising merchandise. "It suits you. You should wear it more."

"I intend to. Seasonally."

She grinned and stole Wren's scarf off his neck in passing, purely on principle, and was gone before he finished saying her name. He didn't chase her. Somewhere in these months he's learned that some thefts are gifts, and that chasing a child through snow is a battle you lose twice.

"She remembers it, you know," Wren said quietly, watching the crowd swallow her. "Your breaking. She was there. She says the storm of you tasted like everyone you ever saved."

"Did she offer to lend it back?"

"Every week. I keep telling her you'd rather earn your own."

I looked at him. Snow in his hair, grey at his temples, that ridiculous hopeful expression he's worn since the docks, since the tea, since a man bought the sea three times trying to find a way to say *stay*. I don't remember loving him. That's the truth of it, the honest wound of it. But I remember the memory of the snowfall, and I know what it is to choose a thing again and again until choosing becomes knowing.

"Wren," I said. "Tell me about the first time you saw snow."

He blinked. "Why?"

"Because I'm going to hear it a hundred times, probably. Because next winter I'll ask again, and the winter after that. Because some things aren't remembered." I took his hand, bare in the cold, and laced my cold fingers through his warmer ones. "They're chosen. Season after season. So start talking, agent of a dead court. We have years of this to get through, and I intend to collect all of it."

He smiled—that smile, the one I'm told undid me once before and appears fully capable of doing it again.

"Well," he said, as the snow came down on the free city, on the sled-children and the open gates and the granary where the future argued about drainage, "it was a lot like this one, actually. Only I hadn't met you yet. So it was missing something."

And we stood there in the falling white, a woman with winter behind her eyes and a man full of stories she was learning by heart, and the snow kept coming down like the world was starting over—which, as far as I'm concerned, it was.

First snowfall. Again.

Again, whenever I choose it.
