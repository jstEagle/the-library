## 1. Charms for the Lawful

There were three rules Tamsin Reyes lived by in Saltmere, and she recited them every morning the way other people said their prayers: *Never charm anything a magistrate might carry. Never let them see your hands move. And never, ever call it magic.*

She was saying them to herself now, under her breath, while she arranged her little table of trinkets on Fishgut Row where the morning fog came off the Sundered Sea thick as wool and everyone's face arrived half-blurred, which was good for business.

"Charms for the lawful!" she called, because irony only worked if you committed to it. "Blessed knots, lucky buttons, buttons that were never lucky in their lives! Fair prices for honest folk who believe in honest things!"

The word *blessed* was legal. The word *lucky* was legal. What the Writ of Sundering had outlawed — what the Wardens with their salt-iron badges would break your fingers over — was the working of it, the actual turning of the world's grain against its natural lie. A knot tied by an ordinary woman was string. A knot tied by Tamsin Reyes, with three specific pulls and a word breathed into the third one, was a luck-knot that would keep a dockworker's footing on ice-slick planks for a season. The difference was invisible. That was the entire art of it.

The fog helped too. Fog was a kind of mercy, in Saltmere. Everything here came in softened — the harbor that wasn't a harbor anymore, just a frozen basin walled off from the open sea by a shelf of grey ice that had not cracked in twelve years; the cranes standing dead over the water like gallows nobody used; the great chain across the harbor mouth, rusted red, that they'd raised in the first winter and never lowered because there was nothing left coming through it worth letting pass.

Tamsin had been fourteen when the Sundering happened. She was twenty-six now, and she had spent those twelve years learning how small a life could be folded, like paper, until it fit inside a lie.

Her first customer of the day was Brannick, who worked the coal barges upriver and had shoulders like a doorframe and a wife who made him buy one of these knots every autumn whether he believed in them or not.

"The usual," he grunted, planting himself before her table. His breath steamed. It was cold for early autumn, colder than it should have been, and had been for years. Everyone said so and no one did anything about it, because what was there to do about weather?

"For you, Brannick, only the finest." She took up a knot from the basket beneath her table — plain brown cord, doubled seven times, finished last night by candlelight with the third pull and the breath-word, *hold*, which was such a small word to do such illegal work. "This one will keep your feet under you when the frost comes down from the north. Tie it to your belt loop. Don't untie it. Untying unbinds."

"Superstition," Brannick said comfortably, paying her two coppers and a bit of tallow he'd gotten somewhere, because everyone on Fishgut Row paid partly in barter and the tallow would be useful for her boots.

"Absolutely," Tamsin agreed. "Pure superstition. Two coppers is a fair price for superstition."

He grinned, tucked the knot away, and clomped off into the fog, and Tamsin watched him go and felt the small warm thing in her chest that she always felt when a charm went out into the world doing its quiet unlawful work. Somewhere tonight a man would slip on black ice and his boot would find its grip instead of the sky, and he'd never know why, and that was fine. That was better than fine. That was the whole point.

*Never charm anything a magistrate might carry.* Because a Warden's badge was salt-iron and salt-iron remembered what touched it, and if a luck-knot's working brushed against a badge during an inspection, the badge would taste the craft and sing about it later. She'd seen a button-seller on Tanner's Wharf lose both hands to a Warden's maul for less. Not killed — the Writ preferred mutilation for hedge-craft, something about the punishment fitting the crime, hands being the instruments. The button-seller had gone back to selling within the month, holding coins between her wrists while someone else counted them out, and the whole row had bought from her after that, quietly, out of respect or guilt, and Tamsin had never decided which was worse.

She sold a *safe-passage* ribbon to a fishwife whose daughter was traveling inland — that one was barely even work, just a wish knotted into cloth, the kind of thing grandmothers had done openly for centuries before the Sundering made wishing a crime. She sold a jar of salve for chilblains that contained no craft whatsoever and was honestly just goose fat and rosemary, because you had to sell some honest things or the dishonest ones started to smell wrong. She turned away a man who wanted a love-charm, as she always did.

"No?" he said, aggrieved. He had the look of a clerk, ink-stained fingers, a face like a complaint waiting to be filed. "It's just a ribbon. You've got ribbons right there."

"They're decorative."

"I'll pay silver."

"Silver doesn't change what I sell." She kept her voice flat and bored, the voice of a woman discussing the price of onions. Inside, her stomach had gone tight, because men who wanted love-charms were the second most dangerous kind of customer, right behind the ones who wanted harm-charms, and both kinds tended to get loud when refused. "Ribbons. Buttons. Knots for luck. Salve. That's the stock."

"You're a fraud, then."

"Completely," she agreed, and smiled at him until he left, muttering.

The truth was she could probably have done it. A nudge, a softening, a little warmth tilted toward whoever he wanted warm toward him — she'd been taught all of it, and more, in the years before Saltmere, in the green hills upcountry where her mistress had found her at nine years old digging herbs out of a churchyard wall and said, *you have the hands for this, girl*, and Tamsin hadn't understood yet what a terrible sentence that could be.

She didn't think about her mistress. There was a rule for that too, though she kept it off the morning list, because the morning list was for survival and that rule was for staying sane.

* * *

By midday the fog burned off to a thin grey gauze and the row filled up. Tamsin liked midday best, when she could stop performing and just watch the city go by — the porters hauling crates of pickled fish that would never again ship anywhere by sea, since the sea itself had withdrawn behind its wall of ice; the coal-smoke rising straight up in windless air; the Wardens walking their rounds in pairs, unhurried, their salt-iron badges dull as old spoons. Twelve years of winter-that-wouldn't-commit, frost in the mornings well into summer, and the city bent around it like water around a stone, the way cities do, until bending looked like standing still.

That was the thing about Saltmere nobody said out loud: the Sundering hadn't just taken the harbor. It had taken the future. Ships couldn't come in. Ships couldn't leave. Half the trade routes died in a single night when the water froze solid from the chain-gate to the horizon — froze in high summer, froze unnaturally, froze in shapes the fishermen swore weren't natural ice, though no one listened to fishermen about unnatural things anymore because everything was unnatural now and you got used to it or you left, and where exactly would you go?

Tamsin had thought once about leaving. Upcountry, past the hills, maybe south where her mistress said the old coven lands still held warmth. But leaving meant roads, and roads meant checkpoints, and checkpoints meant Wardens looking at your hands, and besides —

Besides, Saltmere was where the Sundering had happened, and whatever had caused it, whatever power had torn a harbor loose from the ocean and crowned the wreckage in permanent frost, that power lived here. And Tamsin had a private theory, kept in the same locked room of her mind as her memories of her mistress, that a city full of broken magic was the safest place in the world for a witch in hiding. Too much noise. Her small craft vanished into it like a candle carried at noon.

A child stopped at her table — a girl, six or seven, gap-toothed, staring at the luck-knots with the naked hunger children had for pretty things.

"How much for the blue one?"

"That one's not for sale." It wasn't. The blue cord was hers, tied around her own wrist, renewed each new moon, and it did something she'd never advertised to anyone: it dulled her, in the way a hood dims a lamp. Damped her scent, if you wanted to put it in ugly terms. Made her read, to anything that tasted for craft, as exactly what she appeared to be — a trinket-seller with chapped hands.

"It's pretty."

"It is." Tamsin dug in her pocket and came out with a honey-drop, slightly linty, and traded it for the girl's attention. "Take this instead and tell your mother the trinket-woman says the frost is coming early this year. Have her mend the east window. Superstition, of course."

"Of course," the girl said solemnly, around the honey-drop, and ran off, and Tamsin flexed her fingers and tried not to think about the fact that she'd just given away a free divination. Small ones were legal-ish. Everyone read weather signs. But hers hadn't been reading signs. Hers had been knowing.

She was getting careless. Twelve years in, and the fear was wearing smooth, wearing comfortable, and comfortable fear was the dangerous kind. She made a note to renew the blue cord early this month. She made a note to stop giving away free divinations. She made a note, as she did every few weeks, that notes-to-self were becoming her closest confidants, and pushed that thought back into the locked room with the rest.

* * *

Evening came down early, the way it did now, the sun quitting the sky by late afternoon like a worker with a grievance. Tamsin packed her unsold stock — the salve, the honest buttons, four luck-knots, two safe-passage ribbons — into her basket, wrapped her cash-coppers in a rag, and set out along the row toward home with her collar up and her head down.

Home was a rented room above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, one window, one bed, one stove that ate coal like sin eats a soul. On the way she allowed herself her one indulgence, which was the rag-and-bone cart at the corner of Bellows Lane, because Tamsin Reyes collected books the way misers collected coin: not to read, particularly, but because paper was wealth you could hide in a wall.

"Marta," she said. "What have you got?"

Marta, seventy and built like a barrel of nails, grinned with three teeth and swept a canvas off the day's haul. Broken chairs. A birdcage. A box of books water-stained nearly to paste — a salvage lot off some flooded cellar in the low quarter, and the low quarter flooded whenever the frozen harbor backed up its meltwater, which was often, because frozen water doesn't drain properly, one of the hundred small indignities the Sundering visited on ordinary plumbing.

"Paste," Tamsin said, leafing through. Most of them were. Sermons, mostly — the low quarter had apparently drowned a chapel's library — swollen blocks of pulp fused shut, pages ruined, covers green with mold.

Except one.

It was a fat book, bound in leather gone stiff and dark, and where the others had bloated evenly this one had dried crooked, warped like a body left sitting too long, and the water had gotten into it selectively. Some pages were brick-hard with damp. Others — she could feel it with her fingertips, and her fingertips were trained — others were dry as bone inside, protected somehow, as if the damage had gone around certain pages rather than through them.

Which was impossible. Water didn't choose.

"One copper," Marta said, watching her face. "You've made that face at it already. One copper and don't haggle, my knees are killing me."

"One copper," Tamsin agreed instantly, haggling being pointless when you'd have paid five, and tucked the book under her arm.

She told herself, climbing the stairs to her room with the chandler's cat complaining at her heels, that she'd bought it for the blank pages. Good paper was good paper. A century-old rag-stock beat anything made now, and a hedge-witch needed paper the way a soldier needed boots — for charms, for notes, for the lists she kept making. That was the story. That was the reasonable, lawful, trinket-selling explanation.

She lit her candle, set the book on her table, and opened it.

Mostly blank, as promised — but not entirely. Here and there, in a cramped brown ink faded almost to ghosthood, someone had written. Fragments. She caught a phrase in a margin — *and the crown remembers its maker* — and another, mid-page, in a different, shakier hand: *we should never have tied it to a living line.*

Hedge-witch work. She knew it the way you knew a family accent. The phrasing, the abbreviations, the little sigil shorthand in the margins — someone of the craft had written this, a long time ago, and written it scared.

Tamsin turned pages carefully, her heartbeat picking up in a way she disliked intensely. Rules existed for nights like this. Rule four, unwritten: *never touch strange craft-work.* This wasn't a charm, exactly, but books could be charmed, and this book had pages the water had politely declined to ruin.

Near the back, the paper changed texture under her fingers — that dry-as-bone stock, untouched by the flood — and she smoothed it flat beneath her palm to read what was there.

It was a date.

Not today's date. A date a century old, written in that cramped brown hand and then — this was the part that made her sit down without meaning to — circled. Circled twice, the second circle pressed hard enough to tear the paper, as if the writer had come back to it later and wanted to be sure.

And beneath the circled date, in that shakier second hand, in words she read three times because they refused to make sense and then made sense all at once:

*She will come on this day. A hedge-daughter, hiding in the Sundered City, selling charms for the lawful. She has her mother's hands. God forgive us, we wrote her into it ourselves.*

The candle guttered, though the window was shut.

Tamsin sat very still in her one-room life above the chandler's shop, with her coppers wrapped in their rag and her blue cord on her wrist and her rules recited that morning as always — and outside, for the first time in twelve years, the frost on her window began, very slowly, to grow inward.
