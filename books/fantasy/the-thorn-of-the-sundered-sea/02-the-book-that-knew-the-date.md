## 2. The Book That Knew the Date

The rain came down on Saltmere like it was trying to wash the city off the map, and Tamsin Reyes had three coppers in her apron pocket and a rule against spending two of them on anything she couldn't eat.

She broke the rule at half past four, standing under the striped awning of Quill & Crate, because the book in the window had blank pages.

Not mostly blank. Not the kind of blank that meant a printer had gone under mid-run and someone had tried to salvage the stock. The kind of blank that meant *paper, good paper, laid paper with a tooth to it*, the kind hedge-witches paid a week's earnings for when they could find it, because charm-work wanted paper that could hold an ink of salt and ash without bleeding. She could see the watermark from the street — a little anchor, old style, pre-Sundering — and her fingers itched before her head finished doing the arithmetic.

Twelve years in Saltmere had taught her to want things quietly. Wanting loudly got you noticed, and getting noticed was how hedge-witches ended up in the harbor, or what was left of the harbor, which was a brackish cut of water behind a wall of ice that hadn't melted since before she was born. So she looked at the book the way you look at a stranger's coat: assessing, casual, already calculating how it would sit on your shoulders.

Then she went inside and haggled for it like a woman buying a hymnal.

"Water damage," said the shopkeeper, a narrow man named Pell who smelled of glue and vinegar and who had never once met her eyes longer than courtesy required, which was one reason she kept coming back. "Got it off a barge sale out of the drowned quarter. Whole crate of them. Most are ruined."

"How much ruined?"

"The pages swell. Ink runs. This one—" he turned it over with two fingers, the way you handle something you've already written off, "—this one's mostly front matter. Old devotional, I'd guess. The text's gone soft. But the back half's clean."

He was wrong about that, though Tamsin didn't know it yet. What he wasn't wrong about was the price, which started at eight coppers and ended, after eleven minutes of Tamsin explaining with great sincerity that she was a seamstress and the paper was for pressing collars, at five.

Five coppers. Two left in her pocket. She bought bread on the way home and told herself the arithmetic worked, and mostly believed it.

* * *

Her room above the chandlery was seven feet by nine and held a bed, a stool, a locked box under the floorboard, and everything else she owned, which fit into the space those things didn't take. The rain kept up its argument with the roof. She lit her candle — tallow, stub-end, the kind of economy that would have shamed her mistress — and set the book on the stool, and for a while she just looked at it.

It had a leather cover the color of wet slate, cracked along the spine, swollen at the corners where water had gotten in and stayed. No title. There had been one, tooled into the front, but the tooling had been scraped away — not worn, *scraped*, deliberate, with a blade. Someone had wanted this book anonymous.

Tamsin had rules about books. Rule one: never buy a grimoire. Grimoires were evidence, and evidence got you drowned. Rule two: never buy anything with writing in it that you couldn't account for, because writing was where people hid things, and things hidden in Saltmere were hidden for reasons involving the Wardens' Office. Rule three: blank pages were safe, blank pages were honest, blank pages were just paper waiting to be useful.

This book had seemed like a stack of rule threes bound in slate-colored leather.

She opened it at the back and worked forward, because you always work forward from what's intact. The last third was exactly what Pell had promised: clean laid paper, the anchor watermark crisp, page after empty page smelling faintly of salt and dust. Her hands actually shook a little. A year's worth of charm-paper. Two years if she stretched it. Luck-knots written on paper like this would hold their luck through a whole winter instead of shedding it by Candlemas.

Then she reached the middle, where the water had been, and the pages weren't blank anymore.

They had been blank once — or at least they had been *clean* — but the damp had done something strange to them. Instead of swelling and tearing, they had taken ghost-images, ink from elsewhere bleeding up through the paper like bruises surfacing on skin. Words floated there, browned and broken, whole lines legible and then whole lines gone, as if a book had drowned inside this book and its words were still trying to get out.

Tamsin sat very still.

She knew what water-damage looked like. She had lived above a chandlery for six years; she had dried out more soaked ledgers than she could count, for neighbors who couldn't read and paid her in candles. Water made ink bloom and run. Water did not make ink *arrive*. And yet here were words on pages that Pell — a man who had handled this book enough to price it twice — had sworn were clean.

The candle guttered. She turned the page.

Mostly ruin. A phrase about *the sea remembering*. A word that might have been *crown*. A list of names, all of them dissolved except the last letters. Then a page where the bleeding had pooled instead of spread, and out of the pool rose a single drawing: two figures, small, crude, the work of someone in a hurry. One stood with arms open. One knelt. Between them, a line — a thread, or a road, or a crack.

Beneath the drawing, in the same hurried hand, a sentence had survived almost whole:

*When the winter is oldest, the witch of the low door and the mapmaker who lost his way will be drawn together, and the thorn will know its own.*

Tamsin read it twice. Then she laughed, once, short and ugly, because she had spent twelve years learning to laugh at exactly this kind of thing. Prophecy was bait. Prophecy was what clever people wrote to part fools from their coppers, and she was not a fool, and a scrap of bleeding ink in a drowned book was not destiny, it was coincidence wearing a costume, and she had a luck-knot to finish by morning and no time for ghosts.

Except.

Except that her hands had stopped shaking only when she touched the page, and they had stopped in a way that felt less like calm and more like being *held*.

She should have closed the book then. She wants you to know — whoever you are, whatever you think of her after — that she knew. The knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, cold and specific, and she opened the next page anyway. That is the whole story of Tamsin Reyes, if you want it in one line: she always opens the next page.

* * *

The date was circled on the fourth page past the drawing.

It was circled in red — real red, the expensive mineral kind that doesn't bleed, which meant the circle had gone onto the page dry and clean and *before* the water ever found the book. Around it the ghost-ink swirled, but the circle itself sat sharp and certain, and inside it, in a cramped antique hand:

*the 14th of Deepmonth*

And beneath the circle, smaller, in ink that had faded to the color of old tea:

*she comes to the city with salt in her shoes and a knot of luck in her pocket, and her hands smell of ash, and she does not know whose blood warms her*

Tamsin put the book down. Then she picked it up again, because putting it down didn't help, the words stayed on the page either way, and she read them a third time with the flat, careful attention of a woman checking a lock she already knows is broken.

Today was the 14th of Deepmonth.

She had come to Saltmere twelve years ago, at fourteen, walking the coast road for nine days, and the last day she had walked the tideline below the ice-wall with her boots off because the straps had rubbed her feet raw, and the harbor water had gotten into her shoes. Salt in her shoes. She remembered it because it had hurt, and because her mother had always said pain was a teacher, and because she'd been fourteen and grieving and stupid enough to listen.

A knot of luck in her pocket. She had carried a luck-knot from her mother's hands the entire journey — red cord, three strands, tied in the pattern her mother called *the low door* — and she had carried it until the day she'd understood that carrying it openly in Saltmere was carrying her own arrest warrant, and she had buried it under a stone outside the city walls rather than burn it, because burning it had felt like burning her mother.

Her hands smelled of ash. They smelled of ash right now. Charm-work meant ash — salt-ash and lamp-black, the lawful stinks of unlawful craft — and no amount of lye soap took it fully out of the skin. She raised her hands to her face and breathed in, and there it was, faint and constant, the smell she'd stopped noticing years ago.

Four details. Four small, stupid, checkable details, written in ink older than her grandmother, describing a girl walking toward a city that hadn't outlawed magic yet.

*And she does not know whose blood warms her.*

That line she read four times, and each time her chest got tighter around the swallowed stone. It wasn't a detail. Details could be coincidence — she'd built a whole life on coincidence, on plausible deniability, on being a seamstress with an interest in fine paper. But that line wasn't describing something she'd done. It was describing something she *was*, some fact sitting underneath her skin like a second skeleton, and the book knew it, and she didn't.

The candle went out.

No wind had come under the door. The rain hadn't shifted. The flame simply leaned over and died, the way a flame dies when something large passes close to it, and in the dark her room smelled suddenly of green things — sap, crushed stems, spring — which was impossible in Deepmonth, which was the dead heart of winter, which was the month Saltmere had been dying in for a hundred years.

Tamsin struck a match with hands that were not quite steady and relit the candle, and told herself, in the voice her mistress used to use for correcting sloppy knotwork — firm, patient, disappointed — that fear was just information arriving early. That the sensible response was to put the book in the box under the floorboard, sleep, sell nothing tomorrow, and decide in daylight whether she owned a haunted devotional or a very elaborate con aimed at a buyer she hadn't met yet.

She got as far as closing the cover.

Under her palm, through the wet-slate leather, she felt the pages shift. Not moved — *shifted*, the way a sleeping animal shifts, settling itself deeper. And from somewhere inside the book, from the drowned middle of it, came a smell of crushed thorns and, very faintly, a sound. She would spend the rest of her life failing to describe that sound honestly, because every description sounded mad. The closest she ever managed was: like a door opening at the bottom of a well, far away, patient.

The 14th of Deepmonth. Circled a hundred years ago, in red, dry ink, by someone who had known her before she existed — known her boots, her pockets, her hands, and one thing more, one thing she still didn't know herself.

Outside, the rain thickened toward snow. In the chandlery below, the last apprentice banked the fires and went home. And in the frozen dark beyond the ice-wall, something old began, very slowly, to walk.

Tamsin blew out the candle, put the book under the floorboard, and lay down on top of it, fully dressed, with her knife in her hand.

She slept anyway. That was the worst part. She slept like the dead until moonrise — and woke, at moonrise, to a light in the room that had no source, coming from the floorboard, seeping up between the boards like dawn rising from underneath the world.

The box was unlocked. She had locked it. And the book was already open to the page with her date, and the red circle was glowing like a coal, and the air above it had begun, gently, terribly, to bend — the way air bends over a fire, the way air bends at the edge of a door that is opening where no door has any right to be.

Tamsin grabbed her knife, and the floor took her like deep water takes a stone.
