## 4. Taken by the Page

Tamsin did not sleep.

She told herself it was the storm rattling her shutters, the ordinary noise of an ordinary night in Saltmere, but she'd lived through twelve years of storms and none of them had ever made the hair on her arms stand up before. She sat at her worktable with a lamp turned low and the prophecy book open in front of her, and she watched the circle drawn around today's date as if it might blink.

It didn't blink. It just sat there, brown ink gone rust with a century of damp, patient as a snare.

*The hedge-girl with salt-burned hands,* the old script said beneath it. *She comes to the page when the moon stands over the broken harbor.*

"Broken harbor," she muttered. "Half this city is a broken harbor." Saltmere's lower districts flooded every spring tide; you could not throw a stone without cracking a window that looked out on drowned streets. The line meant nothing. It was a riddle written by a dead fanatic for an audience of no one.

Her hands, though. Her hands were salt-burned. Twelve years of knotting charms and brewing luck-salts and hiding every trace of the craft under the smell of beeswax and vinegar, and her palms had gone rough and pale as driftwood. Anyone looking would see a trinket-seller. The book, apparently, saw something else.

She should burn it. That was rule one, the first rule Maren had ever taught her, back before everything: *if a thing knows your name, put it in the fire.* The book didn't know her name — she'd checked three times, turning every water-warped leaf, reading and re-reading the cramped hand until her eyes ached — but it knew her hands, and that was nearly worse. A name could be changed. Hands came with you.

Tamsin stood, took the book by its swollen spine, and carried it toward the hearth.

The moon cleared the clouds.

She felt it happen the way you feel a door open behind you in an empty house — a change in the pressure of the air, a coldness sliding down her neck. The firelight bent. Not guttered — bent, leaning toward the book like wheat toward wind, and the pages lifted though there was no draft, and the circle around today's date began to drink the light.

"No," Tamsin said, which was stupid, which was the sort of thing people said in stories right before the story ate them. She dropped the book and backed away and the book did not fall. It hung where she'd dropped it, splayed open, its pages riffling upward like something swimming, and the ink was moving now, the letters lifting off the paper in fine black threads that reached for her across the room.

She grabbed the table. The table helped for about a second. Then the threads found her hands — her salt-burned, charm-knotted, carefully hidden hands — and closed around them like cold silk, and the room peeled away.

Not like falling. Falling has direction. This was being *read*, being taken apart word by word and copied somewhere else, and in the half-second before the world went white Tamsin had one coherent thought, furious and small: *I paid good coin for that book.*

Then the floor was gone and the lamplight was gone and Saltmere was gone, and there was only the cold.

* * *

Snow woke her.

Not the sound of it — snow doesn't sound like anything, that was one of the lies of her childhood, because she had grown up inland among hedgerows and never learned what snow sounded like until it fell on canvas and hissed like frying fat. No: the *weight* of it woke her, a soft enormous pressure along her whole left side, and then the cold underneath it, climbing through her coat, her skirt, her skin, announcing itself in her teeth.

Tamsin opened her eyes to a sky full of falling white and no stars at all.

She lay still, because stillness was the first rule of waking up somewhere wrong — don't move, don't speak, take inventory. Fingers: ten, aching. Toes: present, numb. Body: bruised along one hip where she'd evidently landed on something. Magic: she reached for it the way you reach for a knife you keep under the mattress, that low warm hum behind her breastbone that Maren had spent years teaching her to bank and smother — and it answered, faint but there, muffled like a voice through a wall.

That frightened her more than the snow. Her magic had never been quiet.

She sat up. Snow slid off her shoulders in a soft collapse. And she saw where she was.

Ruins. A city of them, or the bones of one — streets she was lying in the middle of, running away from her in four directions between walls that rose three stories and then stopped, snapped off, as if some giant hand had come down flat and sheared the tops off everything. Ice sheeted the cobbles. Snow filled the window sockets of dead houses. Down the long avenue to her left, columns stood in a row holding up nothing, and beyond them, far off, something vast and pale loomed through the weather — a wall, maybe, or a cliff, or a building so large it had stopped being a building.

There was no harbor. There was no sea. There was no smell of salt and tar and fish, the smell that had soaked into her for twelve years, and its absence was the loudest thing about the place.

"This isn't Saltmere," she said out loud, because saying it made it a fact instead of a fear.

"It is not."

Tamsin came off the ground like a cat, stumbling upright, heart slamming — and there was a man sitting against the sheared wall six feet away, snow to his knees, watching her with an expression she couldn't read at all. He was older than her, forty perhaps, gaunt in the way of someone who had once been heavier and had lost it to worry rather than poverty. Grey in his dark hair. Long fingers, ink-stained even now, even here, wrapped around a leather tube he clutched like a weapon. His coat was fine wool, ruined. His boots were city boots. He was going to die in those boots, she thought absently, if they stayed wherever this was longer than a day.

"You were pulled through too," he said. Not a question.

"Where is *through?*" Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Good. Rule two: never let them see the knife-hand shake. "Who are you? What is this place?"

He pushed himself up the wall, slowly, joints cracking, and inclined his head with a formality so out of place amid the ruins that it was almost funny. "Edric Vale. Formerly cartographer royal to the crown of Vael." Something crossed his face when he said *formerly*, something old and bitter. "And this—" he gestured at the dead avenue, the broken columns, the pale mass looming in the snowfall, "—is Caer Sullen. The old capital. Or what the Sundering left of it."

Caer Sullen. Tamsin went very still.

Everyone in Saltmere knew the name the way you know the name of a disease. The old capital, a hundred miles north, abandoned after the Sundering — the night the sea tore itself out of the harbor, the night the king died, the night magic broke bad enough that the survivors wrote outlawry into law and Saltmere had been hanging hedge-witches ever since. Nobody went to Caer Sullen. Nobody *wanted* anything that lived in Caer Sullen.

"The Sundering was a hundred years ago," she said. "This city's been empty for—"

"Yes."

"Then why does the snow look freshly swept off those steps?" She pointed. He followed her finger. Between two fallen columns, a staircase descended into darkness, and its treads were clear — scoured clean, recently, in a place where no one had walked for a century.

Edric Vale looked at the stairs for a long moment. Then he looked at her, really looked, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd seen a hundred times on a hundred faces: *the girl from the page.* His gaze dropped to her hands, curled at her sides, and stayed there a beat too long.

"You bought the book," he said. "Water-damaged. From a rag-and-bone stall near the fish gate. Red binding, missing its clasp."

Ice went down Tamsin's spine that had nothing to do with the weather. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've been looking for it for eleven months." He said it flatly, without apology, and somehow that was worse than menace. "I've been looking for *you*, though I didn't have your face until yesterday. Only your description. Hedge-girl. Salt-burned hands. I'd narrowed it to four cities and Saltmere was always the likeliest." He exhaled, breath smoking. "I'm sorry. I intended to reach you before the moonrise. I was too slow."

"Reach me to do *what?*"

"To warn you." His mouth twisted. "And to ask for your help. Neither of which matters now, since the page has already done its reading. We're both named in it, mistress — I've known my part since I was twenty-two years old and too clever for my own good. It's how I lost the court, actually. Drawing a map I shouldn't have opened." He patted the leather tube against his chest, almost tenderly. "You're the other subject. So am I. The prophecy doesn't fetch one of us. It fetches both."

"I'm not a subject of anything." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "I sell luck-knots to dockworkers. I am nobody's hedge-girl. Whatever your book says—"

"*Our* book," Edric said quietly, "says the winter ends or it doesn't. Says two come to the frozen capital, one by blood and one by debt. Says the crown is worn by the wrong thing." He paused, and for the first time his composure flickered, and she saw the fear underneath — real fear, long-habituated, worn smooth as a river stone. "I've spent eleven months telling myself it was metaphor. Standing here, I find I've stopped believing that."

Somewhere down the avenue, past the columns, past the pale looming mass — a palace, she realized, it had to be a palace — something moved. Not a person. Not an animal. A shape that unfolded the way smoke unfolds, tall and thin and jointed wrongly, black against the whitefall, and then folded itself back into the gloom as if it had never been.

Tamsin's mouth went dry. Beside her, Edric had gone rigid, and his hand found her sleeve — not pulling, just gripping, the grip of a man holding onto the only other warm thing in a dead world.

"We can't stay in the open," he whispered.

"And we can't go down those nice clean stairs either."

"No," he agreed. "But the storm will kill us in an hour, and that thing will kill us faster, and I know these streets better than anyone alive, because I drew them." He finally released her sleeve and unslung the leather tube, and there was something almost grimly funny in his face. "You don't trust me. You're right not to. But I am, at present, the only map you have — and you are, whether you like it or not, the reason the page brought me here. So."

He held out his ink-stained hand. Snow settled on his palm and did not melt.

Tamsin looked at it. She thought of Maren's voice: *if a thing knows your name, put it in the fire.* She thought of the shape unfolding in the distance, and of her magic humming muffled behind her ribs like a prisoner tapping on a wall, and of the hundred-mile walk south that would kill her long before she saw Saltmere again.

Rule three, the one she'd invented herself, the one that had kept her alive twelve hidden years: *survive first. Regret later.*

"Not because I believe you," she said, taking his hand. His grip was freezing and careful, like she was something breakable, and she hated that too. "Because you're the only thing in this city I recognize."

"That," said Edric Vale, glancing once more at the palace looming in the snowfall, at the high broken wall where — for just a heartbeat, she was almost sure — a tall figure stood watching them, crowned and still, "is going to have to be enough."

Behind them, unnoticed by either, the prophecy book lay open in the drift where the page had dropped it, its ink already drying into new words neither of them would read until much too late:

*Both come. One by blood, one by debt. The thorn will know its own.*
