## 5. What the Sundering Broke

Edric Vale had spent the better part of two decades learning how to say terrible things plainly. The crown had taught him that, in its way — a cartographer who flinched from the truth of a coastline drowned a hundred fishermen, and so he had learned to draw hard lines and speak them harder. But standing in the frozen ruin of the old capital's great square, watching the hedge-girl wrap her arms around herself and pretend this was all some elaborate trick, he found that plain speech was not the same as kind speech.

"You're cold," he said. "We should move."

"I'm not going anywhere with you." Tamsin Reyes said it to the snow, to the broken colonnade, to the sky — to anything but him. "This is a dream. Or a trap. Or I hit my head at the market and I'm dying in a gutter on Copper Lane, which frankly would be an improvement."

"Would it?" He kept his voice level. "Dying?"

She glared at him. Up close, without the market crowd between them, she looked younger than the woman who'd sold him a luck-knot three days ago with a smile like a locked door. Younger, and more frightened, and fighting both with the particular fury of someone who had survived by never letting either show.

"Where are we?" she demanded.

"The old capital. Corvane." He gestured at the square around them, at the shattered fountain crusted in ice, at the palace rising above the rooftops with its windows dark as eye sockets. "Or what's left of it. No one has lived here properly since the Sundering."

"There is no old capital. There's Saltmere, and there's the north country, and there's nothing in between but—" She stopped. He watched her stop herself, watch her do the arithmetic she'd been refusing since she woke in the snow beside him. "That's what they teach children," she said quietly. "Nothing in between."

"They teach children a great many convenient things." Edric pulled off his glove and pressed his bare palm flat against the nearest column. Under the frost, the stone still bore the chisel-marks of the masons who'd raised it — and beneath those, fainter, older marks that no chisel had made. He'd mapped this city once, from royal archives, before anyone understood why it mattered. "Come here. Touch this. Tell me what you feel."

"I'm not touching your haunted rock."

"It isn't haunted. It's hungry. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire reason you're standing here instead of asleep in your bed on Copper Lane."

She didn't come closer. But she didn't walk away either, and after twelve years of hiding, he supposed walking away into a frozen city she'd been taught didn't exist was its own kind of impossible. He took pity on her. Or told himself he did.

"Sit, then. Listen. You bought a book for its paper; the least you can do is hear what it cost the man who wrote it."

* * *

He built the story the way he'd once built maps: from the edges inward.

"Before the Sundering, there were nine cities on the coast of Vail," he said. They sat in the shelter of the collapsed colonnade, out of the wind that came off the dead harbor like a blade. "Corvane was the greatest of them, and Corvane's harbor was the wonder of the age — deep enough for a hundred ships, warded against storm and siege alike. And the wards weren't the king's work. That's the part nobody remembers, because it's the part nobody wanted true."

Tamsin's eyes flicked up. "Whose work?"

"Hedge-witches." He said it gently, watching her face. "The old covens of the coast. Salt-craft and knot-work and blood-memory. The harbor's protection was theirs — woven into the water itself, renewed every generation. The king gave them law and leave. It was the one bargain in Vail's history that everyone honored, because everyone could see it worked."

"That's not—" She stopped again. He could see the other sentence behind her teeth, the one about what hedge-witches were now, what they'd been forced to become. "Go on."

"King Aldous the Third ruled for forty years. Good king, by the measures that matter — he fed the poor years, he kept the peace, and when the witches came to renew their bargain, he met them himself at the water's edge. And then, in the fortieth year, something came to court wearing a gift." Edric looked down at his hands. This was the part he'd rehearsed, the part he'd carried alone through disgrace and exile, and saying it aloud to a stranger felt like opening a vein. "A crown. Grown, not forged. Thorn-wood, black as pitch, beautiful beyond reason. A gift from the deep forest for the king's jubilee. And Aldous put it on."

"And?" Her voice had gone very quiet.

"And it fit perfectly. That should have been the first warning. Crowns don't fit perfectly; they're made to be endured. This one fit like it had been measured. Because it had been measured — by something that had been growing toward his head for longer than any of us have been alive." He drew a breath. "The histories call what followed the Withering Year. Crops failed in a single season across nine provinces. Rivers ran thin. And the king stopped sleeping, stopped eating, stopped aging — sat the throne day and night, and the court noticed that where he walked, frost followed. Not metaphorically. His footprints bloomed ice."

Tamsin had gone pale. In the grey light of the ruined square, she looked carved from the same material as the city around them.

"The witches knew what it was," Edric continued. "Their oldest records spoke of thorn-things — rooted hungers, patient things that grow in wild places where old magic was spilled and never cleaned up. They went to the palace to take the crown off him. And that was when everything broke at once. Whether the thing felt them coming, or whether taking the crown would have killed the king anyway — no record survives that tells it straight. What we know is that the night the coven entered the palace, the harbor tore loose."

"Tore loose," she repeated. "The sea doesn't tear loose."

"This sea did." He stood, because sitting made the next part worse somehow, and pointed east, toward where the horizon should have held water and held only white. "The whole harbor. The wards were woven into the water, and when the coven died in the throne room, the weaving came apart — and took the water with it. Nine miles of harbor, ships and fish and the fishing fleet and eleven hundred people who lived on the water, lifted out of the world like a page torn from a book. Gone. Not sunk. *Gone.*"

The silence afterward had weight. Somewhere in the ruins, wind moved through empty windows and made a sound almost like voices.

"The Sundering," Tamsin said at last. "That's what the Sundering was."

"That's what the Sundering was. One night. The king dead on his throne — or mostly dead; we'll come to that, and I'm sorry in advance — the coven dead around him, the crown still on his head, growing. And the sea gone, and with it the weather, because the harbor-wards hadn't only guarded the coast. They'd steered the seasons. Winter came to stay." He turned back to her. "Saltmere exists because of that night, whether it likes it or not. Your city is the largest settlement left on the new coastline. Your harbor is a puddle compared to what was here. And the law against magic — the one you've spent twelve years hiding under — wasn't fear or superstition. It was grief with a statute number. The survivors decided magic had murdered their families, and they wrote that decision into law, and the law has been killing hedge-witches slowly ever since."

"My mother wasn't—"

"I know what your mother wasn't. I know what none of them were." He said it too fast, and saw her notice, and made himself slow down. "I helped write those laws' borders. Did the girl with the luck-knots tell you that yet? No? Well. Now she has. I was the royal cartographer, Tamsin. When they outlawed the craft, someone had to map the boundaries of where it could be practiced, where hunted, where pardoned. I drew those lines. I was very good at my work. And then I found out what the lines were for, and I objected, and objecting cost me my post and nearly cost me more." He laughed, short and bitter. "So understand me. I am not here as the law's man. I'm here because I owe a debt to a hundred dead women, and the interest has come due."

* * *

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that he checked twice that she hadn't simply frozen.

When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone handling something sharp. "Say the rest. The book circled today's date a hundred years ago. It described me. So whatever you think this prophecy wants—"

"What it wants is written on the last pages. I've read them. I've read them forty times." He crouched so they were eye to eye, and hated himself for the next words, and said them anyway, because plain speech was a discipline and he owed her that discipline even if he couldn't yet owe her the truth entire. "The winter ends when the crown comes off the king. The crown comes off when the thorn is unbound. And the thorn can only be unbound by someone carrying the old blood — the blood of the coven that crowned him, that wove the harbor, whose deaths broke everything. Someone like you."

"No." She said it immediately, instantly, the way you refuse a drink you've already swallowed. "No. Whatever you read, whatever that book says — I'm not that. I sell charms to dockworkers. I knot string. My mistress taught me weather-sense and fever-breaking and how to hide, and hiding is the whole curriculum. There is no bloodline. If there were a bloodline, the witch-hunters would have found it generations ago."

"The witch-hunters weren't looking for bloodlines. They were looking for practitioners. There's a difference, and the difference is the point — the blood passed down quiet, through daughters who never lit so much as a candle over a kettle." He watched her shake her head, watched the refusal build walls behind her eyes. "You knotted luck into a dockworker's line three days ago. I watched you do it. You didn't learn that in a week, Tamsin. You learned it in a marrow-deep way, the way you learn a language you were born speaking."

"Then I'll stop." Her chin came up, defiant, tears standing bright in her eyes and refused passage. "There. I'll stop. I'll burn my knots and smash my trinkets and live as honestly as anyone in Saltmere, and whatever the prophecy thinks it wants, it can starve waiting. I didn't ask for this. I didn't ask for any of it — not the book, not the circle, not being dragged through a page into a graveyard city by a man who draws maps for the people who hunt my kind!"

"I know."

"You don't know!" She was on her feet now, shouting into the frozen square, her voice cracking off the ice-glazed stone. "You talk about debts! About dead witches and drowned harbors and a hundred years of winter like they're entries in a ledger you get to balance! I have spent twelve years being small, being careful, being *nothing*, because being nothing meant being alive — and you want me to stand up and be the last daughter of some murdered coven because a dead man's book circled a date? Let the winter stay! Let the whole world freeze! At least the world frozen is a world where I exist!"

The echo came back off the palace walls, and for a moment neither of them moved.

Then Edric did the hardest thing he'd done in twenty years of doing hard things. He didn't argue. He reached into his coat, and drew out the map — his own work, drawn in exile, corrected and re-corrected until his fingers bled — and unfolded it between them on the frozen flagstones. The old capital in fine ink. The palace. And beneath it, in red, the route no living person had ever mapped: down through the cellars, under the foundations, into the space where the drowned harbor used to meet the earth.

"The blight-storms come off the dead harbor every few days," he said quietly. "You'll have seen the sky already. When they come, nothing survives above ground that breathes. We need shelter, and I know where the shelters are, because mapping them was the last honest work I ever did." He began folding the map. "You don't have to be the prophecy's daughter tonight. Tonight you just have to not die. Can we agree on that much?"

Tamsin stared at the map. Then at the palace on the hill, dark-windowed, patient. Something crossed her face that he couldn't read — not fear exactly, and not refusal, something underneath both, the shape of a question she wasn't ready to ask.

"The storm," she said. "How long do we have?"

Edric looked east, at the bruised light gathering over the place where the sea used to be, and felt the old dread settle into his bones like a returning bird.

"Less than an hour," he said. "Run."
