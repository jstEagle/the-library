## 6. The First Long Night

The storm came out of the north like something with a grudge.

Tamsin felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in her teeth, a pressure behind her eyes that she'd learned to read in Saltmere's alleys, where weather was the one law even the smugglers obeyed. She stopped on the frozen road and turned her face into the wind and tasted it: iron, rot, and under it all a sweetness like fruit going bad.

"Blight," she said.

Edric, three paces ahead with his ridiculous coat flapping around him, didn't stop. "What?"

"Storm-blite. It's not weather. Or it isn't *only* weather." She was already moving, scanning the white plain for what she needed. Twelve years of hiding had taught her to read a landscape the way other people read faces. "There'll be a waystation. There always was, on the old post roads. The king's roads had them every ten miles."

"The king is dead."

"His roads aren't." She pointed east, to a low swell in the snow that might have been natural or might have been a roof holding snow instead of shedding it. "There. If we're still walking when it hits, we die. Not freeze — *die*. Blight-storms get inside you. They grow."

Edric looked at the sky, then at her, then at the swell of snow. Whatever he saw in her face made him stop arguing. That, she would remember later. That he listened.

They reached it as the light went strange — greenish, bruised, the sun a pale coin behind thickening cloud. It was a waystation after all: a squat stone building half-swallowed by drift, its door swollen shut with ice. Edric put his shoulder to it twice and failed. Tamsin put her bare hand flat against the wood, found the water locked inside it, and *asked*.

The ice let go all at once. The door swung and Edric stumbled through and Tamsin followed, hauling it shut behind them, and only then did she realize what she'd done — casually, without thinking, the way you scratch an itch — in front of a witness.

Edric stood in the dark interior, breathing hard, looking at her hand.

"It's just a trick," she said. "Anyone could—"

"No." His voice was flat. Careful. "No, it isn't."

* * *

The waystation was one room: a hearth, a bench, a loft for sleeping, a window slitted against the weather. Someone had lived here once. Someone had left a stool, an iron pot, a stack of old straw. The walls were stone older than the Sundering, older than the outlawry — built back when the road had belonged to everyone.

Tamsin got a fire going from the driftwood stacked by the door, because fire was lawful and she clung to lawful things when her hands were shaking. Edric sat on the bench with his map case hugged to his chest like a child's doll and watched her, and the watching had changed. It wasn't suspicion anymore. Suspicion she knew how to carry; suspicion was almost respect.

This was fear.

Outside, the storm arrived.

She heard it coming the way you'd hear a crowd — a vast shuffling multitude sound, nothing like wind. When she risked the shuttered window, the world beyond was a wall of moving grey, and inside the grey were shapes. Threads. Filaments, fine as hair and long as rivers, whipping past the station, and wherever they touched stone they left marks: black veins spreading across the wall outside, frost that wasn't frost but something growing.

"Don't open that again." Edric hadn't moved from the bench.

"I wasn't going to." She let the shutter fall. "How long do blight-storms last?"

"I don't know. I'm a cartographer, not a—"

"A what?" She turned. "Say it. You've been waiting to say it since the door."

He set his jaw. "Since the Sundering, magic has been outlawed in every city that kept the king's peace. I helped draw those wards. I sat in rooms with men who signed the papers, and I drew the lines on maps showing where the hedge-witches lived, so the wardens would know which doors to knock on." He said it fast, like a confession spat out whole. "So no. I don't know how long a blight-storm lasts. But I know what my city decided people like you were."

"People like me."

"Hedge-witches. Charm-sellers. Anyone who carries the old blood."

"And you drew our doors." The fire cracked and spat. She made herself sit down across from him, made her voice stay level, because the other thing in her chest — twelve years old and sharp as a fishhook — wanted to come out screaming. "Did they find much? In Saltmere?"

"Some."

"How many is some?"

"Tamsin."

"*How many?*"

He looked at the fire. "I never saw the lists after the first year. I asked not to see them."

"You drew the maps and then you asked not to see them." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "That's very tidy. That's a very tidy kind of guilt."

"I know what I am." He said it quietly, and that was worse than shouting, because there was no anger in it to push against. Just a man sitting in a stone room with his crime laid out between them like goods on a stall. "I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm telling you so you know exactly who you're stranded with."

* * *

The storm grew teeth as the night went on.

It found the waystation around midnight. Tamsin woke to a sound like scratching at every wall at once, and lay still in the loft straw listening to it, and understood slowly that the sound was not wind. It was the filaments. They had reached the building, and they were testing it, the way a wasp tests a window.

By the time she got down the ladder, black veins had crept through the mortar joints on the north wall and were moving inward, slow as spilled ink. Where they crossed the hearthstone, the fire dimmed, guttering down to coals with a smell of sweetness and rot.

Edric was awake, pressed into the corner farthest from the wall, and his face in the dying firelight did something she had never seen a grown man's face do. It simply stopped pretending. He looked at the creeping black and there was nothing behind his eyes but animal dread.

"They're in the mortar," he said. "They're coming through the—"

"I know."

"There's no door that holds against this. There's no—"

"*I know.*"

And here was the arithmetic of her life, arriving as it always arrived, patient and total. She could stand in this room with a lawful man and watch the lawful dark come through the lawful walls, and they would both be dead by morning, honestly, cleanly, within the rules. Or she could do the thing.

Twelve years she'd hidden it. Twelve years of luck-knots sold as trinkets, of charms disguised as folk custom, of keeping her gift folded up small and tight and quiet as a moth in a matchbox. Because her mistress had told her what the blood was for, and because the wardens took the marked ones away, and because being seen was the first step on a road that ended either on a warden's cart or on an altar.

But the black was crossing the floor now, and it had reached the stool, and the stool was rotting in real time, collapsing into soft black mush with a smell like overripe plums, and Edric was making a small sound in his throat that she would hear for the rest of her life.

"All right," she said, to herself more than to him. "All right."

She knelt on the hearthstone and pulled off her gloves.

Hedge-craft wasn't lightning. Nobody threw fire. What her mistress had taught her, in the years before the teaching went wrong, was older and quieter than that: everything living wants something, and if you can learn what a thing wants, you can bargain. The blight wanted warmth. It wanted the wet heat of living things, blood-heat, breath-heat. It had crossed a frozen country to find theirs.

So she gave it somewhere else to go.

Not a lie — she couldn't lie to it, it would taste the falseness — but a redirecting. She opened herself the way her mistress had taught, the way she hadn't opened in twelve years, and offered the storm a vessel: the dead straw, the rotten stool, the old dry timber of the loft, anything that had ever been alive and wasn't now. *Here,* she said, in the language that had no words. *Here is warmth. Here is death already done. Feed on what's finished.*

The black veins stopped.

Then they turned. All of them, at once, like a flock changing direction, flowing across the floor toward her — toward the bundle of dead straw she held out in both hands — and she fed the storm the waystation's own corpse, piece by piece, keeping it busy, keeping it fed, keeping it *away*, while sweat froze on her spine and her vision tunneled down to a grey ring with black threads moving through it. Somewhere far away Edric was saying her name. She didn't have breath to answer.

The last of it poured into the straw, and the straw went to black dust, and the dust went still, and the pressure behind her eyes let go all at once, and she fell forward onto her hands on the hearthstone with the fire roaring up again beneath her, greedy and gold and ordinary, and she lay there gasping like a landed fish.

* * *

When she could sit up, Edric was crouched a few feet away. Not close. A few feet exactly, measured, the distance a man keeps from a dog that has just shown its teeth.

"You saved us," he said.

"Yes."

"That thing — that storm — it was going to come through the walls, and you—" He stopped. Started again. "What did you give it?"

"Nothing that mattered. Dead things. Old straw. Rot." Her voice sounded scraped. "Blight doesn't want living flesh particularly. It wants warmth and ending. I gave it a better table."

"And if you hadn't been here."

"Then we'd be mulch." She tried to smile. It didn't land; she saw it not land. "You're welcome."

He didn't say it. She watched him try to make himself say it and watch the words die somewhere in his chest, because saying thank-you meant the magic was good, and the magic being good meant the outlawry was wrong, and the outlawry being wrong meant the maps he'd drawn had sent wardens to the doors of people who could have — who had just — saved his life.

She knew that arithmetic too. She'd had twelve years to learn it.

"Your hands are shaking," he said finally.

"They do that."

"What did it cost? The working. What did it cost *you*?"

And there it was, the question she'd spent twelve years making sure no one could ask, because the honest answer was: I don't know yet. Maybe nothing. Maybe a debt that comes due later. Her mistress had always said the blood carried a price written into it, and Tamsin had run rather than wait to find out what the price was, and here she was in a dead king's country paying installments anyway.

"Cold," she said. "It costs cold. Go to sleep, Edric."

He went to the loft, eventually. She stayed by the fire. And in the hour before dawn, watching the light change, she caught him watching her from the top of the ladder — not with fear anymore, exactly. With something more complicated. Pity, maybe. Or recognition. The look of a man seeing a debt he'd forgotten he owed.

Neither of them spoke.

But when they packed the sledges of their lives back onto their shoulders and stepped out into the scoured, silent, blight-cleaned morning, Edric paused at the door and said, without turning around:

"The records. In the chapel at Millbrook, two days on — the old parish records. If your blood matters to the prophecy the way I think it does, there may be answers there. About what you are."

"What I *am*," she repeated.

"What your family was." He started walking. "It might matter. For what's coming."

She followed him into the snow, and told herself the cold in her fingers was only the storm's price, and did not believe it.
