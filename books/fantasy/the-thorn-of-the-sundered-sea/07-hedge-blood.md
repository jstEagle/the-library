## 7. Hedge-Blood

The chapel had been a chapel once. Now it was a cave of ice and pigeon bones with God's face worn off the windows.

Edric found it by accident, which was how he found most things worth finding. They had walked out of the blight-storm's reach at dawn, following the frozen river road north toward what his maps insisted was the town of Ashfall and his eyes insisted was nothing but snow-drifted roofs and a bell tower leaning like a drunk against its own weight. The town was empty in the way places were empty now — not ruined, just *left*, as if everyone had stepped outside one morning and forgotten to come back.

Tamsin went ahead of him through the broken door, because she always did. Twelve days of travel and she still walked like someone who expected the world to apologize for inconveniencing her. He followed, and told himself the tightness under his ribs was cold.

"Chapel," she said, stating it flatly, the way she stated most things. "You dragged us two miles off the road for a chapel."

"I dragged us off the road for shelter before dark. The chapel came with the shelter." He stamped snow from his boots inside the doorway. "And for the parish records. If there are any."

"Records." She turned, and her breath made a small ghost between them. "You're a strange man, Edric Vale."

He was learning not to answer that.

* * *

The chapel's interior had kept better than its exterior. Ice had sealed the windows rather than breaking them, so the light that came through was blue and thick, underwater light, and it fell across pews furred with frost and an altar stripped bare except for dust and a single candle stub that Tamsin, after a moment's hesitation, lit with a match instead of anything else.

He noticed that. He noticed everything about how she handled magic — the way she rationed it like a starving woman rations bread, the way her hands checked themselves mid-gesture. She thought he didn't see. He saw.

"The sacristy," he said. "Parish records would be kept there. Registers, tithes, letters to the diocese. If this town had a hedge-coven attached to it—" he caught himself too late, and watched her shoulders go rigid, "—which is not an accusation, Reyes, it's a guess based on geography. Hedge-witches clustered where the old roads met water. This river used to run all the way down to the Sundered Sea."

"Used to," she said. Everything about the Sundered Sea sat wrong in her mouth, like a stone she hadn't decided whether to spit or swallow.

The sacristy door was swollen shut with ice. He put his shoulder to it twice before it gave, and the smell that came out was paper and mouse and cold iron — the smell of every archive he'd ever loved. For a moment he was twenty-three again, in the map room under the palace, unrolling vellum while the king himself leaned over his shoulder and asked questions no one else bothered to ask.

Then he was thirty-four, and the map room was ash, and the king was dead on the throne room floor with something growing out of his crown.

Tamsin was already pulling boxes off the shelves, efficient and unsentimental, setting aside anything with gilt on it. "If you're looking for treasure—"

"I'm looking for names."

"Whose?"

"Anyone's." He took a box from her, heavier than it looked, and carried it to the altar where the light was best. "A town this size, a hundred years ago, would have kept everything. Births, deaths, who paid their tithes in fish and who paid in favors. And if there was a coven here, the priest either hated them or married them, and either way he wrote about it."

She gave him a look — the look she'd been giving him since the waystation, since the storm, since the moment he'd flinched away from the green light of her working. Fear wearing politeness as its coat. But she kept sorting.

He opened the first box and began to read.

* * *

It took him four hours. He knew because he marked the time by the candle, and by the way Tamsin moved around the chapel — first searching it, then cleaning a corner of it with the methodical aggression of someone who could not stand disorder, then sitting in a pew near the door with her knife out, whittling a piece of driftwood she must have carried from the coast. She never sat with her back to the entrance. He'd stopped commenting on that around day six.

The early registers were ordinary. Baptisms, marriages, a suspicious number of drownings in the years before the Sundering, when the river still reached saltwater. Then, in the third box, under a bundle of tithe receipts, he found the letters.

They were written in a small, cramped hand, ink gone brown, and they were addressed to a name Edric knew from the oldest maps in the royal collection: *Mother Ivell of the Fen Road*. The priest — Father Anselm, according to the flyleaf of the register — had written to her forty-one times over nineteen years, and had kept the drafts, or perhaps the copies, folded into the back of his own ledger like sins.

Edric read the first three standing up. By the fourth he was sitting. By the ninth he had forgotten the cold entirely.

*Ivell writes that the crown was never meant to hold a dead man. I tell her the king is not dead, whatever her girls say. She laughs at me, or I think she does; it is hard to tell in a letter.*

*The hedge-wives came at midnight again. They do not sneak, exactly. They walk as if the night were their kitchen and we are all guests in it. Ivell herself asked after the coronation regalia — asked me, a priest, as if I keep such things under my bed. I said the crown is with the crown. She said that was the trouble with this kingdom. Everything is with the crown and nothing is with the people.*

*She showed me the work today. I will write it plainly because no one reads these drafts but me and God. The crown was made by their hands — the first hands, the fen-hands, seven generations back. Silver and rowan and a living thorn, cut from the boundary tree and bound with blood into the shape of obedience. A guard, Ivell calls it. A watchdog for the king's head. She says it has been dying slowly ever since, the way a transplanted thing dies, and a dying bound thing does not stay obedient. It stays hungry.*

*I have asked her to unmake it. She says it cannot be unmade. It can only be unbound, and unbinding requires the blood that bound it — not any descendant, she says, though any might serve as kindling. It requires the line itself. The maker's line, freely offered, naming itself. She says the crown drinks names the way I drink wine, and if the wrong person speaks theirs over it, they will simply be added to the hunger rather than ending it.*

*I asked her which line. She looked at me for a long time and said, Father, you know which line. You christened half of them.*

Edric set the letter down.

His hands were steady. He noted that the way he noted everything, cartographer's habit, fixing positions: hands steady, breath fogging, blue light, dust, the small sound of Tamsin's knife. His hands were steady and his mind was moving very fast along a route he did not want to be reading correctly.

Because he knew the fen-road families. Of course he knew them. He had spent three years mapping the old kingdom's byways for the Office of Roads, and the fen-road settlements had appeared on his charts as a scatter of names — Halloway, Maren, Ossel, Reyes.

Reyes.

There had been Reyeses on the fen road in his survey year. A ferry family. A woman named Reyes who sold charms at the market cross and was fined twice for it, once by the magistrate and once, informally, by the weather.

And Tamsin Reyes had been trained. Trained properly, secretly, somewhere with a mistress and rules and probably a boundary tree. Somewhere on the fen road, or somewhere like it.

He picked up the next letter. His hands were still steady. It felt like watching someone else's hands.

*The coven keeps its own book of lines, going back past the parish book, past the conquest, past sense. Ivell let me see one page. Seven names written in a column, each crossed through except the last, and beside them a drawing of the crown that made my eyes water to look at. I asked why the last name was not crossed through. She said, because she is not born yet, and when she is, we will have to decide what to do about her.*

*I have prayed on this. I am an old man and my prayers have gotten shorter and more honest. Here is what I understand: the hedge-witches made the crown to guard the king, and the crown has become the thing the king needs guarding from, and the only blood that can loose it is the blood that tied it. And here is what I fear: that when the girl is finally born, the coven will not offer her freely. They will spend her. Freely given and spent are different words, Father Anselm, and I am too old to pretend otherwise.*

That was the last letter in the bundle. After it, the drafts turned to sermons, and then to nothing. The Sundering had come four years later, and whatever happened to Mother Ivell and her girls and her uncrossed name had happened without a witness.

Edric sat in the blue light and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the waystation.

Twelve years. The winter had lasted twelve years. Tamsin Reyes had been hiding her craft in Saltmere for twelve years. She had fled her training twelve years ago — she'd said as much, in the dark of the blight-storm, in the few words she allowed herself about her past: *I left. My mistress didn't approve.* He had taken it for a quarrel, a girl running from a strict teacher. Everyone ran from something. He had run from a burning map room.

But if the dates aligned —

If the uncrossed name had been born roughly twelve years before the winter began, if the coven had spent those years preparing her, if she had run rather than be spent —

Then the prophecy had not chosen her. The prophecy had merely noticed what the coven already knew. The page pulled the key to the lock. That was how prophecies worked; he had studied enough of them in the archive to know they were less like fate and more like cartography — they drew the road, and things rolled downhill along it.

And the price. The court of salt and ice would have the final page; he was nearly sure of it now, sure the way you were sure of a coastline before you'd surveyed it. *A life freely given.* He had read that phrase in fragments already, in the burned marginalia of the palace library, in a smuggler's ballad, in the mad scratching of the last royal alchemist. Everyone remembered the phrase. No one remembered the footnote.

Ivell's footnote. *Freely given and spent are different words.* The blood of the maker's line, naming itself, offering itself — not killed on the steps, not sacrificed screaming, because the crown drank coercion the same way it drank names and grew fat on both. It had to be *given*. Which meant the coven couldn't force her. Which meant, all these years, the whole machinery of preparation and pressure and dread had been aimed at making a girl *choose* it.

Or — and here his thoughts went somewhere colder — aimed at making her, and then waiting for the winter to corner her until choosing was the only door left open.

He looked up. Tamsin was still in the pew, whittling. The candle had burned down to almost nothing. She looked tired and young and stubborn, and she was watching the door the way she always watched the door, and he understood with terrible clarity that he was looking at a person who had already run once from being spent, and would run again, and should run again, because anyone with the right to lay that burden on her had died a century ago and had no business reaching forward through time to collect.

And the winter would kill ten thousand more people before spring came, if spring ever came. Saltmere's harbor was a graveyard of ships locked in ice. The villages they'd passed had shrines stacked with offerings to a saint who never answered. Children born into the long winter who had never seen rain.

Both things were true. Both things were true at once, and there was no map that resolved them, and he was the one holding the information, and she trusted him — not much, not yet, but some, a thin thread of it spun out of shared storms and shared bread — and the thread would not bear the weight of this.

Not tonight. Not dropped on her in a freezing chapel with hours of walking still ahead. Perhaps not dropped at all, but *led* — brought along the road gently, mile by mile, until she saw the shape of it herself and chose with open eyes. Wasn't that different? Wasn't that kinder?

He heard the sentence in his own head and recognized its accent. It was the accent of the Office of Wards, the committee room where he had sat with the magistrates and helped draw the lines that outlawed her craft across half the kingdom, telling himself that containment was protection, that secrecy was safety, that people like her were safer not knowing what they were. He had built a career out of deciding what other people could bear to know.

He put the letters back in the box. All but one. That one he folded small and slid inside his coat, against his chest, where the paper seemed to burn even through wool.

"Anything?" Tamsin called softly, from her pew. "Or is it all baptisms and fish tithes?"

"Baptisms and fish tithes." His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "The usual history of ordinary people."

She snorted and went back to her whittling, and he sat alone at the altar with the closed box and the stolen letter over his heart, and outside the ice-sealed windows the light was beginning to fail, and somewhere north, under all that white, a crowned thing was walking the walls of a dead city, and it had already smelled her blood once.

Tomorrow they would reach the palace outskirts. He had maybe a day to decide what kind of man was going to arrive there with her.

He blew out the candle.

* * *

They slept in the chapel that night, in the pew nearest the stove-less fireplace, wrapped in every blanket they owned. Tamsin fell asleep first, fast and shallow, knife under her hand. Edric lay awake watching the blue windows go black, turning the arithmetic over and over, looking for a different answer.

There wasn't one. There was only the question of who told her, and when, and how much it would cost, and whether the cost was his to pay or hers, and whether a man who withheld the truth to protect someone was a guardian or just a gentler kind of thief.

Near midnight, half-dreaming, Tamsin spoke.

"They made it, you know."

He went very still. "Made what?"

"The crown." Her eyes were still closed. Her voice was the loose, unguarded voice of sleep, and it frightened him more than shouting would have. "My grandmother's grandmother's people. Rowan and silver and a thorn off the boundary tree. We're not supposed to talk about it. It's the first rule." A pause. The wind moved somewhere in the tower. "I never even learned why it was the first rule."

"No?" he managed.

"Mm." She burrowed deeper into her blanket. "Mistress said the ones who made it don't get to say what it's for. Only what it costs."

Then she was breathing slow and deep, actually asleep, and Edric Vale lay in the dark with his hand pressed flat over the letter in his coat, and understood that she had just handed him the last piece without knowing it — and that tomorrow, on the road to the Hollow King's walls, he would have to decide whether to give it back.
