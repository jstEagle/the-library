## 8. The Village That Waited

The village appeared out of the white like something remembered rather than seen.

One moment there was only the plain — a mile of crusted snow with the wind skimming off it in long gray ribbons — and then the road bent between two leaning boundary stones, and there it was: a scatter of rooftops under drifts, chimneys smoking thin and stubborn, a chapel with a bell tower that had been rebuilt at least twice from the look of the timber. Smoke was the thing that undid me. I hadn't realized how much I'd missed it. Woodsmoke meant people, and people meant bread, and bread meant we might survive another night without eating boot leather.

"We should go around," Edric said.

He'd been saying some version of that for three days. Go around the waystation. Go around the frozen river crossing. Go around anything that had walls and warmth and witnesses, because witnesses asked questions, and questions were how you ended up in front of someone with a ledger and a rope. He wasn't wrong. He was just always saying it while his eyes went to my hands, my chilblained fingers, the way I swayed on my feet, and so what he said and what he meant were different things entirely.

"There's smoke," I said.

"I can see the smoke."

"Then you can see that I'm done sleeping in ditches." I kept walking. My boots had gone past wet into some other state, a kind of permanent cold that lived in the bones of my feet now and would probably live there forever. "We're not going to reach the palace by walking ourselves dead. And if this village has fed anyone through twelve winters, they know things about surviving out here that we don't."

"They also know things about the king," Edric said quietly.

That stopped me for half a step. Then I kept going, because he was right about that too, and being right twice before breakfast is an unbearable quality in a man.

* * *

They let us in the way people do when they've stopped expecting strangers: without surprise, almost without curiosity. A woman with a face like a walnut shell opened the door of the first house — the largest, I noticed, though it was no larger than a fishing cottage back home — looked at us both for a long moment, and said, "Travelers. You'll want the common room. Supper's at dark, same as always." As if dark came at the same hour every day, as if supper were a fixed star. Maybe here it was. When your winter never ends, I suppose the clock becomes whatever rituals you can hold onto.

The common room held maybe thirty souls and smelled of tallow, boiled barley, and wet wool. Nobody stared, exactly. They did worse. They *looked*, the way you look at weather coming in — assessing, resigned, already adjusting. An old man moved down a bench to make room without being asked. A girl of perhaps ten brought us bowls of barley soup with a piece of salt fish laid across the top like a blessing, and when I tried to thank her she looked frightened and backed away, and her mother pulled her close and murmured something I caught only the edge of.

"—not to speak to them until they've eaten—"

Until they've eaten. Not don't talk to strangers. Until they've eaten. There was a rule underneath the rule, and rules like that are never made for nothing.

Edric ate slowly, cataloguing. That was his whole self in miniature, I was learning — a man who took the measure of everything because once measuring things had been his job and his pride, and even disgraced, even half-starved, the habit wouldn't die. He counted the heads in the room. He noted the extra places set at the far table, covered with cloths, kept warm near the fire. He watched the door every time it opened.

I watched the walls.

There were carvings in the great beam over the hearth. Old ones, worn nearly smooth, then re-cut more recently by a steadier hand: a crown of thorns, but not the cruel kind from scripture prints — this crown was woven, deliberate, each barb placed like a stitch. Beneath it, a figure robed and tall, hands open, palms up. Receiving. Around the figure, smaller figures knelt in a ring, their faces rubbed away by centuries of thumbs.

"He's everywhere here," I said, low. "Not feared. Honored."

"The Hollow King," Edric said. "Yes."

"You knew."

"I suspected. The villages this far north were always his strongest holdings." He turned his bowl a quarter-turn on the table, precise as everything else he did. "Tamsin. Whatever they offer us tonight, whatever they tell us — listen. Listen hard. But don't promise them anything, and don't tell them what you are."

"What I am," I said, "is a trinket-seller with bad boots."

"Tamsin."

"I heard you." I hadn't promised to obey. There's a difference, and I've spent my whole life living in the space between hearing and promising.

* * *

Supper ended and the ritual began, and I understood then why they'd wanted us fed first.

The bell in the chapel tower rang nine times — nine, not twelve, not any hour that exists — and everyone rose. Not hurriedly. The way you rise for a thing you have risen for ten thousand times, your body knowing the shape of it better than your mind. They filed out into the blue-black cold, carrying lanterns, and Edric and I looked at each other across the table and followed, because the alternative was staying behind in a village where we didn't know the rules, and a stranger who skips the ritual is marked faster than a stranger who breaks a taboo.

They gathered in the square before the chapel, all of them, old men leaning on their sons, mothers holding children wrapped until only faces showed. In the center stood a post, and on the post hung a life-size effigy of the king — carved wood, painted, dressed in real cloth gone gray with years of re-dyeing. The thorn-crown on its head was real. Living, I mean. Someone tended it. Pale shoots had grown down over the carved brow and been carefully, lovingly trimmed back each time, and the cut ends had scarred over into knots like old wounds.

An old woman stepped forward — the walnut-faced one from the door — and the crowd went silent in a way that pressed on the ears.

"Holy King," she said, "who gave his body to the cold so that ours might be spared the fire. Who wears the crown we wove, that we could not wear. Who waits for us at the turning of the year that does not turn." She lifted a small basket of what looked like wheat — actual wheat, golden, impossibly alive in that place — and scattered a handful of grains at the post's base. The grains lay on the snow like drops of blood reversed. "Keep us another season. Keep us until the Sundered comes home."

"Until the Sundered comes home," the village answered, thirty voices in ragged unison, and the little girl beside me — the soup-girl — said it loudest of all, with her whole chest, with her eyes shut, and I felt something crack in my own chest in answer.

Because I knew what that meant. Everyone in Saltmere knew the words; children skipped rope to them. *When the Sundered comes home, the winter goes with it.* It was a nursery rhyme, a nonsense charm, a thing grandmothers said about weather the way they said *rain before seven*. I had never once thought about what it would mean to say those words in a place where the winter actually never left. Where the rhyme wasn't a rhyme. Where it was a prayer you offered at a post with living thorns, in exchange for one more season of barely enough.

They weren't worshipping a monster. That was the thing I couldn't make fit, standing there with my numb feet going number. Everything Edric had told me — the dead king, the thing wearing him, the winter bleeding out of his wounds — painted a horror story. But these people loved him. These people had built their entire lives around waiting for him, around feeding him seasons of devotion like grain scattered at a shrine, and the awful arithmetic of it was that the devotion worked. They were alive. Barely, narrowly, hand-to-mouth alive, through twelve impossible winters, because they believed, and the belief was answered.

Or something was answering. That distinction was going to matter, I could feel it, the way you feel a storm three days off.

Afterward, the walnut woman found us by the chapel wall. She looked at me a long time — longer than courtesy, shorter than threat — and said:

"You're south-folk. Saltmere, by your vowels."

"We're travelers," Edric said smoothly.

"Mm." She wasn't fooled and didn't pretend to be, which was somehow worse than either. "Saltmere forgot him. Twelve winters, and the city behind its walls forgot there was a country out here freezing for its sins." Her voice wasn't bitter. That was the strangest part. It was patient, the patience of stone. "But you'll have come north for him anyway. They always do, in the end. The ones the crown calls." Her eyes went, briefly and deliberately, to my hands. To my chilblained, ordinary, hidden hands. "You'll stay the night. There's a bed in the loft. In the morning, if you're wise, you'll keep walking and never say you passed through. And if you're not wise—" She smiled then, and it was terrible, because it was kind. "Then you'll do what you came to do, and the village will light lamps for you after. We light lamps for everyone the crown calls. It's the least we can do. It's the most we can do."

She walked away before I could find my voice.

"She knows," I said.

"She suspects. There's a difference, and—" 

"Don't quote my own trick back at me, Edric Vale. It doesn't suit you." I leaned against the chapel wall, and the stones were so cold they burned, and I was grateful for it because the burning kept me from thinking too clearly about what the old woman had said. *Lamps for everyone the crown calls.* Plural. Ongoing. A litany of the called and consumed, stretching back twelve winters or further. "How many, do you think? How many people has it called north?"

Edric didn't answer. Which was an answer.

"And my mistress—" The words came out before I could stop them, and I shut my mouth on the rest. But some part of me finished the sentence anyway, silently, in the dark: *my mistress sent girls north, or meant to, or trained them for something, and I ran before I learned which.* The hedge-coven had prayers too. The hedge-coven had grain scattered at the roots of things. I had told myself for twelve years that I'd fled a tyrant, and standing in this square full of gentle, starving believers, I couldn't remember anymore whether the tyranny and the faith had ever been separable.

* * *

The loft above the stable was warm from the animals below and smelled of hay and honest dung, which after three days of snow-ditches felt like luxury. Edric took the side near the ladder. I took the window, out of habit — always the exit, always the escape route, twelve years of sleeping with one eye on the way out.

I couldn't sleep. Through the frost-feathered glass I could see the chapel, and the lamps.

They were lighting them now, one by one, in the chapel windows. Lamps for the called. Somewhere inside that building there was probably a list — names, dates, villages, the long tally of everyone the crown had reached out its thorned fingers for — and every name had a lamp, and every lamp was still burning, because nobody had come back to blow them out. Nobody ever came back. The village waited anyway. That was the whole of their religion, I realized: not worship, exactly. Waiting. They had taken the worst thing that ever happened to their world and made a discipline of hoping at it, daily, in the cold, and the hope cost them everything they had, and they paid it, and paid it, and paid it.

And the winter never broke.

I lay in the hay and listened to the wind work at the roof and thought about the soup-girl saying *until the Sundered comes home* with her eyes shut tight, believing it the way children believe, with her whole body. She would grow up believing it. She would grow old believing it. She would teach it to her own children in a village that had burned through twelve winters of lamps, unless—

Unless.

That was the word I fell asleep on. Not *why*. Not *how*. Just *unless*, small and sharp as a splinter, working its way in.

I woke before dawn to Edric shaking my shoulder, and his face in the lamplight was wrong — pale, set, the guilt sharpened into something with edges again.

"Get your things," he said. "We need to be gone before the village wakes."

"Why? What happened?"

He hesitated. I've since learned that Edric Vale hesitating means he's deciding how much truth the moment will bear, and I've learned to hate it. "Nothing happened," he said. "We're closer than I thought. Two days, maybe three, if the weather holds. We shouldn't spend the time here."

Two days. The palace. The thing on the throne, or beneath it, or walking the walls — he'd never been clear, and I'd begun to suspect he wasn't unclear from ignorance.

I swung my legs off the loft and reached for my boots, and that's when I saw it.

On the windowsill, inside the glass, on the *inside* — a single sprig of pale thorn, fresh-cut, its wound-end still wet with sap that gleamed like ice. It hadn't been there when we slept. Neither of us had put it there.

It was laid across the sill in the shape of a circlet.

A crown, small as a ring, waiting for whoever woke first to understand that the waiting village was not the only one keeping watch — that something out there in the endless white had marked our passage, had reached ahead of us, and had left its calling card on the sill like a host setting out bread.

"Edric," I said. My voice came out very steady, which surprised me. "I don't think we're traveling to the palace anymore."

He looked at the thorn, and all the color left his face. "No," he said. "I think it's traveling to meet us."
