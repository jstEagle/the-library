## 9. The Thorn Wears the Crown

Edric had drawn the palace of Vhalen's Reach four times in his life.

The first time he was nineteen, a junior surveyor with cold fingers and a commission to correct the harbor approaches. The second and third were revisions ordered by men who had never seen the place but wanted their names in the cartouche. The fourth was the ward-map — the one that closed the city to magic, the one that had made his name and unmade everything else. He had known every tower, every buttress, every hidden stair from the under-harbor to the king's garden.

The thing walking the walls now matched none of it.

They had come out of the pine shadow an hour past dawn, following a road that was less a road than a memory of one, and the first sight of the walls should have been a comfort. Stone. Enduring stone. Something that predated the Sundering and would outlast whatever this winter was. Edric felt the relief of it like warmth on his face — and then he saw what moved along the battlements, and the relief died so quickly it left him dizzy.

A figure in a crown, pacing the wall-walk with the slow, even tread of a sentry who has kept watch for a very long time.

"That's him," Tamsin said. Her voice came out flat, which he had learned was what she did instead of screaming. "That's the Hollow King."

"Yes."

"He's *walking.*"

"He is." Edric raised the spyglass he'd carried since Saltmere — a surveyor's habit, the last tool of his old trade he hadn't sold or burned. Through the lens the figure sharpened, and his stomach turned over with the slow, sick patience of a man watching something he'd feared become undeniable.

The king wore robes of state gone grey as ash. He wore the crown of Vhalen's Reach, black iron worked into a circlet of thorns, and Edric had seen that crown once, at a distance, at a coronation anniversary he'd mapped the crowd for. It had been gold then. Whatever wore it now had changed the metal the way a vine changes a fence post — slowly, completely, without asking.

And the king walked wrong. That was the horror of it, the detail Edric knew would follow him into every sleep for the rest of his life: not a shambling dead man's gait, nothing so honest. He walked *well*. Smoothly. With the measured grace of a living monarch reviewing troops, except that no breath stirred the grey robes, and where his face should have been there was only a suggestion of a face, like a portrait left too long in rain — features still present, still arranged in dignity, but running slowly into each other. And through the collar of the robe, up the side of the neck, across the ruined cheek, thin dark lines traced like veins of ore in marble.

Thorn-roots. Under the skin. Everywhere under the skin.

"Is he suffering?" Tamsin asked quietly.

Edric lowered the spyglass. He considered lying, because she looked like someone standing at the edge of deep water, and because lying to her had become — he was ashamed to note how easily — almost habitual. But she'd see through it. She always did. That was the trouble with hedge-witches; they could smell falsehood the way other people smelled smoke.

"No," he said. "I don't think there's enough of him left to suffer. That's worse, isn't it."

She didn't answer. She stood with her hands buried in her armpits against the cold, watching the distant figure pace its endless circuit, and Edric watched her instead of the king because some instinct told him her face would tell him more.

It did. Not fear — or not only fear. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing the shape of a trap she'd already half-known she was in.

"We should keep moving," he said. "There's a shrine village ahead — Ashfall, on the old maps. If anyone survived the winter, they'll be there, and we need food we didn't carry in."

"And if nobody survived?"

"Then we'll know what we're walking toward."

* * *

They reached the outskirts by midafternoon, and Edric understood immediately why the maps had called this district the Garden Quarter, and why no map since the Sundering had troubled to update it.

Palace grounds, once. Terraced lawns and ornamental water and the famous rose walks that had supplied three generations of coronations. Now it was a forest of black thorn.

Not bramble — bramble he could have named, could have charted, could have advised a survey crew how to burn. This grew in architecture. Arches of it spanned the old carriage roads. Walls of it followed the terraces exactly, as if it had found the hedges and improved them. It climbed the outbuildings and burst through their roofs in fountains of dark wood, and everywhere, threading through it like lace through cloth, ran ice — not snow, not frost, but ice that had grown *with* the thorn, frozen mid-bloom around every branch, so the whole quarter glittered like a cathedral built by something that loved beauty and hated everything alive.

"It's quiet," Tamsin whispered.

"I noticed."

"Nothing's quiet. Not even in deep winter. There are birds, or wind, or your own blood in your ears being loud because you're scared." She swallowed. "This quiet is being made. I can feel it pressing. Like the air before lightning, except it never comes."

Edric had spent twelve years telling himself the stories were exaggerated. Dead king pacing the walls, thorn-crown, endless winter leaking from a corpse — peasant talk, grief dressed up as legend. Standing in the Garden Quarter with silence pressing on his eardrums like deep water, he finished, finally and thoroughly, believing.

"There," he said, and pointed.

A gatehouse stood at the head of the road to the outer wall — or had stood. The thorn had taken it the way tide takes a sandcastle, patiently and completely, and now only the arch remained, and in the arch, hanging like a lantern, was a shape Edric took a full ten seconds to identify because his mind refused it.

A man. Frozen upright. Thorn grown through him in a lattice, holding him the way amber holds a fly, and the thorn had bloomed *out of him* — white flowers, hundreds of them, opening from his chest and shoulders and open mouth. His eyes behind the flowers were open too, and clear, and looking at the road, at them, with an expression Edric recognized from dispatches he'd read during the fall: the expression of a man who has just realized the order he was given will kill everyone who follows it.

"A guardsman," Tamsin said. "From the Sundering day. He's been here twelve years."

"Longer than that." Edric's voice sounded far away to himself. "The winter started the night the king died. Everyone says the Sundering and the death were the same night. This man's been standing here since the beginning." He made himself look at the flowered mouth. "The thorn doesn't just kill you. It keeps you. It *uses* you. Roots need somewhere to live."

Tamsin had gone very pale, but she stepped closer to the frozen guard, close enough to touch, and studied him with the same narrowed attention she gave to locks and knots and anything else that might be picked open.

"The flowers," she said. "Hawthorn?"

"What?"

"They look like hawthorn blossom. May-flower. My mistress grew it along the back fence and swore you never, ever brought it indoors." She pulled her hand back from the petals without quite touching them. "Hedge-lore. Hawthorn is a threshold plant. It belongs to doors and boundaries and things that stand between. If someone bound a spirit into a crown, they'd want—" She stopped.

"They'd want what?"

"Nothing. Old wives' rules." But she said it too fast, the way people said *nothing* when it meant *something I'm not ready to tell you*, and Edric filed it away with the other small locked drawers of Tamsin Reyes, of which there were many, and of which he was beginning to understand he would eventually have to open every single one.

He hated how little that thought troubled him. A year ago it would have been strategy. Now it felt like trespass.

* * *

They made camp in the shell of a gardener's cottage whose roof the thorn had used as a trellis, leaving a canopy of branches and ice thick enough to block the sky. Edric got a fire going in the old hearth with wood he'd carried for two days rather than cut anything here — cutting felt like touching, and touching felt like invitation. Tamsin sat with her knees drawn up, turning something over in her fingers.

He saw what it was when she lifted it to the firelight: a sprig of the white blossom, snapped from the guardsman's chest when she passed. She hadn't seemed to decide to take it. Her hand had simply done it.

"You shouldn't have that," Edric said.

"I know."

"Then put it down."

"In a minute." She turned it. The five-petaled flowers caught the light like chips of bone. "It's warm," she said. "Did you notice? Everything in this place is frozen solid and this is warm. Like it just came off a hearthstone." She frowned. "Like it likes me."

"Tamsin—"

"It's all right. I'm putting it down." She set it on the hearthstone at the fire's edge, deliberately, and pushed it a hand-span away, and then sat looking at it as if it might get up and follow her.

Edric wanted to say something steadying and had nothing. So instead he said the true thing, which was a habit he was trying to build and failing at most days: "I've been thinking about the prophecy book. The page that pulled us. It described two figures. One born of the hedge-blood. One who drew the lines." He rubbed his hands together over the flames. "I've been assuming the second figure was me because I drew the ward-lines. Because I'm the one carrying guilt around like a compass needle. But guilt isn't destiny. Maybe I just *want* it to be me. Maybe it's cleaner if the man who broke the world gets to help fix it."

"And if it's not you?"

"Then I'm a disgraced mapmaker who followed a witch into a dead kingdom for nothing, and honestly, Tamsin, that's the better ending. Take it."

She almost smiled. In the firelight, with the thorn-silence pressing outside the broken roof, it was the most human thing he'd seen in days, and he held onto it.

That was when the sprig of hawthorn began to glow.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. It warmed from within like a coal breathed on, white light seeping between the petals, and Tamsin went rigid, and Edric came up off his pack with his knife already in his hand, which was ridiculous, which was a knife against a flower, but his body had stopped consulting him.

"Tamsin," he said. "Move away from it."

"I can't feel my hand." She stared at her right hand, splayed on the hearthstone. "No — I can. That's the problem. I can feel it *too much*. Every finger. Every — oh." Her voice dropped to a whisper of pure dread. "Oh, it knows me."

"What knows you?"

"The thorn. All of it. The whole quarter, the whole — Edric, it's not the sprig. The sprig is just the part I can see. It's reaching. From the walls. From the palace. It's been reaching since we crossed the tree line and I thought it was the cold, I thought the pressure was the cold—" She snatched her hand back and pressed it against her chest, and the light in the sprig flared once, twice, like a heartbeat finding a rhythm it liked.

Then it moved.

Not fast. That was the obscenity of it. The sprig extended a single filament of green, delicate as a hair, and crossed the hand-span of hearthstone she had pushed between them, and touched the hem of her sleeve, and rooted.

"Tamsin!" Edric lunged, knife out, meaning to cut it —

"Don't!" She caught his wrist with her left hand, grip shockingly strong. "Don't cut it, don't touch it, you don't know what it does when it's hurt — my mistress said — you don't cut a binding, you *unmake* it or it takes the wound as a door—" Her words tumbled over each other, hedge-lore pouring out of her in panic, and beneath the panic Edric heard something that chilled him more than any of it: expertise. She knew this thing. Not from books.

The filament sank into her sleeve. Tamsin made a sound low in her throat, not a scream, a held-back scream, the sound of a woman who had learned young that making noise cost extra.

And then it happened to her arm.

He watched it travel. Under the skin, visible as a shadow under ice, a fine dark line rose from her wrist toward her elbow, unhurried, exploratory, and where it passed her skin marked — a thin tracery like ink drawn by a needle-fine pen, a pattern of interlocking curves that Edric's professional eye parsed before his mind consented to understand.

It wasn't random. It wasn't damage.

It was a *cartouche*. A border. The kind of mark a mapmaker drew around territory to say: *this is claimed.*

"No," Tamsin said. She said it several times, quieter each time, a word losing a war. "No, no, no —"

The line reached the crook of her elbow and stopped. The tracery settled into her skin like water into paper, faint now, a bruise-colored filigree from wrist to elbow, and the sprig on the hearthstone crumbled all at once into ordinary ash.

The silence changed. Edric couldn't have said how — the pressure didn't lift, it *turned*, the way a head turns. All across the Garden Quarter, in the arches and the walls and the flowering corpse in the gatehouse, something shifted its attention. He felt it pass over him like a searchlight and move on, uninterested.

Interested elsewhere.

"It marked me," Tamsin whispered. She was looking at her arm. Her face had gone the color of the snow outside, and she was shaking, but her voice, when she mastered it, was terribly steady, the voice of a woman reading her own sentence. "It knows what I am. It's known since the tree line. It's not hunting us, Edric. It let us walk here. We're not travelers who got lost." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "We're *invited.*"

Outside, far off across the black gardens, a bell began to ring.

One bell. Slow. Deliberate. From the direction of the palace — from the walls, from the wall-walk where a crowned dead man paced his twelve-year circuit — a sound with no rope and no ringer and no right to exist, tolling once, and waiting, as if to ask:

*Are you coming, or shall I fetch you?*

Edric took Tamsin's marked hand. She didn't pull away. Together, in the gardener's cottage under the thorn, they listened to the Hollow King call them home.
