## 10. Twelve Years of Running

The cellar smelled of turnips and old stone and, underneath both, the green rot-smell of thorn.

We'd been running since moonrise. The mark on my wrist — a thin black line like a vein that had learned to write — had pulled us through half the ruined capital before Edric found this door: an iron-banded hatch behind a collapsed tannery, leading down into someone's forgotten larder. He'd wrenched it open with his crowbar and all but thrown me down the stairs, and now he sat against the wall beneath the hatch, listening to the wind above while I listened to my own pulse.

The mark didn't hurt. That was the worst part. It was warm, like a hand resting there, patient as a creditor.

"Is it still there?" Edric asked without turning.

"You'd know if it weren't. You keep checking like you expect it to crawl off my arm and bite you."

"It might." He said it flatly, not joking, and went back to listening.

I flexed my fingers. My charm-bag was still slung across my body where I never took it off — twelve years of habit — and inside it were maybe forty knots and braids that suddenly felt about as useful as buttons. Above us the blight-wind moaned through the broken streets, carrying that dry whispering sound, the sound of something growing where nothing should grow.

Hours passed. Or what felt like hours. There was no light down here except the stub of candle Edric allowed us, hoarded in a tin cup, and the dark made time go soft at the edges.

It was Edric who finally broke the silence, which surprised me. In the days since the page had taken us, he'd perfected the art of saying exactly nothing. A man built out of maps and silences.

"You knew," he said. "Back in Saltmere. Before the book. You already knew magic was dangerous."

I looked at him. His face was half shadow, half candle, and very tired. "Everyone knows it's dangerous. It's outlawed."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Outlawed is a law. What you've got is fear. I've watched you handle charms for six days, Reyes, and you don't handle them like a criminal. You handle them like a survivor. Criminals get careless when they think no one's looking. Survivors never do." He turned to face me fully. "Who taught you?"

And there it was — the question I'd been dodging since the frozen chapel, since the records, since he'd come back from those dusty shelves with a look on his face he thought I hadn't seen. *Hedge-blood*, he'd written in his notes. I'd caught only a glimpse. But I'd caught enough to know he was circling something.

"You first," I said. "You tell me why a royal cartographer knows so much about a crown that shouldn't exist, and I'll tell you why I ran."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he'd refuse. Then, instead, he laughed — one short, bitter note with no humor in it anywhere.

"That's a steep price."

"I'm told I drive a hard bargain."

"My father drew maps for the crown," he said. "Harbor charts, mostly. I followed him into the service when I was nineteen. Best years of my life, if you want the truth of it. Surveying the Sundered coast, mapping what the winter took. And then—" He stopped. Started again. "Then the Warden's Office found out I could see wards."

"See them?"

"Lay lines. The old geometry under Saltmere — the wards they put up after the Sundering to strangle hedge-craft out of the city. Most people can't perceive them at all. I could. Like ink showing through paper." His hands moved as he talked, tracing shapes on his knee, and I realized he did that constantly — always drawing, always charting, even in the dark. "They trained me for it. Fourteen years, Tamsin. I drew the new ward-lines myself. Every one of them designed to make your kind's work curdle. To make a luck-knot rot in its maker's hands. I sat at a desk with good light and good pay and I designed the thing that made hiding necessary."

I kept my face still. Inside, something old and tired turned over.

"So yes," he said. "I know about fear. I helped build the machine that manufactures it." He looked down at his hands. "That's my confession. Your turn."

* * *

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. I wrapped my arms around my knees and tried to decide where to begin, and discovered that the beginning wasn't twelve years ago. It was further back than that.

"The coven found me when I was nine," I said. "Mistress Corvane. She ran a chandler's shop near the tanners' row — candles, soap, ordinary things. She took apprentices. Girls whose families couldn't feed them." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when I handled sharp things. "She taught me knot-work. Charm-craft. How to braid intention into wool and thread. How to read a sickroom. All of it useful, all of it small. I loved her for it. For four years she was the closest thing I had to a mother."

Edric didn't move. He'd gone very still, the way people do around wounded animals — or around confessions.

"When I was thirteen, she started teaching me the other lessons. The ones she saved for last." I swallowed. "About the crown. About the Hollow King — though we didn't call him that, we called him the Sleeping Lord, like he was something gentle. She taught me the covenant. The bargain the first hedge-witches struck, back before the Sundering, when they crowned the king to bind the thorn instead of burning it."

"I've read fragments," Edric said quietly. "In the chapel records. They're incomplete."

"They would be. The coven kept the rest in memory, not ink. Ink can be seized." I picked at a loose thread on my sleeve. "Here's the part they don't write down. The binding needs feeding. Not blood — blood's too cheap. It needs a name. A whole name, given freely, by someone who carries the old blood. The witches who made the crown gave pieces of themselves over generations, small surrenders, enough to keep the thorn drowsing. But every few generations the debt comes due in full. One witch. One complete surrender. Name, gift, everything — poured into the crown to keep the king asleep and the winter short."

The candle flame bent in some small movement of air. Neither of us spoke.

"Mistress Corvane chose me when I was thirteen," I said. "I didn't understand what the choosing meant at first. She dressed it up — called it *consecration*, called it an honor, said I'd been marked by the old blood from birth, that my line went back to the first crown-makers. Maybe it did. Maybe that's even true. But I understood eventually. Everyone in the coven understood. There was a girl before me, named Sef. She'd been chosen fifteen years earlier, and when her time came—" My voice caught, and I hated it, and I pushed through anyway. "When her time came, they took her to the standing stone outside the city walls and she walked into the thorn's keeping, and the winter that year ended early. Good harvest. The coven celebrated."

"And you were next in line."

"Next in line." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Twelve years ago, on the eve of my consecration, I packed a bag and climbed over the chandler's wall and walked to Saltmere, because Saltmere was the one place in the world where magic was illegal — do you understand? I didn't run to somewhere safe. I ran to somewhere *outlawed*. Because as long as the craft itself was a crime, no hedge-witch could practice openly, no coven could gather, and no one could drag me to a standing stone and call it holy. The law was my cage, and I locked it myself and slept soundly behind it."

Edric exhaled slowly. "The wards. My wards. You used them as a shield."

"Every day for twelve years." I met his eyes. "So when you say you built the machine that manufactured fear — I want you to know that machine also built the walls of the only home I've ever felt safe in. Isn't that a filthy little joke? The man who strangled hedge-craft in Saltmere is the reason a hedge-witch survived."

He looked at me for a long moment, and whatever moved across his face, it wasn't pity. It was something more complicated and more honest than that — recognition, maybe. Two people discovering they'd been holding opposite ends of the same rope.

"Why tell me now?" he asked.

Because of the mark on my wrist, I didn't say. Because the black line had crawled another inch toward my elbow while we talked. Because the thing wearing a dead king's crown had touched me once, briefly, in the street above, and I had felt it *recognize* me — felt the old blood answer it like a bell struck by a familiar hand — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Mistress Corvane hadn't been lying about one single thing.

But what I said was simpler.

"Because if that thing up there takes me," I said, nodding at my wrist, at the ceiling, at the whole frozen ruin of a kingdom, "then someone should know the truth of how I got here. Not the version where I'm a brave outlaw heroine. The real one. Where I was a child sold to a covenant, and I ran, and running worked right up until tonight." I held up my marked wrist between us, the black line vivid in the candlelight. "And because you gave me your confession first. Fair trade. That's the only kind of trust worth anything."

* * *

After that, something shifted between us. Not warmth, exactly — we were too cold, too scared, too recently strangers for warmth — but a settling, like two planks that had been rubbing finally fitted flush together.

Edric dug hardtack out of his pack and split it without being asked. We ate in silence, and it was companionable silence, which was new.

"The coven's still looking for you," he said eventually. It wasn't quite a question.

"If they're alive. If any of them are alive after twelve years of this winter." I turned the hardtack over in my fingers. "That's what I can't stop thinking about, Vale. The covenant feeds the crown to keep the winter *short*. Someone gives their name, and spring comes, and everyone breathes for another generation. But the crown-maker is dead — you said it yourself, the thorn wears him now, walks him around the walls like a puppet. So nobody's been fed to it. Nobody's paid. Twelve years, and the debt just sits there growing interest." I looked at my wrist. "And now the thorn has found the old blood on its own. It doesn't need a coven anymore. It doesn't need consent. It just needs to catch me."

"Then we don't let it catch you."

"We?"

His jaw tightened. He looked, for a moment, genuinely embarrassed — a man caught volunteering for something. "I spent fourteen years building cages," he said. "I'd like to spend whatever's left building doors. And I have a skill set that happens to include finding ways into places that are locked." He hesitated, and then added, quieter, "Also, I've read the prophecy. Both figures. It doesn't end with one of us."

"It doesn't end with either of us," I corrected. "There's a whole page missing. Water damage, remember? The book I bought for scrap paper." I almost smiled. "We're walking blind into a dead king's palace on the strength of half a poem."

"That's cartography," Edric said. "You start with half the map and you draw the rest yourself."

Above us, the wind changed. I felt it before I heard it — a pressure shift in my chest, the way the air goes strange before a storm. The mark on my wrist warmed, gently, like a cat leaning into a hand.

Edric saw my face change and reached for the crowbar. "What is it?"

"It knows where I am." I pressed my palm flat against the cool stone floor. Through it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, I felt something vast and slow shifting its weight somewhere far above — roots moving under the frozen streets, patient as winter itself. "It's not hunting anymore, Vale. It's waiting. It thinks I'll come to it."

"Do you know what it wants you to find when you get there?"

I shook my head. But deep under the fear, under twelve years of running, something older than both stirred — the inheritance I'd never wanted, waking up in my blood like a second pulse — and it whispered one word, in a voice that sounded terribly like Mistress Corvane's:

*Crown.*
