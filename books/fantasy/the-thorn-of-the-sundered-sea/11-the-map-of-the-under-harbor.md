## 11. The Map of the Under-Harbor

Edric had drawn maps for a living and lies for a career, and the two skills were not as different as he'd once liked to believe. Both required you to decide what the reader needed to see. Both punished you eventually.

They made camp in the shell of a customs house three miles from the palace, on the ridge where the old capital's tax collectors had once counted the harbor's wealth. The building still had its iron counting desks, bolted down against theft, and Tamsin sat on one of them with her boots off, rubbing feeling back into her feet, while the blight-storm outside dragged its claws along the shutters.

She hadn't spoken to him properly since the chapel.

He deserved that. He'd known it since the moment he slid the ledger back under the altar and lied to her face — *nothing useful in there* — and she'd looked at him with those hedge-witch eyes that missed nothing about herbs or knots or people, and said only, *all right*, which was worse than an argument.

The storm would hold them here for a day at least. A day was enough time to do what he should have done in the ruins, or the waystation, or any of the hundred moments when cowardice had chosen his words for him.

"Tamsin."

"Don't." She didn't look up from her foot. "Whatever speech you've been chewing on since the chapel, I don't want it."

"It's not a speech about the chapel."

That got her attention. She lowered her other boot and studied him across the dark room, firelight catching the frost in her lashes. Outside, something vast moved through the storm with a sound like a millstone turning, and they both went still until it passed. The thorn's patrols. They'd been hearing them more often the closer they drew to the palace, and each time, Tamsin flinched at a spot below her collarbone where the thorn's mark sat like a brand.

"Then what," she said, "is it about?"

"The wards."

* * *

He took out his satchel — the good one, oilskin over canvas, the one thing he'd carried out of the capital twelve years ago besides his guilt — and laid it open between them on the counting desk. Ink pots frozen solid and thawed again so many times their glass was crazed. A folding rule. Six pens, two of them usable. And the maps.

"You know what I told you I was," he said. "A cartographer. Disgraced. That part's true. What I didn't tell you is what I was disgraced for."

Tamsin said nothing. She had a way of withholding that was itself an answer: go on, then. Hang yourself.

"Twelve years ago, before the Sundering, the Crown convened a commission. Nine men. Scholars, ward-masters, one priest, one soldier. Their task was to design a containment lattice — a set of wards to be laid across the whole city, binding every working of magic to Crown license. Magic wasn't outlawed yet, understand. This was meant to make outlawing possible. You can't forbid what you can't see. The wards were the seeing."

"And you drew them."

"I drew the survey." His voice came out flat, which was how he knew how much effort it cost. "Every ward needs anchors — points in the world where the lattice fixes itself. Streets, bridges, wells, doorstones. Old stones especially. Hedge-stones, hearth-stones, the standing marks your kind has used since before there was a Crown to outlaw anything. Someone had to walk every street in Saltmere and record them all. Where they were. How strong. Whose blood had fed them, if you could read it."

Tamsin had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear — he'd seen hers by now, and this was different. This was the stillness of someone watching a door she'd suspected was locked swing open onto exactly the room she'd feared.

"My mistress talked about that," she said quietly. "The surveying years. She called it the Counting. She said half the old craft died in it — not because the wards killed anyone, but because once the Crown knew where every hearth-stone stood, the hearths started going cold. Inspected. Licensed. Or just... pulled up."

"I know. I walked behind the pullers sometimes." Edric turned the top map over, because looking at her while saying this was not possible. "I was twenty-six and the finest surveyor the Royal Service had produced in a generation, and I believed the wards were humane. Containment instead of burning. I told myself that right up to the night the Sundering came, and then I told myself I'd only ever held the pen, never voted with it, and then I told myself nothing at all for twelve years, because telling yourself things is easier when you leave the country."

"The Sundering wasn't the wards?"

"No." He spread the second map flat, weighting its corners with ink pots. It was the harbor chart, the last one he'd drawn before everything ended, and even now it hurt to look at: the great crescent of Saltmere Harbor rendered in his own careful hand, depths and moorings and the sea-road marked in blue that had faded almost to memory. "The wards were the knife lying on the table. Something else picked it up. Whatever wore the king — whatever wears him now — used our lattice as the blade. Nine anchor points around the harbor mouth. It pulled them all at once, and the sea tore free of the city along the lines we had drawn for it."

He let that sit. Outside the storm screamed; inside, the fire ticked and settled.

"We built the shape of the wound," he said. "I drew the scar."

* * *

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had lost its edge, which somehow made it harder to bear.

"Why tell me now? Why not at the chapel, when I asked what else you were hiding?"

"Because at the chapel I was still hiding the thing that mattered, and confessing to the small crime felt like a way to keep doing it." He met her eyes. He owed her that much honesty at least. "And because I need you to understand why I know the way in."

"The way in." Her brow creased. "To the palace. We're not — Edric, we agreed we'd find another way. We agreed the prophecy doesn't have to be followed like a recipe—"

"We agreed no such thing. *You* said it, and I nodded, because nodding is my best skill." He pulled the third map from the satchel, and this one he'd drawn fresh, in the waystations and chapels and stolen hours of the past week: the old capital's waterfront, reconstructed from a surveyor's memory that had never once, in twelve years, stopped replaying it. "Look. The Sundering didn't just take the water away. It changed what the water did. The harbor channel dropped forty feet in a single night — the sea didn't recede, it *fell*, pulled through the tear in the world's skin. Everything below the old tide line drowned in the backwash and then froze. The under-city. Warehouse bottoms. Ferry stairs. The tidal tunnels the customs men used to chase smugglers through. All of it still there. All of it under ice."

"You want to go under the harbor."

"The palace gates are watched by things that smell you from a mile off — forgive me, but they smell *you* specifically, and we both know it. The walls are thorn to a depth I don't want to guess at. But the palace was built on the harbor's edge, and like every fortress ever raised by proud men, it kept a private stair down to the water in case its owners needed to run. The Tide Gate. I surveyed it myself. It opens beneath the east bastion, thirty feet below the current ice line, which means—" he tapped the chart, "—which means it's been sealed under the frozen surface for twelve years, unwatched, because nothing that crawls thinks to look down."

"Nothing that crawls," Tamsin repeated. "But the roots. The roots are everywhere. We saw them in the drowned street outside the village — they grow through anything wet."

"They grow through anything *living*. Ice isn't living. That's the whole reason the under-harbor exists as a route at all." He hesitated, then gave her the rest of it, because he was done rationing truth in portions too small to choke on. "There's a second problem, and it's mine, and I'll say it plainly. The Tide Gate is warded. Everything in that palace is warded — it's the seat of the lattice, the first anchor point, the knot the whole net hangs from. And the ward on that gate is in my hand. My notation. My cipher."

"So you can't open it."

"So I can't *break* it. No one can break it; that was rather the point. But a ward is a lock with a grammar, and I wrote the grammar." He allowed himself the smallest breath of something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Twelve years ago I helped build a cage for people like you. Tonight I'm going to pick its lock. If that's not redemption, it will have to do until redemption arrives."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the daylight, or what passed for daylight under the storm's black lid. Edric drafted; Tamsin read the draft the way she seemed to read everything — sideways, skeptically, and with unnerving accuracy.

"This channel here." She touched the ferry stairs. "You've marked it passable. Under how much ice?"

"Six feet, maybe eight. There'll be air pockets under the vaulted sections. The old smugglers' run stayed dry at low tide even after the Sundering, according to the last report anyone filed."

"Filed by whom?"

"By me." He didn't look up from the compass. "Eleven days before the Sundering. I was mapping the tidal tunnels for the customs service. It was supposed to be a routine chart. I found things in those tunnels I didn't put in the report."

That stopped her. "What things?"

"Hedge-markings. Way-marks, your people call them, I think — signs cut into the stone showing safe passage, clean water, places to rest. Fresh ones. Someone had been using the tunnels as a road, whole families by the look of it, moving magic-folk out of the city ahead of the licensing purge." His jaw tightened. "I reported the markings' existence but not their meaning, and the customs men burned the tunnel mouths anyway. Three weeks later the Sundering came and drowned what was left of them. I've spent twelve years assuming I signed the death warrants of everyone who ever walked that road."

Tamsin was silent. Then, in a voice gentler than he had earned, she said: "You don't know that."

"No. I also don't know the opposite. That's the arithmetic of a coward's life — you never get to total the column."

"Well." She leaned over the map, hair falling forward, and he realized she was smiling faintly, or trying to. "Then here's better arithmetic. Whoever cut those way-marks knew the tunnels better than your customs report ever could. And hedge-signs don't rot. If any of your smugglers' road survives under the ice, their marks will show us which stretches hold air and which hold water." She tapped a junction he'd marked with a question. "Here. Look for a spiral with a broken line through it. That means *breath, but barely* — low air, move fast. My mistress drilled the signs into me before I could write my own name."

"You'll come, then." It wasn't a question, but it shook anyway.

"I said I'd see the winter broken. I didn't say I'd do it through the front door." She glanced up, and for a moment the old wariness was gone and something rawer sat in its place. "You should know what you're asking, though. That water will be death to swim. If the ice breaks over us, or the roots find a crack—"

"I know."

"And you can't swim worth a damn. I watched you fall in the melt-stream outside the village. You flailed like a startled goose."

"That," said Edric, "was a strategic assessment of depth."

"Mm." She rolled the map closed with surprising care, as if it were already precious. "We go at first light tomorrow. Storm should break by then — my knee says so, and my knee has been insufferably accurate this entire wretched journey."

He wanted to say more. That he was sorry, in words large enough to hold the wards and the burned tunnels and the chapel lie and every small treachery of a careful man's life. But apology was another kind of map, and he'd learned the hard way that the drawing of a thing is not the thing itself. So he said only:

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She banked the fire and wrapped herself in her cloak on the far side of the counting desk, and her voice came drowsy through the dark. "Ask me again when we're under eighty feet of ice and you're flailing like a goose."

* * *

He did not sleep. Instead he sat by the coals and drew, because drawing was the only prayer he'd ever trusted.

The route took shape page by page: the customs house ridge, the descent to the drowned quay, the breach in the ice above the old ferry stairs where the Sundering's backwash had cracked the freeze and never fully healed. Then the tunnels — the smugglers' road, the hedge-waymarks, the long cold crawl beneath the frozen harbor — and at the end of it, rising like a tooth out of a nightmare, the palace's east bastion and the Tide Gate stair, warded in his own hand.

His own hand. He stared at the cipher notation he'd reproduced from memory and felt the old shame turn over, slowly, like a body in water, and become something else. Purpose, maybe. Or its twin brother, which walks and talks the same but never sleeps.

Near dawn the storm broke, exactly as her knee had promised, and the light that came through the shattered shutters was the strange white light of the endless winter — sun without warmth, day without mercy. Edric folded the finished map into his coat, next to his heart, where he kept the things that judged him.

Tamsin was already awake. She stood at the window with her collar pushed aside, looking down at the mark on her collarbone — the thorn's signature, black as ink, and he saw with a lurch that it had changed overnight. The lines of it had grown. Delicate now, almost beautiful, reaching a half-inch further toward her throat.

"Tamsin."

"I know." She pulled her collar closed with steady hands, but her face had gone the color of the ice outside. "It knows where I am now. It's not searching anymore. It's *waiting*." She turned, and her chin came up, and beneath the fear he saw the thing he'd seen in her since the first night — the stubborn, furious aliveness of her. "So we stop letting it wait. Tide Gate, under-harbor, straight through. Today."

Edric shouldered the satchel. At the door he paused, looking back at the customs house where nine commissioners had once decided the fate of everyone like her, and at the woman who had decided, despite all of it, to trust him with her life.

"Stay close to me down there," he said. "If the ice fails — stay close."

"Lead on, cartographer," said Tamsin. "Show me the way you drew."
