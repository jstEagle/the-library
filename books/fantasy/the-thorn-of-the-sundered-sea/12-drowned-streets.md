## 12. Drowned Streets

The map Edric spread across the waystation table was drawn on paper so old it had gone the color of tea, and it was wrong. He knew it was wrong. He'd said so twice, tapping the harbor's edge where the ink ran into a brown bloom like an old wound.

"This is the harbor as it was," he said. "Before the Sundering took it."

"Then it's useless."

"It's the only map there is." He smoothed the crease with his thumb, careful as a man touching a sleeping animal. "Everything drawn since is guesswork. The royal surveyors stopped mapping the water the day it left. Nobody wanted to remember what was under it."

Under it, then, was Saltmere's drowned heart: three streets, two markets, a customs house, and — if the old charts could be believed at all — a service tunnel that ran from the sunken ferry landing beneath the palace hill. The Under-Harbor. Edric had helped draw its wards when he was twenty-three and ambitious and hadn't asked what the wards were for.

"And you're sure it's still there."

"I'm sure the palace is still there. I can see it from here." He nodded toward the window, where the black shape of the citadel stood against a sky the color of dishwater, thorn-roots crawling down its walls like veins down a corpse's arm. "The rest we find out by drowning or not drowning."

Tamsin folded her arms. "You've done this before. Gone into the water."

"No."

"But you've mapped drowned places."

"Once." His jaw worked. "A lake in the north, when I was still allowed to hold instruments. We walked the ice with ropes and poles and I drew what the soundings told me. It was the last commission I finished before—" He stopped himself, the way he always did, at the edge of whatever story he couldn't finish telling. "I know how ice behaves. That's something."

It wasn't much of something. But Tamsin had spent twelve years making do with not-much-of-somethings, and she'd learned to tell the difference between a man who was lying about being useful and a man who was afraid of being useless. Edric Vale was the second kind. She could work with the second kind.

"When?" she said.

"Tonight. The moon sets late; we'll have dark for the crossing and grey light for finding the tunnel mouth. And the storm—" he glanced at the shuttered window, where the wind had been worrying the boards since noon, "—the storm covers us. The thorn doesn't see well through blight-snow. We saw that at the waystation."

She didn't argue. She was tired of arguing. Somewhere between the chapel and the village that waited, arguing had stopped working, the way a lock stops working — not broken, just worn smooth, nothing left to catch on. Edric had lied to her. She had found the notes. She had screamed at him in a cellar until her voice went raw. And then they had walked out of the cellar together anyway, because the alternative was walking out alone, and alone meant dying in the snow with her name still in her mouth, unspent.

So: the water.

* * *

They went down through the fish-market district, past houses leaning into each other like drunks holding one another up, their windows boarded, their doorways bricked. No one had lived here since the Sundering. The city had simply amputated this part of itself and grown scar tissue over the stump.

At the bottom of Harrow Street the cobbles ended in a shelf of ice, and beyond the shelf, the sea.

Or what had been the sea. It lay flat and white to the horizon, welded to the land, and here at the drowned edge it rose in terraces — frozen waves, caught mid-curl, green-grey glass shot through with silt and shadow. Tamsin stood at the top terrace and looked down into the first layer of ice and saw, dimly, a streetlamp. Still standing. Still holding its lamp, though the lamp had been dark for twelve years.

"Oh," she said, very quietly.

Edric heard it. He looked at her sideways. "You can feel it. Can't you. The water."

She could. Not magic, exactly — or maybe exactly magic, the hedge-kind, the kind that lived in knots and charms and the small warm spaces between things. The drowned streets below them were full of luck-charms. Doorway-horses, threshold-knots, the little woven protections dockworkers' wives hung over their doors against drowning. A whole neighborhood's worth of small hopes, frozen mid-blessing, still faintly humming under the ice like bees in a wall.

"They never stopped believing," she said. "Even after the outlawry. They kept hanging the charms."

"People keep praying in countries where prayer is illegal too." Edric checked the rope at his waist, then hers, testing the knot that joined them — six feet of line, no more, close enough to die together. "Does it hurt? Feeling them?"

"Yes," she said, because it was true, and because she was tired of not saying true things. "Like hearing someone call your name from another room."

He didn't answer that. But his hand came up and touched her shoulder — briefly, awkwardly, the touch of a man who had read about comfort in books — and then he turned and stepped down onto the ice.

* * *

The crossing was worse than she had imagined and she had imagined it generously.

The surface ice held, mostly. It groaned and ticked beneath their boots, and every few yards a seam opened ahead of them with a sound like a struck bell, but the cold had been deep and long and the ice was thick as a church floor. What made it terrible was what was under the church floor.

They walked above the drowned streets. Below them, tilted and furred with pale weed, lay rooftops. Chimneys reached up toward the ice like drowned men reaching for a boat. Once they crossed directly over a market square and Tamsin looked down through four feet of green glass and saw the stalls still standing, the awnings rotted to lace, a child's wooden horse lying on its side in the doorway of a shop whose sign she could almost read.

She kept her eyes on her boots after that.

"Talk to me," Edric said. He'd noticed. Of course he'd noticed; he noticed everything, which was the whole problem with him. "Anything. The weather. Your feet. Tell me about the first charm you ever sold."

"Why?"

"Because your voice keeps the dark off. Both our darks."

So she talked. Low, under the wind, words falling into the ice-cracks. She told him about the luck-knots — three strands, seven turns, tied while thinking of the person's name. She told him about her mistress, Maren, and the stall behind the tannery, and the first coin she ever earned, a bent halfpenny from a fishwife who wanted safe passage for her son's first voyage. She told him the fishwife's son had come home. She did not tell him that she had gone back to thank Maren and found Maren looking at her with an expression she hadn't understood until years later, in a cellar, telling him everything else.

"That's how she marked me," Tamsin said. "Not with a brand. With gratitude. She made me think the magic loved me back."

Ahead, the ice changed.

She felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the hum underfoot, the drowned charms' faint bee-song suddenly smothered, as if a hand had been laid over the hive. Edric stopped. Held up his fist, the old surveyor's signal, and pointed.

Ahead of them, maybe thirty yards, the ice went dark. Not thin — dark. A patch of black glass perhaps ten paces wide, and beneath it, coiled in the drowned street below, something pale and branching. Roots. Thorn-roots, thick as a man's leg, running along the sunken roadbed like a river run backward, and where they passed, the ice above them had gone clear as a window, as if the roots had drunk the cloudiness out of it to see better.

"It's under us," Tamsin breathed.

"It's everywhere under us. This is its city now too." Edric crouched, laid his palm flat on the ice, and was silent a moment, reading it the way he read maps. "The clear patches are its eyes. It grows them where it wants to look. We go around — wide, to the north, along the old channel marker line. Slow steps. Don't let your boots ring."

They went around. Wide, to the north, slow, boots soft on the groaning floor. The rope between them hung slack and swayed. Tamsin counted her breaths the way Maren had taught her, four in, four held, four out, the rhythm that steadied the hands for knotwork, and she watched the black patch slide by on their left like a shark cruising a reef, and she let herself believe, for eleven steps, twelve, thirteen, that they would pass it clean.

On the fourteenth step, the ice sang.

Not cracked — sang. A high pure note rising from somewhere far below, from the drowned streets, from the roots, and Tamsin felt it in her teeth, in the bones of her feet, in the place behind her sternum where her magic lived, and the magic answered it. Before she could stop it. Before she even knew it was happening, the way you answer your own name before you're awake.

The black patch shivered.

"Tamsin." Edric's voice, sharp, low. "What did you do?"

"Nothing. I didn't—"

The ice under the black patch bulged upward. A root-tip pressed against the underside of the frozen surface like a finger pressed against a drum skin, and around it, hairline cracks raced outward, fast, silver, singing.

"Run," Edric said. "Don't run — walk fast — heel to toe, heel to toe—"

They went. The singing followed them, changing pitch, hunting, and new clear patches bloomed in the ice ahead of them like eyes opening, one, then three, then a dozen, and Tamsin understood with a lurch of pure animal horror that the thing below them was not chasing their footfalls. It was chasing her. Her blood. The hum of her, leaking up through her boots, through the rope, through the air, and the roots were turning toward it the way flowers turn toward a window.

The ice broke at the edge of the old ferry landing.

Not under them — beside them, a yard to Edric's right, a hole opening with a roar of released pressure, black water breathing out a fog of cold, and the root came up through the hole. It came up like a spear. Pale, jointed, barbed with thorns the length of knives, it stood swaying above the broken ice for one horrible moment, tasting the air, and then it turned.

It turned toward her.

"Tamsin!" Edric hauled the rope taut and threw himself backward, and the rope snapped her off her stride, spun her, saved her life — the root struck where she had been standing and punched through the ice as if it were bread crust. The hole widened. Cracks ran under her feet in all directions, a silver spiderweb, and she was sliding, sliding on her belly toward the black water with her fingers scrabbling at ice that broke away in plates under her nails—

She went in.

* * *

Cold is a strange mercy. It killed the pain before the pain arrived.

The water closed over her head and the world became a green-black bell, and below her, lit by some glow she refused to look at, the drowned street waited. Lampposts. Doorways. Threshold-charms on the doors, still knotted, still humming, and she grabbed for the nearest one — a doorway-horse, iron, crusted with weed — and her hands closed on it and the hum entered her palms and for one moment, one impossible moment, she could hear them all. Every charm on the drowned street. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A neighborhood's worth of small hopes, singing to her underwater in the dark, saying: *we are still here, we are still tied, we are still waiting.*

And among the songs, another voice. Older. Hungrier. Coming up the street from the direction of the palace, along the roadbed, through the water, wearing the roots like a body.

*There you are,* it said. *Little witch. Little blood.*

Her lungs were burning. Her skirts had filled with water and were dragging her down and the rope at her waist was taut and jerking — Edric pulling, Edric hauling, Edric on his belly at the ice-edge with his arm down the hole, screaming her name into the water in bubbles she could see but not hear.

The root came for her along the street. She watched it come, glowing faintly, unhurried, certain, and she understood that it did not need to hurry. The water was its house. She was the stranger in it, the intruder, the thief of breath, and it had all the time that winter had.

*Maren's voice,* she thought, absurdly, drowning. *This is Maren's voice. This is what she sounded like, underneath. Survival at any price. Give it what it asks. Live.*

Her hands were still on the iron doorway-horse.

Hedge-craft. Twelve years of it, hidden, denied, kept alive in secret like a coal banked in ash. Knots and charms and thresholds — and a threshold was a boundary, and a boundary could be *held*. Not broken. Hedge-witches didn't break things; breaking was for kings and cartographers and things that wore crowns. Hedge-witches held doors shut.

She couldn't speak the words. She had no breath to speak them with. So she spoke them with her hands, on the iron ring of the doorway-horse, the oldest knot there was, the one Maren had called the First Turning: *this door is mine, this line is drawn, what is not invited stays outside.* Her fingers moved in the freezing dark, weaving the charm out of nothing, out of weed and will and the last of her air, and the hum of the drowned charms poured into it, hundreds of small dead hopes lending her their strength because she was theirs, because she was the last one left who could hear them.

The root stopped.

Ten feet away, in the black water, it stopped — recoiled, actually, flinching back from her as if from a flame, thrashing once, twice, and then withdrawing up the drowned street, dragging its hunger with it, cheated.

The doorway-horse crumbled in her hands. Old iron, twelve years drowned; it gave like bread. But the charm held a moment longer than the metal, and in that moment the rope went hard and hauled, and Tamsin rose through the black water toward the ragged circle of grey light where Edric Vale was dragging her out of the sea with both arms and no dignity whatsoever.

* * *

She woke coughing on the ice, on her side, spitting salt and weed, with Edric's coat already off and being wrapped around her shoulders and his hands shaking so badly he could barely work the buttons.

"Breathe," he was saying. "Breathe. You're out. You're out, you're—"

"You pulled me out."

"Of course I pulled you out."

"The rope. You threw yourself back and the rope—" She coughed again, curled smaller inside the coat. "You could have gone in with me. The ice was going under you. I felt it going."

He was quiet a moment. In the distance, the palace squatted against the sky, thorned and patient, and the wind pushed the blight-snow across the ice in long grey veils.

"Yes," he said finally. "It was going."

"You'd have died."

"Yes."

"For me. For someone you lied to. For someone you used." Her voice came out scraped raw, half water, half something else. "Why?"

Edric sat back on his heels. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off. When he spoke, he didn't look at her; he looked at the hole in the ice, already skimming over with new skin, already healing, as if the harbor were closing its eye.

"Because I helped build the cage," he said. "Twelve years ago I drew the wards that made your magic a crime. I told myself it was order. I told myself it was mercy — better outlawed than hunted, better hidden than burned. And then the winter came, and the king died, and I watched a city learn to be afraid of its own hands, and I told myself it wasn't my doing." He exhaled, and the breath smoked and tore away. "I have been telling myself things for twelve years, Tamsin. Pulling you out is the first thing I've done that I don't have to explain to myself later."

She stared at him. Her whole body was shaking, the great slow shaking of a body rewarming, and inside the shaking something else was settling — some load-bearing wall inside her that had been cracked since the cellar, since the notes, since *he knew*, shifting quietly back into place. Not forgiven. Not yet, maybe not ever, fully. But bearing weight again.

"The root stopped," she said. "In the water. It came at me and it stopped."

"I saw."

"It stopped because of the charms. The drowned ones. All those little protections people hung on their doors — they're still down there, still tied, still working, and they answered me." She looked down at her own hands, blue-lipped, trembling, empty. "Edric. The whole drowned city is full of hedge-craft. And it all still works."

Something moved through his face then that she had never seen on it — not guilt, not fear, but the other thing, the rarer thing, the thing maps are made of. Recognition. The look of a man who has been staring at a blank region on a chart for years and has just seen, in the margin, a single inked line that changes everything.

"The tunnel mouth is under the ferry landing," he said slowly. "Two hundred yards east. The wards on it are mine — I drew them, I can open them. And the thorn won't follow you into the drowned streets, because the drowned streets are charmed against it." He stood, and offered her his hand, palm up, an anchor, a rope-end, a door held open. "Can you walk?"

Tamsin took his hand and let him pull her upright, and the harbor groaned beneath them, vast and patient, full of the dead and the tied and the waiting.

"I can walk," she said. "Show me the door."
