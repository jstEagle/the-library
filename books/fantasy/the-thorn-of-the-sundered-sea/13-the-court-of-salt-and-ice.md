## 13. The Court of Salt and Ice

The door under the ferry landing opened the way all Edric's wards opened: reluctantly, and with a sound like a held breath released through teeth.

He had drawn this one eleven years ago, kneeling on wet stone with a scribe's compass and a king's warrant in his pocket, and he had told himself then what he told himself every time he remembered it: *it's only lines on stone. It's only ink. It keeps people safe.* The ward had been part of the Sundering Edicts — every threshold into the old capital sealed against "unlicensed thaumaturgic passage," which was a cartographer's way of writing *no witch may come home*. He had been proud of it. He could still see the pride in his own handwriting, that young confident hand, each sigil placed like a nail in a coffin he hadn't known he was building.

Now he broke his own seal with two fingers and a word of unmaking, and stood aside to let a hedge-witch walk through the door he'd locked.

Tamsin didn't look at him as she passed. She hadn't looked at him properly since the cellar — not since she'd told him about the coven, about her mistress, about the twelve years of running, and he had said all the right sympathetic things while the notes in his coat burned against his ribs like coals. She was trusting him. That was the unbearable part. She had decided, somewhere in the dark under the village, that he was worth trusting, and every step they took toward the palace carried that decision closer to the moment it would break.

*Later,* he told himself, as he had been telling himself for three days. *There'll be time after.*

"After" was a word men like him used to survive their own arithmetic.

* * *

Beyond the ward, the tunnel rose.

It had been a service stair once, for harbor-porters hauling cargo up from the ferry landing to the customs houses above — a hundred and forty steps cut into the cliff's living rock, wide enough for two bearers abreast. Now the sea had taken the bottom third. They waded the first fifty steps through black water thigh-deep and cold enough to make Tamsin hiss through her teeth, and Edric counted the stairs aloud because counting was something useful a coward could do, and because if he stopped counting he would start listening to the water, and the water had things in it that listened back.

At step sixty-one the ice began.

It came up through the flooded stair like frost climbing a windowpane — first a skin, then a shelf, then a solid mass that groaned and ticked around them as they climbed out of the sea and into the mountain's throat. Above them the tunnel narrowed, then widened, then opened without warning onto a gallery, and Edric stopped so suddenly that Tamsin walked into his back.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"The customs house," he said. "Or what's left of the way into it."

The gallery ran along the inside of the cliff, arched and pillared, and every pillar was wrapped in thorn.

Not the creeping black briars of the blight-lands outside — those wild tangles that choked the roads and drank the warmth from the soil. These were deliberate. Cultivated. Each pillar wore its thorns like livery, wound in careful spirals, pruned flat where the wood met stone, and from each spiral hung small shapes that Edric refused, for the length of ten heartbeats, to identify. Then he identified them, because he was a cartographer and identifying things was the whole of his useless gift.

Bells. Small bronze bells, green with age, hung from the thorn on waxen cords. A hundred of them down the length of the gallery. Not one of them moved.

"They're warning bells," Tamsin said. Her breath fogged and did not disperse; the air here held cold the way a cupped hand holds water. "Harbor bells. You hang them on the pilings so you hear the weather coming."

"Yes."

"So why aren't they ringing?"

Edric looked down the long gallery, past the silent bells, past the pillars in their thorn livery, to where the tunnel continued upward toward the lightless heights of the palace, and he understood — the way you understand a map, all at once, top to bottom — what they were walking into.

"Because there's no one left to warn," he said. "And nothing left coming. This isn't a defense, Tamsin. It's a display. It's the thorn showing us what it did to the harbor's voice." He started forward before the understanding could finish settling into fear. "Stay close. Step where I step."

"Why?"

"Because I've mapped places like this." He heard his own voice go flat and professional, the voice he used when the truth was too large for feeling. "Palaces that have been empty a long time develop habits. The architecture remembers the people who lived in it. And this place doesn't remember people. It remembers *him*."

They climbed. The bells hung silent. Once — only once — as they passed the middle of the gallery, every bell shivered at once, a tremor like a struck glass, though no cord moved and no wind stirred. Tamsin's hand found Edric's sleeve and gripped it, and he did not shake her off, and they did not speak about it, and the bells went still again behind them like a held tongue.

* * *

The palace of the old capital had been called the Court of Tides, and Edric had never seen it whole.

No one had, in twelve years. The Sundering had torn the harbor away and the winter had buried everything else, and the maps he had made since — the ones that had earned him his disgrace, the ones he'd drawn from rumor and refugee testimony and the fevered accounts of sailors who claimed to have sailed the frozen channel — those maps showed the palace as a shape with a question mark inside it. *Here be the king,* his younger self had written in the margin, in a joke that had cost him his post. *Here be whatever ate him.*

Now he stood inside his own question mark, and wished, with a violence that surprised him, for his compass and his clean paper and the comfortable distance of not-knowing.

They came up out of the customs gallery into the palace's undercroft — the servants' warren, kitchens and stores and the long stone corridors where the work of a court got done — and the undercroft was intact. That was the first horror. Edric had expected ruin. Ruin he knew how to read: fire, flood, collapse, the honest grammar of disaster. But the kitchens were whole. The copper pots still hung from their hooks. A wooden spoon stood upright in a pot of something long since frozen to amber. An apron lay folded over a chair back.

Everything was simply *stopped*, mid-motion, mid-life, the way a clock stops — not broken but paused, as if the entire palace were a sentence someone had begun and never finished.

"Tamsin." His voice came out strange. "Don't touch anything."

She was already three steps ahead of him, and she wasn't touching anything. She was standing very still in the middle of the great kitchen, turning slowly, and her face — he had learned her face these last days, its stubbornness and its flinches and its terrible quick compassion — her face now held something he hadn't seen on it before. Recognition.

"I know this room," she said quietly.

"You can't. It fell twelve years ago—"

"My grandmother's kitchen looked like this. The pots, the apron over the chair. Hedge-folk copy what works." She touched nothing. Her eyes touched everything. "These were working people, Edric. Whoever froze this place didn't freeze the court. The court ran upstairs. It froze the *hands*."

And then, faintly, from somewhere far above — a floor above, two floors, five — came a sound that stopped both their hearts for the space of a full breath.

Music. Thin, warped, wrong-pitched, like a song played underwater or remembered badly. Strings, or something pretending to be strings.

"The court," Edric said, "is still running."

* * *

They went up because there was nowhere else to go. The prophecy had brought them here; the prophecy's geography, such as Edric had decoded it across a century-old page and a winter of walking, pointed inward and upward, to the heart of the palace, to the throne, to the thing that wore the dead king's crown. If they wanted answers, the palace was the only well left to draw from.

But the palace did not want to give them answers cleanly. The palace wanted to *show* them.

The second floor was the receiving halls, and the receiving halls were full.

They came through a service door into the antechamber of the Hall of Audiences, and Edric's first thought was that they had stumbled into a crowd — and then his mind caught up with his eyes and the crowd resolved into what it was: statues. No. Not statues. People. Dozens of them, courtiers and clerks and guardsmen, frozen mid-flight down the grand corridor toward some exit that no longer mattered. A woman with a candle — the candle still burning, a flame locked rigid in the air, giving off a light that warmed nothing. A man carrying a child, both of them turned back toward the doors they'd fled from, both of them wearing identical expressions of a question they would ask forever. A guard whose sword was half-drawn, the blade caught in the scissor of its own arc, and whose face —

Edric made himself look at the guard's face, because he had spent eleven years drawing this place from secondhand grief and he owed the dead at least the courtesy of being seen clearly.

The guard wasn't screaming. That was the worst of it. The guard looked *patient*. As if he had been told to hold, and was holding, and believed absolutely that someone would come with orders soon.

"How are they alive?" Tamsin whispered. She had gone gray. "Edric. How are they *alive*? I can feel them — there's something — they're not dead, they're *waiting*, I can feel it like — like seeds under snow."

"The winter doesn't kill quickly. That's its cruelty." Edric kept moving, kept his eyes forward, cataloguing doorways the way a drowning man catalogs driftwood. "The blight preserves what it takes. Nothing rots in the blight-lands. Nothing dies cleanly. It just — waits. Twelve years of waiting." He thought of the village that worshipped the Hollow King as a saint, the villagers who lit candles to their own slow freezing, and said nothing more, because more would have been either a lie or a mercy, and he couldn't afford either.

A woman frozen mid-curtsy beside them had a scrap of paper crushed in her fist. Edric eased it loose — the fingers gave with a texture he declined to think about — and unfolded it. A list. Names, in a clerk's tidy hand. Evacuation assignments. *To the ferry landing. To the north road. To the chapel of Saint Vess.* Halfway down, the handwriting deteriorated into a single repeated line:

*the king will not leave the king will not leave the king will not*

He put the paper in his coat, next to his notes, next to his guilt, and kept walking.

* * *

The Hall of Audiences opened before them like a held breath.

It was vast — built to hold a thousand petitioners under a ceiling painted with the harbor in its glory, ships and gulls and the sun on bright water, all of it dimmed now under a skin of frost that turned the painted sea to grey glass. Ice sheeted the pillars. The long carpet down the center aisle was stiff as slate. At the far end, beneath a canopy of state, stood the empty throne, and Edric felt his chest loosen with a relief so sudden and stupid that he almost laughed —

Because the throne was empty. Whatever sat on the throne, whatever ruled this winter, it wasn't *here*, it was walking the walls, it was elsewhere, they had reached the heart of the palace and found nothing at its center but cold air and an old chair—

Then Tamsin said, "Edric. The dais. Look at the dais."

The dais was a raised platform of white stone, seven steps up to the throne. And across its face, carved deep enough to catch the candlelight of the frozen flame still burning in the antechamber behind them, ran words.

Not chisel-work. Grown work — the letters were formed of thorn-wood, black and glossy, rising out of the stone itself like veins surfacing under skin. Edric crossed the hall slowly, boots cracking frost, and climbed three of the seven steps, close enough to read, and as he read, the relief drained out of him entirely and left something colder in its place.

It was the prophecy. Their prophecy. The book in Tamsin's bag, the water-eaten pages, the circled date, the description of a girl with hedge-blood and a man who drew false maps — all of it, here, in the throne room, written in a hand of living thorn a hundred years before either of them was born.

*Two shall come against the crown of winter:*

*a girl of hedge and hiding, carrying the old blood;*

*a man of maps and lies, carrying the old debt.*

*The blood shall call the thorn, and the thorn shall answer.*

*The debt shall hold the door, and the door shall open.*

*And the price of unbinding is written plain,*

*and none who read it may pretend they did not know:*

*a life must be given, freely, at the root.*

*A life freely given. Not taken. Not lost. Given.*

*The thorn accepts no theft. It accepts only gifts.*

Below the last line, smaller, almost an afterthought — though Edric knew better than anyone alive that prophecies did not do afterthoughts — a final verse:

*Let the reader mark which of the two it names,*

*for the blood knows, and the debt has always known.*

Edric read it twice. Three times. The words did not change. The words had never been going to change.

"A life freely given," Tamsin read aloud behind him. Her voice was steady in the way he had learned meant she was holding herself together with both hands. "That's — fine. That's fine. Someone gives a life. There must be — Edric, there must be another way to read it. A life. Any life. Yours, even, if you—" She stopped. He heard her stop, heard the thought arrive and curdle. "You've known," she said. Very quietly. "How long have you known?"

Every answer he owned was a lie or a confession, and he had run out of cleverness for choosing between them.

"The chapel," he said. "Saint Vess. There were records — genealogies, covenant rolls, the founding charters of the hedge-covens. The witches who crowned the first king bound themselves and their line into the crown's making. It's why the thorn marked you on the wall. It's why it's been following us, herding us—" He was talking too fast and knew it and could not stop. "The old blood isn't just a key, Tamsin. The old blood is the *payment*. The covenant was written into your family before you were born. Before your grandmother was born. I should have told you at the chapel. I told myself I'd tell you when there was time, when we were safe, when—"

"When it wouldn't matter anymore." She had gone very still. Somewhere above them, the thin warped music played on, patient as frost. "So the thorn hasn't been hunting us. It's been *escorting* me. All this way. To the root." Her eyes went past him, up the remaining four steps, to the empty throne, and Edric watched her understand the shape of the room they were standing in — not a throne room at all, he realized, not anymore. An altar. Seven steps. A vessel of old blood, delivered.

"No," Edric said. "Listen to me. The page says a life freely given. It doesn't say *yours*. Covenants can be renegotiated — I helped write ward-law, I know how binding language works, there's always a seam—" Even as he said it he heard how it sounded: a man reading the contract he'd drafted, looking for the clause that saved him. "We find the seam. We find another life, another gift, another—"

"Another sacrifice." Tamsin smiled at him, and it was the worst thing he had ever seen, because it was kind. "Like my mistress wanted. Like the coven always did. Just pick someone whose name isn't written in the book." She stepped past him, up the last four steps, and laid her palm flat against the thorn-carved letters, and the whole dais — the whole *palace* — seemed to lean toward her touch like a plant toward light.

Above them, far above, the music stopped.

In the silence that followed, every frozen flame in every antechamber guttered at once, and from the walls, from the direction of the battlements where the Hollow King walked his endless rounds, came a sound Edric had heard once before, on the night the thorn first marked Tamsin — a dry rustling, vast and pleased, like a forest turning over in its sleep.

The thorn knew she had read the price. The thorn knew she was considering paying it.

And somewhere between the seventh step and the throne, in the frozen dark of the Court of Tides, Edric Vale finally admitted what he had known since the chapel, since the map, since the beginning: there was no seam. There had never been a seam. There was only the page, and the girl standing on it, and the few hours left to decide whether the truth he'd hidden would save her — or teach her exactly how to die.

"Tamsin," he said. "There's something else on the page. Turn it over."

Her hand was already moving.
