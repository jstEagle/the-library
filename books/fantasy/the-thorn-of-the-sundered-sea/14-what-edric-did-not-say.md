## 14. What Edric Did Not Say

The Court of Salt and Ice had a scriptorium.

Tamsin found it the morning after they'd read the final page — after *a life freely given* had settled over them both like a second skin of frost. Edric said he needed to check his maps against the old survey charts, and she believed him, because wanting to believe him had become a habit she hadn't noticed herself forming until it was too late to break.

She went looking for him an hour later and found the corridor empty.

His pack was there, though. Leaning against a rotted reading desk in the scriptorium's corner, its buckle loose, its flap half-open — Edric Vale, who cinched everything down tight as a drumhead, who checked the latches twice before sleeping, who had once made her repack her own bag three times because the flint sat wrong. His pack lay open like a mouth.

She should have walked past it. She told herself that later, over and over: *you should have walked past it.* But the flap hung open, and inside, beneath the oilcloth-wrapped rations and the coiled rope, there was a leather folio that did not look like maps.

Her hands moved before her fear caught up with them.

* * *

The folio was old. Older than Edric's careful cartographer's hand, older than Saltmere, maybe. Its cover bore the stamp of the Royal Survey — the same broken-anchor seal that still appeared, half worn away, on the customs houses of the harbor district. Inside were pages in several hands. Some brittle and brown. Some newer, written in ink that had faded to the color of weak tea.

Edric's hand was among them.

She recognized it from the margins of his charts — that precise, upright script, each letter standing at attention. She had watched him write their route in that hand. She had watched him sketch her face once, quickly, when he thought she was asleep, and she had pretended not to see it, and something warm had lodged under her ribs for two days afterward.

Now she read what that hand had written, and the warmth curdled.

*Subject of the second prophecy confirmed. The Reyes girl. Hedge-trained, unregistered, bloodline traceable to the Corvane coven through her mother's mother.*

That was bad enough. That was twelve years of hiding reduced to a line in a bureaucrat's ledger. But she kept reading, because her hands would not stop, because some part of her already knew what was coming and wanted it done.

*Cross-referencing the chapel records against the original coronation rites. The crown was hedge-made. The binding runs on hedge-blood. The unbinding will require the same.*

*The price is not metaphorical. The thorn does not accept substitutes. It takes the name and the gift entire — the witch who performs the unbinding surrenders both permanently. There is no recorded case of recovery.*

And then, on the next page, dated — she counted back, and her breath stopped — dated eleven years before Tamsin had ever bought a water-damaged prophecy book, eleven years before moonrise had pulled her through a page and dropped her in the snow:

*I have found her. Or rather, I have found the line she comes from. The Reyes woman in Saltmere has a daughter. If the prophecy holds, this child is the one. God forgive me for what I am about to set in motion.*

God forgive me for what I am about to set in motion.

Tamsin stood very still in the frozen scriptorium, and the cold came up through her boots and into her bones and stayed there.

He had known. Before the book. Before the date circled in century-old ink. Before moonrise. He had traced her mother's mother's name through records that should have burned with everything else the Sundering took, and he had written it down, and he had waited — or searched, or arranged, she didn't know which was worse — and then he had come to Saltmere and found her selling luck-knots to dockworkers like any other honest criminal, and he had said, *I think you should see something,* and she had gone with him.

She thought of every conversation since. Every night in the waystations when they'd split the watch. The cellar where she'd given him the truth about her mistress, about the coven that had raised her to be a sacrifice, about how she'd run because she refused to be poured out for anyone's salvation — and he had sat there in the dark and listened, and said, *I won't let that happen to you,* and she had believed him, and all the while —

All the while he had known the price was her. Had known it before she was born, possibly. Had carried it folded in his pack next to the rations like a man carrying a receipt for something he'd already purchased.

The folio shook in her hands. She let it.

There was one more page. Newer ink. She read it twice, because the first time the words wouldn't stick.

*She must choose it freely. That is the condition. A life freely given. If she suspects the full weight of it, the choice is compromised — the rite will fail, and the winter will hold, and everything she suffers will be for nothing. Therefore: do not tell her. Let her find the pages herself if she must, but not before the throne room. Not before it can't be undone.*

*Let her hate me afterward, if she lives. Hate is survivable. Winter is not.*

*Let her hate me afterward, if she lives.*

Tamsin closed the folio with hands that felt like they belonged to someone else.

* * *

He was in the map room when she found him — a long gallery off the great hall where the old court's charts still hung behind cracked glass, the Sundered Sea drawn whole and blue, before the Sundering tore the harbor from it. He stood with his back to her, tracing a coastline with one finger, and for one last moment he was just Edric: tired, stooped, ink-stained, hers.

"You knew."

He didn't turn around right away. She watched his shoulders change — watched the cartographer's composure assemble itself across his back like armor being buckled on, plate by plate. When he turned, his face was calm, and that calm told her everything, because a man who didn't know what she meant would have asked.

"Tamsin—"

"The folio." Her voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "In your pack. You left it open."

"I didn't leave it open." Something flickered through the calm then — genuine alarm, quickly buried. "I never leave it open."

"No," she agreed. "You don't. Maybe the palace decided you'd carried it long enough." She held it up between them like evidence at a trial. "Eleven years, Edric. Eleven years ago you wrote my name in here. Well — my family's name. My mother's mother. You wrote that I was the one."

"Let me explain."

"Oh, I've read your explanation." She opened the folio with a snap and read aloud, her voice cracking on the words she'd already memorized in the scriptorium. "*Do not tell her. Let her find the pages herself if she must, but not before the throne room. Not before it can't be undone.*" She looked up. "You were going to march me to that throne room and let me walk into it blind. You were going to let me give everything I am without ever knowing I'd been chosen for it before I could walk."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Yes. That is what I intended. And I'd do it again, because the alternative is telling you now and watching you refuse, and then the winter holds, and the village we slept in freezes another generation into the ground, and the king walks those walls until the world ends. Tamsin, I have spent eleven years knowing this. Do you understand what that's been? Carrying the price in my pocket while I earned your trust? I have hated myself every single day of it—"

"Then why didn't you put it down?" Her voice rose, finally, finally, and the sound bounced down the long gallery and came back at her doubled. "You had eleven years to walk away. You had eleven years to find another way, or decide no way was worth this, or warn me so I could run far enough that no prophecy could reach me. Instead you came to Saltmere and you *recruited* me. You dressed it up as fate. As the book, the date, the page pulling me through — like none of us had a choice. Like it was just happening to us."

"It *was* happening to us!"

"The page pulled me through because it was *written to!*" The words tore out of her before she understood them, and then she did understand, and the understanding was worse than the notes. "You said the prophecy activates at moonrise. You said you'd seen it happen once before, in the archives, a hundred years ago. How did you know what to expect, Edric? How did you know to be waiting?"

He said nothing.

"How did you know to be standing in that square the night I found the book?" she pressed. "How did you know my name before I gave it to you?"

"I'm a royal cartographer," he said quietly. "I know things. It's what I'm for."

"You're a man who helped draw the wards that outlawed magic." She took a step toward him, and he didn't retreat, and she almost wished he had, because his steadiness was unbearable. "You told me that yourself. You built the cage, and then you went looking for the key, and the key turned out to be a girl, and you told yourself that was redemption. Finding me. Guiding me. But you weren't guiding me, Edric. You were *delivering* me. There's a word for a man who carries goods to a buyer."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down on the gallery, on the blue drowned sea in its frames, on the two of them standing in the ruin of a friendship neither of them had named aloud but both had leaned on like a wall.

"I saved your life in the storm," he said at last. His voice had gone rough. "In the drowned streets, I held you up when the roots had your ankle. Whatever I am, whatever I did — that was real. Every hour of it was real."

"I know." And she did, and that was the cruelest part. She thought of the waystation, the blight howling outside, the forbidden light of her own craft reflected in his frightened eyes — and how afterward he hadn't recoiled, hadn't reported her, had simply handed her the tea and said, *thank you for saving my life, hedge-witch,* with the title half apology and half wonder. She thought of him mapping the under-harbor by candlelight, confessing his wards, offering his guilt up like something he hoped she'd take. None of it had been false. That was the horror of it. He had genuinely cared for her, genuinely feared for her, genuinely meant every kindness — and had done all of it inside a lie shaped like a mission, and had told himself the caring balanced out the delivering.

"You should have told me in the chapel," she said. "When you found the coronation rites. When you learned what hedge-blood was for. You had a hundred chances, Edric. You chose this one to be silent, and you chose it because you were afraid I'd say no."

"Yes," he said. "I was afraid you'd say no. Because you would have. Because running away is what you do — twelve years of it, you told me so yourself — and I couldn't—" His composure broke, finally, all at once, the way ice goes. "I could not carry this alone anymore and I could not lose the only person who could end it. So yes. I lied. I let you believe in fate because fate doesn't get a vote. Call it betrayal. Call it what you like. But before you burn it all down — the king is real, the winter is real, the price is real, and you are the only one who can pay it. Whatever you decide about me, decide that too."

"There it is." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Even now. Even caught red-handed, it's still about the mission. Still about what I owe the world." She hugged the folio to her chest like a wound she was keeping warm. "My mistress said nearly the same thing, you know. The night before I ran. *Your life isn't yours, child. It was given to the coven before you were born. Duty isn't a choice.* She even cried a little. I used to wonder how she could cry and still do it. Now I know. You cry, and you do it anyway, and you call the crying proof you're good."

"Tamsin." Her name in his mouth sounded like a plea. "Don't do this. Don't go alone. The throne room is past the thorn-guarded halls, past everything we mapped together — you'll die in a corridor and the winter will outlive us both, and it will have been for nothing, and I will have destroyed you for nothing."

"*We* mapped nothing." She was backing toward the door now, folio clutched tight, and her eyes were burning and she refused to let anything fall. "You mapped. I trusted. Those are different things."

"Then take the folio," he said desperately. "Take the maps, take everything. Just tell me where you're going. Just let me follow. I'll stay ten paces behind, I swear it on the Sundering itself, I'll never speak unless you speak first—"

"Goodbye, Edric."

She turned and walked out of the map room, past the glass-locked sea, past the court frozen mid-flight, and she did not stop when she heard him call her name again — her true name, the one he alone in all the world had been using, the one that now sounded, coming from him, like something he'd taken without asking.

* * *

She walked until the palace's dead halls gave way to thorn.

It grew thicker here, deeper in — black branches furred with frost, arching overhead like the ribs of some enormous beast, and beneath it the air hummed with that wrong, sweet attention she had felt since the walls, the sense of being *noticed*. The mark the Hollow King's thorn had left on her wrist had faded to a pale scar, but tonight it woke, prickling warm, as if the thing behind these branches had heard the door slam on a relationship twelve days in the making and considered it progress.

*Alone,* the dark seemed to say. *At last.*

"Not yet," Tamsin told it, and unwrapped the luck-knots from her hair — the ones she'd braided in Saltmere a lifetime ago, back when her magic was a secret and her name was hers — and began, with numb fingers and a heart like a stone in her chest, to work.

Behind her, faint and fading, she could still hear Edric calling.
