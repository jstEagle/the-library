## 15. Alone in the Long Winter

The palace did not want her there.

Tamsin felt it the way she felt weather coming in her knees — a pressure, a wrongness, a slow gathering weight. The corridors leaned. The doors had opinions. Every few yards the thorn came up through the flagstones like a black vein surfacing under skin, and it was always reaching, always angled toward her as if she were a fire it meant to smother.

She kept walking.

Behind her, somewhere back through the frozen court and the hall of salt-crusted tapestries, Edric was either dead or following. She had not looked back to find out which. Looking back was a luxury for people who hadn't just learned that the man beside them had known — had known from the chapel, from the records, from the very first night — that her blood was the price written into the end of everything. That he'd watched her sleep by waystation fires for six days with that arithmetic folded up inside his coat like one of his maps.

*You knew,* she'd said, and his face had done something terrible and honest, and she'd turned away before he could make words of it, because if he made words of it she might have believed them.

So: alone.

"Fine," she said to the corridor. Her voice came out small and scraped. "Fine. I've been alone before."

The corridor, being a corridor in a dead king's palace strangled by a thorn-thing older than the Sundering, declined to be impressed.

* * *

Hedge-craft, her mistress Maren had taught her, was not power. Power was what the tower witches had, what got outlawed, what burned cities and cracked harbors off their moorings. Hedge-craft was *attention*. It was knowing that rowan bark bittered fever, that iron in the threshold kept out things that walked on lies, that salt remembered where it had been and could be persuaded to remember other places too. It was a hundred small tricks that added up, if you were patient and desperate enough, to staying alive.

Tamsin had been patient and desperate her whole life. The palace didn't stand a chance. Or rather — it stood every chance, and she intended to cheat it anyway.

The first door was locked with more than a lock. Iron-bound oak, and across its seam the thorn had grown a lattice of pale shoots, each one tipped with a bud like a closed eye. When she reached for the handle the buds all opened at once, and they had no pupils, and they looked at her.

She snatched her hand back and sat down on the cold stone to think, which was itself a hedge-trick — Maren's first rule: *when the craft fails, wait. Fear spends faster than sense.*

Locked doors in Saltmere she understood. Saltmere doors wanted keys or crowbars. This door wanted her to want to open it, so the thorn could taste the wanting and follow it home to whatever was left of her name. So she stopped wanting. She took out her kit — a fisherman's awl, a twist of grey thread, three fingerbones from a gull, a stub of tallow candle she'd lit at the shrine-village and never let fully die since — and she went to work sideways.

Thread first. She tied a luck-knot, the same kind she sold dockworkers for two coppers, except this one she tied wrong on purpose: a bad-luck knot, an unmaking knot, the knot you tied when you wanted a thing to come loose. She whispered into the loops the only word of true speech Maren had ever trusted her with, a word that meant roughly *let go*, and laid the thread against the pale lattice like a ribbon across a wound.

The buds shivered. One by one the shoots withdrew from the door-seam, curling inward like fingers unclasping, and behind them the iron lock hung open on nothing at all — because the thorn had been the lock, and the thorn had let go.

"You're learning," Tamsin told herself, and hated how much she sounded like Maren.

Beyond the door: stairs going up, and cold coming down the stairwell like water finding its level. Cold that moved. She lit the tallow stub, cupped it, and spoke to the flame the way you spoke to anything small and loyal — *not yet, hold on, we're not done* — and climbed.

* * *

The second trick cost her blood, and she paid it grudgingly, licking the pad of her thumb afterward like a child who'd skinned a knee.

It happened in a gallery lined with portraits, kings and queens of the old line rendered in oils gone grey with frost-rot. Halfway along, the floor simply ended — not collapsed, but *taken*, the stones replaced by a matting of thorn woven tight as basket-work, and beneath it a dark so complete it seemed less like absence than appetite. The thorn-mat shivered when her boot touched the nearest flagstone. It knew she was there. It was, she realized with a lurch, not a barrier but a mouth, and mouths waited.

Maren's second rule: *never cross a hungry thing by force. Feed it something else.*

What do you feed a thorn grown fat on a dead king's winter? Not blood — blood was the feast it wanted, blood was the whole reason she'd been dragged here by a page of paper a century dead. But hedge-craft was attention, and attention said: the thorn wore the crown, and the crown had been made by witches like her, and things made by witches still remembered witch-logic no matter how far they grew from it. Witch-logic said: a binding wants to bind. Give it a lesser thing to hold and it will hold that instead.

She cut her thumb after all — a thin line, payment, not feast — and pressed it to the grey thread, then to the gull bones, whispering the *let-go* word backwards, which made it a *hold-fast* word. She bound the three fingerbones together into a little effigy, no bigger than her smallest finger, and gave it a scrap of herself: her breath, three times; her warmth, held in her palms; and last, quietly, ashamedly, one true memory — the smell of her mother's kitchen, bread and bay leaves, gone twelve years and hoarded like coin.

Then she threw it onto the thorn-mat.

The effect was obscene. The whole woven surface convulsed, shoots whipping around the tiny effigy, and where they touched it the bone drank the thorn's hunger and the thorn drank the memory and for one moment the whole gallery smelled impossibly, unbearably of bread baking, and Tamsin's eyes filled before she could stop them. Then the thorn-mat stilled, sated and stupid, holding its prize, and she crossed it at a run, boots light on the woven branches, and did not look at the dark below.

She was four steps past the far edge when the gallery behind her exhaled — a long, cold sigh through a thousand thorns, and in it, faint as a voice through a wall, something that might have been her mother's voice saying her name.

Her real name. The one she'd hidden in Saltmere. The one nobody living knew.

"Don't," she said aloud, to the gallery, to the thorn, to herself. "That's not her. That's bait."

But her hands shook for the next hundred yards, and the candle flame bent away from her as if flinching.

* * *

She counted her remaining tricks like coins.

The luck-knots: five left. The salt, half a pouch — enough for two circles, maybe three if she was stingy. The tallow candle, down to a thumb's width. The iron nail she'd pulled from a waystation beam in another life, back when Edric had still been a man she trusted. And one trick she'd never used, because Maren had called it the last trick and meant it the way you mean *last rites*: the hollowing, where a hedge-witch poured out what she was into a working and stood afterward empty, alive but vacant, a house with the furniture gone.

Twelve years ago she had run from a coven that intended exactly that for her — the hollowing, performed slowly, ritually, to feed the crown. She had sworn on her mother's grave she would die empty of nothing but choice.

And now she carried the last trick in her pocket like a knife, telling herself she wouldn't use it, knowing how knives worked.

The palace got worse as she climbed. The thorn thickened until the walls were more branch than stone, and the frost on it glowed faintly blue, and sometimes between one heartbeat and the next she saw figures in it — the frozen court, mid-flight, preserved like flies in amber, faces turned toward doors they'd never reach. A woman in ermine, hand outstretched toward a child already lost to the ice. A guardsman whose spear-hand had frozen mid-swing at a thorn that had since grown straight through his breastplate and out his back, blooming there in a rose of black wood.

Tamsin stopped once, despite herself, and touched the guardsman's gauntlet. Under the rime it was warm. Everything in this place was warm under the frost, warm as a body, because the whole palace was a body now, the Hollow King's body grown vast and architectural, and she was walking through him toward the wound at his center.

"I'm sorry," she told the guardsman, because her mother had raised her to apologize to the dead, and because sorry was the only coin she had left that wasn't spent.

* * *

The blight-storm found her on the upper landing, and it was nothing like the one at the waystation. That storm had been weather. This was *aimed*.

She heard it coming — a sound like a forest clearing its throat — and had time for exactly one salt circle before the landing filled with spores. They came down through the broken roof in a grey snowfall of their own, drifting, delicate, and where they landed on stone the stone softened and grew a fine white fur of corruption. Where they landed on flesh — she'd seen the waystation rats. You didn't want to know about flesh.

Inside the circle she knelt with the candle and did the mathematics of survival. The circle would hold maybe ten minutes. The storm showed no interest in ending. The spores piled against the invisible boundary like snow against glass, and through them she could see the thorn on the walls turning toward her, drinking the blight-light, growing greener and more eager.

Ten minutes. What did she have that lasted ten minutes?

Attention. Attention and a candle and a pouch of salt and the knowledge — Maren's third rule, the deep one, the one the tower witches sneered at because it couldn't be taught in towers — that *every working is a conversation, and conversations can be redirected.* The blight-storm wasn't the thorn's own power. Nothing here was; the thorn was a parasite even in its triumph, wearing a dead king's winter the way it wore his crown. The storm was borrowed. Borrowed things answered to their source. And the source, however distant, however rotted, had once been a hedge-witch's crown, and hedge-craft ran in it like old blood in old wood.

So Tamsin did the forbidden thing, the thing she'd done once at the waystation while Edric watched with fear in his eyes, and this time there was no one to watch and no one to fear her and no one to stop her, which was somehow worse. She opened herself. She let the cold in past the place where most people keep their warmth locked. She spoke the true words — both directions now, *hold-fast* and *let-go*, the whole vocabulary she owned — and she addressed the storm directly, as one practitioner to another, as a granddaughter of the craft to the crippled remnant of the craft's greatest crime.

*"You were meant to keep a king safe,"* she told it. *"He's dead. Who are you keeping safe now?"*

The spores faltered. The grey snowfall hung in the air, suspended, listening — if storms listen; if parasites can be reminded of their instructions.

*"Let go,"* Tamsin said, and put everything she had left into the words, and meant them so hard she tasted copper.

The storm broke apart like a held breath released. Spores drifted down harmless as ash, dead on the air, and through the sudden clear cold she saw the moon through the shattered roof — the first clean sky since Saltmere — and beyond the palace rooftops, far off, the frozen harbor lying flat and white under it like a road to nowhere.

She stayed in the circle a moment longer than she needed to, shaking, empty-handed, hollowed in the small ways that don't show. Using the craft like that, wide-open, unshielded, cost something every time. She could feel the costs accumulating. She was spending herself the way the thorn spent the king.

"Well," she said to the dead spores, to the moon, to nobody. "Only way out is through."

* * *

The throne room door was the size of a cathedral's shame.

It filled the end of the final corridor entirely — black wood banded in silver gone grey, carved over every inch with the old covenant in script she couldn't read but recognized the shape of, because Maren's oldest book had carried the same letters, and Maren had never let her touch that book. The thorn had grown around it but not through it. Even the parasite, it seemed, would not enter here uninvited. Even the thorn knocked.

Tamsin stood before it, swaying on her feet, and took inventory one last time. Two luck-knots. A pinch of salt, enough for one circle barely bigger than her own body. The candle, a thumbnail of tallow guttering on its wick. The iron nail. And the last trick, the hollowing, folded up small in the pocket of her heart where she'd promised herself it would stay.

The door was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything here was warm under the frost, and this was the warmest thing in the world, the hearth at the center of the winter, and standing before it she understood with terrible clarity what the warmth was. It was the king. Twelve years of dying, stretched thin as spun sugar across a kingdom, leaking out of wounds that would not close, and the door was the last skin over the deepest wound, and beyond it the throne room was the wound's interior, and in it —

In it was the price. In it was the bargain the prophecy named. *A life freely given.* She'd read those words in the Court of Salt and Ice with Edric's guilty silence ringing in her ears, and she'd run from them, and now there was nowhere left to run but through a door that led to the giving.

Her hands wouldn't stop trembling. She pressed them flat against the black wood and felt the covenant-script stir under her palms like letters remembering they were language.

"Maren was right," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "About everything. I should've stayed in Saltmere. I should've sold luck-knots till I died old and small and law-abiding." A laugh came out of her that had nothing to do with humor. "But I didn't. And I'm here. And there's no one coming."

No Edric. No coven. No mistress with her gentle, monstrous logic — *survive, child, at any price, the price is always someone else's.* Just Tamsin Reyes, who was not sure anymore that Reyes was even hers to claim, standing at the door of the long winter with a thumbnail of candle and a pinch of salt and whatever a person has underneath the name.

She lit the candle from the stub's last breath of flame, and set it on the floor, and drew the salt circle around it and herself, the smallest circle of her life, barely room to kneel. Then she took out the iron nail and the grey thread and began the last knot — not luck, not unbinding, but the knot her mother had tied in her hair the day they sent her to Maren, the knot that meant *come home.*

The door groaned.

Not opening. Answering. From the far side of all that black wood, something shifted its weight — something vast and patient and crowned — and a voice came through, and the voice was the worst thing yet, because it was kind.

"*Little witch,*" it said, in a tone like a grandmother calling a child in from the cold. "*I have been waiting so long for someone of your blood. Come in. Come in. Let us talk about names.*"

The candles guttered. The salt circle trembled at its edges. And Tamsin, rock-bottom, spent, alone in the long winter with the last trick in her pocket and the last knot half-tied in her hands, lifted her chin at a door tall as her terror and said:

"No. We're not talking about my name."

She pushed, and the door — warm as flesh, yielding as a wound — swung open onto the light of the Hollow Court.
