## 16. The King Beneath

The palace did not want Edric Vale inside it.

He had known that from the moment he crossed the threshold of the drowned gate, wringing seawater from his coat while Tamsin's footsteps faded somewhere ahead in the dark. The walls themselves seemed to lean away from him, the way a crowd leans back when a debtor walks into a room. He had helped draw the wards that sealed this place. The stone remembered. Every corridor he entered, he felt the faint resistance of old geometry recognizing its author and closing behind him like a ledger.

Good, he told himself. Let it remember. Let it count what I owe.

Tamsin had gone on alone an hour ago — no. Not an hour. Time moved strangely here, under the ice, where no sun came and the frost grew in patterns too regular to be natural. Half a day, perhaps. She had found his notes. She had looked at him with an expression he would carry to whatever grave the winter allowed him, and she had said only one word — *why* — and then she had taken her lantern and gone up toward the throne room without waiting for an answer.

There wasn't one worth giving. *Because I was a coward* was not an answer. It was just the truth, wearing different clothes.

So now he followed. Not along her path — the thorn had grown thick through the great stair, a lattice of black wood and white frost that would sense hedge-blood long before she reached the doors. But every palace had two maps: the one drawn for kings, and the one drawn for servants. Edric had spent three years of his disgrace cataloguing the royal archives, and in the third cellar of the Office of Works he had once found a survey of the under-palace, dated forty years before the Sundering. Service tunnels. Cistern galleries. And beneath the throne dais itself, a chamber marked with a single word in the surveyor's neat hand:

*Crypt.*

Kings were buried in tombs, out in the necropolis beyond the city, under marble canopies with their names cut deep. This king had a crypt directly beneath his own throne, surveyed but never finished, and Edric had spent twelve years wondering why, and eleven of those years pretending he hadn't.

He went down through the cistern gallery, following the survey from memory because he had burned his copy — burned it, along with everything else, the night the wards were signed. The gallery was a throat of black ice, the cisterns frozen solid, and here the cold was different. Not the dead cold of winter. A colder thing. A cold with a pulse.

He noticed it first as a rhythm in his teeth. Then in the lantern flame, which shivered at intervals — steady intervals, patient intervals — like a heart beating once every ten breaths. Slow. Enormous. The heartbeat of something that had been dying for twelve years and refused to finish.

Edric stopped at the crypt door.

It was iron, older than the Sundering, and across it someone had scratched wards in the old hedge-script — the same script Tamsin's charms used, the same curves and knots he'd seen her fingers trace absently on tabletops when she thought no one watched. But these were not sealing wards. He knew sealing wards; he had helped design better ones, ones that broke women like Tamsin. These were holding wards. They were drawn from the inside.

Someone had locked themselves in.

His hands were shaking. He made himself look at them, made himself be ashamed of it, and then he pressed his palm flat against the iron.

"Your Majesty," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, because the last time he'd spoken those words aloud they had been in a signature line, beneath a map, beneath a decree. "I helped build your cage. All of them. I'm the one who drew the lines."

The heartbeat in the ice paused.

Then, faintly, from beyond the door: a sound Edric had never heard in twelve years of studying the Hollow King's winter. A voice — ruined, thin, scraped down to almost nothing — said:

"*Lines.*"

Not a question. A recognition. Something on the other side of the iron had heard the word *lines* and understood it as an accusation, or perhaps a confession, and had decided to let the confessor in.

The wards did not fight him. They parted like a hand opening.

* * *

The crypt was small.

That was the first wrongness. Edric had imagined vaults, catacombs, the grand architecture of death that kings commissioned. Instead there was a single round chamber, maybe ten paces across, its ceiling low enough that he stooped, its walls raw stone still bearing the chisel-marks of workmen who had left before finishing. No sarcophagus. No canopy. Just a bed — an actual bed, a soldier's cot with a straw tick — and upon the bed, the king.

Edric had seen the Hollow King from a distance, twice, in the years after the Sundering. Once from a ship's deck before the harbor froze, standing on the palace wall with the thorn-thing wrapped around his shoulders like a mantle, walking with that terrible smooth gliding motion that no living man could manage. Everyone in Saltmere knew the figure. The dead king who walked. The crown that wore the man.

This was not that figure.

The man on the cot was gaunt past reason, gray-skinned, his chest barely moving. His hair had gone white — not aged white but bleached white, drained of color like everything else the winter touched. His eyes were closed. His hands lay folded on his breast, and they were the hands of a working man, scarred and blunt, nothing like the jeweled effigies on the coins.

And from his wounds — from six wounds, Edric counted, one in each shoulder, one over each hip, one at the throat, one at the sternum — the winter leaked.

There was no better word for it. From each wound seeped a slow exhalation of frost, a fine white vapor that rose, curled, and spread upward through cracks in the unfinished ceiling — upward, Edric understood with a lurch, into the palace above, into the sky, into the twelve-year winter that had buried a harbor and starved a coast. The king breathed out, and it snowed. Somewhere far above, weather was being made by a dying man's pain.

The thorn was in him. That was the horror of it — not wrapped around him, worn by him, the way it wore his body on the walls upstairs. It was *rooted*. Black tendrils showed beneath the skin at each wound site, veined and branching, sunk so deep that flesh had grown around them the way bark grows around a nail. The thorn had crowned him here, in this unfinished room, and then it had climbed up through the palace wearing him like a glove, and left this — the true man, the original — bleeding winter into the world from a bed of straw.

Still breathing. Twelve years. Still breathing.

Edric knelt beside the cot because his legs simply stopped being willing to hold him.

"You're alive," he said. It was absurd. Everyone knew the king was dead; the whole theology of Saltmere, the whole machinery of outlawry and fear, was built on the fact of a dead king walking. "You're alive, and we — we built the wards on the story that you weren't. We outlawed magic because we said your death proved magic kills its kings, and you were—"

The king's eyes opened.

They were brown. Ordinary brown eyes, bloodshot, exhausted, human all the way down. They found Edric's face and focused on it with effort, the focus of a man surfacing from very deep water.

"Not," the king whispered. Each word cost him visibly; frost puffed from the wound at his throat with every syllable. "Dead. Never. Dying. Twelve. Years. Of. Dying." A pause. The heartbeat in the ice beat once, and Edric realized with revulsion and pity tangled together that the rhythm he'd felt through the cistern wall was this man's heart, magnified by the thorn, broadcast through the whole frozen structure of the palace like a bell tolling through a body of water. "It keeps me. Dying is. The last thing. That's mine."

Edric's throat worked. "The thorn. The thing upstairs. It—"

"Took my. Shape." The ghost of something moved across the ruined face — not a smile, exactly, but the memory of one, bitter as salt. "Wears me. Walks. My wife thought. She was burying me. Here. Safe. Hedge-women warded. The door." His eyes drifted to the scratched script on the inside of the iron, and grief moved through them, fresh, undiminished by twelve years. "She was wrong. It was already. In the crown. In me. When they carried me. Down."

"The hedge-witches," Edric said. "They made the crown. I found the records — in the chapel at Vael's Cross. The old coven forged it to bind the realm's wild magic, and the binding failed, and the thorn got into it, and—"

"I know." Frost spilled from the sternum wound. "I asked them. To make it. Thought I could. Hold it. A king should hold. What harms his people." The brown eyes closed again. Opened. "A king should not. Ask others. To bleed. For his crown."

And Edric, kneeling in the unfinished dark with a dying man's breath snowing out of his wounds, thought of a girl with a lantern climbing toward the throne room, and felt the words arrive in him like a blade going in.

*A life freely given,* the prophecy's final page had read. *Given, not taken.*

Tamsin Reyes was climbing those stairs believing she had to give hers.

"No," Edric said aloud. The king's eyes opened again, questioning. "No, Your Majesty. You've paid twelve years of dying. You don't have to—" He stopped, because he didn't know how to say it, didn't know if it was even true, only that some part of him had understood, kneeling here, what the unbinding actually required. Not a sacrifice hauled up the stairs in terror. Not a girl spending herself to stop a monster. The thorn fed on given things. Given freely, or not at all.

"It wants. A gift," the king whispered, watching Edric's face with terrible clarity, the way dying men see everything at the end. "It always. Wanted. A gift. I gave it. A crown. It took. Everything else." His hand — slow, trembling, strong underneath the ruin — caught Edric's wrist. The grip was ice, but the will inside it was fire. "Whoever climbs. Those stairs. Tell her. The thorn cannot. Take. What is not. Offered. And it cannot refuse. What is. Offered truly. That is the flaw. In its crown. That is the flaw. In mine."

"What do you mean? Offered truly — offered what?"

The king smiled then, properly, and it transformed his face into someone Edric might have followed gladly, once, in another life.

"My name," he said. "It has been. Trying to take. Mine. For twelve years. It cannot. Because I have not. Given it." The grip tightened. "Find her. Before she offers. The wrong thing. In the wrong way. Out of fear." Frost poured from his lips, and his voice was failing, sinking back toward the long drowning rhythm of his breathing. "Fear makes. Poor gifts. Fear is how. It got. Me."

Above them, muffled through twelve feet of stone and ice, something began to move. A slow, gliding tread, pacing the floor of the throne room. Pausing. Pacing again. The thorn had sensed her — of course it had, it had marked her at the wall, it had been waiting for her blood since the day the page pulled her through — and it was waiting now, patiently, the way it had waited twelve years for a gift freely given.

The tread stopped. Directly overhead. Directly above the place where, if Edric read the survey right, Tamsin must now be standing before the doors of the throne room.

The king's hand slipped from Edric's wrist. His eyes were closing, his voice a thread.

"Go," he breathed, and the winter leaked from the wound at his throat like a final benediction. "Tell the witch. Her name. Is worth more. Than her death. Go — *cartographer* —"

Edric ran.

Up through the cistern gallery, through the throat of black ice, his lantern streaming shadow behind him, taking the service stairs three at a time because every second was a word the king had spent a dying breath to give him. The wards of the palace tried to close behind him and he tore through them, his own signatures, his own guilt, ripping like paper.

The throne room doors stood open ahead, frost pouring through them like smoke.

And on the threshold, lantern raised against the dark, small and alone and utterly resolved, stood Tamsin Reyes — already beginning to speak.
