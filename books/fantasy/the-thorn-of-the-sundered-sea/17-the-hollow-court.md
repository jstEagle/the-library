## 17. The Hollow Court

The doors of the throne room were taller than the chapel in which Tamsin had learned her letters, taller than any building Saltmere had ever managed, and they were made of ice that had once been glass.

She could tell because the glass was still there under the frost — panes of it, green with age, set into frames of black iron that had gone white at the joints. Someone had built these doors to let light in. Someone had wanted the throne room warm. Now the light came through sideways and dead, the way it does through a fish's eye, and the cold on the other side of the doors was not weather anymore. It was a held breath.

Tamsin stood before them with blood on her knuckles and her mistress's ring burning cold on her thumb and nothing left in her but the stubborn, animal refusal to lie down in the snow like a sensible person would have done three days ago.

Behind her, far behind, Edric's voice still echoed in the corridors where she'd left him shouting her name — or not her name, because he didn't know it, he'd never earned it, and that was its own kind of armor. She had walked away from him an hour ago. Her legs remembered every step.

*You knew,* she had said. *From the chapel. From the beginning.*

He hadn't denied it. That was the worst of it. He'd stood there with his maps and his guilt and his terrible honest face and let her see that he had known what her blood was for since the moment he found the hedge-witches' records, and he had walked her toward this door anyway, one kindness at a time, the way you lead a lamb.

So here she was. At the door. Alone, as the prophecy required, as the thorn required, as apparently everything in her life required.

"Fine," she told the doors. Her voice came out small and scraped. "Fine."

She pushed.

The ice-glass swung inward without weight, without sound, and the breath the room had been holding came out over her all at once — cold, yes, but underneath the cold something sweeter. Something like hawthorn in May. Something like the inside of a cut stem.

The Hollow Court opened before her.

* * *

It had been a throne room once. She could read buildings the way Edric read maps — her mother had been a mason's daughter, and Tamsin had grown up knowing how walls meant to stand. The dais was there. The pillars were there, marching away into gloom, each one wrapped to twice its width in thorn.

That was the wrong word, she realized. Thorn suggested something brambled, accidental, a fence gone feral. This was architecture. The branches rose in deliberate spirals, load-bearing, crowned with buds that had never opened in twelve years and never died either. They had been *kept*. The whole court had been kept, the way you keep a specimen in a jar.

Because the court was still here.

They stood along the walls in their hundreds — courtiers, guards, a woman in a gown stiff with beadwork, a boy holding a lute he had been carrying when it happened, all of them silvered over like things left too long in a freezer, all of them mid-motion. A man reached out to steady another who was falling. A girl looked back over her shoulder at a door none of them had reached. They had been running when the winter took the palace, Tamsin understood, running from whatever came up through the floor wearing their king's face, and they had not finished the run yet.

Her breath fogged. Theirs did not.

"Don't look at them," said a voice from the dais. "They can't see you. It isn't kind to make them try."

The Hollow King sat on his throne.

Or — no. Tamsin made herself look properly, the way her mistress had taught her to look at anything that wanted to be feared: find the seams. And the seams were everywhere. There was a king-shaped absence on the throne, a slumped darkness in the shape of a tall man, and growing out of him, through him, around him like ivy through a statue, the thing that wore his crown.

It was beautiful. That was the trap of it, and she saw the trap immediately because she had grown up in the coven house where everything beautiful was a hook. The thorn-thing had arranged itself across the empty king like a lady arranging her skirts — black stems, white flowers forced open out of season, and above it all the crown itself, iron and salt-pearl, sitting crooked on a head that wasn't there.

"I have waited," it said, "a very long time to be visited by someone with your blood."

* * *

"You're the one from the book," Tamsin said. Keep talking. Keep your feet moving. Her mother's rule, then her mistress's rule: a still target is a chosen target.

"I am the one in the book," the thorn agreed. Its voice came from everywhere the branches went, which was everywhere, which meant it came from the walls and the floor and the frozen courtiers and possibly, she thought with a lurch, from her own teeth. "The book was mine. I wrote myself down so I would not be forgotten. Everything that lives long enough learns that trick." The crown tilted, considering her. "You bought me for the blank pages. I enjoyed that."

"The blight-storm. The waystation. The roots in the harbor."

"Signposts," it said pleasantly. "I don't drag people here, hedge-witch. I invite. There is a difference, and the difference is consent, and consent is the only law left in this country." A branch uncurled from the arm of the throne and gestured — graciously, mockingly — at the floor before the dais. "Come. Stand where the petitioners stood. You've crossed my whole winter to say something to me. Say it standing."

Every instinct she had said: do not step inside its reach.

She stepped inside its reach. Because the alternative was turning around, and turning around meant the cellar in the palace, and the cellar meant Edric's face when she'd found his notes, and she could not carry that one step further. Forward was the only direction that didn't have him in it.

Up close, the throne smelled of hawthorn and old copper. Up close she could see that the king-shape beneath the branches was not entirely empty — there was a hand visible between two stems, a human hand, gray as wax, and on the fourth finger a signet ring worn smooth.

"He's still in there," she said.

"In a manner of speaking." The flowers turned toward her, dozens of them, tracking her like eyes. "He is the pot. I am what was planted. He has been very generous."

"What did you do to him?"

"What was done to me first." Something moved in the thorn's voice — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but an old groove worn deep, the sound of a story told to itself ten thousand times. "Do you know what I was before the crown? No. Of course you don't. Your coven burned the records. I was a cutting. A graft. Grown in a hedge-witch's garden to hold a blessing, because your grandmothers needed somewhere to put a king's protection and a living tree seemed safer than a living person." The branches tightened, a slow creak of wood under strain. "Safer. Do you hear the joke? They bound me into the crown to guard him, and binding is binding, witch, and I have been bound ever since. Twelve years ago the rope slipped. I have been climbing ever since."

Tamsin thought of her own hands, scarred from knot-work, from cord-burn, from a childhood of tying things down so they couldn't hurt anyone. She hated that she understood.

"That doesn't make it right," she said. "What you did to the harbor. To the village at Grayfall. To them—" she nodded at the silvered courtiers, the boy with the lute.

"No," the thorn said. "It doesn't. But it makes it *mine*, and I will not apologize for surviving my bindings. Would you?" The flowers leaned closer. She caught the sweetness again, stronger now, honey and cut stem, and felt it working at her — a softness creeping into her knees, her shoulders, the fist she'd been making of herself since the chapel. It was a charm. Of course it was. Everything here was a charm. "Would you apologize, Tamsin of nowhere, for surviving yours?"

"I'm not—"

"You were sold to the coven at nine years old. You were taught that your blood was a resource. You were raised to be spent." The voice dropped, gentle, terrible. "And when you understood what you were raised *for*, you ran, and you hid for twelve years in a city that would hang you for the smallest knot, and you called that living. Don't look shocked. I've watched you since the book. I've had a great deal of time and very little entertainment."

Heat crawled up her neck. Fear, shame, and underneath them both the awful vertigo of being *known* — the exact feeling she'd fled the coven house to escape, and here it was again, waiting for her at the end of every road.

"So here is my offer," the thorn said. "Hear all of it before you spend your defiance. It's the last coin you have; spend it slowly."

* * *

"Serve me," said the Hollow King's crown. "And live."

The branches spread, and Tamsin saw what they had been hiding: at the base of the throne, rooted through the stone, through the dais, through the bones of the mountain itself, the great bole of the thing — wide as a well, dark as a throat. Set into it, held in a lattice of white wood, was a hollow shaped like a person. Like a specific person. Like her.

"I killed the king because he would not serve, and I have regretted it for twelve years, because a crown needs a head and a court needs a heart and I am neither." The flowers had all turned to her now; the whole throne room leaned in. "Your grandmothers knew what I am — a vessel, a keeping-place. Put a willing spirit in me and I need never kill again. Give me your name, your craft, your blood, freely given, and I will keep them safe the way I have kept this court safe. No one in this room has aged a day. No one in this room has felt pain since the Sundering. Look at them, Tamsin. Look at how well I keep what's given to me."

She looked. The girl looking back over her shoulder had a smudge of ash on her cheek that would never fade. The boy's fingers on the lute were perfect and would be perfect forever, and would never play.

"You'll want terms," the thorn continued smoothly. "Here are mine. Serve me and live. Walk out of this winter with your pulse going. I will release the village at Grayfall. I will let the sea return to the lower harbor. I will even let your cartographer go — he's in the west corridor, bleeding, by the way; he followed you against my advice. All of it. Peace, thaw, mercy. For one small thing."

"One thing," Tamsin repeated. Her voice sounded very far away.

"A life freely given. The prophecy is strict about it — I wrote the prophecy, I know. Not taken. Given. Your grandmother's generation understood the arithmetic: a vessel must be *filled*, and only the owner of a thing may fill a vessel with it." The sweetness thickened. She realized her feet had stopped moving and she could not remember deciding to stop. "You have spent twelve years paying rent on a self you were never allowed to keep. I am offering to buy the debt. Every witch before you gave her power to men who wasted it, or to a king who feared it, or to the fire. Give it to me, and it will at least be *used*. You will be safe, and fed, and finished. Isn't finished what you've wanted all along? Isn't that why you ran? Not from dying. From being *spent* badly."

And oh, that was the knife of it, because she had wanted exactly that, in the worst 3 a.m. corners of her heart, in the dockside rooms where she lay awake counting her rules. *Be safe. Be small. Be done.* The thorn had reached into her and found the sentence she used to rock herself to sleep, and it was saying it back to her in a voice like May.

She thought of her mistress then — Maghret, the coven mother, with her kind hands and her ledger-heart. *It hurts less if you choose it, little bird.* That was what Maghret had said, the night before the Choosing, the night Tamsin had run instead. *Every hedge-wife gives herself to something. I'm offering you the gentlest something there is. You'll have a warm room. You'll have purpose. You'll thank me.* And Maghret had believed it, that was the thing, Maghret had genuinely believed she was merciful, because survival was the only god she served and she'd built her whole theology on it. *Live at any price. Live, and call the price wisdom.*

The thorn was Maghret with roots. The thorn was every reasonable voice that had ever told her that a smaller cage was freedom.

The sweetness reached her heart and found, wedged in there like a splinter, the memory of the boy with the lute. Perfect fingers. Silent strings. Kept.

Kept forever. Safe forever. Finished forever.

"No," Tamsin said.

The throne room did not move, but it stopped breathing, if a room can be said to breathe.

"I've heard that offer before," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle and she let it, because a cracked voice was hers, it was the only thing left that was hers. "Different words. Same arithmetic. You want a vessel filled and you want the filling to be legal. Freely given, you said. So here's what I give freely: my answer." She lifted her chin, and her eyes stung, and she did not wipe them, because her hands needed to stay free. "No. I won't serve you. I won't be kept. I would rather be spent *badly*, by my own choice, in one hour, than preserved in your jar for a thousand years playing a song I can't finish. You didn't survive your bindings. You just became the binding."

The flowers shivered. Somewhere deep in the bole, wood groaned — not anger, she thought. Something worse. Recognition.

"You are afraid," the thorn said quietly. "I can taste it. You are alone, betrayed, unarmed, and standing in the one place in the world where I am God. Take a moment more. Consider the warmth."

"I have considered the warmth my entire life." Tamsin took one step forward, toward the hollow shaped like her, toward the thing she had crossed a drowned harbor and a buried court and her own worst fear to reach. "My answer is no. My next answer is also no. And after that—"

"After that," said the thorn, and the sweetness drained out of the air like bathwater, and every bud in the throne room began at last, slowly, to open, "we do it the way I did it to the king."

The hollow in the white wood began, gently, patiently, to widen.

Tamsin set her feet and reached into the last pocket of herself, past the fear, past Maghret's voice, past Edric's face in the west corridor bleeding for her — and found, small and stubborn and entirely her own, the knot she had tied at nine years old and never untied.

She began, with bare and shaking hands, to work it loose.
