## 18. The Unbinding

The throne room had no doors left. The thorn had eaten them years ago, grown its black branches through the oak and the iron and the hinges until doorway and wall were one gnarled thing, and Tamsin stepped through a gap it opened for her like a mouth.

Inside, the cold was different. Not weather. *Intention.*

The Hollow King stood at the center of his ruined court, and up close — finally, horribly close — she could see what she'd only glimpsed from walls and rooftops. He was not walking. He was *being worn*. The thorn ran through him like thread through cloth, roots sunk into the backs of his hands, into his spine, up through the ruin of his throat, and where they entered, his skin had gone gray and papery as old birch bark. The crown sat fused to his skull, black branches woven into bone. His eyes were open. They were the worst part. They were open, and aware, and twelve years deep in a scream he could no longer make.

Behind her, Edric's boots scraped stone. He didn't come closer. She'd told him not to.

"The wards," she said, not turning around. "Now, Edric."

She heard him kneel at the threshold, heard chalk on stone, the scratch of his stylus tracing the lines he'd spent three days mapping in the under-harbor — the old royal wards, the ones he himself had helped design, inverted now, turned from cage to keyhole. Light bled up through the floor seams, thin and gold and trembling.

"It's holding," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Tamsin —"

"Don't." She kept her eyes on the king. "If you say it, I'll think about it, and if I think about it I'll run. Just hold the door."

A long silence. Then, quietly: "I'm holding the door."

The thorn moved before she did.

It came out of the king like a wave off black water — branches peeling away from his body, the whole crown-shape of it rising, unfolding, unfurling across the ceiling of the throne room until the vaulted stone disappeared beneath it and there was only thorn, acres of it, a sky of thorns, and every branch was reaching down toward her with the slow patience of something that had waited a hundred years for this exact moment.

*Hedge-blood,* said a voice that wasn't a voice. It came through her bones, through her teeth, through the scar on her palm where she'd cut herself on the prophecy book's page. *We smelled you coming across the drowned harbor. Little witch. Little last daughter of the crowning.*

"I know what you are," Tamsin said. Her breath fogged and froze midair and drifted down like ash. "You're the bargain my mistress made. You're the survival that eats everything it survives."

*We are winter,* the thorn said, almost gently. *We are what keeps. He was dying. We stopped it. He has not aged a day in a century. Is that cruelty?*

"He's screaming."

*He is preserved.*

"He's SCREAMING." And her voice cracked across the throne room and came back at her off the ice-sheathed walls, and somewhere in the echo of it she heard every villager in Saint Aldhelm's Reach saying their prayers to their frozen saint, and every dockworker in Saltmere chafing their blue hands, and she thought: *twelve years. Twelve winters. All of it this. All of it him.*

The thorn's branches descended around her, slow as a closing fist, and stopped an inch from her skin. Waiting.

*We offer,* it said. *Serve us, and live. Wear the crown when he is done. Rule the long quiet. No more running, little witch. No more hiding your gift behind luck-knots in outlaw cities. You would never hide again.*

And God help her, some part of her wanted it. Some tired, twelve-years-tired part of her heard *no more running* and wept. That part sounded like Mistress Vael. That part said *the coven survives, whatever it costs; you survive, whatever it costs.* Survival at any price. Survival as the only virtue. She'd fled that logic once, across half a kingdom, with nothing but a false name and a bag of harmless trinkets.

"No," Tamsin said.

The branches shivered.

"But here's what I'll give you instead." She pulled the knife from her belt — her hedge-knife, bone-handled, older than the law, the one thing she'd carried out of the coven. "Not service. Not a crown. A trade. Your roots are in him because someone bound them there, and binding is just magic that forgot how to let go. I'm the blood that crowned him. I can unbind you. But unbinding wants a price, and you want a life, so take mine — take what I am — and let him go."

*Your life?*

"My name," Tamsin said. "And my gift. Everything I am. Every charm I ever knotted, every word I ever whispered over salt and thread. Take all of it, in his place, freely given — and then get out of him."

The thorn went very still. Stillness, she realized, was how it listened. A century of listening to a man who could no longer speak, and stillness was its whole language now.

*Freely given cannot be taken back.*

"I know."

*You will be nothing. You will walk out of this palace hollow as he walks now.*

"Then we'll match," Tamsin said, and laughed, and hated the sound of it, because it came out wet. "One hollow thing less in the world. That's still a profit."

Behind her, Edric made a small broken noise. She didn't turn around. If she turned around she'd see his face and his face would be the hardest thing in the room, harder than the thorn, and she needed her hands steady.

She pressed the knife into her left palm and dragged it deep, and held the bleeding hand out flat.

"In the name of the nine hedges and the tenth that burned," she said, and the words rose in her like water finding an old channel, words her mistress had taught her and sworn her never to speak, the crowning-words, the first-words, the ones the hedge-witches had used a century ago to tie a dying king to a living thorn. "I was the knot. I am the loosening. What was bound by my blood is freed by my blood. Take me instead. Take *me* instead."

She spoke her name.

Her true name — the one her mother gave her, the one no law had touched, the one she'd buried under Tamsin-of-no-surname and twelve years of aliases. She spoke it once, aloud, into the frozen air of a dead court, and the moment it left her lips the thorn took it.

There was no other way to describe what happened. It *took* it. The name ripped out of her the way a hook comes out of flesh, and she felt the shape of herself tear along a seam she hadn't known was there, and then the thorn's branches closed around her hand like a lover's fingers and began to drink.

Magic first. That was the strange mercy of it — the magic went easy, went *gladly*, pouring out of her like warmth from an open window in winter, and she felt each craft leave her one by one. The knot-work went, and with it the memory of her grandmother's hands guiding hers. The salt-charms went, and with them the smell of the sea on a summer morning before the Sundering. The healing words went, and the warding words, and the little trick for calming frightened animals, and the trick for finding north without a compass, and she clung to none of them because clinging was taking back and taking back was death, and she had promised. Freely given.

Then the name went, all of it, every name she had ever been, and the world tilted sideways because a person is mostly name, she understood that now, a person is a story told by everyone who ever said *you*, and hers was being read aloud by something else, in a dead king's court, at the bottom of the winter.

She fell. The thorn caught her — held her up by the wrist like a doll, drinking, drinking — and through the gray rushing in her ears she heard the king.

Not a scream. A voice. For the first time in a hundred years, a *voice*, ragged and human and disbelieving:

"—cold," the king was saying. "Oh. Oh, it's *cold.* It's been so long since I felt — who — girl, girl, what have you —"

"Dying," Tamsin tried to say. "You're dying. It's all right. Dying is allowed now."

The thorn convulsed. She felt it fight — felt the whole sky of branches above her shudder and strain against the loosening, roots tearing backward out of the king's spine, out of his hands, out of his throat, dragging against a century of habit. The wards flared gold behind her; Edric was shouting something, holding them open with both hands and his whole ruined conscience, and the light poured through the room like dawn through water.

The crown cracked.

Black wood split from black bone, and the king came apart from the thorn the way a splinter leaves a wound — slowly, terribly, with blood. Real blood, red and shocking after a hundred years of gray. He collapsed onto the steps of his own throne and lay there breathing, human, mortal, free, and Tamsin watched him from the floor where she'd landed and thought: *that's the whole job. That's everything. One old man gets to die as himself.*

The king looked at her. His eyes were ordinary brown eyes, filmed and failing, and they were the most beautiful things she had ever seen.

"Thank you," he said. Or maybe he only mouthed it. Sound was going strange now, coming to her down a long tunnel, because the thorn was finishing its meal, taking the last of her, the dregs, the residue, the final warm coin of what she'd carried into this room.

*Little witch,* it said, one last time, from everywhere. *You gave freely. So we keep faith. Not your life. Only your name. Only your fire. Go and be —*

It paused. Searching. The way she'd once searched a prophecy book for a description of herself.

*— go and be whoever is left.*

And the winter broke.

Not outside — inside. In her chest, where her magic had lived for thirty-one years, there was now a room with the window open and no fire in the hearth, and wind moving through it, and nothing else. She put her hand flat against the stone floor and felt nothing answer. Called inward, the way she'd called ten thousand times, and heard no one home.

Empty. Alive. Empty.

Somewhere above her the thorn-branches were falling, dropping from the ceiling like a forest giving up, turning gray, crumbling to ash before they hit the ground. Somewhere behind her Edric was running toward her, shouting her —

Shouting nothing. Shouting the space where her name should be, and faltering, because there was no hook in the air anymore for a name to catch on. She saw him stop three feet away, see him understand, watch his face do something complicated and terrible.

"Tamsin," he said anyway. Testing it. And it slid off her like rain off glass. Nothing. A word other people used.

She looked past him, at the king on the steps. He was still breathing. Slower now. Each breath a little further apart, and peaceful, so peaceful, a man finally permitted to finish.

"Stay with him," Tamsin said. Her own voice sounded foreign. Unclaimed. "Someone should be holding his hand when it ends. He waited a hundred years for it to be his own."

Edric knelt beside the dying king and took his hand, and Tamsin got up, swaying, and walked toward the gap in the thorn-eaten wall where the doors used to be, because outside, far away, beyond the frozen miles and the drowned harbor and the outlaw city built on a lie about safety —

Outside, the ice was starting to sing.

* * *

She was halfway down the great stair when the first crack came, deep under the palace, under the hill, under the whole dead capital — a sound like the spine of the world adjusting after a long sleep. Dust sifted from the vaulted stone. The frozen fountains in the courtyard below shivered.

And far to the west, past the ruins, past the snowbound villages, past everything — she felt, or imagined she felt, or *hoped* — the Sundered Sea beginning to remember which way home was.
