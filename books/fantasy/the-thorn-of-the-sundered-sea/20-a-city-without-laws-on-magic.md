## 20. A City Without Laws on Magic

The ice broke on a Tuesday.

Tamsin knew because Tuesdays were market days, and the whole of Saltmere had come down to the harbor to watch the Sundered Sea come home. She stood at the edge of the crowd with her hands in her pockets — her empty, ordinary, human hands — and listened to the sound the water made when it remembered itself.

It was not gentle. It was not beautiful, not at first. It was a groan like something vast rolling over in its sleep, and then a crack that ran the length of the frozen strait, and then the sea came through the gap like a herd of animals escaping a burning barn. Grey-green water, thick with broken floes, surging into the harbor mouth where no water had moved in twelve years. It hit the pilings of the old fish-market and spray went up thirty feet, and the crowd made a sound Tamsin had never heard from a Saltmere crowd before — not fear, not the careful flat silence they'd all learned to wear like a coat. It was a roar. Half of it was cheering. Some of it, she thought, was weeping, though nobody would have admitted it.

She didn't cheer. She watched the water take back its harbor and felt the grief move through her like weather, slow and total, and she let it, because she'd learned on the long walk home that fighting it only made it last longer.

*You gave it away,* said the voice in her head that sounded like her old mistress, Maren Crowe, except Maren was three hundred miles south and probably dead or nearly. *You gave away the one thing that was ever truly yours.*

*I gave away a king's life,* she answered it silently. *I gave away a winter. Twelve years of children who never saw spring.* She watched an old man at the water's edge go down on his knees in the slush, laughing, letting the freezing spray soak him through. *I'd do it again.*

*Of course you would,* said the voice. *That's what makes it so wasteful.*

The harbor filled. The Sundered Sea — which for a century had been a name for a wound, a scar on every map Edric Vale had ever drawn — became simply a sea again, moving and alive and indifferent to everything it had missed. Boats that had been frozen into the mud for twelve years lifted on the new tide, groaning, some of them settling and some of them actually floating, and men waded out to them in the shallows like people greeting relatives risen from the dead.

By noon the word had spread through the city faster than any crier could have carried it: the wards were failing.

* * *

Tamsin had felt the outlawry collapse the way you feel a tooth stop aching — suddenly aware of a pressure she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was there.

For twelve years the law had sat over Saltmere like a lid. No craft, no charms, no working of any kind. First offense, confiscation and the stocks. Second, the cutting — a brand on the palm that scarred the nerve-ends and made it physically hard to hold a charm-thread steady ever again. Third offense, they walked you out past the tide-line and you didn't walk back. Everyone knew someone it had happened to. Everyone had learned to be small about the things they could do.

And now, walking home from the harbor through the Warrens, she could feel the lid lifting. Not because anyone had announced it. Because the air itself had changed. Magic had been leaking back into the world since the Hollow King died — she'd felt it on the road, faint as warmth through a wall — but here in the city it came off the streets in waves. Old ward-stones in doorways that had been dead for a century were humming. A gutter-witch's chalk mark on a corner, faded almost to nothing, flickered green when Tamsin passed it. The well at Cooper's Lane, which had tasted of iron for as long as she'd lived in Saltmere, ran sweet.

In the square outside the shambles, a woman stood on a crate doing card-readings openly. Not pretending to tell fortunes with a patter and a wink, the way half the "seers" in Saltmere always had, hedging against the law by being frauds. Actually reading. Tamsin stopped at the edge of the little crowd and watched the cards turn, and saw the woman's fingers move between each draw — a tiny gesture, thumb brushing the pad of each fingertip in sequence, the oldest trick there was, the first thing every hedge-child learned. *Ground the hand, quiet the mind, let the pattern speak.*

The woman was crying while she did it. Nobody in the crowd minded. They were too busy leaning in.

Tamsin turned away before the woman could catch her eye, because she knew what her own face was doing, and she didn't want it witnessed.

* * *

Edric found her that evening at the lodging house on Tanner's Row, sitting in the window of the common room with a cup of tea going cold beside her. He had a way of entering rooms like a man checking whether the floor would hold him — a habit, she supposed, of a life spent mapping coastlines that might be wrong. He set his satchel down, looked at her face, and asked nothing, which was why she could bear him.

"The Council met today," he said, after a while. "Emergency session. I heard it secondhand from a harbormaster who owes me money."

"And?"

"They're calling it the Thaw Edict. Provisional. All convictions under the magic statutes suspended pending review." He said it carefully, the way he said everything, like words were coordinates he had to plot exactly. "They haven't repealed the laws yet. But the stocks are empty. The wardens took down the notice boards this afternoon."

"Pending review," Tamsin repeated.

"There will be arguments. There's a century of law built on top of the ban, and half the Council built their careers enforcing it. It won't be clean, and it won't be quick." He paused. "But it's over, Tamsin. Whatever they argue about, they're arguing about the shape of the ending, not whether it comes. The winter broke. The king is dead and human and buried under a cairn of his own choosing. There's nothing left for the ban to be afraid of."

Except me, she thought. Except everyone like me, coming up out of cellars and back rooms all over this city, blinking in the light, showing hands that haven't worked openly in generations.

She said none of it. She drank the cold tea instead, and made a face at it, and Edric almost smiled.

"I'm going north," he said, when the silence had gone on long enough. "To the archive at Greywater. The survey records from before the Sundering — if the harbor charts survived, I can begin restoring the maps. Properly this time. With the sea on them."

"You don't have to justify leaving to me."

"No," he agreed. "But I wanted to say it aloud to someone." Another pause, and she braced herself, because she knew that particular quality of silence too. "What I did—"

"Don't." She said it without heat. "We settled it. You held the wards open. You carried me out. Whatever else is true about you, those things are also true, and I've decided they weigh more."

"They don't."

"No," she said. "But I get to decide how to count. That's mine now. One of the few things that is." She looked at him then, and let him see that she meant it as absolution and not as comfort, because he'd earned the harder gift. "Go north, Edric. Draw your maps. Write to me."

He nodded once, shouldered his satchel, and paused at the door. "The tea's better at Marlowe's, by the fish-gate. If you're still here when I come back."

"If I'm still here," she said, and didn't look at him until he'd gone, because she wasn't sure what her face would do with that either.

* * *

Spring came to Saltmere the way floodwater comes to a dry riverbed — finding every channel at once.

Within a fortnight the change was impossible to ignore. Green things pushed up through the cracks in the flagstones that nobody remembered planting; the seed had waited a century under stone, patient as anything with roots. The salt-stunted trees along the promenade budded so fast it was almost obscene, whole branches going from grey to green in a week. Children who had never seen rain — real rain, warm rain — stood out in the first storm with their faces up and their mouths open, and their mothers let them, standing in doorways with their arms folded and their own eyes wet.

And everywhere, magic, coming up like the green.

Tamsin couldn't walk two streets without seeing it. An old man re-liming the ward-marks on his doorstep, whistling while he worked, no longer glancing over his shoulder. Two sisters in the Warrens selling charm-braids off a blanket in full view of the street — luck-knots, weather-cords, the same stock-in-trade Tamsin had spent twelve years hiding inside honest trinkets, except now they shouted their prices across the lane and laughed. A boy no older than ten standing on the sea-wall making the gulls wheel with a whistle that had a thread of craft in it, pure showing-off, and no warden in sight, and the gulls wheeling, and the boy lit up like a lamp.

That was the one that undid her, in the end. Not the grand things. The showing-off.

She stood watching the boy for a long time, feeling something tear slowly in her chest, and finally identified it, because she was honest with herself even when it hurt: it was envy. Clean, ordinary, ugly envy. Not for power — God knew she'd spent twelve years trying not to want it, running from the women who'd wanted it *for* her. For the freedom. For a world where the thing you could do was just a thing you could do, like whittling or singing, and you could do it badly on a seawall in front of everyone and nobody would come for you in the night.

She had bought that world. She had paid its price with the only coin she had. And she would never once stand in it.

*You can't spend your life mourning a thing you never wanted,* she told herself on the walk home.

*Can't I?* said the Maren-voice. *It was yours. Inherited, unwanted, yes — but yours. You were the last of a line that crowned kings and sang storms quiet, and you gave it up for a stranger and a city that spent a century hunting your kind. And now the city dances, and you stand in the cold with empty hands.*

"It was never a dance I was invited to," she said out loud, and an old woman passing gave her a wide berth, and Tamsin realized she'd spoken aloud, and laughed — a short, cracked sound — and kept walking.

* * *

She tried to be useful instead. It was the only medicine she trusted.

The thaw had left damage. Cellars flooded, foundations shifted where the permafrost had held buildings up for a decade and then quietly let go, and half the Warrens smelled of rot and wet plaster. Tamsin, who knew how to read a building's bones — a hedge-craft skill, though it needed no magic, only patience and a good ear — went door to door with a mason's level she'd borrowed and told people which walls could be saved. Word got around. By the third week she had more work than she could do and had to start turning people away, which was a strange, painful kind of wealth.

People brought her things, too. That was harder. They'd heard — the story had traveled ahead of her on the road home, growing in the telling — about the witch who unmade the Hollow King. They came to her door with heirloom charms gone dark, asking if she could wake them. With sick children and bad wells and cows that wouldn't calve, asking with that specific hopeful dread people wore when they feared the answer was *no*.

The answer was no. Every time. She'd put her hand on a luck-knot a fisherman's widow pressed into her palms and felt — nothing. Not emptiness exactly. Emptiness implied a container, and the container was gone too. It was like pressing her hand against a wall where a door had once been and finding seamless plaster. She hadn't even known the door was a door until she'd walked through it and it closed behind her.

"I'm sorry," she said, every time. "I can't. What I had — I spent it. All of it."

Most of them understood. Some of them looked at her with a pity that was worse than anger, because it confirmed the thing she was trying not to know. And one woman — young, hollow-eyed, a charm-braid clutched in both fists like a rope over a drop — had grabbed Tamsin's wrist and said, "But you *had* it. You could have taught me. Before. Why does everyone give it up or lose it or hide it? Why is there never anyone left to *teach*?"

Tamsin had no answer that wasn't a wound, so she'd given the woman bread and the address of the mason who owed her a favor, and lain awake most of that night listening to the harbor working in the dark, full of water and ships and life, and grieving.

* * *

The letter came at the end of the month, and it found her the way such things always find you: on an ordinary afternoon, when she was elbow-deep in someone's flooded cellar, hauling sodden wool out of a chest.

A child brought it — one of the mason's daughters, barefoot despite the chill, because children everywhere had decided shoes were optional now that grass existed. "Woman at the gate said give it to the witch on Tanner's Row," the girl reported. "I said you weren't a witch anymore, and she laughed." The girl frowned. "She laughed like she knew something. She had a crow on her shoulder that talked."

Tamsin dried her hands very thoroughly on her apron, which was already filthy, and took the letter.

The paper was good — heavy rag stock, the kind that cost money — and the seal was plain grey wax, unmarked, which told her more than any crest could have. Only one kind of person used blank seals: people who didn't want to be found by anyone who wasn't meant to find them.

She opened it by the window, in the light.

*Sister,* it began, and her stomach dropped, because no one had called her that in twelve years and the word fit her skin like a key fits a lock she'd changed on purpose.

*You will have heard by now that the thaw has reached the low country, and that the old laws are falling like rotten teeth. You will also have heard — we hear everything; we heard of you long before the stories reached the cities — what it cost you. We grieve it. We grieve you, though you live, for we know what it is to survive a loss of that shape.*

*The coven is not what it was. The Sundering scattered us, and the years of hiding thinned us further, and Maren Crowe is dead — died angry, if it matters, which perhaps it will to you. But seven of us remain, gathered at the mother-house in the hills above Greywater, and the old blood runs true in the line still, in daughters and sons who never knew what they carried.*

*Here is what we know that you may not: the giving-up is not always the ending. The crown was made by hands, sister, and what hands make, hands may remake. The thorn took your gift — but gifts given freely leave doors as well as close them. Come to us. Let us read what was written in you when the thorn drank. Let us see if the door opens from the other side.*

*Come home. Or, if you cannot call it home, come anyway. Spring is short in the hills, and we would not have you spend it alone.*

*There is no price on this invitation. We know you will not believe that yet. Come and disbelieve us in person.*

*It was signed with a single mark: a circle of thorns, open at the top.*

Tamsin read it three times. Then she sat down on the windowsill, slowly, because her legs had opinions about standing.

*The giving-up is not always the ending.*

She thought of the seamless wall where the door had been. She thought of the boy on the seawall, and the sisters selling charm-braids in the open air, and the widow whose luck-knot she couldn't wake. She thought of Maren Crowe saying, twelve years ago, with perfect loving certainty: *the blood demands its due, child, and the blood knows best* — and she thought of the seven strangers in the hills offering her, free of charge, the exact same inheritance she had bled out to escape.

*There is no price on this invitation. We know you will not believe that yet.*

"Damn right I don't," she said to the empty room.

But her hands — her empty, ordinary, human hands — had gone still around the letter, and would not stop trembling, and would not, though she ordered them to, let go of it.

Outside, the harbor glittered, full of returning water, and somewhere in the hills to the north seven people who shared her blood were waiting to ask her the question she had crossed a kingdom and surrendered her soul to avoid:

*Who are you, when the inheritance comes knocking again?*
