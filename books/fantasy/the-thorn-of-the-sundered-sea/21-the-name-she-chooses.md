## 21. The Name She Chooses

They came to Saltmere in the third week of spring, when the harbor was finally blue again and the fishmongers had stopped flinching at the sound of cracking ice.

Tamsin saw them before they saw her — three women walking the length of the Strand with the particular gait of people who had crossed a long distance on foot and were too proud to show it. Grey cloaks, travel-worn boots, and at the throat of each one a knot of braided cord that Tamsin recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror. Hedge-cord. The old pattern, the one Maren had tied around her own wrist the day she'd first taken Tamsin's hands in hers and said, *You have it, girl. You have the blood.*

She was minding the stall — her stall, hers and Edric's, wedged between a rope-seller and a woman who repaired nets and complained about both of them — and for one stupid moment she considered packing up the trinkets and walking away. Twelve years of practice at disappearing. Her feet actually shifted.

Then she thought of the cellar under the palace, and the dark, and how she had promised herself, gasping on frozen stone with the thorn's roots still twitching somewhere behind her, that she would never run from anything again.

So she stayed, and arranged her luck-knots, and waited.

The eldest of the three reached the stall first. She was perhaps sixty, silver-streaked and sun-brown, with hands that had clearly worked — real work, soil and salt and thread. Her eyes went over the table: the carved birds, the knotted cords, Edric's maps pinned along the back rail like washing on a line. Then they settled on Tamsin's face, and something moved through them like weather.

"You're the one," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm the one who sells charms for the lawful," Tamsin said mildly. "If you're wanting luck for a journey, I've got knots. If you're wanting maps, my partner draws them. If you're wanting anything else—"

"We know what you did." The youngest of the three spoke up, sharp-voiced, maybe twenty-five, with the same grey cloak and the same cord at her throat but none of the elder's patience. "The winter broke. The King died free. Everyone's telling it different, but we know."

"Everyone tells it different," Tamsin agreed. "That's usually a sign nobody was there."

"We were there," the elder said quietly. "At the end. Far enough away to survive it, close enough to feel it go." She laid her hand flat on the table, beside a carved wren, and Tamsin saw the cord around her wrist had been retied many times, frayed and renewed, frayed and renewed. "I am Sefryn. This is Olla and this is Bright. We are what remains of the covens of the northern coast. What remains of the hedge-witches who crowned the Hollow King, a hundred years ago, and who have been paying for it ever since."

The word landed in Tamsin's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it stopped.

"You're a long way from the coast," she said.

"We came for you." Sefryn's voice was steady, unhurried, the voice of someone delivering news they had rehearsed on a long road. "We felt the unbinding. Every witch alive felt it — a name given up whole, freely, into the roots. Do you understand what that means? There has not been a giving like that since the first crown. The thorn took your magic, yes. But magic remembers where it lived. And so do we."

* * *

They did not talk business at the stall. Sefryn had that much sense, at least. They walked — up off the Strand, past the fish market where the ice-houses stood empty and rusting, up the crooked lanes toward the high terraces where the thaw ran loud in the gutters. Tamsin left a sign on the table for Edric — the little carved gull turned beak-down, their old code for *gone out, mind the shop* — and hated herself a little for how automatic the gesture was, how domestic, how much she did not want to lose it.

Olla walked close to her the whole way, close enough to be rude, studying her like a specimen. Finally she said: "You don't look empty."

"I look tired," Tamsin said. "There's a difference."

"Do you dream?" Olla pressed. "Since it happened. Do you dream of the roots?"

"I dream of the harbor," Tamsin said. "Blue water. Boats. Ordinary things." She let that sit, then added, because cruelty was sometimes just honesty wearing its good coat: "I don't miss it, if that's what you're asking. The magic. I expected to miss it more."

That got her a look from all three of them — Olla's offended, Bright's curious, Sefryn's unreadable as tide-flat.

They ended up in the small square above the terraces, where a fountain had run dry all through the outlawry years and now, absurdly, worked again, throwing up cold spray that the wind kept shredding. Children were daring each other to touch it. Tamsin watched them out of habit — watching children near water had been drilled into her by a city that feared what its witches might drown — and then made herself stop. The laws were dead. The laws had died with the winter. Anyone could see the wards unraveling week by week, the old prohibitions going soft and unenforced, magistrates shrugging, hedge-witches already selling openly in two of the lower markets. A city without laws on magic. Edric said it would take a decade to sort out what replaced them. Tamsin thought it would take longer, and that the sorting would be ugly, and that she was glad, for once, not to be part of the argument.

Sefryn sat on the fountain's rim and gestured for Tamsin to do the same. Olla and Bright hung back, arms folded, a jury in miniature.

"You gave everything," Sefron began — *Sefryn*, Tamsin corrected herself silently; names mattered, she knew better than anyone — "to break the thorn. Your craft, your inheritance, twelve years of hiding and running and starving quietly in an outlaw city. And the covens know it. The covens honor it." She paused. "But the covens also lost things. When the Sundering came, when the king died the first time and the thorn put on his crown, the hedge-witches scattered. Our lineages broke. Our rites went underground. And now—" she spread her hands, "—now the winter is broken, the law is dying, and the old blood is thin. There are fewer than forty of us left, from north coast to south. Forty. And the strongest line of all — the line that made the crown, the line the thorn itself marked — stands here in front of me with empty hands."

Tamsin said nothing. She was already ahead of the woman. She had been ahead of her since the fountain, maybe since the Strand. But she made herself listen to all of it, because she owed the dead that much, and because she wanted to hear exactly how it would be dressed.

"There is a rite," Sefryn said. "Older than the outlawry. Older than the Sundering. The Rite of Return. It cannot give back what the thorn took — nothing can, that gift is spent, it burned in the roots and we do not mourn it, it bought a king's freedom and a sea's return. But the blood remembers. The blood carries patterns deeper than any single witch's power. Given the right hands, the right vessels, the right nights—" her voice dropped into the cadence Tamsin knew intimately, the liturgical murmur every hedge-mistress used when the price was coming, "—the line could be restored in you. Not what you had. Something older. Something greater. You would be the first true hedge-witch in a century, Tamsin Reyes. The covens would kneel to you. The cities would come to *you*, once the laws finish falling. You could teach. You could lead. You could make sure no child ever again has to hide her gifts in a cellar and sell luck-knots to dockworkers with her heart in her mouth."

It was a beautiful speech. Tamsin sat very still and admired its craftsmanship the way Edric admired an old map — noting the skill, noting the care, noting every place where the cartographer had bent the coastline to flatter the patron.

"And the price," she said.

Sefryn didn't flinch. Credit where due. "You know there is a price."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear it in your mouth, not mine."

"The Rite requires a vessel freely given," Sefryn said. "A life offered to the line, to hold the restored pattern while it takes root. Not taken — never taken, that is the old corruption, that is what the thorn was. Given. Willingly. By someone whose blood is strong enough to anchor it." She met Tamsin's eyes. "You know whose blood that is. It has always been yours. It was yours when Maren chose you. It was yours when you ran. It is yours now, empty hands and all."

The square was very quiet. The fountain threw its spray. Somewhere below, a ship's bell rang the hour, and the sound came up clean across the water, unhindered by ice for the first time in twelve years.

"A vessel," Tamsin said. "A person. Someone with the blood, who gives themselves — how completely? Walks into the rite and doesn't walk out? Or walks out hollowed, like I walked out of the palace?"

"It varies," Sefryn said carefully. "With the strength of the pattern. With the depth of the restoration. With—"

"With how much you need," Tamsin said. "That's what Maren called it. Need. She dressed it up as duty, and lineage, and the survival of the craft, and when I asked her straight out — I was sixteen, and I did ask her straight out, once, the only time I ever dared — she told me the vessel's own life was *secondary*. That the craft was bigger than any one witch. That a strong vessel should be honored to serve." She looked at Olla, young and fierce and grey-cloaked, and felt something twist in her chest that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite grief. "Is that what they teach you? That some lives are vessels?"

"No," Olla said — too fast, Tamsin thought.

"Yes," Bright said, at the same moment, and then looked stricken.

Sefryn closed her eyes briefly. "The rite is not the thorn," she said. "We do not devour our own. The vessel is honored. Remembered. Named in every coven's roll until the roll itself crumbles. It is sacrifice, freely chosen, and it saves forty lines of descent from dying out in a single generation. You of all people know what freely chosen sacrifice can buy. You *made* that bargain yourself, six months ago, on the throne room floor."

And there it was. The hook, set neatly, in the exact wound.

Tamsin looked at her own hands, resting open on her knees. Empty hands. She had been looking at them a great deal lately — in the mornings, over the wash basin; at night, by lamplight, turning them palm-up and palm-down like a woman reading a fortune that always came out the same. They were good hands. Scarred from knot-work and knife-work and one bad winter with frostbite nipping two fingers. They tied a beautiful luck-knot. They could gut a fish in four seconds. Last month they had pulled a drowning boy out of the harbor shallows without a single spark of magic, just reach and grip and stubbornness, and his mother had cried on her shoulder and Tamsin had gone home soaked and shaking and happier than she had any right to be.

Six months ago she had given her name and her magic to a thorn-thing in a dead king's crown, and it had been the hardest thing she had ever done, and she did not regret it, and she never would. But she knew — she knew in her bones, in the marrow the thorn had drunk dry — the difference between that night and this fountain. On the throne room floor, the price had been hers to pay or everyone lost: the king, the sea, the city, the world going grey forever. There had been no other door. She had looked for one. Edric had looked for one. There hadn't been one.

Here, in the spring sun, with boats on the water and children in the fountain spray, there were other doors. There were dozens. The covens could scatter wider, marry out, write their patterns down in books instead of blood, do what every living tradition does eventually or die — adapt. They wanted a shortcut. They wanted the old strength back in one generation instead of five, and they wanted it paid for in the currency they had always paid it in, because that was the currency they had.

Because that was the currency *Maren* had taught them to use.

"I'm going to tell you about Maren," Tamsin said.

* * *

"She found me when I was eleven. Beggar's kid, harbor-rat, no family worth the name. She fed me. Taught me letters, knots, which mushrooms lie. For two years she was the closest thing to a mother I ever had, and I loved her, and I believe — I still believe — that some part of her loved me back. Genuinely. Warmly. The way you love something you intend to use." Tamsin kept her voice level. It cost something. "When I was thirteen she told me what I was for. Not cruelly. Never cruelly. She sat me down by the fire and explained lineage, and vessels, and how the crown needed tending, and how a girl with my blood could matter more than a thousand ordinary witches. And she said — I have never forgotten the words — *you'll understand when you're older that being chosen is a kind of love.*"

Olla had gone pale. Bright was staring at the ground.

"I ran that night. Twelve years in Saltmere, hiding, flinching at every grey cloak, selling charms with the fear baked in. And you know what I learned?" Tamsin leaned forward. "Being chosen is not a kind of love. Being *asked* is. Being given a door you can walk through or not — that's love. Everything else is just hunger wearing a mother's face."

She stood. The fountain sprayed. The bell rang its half-hour.

"So here is my answer, and I want you to carry it back to every coven that still has a roll of vessels hidden in a chest somewhere. No. Not today, not ever, not for a hundred years of restored magic and kneeling cities. I keep my empty hands. They're mine. They've earned everything they hold, which is nothing, which is exactly enough."

"Tamsin—" Sefryn started.

"You want the old strength back? Then become worthy of it slowly, the way everything real is built. Teach openly, now that the laws are falling. Write the patterns down. Let the blood thin and the knowledge deepen. And if some girl comes to you with strong blood and hungry eyes, don't tell her she's chosen. Ask her what she wants. And believe her when she answers." Tamsin picked up her satchel. "That's the only rite I'll ever sponsor. Freely given means you can say no. Say no to me, and I'm free. Say no to a vessel, and you're free too. Try it sometime."

She turned to go. Behind her, Olla's voice cracked across the square — young, furious, frightened underneath: "You'd let the craft die? After everything it cost you?"

Tamsin stopped. She thought about the answer for a long moment, honestly, the way the question deserved.

"The craft won't die," she said. "Knots don't die. Herblore doesn't die. Weather-sense, charm-work, the reading of tides and birds and sickrooms — none of it dies, because none of it needs a sacrifice to survive. Only the *hunger* dies. Let that starve." She glanced back, and her voice gentled despite herself. "And if you ever want to learn knot-work, Olla — honest knot-work, the kind sold in daylight — the stall on the Strand takes apprentices. No blood required. Just patience."

She walked down toward the harbor, and the spring air smelled of tar and fish and green growing things, and her hands swung empty at her sides, and they were hers.

Edric was at the stall, re-inking a coastline, and he looked up when her shadow fell across the map, and he didn't ask. He just moved the inkwell aside to make room for her elbows, the way he always did, and after a while she picked up a blank scrap of his good paper and began sketching — not a charm, not a sigil, nothing the old covens would recognize as power at all. Just a picture. A fountain. Three children daring each other to touch the water.

"What's that?" Edric asked.

"An idea," Tamsin said, and reached for a fresh knot-cord, and started tying — not a luck-knot, not today. Something new. Something she'd sell to anyone, witch or magistrate or beggar, with the price marked plain and no fine print anywhere beneath.

Down on the water, a boat was coming in with the evening tide, and its sail caught the last of the light like a held breath, and Tamsin watched it and thought: *tomorrow, the harbor. Tomorrow, the first class.*
