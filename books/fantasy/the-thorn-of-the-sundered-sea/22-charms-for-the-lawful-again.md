## 22. Charms for the Lawful, Again

The first day of the harbor school, nobody came.

Tamsin had expected as much. She'd set up three benches on the old fish-market quay — the one that faced the open water now, the water that moved and breathed and smelled like the whole living sea instead of a held breath — and she'd chalked a sign on a slate propped against a barrel:

**HEDGE-CRAFT, PLAINLY TAUGHT.
KNOTS, CHARMS, WEATHER-SENSE, HEALING-SIMPLE.
NO BLOOD. NO SACRIFICE. NO PRICE BUT ATTENDANCE.
BRING YOUR HANDS.**

Edric had looked at the sign for a long time and then said, "You might want to mention it's free."

"It says no price but attendance."

"Magistrates read *no price* and stop."

"The magistrates," Tamsin said, "are busy reading their own law books and finding out half of them just became kindling." The outlawry hadn't collapsed all at once. It had come apart the way ice comes apart — groaning, cracking, in great shifting plates — once the winter that justified it broke. A law written to explain why the sea froze and the king went silent and the candles burned blue had nothing left to stand on when the sea ran green and silver again and the candles burned like ordinary candles, which was to say, badly, and needed trimming.

So on the first morning of the school, with gulls screaming over masts that actually sailed, Tamsin sat on a bench with a basket of knot-cords and waited, and nobody came, and the tide came in anyway, patient as ever, and she found she didn't mind. She'd spent twelve years learning how to wait. Waiting was nearly the whole of hedge-craft, when you got down to it. Waiting, and watching, and knowing what small thing to do next.

The second day, a boy came.

He was maybe nine, barefoot, with a rope burn on one palm he was trying to hide by closing his fist. He stood at the edge of the quay like the water might bite him, though he was clearly a dock kid born and bred, and he stared at her sign for a long time.

"You the witch?" he said finally.

"I'm a teacher," Tamsin said. "Witch is a word people used to get burned for. We're retiring it. Or keeping it, if you like it better. Both are allowed now."

"My mum says you're the one who ended the winter."

"I'm the one who ended the winter the way a key ends a lock," Tamsin said. "There were a lot of hands involved. Some of them dead a hundred years." She patted the bench beside her. "Come sit. What's your name?"

"Joss."

"Well met, Joss. Give me your hand. Not the hurt one — I see it, don't worry, we'll deal with it second. First I'm going to show you the oldest trick there is, older than me, older than my mistress, older than the crown that ruined everything." She took a length of cord from her basket, plain hemp, undyed, worth a farthing. "It's called paying attention."

She showed him how to feel a knot before tying it. Where the cord wanted to bend. Where it resisted. How a charm wasn't a word or a spell or a bargain but an agreement between your intention and the material in your hand, the way a sail wasn't wind but an agreement with it.

Joss's brow furrowed. "That's not magic."

"That's the secret," Tamsin said. "It never was anything else. Everything else was dressing. And sometimes blood, which is why we're done with it."

By noon there were four more kids. By the end of the week, eleven, plus two grandmothers who claimed they were only there to collect their grandchildren and stayed the whole lesson both times, plus a one-eyed fishwife who asked very practical questions about weather-sense and took notes in a hand like anchor-chain.

On the ninth day, a magistrate came.

Tamsin saw him from across the quay — gray coat, chain of office, the careful walk of a man entering territory where he isn't sure of his welcome. The kids went quiet in the way kids do, a silence with eyes in it. Edric, who was at his table under the awning with his maps spread out — the new maps, the true ones, Saltmere redrawn with its harbor attached, the Sundered Sea crossed out and *the sea* written in — set down his pen.

The magistrate stopped in front of Tamsin. He was younger than she expected. He had ink on his fingers.

"Mistress Reyes," he said.

"Just Tamsin is fine."

"Tamsin." He cleared his throat. "The Council has been reviewing the statutes. Most of them are void — they were emergency measures, tied to the winter, and the winter's over. But a few concern... instruction. Teaching. What used to be called the craft." He looked deeply uncomfortable. "We've rewritten them. Or rather, we've deleted them, mostly, and what's left says that anyone may teach anyone anything, provided no harm is done and no life is taken or given." He paused. "Given especially. There was some debate about that clause. It passed unanimously."

"That was fast work," Tamsin said.

"You unbound a century-old curse with your own name," the magistrate said. "The Council felt it could manage a statute in six weeks." He produced a folded paper from his coat. "Also this. It's nothing official. It's — the Council wanted you to have it, and I volunteered to bring it because I wanted to see the school."

Tamsin unfolded it. It was the old outlawry proclamation, the one nailed to every door in Saltmere for twelve years, MAGIC FORBIDDEN UPON PAIN OF in letters that had faded from red to rust. Across it, in the Council's formal hand: *Repealed. With thanks, and with apologies that thanks arrived twelve years late.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it carefully and put it in her pocket, where the luck-knots used to live, back when luck-knots were contraband.

"Thank you," she said. "Would you like to learn a knot? It's free. The only price is attendance, and you've already paid it."

The magistrate looked at the benches full of children and grandmothers and one fishwife taking anchor-chain notes, and something in his face loosened. "I'm on duty until evening."

"We're here most days," Tamsin said. "The tide doesn't care about our hours, so we try not to either."

* * *

Spring in Saltmere was loud.

That was the thing nobody had warned her about, in all those years of frozen harbor and silent water. Ice was quiet. Ice made the whole city hold its breath — no gulls, no bells buoying in the swells, no creak of ropes, no slap of water against hulls. For twelve years Saltmere had lived inside a held note. Now the note had resolved, and the city rang with itself.

The fish market reopened within a month, and the smell was appalling and wonderful. Ships that had rotted at anchor were refloated, patched, renamed — half the fleet came out of spring with new names, as if a ship that had survived the winter deserved a fresh christening. Sailors painted their bows with knots and charms openly now, big brazen ones, and the harbormaster pretended not to see, and then started wearing one himself, a little woven prosperity-knot on his hatband, and after that everyone stopped pretending entirely.

Tamsin sold trinkets at her stall on market mornings. Honest trinkets. That had been the idea, that night in chapter one of all this — well, not *chapter*, she thought wryly; Edric would say *survey point* — that first evening after the thaw, when she'd sat with a fresh cord and decided she was done selling luck to frightened people. Her stall now offered:

Knots for mooring lines, tied stronger than standard, guaranteed, five coppers. Charms for seasickness, which worked about as well as ginger did, which was to say decently, and cost less than ginger. Weather-cords that told you honestly whether to sail tomorrow — honestly being the operative word; they weren't prophecy, they were pattern, and Tamsin taught every buyer how to read their own cord so they didn't need her at all. Small carved thorn-wood figures, cut from the dead branches that still washed up out of the palace ruins, shaped into cats and gulls and kings, and each one tagged with a slip of paper in Tamsin's blunt handwriting: *This wood was part of the thing that ate a king. Now it's a cat. Things change.*

The cats sold fastest.

And beside her stall, under the striped awning, Edric Vale drew maps.

His restoration had become famous in the way quiet things become famous — slowly, and among people who mattered. The Guild of Chartmakers, which had stripped his name from its rolls after the Sundering wards scandal, had sent him a letter in early spring asking, with excruciating politeness, whether he would consider re-entering their fellowship. He'd read the letter aloud to Tamsin on the quay, in the flat voice he used when he was furious, and then fed it to the cookfire.

"They want me back," he'd said, "now that my name sells maps."

"So write back and tell them—"

"I wrote back and told them I'll teach apprentices here, at the quay, free, and if any of them want to be fellows afterward they can apply themselves." He'd shrugged. "They said yes. Of course they said yes. I'm the only man alive who's mapped the under-harbor."

So now there were map students too, three of them, hunched over drafting tables Edric had built from salvaged ship timber, learning coastlines and soundings and the cartographer's first commandment, which Tamsin had heard him recite so often she could recite it in her sleep: *Draw what is there, not what you fear or wish is there.* He'd spent twelve years drawing wards instead of coastlines, lies instead of truths, and he talked about it the way reformed men talk about drink — constantly, plainly, without shame and without letting himself off.

Some evenings, when the light went long and gold across the water and the students had gone home, the two stalls fell quiet together, and Tamsin would find herself standing at Edric's table looking down at the new map of Saltmere, and Edric would look up from whatever he was inking, and neither of them would say anything, because everything worth saying they'd said already, in a drowned harbor and a falling palace and a cellar where a woman finally told the truth about why she ran.

But some evenings one of them said something anyway.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Edric said, one such evening, cleaning his pen. "The chapel. Where I found the records about the hedge-witches and the crown."

"Don't," Tamsin said.

"No — not the hiding it. I've apologized for that enough times that even you've run out of ways to tell me it's forgiven, which is its own kind of punishment." He smiled briefly. "I keep thinking about what was *written*. They made the crown to bind a king's power gently. Gently. That was the original design. A leash, not a knife. And somewhere in a hundred years of fear, a leash became a hunger, and a covenant became a sacrifice." He set the pen down. "Your craft didn't fail, Tamsin. People failed it. Which means it can be repaired by people too. By you."

"I know," Tamsin said. "That's rather the plan."

* * *

The letter from the hedge-witches' survivors had come in April.

Tamsin had known they existed — scattered remnants of her mistress's order, women who'd fled the coven when it curdled, or been expelled, or simply outlived it. After the winter broke, they'd found each other, the way survivors do, and they'd found her name, the way legends find names, and in April a letter arrived at her stall sealed with wax stamped with the old hedge-sign: the knot with no beginning.

*Mistress Reyes,* it began, and she'd winced at that, at *Mistress*, at everything the word carried.

*The craft survives in us, broken but surviving, as it survived in you. The covens must be rejoined. The old knowledge must be gathered and kept and passed on, properly, as it was meant to be. You are the one who unbound the crown. You are the one the old blood runs truest in. Come to us. Take your place as first among the sisterhood. All that was taken from you will be restored — your training completed, your standing given, and if the old rites require it, well. You have proven you understand what giving means.*

She'd read it three times by the harbor rail, and the third time she heard her own voice in it, or rather her mistress's voice speaking through the polite words, the same music underneath: *you are special, you are chosen, and the price is yours to pay, and isn't it an honor to be asked.*

She'd shown it to Edric without a word. He'd read it, and his jaw had gone tight, and he'd said, "Tell them no," and she'd said, "I'm going to tell them something better than no."

Her reply had taken her two evenings to draft and she'd kept both drafts, because she wanted to remember them.

*Sisters,* she wrote. *Thank you for thinking of me. I won't come, and I won't be first among anyone. Here is what I learned from the last person who called herself my mistress: an inheritance that demands your blood isn't an inheritance, it's a debt with good manners. Here is what I learned from a book that circled my name a hundred years ago: prophecy is just history that hasn't learned to mind its own business. And here is what I learned from dying a little, once, on a throne room floor: the craft was never meant to be a crown or a sacrifice. It was meant to be a knot — something you tie because two things should hold together. If you want to rejoin the covens, come to Saltmere. Sit on a bench by the water. Bring your hands. The only price is attendance. — Tamsin*

She signed it *Tamsin.* Just that. Not her mother's name, not her coven name, not the name the thorn had eaten along with her magic on the palace floor. Just the short sharp name she'd gone by in the markets all these years, the name that fit her like a well-tied knot fits its purpose — snug, honest, holding.

Three women came in May. In June, seven more.

* * *

The strange thing, the thing she was still getting used to, was the emptiness in her hands.

For twelve years her magic had hummed under her skin like a second pulse — the sense of cords wanting to bend, of weather leaning in to be read, of small intentions finding small purchase in the world. The thorn had drunk all of it. Every drop, down to the dregs, taken in exchange for her life, and the trade had been fair and she'd make it again, and again, and again.

But some mornings she'd reach for a cord and the hum wouldn't answer, and grief would hit her sideways, quick and physical, like stepping into cold water.

What surprised her was what remained. Her hands still knew the knots. Her eyes still read clouds. When Joss couldn't get a mooring-charm to hold, she could stand behind him and say *looser, you're fighting the fiber, let the cord agree with you* and he'd get it, because the knowledge had sunk below the magic, into muscle and attention, into the place where teaching lives. She couldn't work a charm anymore. She could still make charmers.

Maybe that was always the real craft, she thought. Maybe the power was always the least of it, the flashy surface over deep water. Her mistress had hoarded power and died alone in a tower of her own bitterness. Tamsin tied knots on a quay in the sun and had a school.

She knew which one she'd choose. She chose it every morning, opening the shutters.

* * *

Midsummer, the harbor filled with ships from up the coast, and among them came a delegation from the Guild of Chartmakers, coming in person at last to see Vale's school, and Tamsin watched Edric meet them at the quay with his best coat on and his worst scowl ready, and she laughed until Joss asked her what was funny and she said, "Nothing, tie your knot," and he did, and it held.

Autumn came, and the fish ran fat, and the first storm of the season was an ordinary storm, a mere ordinary rude mortal storm, and the whole city grumbled about it with unmistakable joy, because grumbling about weather is what living cities do, and Saltmere had twelve years of grumbling saved up.

One evening late in the season, after the students had gone, Tamsin sat on the quay edge with her boots almost touching the water, and Edric came and sat beside her, and for a while they watched the lights come on along the shore, one by one, in windows that had been dark or shuttered or fearful for twelve years.

"Do you ever miss it," Edric said. "Before. The way it was."

"Which before?"

"Any of them." He turned his pen over in his fingers — he carried it everywhere now, a habit, a talisman of the honest trade. "Saltmere under the ice. Your stall in the outlaw years. Even the palace, God help me. There's a version of me that misses having a great enemy. It makes everything simple. You always know what you're against."

"And now?"

"Now I know what I'm *for*," he said, "and it turns out that's harder and much better."

Tamsin nodded slowly. Below them the tide turned, unhurried, the old rhythm restored, the sea coming home twice a day like a dog that had finally been let off its chain.

"I miss my magic on cold mornings," she said. "Just for a moment. Then I remember what it cost, and what it was costing everyone else while I had it, and I get over myself." She picked up a length of cord from her basket — she always had cord within reach; she suspected she always would — and began, idly, without thinking, to tie. Not a charm. Just a knot. A good one. The kind that holds.

"What's that one called?" Edric asked.

"It doesn't have a name yet," Tamsin said. She pulled it snug and held it up against the sunset, turning it, examining her work with a craftsman's eye. "I'm thinking I'll teach it first, next term. Before the weather-cords, before the healing-simple. Right at the start." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, and it stayed there. "Lesson one: this is a knot. It holds two things together. That's the whole of the art. Everything else we'll figure out together."

Down the quay, a boat came in with the evening tide, its sail catching the last of the light, and the harbor — the whole, warm, breathing harbor — rose up gently to meet it, the way it had done for a thousand years before the winter, and would do for a thousand after, and Tamsin Reyes — nameless in the old books, nameless in the old rites, and perfectly, stubbornly named in every way that mattered — watched it come home.
