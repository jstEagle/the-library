## 1. The Dying Man's Ink

The commission should have been simple. That was Ansel Voss's first mistake: believing anything in Greyhollow could be simple.

She sat in the back room of the Saltcellar Inn with her ink pots arranged in a neat row, finishing the last strokes of a map for Master Odell the wool-factor — a map of his warehouse routes, annotated with the locations of every rat-hole, loose board, and suspiciously generous customs clerk between here and the coast. It was honest work, or as honest as work got when its entire purpose was helping a man cheat the toll assessors. Ansel had long ago made peace with that particular shade of gray. A cartographer who only drew maps for saints would starve, and besides, saints never tipped.

"There," she murmured, dotting the final *i* on "Tollgate Row" and blowing gently across the wet ink. The candle guttered. Outside, Greyhollow was doing what Greyhollow always did in autumn: raining sideways, complaining about it, and charging extra for firewood.

Master Odell appeared in the doorway, round as one of his own wool bales, wringing his hat.

"Well?" he said. "Is it done? Can I see?"

"You can see when I'm paid."

"I've known you six years, Voss, and you still talk to me like I owe you money."

"You do owe me money." She slid the finished sheet into a linen sleeve and held out her hand. He sighed the sigh of a man parting with coin he'd rather have gambled away, counted out silver piece by piece onto her palm, and then, unable to help himself, peeked at the map.

"Blessed Thorns," he breathed. "You even marked where the assessor's clerk takes his lunch?"

"He takes his lunch where he can see the harbor. Habit of vain men." Ansel capped her ink. "Don't thank me. Just don't get hanged."

"No chance of that. Hanging's for smugglers, and I'm merely a patriot." He tucked the map inside his coat with the tenderness of a man hiding a love letter, paused at the door, and added, more quietly: "Storm's coming down off the Thornfell passes tonight. You ought to bar your door."

"I always bar my door."

"Mm." Odell gave her a look she pretended not to understand, and went.

Ansel tidied her tools slowly. She had a room at the end of the inn's upper corridor — chosen deliberately, because it had one door, one window too narrow for anything larger than a cat, and a landlady, Mistress Brakel, whose hearing was rumored to rival the mountain's own echo. Ansel had learned young that safety was not something you were given; it was something you drew, line by careful line, around yourself. Walls. Locks. Distance. She was very good at distance.

She was climbing the stairs with her satchel over her shoulder when the storm arrived all at once, like an uninvited guest kicking open the door. Rain hammered the shutters. Somewhere below, Mistress Brakel could be heard informing the weather, at volume, exactly what she thought of it.

And then came the knock.

Not a knock, precisely. More of a collapse against wood — a soft, terrible thud, followed by a sound like a man trying to breathe through water. Ansel froze halfway up the stairs, hand going instinctively to the knife at her belt.

"Brakel!" someone called from the common room. "There's a man at the door! Half-dead, by the look—"

"Then half-dead is what the gutter's for!" Brakel's voice, unmoved. "We're closed!"

Ansel should have kept climbing. Every sensible instinct she owned — instincts that had kept her alive through nineteen years of orphanhood and seven years of freelance cartography in towns where freelance cartographers occasionally vanished — told her to keep climbing.

Instead she found herself descending again, past the startled pot-boy, to the heavy front door of the Saltcellar.

The man on the step was dying. There was no gentler way to put it. He was perhaps fifty, grey-bearded, dressed in traveling leathers that had once been fine and now were ruined — soaked through, slashed across the chest, and dark with blood that rain could not wash away. One hand pressed uselessly against his ribs. The other clutched something flat and folded against his body as though it were worth more than the life leaking out of him.

His eyes found hers and fixed there with a desperate, terrible clarity.

"You—" he rasped. "Voss. The mapmaker. They said — Greyhollow — the one who reads roads—"

"Who told you my name?" Ansel asked, crouching despite herself. "Who did this to you?"

He didn't answer. He couldn't, probably. What he did instead was worse: he reached up with his blood-slick hand and pressed the folded thing into hers, folding her fingers over it with strength that surprised her, that came from somewhere deeper than the wound.

"Nine nights," he whispered. His breath fogged and faded in the cold air. "Nine nights, and then they come for her. You have to — you read roads no one else — please. Please. She's all that's left."

"Who? Who comes for her? Sir — sir, stay awake—"

But the light in his eyes was already going out, dimming like a lamp starved of oil. He said something else, so faint she had to lean close to catch it, and what she caught made no sense at all:

"The ledger... check the ledger..."

Then his head fell back against the doorframe, and he was gone. Just like that — between one heartbeat and the next, between the rain and the silence after it.

For a long moment Ansel knelt there in the doorway, rain spattering her shoulders, holding a dead stranger's hand around a dead stranger's package.

"Well," said Mistress Brakel behind her, arms crossed, unimpressed by tragedy as only a Greyhollow landlady can be. "That'll be trouble. Dead men are always trouble. Especially ones that die on your doorstep calling your name."

"He knew me," Ansel said quietly. "Or knew of me. Brakel, who talks about me? Who knows I'm here?"

"Half the county knows the Voss woman draws maps that find things other people lose. That's the trouble with being good at something, girl. Word gets around." The old woman peered down at the corpse with distasteful sympathy. "Coroner will want him. And whatever's in his hand, likely."

Ansel looked down at the folded object. Blood had soaked into its edges. Some deep, stubborn instinct — the same instinct that made her check a lock twice — told her that if this thing went to the coroner, it would go to whoever had killed this man, because in border towns like Greyhollow, coroners had masters too.

"Private commission," she heard herself say. "Client died before delivery. Standard practice — the work reverts to the maker."

"That is not remotely standard practice."

"It is tonight." She met Brakel's eye. "I'll pay for the mess. And the pot-boy keeps quiet, yes?"

"For double the mess fee, everyone in this inn is blind and deaf." Brakel turned and bellowed up the stairs anyway: "Tam! Fetch the tarpaulin and stop gawking!"

* * *

Upstairs, alone, with the door barred and the candle trimmed low, Ansel finally unfolded the map.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing — before fear, before sense — it was simply, achingly beautiful. Drawn on hide rather than paper, in inks that shifted color when the candlelight moved: iron-grey to river-green to a red that was not quite red. The hand was masterful, better than hers, which annoyed her immediately and enormously. But it was the content that stopped her breath.

Roads. Dozens of them, threading through the Thornfell mountains like veins through a wrist — and she recognized none of them. Ansel Voss had spent seven years learning every smuggler path, goat track, and forgotten pass from Greyhollow to the coast. She could draw the Thornfells from memory. These roads were not in any memory. They bent wrong. They doubled back through places they'd already been. Several of them appeared to move — she was nearly certain three of the lines were in slightly different positions than they had been when she'd first looked, though the map hadn't been touched.

And at the heart of them all, drawn in that not-quite-red ink, a small dense symbol like a weeping tree.

Beneath it, in a fine cramped hand, a single line:

*Nine nights. Then the debt collects itself.*

Ansel set the map down. She stood. She walked to the hearth, took up the poker, and stirred the coals until they glowed.

This was the sensible course. A stranger dies on her doorstep with a stolen-looking map to somewhere impossible, whispering warnings about ledgers and debts. Every story she'd ever heard ended badly for the person holding such a map. Burn it. Bury the man's memory with his body, collect no inheritance but the rain, and go back to drawing rat-holes for wool-factors.

She held the map over the coals.

The heat rose against her palms. The hide began to warm, and in the shifting glow the inks seemed to move — truly move — the roads crawling like living things, and for just a moment she thought she saw, among the tiny marks along the smallest road, a figure. Small. Standing still. Waiting.

A girl, maybe. Someone waiting for a rescue that required a mapmaker who could read roads no one else could.

"Oh, damn it," Ansel said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead man, to herself. "Damn it all."

She pulled the map back from the fire. Her hands were shaking, which infuriated her. She was not a rescuer. She was a professional. She drew lines; she did not walk them. She had built her whole life out of locked doors and safe distances, and here she was at midnight, heart pounding over a dead stranger's dying wish, feeling — gods help her — *obligated*. He had pressed it into her hands. He had spent his last breath on her name. Whatever terror had driven him down the mountain and through the storm, it had chosen her, out of everyone in the world, to carry it forward.

She wanted to burn it. She wanted it so badly her teeth ached.

But she kept seeing his eyes. Not the pain in them — she'd seen pain before, plenty, in mirror and street. The fear. Fear for someone else, sharp enough to outlast his own dying. Ansel knew exactly one thing about that kind of fear: it wasn't for sale, and it wasn't fake, and ignoring it was the sort of decision a person had to live inside forever.

She spread the map flat on her table and pinned its corners with her ink pots.

"Nine nights," she muttered. "Fine. Nine nights. I'll read your wretched roads, I'll find out who 'she' is, and then, I swear it, I am going back to cheating toll assessors like a respectable woman."

Outside, the storm howled down off the Thornfells, rattling her shutters like fingers testing a lock.

And far beneath the mountains, in a court that kept accounts in souls, something opened a ledger and made a note.
