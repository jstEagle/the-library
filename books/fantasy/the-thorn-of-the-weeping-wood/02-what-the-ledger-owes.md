## 2. What the Ledger Owes

Ansel Voss had three rules about strangers who died on her doorstep: bury them, take nothing they offered, and never, under any circumstances, follow their maps.

She had already broken the third rule by dawn, which annoyed her, because the first two had been so easy. The stranger — she'd learned his name was Edran from a fraying tag sewn into his collar, the sort of tag a worried sister stitches in — lay decently buried behind the woodshed, under a cairn of grey stones with a copper penny on top for the crossing. Rule one, observed. Rule two, technically observed: she hadn't *taken* the map. It had simply refused to leave. She'd thrown it in the hearth twice, and twice the fire had bent around it like water around a stone, and the third time she'd decided that a map which declined to burn deserved, at minimum, a professional appraisal.

That was the story she told herself, anyway, as she packed her satchel in the blue hour before sunrise: that this was due diligence. That a cartographer's first duty was to the truth of any document, even a cursed one. That she was not, *not*, walking out of Greyhollow because a dying man's terror had hooked itself under her ribs like a fisherman's barb.

The map's first road began at the well in the market square, which was a stupid place for a road to begin, because the well was surrounded on all sides by cobbles and a cheese stall. But when Ansel held the paper up to the grey light, the ink did something ink should never do: it settled, the way silt settles in still water, and a thin line unspooled from the drawn well on the page and pointed — unmistakably — north, past the tannery, into the hills.

"Well," she said to the well. "You could have mentioned this years ago."

The well said nothing. Wells rarely did. Ansel had spent thirty-one years talking to geography and had learned to accept its silences as a form of professional courtesy.

She bought bread, a wheel of hard cheese, and — after a moment's internal argument with her better judgment — a second knife. Then she followed the ink out of Greyhollow.

* * *

The road the map described was not, in any honest sense, a road. It was a *suggestion* of a road. It appeared as a seam of paler earth between the bracken, vanished under a fallen beech, reappeared on the other side as if the tree had politely stepped around it. Twice it crossed a stream without troubling itself with a bridge or even, as far as Ansel could tell, getting wet.

She stopped at midday on a bald hilltop, spread the map on a flat stone, and did what she did best: she *read* it. Not the ink — the land. Every map was a translation, and a good cartographer checked the translation against the original. The hills to the north. The split crag like a rabbit's ear. The old shepherd's wall running wrong-angled down the slope, which no honest wall did.

It all matched. Which was the problem, because Ansel had charted these hills four years ago for a wool merchant, and there was no road here. There had *never* been a road here. The seam of pale earth ran through country she knew like her own kitchen, and it ran through places where a road could not be — across the bog at Fen Hollow, through the standing stone that had stood since before anyone stood anything.

"Smuggler's road," she murmured. The stranger had whispered something before the end — *nine nights*, and a word she'd taken for delirium: *Thornfell*. Every child in the borderlands knew the stories of Thornfell's shifting ways, the roads that moved like weather, the court beneath the mountain that bought and sold things no honest market stocked. Ansel had always filed those stories under *geography, folkloric* and gotten on with her life.

The map in her hands was not folkloric. The map was *winning an argument with reality*, and reality was losing.

She was still crouched over it, making a small precise note in her fieldbook — *road exists; road should not; investigate or burn* — when she heard the footsteps.

There were too many of them, and they were too quiet. That was the detail her body understood before her mind did: six sets of feet making the sound of one. Ansel closed the fieldbook, stood slowly, and turned.

They came up the hill out of the bracken like herons wading out of reeds — tall, grey-cloaked, and wrong in a way her eyes kept trying to correct. Their faces were smooth and hollowed, as if someone had scooped out the important parts and left the architecture. Their eyes were the worst. Not empty — *accounting*. They looked at her the way a tax assessor looks at a house.

"Ansel Voss," said the foremost one. Its voice was dry paper. "The collateral."

"I'm sorry," Ansel said, "I think you have me confused with someone worth money."

The thing tilted its head, and the others tilted theirs in the same motion, at the same angle, like a field of wheat in wind. "The debt is not yours. The name is. Names travel. You are carrying."

"I'm carrying bread, cheese, and a professional grudge against whoever drew this map." She took one careful step backward, toward the standing stone. "If this is about the commission for the wool merchant, I'll have you know my rates are fair and my borders are accurate."

The foremost collector raised a hand. Its fingers were too long and ended in nibs — actual pen nibs, black iron, glistening. Ansel's cartographer's brain noted, with detached horror, that they were *dip pens*. Living ones.

"The mark," it said. "The ledger requires the mark."

What happened next, Ansel would reconstruct later in her fieldbook with the dispassion she brought to all difficult terrain, though her hand shook while writing it. The collectors did not run. They *arrived* — one moment at the hill's foot, the next an arm's length away, the space between simply declining to exist. She got her knife up. It did not matter. A nib-finger touched the back of her right hand, the drawing hand, and cold ink poured in — not onto her skin but *through* it, a sigil blooming under the surface like a bruise made of script. She couldn't read it upside down, but she could feel it: a weight, a *claim*, the sensation of being an entry in a book she'd never opened.

She was still staring at her own hand, at the fine black lines coiling beneath it, when the hilltop changed.

It changed the way a room changes when a large, dangerous dog walks in and lies down by the fire. The collectors stopped. All six. Their accounting eyes flicked past Ansel to a point over her left shoulder, and for the first time since they'd arrived, something moved in those hollow faces that looked almost like fear.

"You're a long way from your shelf," said a voice behind her. Low, rough, amused in the way a scar is amused.

Ansel turned her head just enough to see him: a man leaning on the standing stone as if he'd been there all afternoon, though the stone had been bare ten seconds ago. He was weathered to the color of old rope, with a beard going iron at the jaw and a face that had been broken at least once and reassembled by someone in a hurry. A long scar ran from his left ear to his collarbone, pale as a river on a map. He was not holding a weapon. He did not appear to need one. The air around him had a pressure to it, like the moment before a storm decides.

"You," said the foremost collector, and its paper voice cracked. "*Warden.*"

"Former," the man said pleasantly. "But thank you for remembering. Now, the ledger's rules — and you do love the rules — say a marked name can't be collected while contested. Well." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "I'm contesting."

The collectors looked at each other. Six heads, one motion. Whatever they saw in the man's face, it settled the matter. They folded backward into the bracken the way a page folds shut, and the hilltop was ordinary again — wind, heather, a standing stone, two people and a map.

Ansel's knees informed her, quietly but firmly, that they were done. She sat down on the flat stone, mostly on purpose.

"Right," she said, when her voice agreed to work. "You're going to explain that. All of it. Starting with what they wrote on my hand, and ending with why they were afraid of you, and I'd like the middle part too, the part where you appeared out of a rock."

The man crouched, keeping a respectful distance, and studied the sigil on her hand with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone reading bad news in a document they'd hoped was a forgery.

"Corvin," he said. "And I'll explain, but you're not going to like it, and I'd rather you were sitting down. Which you are. Good. Efficient."

"I'm a cartographer. I like terrain assessed before I cross it."

"Then here's the terrain." He nodded at her hand. "That's a collector's mark. It means you're entered. Somewhere under Thornfell mountain there's a court — the one from the stories your grandmother told to keep you off the passes. It keeps a ledger. Every soul that's ever been bought, sold, or held as surety, written down in ink that doesn't dry. They don't collect the person, mind. They collect what's *owed* — and what's owed is the soul itself. You're not a debtor, Ansel Voss. You're collateral. Someone put you up against a debt, a long time ago, and the court has come to collect."

Ansel laughed. It came out wrong — too high, too short, a laugh with a crack down the middle. "Collateral. I'm thirty-one years old. I've lived in Greyhollow my whole life. I was a foundling; the midwife left me at the temple with nothing but a blanket and my own name. Nobody *sold* me. There was nothing to sell."

"That's what I would have said," Corvin said quietly, "before I worked there."

The wind moved the heather. Somewhere below, the pale seam of the road went on winding north, patient as a signature waiting to be signed.

"Show me the ledger," Ansel said.

Corvin blinked. Whatever he'd expected — screaming, denial, a knife — it wasn't that. "You understand what I just told you? They've marked you. They'll come back, and there'll be more next time, and the mark means they can *find* you anywhere between here and the sea."

"I heard you." Ansel stood, brushed off her skirts, and picked up the map. The ink on it had shifted again — the road now bent east, toward the mountains, as if it had been listening. "I've spent my whole life drawing other people's borders, Corvin. Roads I've never walked, for merchants I've never met, to places that don't want me. And now someone — *something* — has written a claim on my hand without so much as a by-your-leave, and it expects me to sit in Greyhollow and wait to be collected like a debt at quarter-day." She rolled the map, and her voice came down to something cold and steady, a line drawn true. "I want to see the page my name is written on. And then I want to see what it costs to cross it out."

Corvin looked at her for a long moment. Then he grinned, slow and crooked, like a door opening on a house you'd been warned about.

"Nine days' walk," he said. "And the road moves."

"Then we'd best not lose it," said Ansel Voss, and stepped onto the road that did not exist.
