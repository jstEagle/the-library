## 3. Nine Nights on the Smuggler's Road

Ansel had decided, somewhere between the second ambush and the third mile of switchbacks, that she hated Corvin.

Not the way she hated the collectors — that was a clean, cold hatred, like hating a rockslide. No, she hated Corvin the way one hates a leaky roof: persistently, and with the growing suspicion that he was going to be her problem for a very long time.

"Left at the split birch," he said, riding a half-length behind her mule.

"There is no split birch. There's a split *oak*, and it's a mile on, and if you'd read my maps instead of fondling them you'd know that."

"I've walked this pass since before you could hold a stylus, cartographer."

"Then you've been walking it wrong for a long time." She nudged the mule past a fallen scree and didn't look back. "The roads in Thornfell don't hold still, warden. What you learned is dead. What I chart is alive."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had that careful, scraped-bare quality she was beginning to recognize as his version of honesty. "That's why I need you."

"I know. It's the only reason you haven't robbed me and left me in a ditch."

"Also you'd fight back, and my shoulder's still seeping."

"It is not *seeping*, it's weeping, and it's your own fault for stepping in front of a collector's hook." She said it crossly, but that evening she boiled willow bark into his tin cup without being asked, and he drank it without thanking her, and between the two of them they managed something that almost resembled courtesy.

* * *

Corvin counted the nights the way other men counted coin.

One. He'd spent the first night watching the cartographer sleep with her hand curled around the dead man's map, and he'd thought, *she doesn't even know what she's carrying yet.* He'd been wrong, as it turned out. She knew. She'd known since the collectors called her *collateral* and pressed the sigil into her skin, and she'd kept walking anyway, which was either bravery or a particular species of stubbornness he'd only ever seen in people the court had already marked.

Two. She caught him studying the sigil on her hand by firelight and didn't flinch. "How long do I have?" she asked.

"Nine nights from the ink. You're on two."

"Then stop wasting them staring at me and tell me what the court fears."

He told her. Some of it. That the court beneath the Weeping Wood ran on its ledger the way a heart runs on blood. That the ledger could not be burned, only *outbid*. That the wardens had one blind spot, and it was the roads — the smuggler ways that shifted like weather, that no warden had ever charted because charting them required a soul the court couldn't buy.

"Why couldn't it buy one?"

"Because the roads don't answer to the sold," he said, and watched her absorb that, and hated himself a little for how easily the lie sat in his mouth. The truth was uglier and he wasn't ready to hand it to her. The truth was that the court had tried to buy a road-walker once, decades ago, and the warden who processed the contract had made sure the price was never paid — had sold the child's name into the ledger and pocketed the difference, and had told himself for thirty years that it was mercy.

Three. She caught a fish with her bare hands, which he found absurd, and cooked it badly, which he found forgivable, and over the fire she said, "Tell me what you did. To be disgraced."

"No."

"You'll have to eventually."

"Then I'll eventually." He poked the fire. "Ask me something cheaper."

She thought about it with her whole face, which was going to be a problem for him later, he could already tell. "All right. What's the Weeping Wood weep *about*?"

"Sap," he said. "It's just sap."

"Liar."

"Cheaper question."

* * *

On the fourth night the pass closed.

Ansel felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the way the wind moved through the col, the particular hush of a road deciding to be somewhere else. She'd grown up on these mountains, orphaned into them, apprenticed to a dead surveyor who'd taught her that Thornfell's paths were less geography than *mood*. Most people fought that. She'd learned to listen.

"Camp here," she said.

"Here?" Corvin looked at the ledge — a shelf of granite barely wide enough for two bedrolls, exposed to the wind, with a drop on one side that went down long enough to have opinions about you. "There's a waystation two miles—"

"Two miles back the way the road *was*." She was already unsaddling the mule. "The pass is closing. It does this. It'll reopen at dawn on the far side of the ridge, and if we're on it when it closes, we'll be on the far side of *nowhere*."

He stared at her. "You can feel that."

"I can feel that."

He helped her pitch the shelter without another word, but she caught him watching her afterward, that same careful stare, like a man re-reading a map he'd memorized wrong.

* * *

The wind screamed all night and the fire fought to live, and somewhere in the small hours Corvin gave up pretending to sleep.

He'd processed hundreds of contracts in his warden years. He knew the sound a soul made when it was entered into the ledger — a small sound, a *tick*, like a quill nib catching. He'd heard it the night a road-walker's infant daughter was entered, price waived, seller unnamed, and he'd told himself for thirty years that waiving the price was kindness.

He'd told himself a great many things for thirty years.

Across the fire, the cartographer slept sitting up, as she did, her marked hand open in her lap like something that had given up hiding. In nine nights the court's collectors would come for her, and the ledger would tick, and there was nothing in Thornfell — not her roads, not his guilt, not all the willow bark in the world — that could un-tick it.

Unless the ledger could be outbid. Unless someone offered the court something it wanted more.

He looked at her hand. At the ink-sigil, already fading at the edges the way the court's marks faded when they were close to collection.

*Ask me something cheaper*, she'd said, and he'd dodged, and he'd keep dodging, because the expensive answer — *I'm the reason your name is in that book* — was a debt no firelight could soften.

"Corvin." She'd woken without opening her eyes. "You're staring again."

"Go back to sleep, cartographer."

"You first."

He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth he'd permitted himself in thirty years, and it frightened him more than the hounds at the tollgate ever would.

* * *

The fifth day brought the high country, and with it the first of the shifting roads.

Ansel had charted these her whole life and still they took her breath away: a trail that unspooled ahead of them through the scree like thread off a spindle, bending where no trail should bend, doubling back through a stand of pines that had not been there an hour before. Corvin rode it in silence, and she could feel him wanting to ask *how*, and she let him want.

At a fork where three paths braided together and came apart again, she stopped and dug out her stylus and a scrap of vellum and sketched, quickly, the shape of the day's road as she'd felt it.

"You're charting a road that won't exist tomorrow," Corvin said.

"I'm charting *how it moves*." She blew the ink dry. "The road's not the thing. The road's habit is the thing. Every pass in Thornfell has a temperament. This one's a coward — it runs from weather, from riders, from anything loud. The one after is vain; it likes to be admired, so it holds still if you compliment it."

"You're joking."

"Compliment the next pass, warden. I mean it. Out loud. It works better if you mean it."

And God help him, an hour later, she caught him murmuring *fine ridge, good stone, very handsome col* to a mountain pass, and the trail held steady beneath them all the way to the saddle, and the look on his face was the single finest thing Ansel had seen in a year of hard roads, and she laughed until her ribs hurt, and he let her, and something between them quietly changed its temperament and held.

* * *

Sixth night. The fire was good and the wind was kind and Corvin, unasked, told her a story that was almost the truth.

"There was a warden," he said, "who believed the court could be worked from the inside. Who thought if he was clever enough, patient enough, he could slip souls out through the cracks. And one night a man came to sell — not his own soul, someone else's — and the warden saw a way to save the sold one, and he took it, and it cost him everything, and it didn't even work."

Ansel fed the fire a branch and watched it catch. "Is that your confession?"

"It's a cheaper question's answer."

"It's a story about a fool."

"Yes," Corvin said. "It is."

She looked at him across the flames, at the scar and the seeping shoulder and the thirty years sitting on him like wet wool, and she thought: *he's lying to me about something, and it's something large, and I'm going to find out what it is before the ninth night, because I do not walk into courts blind.*

But she also thought: *he complimented a mountain because I told him to. He'll hold a gate for me. Men who'll do the second will usually do the first, but men who do the first will not always do the second, and I'd rather have the first kind at my back in the Weeping Wood.*

"All right, fool," she said aloud. "Sleep. Tomorrow we compliment our way over the shoulder of the world, and the night after that, we knock on the devil's door."

* * *

On the eighth night, from a ridge above the tree line, Ansel saw it for the first time: a darkness in the valley below that was not shadow, a forest that seemed to be *leaking*, silver threads running down from every crown of every tree like a wood gone weeping all at once.

Beside her, Corvin had gone very still.

"That's it," she said. "That's the Weeping Wood."

"That's the Weeping Wood." His voice had gone strange. "Cartographer — before we go down. There's a tollgate at the heart of it. The hounds that keep it don't take coin. They take memories. One each, to pass."

Ansel watched the silver threads catch the last of the light and thought of her mother's face — the one face she owned, the one the orphanage had never managed to take.

"How much does it cost to get *out* again?" she asked.

Corvin didn't answer.

And far below, at the forest's black edge, something lifted its head and looked up the ridge at them, and began, patiently, to climb.
