## 4. The Weeping Wood

The hound — for that was what it proved to be, though it climbed like a man and watched them like a debt collector — did not follow them into the trees.

Ansel noticed this the moment they crossed the forest's edge. The thing had come within fifty paces of their campfire in the night, close enough that she could see its ribs moving beneath skin the color of wet slate, and then it had simply stopped, sat down at the treeline, and waited. It was waiting still, a grey smudge between two bleeding trunks, patient as a stone.

"They don't come in," Corvin said, when she pointed it out. He was tightening the strap on his pack with more force than the strap required. "The wood is the court's larder, not its hunting ground. Things that go in are already claimed."

"That's meant to comfort me?"

"It's meant to inform you. Comfort's above my pay." He glanced back once at the hound, and something passed over his face — recognition, maybe, or an old apology — before he turned and led them under the branches.

The first thing Ansel understood about the Weeping Wood was that every map she had ever drawn had lied to her by omission. She had charted coastlines and mountain passes, smuggler roads and river fords, and never once had she drawn a place that *grieved*. But these trees grieved. Sap ran down their bark in slow, glassy threads, catching the grey light and holding it, so that the whole forest seemed hung with silver cords of weeping. Where the sap reached the roots it pooled in small clear wells, and the wells did not reflect the sky. They reflected other things.

She caught her own face in one, walking past, and looked away quickly. Her reflection had been smiling. She hadn't been.

"Don't drink," Corvin said without turning around. "Don't touch. Don't answer anything that calls your name."

"Does much call my name here?"

"Not yet."

*Yet.* She filed the word away with all the other small knives he kept handing her and walked on.

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow of three great trunks whose canopies knit overhead like clasped hands. Corvin built no fire; he said fire drew things in the Weeping Wood, and instead produced a lump of pale stone that gave off light like a moon held underwater. Ansel spread her maps across her knees by its glow and tried to work.

It was impossible work. Every time she set pen to paper her hand drifted toward the dead man's map — folded now in her breast pocket, warm as a living thing, which she knew was nonsense and believed anyway. She had studied it a hundred times since Greyhollow. It was beautiful in the way that only desperate things are beautiful: roads inked and re-inked, passages annotated in a cramped, careful hand, one margin note repeated over and over until the ink had worn the paper thin:

*Nine nights. Nine nights. Nine.*

"You knew him," she said to Corvin, not looking up. "The man who died at my door. You went quiet when I described him. You've been going quiet at odd moments ever since. Who was he?"

Corvin fed another branch to his pale stone light, though it needed nothing. "His name was Aldous Crane. He was a warden of the court, same as I was. Better than me, honestly. Wardens don't get pensions, Voss. They get assignments. His was collection."

"Collection of souls."

"Collection of debts. Souls are just how the ledger keeps score." He was silent a moment. In the underwater light his scars looked like rivers on a map of some country Ansel was glad she'd never charted. "Aldous stopped collecting eleven years ago. Walked out of Thornfell, burned his sigils, changed his name twice. I helped him do it, the second time. Then I lost track of him."

"And the court let him go?"

"The court doesn't *let* anyone go. It waits. Debts mature." Corvin looked at her then, and his eyes were very tired. "You should sleep. Tomorrow we reach the tollgate, and you'll want your memories sharp for it."

But sleep, when it came, was not rest.

* * *

She dreamed of ink.

Not her ink — she knew her own hand the way she knew her own heartbeat, every flourish and abbreviation — but someone else's, someone writing with a steady, careful pressure, filling in a ledger line by line. In the dream she stood behind the writer's shoulder and read over it: names, prices, dates. And the hand belonged to a man she recognized.

Aldous Crane. Younger than the bloodied stranger who had died against her door, younger by years of hard road and fear, but the same face — the same deep-set, honest eyes, the same mouth that looked built for apologizing. He wore the grey-and-silver livery of the court's wardens, and he wrote, and wrote, and each name he entered made a sound in the dream like a bell dropped down a well.

Then the dream shifted, the way dreams do when they belong to someone else, and Ansel was watching through Aldous's eyes instead of standing behind him.

She saw a woman — worn, laughing, hair escaping its braid — holding a baby in a doorway. She saw Aldous look at the baby with an expression Ansel had no map for, something too large for the dream to hold, and she heard him say words that came to her not as sound but as weight:

*I'll fix this. However it costs. I'll fix it.*

And she saw the ledger again, saw his own hand write a bargain into it, and the ink of that entry was different from all the others — darker, wetter, as though the page itself bled to receive it:

*Aldous Crane. One soul, freely given. In exchange: nine nights of grace for Maren Crane, sister, beginning upon his death.*

Nine nights.

The dying stranger had pressed the map into her hands and whispered *nine nights*, and she had thought it a warning, or a riddle, or the babbling of a frightened man. It was none of those things. It was a countdown. He had sold himself — the whole of himself, everything he was and might have been — to buy his sister nine nights of running, nine nights in which the collectors' hands would be stayed. And then, because nine nights is not enough to outrun a court, he had done the last thing left to a drowning man.

He had passed the debt to a stranger with quick hands and a cartographer's eye, and died trusting her with it.

Ansel woke with wet cheeks and no memory of starting to cry.

* * *

Corvin was awake, of course. He was always awake; she suspected the man slept like a cat, in stolen minutes and with one ear cocked. He said nothing about her red eyes, which she appreciated, and handed her a strip of dried apple, which she appreciated less but ate anyway.

"In your dreams," she said. "Do you ever see the wardens? What they were?"

Something flickered across his face — there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Sometimes."

"I saw Aldous. Before. When he still wore the livery." She turned the apple strip over in her fingers. "He wasn't collecting for himself, Corvin. He was buying someone out. A girl called Maren. The ledger says *sister*."

For a long moment Corvin didn't move. The pale light made him look carved from something older than flesh. Then he said, quietly, "Is that what it says."

"You knew."

"I knew Aldous had a reason. Men like him don't break for no reason." He looked away, up through the canopy at a sky the bleeding trees had strangled to threads. "I didn't know the reason. That's the truth, Voss, whether you believe it or not."

"What happened to her? Maren?"

"She was collected anyway. Grace runs out; grace always runs out. The court doesn't kill what it owns — killing wastes stock. They keep souls the way you'd keep wine. Some vintages they open. Some they cellar." His voice had gone flat, the flatness of a man walking carefully across ice he already knows is rotten. "If Maren Crane is still in the ledger, she's still in the cellars. Faded, maybe. Half-remembered. But keepable things can sometimes be unkept."

Ansel looked down at the dead man's map in her lap, at the cramped, hopeful, terrible handwriting, and felt grief and guilt twine together in her chest like the roots of the weeping trees. Grief for Aldous Crane, who had paid everything and bought almost nothing. Guilt because she had wanted, for one black moment in Greyhollow, to burn his map. Because she had taken his commission money and thought *trouble finds me*. Because a man had died at her door and her first instinct had been to count the cost to herself.

"He trusted me," she said. "He didn't know me. He had nine nights to find anyone in Greyhollow and he chose the mapmaker."

"Maybe he chose the only one who could read the roads."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Maybe he chose the only one who'd say yes."

Corvin was quiet for a while. When he spoke again, his voice had a strange gentleness she hadn't heard from him before. "You should know something about the tollgate before we walk into it. The hounds take one memory each. They choose which one — you don't. Most people lose something small. A childhood dog. A rainy afternoon. But the gate smells what you're carrying, and it takes what will hurt most to give. That's how it knows the payment's good."

"Then it'll take my mother's face," Ansel said. "It's the only thing I've got that's worth anything."

She expected him to argue. He didn't, and somehow that was worse.

Instead he stood, and shouldered his pack, and looked north through the silver-threaded gloom, where the trees grew taller and closer and the sap-runs thickened into ropes of glass. Somewhere in that direction, faint as a pulse, the ground carried a rhythm up through Ansel's boot-soles. Slow. Patient. Like breathing. Like something vast beneath the earth, turning over in its sleep.

"It knows we're coming," Corvin said. "It always knows. Come on, cartographer. Day six. Three nights of grace left, if Aldous counted true."

Ansel rose, folded the dead man's map with hands that almost didn't shake, and followed him into the deeper dark of the Weeping Wood — where, at the edge of her hearing, something began to weep that was not a tree.
