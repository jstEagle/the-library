## 5. Tollgate of Teeth

The trees began to weep on the third morning.

Ansel had seen forests do strange things in Thornfell — pines that grew upside down, birches with bark like old letters — but nothing prepared her for the sap. It ran down the trunks in slow, glistening ropes, catching the grey light until the whole wood seemed to be crying quietly about something it refused to name.

"Cheerful place," she said.

Corvin walked ahead, one hand resting on the strap of his pack, and did not answer. He'd been quiet since dawn. Quieter than usual, which was saying something; Corvin's silences came in grades, and Ansel had learned to read them the way she read contour lines. This one said *I know where we're going, and I don't love it.*

"You could just tell me," she offered. "Whatever you're not telling me. Save us both the brooding."

"I'm not brooding."

"You've been chewing your lip since the river. That's brooding. That's practically a sonnet of brooding."

He glanced back at her, and for a moment she caught something flicker behind his eyes — guilt, maybe, or fear wearing guilt's coat. "There are things about this wood," he said carefully, "that are better learned by walking into them."

"Wonderful. A surprise party."

The road narrowed as they went deeper. That was the wrong word for it — *road* implied something made — but Ansel could feel the way beneath her boots, the faint pull of passage, the sense that feet had traveled here before and the ground remembered them. She'd felt it all through Thornfell, on every smuggler track she'd ever charted: roads were not places, they were habits. And habits could be broken.

Here, though, the habit was old. Older than anything. The ground beneath them had been walked so long and by so few that each step felt like interrupting a conversation.

By midday they could hear it.

At first she thought it was wind. Then water. Then, horribly, breathing — great slow lungs somewhere ahead, drawing air in and letting it out with a sound like a millstone turning.

Corvin stopped so suddenly she nearly walked into his back.

"Tollgate," he said.

---

It stood in a clearing where no clearing should have been, because the trees around it leaned *away*, the way people lean from a beggar or a fire. The gate itself was iron gone green with age, tall as three men, its bars shaped — Ansel looked twice, then wished she hadn't — like teeth. Long ones. Curved ones. The kind of teeth that belonged in stories told to children who wouldn't sleep.

And before it, lying in the weeping grass like hounds before a hearth, were the Ferryman's dogs.

They were enormous. They were also, Ansel noted with the part of her brain that never stopped working, wrong in the specific way that fae things were wrong: their shadows lay at angles their bodies didn't allow, and when they breathed, the sap on the nearest trees ran a little faster, as if the whole wood flinched.

One of them lifted its head. Its eyes were the color of coins at the bottom of a well.

"They'll want payment," Corvin said quietly. "Not coin. Not blood. They want a memory. One, freely given, per traveler. Take more and they take the rest by force."

"A memory." Ansel kept her voice flat. "Of what?"

"That's the cruelty of it," Corvin said. "You choose. You stand there and you choose what you can live without, knowing you won't know what you lost until it's gone."

The hounds rose. All six of them, in unison, without a sound — and that was worse than barking, somehow. Barking would have been honest.

Ansel's hand found her satchel, where the dead man's map slept folded against her ribs. Nine nights. Four spent already. Maren was fading somewhere below the mountain, half-remembered even by herself, and here was a gate demanding tolls like any bridge in any border town, except the coin was *her*.

"All right," she said. "Fine. We pay. We walk. We don't look back. That's how these things work, isn't it?"

"That's how most of them work."

"*Most.*"

"Keep walking, Voss."

---

She chose standing at the edge of the clearing, while Corvin spoke to the hounds in a low language that sounded like gravel poured over velvet. She chose because choosing was easier than watching him choose, and because she already knew.

Her mother's face.

She'd been rationing it for years anyway, the way you ration the last of anything precious. Ansel had been left on a chapel step in Greyhollow with nothing but a blanket stitched with an initial — *A*, or possibly a poorly made compass rose; she'd decided long ago it was a compass rose, because that was the kinder story — and the memory of a face was the only inheritance she'd ever carried. Warm. Brown-eyed. Laughing, she thought, though she could no longer swear to the laugh.

Every year the face grew softer, the way footprints fade on a well-worn road. She had been losing it by inches her whole life. Better to give it whole, then, on purpose, at a price of her own choosing, than to wake one morning at sixty and find it simply gone, spent on nothing.

That was what she told herself, anyway, standing in the weeping grass with her heart going like a hammer on wet leather.

"Voss." Corvin's voice, careful. "You don't have to—"

"It's my toll to pay." She stepped forward before courage could reconsider.

The nearest hound lowered its great head until its breath washed over her — smelling, strangely, of rain on hot stone, of summer she couldn't place. *Couldn't place.* Even now, places slipped. She thought of a kitchen. A window. A woman humming—

A woman humming what?

*Now,* Ansel thought, panicked. *Hold it now, hold every piece—*

She opened her mouth and gave it away. Not in words. In the way you give anything to the fae: completely, and all at once, before the giving can hurt enough to stop you.

The hound closed its eyes. When it opened them, it inclined its head — almost courteously — and the gate's iron teeth parted with a groan like a ship coming apart.

Ansel stood there a moment, waiting to feel lighter. Grief, she'd always assumed, had weight.

She felt exactly the same weight as before. That was the worst part. She simply couldn't say anymore what the weight was *for*.

"Come on," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Daylight's wasting."

Behind her, she heard Corvin speak low and fast to the second hound. She heard the words — she was sure she heard the words — but they slid off her mind like water off waxed canvas, and by the time she turned to look, he was already walking toward her, face composed, hands empty.

"What did you give?" she asked.

"A bad winter," he said. "Famine year. Lots of unpleasant details." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and it was a very good smile, and later — much later — she would understand it was the best lie he ever told her, because it was built entirely out of true-shaped pieces.

"Your name isn't in a famine, Corvin."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It is not."

She let it go. There was a gate open and a sister dying and four nights left, and a man's private sorrows were his own toll to count.

They passed under the teeth together. Above them, the iron groaned; behind them, it closed.

Neither of them looked back.

Which was why neither of them saw the hound that had taken Corvin's payment lift its head and *speak* — one word, in a voice like a bell dropped down a well, a word that the wood itself seemed to taste and find familiar:

*"Warden."*

---

On the far side, the forest changed.

The weeping stopped. The trees stood dry and straight, almost respectful, and the path beneath their boots grew smooth as a stair. Ansel felt the shift in her bones before she understood it with her head — the particular pressure of approaching something important, the way the air thickened near a border crossing.

"We're close," she said.

"Very." Corvin checked the sky out of habit, though there was no sun to check it against. "Tonight, we sleep at the wood's edge. Tomorrow, we go under."

"Under." She rolled the word around. "Into the court."

"Into the court." He said it lightly. Too lightly. Like a man whistling past a grave he'd dug himself.

Ansel stopped walking.

Four days of shared fires. Four days of his dry humor and his careful silences and the way he always, *always* positioned himself between her and whatever waited ahead. She'd told herself it was protectiveness. Standing in the dry heart of the Weeping Wood, with the dead man's map warm against her ribs, another explanation occurred to her — one shaped like a warden who knew these woods too well, whose lip-chewing had started days before the tollgate came into view.

But the hounds had fed, the gate had closed, and Maren had perhaps five nights left, and suspicion was a luxury priced higher than memories.

"Corvin," she said instead, "when we get down there — if it comes to a choice between your skin and mine—"

"It won't."

"People say that right up until it does."

He looked at her then, really looked, and something moved across his face too fast to chart. "Get some sleep, Voss," he said gently. "Tomorrow you'll want your wits about you."

She made her bed against a root as wide as a table, wrapped her cloak tight, and dreamed — for the first time since Greyhollow — not of the dead man, but of a kitchen. A window. A woman with her back turned, humming.

When Ansel woke with tears on her cheeks, she could not have said why, and that frightened her more than the gate's teeth ever had.

Above her, Corvin sat awake by the cold fire, staring at his own open palm as if reading something written there.

As if checking whether his own name still fit it.
