## 6. The Court Beneath

The entrance to the court was a door that had no business existing.

It stood in the side of the mountain like a wound that had healed wrong — a slab of black stone set into grey granite, unmarked by trail or track, invisible from every angle except the one at which you already stood. Ansel knew this because she had spent the better part of an hour proving it. She'd circled the slope twice with her compass out, watching the needle spin like a drunk trying to find north, and every time she turned her back on that stretch of cliffside, the door politely ceased to be there.

"It's shy," Corvin said.

"It's *smug*," Ansel said. "There's a difference."

"There is," Corvin agreed gravely, "and I've been lied to about which one this is for forty years." He rolled his shoulder — it clicked in three places, a sound like someone snapping kindling — and looked at her. "Once we're through, you're Sera Vane. Broker of the Vane line, third daughter, dealing in mid-grade souls, nothing flashy. You haggled your way past the outer tolls; you're tired, rich, and bored. Wardens don't trouble bored brokers. Bored brokers mean money, and money means the wardens can go back to ignoring their posts."

"And you?"

"I'm your hired muscle. I stand behind you, look menacing, and say nothing."

"You? Say nothing?"

"I contain multitudes," Corvin said. "Most of them are quiet."

She looked down at herself. The clothes they'd taken from a smuggler cache two nights ago — waxed coat, high boots, a broker's chain of office that was mostly brass and entirely fake — hung well enough. Her hand still bore the collectors' ink-sigil from the ambush, a small dark whorl like a fingerprint made of night. She'd tried scrubbing it with snow, ash, and once, in desperation, pine resin. It had only gotten sharper.

Corvin claimed it was a courtesy mark. *The collateral*, he called it. She was trying very hard not to think about what that meant.

"Last chance to turn around," he said, and there was something under the dryness of it — something almost hopeful, as if part of him wanted her to take the exit.

"The ledger has my name in it," Ansel said. "I'm not turning around. I'm going to read my own entry, find out who sold me, and then I'm going to decide how I feel about it. Probably with fire involved."

"That's the spirit," Corvin said. "Though between us — the last person who went into that court planning to decide how they felt later didn't get the chance."

"Comforting."

"I'm told I have a gift."

He put his palm flat against the black stone. The sigil on Ansel's hand warmed, then burned, then — with a sensation like a page turning somewhere behind her ribs — the door opened inward, onto darkness, and the mountain breathed them in.

* * *

The descent took longer than any descent should.

Stairs cut from living rock spiraled down and down, lit by veins of pale lichen that glowed like drowned moonlight. The air grew warm and thick, smelling of iron and honey and something older underneath — the smell of a room that has been closed for centuries and is not pleased about being opened.

Ansel counted the turns out of habit. At forty she stopped counting, because the number had stopped meaning anything. At some point she realized the stairs were breathing. Slowly. Rhythmically. The whole stairwell rose and fell beneath their boots like the flank of an enormous sleeping animal, and neither she nor Corvin remarked on it, because remarking on it would have made it worse.

"How far down?" she asked finally, mostly to hear a voice.

"Far enough that the sun forgets you exist," Corvin said. "The court doesn't like daylight. Daylight is honest, and honesty makes the prices go down."

Ahead, the stairs opened at last into a cavern so vast that Ansel's cartographer's soul briefly left her body. She had drawn the Thornfells a hundred times — every pass, every ridge, every smuggler's cut — and never once suspected that under all of it sat this: a cavern the size of a valley, its ceiling lost in glowing haze, its floor carved into terraces that stepped down toward a central hall like rings of an amphitheater. And everywhere, everywhere, were people. Brokers in silk and rust-colored wool. Wardens in grey, faces smooth as river stones. Couriers carrying lacquered boxes that whispered when they passed.

And above it all, suspended in the cavern's heart on chains of green glass, hung a book.

Not a large book, from this distance. Perhaps the size of a paving stone. But light came off it the way heat comes off coals, and even across the whole breadth of the cavern, Ansel felt it looking at her.

"The ledger," Corvin murmured.

"I gathered."

"Don't stare at it. Staring is asking, and asking is bidding."

"What happens if I bid?"

"Then you owe it something," Corvin said, "and it always collects more than it quotes." He steered her gently by the elbow, turning her away from the hanging book with the practiced motion of a man redirecting a child from a cliff edge. "Wardens' station is along the eastern terrace. I'll draw the pair on duty — old debts, they'll want to collect from me personally. That gives you perhaps a quarter hour alone with the clerk."

"The clerk. What's the clerk like?"

"Small. Polite. Terrible." Corvin's jaw did something complicated. "It will answer any question truthfully, if you ask it precisely. Precision matters. Ask it 'who sold me' and it may say 'a buyer.' Ask it 'who entered my name into the ledger, and what was given in exchange,' and you'll get the whole ugly shape of it."

Ansel nodded. Then, because her hands wanted something to do besides shake: "And if the quarter hour runs out?"

"Run," Corvin said. "You're the only person alive who reads these roads. Even underground, the ways shift for you. Trust that. It's why they'll bargain with you eventually, whether either of us plans it or not."

He said *eventually* the way other men said *I'm sorry*. She chose not to examine that. There would be time for examining later, assuming there was a later, which was becoming an increasingly theoretical concept.

Corvin walked east, unhurried, hands loose at his sides, wearing the face of a man arriving exactly where he intended to be. Two wardens peeled off their post to meet him, and Ansel heard only the beginning of it — a warden saying, in a voice like gravel settling, *"You have a great deal of nerve"* — before the crowd closed over them like water.

She turned west, lifted her chin, and became Sera Vane: tired, rich, bored.

* * *

The clerk's alcove was tucked beneath the western terrace like a barnacle on a ship's hull, and the clerk itself was small and grey and folded up on a stool, sorting quills by length with fingers too long and too many-jointed. It looked up when she approached. Its face was pleasant the way an empty house is pleasant.

"Broker," it said. Its voice was soft as dust falling. "How may the ledger serve?"

"Sera Vane," Ansel said. "Vane line, third daughter. I'm here to verify a holding." Her heart was hammering; she made it sound like boredom. "A private verification. Discreet."

"Discretion is our oldest product." The clerk gestured to a stool opposite its own. Between them stood a lectern, and on the lectern lay a small slate board and a piece of chalk. "Names are not spoken aloud below. Write yours, and the ledger will confirm what is owed or held."

Ansel picked up the chalk. Her fingers remembered the ambush, the collector's thumb pressing the sigil into her skin, the hollow-eyed voice saying *collateral* like it was reading off a list. She wrote: *Sera Vane.*

The chalk-line sank into the slate and vanished. A pause. The clerk tilted its head, listening to something very far away, and its pleasant empty-house face flickered — the smallest crack, like ice over deep water.

"That is not the name the ledger knows you by," it said.

Ansel's mouth went dry. "Brokers use working names."

"Mm." The clerk folded its too-many fingers. "The ledger does not deal in working names. The ledger deals in truths. Shall I fetch the true entry?"

Every instinct she owned — every instinct that had kept her alive through twenty-seven years of orphanhood, of border towns and shifting roads and trusting no one further than arm's reach — screamed at her to write another name, any name, and walk away.

Instead she wiped the slate with her sleeve and wrote: *Ansel Voss.*

The chalk-line vanished. This time the clerk did not pause. This time the entire alcove paused — the lichen-light dimmed, the distant murmur of the market hushed, and overhead, faint but unmistakable, the great hanging book turned a page.

"Ah," the clerk said softly. "The collateral arrives."

"I'm not collateral." The words came out steadier than she deserved. "I'm here to read my entry. All of it. Who entered my name, when, and what was paid."

The clerk regarded her for a long moment. Then it reached beneath its stool and drew out a slim volume bound in bark-grey leather, and Ansel understood with a lurch that the great hanging ledger had daughters, thousands of them, and that hers had just been carried up from whatever deep place daughters sleep.

It opened the volume flat on the lectern and turned pages with terrible gentleness, and Ansel read upside-down, the way cartographers learn to, and there it was:

*Voss, Ansel. Entered: midwinter night, year of the Long Frost. Seller: [the name was written in a script she did not recognize, flowing and deliberate, like water deciding where to go]. Price tendered: one life entire, held in trust against maturity. Terms: the child lives free until claimed. Grace period: indefinite.*

Below it, in fresher ink, a single annotation: *Claim deferred. Interest accruing.*

Ansel stared at it until the letters swam. "Midwinter night," she said. "That's — that's the night I was born. My mother died that night. In a village called Lowmere, giving birth to a foundling nobody—"

"Lowmere," the clerk repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That is where the seller traveled from. On foot. Through the Long Frost. Carrying you." Its head tilted again. "You believe yourself a foundling."

"I *am* a foundling."

"You are a purchase," the clerk said, without cruelty — that was the worst of it, the terrible gentleness, the way it stated facts like reporting weather. "Someone loved you enough to walk through a killing winter to pay for your life. The price was steep. Souls are our only coin, and the seller gave—" it paused, consulting the page, and for the first time its pleasant face showed something that might, in a larger creature, have been discomfort. "The seller gave everything. Not their own soul. That would have been simple. They gave *all future claim upon you*. Every right, every debt, every bond of blood between you, surrendered at once. Such a sale leaves a wound in the world. We felt it in the ledgers for a season after. Pages wept ink for weeks."

The cavern seemed very far away. Ansel gripped the edge of the lectern because the floor had developed opinions about spinning.

Her whole life, she had built herself on one clean foundation: no one gave her away, no one sold her, she was simply *left* — an accident of weather and bad luck, owing nothing to anyone. It had been a hard foundation but an honest one, and she had stacked a whole self on top of it, stone by stubborn stone.

And now the clerk was telling her that the first act of her existence had been an act of love so enormous it had wounded reality. That someone had walked into a killing winter carrying her. That she had been bought the way you buy a candle in a storm — desperately, gratefully, at any price.

"They loved me," she said. Her voice sounded strange, borrowed. "Whoever it was. They loved me enough to—"

"The ledger records transactions," the clerk said, "not hearts. Though the two are often entangled, which is why our business thrives." It began, delicately, to close the volume. "Your grace period ends soon. Nine nights, I believe. The claim comes due at the next new moon. Unless—"

"Unless what?"

"Unless a settlement is reached." The clerk's eyes, small and grey and patient, settled on her. "You did not come all this way merely to read, broker-that-is-not-a-broker. You came to negotiate. Everyone who descends to the true ledger comes to negotiate. Shall I inform the wardens that the collateral wishes to speak with the Judge-King?"

Behind her, across the vast murmuring cavern, Ansel heard the beginning of shouting — Corvin's voice, cracking open like a whip, wardens' voices answering, the crowd surging toward the eastern terrace like a tide changing its mind.

Her quarter hour was up.

"Yes," Ansel said, standing, her legs unsteady under her, her mind moving faster than it ever had. "Tell the Judge-King the collateral wants to talk terms."

But the clerk was no longer looking at her. It was looking past her shoulder, down the terrace steps, where a figure was ascending out of the crowd — a girl, young, barefoot on cold stone, half-transparent, like a window with someone standing behind it.

The girl stopped. She looked at Ansel with eyes the exact grey-green of the dying stranger's, and said, in a voice like wind through the Weeping Wood:

"You have his map. Do you have his last words too?"
