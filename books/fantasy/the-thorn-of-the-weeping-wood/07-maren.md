## 7. Maren

Maren had learned to count the days by the dripping of the sap.

One drop for morning. One for evening. The trees above the cavern wept through the stone in slow amber threads that fell into channels carved along the floor, and the whole court drank the sound the way a drunkard drinks — endlessly, and without tasting it. She had counted nine hundred drips since they brought her below, or perhaps it was nine hundred thousand. Time down here was a ledger too, and hers had been written by someone else.

She sat in her alcove of the keeping — a hollow in the cavern wall lined with moss the color of old pewter, soft as a bed and just as indifferent. Half of her was there. That was the trouble. The other half had been sold off in pieces she could not name: her sixth summer, gone; the smell of bread baking, gone; the sound of a man humming while he mended nets, *mostly* gone, like a song heard through a wall.

She knew what remained of her was fading because the moss had begun to grow *through* her. Little silver threads at her ankles, patient as interest accruing.

"You're leaking," said the clerk.

The clerk was a tall, apologetic creature made mostly of ink stains and regret, and it came twice a day to check her entries. It held a book so large it required both arms and, Maren suspected, part of its soul.

"I'm not leaking," Maren said. "I'm evaporating. There's a difference. Leaking is dramatic. Evaporating is dignified."

"Ah." The clerk licked its thumb — a gesture that left a small black smudge on its tongue, which did not seem to bother it — and turned pages. "You've lost another memory overnight. The one about a dog."

Maren's chest went cold. "I didn't have a dog."

"You do. Did. There's an entry here — 'one dog, brown, devoted.'" The clerk peered at her with something almost like kindness. "It's been sold already, I'm afraid. To a merchant's daughter who wanted a childhood she never had. You wouldn't believe how many people want a childhood they never had."

"Well," Maren said, "now I'll spend eternity wondering if I loved it. Thank you. That's much worse than missing it."

"That's generally the case," the clerk agreed, and shuffled away, its pen scratching behind it like a small obedient animal.

Maren leaned back against the moss and closed her eyes.

Here is what she still had, and she guarded each piece like coins sewn into a hem:

A name. Maren.

A face. Her father's — though she did not know it was her father's anymore; it was simply a face now, kind-eyed, salt-rimed, humming over mended nets. The memory of *who he was* had been the first thing taken, all those years ago when the warden came with his ledger and his soft voice and his promise that the family would be *kept whole*, only distributed differently.

And a debt. Nine nights of grace, purchased with a soul, renewed again and again until the buyer ran out of soul to pay with. Someone had kept paying. For years. Someone had paid everything.

She had asked the clerk once, early on, before hope wore itself smooth: *"Who keeps paying?"*

And the clerk had looked genuinely sorrowful and said: *"I couldn't say. Entries are confidential. But whoever it is, they're nearly empty now. When the account runs dry, you go into the general stock. It's nothing personal. Nothing down here ever is."*

*Nothing personal.* That was the court's whole theology, Maren thought. Sin without sinners. Theft without thieves. Just ledgers, and clerks, and moss growing through your ankles while everyone apologizes beautifully.

* * *

The commotion started three alcoves down.

Maren heard it first as a change in the sap's rhythm — a stutter in the drip, as if even the trees were listening. Then voices, low and urgent. Then footsteps, quick and wrong, because nobody walked quickly down here except wardens chasing debts, and these steps were lighter, human, terrified.

She slid from her alcove into the corridor, moving the way she'd learned to move — half-here, half-not, a smudge of a person. The faded were invisible if they wished to be. It was the one mercy the court allowed, probably because it cost them nothing.

A woman rounded the corner.

She was mud-splattered and mountain-worn, with a cartographer's calloused hands and eyes that moved the way Ansel Voss's maps moved — always ahead of where she stood, reading the world like terrain. Maren knew her instantly, though they had never met. The dying stranger had carried her likeness folded against his heart, next to the map, and somewhere in what remained of Maren's memories there lived a description: *a woman who draws roads no one else can see.*

"You," Maren whispered, and then, absurdly, because she was her father's daughter even if she'd forgotten being anyone's daughter: "You're standing in the middle of the corridor. In the court. Under the Weeping Wood. Have you *met* this place?"

The woman spun, a knife already half-drawn, then stopped. Stared. "You can see me?"

"I live here," Maren said. "Sort of. About sixty percent of me lives here. The rest is spread across various accounts." She tilted her head. "You're her. The cartographer. He showed me your drawing. Well — he showed someone the drawing, years ago, and I remember being told about it, which is nearly the same thing down here."

Ansel Voss crossed to her in three strides and took her hands, and Maren felt it like sunlight through fog — warmth arriving from very far away.

"He's dead," Ansel said. "The man who gave me the map. He died at my door. He said nine nights. He said—" Her voice cracked, steadied. "He said he was buying time for his sister."

Something in Maren went very quiet.

"Sister," she repeated.

"That's what he called it. That's what Corvin said. His sister—"

"Corvin." The name landed in Maren like a dropped stone into a well, and the well went on echoing long after. She pulled her hands free, slowly, and pressed them flat against the cavern wall to steady herself. The moss accepted her weight gratefully. Everything down here accepted things gratefully; it was how they got you.

"You know him," Ansel said. It wasn't a question.

"I know the name." Maren laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and glassy. "There was a warden. Years ago. He came to our house on the coast with a ledger under his arm and kindness all over his face, and he explained — so gently, so *reasonably* — that my father owed a debt, and that debts to the court are paid in what a person carries, and that a child's memories weigh more than you'd think. Fresh ones. Unspent." Her voice flattened. "He processed the sale himself. He wrote my name in the book while my mother screamed, and then he apologized. That's the detail I keep. Everyone down here does terrible things, but he *apologized*. He made it feel like weather. Like grief was just something that happened to you, rather than something done to you."

"His name," Ansel said quietly. "The warden's name."

"They called him Corvus, then. Corvin, after he fell. After his 'betrayal.'" Maren watched Ansel's face and saw the exact moment the ground opened beneath her — the flinch, the denial rising and dying in the same breath. "Oh," Maren said softly. "Oh, you didn't know. You've been walking beside him. Trusting him. Sharing firelight with the man who sold me."

"He said—" Ansel's jaw worked. "He told me he betrayed the court. That he freed souls, that they cast him out, that he'd spent twenty years—"

"And maybe some of it's even true," Maren said. "That's the worst of it. Down here, the lies are load-bearing. Take one out and the whole story collapses." She wrapped her arms around herself. "But ask yourself what a man gains by betraying the court and staying useful to it. Ask why the collector's hounds let *him* walk. Ask why a warden who fell would know exactly which roads shift and exactly which don't."

Ansel said nothing. Her hand had drifted, unconsciously, to the sigil-marked palm — the mark the collectors had given her, the word *collateral* carved in ink. Maren looked at it and understood, suddenly, the shape of the whole machine.

"And him," Maren said. "My father. The man who died at your door."

"Your—" Ansel stopped. Started again. "He told me he was her brother. Maren's brother. He said—"

"He would have." Maren's eyes stung, and she was surprised by it; she hadn't thought she had enough left to cry with. "Because 'brother' buys sympathy and 'father' buys questions. Because he sold pieces of himself first, long ago, to keep the payments coming, and one of the pieces he sold was the truth of what he was to me. He literally paid away the memory of being my father, Ansel. He's been dying by inches for fifteen years, sending soul after soul into that account, and near the end he couldn't even remember *why* he was doing it. Only that he had to. Only a debt with the reason scraped out." She laughed again, wetter this time. "That's what this place does. It doesn't take your love. It takes the *name* of your love, and leaves the love running loose with nowhere to live. He found you because some scrap of him remembered you mattered. A face in a fold of paper. That's all he had left of me. That's all he had left of *himself*."

Ansel had gone pale as the pewter moss. She braced a hand against the wall, breathing carefully, the way people breathe when they are deciding whether to break or to burn.

"The ledger," she said at last. "Your entry. Your father's entry. Is there anything in it about—"

"Everything's in it," Maren said. "That's the horror and the hope. Nothing down here is destroyed. It's all just... filed." She glanced up the corridor, toward the distant amber glow of the counting hall, where the great ledger sat open beneath a canopy of weeping roots. "If you wanted to unmake what was done — truly unmake it — you'd have to reach the book. And the book is guarded by everything this place has, which is considerable, because the book is the place."

* * *

They crouched together in the shadow of an alcove when the wardens' patrol passed — four of them, tall and gray, their faces smooth as unwritten pages. Maren had faded herself nearly to transparency, and she pulled Ansel's sleeve until the woman dimmed her lantern and held still.

When the footsteps faded, neither of them moved right away.

"I should kill him," Ansel said finally. Her voice was level in the way water is level — calm until you notice how deep. "Corvin. When I see him again. I want you to know that's where I am. Twenty years of firelight stories and every single one might be a receipt."

"Yes," Maren said simply.

"But he knows the court. And I need the court broken open, and you need to be out of it, and I can't do either alone." Ansel exhaled slowly. "So I'm going to walk back out there and stand next to him and let him think I believe him, and I am going to hate every minute, and I need you to tell me if that makes me a coward."

Maren considered it. Fading, file-bound, sixty percent present, she considered it with everything she had left.

"It makes you my father," she said. "He spent fifteen years smiling at the people who owned him because it was the only way to keep paying. If you can do it for nine nights, you can do it knowing why."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. Something passed between them that had no name in any ledger — recognition, maybe, or inheritance.

Then Ansel stood, drew her hood up, and turned toward the counting hall. "Stay hidden. Stay *here*, whatever happens. I'm going to get us both out."

"You should know," Maren said, and her voice caught on the words like a nail on cloth, "the clerk mentioned something, last visit. The Judge-King has been asking about you. By name. *Ansel Voss.* He doesn't ask about people unless they're already in the book."

Ansel paused at the edge of the light.

"I'm not in the book," she said. "I'd know. I've seen what's in the book."

"No," Maren agreed softly. "You'd know." She watched the cartographer walk away, toward the ledger, toward the truth, toward whatever was written in her line. "You'd know exactly."
