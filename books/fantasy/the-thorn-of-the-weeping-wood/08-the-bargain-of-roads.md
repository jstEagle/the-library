## 8. The Bargain of Roads

Ansel had killed men in her imagination more times than she could count — usually over bad coffee or a client who insisted the mountains were "more or less symmetrical." She had never actually considered doing it until Corvin sat down across from her in the dark of the way-station and said, "You look like a woman doing arithmetic."

"I am," she said. "I'm working out how many ways there are to kill a warden in a room with one door."

"One door," Corvin said, "but you drew three on that map of yours. Don't think I didn't notice."

She hadn't drawn them. Not yet. But it was flattering to be caught ahead of schedule.

Maren slept in the corner, wrapped in Ansel's coat, breathing like something that had forgotten how. Half-faded, the clerk had called her. Ansel kept looking at her to confirm she was still solid — the curve of her shoulder, the chapped hands of someone who had worked at something real once, before the court took the reality out of her. Every time Ansel looked, Maren was still there. Every time, some small animal inside Ansel unclenched by a single degree.

Corvin watched her watching. He had the decency to look ashamed, which was more than she'd expected from him, though not nearly as much as she intended to collect.

"Say it," she said. "All of it. In order. Leave nothing out for my comfort, because I promise you I will notice, and I will be *unkind* about it."

* * *

He told it plainly, which was the only thing he did well anymore, she thought. Twenty-two years ago he had been Warden of the Eastern Vein, keeper of the toll-roads, and a man named Aldric Vey had come to him with an infant in his arms and a desperate arithmetic of his own: his soul against his daughter's nine days old, or his daughter's soul against his own freedom, and no third line on the page.

"You processed the sale," Ansel said.

"I wrote the entry." His voice didn't waver, but his hands did; he folded them flat on his knees like a man pinning something down. "Aldric Vey. Sold: one daughter, unnamed. Price: his own soul, deferred. Term: the girl lives free until the debt comes due." He looked up. "I told him there was a third option. There always is. The court just never shows it on the ledger."

"And the third option was?"

"A warden who looked the other way while a father carried his daughter out through the Weeping Wood and gave her to a cartographer's family in Greyhollow, with a false name and a true blessing." A pause. "And then a warden who took payment from the court anyway, years later, when they came asking where the child had gone, because I was tired and poor and they offered me back my own name, which I had sold them long before."

Ansel sat very still. Outside the way-station's broken shutter, wind moved through the pass with a sound like pages turning.

"So you betrayed us twice," she said. "Once for money. Once before that, for love, which is somehow worse, because it means you knew exactly what you were selling."

"Yes."

"Good." She stood. "Now get up. You're going to help me rob them."

Corvin blinked. "Rob—"

"The court. Think, man, use whatever's left of that warden brain. What do they actually have?" She began pacing, and the pacing helped, the way drawing helped, her thoughts finding their roads. "Souls? They don't *have* souls, they have *paper*. Debts. Entries. The whole mountain is a counting house sitting on top of a vault full of nothing but ink. And what does the ink buy them?"

"Power," Corvin said slowly.

"No. *Roads*." She stopped. "Every hidden way through Thornfell, every smuggler's path, every door that isn't a door — they hold them all. That's why collectors can appear anywhere, why debts come due wherever you run. They own the between places. And I—" she tapped her own chest, "—I am the only person alive who can chart those roads without their permission. The dying man's map proved it. Your court spent twenty-two years unable to find one infant because she traveled with me."

Understanding moved across Corvin's scarred face like weather. "They can't map you."

"They've been trying since I was nine days old. So we're going to walk into their court, and I'm going to offer the Judge-King a trade so obscene he'll have to hear it to the end." She smiled, and felt her own smile and did not like it much, but kept it anyway. "Me. The living key to every shifting road. Freely given, freely entered in the ledger. In exchange: Maren's soul returned whole, both ledgers burned — hers and mine — and every road they hold opened, forever, to anyone who walks them."

"That would end them," Corvin said quietly. "The monopoly is the court. Without it, they're just clerks in a cave."

"Yes. Which is why he'll say yes first and renege second, and why you're going to stand behind me the entire time ready for exactly that." She turned to face him fully. "This isn't forgiveness, Corvin. When this is done, if any of me is left to carry a grudge, I'll carry it. But right now your guilt happens to point in a useful direction, and I intend to spend it down to the last coin."

For the first time since she'd known him, Corvin laughed — a short, cracked sound, like a door opening on a cold morning. "Gods help me," he said, "you'd have made a fine broker."

"No," said Ansel. "A fine thief. There's a difference. Brokers believe in the ledger."

* * *

The Judge-King received them in a hall carved from the mountain's living bone, lit by lamps that burned without oil and cast shadows that arrived slightly before their owners did. Ansel walked in with her chin level and her heart hammering a rhythm she privately categorized as *entirely professional*.

The Judge-King was not what the stories promised. No crown of thorns, no throne of teeth. He looked like a tired accountant with too many rings, and his voice, when it came, came from everywhere at once, including, unpleasantly, from inside Ansel's own teeth.

"The collateral returns," he said. "How enterprising. Most debtors run until we collect. You ran *toward* us."

"I came to renegotiate." She let the silence stretch, because she had learned from watching Corvin that power in this place lived entirely in whoever spoke second. "Your ledgers hold two entries of interest. Maren Vey. And Ansel Voss." She heard Corvin's breath catch beside her — she hadn't told him that part either, the clerk's claim about the night of her birth, the buyer who loved her enough to pay. She wasn't sure she believed it. She wasn't sure it mattered. "One soul, half-faded, worth almost nothing now. One soul, whole, unspent, and carrying something your court has hunted for twenty-two years."

"And that is?"

"Me." She spread her hands, the ink-sigil still dark on her palm. "Not my soul. My *self*. Every shifting road through Thornfell lives in here" — she touched her temple — "charted and recharting. You cannot map me. I've checked your work. But I can give myself freely, entered whole, body and road together, and your monopoly becomes permanent. Eternal. No more wardens chasing rumors. Just ask me, and every hidden way opens."

The shadows leaned in. Somewhere far above, the mountain made a low sound, like a singer clearing her throat.

"And the price?"

"Maren Vey's entry dissolved. Soul restored, whole, unfaded. Both ledgers — hers and mine — struck clean. Not transferred. *Struck*. And the roads themselves freed to all walkers, as part of the consideration." She held his gaze, which felt like holding her hand over a candle and insisting the flame was warm. "You may verify my offer however you like. Send your readers into my mind. I want witnesses. I want it *entered*, tonight, before I lose my nerve or you find a loophole."

The Judge-King smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has just been handed a map to everything.

"Witnesses," he agreed pleasantly, "and a scribe. We are a court of law, after all."

Behind her, barely audible, Corvin murmured: "That was too easy."

"Yes," Ansel said, smiling at the Judge-King with every tooth she owned. "I know. Get ready to run."
