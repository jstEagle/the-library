## 9. Where the Mountain Sings

The Judge-King's acceptance lasted exactly as long as it took him to smile.

Ansel would remember that later — the smile itself, a slow unfurling like frost creeping across a windowpane, beautiful and entirely wrong. The bargain had been struck in the great cavern where the court held its sessions, beneath chandeliers of dripping stone and the soft blue glow of the ledger, which hung in the air like a captive aurora, its thousands of names turning slowly page over page. She had spoken her terms clearly: herself, the living key to every shifting road in Thornfell, freely given, in exchange for Maren's soul released whole and both ledgers burned of their entries.

The Judge-King had said yes. He had even said *freely given*, tasting the words like a man sampling wine he intended to water.

"Then we're done," Ansel said, and reached for Maren's hand.

The girl stood beside the dais, solid now, her edges no longer bleeding into the air the way they had in the keeping-rooms below. Color sat in her cheeks again. She looked at Ansel with an expression caught between gratitude and something older and sadder — the look of someone who had spent years learning not to hope, and didn't quite know what to do with it now that it arrived uninvited.

"Not quite," said the Judge-King.

Ansel felt it before she understood it: a tug behind her sternum, gentle as a fisherman testing a line. The ink-sigil on her palm — the mark the collectors had branded there on the first road, the one Corvin had sworn was only a tracker — began to warm.

"The bargain," she said carefully, "was myself in exchange for—"

"For your knowledge of the roads. Yes." The Judge-King descended one step from his throne of woven roots, and the whole cavern leaned in with him; she could feel the mountain adjusting its weight, the way a cat shifts before it pounces. "And so you shall give it. All of it. Every road, every hidden way, every secret pass your clever hands have ever charted — poured out of you, root and branch, until nothing remains but the vessel. The vessel we keep."

Cold walked up Ansel's spine. "That wasn't the terms."

"The terms were mine to interpret. They always were." He spread his hands, and around them the wardens moved — silent, gray-robed, faces like unfinished sketches. "A soul freely offered cannot be refused. But a soul, little cartographer, is more than a name in a ledger. It is everything a body knows." His smile widened by one tooth. "You brought us the map. We simply intend to keep the whole book."

Beside her, very quietly, Corvin said: "Run."

She ran.

* * *

They ran because running was the only thing the court did not own.

Ansel had charted smuggler roads for eleven years; she knew the difference between a path that existed and a path that merely *believed* it existed, and the corridors of the court beneath the mountain were the second kind — passages that held their shape only so long as no one doubted them. So she did not doubt. She seized Maren's wrist and Corvin's sleeve and hurled all three of them down a side gallery that, three heartbeats earlier, had been solid bedrock, and it became a corridor because she insisted it was one, and behind them the wardens' footsteps struck stone that hadn't finished deciding to be stone.

"This way," she gasped, "is a road—"

"It's a wall," Corvin said.

"It's whatever I say it is. That's the trick of this entire mountain. Nobody maps Thornfell. Thornfell maps itself, and it maps whatever it's afraid of." She hauled them left through a seam of blue light, past shelves of bottled voices, past a warden who turned too slowly, his sketch-face smearing as he reached. "It's afraid of me."

The sigil on her palm burned white-hot. Somewhere above, or below — direction meant nothing down here — a bell began to toll, deep and slow, and with each stroke the tunnels around them shuddered like a sleeping animal being prodded.

"That's the mountain waking," Corvin shouted. "When the Judge-King reneges, he doesn't send wardens after long. He closes the ways. All of them."

"Then we'd better be quick about disagreeing with him."

Maren laughed — actually laughed, breathless and wild, and for one absurd moment Ansel thought: *she's her father's daughter after all.* Then they plunged into the first true road, the one Ansel had scrawled into memory from the dying man's folded map, and the world outside fell away.

* * *

The smuggler roads were hers in a way nothing else had ever been.

Not owned — never owned; ownership was the court's disease. But known. Loved, even, in the way she loved the scratch of good ink and the smell of fresh paper and the particular silence of a high pass at dawn. She read the roads now the way other people read weather: here a bend that wanted to become a staircase, there a tunnel dreaming of being a river. She talked to them under her breath — *not yet, hold, turn* — and they obeyed, grudgingly, like old horses who respected the hand that fed them.

Behind them, the mountain sang.

That was the only word for it. A vast harmonic moan rolling up through the rock, the sound of a thousand sealed passages groaning open and shut at once as the court tried to slam every door in Thornfell. Dust sifted from ceilings. Stalactites snapped off and shattered like dropped glassware. Twice a passage died behind them mid-stride, the road curling inward on itself with a sound like a swallowed scream, and twice Ansel wrenched open another one ahead by sheer stubborn certainty.

"Left," she said. "There's a fault line that runs to the old tollgate. It's not on any map."

"How do you know it exists?" Maren panted.

"Because I just invented it, and the mountain believes me."

Corvin made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by terror. "Eleven years I wondered why the court feared cartographers. Eleven years." He glanced back at the collapsing dark. "They don't fear the maps. They fear the mappers."

"They should have feared me sooner," Ansel said, and meant it, and felt something fierce and unfamiliar kindle in her chest — not dread, not anymore. Joy. Terrible, blazing joy, the joy of a hunted thing that has finally decided to be the hunt.

The fault-line road narrowed. The singing rose. And ahead, glowing faintly in the dark like a held breath, stood the last gate.

* * *

It was the tollgate — the same one, though they'd left it leagues and a lifetime behind them — or its twin, or perhaps gates like this grew wherever the mountain needed a throat. Its teeth hung from an arch of black wood, and beyond it Ansel could see it: moonlight. Actual moonlight, silvering a slope of scree, the real night sky of the world above.

The gate was closing. Slowly, joint by joint, like a patient animal going to sleep.

"We'll never all make it," Corvin said.

"Don't be dramatic, there's time—"

"There isn't." He stepped past her, and when he turned, his face in the gate's glow looked older than she'd ever seen it — older than disgrace, older than betrayal, older than the young warden in the dying man's memories who had stamped a ledger and ruined two lives to save his own skin. "The gate needs holding from this side while you pass. Someone has to stand in the teeth and keep them honest. And someone owes a debt, Ansel Voss, that nine nights of guide work was never going to cover."

"You processed the sale," she said. Flat. Not a question. "You can pay for it with more than a gate."

"I know." Something that was almost a smile. "That's rather the point."

He put his scarred hand flat against the arch, and the teeth stilled. The mountain's song changed pitch — furious now, dissonant, the sound of a ledger discovering an entry it could not collect.

"Go," Corvin said. "Take her up the scree and don't look back. If you look back, the gate will know you doubt it, and gates feed on doubt."

"What happens to you?"

"Same thing that happens to everyone who deals with this court, eventually. I get collected." He shrugged, easy as a man discussing weather. "But I'll be the first one in history who walked in owing them and walks out of the story owing nothing. There are worse fates. I've checked."

Maren touched his arm. "You were the warden," she said quietly. "You were there. When my father—"

"I was there." His voice cracked clean down the middle. "And I have been walking back toward this moment for thirty years. Let me arrive."

Ansel took Maren's hand. The gate's teeth hung an arm's length apart, trembling, and beyond them the moonlight lay across the scree like a road home.

"Corvin," she said. "Your name. What did you pay at the tollgate?"

He looked surprised — then pleased, genuinely pleased, as if she'd asked after an old friend. "Wouldn't you like to know."

"Tell me anyway."

He leaned close, and in the roar of the singing mountain, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, he gave it to her — four syllables, worn smooth as river stones, a name that had belonged to a boy before the court got hold of him. Then he straightened, squared his shoulders against the closing dark, and set his back to the gate like a man leaning into a storm he had finally stopped running from.

"Now go," he said. "Outrun your fate. It's slower than it looks."

Ansel went.

* * *

They came up out of the mountain into moonlight and cold clean air, and behind them Thornfell howled — a long, ragged, furious note that shook loose stones down the scree and sent birds up screaming from the Weeping Wood. The gate's mouth glowed once, gold-white, like a coal breathed upon.

Then the mountain closed its throat, and the singing stopped, and the night was suddenly, enormously quiet.

Maren collapsed onto a boulder, laughing and crying in equal measure, whole and breathing and free. Ansel stood looking back at the unremarkable slope of rock where the gate had been, her palm still burning, four syllables resting on her tongue like a coin she hadn't yet decided to spend.

Somewhere below, a debt had just been paid in full.

And somewhere in the ledger of the court — if any ledger survived what Ansel had done to the roads on her way out — two names had gone strangely, permanently blank.

Maren wiped her face. "What do we do now?" she asked. "I don't remember anything. I don't remember *him*. I don't remember—" Her voice caught. "I don't even remember what was taken from me."

Ansel opened her mouth to answer, and found she didn't know her own.

Because the last thing she had promised the mountain — screamed into the fault line as the passages died behind her — had not been a threat. It had been a price. *Take my name instead of my self*, she had told it, *take what the ledger holds*, and the mountain, greedy and dying and eager to keep *something* of her, had accepted.

She looked down at her hands, the cartographer's hands, steady as ever.

She could not recall what she had been called before tonight.

"Maren," she said slowly, trying the girl's name like a key in an unfamiliar lock. "Do you happen to know who I am?"

Maren blinked at her, moonlit and bewildered. "No," she said. "Should I?"
