## 10. Unmapped

The sun was the first thing that convinced me we had made it out.

Not the light itself — I had seen light in the court beneath the mountain, thin and borrowed and the color of old bone — but the *weight* of it. Morning sun has weight. It presses on your shoulders like a hand from someone who loves you and isn't asking for anything. The court had never once put its hand on me without expecting payment.

I sat on a flat stone at the mouth of the pass with Maren's head in my lap, and I watched the sun climb over Thornfell like it had all the time in the world, and so did we. That was the strange part. Nine nights of running, of collapsing roads behind us, of Corvin's voice fading down a corridor of screaming stone as he held the last gate — nine nights with no room to breathe — and now the world had gone soft and slow as honey off a spoon.

Maren breathed. That was enough. For a while, breathing was going to be enough for both of us.

She woke near midday, the way people wake when nothing is hunting them: gradually, crossly, with one eye open first.

"Where," she said, "am I lying on a rock."

"You're welcome," I said.

"I was dreaming." She sat up too fast, winced, pressed her palm between her brows. "Someone was singing. Under the ground. It was awful and beautiful and—" She stopped. Frowned at the mountains as if they'd said something rude. "No. It's gone. Why can't I hold onto it?"

"Because you're not supposed to." I kept my voice easy. Easy cost effort; I'd had practice by then. "You slept badly. You dreamed strangely. Now you're awake, and there's a stream about twenty paces east, and if you're very charming I'll share the bread."

She looked at me for a long moment. There was something in her face — a hollow where a memory should have sat, the way a tooth socket feels after the pulling — but she didn't press at it. People are wise that way without knowing it. They leave the sore places alone.

"You're the map woman," she said finally. "The one Da— the one who came to us. Ansel."

"That's me." The name sat oddly in my mouth, like a coat borrowed from a friend. Not wrong, exactly. Just not mine, not anymore. My name had died in the ledger along with every other record of what I'd been willing to pay. What remained was this: a word I'd chosen to answer to, because a person needs something to shout across a campsite.

"Maren," she said, and offered her hand, formal as a duchess, which made me laugh for the first time in what felt like years.

We ate the bread by the stream. She talked. That was the miracle of her, I decided — the court had kept her half-faded for years, had sipped at her like wine, and here she was talking with her hands, telling me about Greyhollow, about the tanner's dog she'd befriended, about how she intended to eat an entire pie in one sitting and no one could stop her. She remembered the world above. She remembered love, in the general way — that she'd been loved, that someone had spent everything to buy her time. The specifics were gone. The court had taken them as its final spite, or perhaps as its final mercy; with that place it was always hard to tell the difference, and I wasn't going to be the one to tell her which gift she'd received.

I would carry both halves. That was the arrangement now, whether I liked it or not. She got the life; I got the ledger of it, written nowhere, weighing everything.

* * *

That evening we made camp in the lee of a hill, and I took out my kit, because some habits are load-bearing.

I had no commissions pending. The road behind us was rubble; the court beneath the mountain was sealed, or buried, or sulking — I genuinely did not know which, and I had decided to treat all three possibilities with the same respectful silence one gives a sleeping dog. No one would ever again need me to chart the smuggler roads. The only person alive who could read them had fed them into the dark one passage at a time while the mountain sang its fury through her teeth.

So I took out a fresh sheet of good paper, and I sharpened my pen, and I sat there like an idiot.

Maren watched me over the fire. "What are you making?"

"A map."

"Of what? We're in the middle of nowhere."

"That," I said, "is rather the point."

Here is the trouble with being a cartographer whose entire self was built out of other people's hidden ways: take away the roads, and what's left? I had spent my life believing I was the only thing standing steady while the world shifted around me. Then I'd learned I was inked into a ledger before I drew my first breath — bought, priced, collateral, a debt walking around in boots — and the steadiness had turned out to be just another kind of shifting, slower than the rest. Every road I'd ever drawn, I'd drawn to prove I belonged somewhere. To whom, I couldn't have said. A clerk beneath a mountain, maybe. A mother I'd paid a memory to forget.

I dipped the pen. I held it over the page.

And I drew nothing.

The blankness stared back at me, white and patient, and after a while I understood it wasn't emptiness at all. It was the first honest map I'd ever made. Every other chart I'd drawn had lied a little — had pretended the world would hold still, that the passes would stay where I put them, that a life could be fixed in lines and sold to the highest bidder. This one told the truth: here is the territory, and it is yours, and it goes everywhere, and no one has marked the way for you because no one owns you enough to try.

"Ansel?" Maren said.

I blew on the ink — all none of it — and handed the page across the fire. "Directions," I said. "To a life neither of us owes anyone. Follow any line you like. They all lead out."

She turned the blank sheet over, frowning, certain she was missing something, and I let her. Some gifts take a decade to unwrap. But she smoothed it carefully against her knee, and folded it with the reverence people usually reserve for letters from the dead, and tucked it inside her jacket over her heart, which was more or less where I'd intended it.

"The pie town is south," I added. "Two days' walk. I'm told the crusts are criminal."

"Criminal how?"

"Crimes of passion. You'll see."

* * *

We walked south together, because it turned out neither of us had anywhere else to be, and because grief travels lighter in company. She didn't know yet what she'd lost. I knew exactly, and I found that saying nothing was its own kind of payment — the last toll I'd owe anyone.

On the third morning, at a crossroads outside a town whose name I never learned, a peddler's cart rattled past, and the peddler called out cheerfully, asking if we wanted news from the north.

"No," Maren said.

"Yes," I said, because I am apparently incapable of leaving a door entirely shut.

"They say the mountain's quiet," he offered, shrugging. "Smugglers swear the old roads are gone — whole passes closed up like scars. Half the guild's in a panic. The other half says good riddance." He tipped his hat and rolled on toward market, jingling.

Maren looked at me. "You wouldn't know anything about that."

"Me? I'm nobody. Ask anyone." I meant it as a joke. It came out as a creed, and I found, examining it, that it fit better than anything I'd worn before. Unmapped. Unowned. A woman with a borrowed name, a blank page in her pocket, and every road ahead unmarked — which is another way of saying *open*.

We went on toward the pies. Behind us the mountains stood silent, keeping whatever they kept. And somewhere under my ribs, faint as sap rising in spring, I felt the smallest tug — northward, downward, the shape of a gate held shut by a man who'd finally paid his debts — and I let it pass through me and out, the way weather does.

Then I took Maren's arm, chose a direction purely because the light was nice that way, and stepped off the edge of every map that had ever tried to hold me.

It was, I am pleased to report, the easiest road I ever walked.
