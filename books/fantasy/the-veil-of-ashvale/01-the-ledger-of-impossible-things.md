## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to a stub the color of old bone, and Rosalind Brightwater still had not finished the entry.

They sat in the counting room at the back of the Ashvale guildhouse, where the walls were lined with ledgers going back two hundred years—the spines cracked, the ink faded from iron-gall brown to the pale ghost-color of forgotten promises. The air smelled of tallow and dust and the faint mineral tang of the river stone the building was cut from. Somewhere above, the floorboards creaked as Sable came home over the rooftops, quiet as falling snow, and somewhere below, the kettle in the kitchen sang its one thin note.

Rosalind dipped the pen. Wrote nothing. Dipped it again.

The problem sat before them on the desk: a small glass bottle, stoppered with wax the shade of dusk, which hummed when you held it near your ear. Inside, if you knew how to listen—and guild-trained ears learned quickly—a lullaby moved like smoke in water. It had been traded to them three nights ago by a fisherman's widow who could no longer bear to hear it, and now it needed recording, appraising, shelving. Standard work. Bread-and-butter work for a guild that trafficked in what no court would touch.

And yet Rosalind's hand would not steady.

*Item:* one bottled lullaby, provenance Marrowgate ferry-road. *Seller:* Wren Aldous, widow, paid in full and fairly. *Contents verified by listening.* *Condition—*

Condition. Rosalind pressed the bottle to their ear again.

The lullaby inside had changed.

Three nights ago it had been a mother's voice, low and looping, singing about a boat and the moon on the water. Tonight the melody was the same but something under it was wrong—a hollowness, a place where a note should have been and wasn't, like a tooth missing from a comb. As if the song itself were forgetting how it went.

"You're still at it."

Corvane filled the doorway the way he always did, too broad for the frame, silver-shot beard trimmed short, his guildmaster's chain hidden under his coat as it always was because *we stay invisible, Brightwater, that's the whole bloody point of us*. He carried two cups of tea and the expression he wore when he'd been standing outside a door deciding whether to knock for longer than any honest man would admit.

"The entry isn't done," Rosalind said.

"I can see that. You've written *condition* four times." He set a cup at their elbow without asking. "Four times, Rosalind."

"Verification takes as long as verification takes."

"Mm." Corvane lowered himself into the chair across the desk—it groaned under him, everything groaned under him—and looked at the bottle with the flat professional attention of a man who had spent forty years learning not to flinch at impossible things. "Sing it to me."

"It's not mine to sing."

"Then tell me what you hear."

Rosalind hesitated. In the guildhouse, hesitation cost more than error; errors could be corrected in the ledger, but hesitation meant you were hiding something, and hiding was the one crime the rules did not forgive. *Trade fairly. Record everything. Stay invisible.* Three laws, carved into the lintel over the front door in letters worn smooth by a century of hands touching them for luck.

"There's a gap in it," they said finally. "A missing note. It wasn't there three nights ago."

Something crossed Corvane's face—quick, controlled, gone. Rosalind had watched the man negotiate with ghost-brokers and demon-tailors and once, memorably, with a tax assessor, and they knew every one of his faces. This one he didn't know he was wearing.

"Put it in the vault," he said. "Deep shelf. Not for trade."

"That's against the second rule. We record everything, we sell everything fairly—"

"We record everything," Corvane said. "I never said we sell everything. There are things in that vault that have been waiting thirty years for the right buyer, Brightwater, and some of them will wait thirty more. Write it down. Shelf it. Move on."

It was the *move on* that told Rosalind there was more to move on from.

* * *

The vault was beneath the counting room, down a stair cut into the river stone, past shelves of things that made the candleflame lean away from them. A jar of last words. A key that opened only regretted doors. Seven letters addressed to people not yet born, sealed in wax that wept. Rosalind shelved the lullaby between a mirror that showed tomorrow's weather and a small tin of borrowed courage, wrote the entry fair and full in their own neat hand, and stood a moment in the cold dark listening to the building settle around its secrets.

Their oath-mark ached.

Not burned—ached, deep, the way an old break aches before rain. It sat on the inside of their left wrist, a small gray spiral like a fingerprint pressed into the skin by something vast and patient, the mark Veyra set on every soul who swore to the silent god. The god of oaths kept and debts honored. The god who had not spoken in eleven months.

That was the thing under the routine like a stone under shallow water. Everyone in the guildhouse felt it; nobody said it. Veyra had never been a chatty god—even in the old stories the divine silence was part of the doctrine, *the god speaks through the keeping, not the telling*—but silence had a texture, and this silence was different. This was the silence of a room after someone has quietly left it. Eleven months of praying into a well and hearing no rope pay out, no splash, nothing. Eleven months of oath-marks that warmed no more than ordinary skin.

Rosalind pressed their thumb against the spiral until it hurt.

*If you're there,* they thought, in the wordless way the faithful learn to think at gods, *tell me what I'm supposed to do about the lullaby.*

Nothing answered, which was answer enough, and worse than nothing would have been.

* * *

Supper was beans and black bread and Sable narrating her week with her boots up on the bench, which was how she narrated everything. She was small and quick and pretty in the way a knife is pretty, and she had the particular stillness of a person who spent her working hours inside other people's heads.

"So the mark says the memory's genuine," she was saying, turning a spoon of stolen—legitimately purchased, she'd insist—honey over her bread. "Genuine grief, genuine guilt, the whole vintage. But here's what I don't like. The edges are chewed. Something's been at it. Like—" she hunted for it—"like mice in a granary. Not eating the grain. Just... tasting."

"Tasting," repeated Tam, the guild's bookkeeper's apprentice, seventeen and permanently alarmed.

"Memories get chewed on all the time, girl. Dreams gnaw them, grief gnaws them. But these weren't one man's memories. I pulled from six different clients this month. All of them chewed the same way. Same teeth." She looked around the table, and her eyes stopped on Rosalind a half-beat too long. "Same bite."

The kitchen went quiet except for the kettle, which had never really stopped singing.

"Borderlands?" Rosalind asked.

"Every one of them from within a day's ride of the border wards." Sable said it lightly, the way she said dangerous things, dressing them in flippancy so they'd pass inspection. "Probably nothing. Probably just the wards doing ward things. They've been humming louder all autumn, ask any farmer."

But her eyes hadn't moved off Rosalind, and Rosalind understood why when Corvane spoke from the head of the table, mild as milk:

"Brightwater. Walk with me."

* * *

They walked the top corridor, where the windows faced east toward the border hills, black against a sky that had been strangely clear all season. Corvane walked slowly, hands clasped behind his back, and for a long while said nothing, which from Corvane was a speech.

"Eleven months," he said at last.

Rosalind kept their face level. "Sir?"

"Don't sir me. Eleven months since Veyra went quiet, and you've said nothing, and I've said nothing, and we've both been very polite about it, and meanwhile my best ledger-keeper writes *condition* four times and can't finish an entry." He stopped at the window. Below, in the yard, the child-shaped shadow of Tam was hauling water, and the light of the kitchen fell across the stones like spilled gold. "I'm asking you plainly, Rosalind. What does your mark tell you?"

The truth rose in them like floodwater. They held it back out of habit, out of law—*stay invisible*—and then heard Corvane say, gently, "Invisibility is a tool, Brightwater. Not a tomb. Speak."

"It tells me nothing," Rosalind said. "That's what it tells me. For fifteen years it has been—" they searched— "warm. Not loud. Veyra doesn't do loud. But warm. Like carrying a coal in your wrist that never goes out. And eleven months ago the coal went out, and I keep telling myself that's doctrine, that's the long silence, that's the god testing the faithful, but." They stopped. Their jaw ached; they'd been clenching it for eleven months too. "But it feels less like being tested and more like being *left*. And I don't know which is worse, sir, and I don't know what a mercenary does with a god that won't answer, and I'm tired of pretending the ledger balances when it doesn't."

Corvane nodded slowly, as if Rosalind had confirmed figures he'd already run himself.

"When I was your age," he said, "the guild took a contract no one else would touch. A village east of here had lost its name—not forgotten it, *lost* it, like a button off a coat. Every soul in it. They could say 'we live...' and then nothing. We found who'd taken it. That's not a story for tonight." His reflection in the dark glass was old and tired and watching Rosalind with something that looked terribly like apology. "What matters is what the contract taught me. Debts don't vanish, Rosalind. Not debts of coin, not debts of memory, not debts of faith. They sit. They gather interest. And invisibility—" he tapped the window, toward the border hills, toward whatever was out there chewing on the world's edges—"invisibility is just a debt you've taken out against the day you'll need to be seen."

"You're frightening me, guildmaster."

"Good. Fear recorded is fear managed. Second rule applies to feelings too." He turned from the window. "Finish the entry tomorrow. Sleep tonight. And Rosalind—if the mark starts to burn, you wake me. Whatever hour. Whatever reason."

"Burn? It hasn't burned in years. It barely—"

Above the border hills, where the sky had been clear and star-strewn all season, a star went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. Went out—the way a candle goes out when pinched, quick and total, leaving a hole in the constellations like a missing tooth in a comb.

Rosalind's oath-mark ignited with cold fire, a frost-white blaze up the veins of their forearm, and across the sky, one after another after another, the stars began to go dark.

Corvane's hand closed hard around their shoulder.

"Wake the house," he said quietly. "All of it. Now."
