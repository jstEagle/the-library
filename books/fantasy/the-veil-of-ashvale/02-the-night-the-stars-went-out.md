## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards did not fall all at once. They fell the way a garment falls when someone draws a knife along its seam—slowly at first, with a whisper, and then all at once in a long, terrible sigh of silk.

Rosalind heard it from the guildhouse roof, where they had gone to escape the heat of the counting room and Corvane's questions. It came out of the west, rolling across the hills like weather: a sound too high to be thunder, too wide to be a scream, a tearing that went on and on until Rosalind's teeth ached with it and the lamp-flames in the streets below bent sideways as if flinching.

Then the stars began to go out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out*—each one pinched between an unseen thumb and forefinger, snuffed with a precision that was worse than any storm. Rosalind watched the Shepherd's Crook vanish star by star, then the Lantern-Bearer, then the long silver river of the Veil itself, that band of light every child in Ashvale learned to navigate by before they learned their letters. Where the constellations had been, there remained only blankness—not darkness, which has texture and depth, but an absence so complete it seemed the sky had been cut away and something flat and featureless hung behind it.

"Veyra," Rosalind said, because the word was habit older than thought.

The oath-mark on the inside of their left wrist said nothing. It had said nothing for three months now—a small sigil like a closed eye inked into the skin by no needle Rosalind could remember, warm as banked coals for as long as they had borne it. Warm even in winter. Even underwater. Even the night in Marrowgate when they'd pulled a drowning child from a flooded cistern and the mark had glowed like a candle through their sleeve.

Now, for the first time in eleven years, it burned *cold*.

It was not pain, exactly. It was the opposite of pain—the sensation of a limb remembering frostbite, of blood pulling back from the skin. Rosalind pressed two fingers against it and felt the cold pulse once, twice, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

Below them, Ashvale was waking up. Doors opened. Dogs began to howl in a register Rosalind had never heard from dogs before—flat, arrhythmic, wrong. Somewhere down Copperlane Street a woman started screaming her husband's name, over and over, and somewhere else a man answered, but his voice shook as though he were not entirely certain it belonged to him.

Rosalind stood up on the roof tiles, ledger still tucked under one arm—the ledger, always the ledger; Corvane would have it audited even at the end of the world—and made themselves look at the blank sky until their eyes watered.

*Record everything,* said the first law of the Guild of Impossible Trades.

So Rosalind took out the little wax pencil they kept behind their ear and wrote, in the margin of page four hundred and twelve beside the entry for the bottled lullaby:

*Night of the Torn Silk. Stars extinguished: the Crook (7), the Lantern (5), the Veil (uncountable). Sound heard citywide. Mark cold. God silent.*

They hesitated, then added:

*I think we are being erased.*

By midnight the guildhouse common room smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and fear.

The refugees had begun arriving within an hour of the tearing—not in the great weeping caravans of war or famine, but in ones and twos, families who had been traveling anyway and found the road ahead of them suddenly full of people walking the other direction. They came up from the borderlands, from the villages past Thornhollow where the old wards had stood since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. They came carrying what they could, and what they could carry was less than it should have been.

Rosalind saw it in the way they answered questions.

"I'm looking for my daughter," said a miller with flour still ghosting his sleeves. "Tall girl, sixteen, red hair. Her name is—" He stopped. His mouth kept moving for a moment, shaping nothing, like a door opening onto a bricked-up wall. "Her name is—" His face did something terrible and quiet. It didn't crumple. It *smoothed*, the grief sliding off it like water off glass, and he smiled vaguely at Rosalind and said, "I had a daughter, I think. Did I have a daughter?"

Behind him, a woman clutched a child's shoe in both hands and could not say why.

Rosalind had spent fifteen years in the impossible trades. They had appraised haunted mirrors, brokered peace between feuding ghosts, sold a man his own death and watched him thank them for it. They knew every shape fear could take, every flavor of loss that walked through a guildhouse door. But this was not loss. Loss leaves a shape behind—an absence you can trace with your fingers, a wound you can name. This was the wound forgetting it was a wound. This was the scar tissue growing over smooth and unbroken, and the person beneath it smiling.

Sable appeared at Rosalind's elbow without sound, as she always did, a lean shadow in a coat with too many pockets. She had a cup of broth in each hand and was distributing them to the shell-shocked with the brisk tenderness she showed only to strangers and never to friends.

"You're seeing it," she murmured, not quite a question.

"The gaps." Rosalind kept their voice low. "Names first. Faces second. That miller lost his daughter whole—name, face, the fact of her. But he kept the shoe." Rosalind nodded toward the woman. "She doesn't know whose it is."

"It eats from the edges inward." Sable handed a bowl to a boy of maybe nine who sat staring at the fire with the particular stillness of the recently emptied. "I've stolen enough memories to know how it's done. You don't take the middle of a thing. You take the seams. Unpick the connections, and the memory comes apart on its own like rotten cloth." Her jaw tightened. "Whoever's doing this is very good at it. Better than me. Better than anyone I've ever met."

"Could you take it back? What they've lost?"

Sable was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "Memories don't come back once they're truly taken, Roz. I told you that when you asked what happened to my—" She stopped herself, and Rosalind, courteously, looked away. "Whatever's holding them now isn't going to give them back out of kindness."

Outside, another star went out. Through the common room's deep windows they saw it happen—the last bright point of the Plowman winking away like a lamp carried into another room—and a ripple of moans went through the refugees, and someone began to pray, and someone else began to laugh, high and unsteady, the laugh of a mind choosing madness as the kinder door.

Rosalind wrote it all down. The miller and the shoe. The boy by the fire. The count of the missing stars, updated. Their hand was steady. They were proud of that, later—that the hand stayed steady even while everything beneath it roared.

Corvane came down near the second hour. He moved slowly these days, favoring his bad hip, and he wore his guildmaster's chain even at midnight as though it were armor. He surveyed the crowded room, the hollow-eyed travelers, the fire, and finally Rosalind at their table with the ledger open and the wax pencil worn to a stub.

"How many?" he asked.

"Forty-one come through the doors so far. Eleven with visible gaps. Probably more I can't see."

"And the sky?"

"Nineteen stars. Two full constellations gone."

Corvane nodded slowly. He lowered himself into the chair across from Rosalind with the care of a man conserving himself for something, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because there was a question hanging between them that both had been avoiding for three months, and tonight it would not be avoided any longer.

"You felt it," Corvane said. It was not a question either. "When the wards tore. Your mark."

Rosalind turned their wrist upward. In the firelight the sigil looked ordinary—ink-dark, inert—but when Corvane leaned close, he drew back sharply, as if from an open cellar door.

"It's cold," he said. "Veyra's mark is never cold."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "It isn't."

Three months ago, the god had gone silent. Not dead—Rosalind would have known; every oath-bearer would have known; the silence of a god's death was supposed to be a bell tolling through the bones of all its faithful. This was different. This was a room where someone had been speaking your name your whole life, and then one morning the voice stopped mid-word, and the quiet that followed was not empty but *attentive*. Three months of writing prayers into ledgers and getting no answer. Three months of Corvane asking, gently, in the way of a man who already suspects the answer, *Has it spoken to you? Has anything changed?*

Tonight the thing they had both feared had arrived wearing a torn sky.

"Guildmaster," Rosalind said quietly, "the wards on the border haven't failed since the founding. They're not machines. They're vows—old ones, sworn by the first king and renewed by every crown since. For them to tear like cloth, either every warden on the line broke faith at once—"

"—or whatever holds them was broken from the other side," Corvane finished. His eyes went to the window, to the blank patch where the Veil should have streamed. "Or from *above* it."

The words dropped into the silence between them like a stone into a well. Rosalind listened for the bottom and heard none.

"There are stories," Rosalind said carefully. "Old ones. Ledger entries from before the guild went quiet about such things. Things beyond the wards that the first king shut out, and the price he paid to do it."

"I know the entries you mean." Corvane's voice had gone strange—careful, almost guilty. "I've read them."

"Corvane. Those pages were sealed. Burned, according to the index."

For a long moment the guildmaster of the Ashvale Guild of Impossible Trades looked at his most reliable keeper of accounts, and Rosalind watched him weigh something—watched the scales tip and settle and tip again—and then, instead of answering, he simply said:

"Tend your refugees, Brightwater. Get them fed, get them sleeping, get their names written down while they still have names. All of them. Every soul that crosses this threshold tonight goes in the ledger."

"That's not guild procedure for transients—"

"It is procedure *tonight*." And there was something in his face then that Rosalind had seen only twice before: once when a rival guild tried to burn them out, and once at the funeral of Rosalind's first partner. It was the look of a man standing in front of a door he intended to hold shut for as long as his body allowed. "Write them all down, Rosalind. Names, faces, the memories they've lost and the ones they've kept. Everything."

"Why?"

Corvane rose, gathered his cloak, and paused at the edge of the firelight.

"Because," he said, "someone is keeping a list too. And I'd rather ours be longer."

He climbed the stairs slowly, favoring his hip, and did not look back.

Near dawn, the oath-mark woke.

Rosalind was at their table still, the ledger before them filled with forty-one entries in their small precise hand—names where names could be had, descriptions where names had been eaten, and beside each one, in a column they'd ruled themselves with a straightedge, a tally of what was missing. *Daughter, entire. Mother's lullaby, melody retained, words gone. Own childhood, ages four through nine.* It read like an inventory of a plundered house. It read like the work of the thing they had begun, privately, to call the Hollow King—for what else did you call a thief so vast it stole the sky?

The cold in their wrist shifted.

Rosalind went still, pencil hovering. Around them the common room slept—refugees bundled in blankets, Sable curled in the window seat like a cat who trusted nothing, the fire burned to amber coals. And under Rosalind's skin, the sigil that had been silent for three months and cold for six hours began, very faintly, to *pull*.

Not warmth. Not the old familiar hum of Veyra's attention, which had always felt like sunlight through a jar. This was a current, directional, urgent—the sensation of a fishhook set behind the navel, drawing them westward. Toward the border. Toward the torn wards.

Toward, Rosalind realized with a clarity that settled over them like frost forming, *whatever had eaten the stars*.

And threaded through the pull, thin as wire, almost too faint to be sure of, came something that might have been a voice—or might have been only the memory of one, the way an amputated hand remembers holding.

*Ros—*

One syllable. Half a name. Then nothing, and the cold again, and the hook.

Rosalind sat in the grey light before dawn with their heart slamming and their breath fogging, and understood three things with absolute certainty.

First: Veyra was not silent. Veyra was *faint*—a shout heard through miles of stone, a fire seen through a closing door.

Second: whatever had done this to the sky was doing it, right now, to their god.

Third: the ledger in their hands, with its forty-one names and its columns of loss, was not merely a record. It was a weapon. Because a thing that ate memory would fear nothing in this world more than an account of everything—and the Guild of Impossible Trades had been keeping accounts for two hundred years.

Rosalind dipped the wax pencil, turned to a fresh page, and ruled a new column down its length. At its head, in letters that did not shake, they wrote:

*What We Owe.*

Above them, through the leaded glass, the eastern sky was going pale—and in that pale wash, where the first stars should have been fading into morning, there was nothing left to fade. The whole heaven overhead was blank as an unwritten page.

Somewhere out on the dark road, hooves. Many of them, riding hard toward Ashvale.

Rosalind closed the ledger, held it a moment against their chest like a shield, and went to wake the others.
