## 3. What the Memory-Thief Stole

The rain had followed Sable all the way down from the borderlands, three days of it, as if the sky itself were trying to wash something off her that would not come clean. She rode through Ashvale's south gate at dusk with her hood up and her hands shaking so badly she could barely hold the reins, and she did not stop at the stable, or the cookhouse, or her own narrow room above the counting floor. She went straight to Rosalind.

She found them in the scriptorium, hunched over the great ledger with a lamp turned low, checking figures by the light of a single tallow candle because Corvane believed waste was a sin and lamplight was a kind of waste. The room smelled of ink and dust and old vellum, of the particular sweetness that paper takes on when it has been handled by many careful hands across many years. Rosalind looked up when the door opened, and whatever they saw in Sable's face made them set down their pen at once.

"You're four days early," Rosalind said. "And you look like someone walked over your grave."

"Someone did." Sable's voice came out wrong, scraped thin. She closed the door behind her and stood with her back against it, dripping onto the flagstones, making no move to come further into the room. "Rosalind. I need to tell you what happened, and I need you to not interrupt me, because if I stop I don't know that I'll start again."

Rosalind said nothing. They simply waited, the way they always waited, patient as a held breath, and Sable hated how much that patience helped.

* * *

She told it in order, because order was all she had left.

The job had been simple. Simple jobs paid best and asked least: a client in the border town of Vell's Crossing, met in the back room of a dyer's shop that smelled of mordant and wet wool. A merchant, he'd claimed, soft-spoken, gloved even indoors, his face forgettable in the way faces sometimes are—not plain, just *unfixed*, like a portrait painted in water and left out in the damp. He wanted a memory stolen from a warden garrison clerk. Nothing valuable. A delivery record, a signature, an afternoon. Sable had done a hundred like it.

"I took it clean," she said. "You know how I work. I sat across from him in a tavern for two hours and let him talk about his mother's stew, and I slipped in under all that and lifted the afternoon right out of him like a splinter. He never felt it. He'll wake tomorrow thinking he spent that day shoveling his neighbor's walk." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "I carried it home in a glass vial, silver-gray, ordinary. And then I went back to the dyer's shop to deliver."

"And?"

"And he paid me." Her hands were flat against the door now, pressing, as if the wood might give way into some other room where none of this was true. "He counted out the coin himself, every piece, and he watched me take it, and he smiled—Rosalind, he *smiled*—and he said, 'There. Now we're both satisfied.' And then he reached across the table, quick as a snake, and touched two fingers to my wrist, and he took the memory of being paid."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain kept falling, steady and indifferent.

"He didn't rob me," Sable went on, and now the words came faster, tumbling, because she had held this alone for three days and three nights and the holding had grown teeth. "That's the thing you have to understand. Thieves get robbed. That's the trade; I've robbed and been robbed and it never once kept me up at night. But he *paid* me to forget being paid. He wanted the transaction to have only one side. He wanted me to remember doing the job and not remember why, not remember who, not remember the coin that should be in my pocket—and when I got back to my room in Vell's Crossing and found my purse lighter than it should be and couldn't account for it, I thought I'd been cheated by a cutpurse on the road. I nearly turned around and hunted the man down to *thank* him for the lesson."

She pushed off the door at last and crossed the room in three strides, pulling something from inside her coat—a scrap of oilcloth, wrapped tight, tied with waxed thread. She laid it on the ledger between them with the reverence of a woman setting down a live coal.

"But here's what he didn't count on," she said. "Here's what saved me. When he took the payment-memory, he wasn't careful. He was greedy. He grabbed a whole handful, and some of what came away with it wasn't mine."

Rosalind unwrapped the oilcloth slowly. Inside lay a sliver of memory pressed thin as leaf-gold, translucent, shot through with colors that moved when you weren't looking directly at them—the way memories did when they were fresh, before they settled and dulled. Guild-trained eyes knew the difference. This was fresh. This had been torn living from someone's mind within the last season.

"It's royal," Sable said quietly. "Look at the seal-work woven through it. That's the privy cipher. I've fenced court memories before, little ones—a chamberlain's embarrassment, a princess's first kiss. They carry the cipher like watermarks. This is deeper than anything I've ever seen leave the palace walls. This is *throne-room*. This is the king's own—"

She stopped. Rosalind had tilted the sliver toward the candlelight, and the light passed through it and threw an image onto the plaster wall behind them, the way a lens throws an image, blurred and swimming and unmistakable.

A throne room. Pillars going up into shadow. Courtiers arranged in rows like cut flowers. And on the throne, crowned, robed in ermine, seated exactly as a king should sit—

A figure with no face.

Not hidden. Not shadowed. The place where a face should be was smooth as the back of a spoon, and the crown floated a half-inch above nothing, and the courtiers below knelt to it with their heads bowed, and not one of them, in all that assembled splendor, seemed to find anything strange.

"That's not possible," Rosalind said. Their voice was very level, which Sable knew, after six years of working beside them, meant the opposite. "Kings have faces. Memories preserve faces better than anything. Even dead kings keep their faces."

"I know what I saw." Sable pulled the scrap back and rewrapped it, fast, as though it might look back at her. "And there's more. There's a sound caught in it, under everything. Do you hear it? Listen."

They listened. Beneath the imagined murmur of court, beneath the rustle of silk and the echo of some distant choir, there was a sound like tearing silk—long, slow, continuous, patient. The sound of something being unraveled. Sable had heard it once before, on her last night in Vell's Crossing, when she'd stood at her window and watched the stars above the border hills go out. Not clouded over. *Out.* One by one, in order, like lamps being snuffed by a careful hand moving down a corridor.

"The wards fell the night I left," she said. "I heard them go. Everyone heard them go, and then everyone stopped hearing them, and by morning the town was talking about the weather. Rosalind, they talked about the *weather*. I asked the innkeeper's daughter about the stars and she looked at me like I'd asked her about the color of Tuesday. It's not just the wards. Something is eating the remembering itself, and it started at the border, and it's wearing the king."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The candle burned. Somewhere below them, the guildhouse went about its evening—the clink of the cookhouse, Corvane's low voice correcting someone's arithmetic, the ordinary music of a place built to stay invisible. Sable thought about the guild's three laws, carved small above the counting floor: *Trade fairly. Record everything. Stay invisible.* She had lived by them since she was fourteen years old and stealing bread with other people's hunger still warm in her mouth. She had never once thought about what would happen if invisibility stopped being a choice.

"If we take this to Corvane," she said, "he'll tell us to seal it in the vault and forget we ever saw it. That's the law. Stay invisible. Don't touch crowns."

"Yes," Rosalind said.

"And if we take it to anyone outside—if we sell it, or show it to a magistrate, or shout it in the market square—we're dead. Wardens at the door by morning. You know what happens to people who say the king has no face."

"Yes," Rosalind said again.

"So we do nothing. We put it in a drawer and we wait for the world to make sense again." Sable heard her own voice shake. "Except I can't, Rosalind. I can't stop seeing it. Every time I close my eyes I see that throne room, and the worst part—the part I haven't said yet—is that I checked. Before I came here, I checked my own head the way I'd check a lockbox. And there are gaps. Little ones. My mother's maiden name—I can't find it. The name of the village I was born in—it's *smooth*, like a stone where a word used to be. I stole memories for eleven years and I never once lost one of my own, and now they're going missing like coins from a torn pocket, and I don't think it's an accident, and I don't think it's age, and I am *afraid*."

The word hung in the lamplight. She had not said it aloud to anyone in twenty years. Fear was a currency she never spent.

Rosalind rose from their chair. They crossed to the shelf where the guild's sealed cases lived, and instead of opening it, they stood with their hand flat against the wood, thinking. Sable watched them and understood, suddenly, why Corvane trusted this person with the ledgers—why the guild's whole quiet machinery of impossible trades rested on these narrow shoulders. Rosalind Brightwater did not decide quickly. But when they decided, the decision was already true.

"Here is what I believe," Rosalind said finally, turning. "I believe your client was not a merchant. I believe he was a servant of the thing on that throne, and he let you see what he let you see—or he was careless, which frightens me more. I believe the gaps in your memory and the dark stars and the fallen wards are one debt, coming due all at once. And I believe—" they paused, and their hand went unconsciously to the oath-mark at their wrist, the cold brand of the silent god Veyra, "—that our god went silent for a reason, and the reason is wearing a crown."

Sable stared at them. "You've felt it too. The silence."

"Veyra hasn't spoken to me in months. I told myself it was doubt. Doubt is honest; gods forgive doubt." Rosalind's jaw tightened. "But doubt doesn't feel like this. This feels like a room where someone used to be."

The rain intensified against the shutters, and in its sound Sable heard again that other sound, the tearing-silk patience of it, and she made her decision the way she made all her decisions: fast, whole, without looking back.

"Then here's my offer, and it's not a thief's offer, so listen carefully. I'm giving you this memory-scrap. Not selling. Giving. Guild law says everything gets recorded and priced, but I'm telling you the price of this one is that we do something, together, and I won't be paid for it." She held Rosalind's gaze. "You're the keeper of the ledgers. If anyone can find out what our god was trying to tell us before it went quiet—if anyone can read the truth in those pages—it's you. So I'm putting my fear in your keeping, Rosalind Brightwater, the same as I'd put a jewel. Keep it safe. Tell me what to do with it."

Something moved through Rosalind's expression—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. When they spoke, their voice was rougher than usual.

"Fear shared is fear halved," they said. "My grandmother's saying. She also said never trust a saying you can't steal from." They extended their hand, and Sable took it, and the grip was warm and steady and did not shake at all. "We go to Corvane. Tonight. Together. And Sable—if the guildmaster orders this sealed and buried anyway, then we'll decide what kind of invisible we're willing to be. Because I've been reading the old entries, the ones in the god's own hand, and there are pages missing from the ledger. Torn out. Someone in this house has been keeping secrets from us, and I intend to learn whether they were protecting us or preparing us."

Sable felt the ground shift beneath her, the way it shifted before a wall came down. She thought of the faceless king, the dying stars, the smooth stones where her childhood words used to be. She thought of the guild laws, and how the third one had always really meant *survive*, and how survival was starting to look like something other than hiding.

"Alliance, then," she said.

"Alliance," Rosalind agreed. "Fear shared."

Outside, the rain went on falling over Ashvale, and above the clouds, where the constellations should have been wheeling bright and whole, the sky kept its new and terrible blankness—and somewhere in the borderlands, in a dye-shop that smelled of mordant and wet wool, a soft-spoken man with an unfixed face was surely reporting that a thief had seen too much, and that the ledger-keeper of the Ashvale Guild had begun, at last, to ask questions.

The knock at the scriptorium door, when it came, was gentle. Corvane's voice, low and tired, through the wood:

"Sable. Rosalind. I think it's time you both saw what I've been keeping."
