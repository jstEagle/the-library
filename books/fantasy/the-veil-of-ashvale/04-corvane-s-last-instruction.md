## 4. Corvane's Last Instruction

The scriptorium smelled of tallow smoke and old vellum, of iron gall ink and the particular dust that gathers only in rooms where paper has been loved for longer than the people in it have been alive. Corvane had lit every lamp. That was the first wrongness Rosalind noticed as they followed him in—Corvane, who counted candle stubs the way misers counted coin, who kept the guildhouse dim on principle because *light costs, Brightwater, everything costs*—had lit all seven lamps, and the shadows had been driven back into the corners like debtors.

Sable came in behind them and shut the door with a soft click, then set her palm flat against the wood. Rosalind knew what she was doing without being told: listening. The thief had a way of attending to buildings the way other people attended to conversations.

"No one followed him up," Sable said quietly.

"I should hope not," Corvane said. "I've been taking the long stairs since the stars went out." He moved to his desk—the great scarred oak that had belonged to every guildmaster before him, its surface grooved with two centuries of ledgers laid down hard—and stood over it a moment with his hands pressed flat, as if holding something down. He was not a large man. He had never been a large man; he was all knuckle and elbow and dry voice, a figure assembled from spare parts and stubbornness. But standing there in the lamplight with his shoulders set, he looked for the first time in Rosalind's memory like what he actually was: the keeper of the Ashvale Guild, third of his line, sworn to trade fairly and record everything and stay invisible.

He looked like a man about to stop being invisible.

"You'll want to sit," he said.

Rosalind did not sit. "Guildmaster—"

"Sit anyway, Brightwater. It's going to be a long night, and you'll want your legs under you when it ends." There was no humor in it. Corvane's jokes were usually dry enough to strike sparks from; these words came out damp, heavy, like things pulled up from deep water.

Rosalind sat on the reading bench beneath the eastern window. Sable perched on the arm of it, coiled, her stolen eyes—no, Rosalind corrected herself, her *own* eyes, just borrowed often enough that it felt like thievery—moving between the two of them.

Corvane opened the desk.

Not the drawers. The desk itself. He worked at something along the front edge—a seam Rosalind had sat beside a thousand times and never seen, disguised by grain and stain until his thumbnail found the hidden catch—and a panel swung inward, and from the hollow heart of the oldest piece of furniture in Ashvale Guildhouse, Corvane lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord gone brown with age.

It was thin. Thinner than it had any right to be, for the weight of the silence around it. When he set it on the desk, the lamps flickered—not from any draft. Rosalind felt the oath-mark on their inner wrist go cold, colder than it had burned three nights past when the stars went out, cold as a coin left overnight in winter grass. Cold the way it had been cold once before, years ago, the last time they'd stood in this room and asked their guildmaster why Veyra had stopped speaking.

"Three days ago," Rosalind said, "when I asked you about the silence. You told me gods keep their own counsel."

"I told you what would keep you safe." Corvane unwound the cord. "There's a difference between keeping counsel and lying, Brightwater, though the ledger doesn't always distinguish them kindly." His fingers, usually so steady over entries and audits, fumbled the last knot. Sable leaned forward without seeming to move and picked it loose for him.

He drew back the oilcloth.

Pages. Loose pages, perhaps twenty of them, cut raggedly from some larger binding—cut, Rosalind saw at once, by someone in haste or terror, the edges torn where the knife had slipped. The paper was wrong. Not vellum, not rag paper, nothing milled by any mill Rosalind knew. It had a faint grey sheen, like skin under frost, and when the lamplight crossed it at an angle the surface seemed almost to breathe.

And the writing—

Rosalind had spent eleven years keeping the guild's ledgers. They knew every hand that had ever held a guild pen: Corvane's cramped economy, old Master Ellery's flourishes, the apprentice scrawls, their own careful upright letters. They knew the ledger's conventions the way priests know liturgy.

This was the god's hand.

They had seen it exactly once, seven years ago, on the flyleaf of the founding ledger—an inscription no one was permitted to copy, which Rosalind had nevertheless copied from memory into the vault behind their own ribs where they kept the things that mattered. A script that moved. That was the only honest way to describe it: letters that shifted their angles slowly while you watched, so that reading them was less like reading than like catching. Ink the color of deep water at dusk.

The pages on Corvane's desk were written in that hand. And the ink was fading.

"It's dying with him," Corvane said. He said it simply, the way he reported losses in an audit. "The script. Since the wards fell, the entries have been going grey, entry by entry, like embers going out. I've watched it happen for three nights." He turned the top page toward the lamp, and Rosalind saw it: lines of living script above, and below them, creeping upward from the foot of the page like frost climbing a windowpane, blankness. Not erased. *Unwritten.* The paper where those words had been was smooth and innocent, as if nothing had ever been inscribed there at all.

"What is it?" Sable asked. Her voice had lost all its thief's silkiness. "What are these?"

"The god's ledger," Corvane said. "Veyra's own."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a floor does when a ship changes tack. Rosalind heard themselves ask, "The guild ledger has a—there's another ledger?" and hated how young their voice sounded.

"There is only ever one ledger," Corvane said, "and we keep copies. That's what a guild is, Brightwater. We're the copyists. Trade fairly, record everything, stay invisible—we thought those were our rules. They were our *duties*. The rules were the god's." He touched the top page with one blunt finger, not quite tracing the script, the way a man might touch a sleeping animal he didn't dare wake. "Every impossible thing the guild ever brokered—every bottled lullaby, every unspent grief, every year sold off a life—every one of those trades was recorded first here, in this hand, before any clerk put quill to paper. Veyra wrote the true ledger. We wrote the fair copy."

"And you tore pages out of it," Sable said softly.

"I hid pages out of it. There's a difference there too, though again—" his mouth twisted, "—the distinction may be lost on everyone now." He drew a breath that shuddered at the bottom of it. "Twenty-two years ago, the year I inherited this desk, a page went missing. Torn out, I assumed—a theft, a scandal, a thing to be handled quietly, per the third rule. I searched. I interrogated. And then I found the rest, hidden behind the panel by my predecessor, with a note in his hand: *If the god goes silent, burn nothing. Give these to whoever keeps the ledgers after you, and pray you never have cause.*"

"Why hide them at all?" Rosalind asked. "Why not read them? Why not—" but even as they said it, they understood, and the understanding arrived like cold water finding the crack in a boot. "Because reading them meant knowing. And knowing meant the third rule was broken forever. Once you knew, you couldn't be invisible anymore."

Corvane looked at them with something that was almost pride and mostly grief. "Eleven years I've kept this desk and trained you to keep the next one, and you still see the shape of a rule faster than anyone I've taught." He turned back to the pages. "Yes. For twenty-two years I have known that there were answers in this drawer, and I chose not to read them, because a man who reads them becomes a target, and the guild survives by having no targets. I told myself it was discipline. Some nights I even believed it."

"And three nights ago," Rosalind said, "the stars went out."

"And I ran out of nights worth buying with ignorance." Corvane began turning pages, carefully, with both hands, as if handling a wounded bird. "Read with me. Both of you. The script fights a little—it wants to be caught, not read—but it will hold still for oath-bearers. It always did."

Rosalind leaned over the page, and the moving letters swam, resisted, and then—as their oath-mark flared cold against their wrist—settled, and gave themselves up.

*Entry the nine-hundredth-and-fortieth,* the page began. *In the year the first king crossed the wards.*

The prose beneath the heading was not like any ledger Rosalind had ever kept. It was accounting, yes—but accounting at a scale that made their skull ache, numbers that tallied things Rosalind had no column for:

*Brought across: the king, whole. Brought across: his shadow, which he begged us to leave, and which we could not leave, for it had already learned his name. Brought across: the thing that walks in the shape of a crown's approval. It does not eat bread. It eats the remembering-of-bread. It does not kill. It makes the world agree that the living were never born. Ward the border. Ward the border. Ward the border. Count the stars nightly. If any star fails to answer, the count is broken, and the thing is feeding.*

"Ward the border," Sable whispered. "Three times. Like a man repeating a lock-word."

"Like a god repeating a warning nobody heeded," Corvane said. He turned pages with increasing urgency, showing Rosalind entry after entry across years Rosalind could not map to any calendar—the god's reckoning used stars, not dates, and half the star-names were ones Rosalind had never learned because those constellations had been blank for three nights now. Entries about the first king's slow change: how his laughter grew borrowed, how his memories began arriving secondhand, how the courtiers learned to say *as the king remembers it* instead of *as it happened*, and none of them noticed they'd changed their speech. Entries about wardens posted along the border whose oaths the god rewove strand by strand until the wards were less a wall than a woven spell, each warden a thread.

Then, near the end of the bundle, the handwriting changed. It was still the god's script, still moving, but it had gone ragged, hurried, the letters lurching like a runner stumbling. And the ink was nearly white with age—or with something else.

Corvane stopped on that page and could not seem to make himself speak for a moment. When he did, his voice was rough. "This is the last entry. I've read it four times since the wards fell, and each time I understand less and believe more."

Rosalind read.

*It is inside the walls. It is inside the king. It has been inside the king for longer than the court can remember, which is precisely the point. I have bought time with silence—I made myself small and quiet and forgettable, and it has not found me yet, for I am the one thing in this realm it cannot taste without choking. But it is learning hunger the way kings learn taxes: patiently, and everywhere.*

*It can be unbound. Hear me, whoever reads: it can be unbound, but not by steel and not by prayer and not by any power I can spend from outside. It must be burned out from within, and the fire must be carried in a vessel that loves something more than itself. My fire is too far away to carry. I am going to make myself smaller still. If I succeed, I will be silent, and my faithful will hate me for it, and that hatred will keep them looking outward, and looking outward may save them.*

*I am sorry. I have recorded everything else in this ledger truly, so let me record this truly too: I am afraid.*

*Unbind him. Whatever it costs. Unbind him.*

The last words were scored so deep the paper had nearly parted. Below them, the frost-blankness had climbed halfway up the page, eating the god's final paragraphs from underneath, and Rosalind understood with a lurch that they were watching the same hunger that had emptied the borderlands working its way through the last surviving testimony of a god.

"He didn't abandon us," Rosalind said. Their voice came out strange, flattened, as if spoken by someone else wearing their mouth. "Seven years of silence. Seven years I thought—" They stopped. The anger they had carried so long it had become part of their posture, part of how they held their shoulders, suddenly had nowhere to attach itself, and its absence left them dizzy.

"Later," Corvane said gently. "Grief later. Work first. That's the guild way." But his hands were shaking as he squared the pages. "Here is what matters. The thing wears the king. It has worn him for a generation—perhaps since before you were born, Brightwater, certainly since before mine. It feeds on memory to sustain its shape, and now that it has torn the wards and begun to feed openly, it will grow bolder. Cities next. Then the kingdom's whole past, and then there will be nothing left to remember that anything was ever otherwise." He looked up, and in the lamplight his eyes were wet and absolutely clear. "The pages say it can be unbound. The pages say how—mostly. Some of the instructions are already eaten." He tapped the frost-grey margins bitterly. "Of course they are. It got cleverer in twenty-two years. So we reconstruct. You and I and anyone else we can trust, we rebuild the rite from what remains, and then—"

The lamp nearest the door guttered.

Not guttered—*stopped*, flame snuffed as neatly as pinched by invisible fingers, and in the same instant Sable came off the arm of the bench and onto her feet with no sound at all, one hand out flat toward the door, her head cocked.

"Stairs," she breathed. "Someone on the stairs. Heavy boots, trying to walk quiet. Three—no, four."

Corvane moved faster than Rosalind had ever seen him move. The pages went into the oilcloth, the oilcloth went against Rosalind's chest, shoved there hard enough to hurt, and Corvane's hands closed over theirs, folding their fingers shut around the bundle.

"Hearth passage," he said, low and rapid. "Behind the map case. Take them down to the tannery and don't stop for anything—"

"Guildmaster, no—" 

"Brightwater." He gripped their wrists, and his eyes held theirs, and for one suspended moment Rosalind saw clearly everything his dryness had hidden for eleven years: that he had loved this guild the way a lighthouse loves the sea, helplessly and at cost, and that he had known, since the moment he opened the drawer tonight, what opening it would summon. "You are the keeper of ledgers now. That is not a job. It never was. It is a *line*, and it runs through me to you, and it does not break while either of us draws breath. So draw breath elsewhere. *Go.*"

The door burst open.

Not forced—opened, with terrible politeness, by a man in the grey-lacquered half-armor of a royal warden, the king's seal bright on his breast: a crowned tower stamped in white gold. Behind him, filling the stairwell, three more, and the lamps along the walls dying one by one as they entered, as if the light itself preferred not to witness.

"By warrant of the crown," the warden said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst thing, Rosalind thought distantly, pressing back against the map case with the pages crushed against their heart—that it was pleasant, mild, a voice for reading proclamations in market squares. "Guildmaster Corvane of the Ashvale Guild. You are charged with trafficking in contraband remembrance and with withholding crown property." His gaze touched the open desk, the hollow panel, and curved slightly at the corner, like a smile that had gotten lost on the way to his mouth. "We'll take the god's pages now."

"They're not the crown's property," Corvane said. He had placed himself between the soldiers and his people, this small dry unarmored man, and his voice did not shake at all. "Nothing in this house belongs to the crown. That has been the law of Ashvale since before your seal had a king inside it."

"Law," the warden said, tasting the word. "The king remembers the law differently."

"The king," Corvane said, "doesn't remember anything. That's rather the whole of it, isn't it?"

Something moved behind the warden's face—something that was not the warden, something using the warden's muscles the way a hand uses a glove—and for a heartbeat every lamp in the room flared green-white, and Rosalind felt the pages against their chest go hot as a brand, and the oath-mark on their wrist screamed cold.

Steel came out.

Afterward Rosalind could never assemble the sequence honestly. There was Sable moving like thrown silk, and a table overturned, and the smell of blood shockingly loud in a room that smelled of ink, and Corvane saying—calmly, terribly, in the tone he used for closing an audit—"Brightwater. The passage. *Now.*"

They went. That was the wound they would carry longest, they thought later: not the sight of what the steel did, but the fact of their own legs obeying, carrying them and Sable through the hidden door behind the maps while their guildmaster stood alone in the lamplight with twenty-two years of chosen blindness come due.

But at the mouth of the hearth passage, in the dark that smelled of soot and older fires, Rosalind stopped—one breath, half a heartbeat, disobedience measured in inches—and looked back.

Corvane was on his knees. The warden stood over him with the sword, and the other three had not moved at all, had not helped, had only watched with the vacant patience of furniture. Corvane's hand was pressed to his side, and between his fingers the blood came bright and steady, and still he was looking—not at the soldier, not at the door where his people had vanished, but at the desk, at the empty open panel, and he was smiling. Small. Dry. Struck through with relief.

His eyes found the shadowed gap of the passage. Found Rosalind. Rosalind would swear afterward that in that dark, across that room, with death standing over him in the king's white-gold seal, Corvane saw them plainly.

His lips moved. No sound came—or sound came and the hungry air ate it—but Rosalind read the shape of it the way they read all the guild's oldest scripts, by heart, by inheritance, by the line that ran through him into them and did not break:

*Unbind him.*

Then the door behind the maps swung shut on its counterweight, and the dark took them, and the last thing Rosalind carried down into the earth was the sound of a body settling onto a scriptorium floor with the soft, terrible weight of a ledger being closed.

* * *

The hearth passage let out into the tannery yard behind the guildhouse, where the lime pits breathed their sour stink into the rain, and Sable caught Rosalind by the collar before they could turn back toward the house.

"Don't," she said.

"He's—" Rosalind's voice cracked apart. "Sable, he's *back there*—"

"Yes." She held them, this woman who stole memories for a living, and her grip said she knew exactly what she was holding and what it weighed. Rain ran down both their faces. From the direction of the guildhouse, faint through the drumming water, came voices giving orders in that pleasant, market-square tone, and the creak of the scriptorium door, and—once, briefly, horribly—a sound like many pages being turned.

"They'll take the rest of the ledger," Rosalind said numbly. "The fair copies. Everything."

"No." Sable's eyes had gone distant, unfocused, the look she wore when she was walking through someone else's head. "No—they're burning the scriptorium. All of it. They want no records left anywhere." She blinked, refocused, and Rosalind saw that her hands, gripping their collar, were trembling. "Rosalind. The pages. Are they—"

Rosalind opened their coat. The oilcloth bundle sat against their chest where Corvane had thrust it, and through the wrapping they could feel warmth—not the false warmth of the body, something stranger, steadier, like a coal banked for a long night. Against their wrist, the oath-mark had stopped screaming and settled into a low cold ache that felt, unmistakably, like attention.

Twenty-two years Corvane had kept them hidden. Twenty-two years of discipline and darkness, and at the end he had spent his last breath not on a cry for help but on an instruction.

Rosalind closed their coat over the god's dying words and looked east, toward the capital, where a faceless thing sat wearing a king.

"We bury him at dawn," they said, and their voice came out level, and cold, and certain, the voice of a keeper of ledgers entering a loss into the record. "And then we learn what he died leaving us. Every word. Before it fades."

Above them the rain kept falling through a sky with no stars, and somewhere in the dark of the coat, the pages waited—half-eaten, half-alive—like a god whispering through clenched teeth: *unbind him, whatever it costs.*
