## 5. The Warden Who Defected

The rain had followed Idris Vale for eleven days.

It came down the mountain passes with him like a debt collector, patient and indifferent, soaking through his warden's grey until the cloak hung off his shoulders as heavy as guilt. He had stopped trying to stay dry somewhere past the second ridge. Now he only counted things—the way he always did when his hands wanted to shake. Eleven days of travel. Nine hours since he'd last slept under something that wasn't open sky. Four arrows left in his quiver, because the fifth had found the thigh of a brother warden named Tamms, and Tamms had screamed his name as Idris rode away, and that scream was the loudest thing in the world some nights.

Three days since the stars went out over Ashvale.

He could still see where they should have been. That was the worst of it—not darkness, but absence with a shape. The Hunter's Bow hung blank above the eastern hills, its seven stars gone like teeth pulled from a jaw, and every traveler on the road looked up at the gap without seeing it. That was the other horror: no one else remembered the stars at all. He'd asked an old woman selling chestnuts in the last village if she'd noticed the sky change, and she'd smiled at him with warm, empty eyes and said, *What sky? Oh—yes, it's night.*

Idris had ridden out of that village fast enough to leave mud on the shutters.

*You let them fall,* the order's tribunal would say, when they caught him. They would catch him. Wardens always closed their hunts; it was the one prayer they kept. *You stood the wall at Cindermarch and you watched the wards unravel and you did nothing.*

And the terrible thing, the thing he couldn't carve out of himself with any knife he owned, was that they were right. He had watched. The wards hadn't broken—they had *unwoven*, thread by silver thread, in a pattern too deliberate for collapse. And standing on the wall in the hour before dawn, sword drawn against nothing, Idris Vale had felt his own memory begin to fray at the edges like wet rope, and he had been afraid, and fear had made him slow, and by the time he'd shouted the alarm the last thread had come loose and the thing on the far side of the wards had already stepped through wearing a king's shadow.

So now he rode toward Ashvale town in the rain, with a stolen dispatch tube strapped inside his coat like a second heartbeat, hunting the only people in the realm who might know what he had failed to stop.

The Guild of Impossible Trades. Corvane's people. Ledger-keepers, smugglers of things that shouldn't be traded, worshippers of a god who had gone silent as a held breath. His commander had called them parasites before ordering the guildhouse burned. Idris had called them his last hope, which was, he supposed, why he was riding alone through the rain while the rest of Third Company sharpened their warrants.

* * *

Ashvale town smelled of woodsmoke and river-mud and, underneath both, a faint sweetness like fruit going soft—fear, Idris thought, given time to ripen. Refugees from the borderlands had come in waves all week; he passed them huddled under the eaves of the tannery row, families wrapped around bundles they'd carried too far, children staring at strangers with the flat, searching look of those who have forgotten someone and can't say who.

One girl, maybe six, reached up as he passed and caught his stirrup.

"You're sad," she said. Not a question.

"Get your hand off the horse, little one."

"He's not yours," she said, still holding on. "Nothing's ours anymore. Mama says." She let go then, wandered back into the huddle of blankets, and Idris sat in the saddle for three full breaths, watching her go, feeling the words settle into him like silt.

The guildhouse stood at the end of Rope Lane, where the street gave up pretending to be respectable and became stairs instead—a narrow stone building squeezed between a chandler's shop and a shrine to some hearth-god whose niche had gone cold. No sign hung above the door. Of course there was no sign. A guild that traded in impossible things didn't advertise; you knew the door by what it cost you to find it.

Idris dismounted, tied his horse where he could see it, and stood in the rain looking at the door like a man reading a confession.

His hand was still raised to knock when it opened.

The woman in the doorway was lean and dark-eyed, hair cropped short as a mercenary's, with a ledger tucked under one arm as naturally as another woman might carry a child. She looked at him the way an auditor looks at a suspicious entry—top to bottom, once, thoroughly—and he watched her take in the warden grey, the soaked cloak, the sword, the four arrows, the three-day beard, the wound crusted along his forearm that he'd stopped noticing around day six.

"Wardens don't knock," she said.

"This one does."

"Wardens burn doors."

"This one's asking to be let inside one."

Her eyes narrowed. Behind her, deeper in the house, lamplight moved across a wall of shelves, and Idris caught the smell of ink and candle-tallow and old paper—that particular smell of places where things were written down so they couldn't be denied. Something in his chest unclenched half a degree.

"I need to speak to Corvane," he said. "It's about the border wards. About the stars." He hesitated, then made himself say the rest, because lying to this woman felt like lying to a scale. "I was there when they fell. And my own order is hunting me for knowing it. So either you shelter me or you turn me out, mistress, but either way, someone has to hear what I carried out of Cindermarch before the people who sent me finish the job they started on my memory."

Rain ran off his chin. Somewhere behind the woman, a floorboard creaked—the deliberate creak of someone choosing to be heard.

She didn't move from the doorway. "Names," she said. "Yours first."

"Idris Vale. Border Warden, Third Company, Cindermarch garrison. Formerly."

"And you know mine?"

"No," he admitted. "But I know the guild keeps ledgers, and ledgers keep names, and I'm betting yours is written in more than one."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one deciding whether to haunt the place. She stepped aside.

"One rule," she said. "In this house, everything gets recorded. What you tell us goes in the book, and the book doesn't lie. If that frightens you, the rain's still falling."

"It's the first thing in eleven days that hasn't frightened me," said Idris Vale, and stepped across the threshold.

* * *

They put him in a room with a table, two chairs, and a lamp turned low enough to make honesty feel like intimacy. The dark-haired woman introduced herself as Rosalind Brightwater and said nothing else about herself, which told him she was either very important or very careful, and he suspected both. With her came a second woman—Sable, she was called, all quick hands and quicker eyes, who circled Idris once like a cat auditing a dog and settled onto the windowsill rather than a chair, boots swinging, close to the escape route. He noted it professionally. He'd have done the same.

Corvane came last.

The guildmaster was older than Idris expected—grey-bearded, broad through the shoulders, with the kind of face that had been weathered by decisions rather than seasons. He set a ledger on the table, opened it to a fresh page, dipped a pen, and looked up.

"Begin at the beginning," Corvane said. "Not the beginning you think we want. The true one. We'll know if you trim it."

So Idris began at the true beginning.

"The wards were woven before my grandfather's grandfather drew breath," he said. "Silver-thread lattice anchored at nine points along the Veil—Cindermarch was the fourth anchor, the strongest. My whole posting was maintenance and vigilance. Nothing crosses the Veil. Nothing has crossed the Veil in living memory. You stand that wall long enough, you stop believing in the other side of it. It becomes a story you're paid to guard." He turned his hands palm-up on the table. "I stopped believing. That was my first failure."

Rosalind wrote. Sable watched.

"Eleven days ago, third watch, the lattice started moving. Not breaking—*unweaving*. Threads lifting out of the anchor-stones one at a time, smooth as a seamstress pulling basting stitches. Deliberate. Skilled. Someone or something knew exactly how the wards were made, and took them apart in order." His voice went flat; he let it. Flat was how he kept the shaking on the inside. "I sounded the horn. By the time the garrison mustered, the last thread was loose, and the sky over the pass—" He stopped. Started again. "There was no sky over the pass. There was a shape where sky should be. Like a hole cut in the world with the edges still curling.

"And then the stars here—" he gestured upward, toward Ashvale's ceiling, toward everything beyond it, "—went out. One by one. And every soul in the garrison except me forgot they'd ever been lit. I watched Sergeant Omma look straight at the Hunter's Bow and ask me why the night was so bare. She's served twenty years. She taught me the constellations. Her own hand drew them on the mess-hall slate every winter, and she looked at the empty sky and asked me what I was staring at."

The lamp hissed softly. No one spoke. Rosalind's pen had stopped moving.

"There's more," Idris said. "That's the part I stole."

He worked the dispatch tube from inside his coat—sealed with the king's own black wax, the crowned seal that every subject of the realm was raised to love—and laid it on the table between them like a dead thing.

"Orders came to the garrison the morning after the fall. From the capital. Sealed with that." He tapped the wax. "The orders said: the ward-fall was sabotage by foreign agents. Capture and hold any witness. Allow no independent investigation. And—" he made himself say it evenly, "—any warden who reports anomalies in his own memory is to be relieved of duty and remanded to the king's physicians for treatment."

"Treatment," Sable repeated, from the windowsill. Her voice had gone quiet in the wrong way.

"For remembering," Idris said. "Yes."

He leaned forward. "I've been a warden for fourteen years. I know what sealed orders look like when they're real and when they're a leash dressed up as law. This one was written by something that wanted us docile. And I've spent eleven days being hunted by men I ate beside, men I'd have died for, because I read those orders and asked *who benefits*, instead of kneeling and saying thank you." His hand curled slowly into a fist atop the table. "I let the wards fall. Maybe I could have stopped the unweaving if I'd been faster, braver, less afraid—I'll wear that failure until they bury me. But I will not wear ignorance. Someone took those wards apart on purpose, and whatever did it is sitting in a palace right now, wearing a crown, eating memories out of this kingdom one town at a time. And nobody with rank or power will even say the words."

He sat back. It was done. Whatever they decided, at least it had been said aloud, recorded, witnessed. Say it aloud and it exists; that was the first thing they taught you in the warden corps, and the last lesson he had left.

Corvane looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked at the ledger, and for a long moment her pen hovered above the page, and Idris realized with a strange lurch that she was afraid—he could see it, fine as a crack in glaze. She was afraid of something in what he'd said. Not of him. Of what he'd confirmed.

"The god," she said quietly. "Our god. Veyra. Hasn't spoken in weeks. The oath-marks have gone silent." She met his eyes. "You're describing a kingdom being eaten. Our god went quiet right before the meal began. Those facts want to be neighbors."

"They're more than neighbors," Corvane said. His voice was gravel over something buried. He had not touched his pen once during Idris's account. Now he closed the ledger gently, as though it were sleeping. "Warden Vale. You understand our position. The guild survives by staying invisible. Invisible means no soldiers at our door, no royal attention, no fugitive wardens leaving boot-prints on our stairs. Sheltering you is against every law we keep. It paints a target on this house in letters tall enough to read from the palace."

"I know," Idris said. "I'm not asking you to be wise. I'm asking you to be what you claim to be. Traders in impossible things." He spread his hands. "Here's an impossible thing: a warden offering the truth, free, no price attached, to a guild that prices everything. Take it or refuse it. But refuse it knowing what I know—that your god's silence and my stars' silence are the same silence, and it has teeth."

Sable laughed—one short, sharp note, quickly strangled. "Oh, I hate him," she said to Rosalind. "I hate how much sense he makes."

"Sable."

"I'm serious. Look at him. He sits there all honorable and damp like a stray hound that knows exactly how pathetic it looks. And he's *right*, which is worse." She slid off the windowsill and landed lightly, arms folded. "Rosa. The scrap I brought back—the royal memory with the faceless crown? His dispatch tube is the same wax. Same seal. Same rot underneath. These threads braid together whether we invite them to or not."

Rosalind was silent a moment longer. Then she turned to a clean page, and Idris watched her write as she spoke, each word set down like a stone laid in a wall:

"*Entry. Idris Vale, former border warden, Third Company. Arrived seeking sanctuary. Claims firsthand knowledge of the ward-fall at Cindermarch. Carries sealed orders bearing the royal seal, contents consistent with suppression of witnesses. Vouched for by no one.*" Her pen paused. She glanced up at him—assessing, weighing, the auditor's gaze one final time. "*Vouched for by the evidence.*"

She signed it. Corvane added his mark beneath hers without a word, and something in the gesture struck Idris harder than any speech: two keepers of records binding themselves to his account, making it permanent, making it undeniable, making it *matter*. In fourteen years of service he had never seen anyone do that with the truth. Report it, yes. Bury it, often. Never enshrine it.

"You understand what you've just bought," Rosalind said, capping the ink. "Sanctuary isn't charity. Under guild law it's a contract. You shelter under our roof, you work under our rules. Trade fairly. Record everything. Stay invisible—until invisibility stops serving the truth, and then we decide together what breaks first." She pushed the lamp a fraction closer, and the light caught the edge of her oath-mark where it showed at her collar: a small sigil, faintly luminous, like a coal refusing to die. "And you train. Whatever's coming for this kingdom, it won't be stopped by testimony alone."

"I'm a soldier, not a saint," Idris said. "Training I can do."

"Good." For the first time, she smiled fully, and it changed her face entirely—weariness and iron and something almost like hope, all at war behind it. "Because I have a feeling the impossible trades are about to get much more impossible, and I'd rather have a man who knows which end of the sword is which standing next to me when they do."

* * *

They gave him a cot in the cellar archive, among the shelves of ledgers going back a hundred years, and Idris lay awake listening to the house settle and the rain give up at last against the high window.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stairs. Sable, wrapped in a shawl, carrying two cups of something steaming. She handed him one without asking and perched on a crate of records across the aisle.

"For the record," she said, "I still think you're trouble."

"Noted for the record."

"But." She blew on her cup, and in the lamplight her quick face went still in a way that made her look years older. "The client who hired me last month. Paid me good coin to steal a memory, and then paid me again to forget that he'd paid. I thought it was paranoia. Rich men are paranoid. But if there's something in the palace that eats memories—" She shrugged, shawl slipping off one shoulder. "Then I'm not paranoid. I'm early. And so are you."

She drained her cup and stood, pausing at the stair.

"Sleep, warden. Tomorrow Corvane shows us why he's really been keeping the god's silence quiet. And trust me—" her eyes flicked toward the upper floors, where lamplight still burned in the guildmaster's study, "—whatever he's hiding behind that locked drawer, it's bigger than a defector. Bigger, maybe, than all of us."

She climbed the stairs and was gone, and Idris Vale lay in the dark among a hundred years of honest books, holding a warm cup in both hands, and for the first time since the stars had gone out, he let himself believe the night might end.
