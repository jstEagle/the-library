## 6. A Child Who Remembers Tomorrow

The knock came at the guild gates three days after Idris Vale arrived, and it was not a knock so much as a persistence—a small fist against old iron, patient as rain.

Rosalind heard it from the counting room, where they had been reconciling a column of debts that refused to sum. The ledger lay open before them, Corvane's ledger now, its hidden pages tucked beneath their palm like a wound kept warm. They had been reading the god's hand for hours and understood almost none of it, and the frustration sat in their chest like swallowed smoke.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Not the rhythm of a beggar. Beggars hammered. Not the rhythm of a soldier either—Idris still knocked like a man expecting the door to open onto an arrest. This was something else: steady, unhurried, the knock of someone who knew they would be let in eventually because they had already seen it happen.

Rosalind set down their pen and went down through the guildhouse, past the shelf of bottled lullabies and the drawer of borrowed shadows, past the kitchen where Sable sat sharpening knives she never used for cutting. She looked up when Rosalind passed.

"Someone's at the gate," she said. It was not a question. Sable always knew things a half-second before they happened; it was why her memories were worth stealing and why stealing them was dangerous.

"A child," Rosalind said, and then frowned, because they didn't know that yet. "I think it's a child."

Sable's knife stopped mid-stroke. "Say that again."

"I don't know why I said it." But Rosalind did know, in the way they sometimes knew things—the oath-mark behind their ear prickling cold, Veyra silent and yet somehow *leaning*. They walked out into the grey morning together.

Ashvale had been holding its breath since the stars went out. The streets were quieter than they should have been, shops shuttered not from fear but from a strange collective forgetfulness, as though half the city had woken unsure whether it was Tuesday or whether Tuesdays had ever been invented. Refugees still trickled through the east gate with their hollowed eyes and their pockets full of things they couldn't explain owning. The wards were down. The sky was wrong. And into all of this, a child stood at the guild gates with her small fist raised for another knock that would never be necessary.

She was perhaps eight years old, maybe nine—children who remember too much are hard to age. She wore a coat two sizes too large with the sleeves rolled six times, and mud to her knees, and no shoes that Rosalind could see. Her hair was dark and uncombed and full of road-straw. She looked up at them through the bars with eyes the color of strong tea and said:

"You're taller than I remembered. Well. Than I'll remember."

Rosalind's hand found the gate latch without consulting them. "Who are you?"

"No one yet." She said it easily, the way other children recite their names. "That's rather the point. Can I come in? My feet are cold and tomorrow is very far away when you're walking toward it."

Behind Rosalind, Sable made a low sound—not quite laughter, not quite alarm. "Rosalind."

"I heard her."

"You don't understand. I've been listening at doors my whole life, and I have never—" Sable stopped herself, which was unlike her. "Open the gate before I do."

So Rosalind opened the gate, guild law be damned, and the child walked into the Ashvale Guild of Impossible Trades as if she were coming home from a long errand, and somewhere in Rosalind's chest the cold mark behind their ear warmed by a single degree, which was more warmth than Veyra had offered in a year.

* * *

They fed her first. Guild law was clear on this point, older than any of the rules about fair trade or invisibility: you fed whoever crossed your threshold, because hunger made liars of everyone and the guild dealt only in truths, however impossible. Wren—the cook, a broad soft-spoken woman who had once traded three years of her own laughter for her brother's life and gotten the better end of the bargain—set out bread and honey and salted fish and watched the girl eat with the wariness of a woman who had seen refugees arrive empty before.

The girl ate neatly. That was the first uncanny thing. Refugee children wolfed food like wolves; they had learned that meals might be the last thing ever given to them. This child ate as though she were tasting each bite twice—once for now, once for later—and halfway through the bread she paused and said, without looking up, "Wren's brother is going to write to her in the spring. He remembers her laugh even though he gave it back crooked. She should know that. It will matter to her on a day she hasn't had yet."

Wren dropped a spoon. It rang against the flagstones very loudly, and nobody moved to pick it up.

"Second uncanny thing," Sable murmured to Rosalind. "She knows names she hasn't been told."

"First was the gate."

"Third is coming. Watch."

The child finished her fish, wiped her fingers carefully on the enormous coat, folded her hands, and looked directly at Rosalind across the table. Up close her tea-colored eyes had a depth that did not belong to a child, like wells dug deeper than the water table, and when she spoke her voice stayed level and small and terribly certain.

"There's going to be an argument tonight. About me. Idris is going to say turning me out is safer, and Sable is going to say nothing is safe anymore so the word has stopped meaning anything, and you're going to stand in the doorway and listen and feel like a hypocrite because guild law says stay invisible and you broke that rule the moment you opened the gate." She tilted her head. "You're feeling it now, actually. A little early. Sorry."

"I haven't decided anything," Rosalind said.

"Yes, you have. You decided at the gate. The argument is just for everyone else's benefit, so they can feel they chose too. That's how you lead. Corvane taught you that." Something flickered across her face then—the first crack in the certainty, quick as a bird's shadow. "I'm sorry about Corvane. You carry him very carefully. Like a cup filled too full."

The room went silent except for the fire. Idris, who had appeared in the doorway drawn by the smell of food and the sound of prophecy, had gone the grey-white of old ash. Rosalind became aware that their hands were shaking, and pressed them flat against the table.

"Child," Rosalind said quietly, "what are you?"

For the first time since the gate, she hesitated. She looked down at her folded hands, and she looked suddenly, overwhelmingly young—the coat swallowing her, the straw in her hair, the mud dried in cracks on her bare ankles.

"I don't know," she said. "That's honest. Everything else I say is true but I don't know what I am. I woke up on the Marrowgate road eleven days ago knowing things. All of them true so far. I walked here because you were the only place I could see clearly." She lifted her eyes again. "And there's a throne room, and it's burning, and you're standing in it. That's the clearest thing of all."

* * *

The argument came that night exactly as promised, though Rosalind took no pleasure in being right.

It happened in the common room, around the long table scarred by a century of guild dealings. The child had been put to bed upstairs in the blue room, under Wren's guard, and the five of them who remained—Rosalind, Sable, Idris, Wren, and old Tammas the archivist, who rarely left his alcove and had emerged now looking like a bat surprised by dawn—sat with wine nobody drank and considered what could not be considered.

"She stays," Sable said, before anyone else could draw breath.

"Sable—" Idris began.

"No. Listen to me. I have been inside more heads than you've had hot dinners, warden. I know what fraying memory looks like. It looks like moths got at it. Edges gone, middles hollowed, people papering over the holes with guesses. Everyone coming out of the borderlands is like that now. Half of *Ashvale* is like that—you've seen them, standing in doorways trying to remember why they came outside." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the silver rings on her fingers, one for every memory she'd stolen and regretted. "That child's mind is whole. Whole like nothing I've ever touched. When I brushed against her at supper—just a brush, barely a fingertip—it was like looking down a well so deep and clean you could see the stars at the bottom. In *this* sky? With our stars dead?" She shook her head slowly. "Whatever she is, whatever's happening to the rest of us, she's the opposite of it."

"That's exactly what frightens me," Idris said. He sat with his bandaged shoulder angled away from the fire, the wounds from his flight from the Order still healing angry beneath the linen. "Everything the Hollow King wants, it gets. Everything that resists it dies or runs. And now a child walks out of nowhere carrying a mind it apparently cannot touch, straight to the one guildhouse in the kingdom holding pages written by a devoured god." His jaw worked. "Either she's a miracle, or she's bait, or she's both. Miracles and bait look identical until the trap closes."

"Bait doesn't get hungry," Wren said mildly. "Bait doesn't ask for seconds of honey-bread."

"Bait doesn't need to."

"Enough." Tammas had spoken perhaps forty words in the year Rosalind had known him, and the room stilled for all forty. The old archivist's eyes were wet and wide. "I catalogued the ledgers for thirty years. I read the entry from the year the second king died, the plague winter. There was a record—struck through, but ink holds if you hold it to light—of a child found on the temple steps who foretold the succession. The priests called it a vessel. The crown called it a threat." He wiped his nose with a shaking hand. "The entry ends there. Struck through. By whose order, the ledger does not say."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them floorboards creaked—Wren pacing past the blue room, keeping her watch.

Rosalind had been quiet through all of it, standing near the doorway exactly as predicted, feeling exactly like a hypocrite. Now they came to the table and laid one hand flat on its scarred wood.

"Here is what I know," they said. "Three weeks ago I audited a bottled lullaby and worried about column sums. Then the wards fell. Then Corvane bled out on his own floor telling me to unbind a king, and now I keep his ledger and his ghosts. Every plan I have made since the stars went out has been a guess wearing a confident coat." They looked around the table, at each face in turn. "But I know this: the Hollow King eats memory. It unmakes the past. And a child made entirely of future walked to our gate and asked to come in. If we are at war with a thing that devours what was, then maybe what comes next is a weapon too. Or maybe she's just a lost little girl who knows too much. Either way, guild law says we feed whoever crosses our threshold, and guild law says we shelter those who ask, and I have broken enough of Corvane's rules lately that I'd like to honor the oldest ones."

"And if she leads the wardens to us?" Idris asked. Not cruelly. Honestly. That was the thing about Idris—his suspicion was a form of care, the vigilance of a man who had failed to protect something once and meant never to again.

"Then we'll know soon enough," Rosalind said. "And we'll fight. That's what we do now. Apparently."

Sable snorted. Wren smiled into her cup. Even Tammas managed something adjacent to a smile. Idris held out longest, staring into the fire, and then he exhaled the way men exhale when surrendering to families they never asked to join.

"She sleeps with a knife under her pillow," he muttered.

"She's eight," Wren said.

"She's *bait*."

"Then the knife stays," Rosalind said, and felt the strangest flutter of something that might, in better times, have been laughter.

* * *

Later—past midnight, when the house had settled into its creaks and breaths—Rosalind climbed to the blue room to check on their impossible guest, and found Sable already sitting cross-legged in the hallway outside the door like a cat guarding a mousehole.

"You should be asleep," Rosalind whispered.

"So should you."

"I'm the guildmaster. Sleep is a luxury of the employed."

Sable huffed a quiet laugh and patted the floor beside her. Rosalind lowered themselves down against the wall, and for a while they simply sat in the dark together, listening to the small sounds of a sleeping child breathing on the other side of the oak.

"She talked to me today," Sable said at last, very softly. "While Wren was bathing her. Getting the road off her." A pause. "Do you know what she said? She said, 'You stole your mother's face from yourself, but you kept it in a locket. You keep it in a locket because giving it back feels like losing her twice.'" Sable's voice did not break, but it went thin as wire. "I have never told anyone about the locket. Never. Not under torture, not drunk, not grieving. How does an eight-year-old off the Marrowgate road know what's in my—"

"Maybe she really does see tomorrow."

"Tomorrow isn't the problem." Sable turned to look at Rosalind, and in the dark her eyes caught the candlelight from the stairwell like a thief's eyes catching lamplight. "Rosalind, I touched her mind. Just a brush, I told you that. But I'm good at reading what I touch, and what I saw wasn't just intact. It was *ordered*. Memories laid out like a ledger. Like someone had already catalogued her whole life—including parts she hasn't lived yet—and filed it all under a heading." She swallowed. "Do you want to know the heading?"

Rosalind realized their heart was pounding. Behind their ear, the oath-mark had gone cold enough to ache. "Tell me."

"'Evidence,'" Sable whispered. "Every memory in that child's head is filed under evidence. Like she's a case someone is building. Like she was sent as proof."

"Proof of what?"

And from inside the blue room, small and clear and awake, the child answered through the door.

"Of the burning," she said. "Go to sleep, Rosalind Brightwater. Thursday is coming, and you'll want your strength. On Thursday I tell you about the throne room, and what you're holding in your hands right now, and why the god isn't silent after all." A pause, and then, gentler, almost tender: "You're holding the last page. You've been holding it for three days. It's time to stop being afraid of it."

Rosalind's hand had drifted, without their noticing, to the inner pocket of their coat—to Corvane's hidden pages, to the final leaf written in the god's own hand, which they had carried against their heart for three days and three nights without daring to decipher it.

Outside the window, above the dead constellations, one star flickered.

Just once. Like an eye remembering how to open.
