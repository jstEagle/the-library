## 7. The City That Forgot Its Name

Idris Vale had spent eleven years learning to read a country the way other men read faces, and what he read from the road as they crested the last hill before Marrowgate made his hands go cold on his reins.

The city sat in the river's elbow like it always had—grey stone and slate roofs, the great water-stair descending to the Marrow where barges should have been stacked three deep. But there were no barges. There was no smoke from the tanneries. And above the eastern gate, where the painted sign of the city had stood since before his grandfather was born—the proud blue letters MARROWGATE, GATEWAY OF THE RIVER TRADE—the wall was blank. Not weathered. Not defaced. Blank, the way a page is blank after ink has been lifted off it, and he knew that difference because he had seen it once before, on the night the wards fell, when the border stones went smooth as river pebbles under his palms.

"It's not burning," Sable said beside him. She had her hood up even though the afternoon sun was mild, which meant she was frightened; she only wore the hood in daylight when she wanted the world kept at a distance. "I expected fire. I don't know why I expected fire."

"Because fire leaves something," Rosalind said quietly.

They came down through orchards gone untended, fruit swelling unpicked on branches, wasps drunk among the windfalls. No one had come for the harvest. That was the first intimate wrongness of it: a city does not forget its fruit. A city's hunger is older than any king.

At the gate there was no guard. The guardhouse door stood open. Inside, a warden's spear leaned in its rack with dust along the shaft, undisturbed, and Idris felt that like a slap—the order did not abandon posts. The order *died* at posts. Whatever had emptied Marrowgate had not needed violence, or had taken the memory of violence first, so that no one thought to raise a hand.

They walked their horses in.

* * *

The streets were clean. That was the thing that would stay with him longest, he suspected, longer even than the faces. Someone swept them. Brooms leaned against doorways. A baker's window held loaves arranged with care, browned to perfection, and behind the counter the baker stood watching the street with a smile of such serene vacancy that Idris stopped in front of the shop just to see if the smile changed.

It didn't. It wasn't an empty smile, either—that was the horror of it. It was a full smile, warm, generous, the smile of a man greeting someone he loved. It simply had nothing behind it. It was a smile worn by a face that had forgotten whose face it was.

"Good morning," Idris said.

"Good morning," the baker said pleasantly. "Lovely day."

"What's your name?"

The smile held. The eyes held. And then, gently, like a man searching pockets he knows are empty: "What a lovely day," the baker said again, and turned back to his loaves, and began—this was the part that made Sable make a small sound behind him—to arrange them again. He had already arranged them. He would arrange them until the bread went stale or forever, whichever came first.

"He doesn't know," the child said. She had been silent since the hilltop, walking between Rosalind and Sable with her hand tucked in Rosalind's, and now she looked up at the baker with an expression far too old for her face. "He doesn't know that he doesn't know. That's why they're all smiling. If you can't remember losing something, you can't grieve it." She paused. "The crier will tell you. He always tells you."

"The crier?" Rosalind asked.

She nodded toward the square.

* * *

They heard him before they saw him: a bell, rung with perfect regularity, and a voice trained by decades of practice into carrying across a whole town.

The town crier of Marrowgate stood on the steps of the moot-hall in a coat of faded scarlet, bell in hand, and rang it, and cried:

"Hear ye! Hear ye!"

And then nothing.

He waited the proper interval. He rang again. "Hear ye! Hear ye!" And then nothing, again, his mouth open on the shape of words that were not there, his face composed in the professional calm of a man mid-announcement. Around the square, perhaps forty residents stood listening with their pleasant vacant smiles, attentive as congregations, receiving the news that did not exist.

Idris watched four cycles of it before he could make himself look away. Eleven years a warden, and he had stood in squares while criers read tax rolls, war levies, plague quarantines, executions. The crier's voice was the voice of the realm itself, the daily proof that things were known and said and therefore real. This man had kept the form of it perfectly and lost every drop of content, and no one in the square noticed, because noticing required remembering that announcements used to say something.

"It feeds here," Rosalind said. Their voice was flat, controlled, the ledger-voice Idris had learned to dread a little, because it meant they were filing this away somewhere it would burn later. "This isn't a wound. It's a larder."

Sable had drifted to the edge of the square, to a well where a woman drew water with unhurried competence. "Ma'am? Your name? What do you call this place?"

"Marrowgate," the woman said promptly, smiling.

Relief flickered—and died, because the woman kept talking, still smiling, drawing her bucket. "Marrowgate. It's written on the signs. It's what people say." She held the bucket out, offering. "But when I try to think of my mother calling me home for supper, when I try to hear her voice saying it—" The smile did not waver, but something moved behind her eyes, deep down, like a fish under ice, a single convulsion of drowning. "There's a hole shaped like her voice. Isn't that funny. There's a hole shaped like everyone's voices."

Then the fish went under, the ice closed over, and she smiled again. "Lovely day."

Sable stepped back from the well as if it had bitten her.

"They keep the words," the child said softly. "It takes everything underneath them. They can say Marrowgate but they can't mean it. That's how it eats without starving them—it leaves the husk of the name so the body keeps working. Like leaving the rind after you've eaten the fruit."

Rosalind looked down at her. "How do you know that?"

"I remembered it yesterday." The child shrugged, small and matter-of-fact, and Idris felt the familiar chill he always felt when she said things like that, in that tense, about days that hadn't happened yet.

* * *

They made camp that night in the moot-hall rather than an inn, because the innkeeper had greeted them warmly and then asked them, twice in the same hour, whether they'd be wanting a room, having entirely lost the thread of their arrival each time. In the hall, at least, the emptiness was honest. Idris took first watch at the doors out of habit, though nothing in Marrowgate seemed capable of threatening them, and that was its own kind of terrible. An empty country cannot be defended against. You can fight an army. You cannot fight a smile.

The others slept. Rosalind slept badly, as they always did, one hand curled around the wrapped pages of the god's ledger even in dreams. The child slept like a stone, untroubled, and Idris had begun to wonder what kind of mind could rest easy inside a future it had already seen.

Near midnight, he heard the crier.

Distant, faint, across the dark city: the bell. *Hear ye. Hear ye.* Ring. Silence. Ring. All night. The man never stopped. Perhaps he never would. Perhaps some fragment of duty had survived the hollowing, the last stubborn bone of who he'd been, ringing news to a city that could no longer hold it.

Idris sat with his back to the door post and found, to his shame, that he was weeping a little, silently, for a man whose name he would never learn.

* * *

In the morning, his own name began to slip.

He noticed it first at the well where they filled their waterskins. A boy—vacant-eyed, smiling—asked him who he was, politely, the way children do, and Idris opened his mouth and reached for *Idris Vale, Warden of the Eastern Marches, third son of House Vale of*—

and got the warden. Got the rank, the order, the eleven years of service, all intact, all crisp. And behind it, where *Idris* should have been—a sound, a shape, the feel of a word his mother must have spoken over his cradle—there was smoothness. Blankness. The same awful smoothness as the gate wall.

His heart slammed. He tried again, deliberately, hauling on the thought like a man hauling a rope from deep water. *My name is—*

The well water caught the light. The boy smiled.

"My name is Idris Vale," he said aloud, and hearing it in his own voice steadied him, anchored him, and the shape came flooding back so hard his knees nearly buckled—his mother's kitchen, his father saying it sharp when he'd shirked the woodpile, the way it sounded shouted across a muster field. There. Whole. His.

For now.

"You felt it." Sable was suddenly at his shoulder, low and urgent. She'd seen it—he didn't know what she'd seen, some flinch, some gap—and she gripped his sleeve with fingers that could pick a duke's pocket unseen. "Tell me exactly what you felt."

"It took the name. Just for a breath. Left the rank, left everything else, took the *name*." His mouth was dry. "Why the name?"

"Because names are the deepest water," Sable said. "Everything else floats on top of them. Rank, trade, family—you can rebuild those from paper. The name is the wellspring. If it goes for good, the rest drains after." Her grip tightened. "It's feeding on you because you're the only one here who remembers being someone else. These poor souls have nothing left to take. You're fresh meat in an empty pantry."

"And the rest of you?"

"We're guild. We're bound to ledgers and oaths and a god, even a silent one. Our memories have anchors in them." She glanced back toward the moot-hall, where Rosalind stood in the doorway watching them, and lowered her voice further. "Mostly. Rosalind says the pages are pulling at us too. Everything in Ashvale is pulling at us now."

That evening Rosalind gathered them in the moot-hall and laid it out with the terrible clarity of an audit: the Hollow King was not merely erasing the kingdom out of malice. It was eating. A parasite the size of a crown required sustenance the size of a nation, and it had begun with the borders—far from the capital, where its feeding would be slow to be noticed—and it was working inward, city by city, draining memory into itself the way leeches drain blood. Marrowgate was not destroyed. Marrowgate was *stored*.

"How long has it been feeding here?" Idris asked.

"Judging by the dust patterns and the state of the bread?" Rosalind's jaw was tight. "Weeks. Maybe two months. Which means the capital still has its mind—but it's next. And it means the thing we're walking toward is stronger now than it was the night the stars went out."

The child, sitting cross-legged on the moot-hall floor, spoke without looking up from a knot she was tying and untying in a length of string. "You'll lose it worse than a breath, before the end. Your name. You'll have to be given it back."

Idris stared at her. "By whom?"

She tied another knot. Untied it. When she finally looked up at him her eyes were grave and gentle and quite certain.

"I don't remember," she said. "Ask me tomorrow."

* * *

They left Marrowgate at dawn, and Idris did not look back at the blank gate wall, because he had learned something standing in its shadow: the city was not dead, and that was the worst of it. Somewhere in whatever passed for the Hollow King's keeping, forty thousand lives sat stored like wine in a cellar—every mother's voice, every wedding day, every name—and the thief who held them wore a crown and smiled the baker's smile.

As they rode east onto the high road toward the capital, he tested himself, as he would now test himself every hour of every day: *My name is Idris Vale.*

It came. It cost a little more each time, like a rope hauled from deeper water.

And on the wind behind them, thin and clear across the empty city, the bell rang, and the crier cried, *Hear ye, hear ye*, to no one, about nothing, forever.
