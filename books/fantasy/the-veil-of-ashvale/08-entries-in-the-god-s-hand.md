## 8. Entries in the God's Hand

The undercroft of the Ashvale guildhouse smelled of tallow, dust, and old ink—the particular perfume of a place where important things were hidden by being made boring. Rosalind had always loved that smell. Tonight it tasted like a held breath.

They had locked the door behind them twice, once with iron and once with salt, and still Rosalind lit no candle until the third hour past midnight. The guildhouse above slept in fits. Idris paced somewhere on the second floor, his boots making the slow metronome of a soldier who had never learned to rest. The child—still nameless, still impossible—did not sleep at all but sat in the kitchen humming to herself, and the sound of it drifted down through the floorboards like water through a ceiling crack. Sable was out. She had not said where. Rosalind suspected she was stealing something, or giving something away; with Sable it was often both.

Rosalind set Corvane's pages on the table.

Seven of them, torn from a ledger older than the guildhouse itself, older perhaps than the guild. Corvane had kept them sewn into the lining of his coat for eleven years, and now they lay in the lamplight like shed skins, thin as onion paper, browned at every edge. The handwriting on them did not look human. That was the first thing Rosalind had noticed, the night Corvane pressed them into their hands with blood already darkening between his fingers, and it was the first thing they noticed again now: the script moved. Not literally—not when watched directly—but whenever Rosalind looked away and back, the letters seemed to have settled differently, the way sand settles after wind.

Veyra's hand. The god's own writing, before the silence.

Rosalind pulled their chair close, flexed their oath-marked wrist—it ached tonight, a cold ache like a splinter of winter under the skin—and began.

* * *

Deciphering Veyra was not translation, exactly. The god had written in the old ledger-tongue, the cipher-script every Ashvale clerk learned first and forgot last, but where a human clerk used the cipher to hide numbers, Veyra had used it to hold meaning too large for plain speech. Each glyph was a lockbox. You did not read it so much as open it, carefully, the way you'd open a box you suspected might contain something alive.

*The king was young,* said the first page, once Rosalind had worried the opening lines loose from their knots. *The king was kind. This is why it chose him.*

Rosalind sat back. Read it again. The lamplight guttered, though there was no draft.

The first king. Everyone knew the story the way everyone knew the shape of their own teeth: Aldric the Wardwright, who had walked beyond the borders of the world when the world was smaller, who had built the great wards against what lived outside them, who had come home crowned and quiet and ruled forty years of peace before dying old in his bed. A founding myth. Children learned it with their letters.

*He went out to build walls,* the page continued, *and he built them well, and something studied him while he worked. It learned the shape of a man by watching one. And when he walked home through the last gate, he believed himself alone.*

Something followed him back from beyond the wards.

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. They reached for the water flask, found their hand unsteady, drank anyway. Outside, far off toward the border, the sky gave a soundless flicker—another constellation going out, another ward folding up like a dropped banner—and beneath the floorboards the child's humming stopped for three heartbeats, then resumed, softer, as if she had heard it too.

*It is patient,* wrote the god. *It does not eat flesh. It eats the having-been. It wears a man the way frost wears a window, and the man beneath grows thinner each winter, until one day there is nothing left to warm, only the pattern of him, held in ice.*

The thing that wears the crown. Corvane's words, dying on the floor of his own study. Rosalind heard them now in the god's older, colder phrasing, and understood that Corvane had simply been quoting—passing down, all these years, a truth he could not act on alone.

The second page was worse. The second page was arithmetic.

*It has fed for a generation,* Veyra wrote. *A king's memories are rich—a crown is nothing but memory given weight—and it grew strong on him. But a single man, even a king, is a small country. It wants more. It wants the granaries of a nation. It has begun to reach through the wards it learned to make, and where it reaches, the stars go out, because a star is only a memory the whole sky agrees upon.*

Rosalind thought of the refugees in the guildhall, the ones with gaps where their mothers' faces should have been, the ferryman who could not remember the river he'd poled for thirty years. Thought of Marrowgate, which Idris swore they would find empty of names. The Hollow King was not merely hungry. It was *farming.*

And Veyra—

Rosalind turned to the fifth page and the cold in their wrist flared so sharply they gasped. The mark burned white against their skin, and for a moment—one long moment—they felt something move behind it, vast and distant, like a whale passing beneath a rowboat. Then it faded, leaving only the ache and the certainty that somewhere in these pages was the reason their god had gone silent, and that the reason was not abandonment.

*You will hate me for this entry,* the fifth page began. *Good. Hatred means you are reading closely.*

*There is a way to unmake it. There is only one way, and I am sorry, because it is a merchant's solution and you will recognize its shape: nothing is unmade without payment. Divine fire can burn the parasite free of its host—but fire must be carried, and a vessel must carry it, and the vessel must be willing. Not brave. Willing. Bravery is a mood; willingness is a contract. And the price of carrying my fire is this: the vessel pays in memory. All that they most treasure, peeled away as the flame takes hold. The fire feeds on love to burn hot enough. That is what love is *for*, in the end. I am sorry. I told you that you would hate me.*

Rosalind read it four times. The room got very small around the page, very bright, very quiet.

Then they did what any Ashvale clerk would do. They took out a fresh sheet, uncapped their pen, and began an audit.

* * *

The mathematics were brutal in their simplicity.

Item: one parasite, wearing the crown of the realm, feeding on a kingdom's remembered past. Item: one method of removal, requiring divine fire. Item: one god, currently silent—currently, if the fifth page's trembling script meant what Rosalind feared, *devoured*, eaten first and worst, swallowed before the wards fell so that no faithful voice would rise to warn anyone. Item: one vessel, willing, who would walk into the throne room carrying fire in their bones and walk out—or not—with the dearest shelves of their mind scraped bare.

Rosalind wrote *vessel* at the top of a column and stared at it for a long time.

The obvious answer was themselves. Had been themselves since Corvane died saying *unbind him* with his blood cooling on the boards. Rosalind Brightwater, mercenary, keeper of ledgers, bound by oath to a silent god whose mark had just burned for the first time in years. Who else? Idris was wounded and carried guilt enough to sink him; the flame would find his guilt and feed on it and leave him hollow before he ever reached the capital. Sable stole memories for a living—to ask her to pay her own was a cruelty dressed as a question. And the child. No. Never the child. Rosalind would burn the guildhouse down around that idea before letting it stand.

But willingness was a contract, and contracts required honest accounting, and honest accounting required knowing precisely what one stood to lose.

That was the part Rosalind kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. *All that they most treasure.* Not chosen. Not negotiated. The fire would take what it found most precious, and no one—not even the keeper of the guild of impossible trades—could fully audit their own heart in advance.

Rosalind looked down at their own hands in the lamplight. Scarred knuckles. Ink stains that never quite washed out. The oath-mark coiled pale at the wrist like a sleeping adder.

What did they treasure?

The list assembled itself unbidden, treacherously, item by item, and Rosalind let it, because a clerk who flinched from the numbers deserved the ruin that followed.

Corvane's voice saying *good work, Brightwater*, gruff and rare as summer snow. The smell of the guildhall at dawn. The particular way Sable smiled when she'd gotten away with something, which was different from the smile when she hadn't, and only family could tell the difference. Idris standing watch at the gate without being asked. The child asleep in a chair, humming even in dreams. The weight of the ledger in the hands at year's end, every trade recorded, everything accounted, everything *kept*. The memory of their mother teaching them to write their name in the dirt behind a house that— 

The thought snagged. Frayed. Rosalind reached after it and found only smoke where the image should have been, a house-shaped absence, a warmth-shaped hole.

Their blood went cold.

They checked the refugees' accounts in their head, the pattern Sable had described, the gaps the Hollow King left behind—and there it was, faint but unmistakable, the fine grey erosion at the edges of their earliest years. The Hollow King had touched them already. Perhaps it touched everyone now, everywhere, a tide so slow it could be mistaken for ordinary forgetting. Or perhaps—Rosalind's eyes went to the seventh page, the last one, the one they had saved for last the way one saves the worst news—an oath-bearer's mark was a door, and doors opened from both sides.

They turned the seventh page over with fingers that were not entirely steady.

*One more thing,* Veyra had written, and the script here was cramped, hurried, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through. *If you are reading this, I am gone—eaten first, silenced first, because I stood between it and the wards and I was not strong enough. Forgive me. But hear this: it fears the ledger. Not the paper. The *act*. A record is a refusal to forget, and refusal is the one taste it cannot abide. Write everything. Write it in your own blood if you must. What is written cannot be wholly eaten, and what cannot be wholly eaten cannot be wholly lost.*

*I hid myself in my faithful. Look for me at the mark. When the hour comes, I will need every scrap you can give me, and I will spend it all, and I will be ashamed, and it will be worth it.*

*Trade fairly. Record everything. Stay invisible no longer.*

*—V*

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

Rosalind sat for a long time in the ringing quiet, the seven pages spread before them like a map of a country made entirely of costs. Then they drew the fresh sheet closer and, in a clerk's neat hand, began the inventory they had been avoiding:

*Things I might lose:*

They wrote *my mother's face* and stopped, because they could no longer be sure it was still theirs to lose. Wrote *the guildhall at dawn.* Wrote *Sable laughing.* Wrote *Idris's terrible tea.* Wrote *the child's name, when she finally gives it.* Wrote, after a long pause, *the feeling of Veyra's mark burning cold, which I complain about and would not trade for anything.*

The list ran to two pages. Rosalind read it over, folded it once, twice, and slid it into the inner pocket where Corvane's pages had lived—no. Where they *should* live. Rosalind took them out again instead, all seven, and considered.

*What is written cannot be wholly eaten.*

Before dawn, Rosalink copied every page out in full, in their own hand, in ledger-ink mixed with a drop of their own blood from a pricked thumb—old guild trick, binding ink, the kind used for oaths and debts that mattered. The copies went into the false bottom of their strongest strongbox, and the originals went back against their ribs, next to the folded list of treasures, next to the heart.

Above, the sky was turning the grey of old ash. Somewhere toward the capital, another star went dark. In the kitchen, the child had begun humming again, and this morning—for the first time—the tune resolved into words, small and clear, drifting down through the floorboards:

*"She walks in with fire in her chest / and pays with all she loves the best—"*

Rosalind closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a coffin or a cradle, and went upstairs to wake the others.

They had a journey to finish, and a bill to settle, and for the first time since the stars went out, Rosalind Brightwater knew exactly how much it would cost—and paid forward the only coin they had left, which was the decision to go anyway.
