## 9. The Toll Bridge of Small Mercies

The river had no name anymore.

That was the first thing Sable noticed about it—before the mist, before the bridge, before the figure standing at its center with a lantern that burned the wrong color. The water slid past beneath them, grey-green and patient, and where a river should have carried a name it carried only a kind of listening silence. She'd grown up on rivers. Rivers gossiped. They told you where the fords were shallow and where the drowned things slept, and if you were quiet enough and paid in small kindnesses—a coin tossed, a song hummed—they sometimes told you more than you wanted to know.

This river said nothing at all. It had forgotten how.

"Cheerful," Idris muttered beside her. His arm was still bound from the inn, his color somewhere between parchment and worse, but he stood at the rail of the cart like a man who refused to be carried anywhere he could walk to. "A nameless river feeding a nameless country."

"The Hollow King's reach," Rosalind said quietly. They rode in the back of a farmer's cart they'd traded three honest hours of labor for, because Sable had insisted they stop stealing things. Even carts. *Especially* carts, she'd said, when the roads started filling with people whose eyes had gone soft as candlewax. You couldn't tell anymore who was watching for the crown and who was simply too hollowed-out to care. "It spreads like rot through wet timber. First the edges go soft. Then the middle forgets what it was holding up."

At the front of the cart, wedged between sacks of turnips with her chin on her knees, the child watched the bridge come toward them out of the mist and smiled the smile that always made Sable's skin prickle—the one that said *I've seen this already, and it isn't the worst part.*

"What is it?" Sable asked her. Low, so Rosalind wouldn't hear and start worrying in that particular way of hers, jaw tight, thumb moving over the oath-mark like worry-beads.

"You're going to give something away," the child said. Not unkindly. Children never understood that some sentences are knives no matter how gently you hold them.

"I don't give anything away," Sable said. "I'm a professional."

The child just kept smiling at the bridge.

* * *

The bridge itself was old work, older than any kingdom—black stone pillars rising out of the river without mortar or seam, as if the world had grown them rather than built them. Nine arches. Sable counted twice, because counting was a habit and habits kept thieves alive. Nine arches, and at the center of the span, exactly where the fifth arch would put him, stood the toll-keeper.

He was tall the way a doorframe is tall: functional, patient, impossible to get around. He wore robes the color of wet slate, and his face—

His face was fine. That was the unsettling thing. In Marrowgate they'd seen faces gone blank, smiles painted over nothing. But this man's face was whole, lined, human, with tired eyes and a beard going silver at the corners of his mouth. He looked like someone's grandfather. He looked like every ferryman and gate-guard and border-clerk Sable had ever charmed or cheated, all of them folded into one body waiting under a lantern that burned pale gold, then pale blue, then pale gold again, keeping time with nothing.

"Ho, the bridge," Idris called, because soldiers called before they approached even when there was no one left to answer.

The toll-keeper raised one hand. It was not an unfriendly gesture. It was not anything at all, which was somehow worse.

"Crossing is permitted," he said. His voice was warm and worn, like a stair polished by centuries of feet. "Five travelers, one cart, one mule, one turnip-scented future. The toll is one true memory per soul."

Sable felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped into still water. Around her, the others went very quiet.

"That's not—" Idris started.

"It is," the toll-keeper said mildly. "You may refuse. Many do. There is a ford two days north." He paused, and something almost like humor moved through his tired eyes. "There was a ford two days north. Whether there still is depends on how much of this country remains by the time you arrive."

Rosalind stepped forward, ledger satchel riding their hip the way it always did, close as a holstered weapon. "What counts as true?"

"A fair question." The toll-keeper inclined his head, and Sable realized he was pleased—genuinely, faintly pleased—to be asked. "Not your worst memory. Fear is cheap; fear remembers itself whether you want it to or not. Not your most recent; recent things haven't taken root. A true memory is one that made you. Take it out and you are measurably a different person. I will know. The river will know." He gestured with the lantern, and the flame inside guttered blue, and for half a heartbeat Sable saw shapes in the light—faces, hundreds of them, drifting like leaves on the current below the bridge. "And I will keep it safe. That is my function. What the river holds, the river holds against the dark. When the stars relight—" he stopped, frowned slightly, as if the sentence had been written by someone else, "—when the stars relight, everything here goes home."

"You know about the stars," Rosalind said sharply.

"I know about everything that crosses," the toll-keeper said, "which lately is less and less. Mostly it is wardens carrying orders they can't read, and farmers carrying griefs they can't set down, and once, a week ago, a child." His gaze slid past them all, down the road they'd come by, and softened. "She turned back. Said she wasn't finished being herself yet."

Sable's stomach dropped. The child on the cart, meanwhile, hopped down and walked straight up to the toll-keeper and looked at him the way she looked at everyone: like a door she intended to open.

"He'll let us pass," she announced to the group at large. "One of us pays. The rest cross free."

"That's not what he said," Idris growled.

"That's not what I *said*," the child agreed serenely.

The toll-keeper looked down at her, and for the first time his expression cracked—not into suspicion but into something rawer. Recognition, maybe. Or dread wearing recognition's clothes.

"Little prophet," he said quietly. "You shouldn't be able to keep what you keep."

"I know," she said. "Isn't it wonderful?"

* * *

They camped that night within sight of the bridge, because Rosalind refused to pay anything at dusk. "No one negotiates well tired," they said, "and no one gives away something true with cold hands." So they built a small fire in the lee of a fallen milestone—the milestone itself blank, its carved town-name sanded smooth as an egg—and ate turnip stew and argued.

"We split it," Idris said. "Everyone gives a little. Five shallow cuts instead of one deep one."

"No." Rosalind didn't even look up from the god-pages, which they'd been reading by firelight with the intensity of a woman defusing something. "He said a true memory. One that made you. There's no such thing as a shallow cut there. Either it matters or it doesn't count."

"Then we go around."

"There is no around. There's two days north to a ford that may not exist anymore, with your wounds, in country that's forgetting faster every day, dragging a child." Rosalind finally looked up. Firelight did something complicated to their face—half shadow, half resolve. "We cross tomorrow morning. And we cross honestly. I've been thinking about it all evening."

"Of course you have," Sable said. "Ledger-keeper."

"Everything gets recorded. Everything gets paid. That's not sentiment, Sable, it's architecture." Rosalind tapped the oath-mark at their wrist, the cold-burning sigil that had led them all out of Ashvale like a compass needle pointing at ruin. "My guild, my crossing. I'll pay. My childhood is mostly hunger and a mother I've already mourned. It's the cheapest true thing I own."

"No," said four voices, including the child's, including, absurdly, the mule's—which had turned its head around at the exact moment and blown air through its lips in a way that could only be interpreted as dissent.

"It's not yours to volunteer anyway," the child added. "That's the whole point of a true memory. It has to be given, not assigned. He'll taste the difference."

"You've met him?" Idris demanded.

"I remember meeting him," she said, with her maddening precision. "Tomorrow."

Sable lay back against the cart wheel and stared up at a sky with holes in it. Constellations gone dark like windows in a burned house. Somewhere up there, the Weaver—the constellation her mother had named her under, back when names came with lullabies attached—was just a blank patch, a negative space shaped vaguely like a woman holding thread.

Her mother's face lived in that blank patch, if Sable was honest. Every memory she had of it was tangled in starlight: learning the constellations on a rooftop in Cinderside with a thin blanket and a thinner patience, her mother's finger tracing the Weaver's line while her voice did that low humming thing, and Sable looking up from the stars to the face above her and thinking, with the absolute certainty available only to children and drunks: *this is the safest place in the world.*

She had stolen thousands of memories since. Griefs, secrets, first loves, dying words. She knew exactly what a true memory weighed, because she'd carried so many in other people's heads. And she knew what hers would be, and she had known it since the moment the toll-keeper spoke, the way a gambler knows the card before it turns.

Which was why, of course, she planned to cheat.

* * *

Morning came up grey and slow, mist lying on the river like a sheet over a body. Rosalind went first, alone, over Sable's objections, over everyone's objections—but the toll-keeper listened to whatever Rosalind offered, nodded once, and touched the lantern flame briefly to their brow. Rosalind crossed all nine arches slowly and came out the other side with their hand pressed flat over their heart and an expression Sable had never seen on them before: the look of someone who has set down a heavy bag and immediately misses its weight.

"What did you give?" Idris called across.

Rosalind shook their head. "Ask me in a year. If I still miss it."

Idris paid next—a memory he wouldn't name, though Sable watched his shoulders change as he came off the bridge, some old knot of guilt sitting differently, lighter and stranger. Then the child strolled up, and the toll-keeper knelt, and they spoke too low for anyone else to hear, and the lantern flared white, and the toll-keeper actually laughed—one short, startled bark, like a man remembering laughter from a manual. She crossed free. She'd known she would.

Which left Sable.

She walked onto the bridge with three false memories stacked in her mind like marked cards, and her palms sweating, and the plan already running.

Here was the thief's craft of it: a memory is a structure. It has load-bearing walls—emotion, sensory anchor, consequence—and it has decoration, detail laid on top. Most people couldn't tell wall from wallpaper. Sable could build a memory the way she could pick a lock: fast, seamless, indistinguishable from the original to anyone who didn't hold the key. She'd prepared three. Her first kiss, rebuilt around borrowed feeling. A night of rain in a guild safehouse, thickened with invented warmth. The death of a dog she'd never owned, weighted with grief she'd skimmed off a mark years ago and never spent.

All true-shaped. All hollow-centered.

The toll-keeper waited at the bridge's center, lantern held low, and studied her the way a jeweler studies a stone that's trying too hard to sparkle.

"Sable of nowhere in particular," he said. "Thief of memories. You've sold what you've stolen?"

"Some of it. Kept some. Returned a surprising amount, actually, if you must make it sound noble."

"I mustn't. But I notice you didn't deny it." He extended his open palm. "Your toll."

She gave him the first false memory, and watched it die.

That was the only word for it. It left her mind cleanly, smoothly, a perfect extraction—and hit his open palm and unraveled like smoke, like a lie told too long, the borrowed grief and invented rain coming apart into component nothing. The lantern flame flickered grey.

The toll-keeper sighed. It was the sigh of a man who had done this ten thousand times and been disappointed perhaps nine thousand and nine hundred.

"Try again."

The second one lasted longer. He even smiled at it, sadly, before it dissolved. The third he rejected before it fully left her head, waving it off mid-extraction so it snapped back into her skull like a rubber band, leaving her wincing.

"Child," he said—and she hated how gently he said it—"you cannot cheat a toll of truth. Not because I am clever. Because the river is honest, and honesty is the one substance in creation that cannot be counterfeited. You can fake gold. You can fake love. You cannot fake the thing that made you." He lowered the lantern, and the blue-gold light washed over them both, and in the current below Sable saw the stored memories drift past—whole lives, held safe, glinting. "Give me the real one. Whatever it costs. It goes home someday. I promise you that, and my promises are the oldest thing on this bridge."

"And if the stars never relight?" Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "If the Hollow King wins? Then my mother's face sits in a jar under a bridge forever, and I spend the rest of my life not knowing what I lost."

"Yes," he said simply. "That is the wager every honest person makes. That the dawn is real."

Sable stood on the ninth-arch bridge over the nameless river, under a sky full of missing stars, and thought about the guild laws she'd mocked for six years. *Trade fairly. Record everything.* Corvane dead in a doorway with the god's pages spilling from his coat like molting feathers. Rosalind walking into fire with their eyes open. A soldier bleeding for strangers. A child carrying tomorrow like a basket of eggs, careful not to spill a single one.

Everyone on this road was paying true prices now. Everyone but her.

"Well," she said aloud, to the river, to the lantern, to the blank patch of sky where the Weaver used to hang, "the one thing I never stole. Figures I'd end up giving it away."

She reached into herself and found it where it had always lived: rooftop, blanket, starlight, the humming voice, the finger tracing the Weaver's line, and above it all—the face. She took her time. She wouldn't get to take her time again. She memorized the memorizing: the laugh-lines deep as channels, the eyebrow that arched higher on the left, the way her whole face reorganized itself around joy like iron filings around a magnet, the specific brown of her eyes that was also, depending on the light, the specific gold, and the way she said Sable's name—her *real* name, the one nobody living knew—as if it were a good idea she'd had.

"I'm sorry," Sable whispered to her, wherever mothers go. "I'm keeping everything else. The voice. The hands. The humming. Just—the face. Just for now."

She opened her hands, figuratively speaking, and let it go.

The leaving hurt exactly as much as the toll-keeper had promised, which is to say: it did not feel like losing an object. It felt like a tooth coming out. Like a room in her house she would walk past forever and always feel the draft. She staggered; the toll-keeper caught her elbow with a hand cool and steady as riverstone; and the memory left her in a long silver thread and coiled into the lantern, where it flared once—starlight-colored, Weaver-colored—and drifted down to join the river's hoard.

For a moment, Sable stood on the bridge and tried to look back at her mother and found, where the face had been, a warm shapeless glow, like a person remembered through frosted glass. She knew the glow loved her. She could not remember loving it back with her eyes.

She was crying, she discovered, which annoyed her immensely.

"There," the toll-keeper said softly. "Paid in full. Cross, thief of memories. And when you reach the capital—and you will reach it, the river bends all its honest ones that way—remember what it felt like, standing here. Remember what you were willing to lose. You'll need that weight where you're going. The throne room is heavier than bridges."

She crossed the nine arches with her hand pressed over her heart, mirroring Rosalind without meaning to, and came off the far side into a small silent crowd of her family. Rosalind's eyes went straight to her face, and whatever they saw there made them step forward and do the rarest thing in Sable's considerable experience: they hugged her. Briefly, fiercely, saying nothing, because ledger-keepers know that some entries need no annotation.

"What did you give?" Idris asked gruffly, when she pulled back.

"Nothing important," Sable said, wiping her eyes with her sleeve, and lied beautifully, and hated that it was the last beautiful lie she had easy access to. Then she looked up, past them all, at the sky.

Because the toll-keeper had cried out. A short, sharp sound, nothing human in it, more like a bell struck wrong—and when she turned, the lantern at the bridge's center was burning black. Not dark. *Black*—a flame of perfect absence, drinking the mist, drinking the light, and the toll-keeper stood gripping the bridge-rail with his whole human face gone rigid with something she'd never expected to see there:

Fear.

"That's not possible," he was saying, to the river, to himself. "Nothing crosses *back*. Nothing comes *upstream*—"

Far down the river, in the direction of the capital, the nameless water had begun to run backwards.
