## 10. Wardens at the Inn

The inn was called the Drowned Lantern, though it stood two days' climb above any water deeper than a stream. Idris had asked the innkeeper about the name once, on the way up—before everything—and the old woman had shrugged and said that all lanterns drown eventually; some just take longer than others.

He thought of that now, standing at the rain-streaked window while the others slept, and wondered which kind he was.

Below, in the valley, torchlight moved along the switchback road. Not travelers. Travelers did not march in ranks. Travelers did not carry their fire low and shielded, the way soldiers did when they hoped to arrive before they were seen. Idris counted the lights twice, then a third time, because his hands had begun to shake and he wanted to be sure the shaking was not lying to him.

Nine torches. A warden patrol was six. Three extra meant an officer. An officer meant someone who knew exactly whom they were hunting.

Behind him, the common room breathed: Sable curled in a chair with her boots off and her knives within reach even in sleep, one hand resting on the child's shoulder where the girl had fallen asleep against her. Rosalind sat at the corner table with Corvane's pages spread beneath a shuttered lamp, reading by slivers of light, as they had every night since Marrowgate. The child stirred and murmured something in a language no one had taught her.

Idris should have woken them an hour ago. He had watched the torches instead, telling himself he was waiting to be certain, when the truth was simpler and uglier: he wanted one more hour of being a man sitting in a warm room, rather than a man about to face the order he had abandoned.

The torches reached the last bend below the inn and stopped. Forming up. Checking weapons.

He woke Rosalind first, because she was the one who decided things. He put two fingers on her wrist, and her eyes opened without her body moving at all—the stillness of a creature that had survived by never startling.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Nine. Officer's detail." He swallowed. "I know whose."

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. "Commander Ashen?"

"Veyra's mercy," he said, which was what wardens said instead of yes.

* * *

They had perhaps twenty minutes, and they spent them badly and well, the way desperate people do. There was no back door—the Drowned Lantern clung to its shelf of rock like a barnacle, one road in, one road out. There was the stable, which led to a goat track that would break a horse's leg in daylight and their necks in the dark. There was the loft, the cellar, the chimney too narrow for anything but smoke and regret.

Sable stood at the middle of the room turning her knife over and over. "We run for the track. Horses die, we don't."

"We don't know that," Idris said.

"I do. I've been poor longer than you've been noble-adjacent." She flicked the blade closed. "Goat track. Now, while they're still arranging themselves. Officers love arranging themselves."

"No." Rosalind said it quietly, and the room went quiet around the word. She had not looked up from the pages. Now she did, and her eyes found Idris, and he understood that she had understood before any of them. "They're not here for all of us. Look at how they've stopped. They're waiting to be announced. That's not a hunt formation. That's a summons."

"They'll burn the inn if we refuse."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "So we don't refuse. We answer. And when they ask who let the wards fall—" she folded the pages closed, gently, the way you close a hand over something living, "—they will want one name, and we will not give it to them."

The child woke then, or had been awake all along. She climbed down from Sable's chair and crossed the floorboards barefoot to stand in front of Idris, looking up at him with those eyes that were old in a way that made his skin crawl and ache at once.

"The soldier falls twice," she said.

"I know," Idris said. His mouth was dry. "You told me."

"Not yet," she said, and touched his knee, briefly, the way children bless things without knowing they're doing it. "But soon."

* * *

They came through the front door without knocking, because doors were for places that had a right to refuse entry. Six wardens filled the common room in a practiced fan, spears angled down but ready, and behind them, shaking rain from a cloak the color of deep water, came Commander Elira Voss.

She had not changed. That was the cruelty of it. Idris had spent three months imagining her older, harder, marked by whatever had happened on the border the night the wards tore—imagining that the event had left its print on her the way it had left its print on him, hollowing out some necessary room. But she stood there square-shouldered and gray-eyed, forty years old and iron all the way through, and the only change was a new stitching of silver braid at the collar, which meant commendation, which meant the order had decided the fall of the wards was no one's fault, or everyone's, or his alone.

"Idris Vale." She said his name like a report. "Deserter. Turned coat. Consorting with guild criminals and—" her gaze passed over the child and lingered there, and something flickered behind the iron, quickly buried, "—a child you have no sanction to carry."

"Hello to you too, Commander."

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip across the space between them, and the nearest wardens flinched, which told Idris things about the morale of the Sixth Company that he filed away. "Don't make this small talk. Do you know what you cost us? Do you know what the border looks like now? I rode the line at Wraithmere last month. There are villages out there where the people smile at you and cannot tell you their own mother's name. There is a fort where my own garrison stood at attention for me and could not remember why they were standing." Her jaw worked. "And your name is on the warrant that started it."

"That's not possible," Sable said from the wall, lazily, dangerously. "He wasn't important enough to start anything. No offense, soldier boy."

"Sable," Rosalind said mildly, and Sable subsided with the air of someone obeying under protest.

"Commander," Idris said, and stepped forward, away from the family, because this part was his and he would not let it splash onto them. "I didn't desert. I was relieved of duty for asking questions. You were there."

"I was there," Voss said, "and I signed the paper, because you were asking questions that undermined the realm's confidence in its own protection during a crisis. Questions about the king, Idris." She said the word *king* carefully, the way people step around a hole in the floor. "There are things a soldier doesn't get to wonder about."

"And look how well the wondering-less is going." He gestured at the rain-dark window, at the blank stretch of sky beyond it where the Shepherd's Chain used to hang. "The stars are out, Commander. All of them. Permanently. And you're spending nine riders chasing a clerk, a thief, and a little girl up a mountain, because a piece of paper says I'm the reason. Who wrote that paper? Did you ever see the seal itself, or just a copy?"

Something shifted in her stance—half a degree of weight off the forward foot—and Idris, who had drilled beside this woman for eleven years, read it the way he read weather.

"You saw a copy," he said softly.

"I'm not here to debate provenance." But her voice had lost its whip-crack. One of the younger wardens glanced at her sideways, and Idris saw it again: the company was not iron. The company was iron-plated wood, and the rot had gotten in.

Rosalind rose from the table. They did it slowly, hands visible, the ledger held open against their chest like a shield or an offering. "Commander Voss. My name is Rosalind Brightwater, of the Ashvale Guild. Under guild law, anyone may request inspection of our records, and I am prepared to show you every entry concerning Idris Vale, who has been in our custody—or our company, choose your word—since the night the wards fell. Every entry. Signed, dated, witnessed."

"Why would I trust a guild ledger?"

"Because," Rosalind said, "it's the only book left in this kingdom that remembers yesterday correctly. Ask yourself, Commander. When did you last sleep a full night? When did you last wake knowing what day it was? Everyone's memories are fraying now, except around the Hollow—" they stopped themselves, smoothly, but the word *Hollow* hung in the air like smoke, and Voss went very still.

"Where did you hear that name?"

"From the same place you did," Rosalind said. "In the silence after you said it aloud to yourself, alone, in the dark, and immediately pretended you hadn't."

One of the young wardens made a small involuntary sound. Another lowered his spear an inch. Voss's knuckles whitened on her own weapon, but she did not raise it, and the room balanced on a point finer than any knife.

It was at that moment, naturally, that the door banged open and the tenth man entered—the one Idris had missed, the one who had ridden apart from the torches, dark on dark—and everything went to ruin.

* * *

He wore no officer's braid. He wore the plain gray of the Crown's household guard, and he carried a sealed writ in a lacquered case, and he walked past Voss as though she were furniture, which told Idris precisely what the chain of command had become.

"Lieutenant Vale," the man said. He had a voice like dry paper. "By authority of the King's Own Seal, you are remanded to the capital for questioning regarding sedition, ward-treason, and trafficking in proscribed texts." His eyes moved to Rosalind, to the ledger, and stayed there with a hunger that had nothing human in its patience. "The book comes with him."

"There it is," Sable murmured. "There's always a book."

"Commander Voss," Idris said loudly, deliberately, "I am placing myself under your arrest. Yours. Not his." He turned and looked at her, and let eleven years stand in his voice. "You took your oath on the border stone at Wraithmere, same as me. *To hold the line between the realm and what waits beyond it.* Read the room, Elira. Something crossed. Something is wearing the throne. And this man—" he pointed at the gray-clad courier, "—doesn't smell like the palace. He smells like the thing the palace is standing in front of."

For one heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—the whole common room was the border stone at Wraithmere, and both of them were twenty-six years old and swearing on wet granite.

Then the courier moved, faster than a man should move, and the writ-case fell open, and what came out of it was not paper.

Idris had fought things beyond the wards. He knew the taste of wrongness, cold and mineral, like biting slate. This was different. This was absence—a shape made of where-things-weren't—and it went through the nearest warden like wind through a doorway, and the warden dropped his spear and stood smiling, vacant, already forgetting his own name, and the child screamed, high and clear:

"The soldier falls twice!"

Idris was moving before the words finished. Not thinking—thinking was for afterward. He got between the absence-shape and the table where Rosalind had snatched up the god's pages, and he raised his warden's spear in the grip they'd beaten into him at fifteen, and he set himself the way the drill-masters set their statues: immovable, expendable, holding the line.

The absence hit him like the memory of cold. It poured through the spear—it poured through *him*—and it began to drink. He felt it take the color of his mother's kitchen, the sound of his brother laughing, the name of the girl he'd almost married in Wraithmere, peeling them away like bark off wet wood, and he planted his feet and thought, with a clarity that surprised him: *so this is what guilt is for. It gives you something to burn.*

Because underneath the theft, something else caught light. The guilt—the wards, the night, the torn silk sound, the villages—he had carried it like a stone in his chest for months, and now the thing drinking his memories had to swallow that too, and guilt, it turned out, was heavy. Guilt was ballast. The absence slowed in him, thickened, gagged—

—and Voss's spear took the courier through the chest.

Not the absence-shape; that scattered, shrieking, a sound like tearing silk, the sound, oh gods, the sound from the wall that night—but the man beneath it, the gray-clad body, folding around her blade with a look of genuine surprise, as if it had been a long time since anyone had remembered to kill him.

The remaining wardens stared at their commander. The vacant one swayed, smiled, collapsed. Rain hammered the roof. Voss stood over the courier's body breathing hard, silver braid soaked dark, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"Sixth Company! Weapons down. All of you. *Down.*" And then, quieter, only for Idris, only for the boy she'd once signed away on a piece of paper she'd never verified: "Wraithmere stone, Vale. I remember it. I still remember it."

"So do I," Idris said, and slid down the wall, leaving a red smear, because somewhere in the exchange of all those memories he had apparently taken steel as well as loss. He pressed both hands to his side and felt the warmth pulsing between his fingers, steady and unhurried, the way blood is when it means business. "Mostly. Mostly do."

Sable was there instantly, ripping open his coat with a thief's economy, and Rosalind was there, and the child climbed into his lap as if she belonged there and pressed her small palms flat against the wound, and the strangest thing happened, which was that the pain went quiet, humming, like a lamp behind cloth.

"Twice," Idris said to her. His vision was going soft at the edges. "That was one."

"I know," she said. "Don't spend the second one tonight."

* * *

They buried the courier in the stable-yard before dawn, under stones, and Voss spoke the warden's burial words over him even though none of them were sure he deserved them, because that was what the words were *for*, she said—for the ones you weren't sure about.

Then she drew up her six surviving soldiers—five awake, one half-empty and blinking at the world like a man newly arrived in it—in the yard in the thin gray light, and gave them a choice in a voice that did not shake, though her hands did.

"The warrant was forged. The seal is compromised. As far as I can determine, the realm currently has no lawful authority above the rank of whoever can still remember their own mother." She looked at each of them in turn. "Anyone who wants to ride back down and report me for treason, ride now. No one will stop you. Anyone who wants to hold the line—the actual line, the one we swore on—is welcome to follow a deserter and a guild clerk east, to the capital, to do the single most illegal and possibly the only useful thing left in this kingdom."

Every one of them stayed. Even the half-empty one, who said, "I don't remember why, but it feels right," and Voss closed her eyes for a moment as if that hurt worse than refusal would have.

Idris lay propped in the doorway with Sable's field-stitching pulling at his flank and watched them saddle up, and felt the strange lightness in his chest where the stone of guilt had been. The absence had taken his mother's kitchen, his brother's laugh, a girl's name. It had tried to take the guilt too. But the guilt, it seemed, had already burned itself into something else—something with a spine. Purpose, maybe. Or just direction.

"Can you ride?" Rosalind asked, crouching beside him.

"I can fall off a horse with tremendous dignity," Idris said. "East?"

"East." Rosalind's hand rested briefly on his shoulder, warm through the bandages. Then they paused, looking at the gray horizon, and their voice dropped low, meant only for him. "Idris. The pages. Last night, after—there was an entry I couldn't read before. The ink changed. It's fresh. Veyra's hand, writing now, or writing from wherever it is." Their throat worked. "It says: *the door in the palace remembers me. Bring the flame to the door.*"

Above the eastern mountains, where the sky should have held the rising sun, there was only a pale and starless glow—except that Idris, squinting, half-certain it was blood loss playing tricks, thought he saw one point of light where no star had burned in months. Small. Faint. Like a lamp behind cloth.

Like something remembering how to shine.

"Then we'd better not keep a door waiting," he said, and reached for his horse.
