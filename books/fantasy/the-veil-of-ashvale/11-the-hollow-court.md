## 11. The Hollow Court

The capital had a name once. Rosalind was almost certain of it.

They stood on the pilgrims' road with the morning fog pooling in the low places, and Idris said, "Caldermoor," and then frowned, as if the word had slipped out of his mouth before he'd checked its papers. Sable said nothing. The child—still nameless, still humming that tune no one had taught her—tugged Rosalind's sleeve and pointed at the city spread below them like a spilled purse of grey coins.

"It used to be gold," she said. "I remember it being gold."

"You've never seen it," Rosalind said gently.

"I remember tomorrow, though." She shrugged, small shoulders under an oversized coat they'd bought her in Marrowgate's empty market. "And yesterday. It's only today everyone else is missing."

Rosalind did not answer that. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie, and guild law—the old law, Corvane's law—held that a lie spoken aloud was a debt incurred. They had enough debts.

They went down into the city as ledger-clerks, because clerks were invisible everywhere and doubly so here, where invisibility seemed to have become the civic virtue. Grey coats, grey satchels, ink-stained fingers that Sable had manufactured with a walnut shell and a candle flame until even Rosalind flinched looking at their own hands. The oath-mark beneath Rosalind's cuff ached dully all the way through the gates, like a bruise pressed against cold iron—a warning or a homesickness, they couldn't tell which.

The gate wardens didn't check their papers. That was the first horror. A warden glanced at them, saw grey, saw clerks, and waved them through with eyes like windows in an unlit house. In Ashvale, gate wardens had memorized faces the way priests memorized psalms. Here the faces were simply not worth the effort of keeping.

"Yesterday never happened," the child said softly, watching the warden turn away. "That's what this place smells like."

"And what does that smell like?" Sable asked.

"Like a room after a funeral," the child said. "When everyone's too tired to talk about who died."

* * *

Inside, the city performed itself.

That was the word Rosalind kept returning to as they walked the processional avenue toward the palace: *performed*. The shopfronts were open and stocked, but the shopkeepers greeted every customer with the same three sentences, in the same order, with the same cadence—Rosalind heard the identical greeting four times in two streets, word for word, like a bell rung on the hour. The cobbler mended shoes. The baker sold bread. But ask the baker where he'd learned his trade and he smiled—everyone smiled here, wide and warm and terrible—and said, "I have always been a baker."

Not: *my father taught me*. Not: *since I was fourteen years old*. Always. As if the past were a country none of them held passports for.

In a fountain square, nobles had gathered for what passed, now, for court life in miniature. Rosalind steered their little company to the edge of it, pretending to consult a ledger, and watched.

A countess in silver-grey was telling a story to a circle of admirers. Rosalind listened with the professional ear of someone who had audited ten thousand entries and knew when numbers refused to add up.

"—and then my grandfather, gods keep him, turned to the ambassador and said, *your king's wine is watered and your honor is not*, and the hall laughed until the chandeliers rang—"

Rosalind had heard that story before. Not similar. Identical. Word for word, gesture for gesture, down to the pause before *chandeliers*, down to the way the countess touched her fan to her lips. And three days ago, in a different quarter of the city, a merchant had told the same story about *his* grandfather.

Borrowed memories. Recited like scripture, worn like secondhand clothes. The Hollow King wasn't just devouring memory—it was redistributing the scraps, feeding its court on a common stock of anecdotes so that no one owned anything of themselves anymore. The countess laughed her rehearsed laugh, and Rosalind felt anger rise in them like floodwater, slow and cold and certain.

Beside them, Idris had gone rigid. "The man by the fountain," he murmured. "Grey cloak. Watch him."

Rosalind watched. The man stood at the square's edge doing nothing at all, and there was something wrong with the way he did nothing—too still, too patient, a body without an occupant. Then a serving girl crossed behind him carrying a tray of wine cups, and Rosalind saw it: the fountain's water, the marble rim, the girl herself—all of it reflected in the stone basin, in the wine, in the polished shields of the palace guard beyond—and the man cast no reflection anywhere. Not faint. Not distorted. Absent, the way a word is absent from a page where it should be.

"Servants without reflections," Sable breathed. "I read a rumor about this in a smuggler's ledger out of Caldermoor. Paid forty crowns for it and thought I'd been robbed."

"What happens to the reflections?" the child asked.

No one answered. Some questions you don't answer in a place like this, because the air seems thin enough already, and words have weight.

* * *

The palace rose at the city's heart like a wound dressed in silk. It had been beautiful once—Rosalind could see the bones of beauty in it, the soaring colonnades, the gardens pruned into geometric perfection—but everything was *too* perfect now, the way a corpse can look more peaceful than the living. The gardens held flowers in flawless bloom though the season was wrong for it. The fountains ran clear though the river through Marrowgate had run grey. Nothing decayed here. Nothing aged. Yesterday never happened, and so nothing ever got worse, and nothing ever got better either. Everything simply *was*, suspended like a fly in amber.

At the Gate of Hours—the great western entrance, flanked by statues of the first king and his god—petitioners queued in a silence that was worse than noise. Rosalind counted forty-three of them and noted, with the part of their mind that never stopped auditing, that not one of them shifted weight, coughed, or spoke. They stood like furniture awaiting a room.

A chamberlain in dove-grey moved down the line with a slate. "State your petition," he said to each in turn, and each gave an answer so bland it barely qualified as speech: a request for a tax remission, a plea for a marriage dispensation, thanks for the king's long and gracious reign. Each petitioner was waved onward into the palace depths with the same mechanical grace.

Then the chamberlain reached Rosalind.

"State your petition."

Rosalind stepped forward and let the guild do what the guild did best. "We bring a trade," they said, loud enough for the nearest petitioners to hear. "An item for His Majesty's consideration. Impossible provenance. Certified by the Guild of Ashvale."

Something flickered in the chamberlain's face—not interest exactly. Hunger, maybe, wearing interest's borrowed coat. "The crown does not purchase trinkets."

"The crown purchases memories," Rosalind said quietly. "This is close kin."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the queue, a petitioner swayed slightly, like a candle guttering. The child's hand found Rosalind's and gripped it hard.

"Name the item."

"A vial of unspent grief," Rosalind said.

It had cost them two days and a night in Marrowgate's abandoned temple district to prepare it, working from a formula in Corvane's hidden pages—the god's own hand describing how feeling could be drawn off and bottled, the way lullabies could, the way luck could. Unspent grief was the rarest vintage: sorrow belonging to a loss that hadn't happened yet, grief held in reserve, pre-paid against a death or a departure still to come. Rosalind had filled the vial themselves, drawing it out of their own chest the way you'd lance a wound, and the ache of its absence sat in them now like a hollow tooth. Grief for what they might lose. For whom. They hadn't let themselves finish that thought while bottling it, and they didn't now.

The chamberlain's slate trembled, very slightly. "Such things are not traded."

"Everything is traded," Rosalind said. "Your majesty's court taught the guild that. Or perhaps we taught the court. It's difficult to remember." Let the insolence sit there, small and sharp, a pin under a cushion. "One audience. Five minutes. If the king is not intrigued, we leave, and the vial goes to a buyer in Vhalen who pays double."

The chamberlain stared at them for a long moment with eyes that reflected nothing—not light, not Rosalind, not the sky. Then: "Wait here. Do not speak to the other petitioners. Do not give them anything."

He glided away up the steps, and Sable exhaled beside Rosalind, a long controlled breath. "You enjoyed that."

"No."

"You're lying. Guild law."

"Guild law also says record everything," Rosalind murmured, "and some things I'd rather not put on paper." They looked up at the statues flanking the Gate of Hours—the first king, young and crowned, and beside him a robed figure whose face had been worn smooth by centuries of weather, or by hands, or by something hungrier than either. Veyra, rendered in stone. Even the sculptors hadn't dared carve the god's face; you showed it only to the faithful, and only in dreams. Rosalind's oath-mark burned colder than the stone ever had.

"They're going to take us inside," Idris said. It wasn't a question. His hand rested near the knife at his belt the way other men rested hands on prayer beads. "Once those doors close—"

"Then we learn what we came to learn," Rosalind said. "That's the trade. We always knew the price would be paid on the other side of a door we couldn't reopen."

The child tugged Rosalind's sleeve again. Her face, when Rosalind looked down, was pale but steady—steadier than any child's face had a right to be in front of that gate.

"When we go in," she whispered, "don't eat anything. Don't drink anything. And when the king looks at you—" she swallowed— "he'll know what you're carrying. Under your coat. Not the vial."

Rosalind's blood went cold and stayed there. "What else am I carrying?"

She didn't answer. She pressed closer to Sable instead, and Sable put an arm around her with the fierce, unpracticed tenderness of someone who'd stolen a hundred childhoods and never been given one.

The doors of the Gate of Hours opened without anyone touching them.

Beyond, the hall ran back farther than any hall had a right to, lit by chandeliers burning with a light that cast no shadows, and at the far end, small with distance, a throne. On the throne, a figure in a crown.

The chamberlain returned and inclined his head. "His Majesty will see the traders. Come. Leave your shadows at the door."

Rosalind looked down. Four shadows lay pooled on the flagstones in the afternoon sun—one small, one slight, one broad, one their own.

They walked through the gate, and the doors closed behind them, and when Rosalind glanced back over their shoulder—

their shadow was still standing outside, waiting patiently among the petitioners, wearing their shape, and slowly, as they watched, it began to smile.
