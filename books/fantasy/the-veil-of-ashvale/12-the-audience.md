## 12. The Audience

The vial of unspent grief weighed less than a breath.

Rosalind had carried it for three days now, tucked against their sternum inside the false seam of their clerk's coat, and still they could not stop feeling it there—the way one feels a splinter too small to see but too deep to ignore. Grief, the guild's traders knew, was the heaviest thing a person could carry and the lightest thing to sell, because no one who owned it truly wanted it, and no one who wanted it understood what they were asking for. This particular grief belonged to a widow in the Lamplight Quarter who had never once wept for her drowned son. Twenty years of it, pressed into glass by Sable's careful hands, still warm as a fever.

It would buy them an audience with the king of a kingdom that could not remember yesterday.

Rosalind stood at the edge of the Hollow Court's antechamber and tried to breathe like a clerk. Clerks breathed shallowly, apologetically, as though air itself were a resource they hadn't budgeted for. They wore grey. They carried documents. They apologized for existing. Rosalind had spent two days learning the posture from watching the palace's real clerks shuffle past—shoulders curved inward like parentheses, eyes fixed on the middle distance where nothing lived—and they thought they had it down until the doors opened and every lesson fled their body at once.

The throne room was white.

Not clean-white, not marble-white. White the way a page is white before the words come, or after they've been scraped away. The walls were pale stone that seemed to drink the torchlight rather than reflect it, and the banners hanging from the rafters were blank—no sigil, no color, just long rectangles of undyed cloth that stirred in a wind Rosalind couldn't feel on their skin. Where stained glass should have told the stories of kings dead a thousand years, there were only panes of frosted clarity, letting in a light that came from nowhere, because outside, Rosalind knew, the sky held no sun worth naming. The stars had been out for weeks. The constellations over the capital were empty sockets.

And at the end of that long white hall, on a throne carved from what looked like a single piece of driftwood bleached bone-pale, sat the king.

He was smaller than Rosalind expected. That was the first obscenity of it—that the man who had eaten a kingdom's memory star by star should be so *small*, a narrow figure in robes that fit him poorly, hands folded in his lap like a patient grandfather's. His hair was white without being silver. His face—

His face was fine. That was the horror. It was a perfectly ordinary face, lined and human, with tired eyes and a mouth that might once have smiled easily. Sable's stolen scrap of royal memory had shown a crowned figure with no face, and Rosalind had braced for that, for the smooth blankness, the void wearing velvet. But this was worse. This was a man's face, worn like a coat over something else, and when the king lifted his head to look at them, Rosalind saw how the eyes did not quite move together, how the gaze arrived a half-second late, like sound across a valley.

*There you are,* said something behind the face, in a voice that was not a voice. It arrived in the marrow of Rosalind's bones, in the oath-mark beneath their left collarbone, which went cold as riverbed stone.

They had rehearsed this. They had rehearsed it in the undercity safehouse while Sable counted exits and the child drew pictures of tomorrow on the floorboards in spilled tea. *Kneel, present the vial, speak only when spoken to, do not look at his hands.* The protocol of audiences was old law, older than the wards, and clerks survived courts by vanishing into its rituals.

Rosalind knelt. The flagstones were warm. That was wrong too—they should have been cold, everything in this palace should have been cold, but warmth pooled here like blood pooling under a body, and Rosalind understood with sick certainty that the warmth was *fed*. Something down here ate constantly, and the heat of it rose through the stones like the heat of a full belly.

"Rise," the king said aloud, and his voice was an old man's voice, kind and cracked, and underneath it ran the other voice, the marrow-voice, dry as pages turning. "You bring tribute."

"Your Majesty." Rosalind rose and produced the vial, holding it out on both palms the way the guild taught—openly, so the buyer could see there was no trick in the hand. "A grief, twenty years aged, never once spent. From the Lamplight Quarter."

The king's eyes fixed on the vial, and for a moment—just a moment—the thing behind his face leaned forward so hard that Rosalind felt it like a change in pressure, like ears popping at altitude. The hollow eyes went wet. The human mouth trembled.

"He drowned," the king whispered. "My boy. He drowned in the lake at Verrenholt, and I—" The voice caught. Real tears, or the shape of them. "I have not been permitted to grieve. There is always so much to forget. So much that must be made room for."

Rosalind's skin crawled. Because the grief in the vial was a woman's grief for her son, and the king was speaking of *his* son, and the parasite was doing what parasites did: borrowing. Wearing. Reaching into Rosalind's offering and trying it on, the way a thief tries on a dead man's boots.

"You may keep it," Rosalind said carefully. "It is yours, Majesty. Freely given."

"Freely." The word turned over in the marrow-voice like a coin being tested between teeth. "Nothing here is freely given. Nothing has been freely given for thirty years. Do you know what I pay, little clerk? Do you know the price of a throne?" The king's hands unfolded, and Rosalind made themselves not look away. "Every morning I wake and I am shown what I was. Every night it is taken back. I have forgotten my wife's voice. I have forgotten the war I ended. I have forgotten whether I ever loved this crown or merely wore it until it grew into me. And still it wants. Still it reaches further. Last month it took Marrowgate. Did you see Marrowgate, clerk?"

"I passed through it, Majesty."

"Did you see the faces?" The king smiled, and the smile did not belong to him; it was stretched over the other thing like sailcloth over a frame. "They smile beautifully now. No grief, no grudges, no names to be ashamed of. A perfect city. An empty one." He leaned forward on the driftwood throne, and the warmth in the floor rose another degree, and the oath-mark under Rosalind's collarbone burned cold enough to crack. "But you already know all this. Don't you?"

The marrow-voice changed. It stopped performing the king and became itself entirely—a vast dry rustle, a library burning slowly, a whisper with the mass of a mountain behind it.

*I know your mark.*

Rosalind kept their face still. Clerk-still. Parenthesis-shouldered. Inside, everything screamed.

*Veyra's mark. The circle broken, the line through it, the sign of the silent god. I tasted it on the border wards the night they fell. I have been looking for it ever since. Little bearer. Little leftover. You are a crumb of a meal I finished years ago.*

"Your Majesty honors me," Rosalind said, and their voice came out level, which they would remember later as the finest piece of work they'd ever done, finer than any ledger entry, any impossible trade. "I don't understand what mark you mean."

*Do not lie to me.* The pressure in the room doubled. The blank banners snapped in that unfelt wind. Somewhere above, in the frosted glass, a pane cracked with a sound like ice on a pond. *I have eaten gods. Do you think I cannot read a servant's skin?*

And then—it paused. Rosalind felt the pause the way one feels a predator deciding. The whole white hall held its breath, and the warmth withdrew a finger's width from the flagstones, and the thing behind the king's face considered them with the patience of something that had worn a monarch for a generation and could wear a century more.

*But you interest me,* it said, softer now. Almost gentle. The gentleness was the worst thing yet. *You walked into my court carrying a god's scar and a bottle of grief, and you knelt, and you lied well. That is craft. I respect craft. So I will make you an offer, bearer, and you will listen, because no one else in this kingdom can offer it.*

The king rose from the throne. He descended the three steps slowly, an old man's descent, one hand on the rail, and Rosalind stood frozen at the base of them watching death walk closer in ill-fitting robes.

*You want to know why your god went silent.* The words poured into Rosalind's bones like cold water finding cracks. *Thirty years you have prayed into a well with no bottom-sound. Thirty years the guild has traded in impossibilities and called it faith. I know why Veyra sleeps. I know what happened at the wards before the wards fell. I was there. I am the only thing left that was there.*

The king stopped one step above Rosalind. Close enough that Rosalind could smell him—dust, and candle smoke, and beneath it something faintly sweet and rotten, like fruit gone over in a locked room.

"Serve me," the king said aloud, in the old man's voice, and the pathos of it was weaponized, honed. "Carry my messages. Read my ledgers—I am told you are very good with ledgers. And I will tell you what became of your god. Every hour of it. Every scream." His ruined eyes searched Rosalind's face. "Is that not worth more than defiance? What is loyalty to a silence? What has the silence ever given you but cold marks and unanswered nights?"

And Rosalind—who had asked themselves that same question in the dark of the guildhouse, in chapter after chapter of Corvane's hidden pages, on every night since the stars went out—felt the question land exactly where it was aimed.

That was the trick of it. That was the thing they would tell the guild later, when telling was possible again: the Hollow King did not lie. It offered true things. It offered the answer Rosalind had bled for, the explanation that had kept them awake, the reason behind the silence that had hollowed thirty years of prayer. All it cost was everything else.

"No," Rosalind said.

The word came out small. They gave it weight anyway.

"No," they said again, louder, and unbuttoned the false seam of their clerk's coat, and let the folded pages fall open in their hands—Corvane's pages, the god's own handwriting, ink brown-black and alive, entries describing *the thing that wears the crown* in a script that moved like water moving under ice.

The effect was instantaneous and terrible.

The warmth died. All of it, everywhere, at once—the flagstones went cold as grave dirt, the candles guttered flat, and the thing behind the king's face flinched backward up the steps like a hand snatched from a stove. The king himself staggered, caught the rail, and for one unguarded moment his face was only a face, exhausted and mortal and *frightened*, and Rosalind saw the man underneath the parasite clearly for the first time: a prisoner, watching someone raise a weapon he'd been told could free him.

*Put those away.* The marrow-voice shook. Actually shook. *Where did you get those. Those are mine. I burned—*

"You burned what you found," Rosalind said. Their heart was slamming against their ribs hard enough to hurt, and their voice barely obeyed them, and none of it mattered, because they had read these pages until they knew them like their own pulse, and fear had finally met its match in fury. "You didn't burn these. Your master's master missed some. And in them, it says what you are. It says your name."

*Those pages are nothing. Paper. Ink. Dead god's scribbling—*

"It says how to unmake you."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. In it, Rosalind learned the last thing they needed to learn in this room: the Hollow King feared exactly one object in all the world, and Rosalind was holding it, and the parasite knew it, and it could not simply take the pages because—Rosalind realized, watching the thing calculate behind the king's twitching face—it did not know if destroying them was possible, or what would happen if it tried.

Then it stopped calculating, and chose violence instead.

*Guards,* the marrow-voice said, aloud now, through the king's mouth, in the king's cracked voice: "Guards! Seize the clerk!"

The doors at the far end of the white hall boomed open. Rosalind was already running.

They would remember the crossing of that hall in fragments for the rest of their life, in the way trauma keeps its own ledger. The frost spreading under their boots from the open pages. A guard's halberd sweeping low and Rosalind going over it in a roll that tore their sleeve and the skin beneath. The second guard ahead, and no time to think, and the child's voice arriving unbidden in memory—*you stand in a burning throne room*—except there was no fire, there was no fire anywhere, the vision had lied or she had seen wrong or—

The brazier hit the floor.

Rosalind never knew, afterward, whether they struck it deliberately or simply collided with it mid-flight. But it went over in a wheel of white flame, and the oil in it caught, and the flame ran along the runner-carpet toward the dais like a lit fuse, and the throne room began—at last—to burn.

* * *

Sable was waiting at the servants' door, because Sable was always waiting at the door everyone forgot existed.

She had spent the audience crouched in the passage beyond it with her ear to the wood and her knives loose in their sheaths, and when the shouting started she had done what she did best: she had stolen something. Not a memory this time. A moment. She slipped through the door as the guards' attention broke toward the fire, and she put herself between Rosalind and the closing gap of the passage, and her knife took the first pursuer in the shoulder—not to kill, Sable never killed if cleverness would serve, but to fold him onto the floor where his fellows would trip over him.

"This way," she said, and her voice had no room in it for anything but motion. "Move, Brightwater, *move*—"

They ran. Through the servants' passage, past a scullery where a boy with no reflection stared vacantly at the flames reflected in his wash-water, down stairs slick with grease, into the undercity's throat, where the smuggler lamps burned green and the walls sweated and the sounds of the palace above dissolved into a distant roaring like surf.

Only in the safehouse, with the door barred and the child asleep against Idris's unwounded side and Sable pressing a cup of something bitter into Rosalind's shaking hands—only then did Rosalind open their fingers and find they were still holding the pages, creased and sweat-damp and whole.

Sable looked at them for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving bark, the laugh of someone who had watched her friend argue with a god-eater and live.

"It flinched," Rosalind said. Their voice sounded far away to them. "Sable. When I opened the pages, it *flinched*. It offered me everything—answers, service, the truth about Veyra—and the moment it saw that handwriting, it forgot all of it. It's afraid. The thing that ate our god is afraid of its homework."

"Not its homework." Sable set down her cup and took one of the pages, turning it toward the green lamplight, and her thief's eyes read what Rosalind's had taken weeks to decipher. "Its *bill*. Look at the marginalia—it's accounting language, guild shorthand, the same notation Corvane used. Veyra wrote these like a creditor writes a debtor. Itemized. Witnessed. *Owed.*" She tapped the page. "This isn't scripture, Roz. It's a record of debt. Everything the Hollow King took, written down, signed, and payable on demand."

Rosalind stared at her, and the terror and the defiance in their chest finally resolved into something steadier than either.

An itemized debt. A witnessed claim. A ledger entry thirty years outstanding, with the debtor currently sitting on a throne made of driftwood, and the creditor's collection agent standing in an undercity safehouse holding the only copy in existence.

"It fears the pages," Rosalind said slowly, "because the pages are proof. Of what it owes. Of what it took. Of what it has to give *back*."

"And page nine," Sable said quietly, "the one Corvane died hiding—the one you haven't finished reading—"

Rosalind had it out before she finished speaking, smoothing it flat on the table between the lamp and the sleeping child, and the god's living script swam up at them through the brown-black ink, and the first line of it read:

*What was devoured is not destroyed. What is owed may be reclaimed. Let the bearer count the cost, and let the bearer beware: the fire asks for memories, and it does not ask twice.*

Above them, muffled by fathoms of stone, the palace burned and burned, and somewhere in the white halls the Hollow King was learning what it felt like to owe.
