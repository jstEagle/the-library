## 14. The Price of Fire

The undercity of the capital smelled of wet stone and old smoke and the particular sourness of fear that had been living in a place too long. Sable knew that smell. She'd grown up inside it, in the crawl-spaces beneath Marrowgate's fish markets, before anyone had taught her that memory could be lifted out of a head as gently as an egg from a nest.

Now she sat on an overturned wine cask in a smuggler's chapel that hadn't seen a smuggler in thirty years, turning Corvane's pages over in her hands like they might bite.

They had spread them across the altar—a slab of black stone worn smooth by decades of contraband and prayer—and Rosalind stood over them with a candle stub, reading Veyra's script aloud in a voice that kept catching on things.

"Listen to this one." Rosalind's finger traced a line of writing that seemed to move when Sable wasn't looking directly at it. "'The unbinding requires a vessel. The flame must be carried, not thrown. It must be *given*, freely, by one who has something left to lose.'" Rosalind paused. "And then—here—the cost. 'The vessel pays in kindling. What burns brightest in them feeds the fire first.'"

"Meaning what, exactly?" Idris asked from the doorway, where he'd stationed himself out of habit even though his ribs were strapped so tight he breathed like a man carrying broken glass. "Speak plainly. I'm a soldier. Metaphors get people killed."

"Meaning," Rosalind said, "that whoever carries the god's fire to the crown pays for it with their dearest memories. Not random ones. Not the ones you'd trade away anyway. The ones that burn brightest." They looked up, and in the candlelight their face was doing the thing Sable hated most—the thing where Rosalind decided something alone and then informed everyone else of it afterward, like a guildmaster announcing policy instead of a person asking for help. "The brightest memories go first."

The silence that followed had weight. Sable felt it settle on her shoulders like wet wool.

"Well," she said, because someone had to. "That's a shit price."

"Sable," the child said reproachfully, from her corner, where she sat cross-legged with her back against a saintless alcove. Nobody had learned her name yet. She offered it like a coin she was saving for later.

"It is, though." Sable hopped off the cask. "You're all sitting there doing arithmetic. I can see it. You're working out what your brightest things are and whether you can afford to lose them, and meanwhile—" she jabbed a finger at Rosalind "—meanwhile our fearless ledger-keeper is already three entries ahead, deciding it's going to be them, because of course it is, because Rosalind Brightwater would rather die than let anyone else pay a debt on their behalf."

"That isn't—"

"Don't." She'd spent years reading faces professionally; Rosalind's was an open book written in terrible handwriting. "I've known you six years. You alphabetize your guilt. So let's skip the part where you give us a speech about burden and duty, and get to the part where we tell you no."

Rosalind set down the candle. When they spoke, their voice was the guild-voice, the flat fair-trade voice, the one that ended arguments about contracts: "Guild law says the keeper of the ledger bears the keeper's risks."

"Guild law also says trade fairly," Sable shot back, "and there's nothing fair about you burning yourself hollow while we stand around being *kept safe*. We're not cargo, Ros. We're not entries in your book."

* * *

It became, in the way of families everywhere, an argument.

Idris went first, because of course he did—he'd been rehearsing it since the inn, since the commander's blade had opened him up and he'd gotten back up anyway. "I'm already half-paid-for," he said, arms crossed over his bandages, jaw set like masonry. "Wardens are trained to carry things into fires. It's the whole profession. Give me the flame and a clear path and I'll walk it."

"You can barely climb stairs," Rosalind said.

"I climbed them yesterday."

"And fell down them."

"I *arrived* at the bottom quickly. There's a difference." But his hand had gone to his ribs without his permission, the old soldier tell, and Sable saw him register the truth a half-second after everyone else did: his body was a ledger of debts, and the interest was coming due. He sat down heavily on a crate and didn't argue further, which frightened her more than shouting would have.

Then Sable made her own case, because if she didn't, the silence would fill with worse things. "Think about what actually works here," she said. "The parasite eats memory. Fine. I've been handling stolen memory since I was nine years old. I know how it moves, how it weighs, how it lies. If anything in this world knows how to carry something precious through a thing that wants to devour it—it's me." She kept her tone light, professional, the tone she used with clients. "Also, frankly, my inventory is mostly stolen goods. Emotionally speaking. Half my brightest memories are other people's memories, which means I'm uniquely qualified to—"

"No," said the child.

Everyone turned. She hadn't moved from her alcove. Her voice was small and absolutely level, the way water is level.

"You'd drop it," she said to Sable. "Not because you're weak. Because you've never once carried anything you wouldn't put down to save yourself. That's how you survived. And the flame will know." She tilted her head, considering Sable with those eyes that had looked at tomorrow and come back. "You'd put it down at the last moment. Not out of cowardice. Out of habit."

Sable opened her mouth. Closed it. The strange thing about being told your future by a child who remembered it was that arguing felt like arguing with a stone wall that loved you.

"That was cruel," she managed finally.

"Yes," the child agreed placidly. "But true things usually are, and I only have room in my head for true ones."

Then Idris tried again—the wardens could be spared, there were hundreds of them, any one of them might—until Rosalind cut him off with a single raised hand, and then it was Wren's turn, except Wren didn't argue. The disgraced warden simply stood, took the oath-sigil from around his neck—the one they'd stripped him of when they cast him out—and laid it on the altar beside the pages.

"I broke every rule they gave me trying to report what happened at the border," Wren said quietly. "Let me keep one rule of yours. Let me be useful."

"You *are* useful," Rosalind said. "You're the reason we got through the gate district alive."

"Then let me be more."

"No."

It came out harder than Rosalind probably intended, and Sable watched them hear it land, watched them soften it a degree. "Wren. If the flame takes everything bright from whoever carries it—if it strips a person down to the wick—then the person who walks out of that throne room won't be the person who walked in. I need the people who walk out with me to still be *people*. I need you to remember why we came. Someone has to."

"And you don't apply that logic to yourself?" Idris asked.

Rosalind didn't answer. Which was an answer.

That was when Sable understood, with the cold clarity of a lock giving way under her picks, that this wasn't a debate. It had never been a debate. Rosalind had done the arithmetic days ago—in the dark of some watch, probably, alone, the way they did everything—and the rest of this was theater. A courtesy performed for a family that deserved to be consulted but not obeyed.

The realization made her furious in a way that had nowhere to go. Fury at Rosalind for deciding. Fury at herself for understanding it perfectly, because she'd have done exactly the same thing. Fury at the god, at the king, at the parasite, at whatever architecture of the universe had written a story where devotion was measured in subtraction.

"We should vote," Idris said.

"On what?" Rosalind asked gently. "There's one volunteer who meets the requirements."

"The requirement is willingness."

"The requirement," Rosalind said, and now the guild-voice cracked just slightly, showing the person underneath like light through a shutter, "is that I can live afterward. If any of you walk into that throne room, I can't. That's not law. That's just true. Write it in whichever ledger you like."

Nobody had anything to say to that. The candle guttered. Somewhere above them the bell had stopped ringing hours ago, and its absence was somehow louder.

It was the child who broke the silence, standing, brushing off her dress with grave ceremony, as if the next thing were an appointment she'd long since memorized.

"It's settled, then," she said. "Ros carries the flame. Ros pays the toll. And it happens tomorrow night, when the palace changes its face." She looked at each of them in turn—Iris, Wren, Sable—and her expression held something Sable had never seen on a child's face before: the patience of someone attending their own funeral and finding the eulogy accurate.

"How do you know all this?" Wren asked hoarsely.

"I remember it," she said simply, and walked toward the stair that led deeper into the undercity, where the others had found beds among the old smugglers' bunks. "Come on. Tomorrow's a long day, and I already know most of it, and none of it improves with waiting."

* * *

Sable didn't sleep.

She told herself it was watch rotation. She took the middle shift honestly enough, perched in the chapel doorway with a knife across her knees, listening to the capital breathe above them—that vast slow respiration of a city pretending to remember itself. But when her watch ended she didn't lie down. She climbed instead, hand over hand up the rusted ladder rungs to the grate that opened behind a dead fountain in a forgotten square, and sat in the cold looking at the palace.

It glowed faintly against the starless sky. Not lit—*fed*. A soft pale luminescence, like the sheen on milk left out too long. The constellations above it were blank patches, holes cut in the velvet, and Sable found herself staring at one of them—the Loomweaver, seven stars that had hung over Ashvale all her life—and feeling the specific vertigo of a thief discovering that something had been stolen from *her*, from everyone, on a scale that made her whole profession look like pickpocketing crumbs.

Footsteps on the ladder below. Small ones.

"I thought you'd be here," the child said, hauling herself up with the competence of someone who'd done it before, or would, or both. She settled beside Sable on the frozen rim of the fountain and pulled her knees to her chest. For a while neither of them spoke. The palace glowed. The empty sky gaped.

"Tell me something true," Sable said at last. "You've been rationing them all week like a miser. Spend one on me."

The child considered. Then, without preamble: "When Ros comes out of the throne room, she won't know your name."

The words went through Sable like a pick slipping past the wards of a lock she hadn't known was locked.

"She'll know she loves you," the child went on, in that small level voice, watching the palace. "That stays. Love doesn't live in the same place the memories do—I checked. But the rest goes. Your name. How you met—you'll be a stranger holding her hand, and she'll let you, because her body will remember what her mind can't, and she'll cry and not know why." The child hugged her knees tighter. "She loses her childhood. The harbor at Ashvale, the woman who raised her—gone. She loses the day she took her oaths. She loses the lullaby—the bottled one, the first entry she ever audited. She'll hold the bottle and feel like it matters enormously and have no idea why." A pause. "And she loses the memory of deciding to do this. Which is the cruelest one, because it means she'll wake up diminished and never know she chose it. Never know it was a gift she paid gladly. Everyone else will have to remember it *for* her."

Sable discovered she was gripping the edge of the fountain hard enough to hurt. "How do you know?"

"Because I was there," the child said. "Tomorrow. Or I remember tomorrow. It's the same thing to me and I've stopped asking which." She turned and looked at Sable then, and her eyes were ancient and six years old simultaneously, and terribly kind. "I'm telling you so you can decide what to do with it. You're the keeper of stolen things. This is a stolen thing. The knowledge of what someone sacrificed, when they can't remember sacrificing it." She slid off the fountain, landing lightly. "Most people would keep it. Put it in a locket somewhere. You like lockets."

"Do I?"

"You will," she said, and almost smiled. "Here's what I think you should do instead. Don't keep it safe. Spend it. Every day, tell her one piece. 'You chose this.' 'You were brave.' 'You liked the harbor best at dawn.' Make her a new memory out of the old one, over and over, until the new one is brighter than what burned. That's the only theft I ever approved of—stealing back what the fire took, a piece at a time, forever." She started toward the ladder, then stopped, silhouetted against the glow of the palace, tiny and absolute.

"Oh," she added, as if remembering an errand. "And Idris falls twice. Tomorrow. Once at the gates, and once—" her voice did something complicated, "—once later. I haven't gotten to that part yet myself. Be gentle with him either way."

Then she was gone down the ladder, and Sable sat alone in the frozen square with the palace glowing its fed and fat glow, holding a future in her hands the way she'd once held a client's bottled grief: carefully, reverently, and with the growing certainty that some things, once carried, change the carrier.

Above the capital, in the hole where the Loomweaver used to hang, one star flickered.

Just once. Like a lamp turned down low. Like an eye, in the dark, opening a crack.

Or like a god, Sable thought, getting slowly, painfully, stubbornly ready.

She went back down the ladder to tell the others: tomorrow night, the gates, the throne room, the fire. And underneath all of it, folded small against her heart like the last thing she'd ever steal, the sentence she would spend the rest of her life paying out:

*She chose it. Remember that for her.*
