## 15. Idris Rises

The fever broke on the third night, and Idris Vale woke to the sound of a child humming.

He lay in a narrow room above a dyer's shop in the capital's undercity, where the walls wept dye-stains in colors that hadn't been legal for a century—madder-red, woad-blue, a green so old no one remembered its name. The ceiling beams were low and black with smoke. His shoulder was bound in linen gone stiff with dried blood, and beneath the bandage his wound pulsed like a second heart, slow and stubborn, refusing either to heal or to kill him. The commander's blade had gone deep at the inn, deep enough that Sable had sworn under her breath in three languages while she stitched him, deep enough that Rosalind had looked at him with an expression he'd spent his whole life trying not to earn: pity.

He had lain still for three days and listened to the family argue over what to do with him.

He heard them now through the floorboards—Rosalind's low voice laying out the plan in ledger-clerk's cadence, Sable interrupting with objections sharp as stolen knives, the child's voice threading between them like bright wire. And beneath it all, the humming. It drifted up through the cracks in the boards, a song in no key Idris knew, and when he closed his eyes he saw the border again, the wards coming down in a sound like tearing silk, and himself standing on the wall with his sword drawn and nothing to draw it against.

*You let them fall,* his own order said, whenever the fever spoke. *Or you failed to stop what did. There is no third thing.*

But there was a third thing. He had learned it from a mercenary who kept ledgers and a thief who paid honest tolls and a girl who remembered tomorrow. There was always a third thing, if you were willing to pay for it.

He sat up. The room tilted; he let it, breathing through the gray rush until the world agreed to hold still. Then he swung his legs off the cot and went down the stairs one careful step at a time, because a soldier who could not walk to his own war had no business planning it.

* * *

They were gathered around the long table in the back room, and they stopped talking when he appeared, which told him everything about how they'd been talking.

"You should be lying down," Rosalind said.

"I've been lying down for three days. The palace hasn't moved." He lowered himself onto the bench across from them, and his shoulder screamed, and he ignored it the way you ignore weather. "Tell me the shape of it. All of it."

Rosalind glanced at the child—the girl they still hadn't named, sitting cross-legged on the table itself because chairs, she had declared, were for people whose futures were their own business—and then back at him. "We go in as clerks. Sable and I carry the pages into the throne room. The unbinding needs a vessel willing to burn, and that's me, and no, before you start, it's decided."

"It's not," Sable said flatly. "Nothing's decided until—"

"It is," Rosalind said, and there was something in their voice Idris recognized from muster-fields and war-councils: the voice of someone who has already paid the price in their head and is only waiting for the world to catch up. "What I need is time. The Hollow King will feel the flame the moment Veyra kindles. It will call every warden-soldier in the city down on the throne room before the fire finishes its work. I need those soldiers looking somewhere else." They met his eyes. "I need a diversion at the gates, and I need wardens to run it, and there's exactly one man alive who can make scattered border wardens believe the truth fast enough to matter."

"The truth being," Idris said, "that the king is dead and something else wears his face."

"The truth being that your order has been guarding a parasite for a generation," Rosalind said gently. "And that some of them already suspect. You told us so yourself."

"I told you some of them *dream wrong.*" He rubbed his eyes. "Wardens don't suspect, Brightwater. Wardens obey. That's the whole architecture of us. You don't build a wall out of stones that think."

"No," said the child.

Everyone turned. She was watching Idris with those eyes that seemed to have already seen whatever he was about to do, and found it acceptable, which was somehow worse than disapproval.

"Walls are made of stones that stand," she said. "Standing isn't the same as agreeing. Some of your stones have been waiting a long time for someone to tell them they're allowed to fall *toward* something instead of away."

Idris stared at her. "That's not how orders work."

"That's not how *walls* work," she said, with the immense patience of the very young explaining stone masonry to masons. "I've seen the gates. In the tomorrow I remember. There are forty-one of them, and eleven of them turn around. Eleven is enough. But—" and here her voice did something terrible, going light and sing-song the way it did when the future came through her like water through a cracked jug, "—the soldier falls twice. Once at the gates, standing up. Once after, getting home. The first one doesn't kill him. The first one is just the falling."

The silence in the room had weight. Sable's hand had gone white-knuckled on her knife-hilt. Rosalind's jaw was tight.

"And the second?" Idris asked quietly.

The child looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in the tomorrow behind her eyes, she chose—deliberately, kindly—not to say. "Ask me again when the stars come back," she said. "If I'm still remembering by then."

* * *

Finding the wardens took two days, and Idris spent both of them learning to walk like a man who intended to keep doing it.

The border order had never been numerous—a few hundred sworn along the whole reach of the wards—and of those, perhaps sixty had been rotated into the capital over the years, folded into the palace guard, scattered among the gate-companies, their oaths quietly repurposed by men in high collars who spoke of the king's peace in voices that never quite warmed. They were easy enough to find once you knew the sign: wardens stood with their weight even on both feet, always, a habit beaten in on the border where footing lied. Idris walked the markets and the barracks-taverns and the shadowed colonnades of the lower city, leaning on a walking stick he hated, and counted stones.

He found the first in a fish-market, a young woman named Brannoc checking cargo seals with the precise, joyless thoroughness of someone who had once guarded the edge of the world and been given inventory duty instead. He found three more sharing a table at the Drowned Lantern, drinking in the particular silence of men who had stopped believing what they were told but not yet decided what to do about it. He found Sergeant Ovist—old Ovist, who had trained him on the wall at Cael Marrow, who had taught him that a warden's word was the only currency that didn't inflate—hauling watch-duty at a granary gate, gray now, slower, but standing with his weight even on both feet.

Ovist looked at him for a long time. Looked at the walking stick. Looked at the scar tissue visible above his collar where the commander's blade had opened him.

"They told us you fell at Thornway," Ovist said. "Deserted, then died running. Both in the same sentence, which was clever of them."

"I fell," Idris said. "Just not the way they meant." He laid the scrap of royal memory on the barrel between them—the one Sable had stolen from a courtier's mind, showing a crowned figure with no face beneath the crown. "Look at this and tell me what your oath protects."

Ovist looked. Idris watched thirty years of service rearrange themselves behind the old sergeant's eyes, watched grief and fury arrive together like twins.

"Gods' mercy," Ovist breathed. "That's what took the wards."

"That's what *wears the king.* And in four days, a handful of people are going to try to burn it out of the world, and they'll need the palace gates full of noise instead of swords. I'm asking, Ovist. Not ordering. Asking."

The old sergeant was quiet for a while. Somewhere behind them, a town crier opened his mouth and announced nothing at all, smiling vacantly at a crowd that smiled back, and Ovist flinched at the sight of it the way all of them flinched now.

"Eleven," Ovist said finally. "Give me until the fourth day. I can promise you eleven who'll turn, and maybe three more who'll just be slow about drawing."

"Eleven is enough," Idris said, and thought of the child's voice, and wondered if she had counted too.

* * *

The night before, they gathered in the dyer's back room, and it felt—for all the dread in it—like the last supper of a family that refused to use that word.

Sable checked and rechecked her kit with the grim focus of a woman arming herself against her own conscience. Rosalind sat with the god's pages spread before them, lips moving silently over the old script, committing it to memory because memory was the one thing in the kingdom that could no longer be trusted to stay put. The child slept with her head in Sable's lap, humming that nameless song in her sleep, and Sable stroked her hair with fingers that trembled only slightly, and pretended they didn't.

Idris stood at the window, looking up through the soot-grimed glass at a sky with holes in it. Where constellations should have wheeled, there was only blankness—smooth, starless patches like scars on the dark. The Shepherd. The Lantern-Bearer. The Weeping Sisters, all five of them, gone. His whole life he had navigated by those lights; half the warden's craft was reading the sky, and the sky had been lying silent for months.

"You'll hold the gates," Rosalind said beside him. Not a question.

"For as long as a diversion can be held." He kept his eyes on the empty sky. "Brightwater. When the flame takes you—when you're paying—you won't remember why you're burning. Do you understand that? Whatever Veyra gives you to feed it, you'll give it gladly and forget you ever gave it. Everything you are doing right now, everyone in this room—you're spending them."

"I know the price of things," Rosalind said. "I keep ledgers."

"And who balances yours? After?"

Rosalind was quiet. Down in the street below, a patrol passed with the shuffling, synchronized step of the hollow ones, boots striking cobbles in perfect unison, no echo of individuality in it at all.

"Sable," Rosalind said at last. "The child. You. Someone remembers. That's the whole point of what we built, Idris. That's why the guild exists now. Somebody keeps the record when the keeper burns theirs." They looked up at him, and their oath-mark caught the lamplight, faintly warm. "Hold my place. That's your order. From me, not the crown."

Idris swallowed past something in his throat. "Yes, Guildmaster," he said, and it was the first time he'd used the title, and they both pretended his voice hadn't cracked on it.

* * *

The palace gates of Velesserin were built to withstand armies: three rings of black iron and granite, murder-galleries above, a garrison of four hundred. Against that, Idris brought eleven wardens, three sympathizers slow to draw, a cartload of official grievance, and the oldest weapon his order possessed—a warden's word, spoken aloud, in front of witnesses.

At the changing of the morning watch, when the gates were crowded with supply wagons and shifting companies, Idris walked up the center of the approach road on legs that wanted very much to betray him, and raised his voice in the carrying bellow they had drilled into every recruit on the border, the one designed to cut through gale and siege alike:

"WARDENS OF THE VEIL! Your king is dead! Something wears him! Look at the sky and count the missing stars—look at the criers who cry nothing—look at your own dreams and tell me they've been *clean!* I am Idris Vale, sworn at Cael Marrow, and I am telling you the wall you guard faces the wrong way!"

For a heartbeat, nothing. The great gates stood silent. A captain on the wall began to shout for his arrest.

Then Ovist stepped out of the third company and stood with his weight even on both feet, and said, in a voice like bedrock: "I stood the wall for thirty years. I know what the veil sounds like when it tears. I heard it in my sleep the night the wards fell, and I have never once dreamed clean since." He drew his sword—not against the crowd, but planted point-down before him, hands resting on the pommel, the oldest posture of testimony. "I say the Vale speaks true."

And one by one, like lamps catching, they turned. Brannoc. The three from the Drowned Lantern. A scarred lieutenant named Quill who wept openly and did not seem to know it. Ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen—wardens stepping out of line, planting swords, bearing witness, while the rest of the garrison milled in confusion because no one had ever drilled them on what to do when the wall itself started testifying.

It might have worked cleanly. It might have ended with words instead of blood.

Then the horns sounded from inside the walls—not alarm, something older and wronger, a note that made teeth ache and thoughts slide—and the gate companies moved all at once, smooth and synchronized and *together*, the way the hollow ones moved, four hundred soldiers with one will pouring through the gates like water finding a crack. And Idris understood, with the cold clarity of old battles, that the thing in the throne room had felt the kindling begin—that somewhere above them Rosalind Brightwater was walking toward fire, and the parasite had reached out with everything it wore.

"HOLD THE GATES!" Idris roared, and drew his sword, and the morning broke into iron.

They held. Gods help him, thirteen wardens and a limping soldier held three rings of gates for eleven minutes—Icounted them, he would think later, I counted every one—using choke-points and testimony and the desperate arithmetic of men who knew they were buying seconds for someone else's miracle. Ovist went down at the inner arch defending a fallen recruit, still shouting the litany of the wall. Brannoc fought like someone settling a personal account with the dark. And Idris stood at the center of it, shoulder screaming, blade singing, holding the line the way he had failed to hold the wards—

—and then a spear took him under the ribs, from the side, from a boy-soldier with vacant eyes, and the world tilted, and the stones rose up gentle as a mother.

He fell once, at the gates, standing up. Just as she said.

The last thing he saw before the dark took him was the sky—or rather, the absence tearing open above the palace, a column of light and ash where the throne room burned twice, and within it something vast and winged and *golden* thrashing free of a dying crown, and the wardens around his body holding the gates still, holding, holding, because a warden's word does not end when the warden falls.

*Falling's not dying,* he thought, sinking. *She said. First one's just the falling.*

Somewhere beyond the gates, a child hummed a song no one had taught her, and waited for the second fall, and hoped—against every tomorrow she remembered—to be wrong.
