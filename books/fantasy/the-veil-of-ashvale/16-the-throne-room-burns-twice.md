## 16. The Throne Room Burns Twice

The palace was already on fire when we reached it, and the fire was wrong.

It did not roar. It whispered. Flames moved along the colonnade like ink spreading through wet paper, slow and deliberate and utterly silent, licking at banners that should have burned a hundred years ago and had not. The banners were old—older than the dynasty that hung them, older than any king I could name—and they burned now with a patience I recognized. This was not destruction. This was an appetite finishing a meal.

Sable walked beside me with her hood up and her hands empty, which for Sable was the loudest scream she knew how to give. The child rode on Idris's shoulders above us, small fingers laced into his hair, humming that song no one taught her—the one that sounded like a lullaby played backward, grief before comfort, ash before wood.

"The gates held longer than you said," Idris murmured. His voice came through his teeth; the wounds from the inn had gone septic in the way of wounds taken near the Hollow King's territory, dark-edged, reluctant to close. He had wrapped himself in bandages and defiance in roughly equal measure, and both were failing.

"I said the gates would fall," the child said placidly. "I said nothing about when."

"You said—" He stopped. Adjusted. "Fine."

"Also you're going to fall down again soon," she added helpfully. "But not forever. I checked twice."

"Sparrow." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "Not now."

She went quiet, which was its own kind of obedience, because Sparrow obeyed almost no one and never without deciding it was her own idea first. Above us, past the smoke that rose in columns too straight to be natural, the sky over the capital was a black so complete it looked painted. No stars. Not here. Here even the memory of stars had been eaten.

We came into the outer court through a servant's passage Sable had mapped three nights ago, back when the palace still pretended to have servants. The corridor smelled of cold tallow and something sweeter underneath—myrrh, maybe, or the smell of a room where someone has died and no one has opened the window. Our disguise was laughable by daylight standards: ledger-clerks' grey, ledgers themselves strapped beneath our coats, Corvane's hidden pages pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. But the palace had stopped checking faces weeks ago. It only checked whether you remembered why you'd come.

I remembered everything. That was the whole point of me.

* * *

The throne room doors were taller than the guildhouse front, and they stood open, and this frightened me more than soldiers would have. A king who bars his door fears something. A king who leaves his hall open to a burning building has either surrendered entirely or is setting a table.

We crossed the threshold together, all four of us, because Idris had refused—with more heat than his lungs could afford—to wait outside as planned, and Sable had refused to let me go anywhere without her, and Sparrow had refused on principle to be told what she was and was not part of. So much for the plan. Plans, Corvane used to say, are just prayers with better grammar.

The throne room stretched ahead of us like the inside of a held breath.

Pillars rose into darkness that swallowed their capitals. The floor was black marble veined with white, and the white veins moved—I made myself look twice to be sure, and they moved, slow rivers of pale light crawling under the stone like worms turning in fruit. Braziers lined the walls, each one lit, each one burning that same whispering wrongness. And at the far end, on a dais of seven steps, sat the throne.

On the throne sat the king.

He was smaller than I expected. That was the first cruelty of it. After everything—the fallen wards, the extinguished stars, Marrowgate smiling its vacant smile, whole cities hollowed out like gourds—I had built him vast in my mind, a colossus of hunger. Instead there was a thin man in a crown gone brown with age, hands folded in his lap, feet not quite reaching the bottom step of his own dais. He might have been asleep. He might have been dead. For one obscene moment I hoped he was dead, that all of this had ended while we climbed the hill—

And then he raised his head.

His face was wrong in the specific way Sable's stolen scrap had shown: features present but uncommitted, arranged like a mask worn slightly loose, eyes that were the right color and the right depth and entirely empty of anyone home. He smiled. The smile fit him no better than the rest.

"Veyra's hand," he said. His voice was many voices laid thin over one another, a choir heard through a wall. "I wondered when you would come. You took the long road."

"We took the honest road," I said.

"Honesty." He tasted the word. Behind him, the white veins in the marble brightened. "Your god was fond of that word. Fond of all its little words. *Trade fairly. Record everything.*" The choir-voice curled around the guild laws like fingers around a throat. "Do you know what your god's last word was? Before I drank it?"

My oath-mark burned cold against my wrist, the old cold, the deep cold, and I let it burn and did not let my face change. Ten years of playing cards with men who cheated and killers who didn't know they were liars had taught me this much: when the monster offers you its best knife, thank it, and ask to see the edge.

"No," I said. "But I've read its handwriting. That was enough."

* * *

We walked forward. All four of us, though every step felt like wading upstream through a river made of attention. The Hollow King watched us come, and I understood with terrible clarity that this was the true throne room—not the marble, not the dais, but the reach of its regard, and we were crossing it, and it was letting us, and the letting was the trap.

"Stop there," it said when we were ten paces out, and we stopped, because the air itself thickened like pitch. "Close enough to talk. Far enough that your little thief cannot reach what keeps me warm." Its empty eyes slid to Sable, and Sable, to her eternal credit, stared back with the flat insolence of a woman who had pickpocketed scarier things than this and lived. "Yes. I know what you are. Memory-thief. Did you wonder why your client paid you to forget paying you? Because forgetting is my coin, little ghost, and I do not like competitors in my own market."

"I wondered," Sable said lightly, "if he'd tip."

Something moved behind the king's face—a twitch, almost amusement, almost rage, impossible to say which owned more of it.

"And the soldier." The gaze rolled to Idris, and Idris swayed and did not fall, gripping his spear like a pilgrim grips a staff. "The warden who watched my wards fall and ran. Do you know, I offered your commander a place at my table? He refused so *loudly*. You are quieter. I appreciate quiet."

"He's louder than you think," Sparrow said from Idris's shoulders, and the Hollow King's regard turned to her and—for the first time—hesitated.

It looked at her the way a man looks at a ledger entry that does not balance. "You," it said slowly, "should not be full."

"She isn't," I said. "She's *ahead*. There's a difference, and it matters, and you're about to learn how much."

The choir-voice laughed. It was the worst sound yet—all those layered voices laughing out of sync, like a crowd hearing different jokes. "Bold clerk. Bold little family. Do you know what I am doing right now, while you posture? I am eating the eastern quarter. The dockworkers forgot their mothers this morning. By dusk the harbor will forget the sea. And tonight—" the king spread his uncommitted hands, gracious as a host unveiling a feast— "tonight I finish the capital, and then the kingdom, and then I go looking for whatever is left beyond the wards. Your god built those wards, you know. To keep me *out*. It failed. It always fails. Gods fail because gods are only memories that learned to stand up, and I eat memories."

"That," I said, "is the single most interesting wrong thing anyone has ever said to me."

I stepped forward. The air resisted me like deep water, but it parted, because part of the bargain it thought it was making required me closer, and parasites are patient, and patient things always believe the next mouthful will be the last one they need.

"Let me tell you what I've read," I said, and drew Corvane's pages from inside my coat, and held them up, and watched the whole room flinch—the flames guttering, the veins in the marble recoiling, the king's loose face tightening like a fist closing. "In Veyra's own hand. Written before the silence. Shall I recite?"

"Do not." The many voices cracked against each other.

"*The thing that followed the first king home from beyond the veil is not a god,*" I read, "*though it wears divinity the way a tick wears blood. It cannot abide its own account being kept. What is written of it, written truly, written in faith, binds it.*" I lowered the pages. "You fear this paper. You have feared it for a generation. That's why you silenced every temple, why you burned the archives of Vel Marris, why your wardens tore apart my guildmaster's office looking for these. You found him instead. You killed him. But you never found the pages."

The silence that followed was the first honest silence the throne room had ever held.

"They are here," the Hollow King said at last, very quietly. "Under your coat. Against your heart. You brought the one thing in this world that can unmake me directly to my seat." Its head tilted, birdlike, wrong. "Why?"

And here it was. The moment everything bent toward. I made my voice steady, made it warm, made it the voice I used across bargaining tables when I needed a man to believe I was telling him the truth precisely because I wanted him to believe it.

"Because I want to trade," I said.

* * *

Sable's breath caught behind me. Good. Let it hear that too. Let it hear doubt in the ranks—it would make the lie sit better in its ears.

"Trade," the Hollow King repeated.

"You said it yourself. Gods are memories that learned to stand up. Fine. Then hear the truth of mine." I let my hand drift to my oath-mark, and let the pain show in my face, and none of that was performance, which is the secret of every good lie: build it on something real. "Ten years ago I swore myself to Veyra, and for nine of those years it answered me. Every morning. Every audit. Every impossible trade we closed fair and square. And then, one year ago—silence. No reason. No farewell. Nothing." My voice roughened, and I let it. "I have spent a year hating it for that. A year wondering what I did. And then I find these pages, and I find out what my god really was, and what it hid, and what it cost—and I am done being faithful to a thing that abandoned me."

The flames along the pillars leaned toward me like grass bending under wind.

"But you misunderstand these pages if you think they only bind you," I went on, softer now, the confessional voice, the voice that opens strongboxes. "Read further. Read what I read. *What is bound may be bargained. What is bargained may be transferred.* The binding runs through the vessel, Hollow King. Through whoever carries the flame. Burn these pages in the presence of a willing vessel of Veyra's fire, and the binding passes—to the vessel. To the god. Locked away somewhere even you need never taste again."

Lies. Every word after the first sentence, lies stitched from half-truths like a fence stitched from wire. There was no transfer clause. There was no escape written anywhere in Veyra's hand. There was only the unbinding, and the price of fire, and the vessel—and the vessel was me.

But the parasite had spent thirty years eating minds, not reading hearts. It knew hunger intimately and faith not at all. It could not imagine a woman walking willingly into a furnace. It could only imagine a woman negotiating her way out of one.

"A vessel," it said slowly. "Of divine fire."

"One oath-bearer, freely given. Me." I held its empty gaze and did not blink, though my eyes watered from the whispering heat. "You get the god sealed beyond your reach forever. I walk out of here alive, with my people, with my life, having sold the thing that abandoned me. Everyone profits. That's not faith, Hollow King. That's commerce. And unlike my god—" I let the bitterness ring like a dropped coin— "I always settle my accounts."

The throne room breathed.

I felt it considering—actually felt it, a pressure against the inside of my skull, delicate and enormous, riffling through me the way Sable riffled through a stranger's memories. Looking for the flaw in the ledger. Looking for the tell. And I gave it what it wanted: I gave it the year of hurt, the genuine ache of the unanswered morning prayers, the cold mark on my wrist that had burned and burned with nothing answering on the other side. I gave it my anger, which was real, and buried my love beneath it, which was realer.

Behind the king's face, something decided.

"If this is deceit," it said, "I will drink your child's tomorrow. I will drink the thief's every yesterday. I will leave the soldier a nameless husk that salutes shadows until it dies of thirst."

"You won't need to," I said. "Because I'm not lying."

Which was, of course, the most beautiful lie of all.

"Then bring the pages," said the Hollow King, and rose from the throne, and the crown lifted with it, and beneath the crown the king's borrowed face began—at last, horribly—to smile with genuine hunger. "Bring the fire. Bring your god's last ember, little clerk, and set it in my hands."

*I will consume it whole,* the many voices sang together, sweet and certain and starving. *As I consumed it before. As I will consume everything.*

I walked toward it. Sable did not catch my sleeve, because we had agreed she wouldn't, and because she knew—if she knew anything in this broken world—that some trades must be seen through to signing. Idris planted his spear and stood straighter than his wounds allowed. And Sparrow hummed her backward lullaby, soft enough that only I could hear it, and I understood the words at last, the ones she'd never say aloud:

*Almost. Almost. Don't stop now.*

Seven steps up the dais. One thin king. One crown full of night.

I opened my coat and took out the pages, and the flame in my chest—the ember Veyra had hidden in me a year before the silence, banked low all this time, waiting, *waiting*—rose toward my throat like a swallow returning.

The Hollow King reached out its hands to receive its final meal.

And I lied to it with my whole heart: "This won't hurt you at all."

The fire answered me.
