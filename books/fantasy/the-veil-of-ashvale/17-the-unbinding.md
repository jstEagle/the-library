## 17. The Unbinding

The throne room was burning the way a memory burns—not all at once, but from the edges inward, curling and browning and going soft at the corners until only the bright center remained.

Rosalind stood in that center.

The banners along the walls had caught first, centuries of heraldry unraveling into embers that drifted like slow red snow. The great windows had burst outward in the first moments, and through their empty frames Rosalind could see the sky—the true sky, starless still, but trembling now, as if something vast were pressing against the veil of it from the other side. Below, faint and far away, Idris's diversion roared at the palace gates: horns, shouts, the crash of wardens holding a line they had no right to hold. Sable had gone with them, against every argument Rosalind had made, because Sable did not argue with people she loved; she simply kissed their foreheads and disobeyed.

And the child—Wren, though she would not say so for another year—had pressed her small hand to Rosalind's chest before they parted and said, with terrible gentleness:

"You'll forget my face. Don't be sad when you can't find it. I'll come remind you."

Now Rosalind stood alone in the burning heart of the Hollow Court, and the Hollow King stood across from them, wearing his borrowed body like an ill-fitted coat.

The king had been tall once. Rosalind could see the ghost of it in the shoulders, the long hands, the way he held his head as if accustomed to crowns heavier than the one he wore. But the parasite had worn him for a generation, and the man beneath had been hollowed to a husk—a frame of bone and habit, skin like parchment on which someone had written too many drafts. His eyes were not eyes. They were apertures, dark and patient, places where light went and did not return.

"You brought me a gift," the Hollow King said. Its voice came from everywhere in the room at once—from the smoke, from the stones, from the dead air between Rosalind's heartbeat and the next. "I felt it cross my threshold. Divine fire. Veyra's fire." Something moved beneath the king's skin, a ripple like a fish under ice. "You cannot imagine how hungry I have been."

"I imagine it well," Rosalind said. "You ate a god."

"A god," the thing repeated, and for the first time its voice cracked—not with feeling, Rosalind thought, but with appetite. "Do you know what memories taste like, little keeper? A kingdom's worth? Every first kiss and every funeral, every name a mother gives her child in the dark? I have dined on this realm for twenty years, and still—" the aperture-eyes fixed on Rosalind, "—still I hunger. But your god. Your god is a vintage. Twenty years I have digested its silence, and there are pieces of it left in this world, aren't there? In you. In your mark. In every fool who ever swore an oath at its cold altars."

It took a step closer. The floorboards charred where it walked, not from flame but from absence—as if the stone forgot how to hold weight.

"Give me the vessel," it said. "Give me the flame, and I will show you your god's last moments. Every prayer it failed to answer. Every reason it abandoned you. You want that, don't you? You have wanted it since the night the stars went out. *Why.* That is the question that has been eating you alive. Serve me, and I will answer it."

And here was the terrible truth, the thing Rosalind would carry into whatever came after: the offer was not a lie. It was precisely shaped, cut to fit the wound, the way a good thief cuts a key. Rosalind wanted to know why Veyra had gone silent. Had wanted it the way the drowning want air, the way the grieving want one more hour.

That was why the bargain would work.

"Yes," Rosalind said aloud, and let the word shake, let the longing show through it like light through a cracked door. "Yes. Take it. Take everything."

They opened the ledger.

Corvane's pages lay inside it now, sewn back where Corvane had hidden them—in the spine, in the binding itself, because the guildmaster had understood that the safest hiding place for a holy text is inside a book of accounts no thief would ever covet. The pages fell open to the entry Rosalind had spent three sleepless nights deciphering, written in Veyra's own hand, in script that looked less like letters than like frost forming:

*To unbind what wears the crown: carry the fire to it, and let it drink. It cannot refuse a meal of godhood. And while it drinks—*

While it drinks, Rosalind finished silently, *it is drinking.*

*The flame does not destroy the devourer by force. Force it has eaten for centuries. The flame destroys it by fullness. No vessel built to consume can survive being consumed. Feed it until it bursts.*

*But hear me, whoever reads this, whoever loves me enough to do this thing: the fire must be fed. Fire is memory. Fire is love. The carrier will pay in the coin of their own dearest remembering, and the price will be paid in the order the price always comes: first the small warmth, then the large, then the self.*

*I am sorry. I was devoured before I could warn you. I hid myself in you because you were the only vaults I trusted.*

*Burn brightly. I will meet you in the burning.*

The Hollow King crossed the remaining distance between them in three strides that covered more ground than three strides should. Its hand—cold, so cold, cold as the oath-mark had burned these many weeks—closed around Rosalind's wrist where the mark sat like a brand of ash.

"Then we agree," it whispered.

"No," Rosalind said. "We trade. Fairly. Those are the terms. Guild law."

For one instant—one flickering, smoke-choked instant—the apertures of its eyes narrowed with something almost like respect. Almost like fear.

"Guild law," it echoed.

And Rosalind struck the oath-mark with their palm, hard, the way one strikes flint, and said the words Corvane had died whispering:

"*Unbind him.*"

* * *

The flame did not begin in Rosalind's hand.

It began behind their eyes.

The world went white and then went inward, and Rosalind found themselves standing—impossibly, lucidly—in a corridor of their own life, doors lining both walls, each door glowing faintly from within. And down the corridor, walking toward them with the unhurried grace of something that had waited twenty years for this meeting, came Veyra.

Not the silent god. Not the cold absence Rosalind had prayed to for years. This Veyra was *light*—a figure of silver and ember, vast even folded down small, with eyes like the space between stars and a face that was somehow every face Rosalind had ever sworn an oath beside.

*"Little keeper,"* the god said, and its voice was the sound the wards had made when they tore, and also the sound a mother makes over a sleeping child, and also, underneath everything, a roar held barely in check. *"Are you certain?"*

"They're eating me," Rosalind said. Their voice sounded strange here—thin, mortal, beloved in its smallness. "I can feel it starting."

*"Yes. Give willingly, and it takes slowly. Fight, and it takes fast. So do not fight. Do exactly what you have done your whole life, mercenary. Keep the ledger. Let it take what it takes, and know that I am keeping count."*

"It's going to eat you too. Through me. That's what you want, isn't it—it wants you whole, and whole is how you break it."

Veyra smiled, and the smile was dawn breaking over Ashvale, and it broke Rosalind's heart clean in two.

*"Twenty years I have been crumbs in the mouths of my faithful,"* the god said. *"Tonight I am bread again. Tonight I am a feast. Let the parasite gorge itself on everything I am—and then, when it is swollen past bearing, we will show it what happens to a thing that swallows the sun."*

The first door opened.

* * *

In the throne room, the Hollow King drank.

Rosalind felt each memory leave the way one feels a tooth leave the jaw—a bright wrong absence, a socket where something lived. The parasite's grip on their wrist tightened with each swallow, and the king's body began to change: filling out, flushing with stolen vitality, the parchment skin taking color, the frame straightening as it fed. It was becoming beautiful. That was the horror of it. It wore Rosalind's childhood like fine cloth.

First went the small warm things, just as the page had promised.

The smell of rain on the guildhouse roof. The taste of Corvane's terrible spiced wine, drunk every solstice out of loyalty rather than pleasure. The name of the innkeeper in Marrowgate who had slipped them extra bread. The way morning light came through the eastern window of the counting room, laying gold bars across the ledgers. Small warmths, guttering out one by one, and with each one the Hollow King grew brighter and hungrier and *closer*, its mouth—when had it grown a mouth, a real one, ringed with teeth like the inside of a bell—

"More," it breathed. "There is more in you. I can feel it. Deeper. Older."

Deeper. Older.

Sable's laugh, the first time Rosalind heard it, in a locked room full of stolen things. Gone.

The weight of Idris's shoulder against theirs on the road out of Marrowgate, two tired soldiers of different wars leaning the same direction. Gone.

Wren's hand on their chest. *You'll forget my face.* Gone—gone already, gone before she'd finished saying it would go, and Rosalind reached for it and found only a smooth white wall where the child should have been, and understood for the first time in their life what the refugees at the gates had carried. Not emptiness. Emptiness would have been mercy. This was knowing that something precious had existed in a place where nothing now remained.

"Do you feel it?" the Hollow King crooned. It was radiant now, towering, its borrowed body shedding years like water. "Do you feel yourself becoming *mine*?"

"I feel you getting full," Rosalind said.

Through the white blaze of the god's presence, through the corridor of doors where Veyra walked beside them touching each threshold as it opened—*this one I keep, this one it may have, this one we give freely*—Rosalind felt the change beginning.

The parasite was swelling.

It had eaten a kingdom piecemeal for twenty years, sipping, rationing, sustaining itself forever on a realm-sized diet of small losses. It had never been offered a *meal*. It had never been offered a god poured through a willing vessel, undiluted, uncapped, the whole silver burning river of it. And gods, unlike memories, do not diminish when consumed. Gods expand to fill whatever contains them.

The Hollow King's body creaked.

"What—" it said, and its voice was losing its everywhere-ness, pulling inward, localizing, becoming the voice of one throat instead of a hundred shadows. "What is *in* you—"

"Everything I ever kept safe," Rosalind said. "You should have read the ledger first. Rule one: record everything. Rule two—" they were laughing now, laughing through tears they couldn't remember the names of the people they were crying for, "—rule two is *trade fairly*. You never once traded fairly."

The king's skin split along the arms like overripe fruit.

Beneath it was not flesh. Beneath it was *hunger*—a shape of pure appetite, ancient and enormous and suddenly, catastrophically overstuffed, a thing that had made itself into a bottomless vessel and was now discovering, with the slow dawning horror of the very old, that there is no such thing as bottomless.

The dying monarch inside it surfaced like a drowning man breaking water. For one moment Rosalind saw him plainly—an old man's face, exhausted and astonished, eyes suddenly human and wet and wide with decades of captivity.

"It's *heavy*," the king gasped. "Oh—oh, it's heavy, what *is* it—"

"Hold on," Rosalind told him. "One hour. Hold on one hour and it ends."

And then Veyra stopped walking down the corridor, turned to face the final door—the last door, the one at the end, the one radiating heat like a forge mouth—and put both luminous hands against it, and looked back at Rosalind with an expression no theology had ever managed to describe.

*"This one is mine,"* the god said. *"The oldest entry. Do you remember writing it?"*

Rosalind didn't. That memory was ash already; they could feel the hole where it had been, and knew without knowing how they knew that it contained a snowy night, a bloodied stranger, an oath spoken over a ledger, the founding of everything.

*"No,"* Rosalind admitted.

*"Good,"* said Veyra, and there was grief in it, and pride, and love beyond all measuring. *"Then you cannot hesitate. Open it for me, keeper. Open everything. Let me out."*

Rosalind opened the door.

* * *

Light did not fill the throne room. Light *became* the throne room, and the walls, and the concept of rooms.

Veyra came out of Rosalind's oath-mark like a river out of a broken dam—twenty years of devoured godhead rekindled by a mortal's freely paid memories, roaring up through the mark, through the arm, through the whole standing body of one stubborn guild clerk who had audited their way to the end of the world. Rosalind felt the god pass through them and knew, in the last clear instant, that they were the wick and Veyra was the candle and this was what the page had meant: *burn brightly. I will meet you in the burning.*

They met.

The Hollow King screamed.

It was still trying to hold all of it—the kingdom's memories, the stolen years, the royal body it wore, and now an entire god pouring into it faster than any vessel could contain. The shape beneath the king's skin thrashed, expanded, thinned to translucence at its edges as the sheer volume of what it had swallowed began to tear it apart from within. It lashed out blindly, apertures whirling, and where it struck the burning air simply closed around it like water around a stone.

*"Together,"* Veyra said—or thought, or was, the distinction dissolved somewhere above the flames. *"Keeper. Last entry. Write with me."*

And Rosalind, half-gone, half-lit, standing in a fire that did not hurt because it recognized them, raised their hand and wrote in the air in Veyra's frost-script the simplest and most terrible entry the guild had ever recorded:

*Received: one god, returned. Received: one parasite, surrendered. Payment rendered in full by R. Brightwater, who regrets nothing and remembers less. Account closed.*

The Hollow King burst.

There was no explosion. There was an *unmaking*—the parasite coming apart into the ten thousand fragments of everything it had ever eaten, every memory it had hoarded tearing loose and streaming upward through the shattered roof into the waiting dark, homeward, like birds released. The hunger-shape thinned to a scream, thinned to a whisper, thinned to a single dark mote that Veyra's light touched gently, almost kindly, and extinguished.

And the king fell.

Rosalind caught him. Some part of them that still worked on instinct—guild training, muscle memory deeper than memory—got an arm under the old man's shoulders as the divine fire withdrew, folding back down into the oath-mark, dimming to a coal, to a spark, to a faint warm pulse like a second heartbeat.

The throne room was ash and starlight. Because—Rosalind lifted their head, and their breath caught in whatever remained of them—through the ruined roof, the sky was changing.

One star. A single point of light, tentative as a struck match, kindling above the capital.

Then two. Then five.

The old man in Rosalind's arms was breathing shallowly. His eyes fluttered open—human eyes, mortal eyes, eyes with perhaps an hour left in them—and he looked up at the face of the person who had saved him, and saw a stranger.

"Who," the king rasped, "are you?"

Rosalind opened their mouth to answer.

And found, with a calm that was somehow worse than panic, that they did not entirely know.

The mark on their wrist pulsed warm—*here*, it seemed to say, *I am here, you are here, that much remains*—and above them the stars kept relighting, constellation by constellation, and somewhere beyond the palace gates a child was humming a song no one had taught her, waiting to come remind them.

"My name," Rosalind said slowly, testing the words like thin ice, "is Brightwater. I believe—I believe you should tell no one else that yet. Not until I'm sure I've earned it back."

The dying king almost smiled.

"An honest merchant," he whispered. "In my court, at last."

Above the burning hall, the constellation of the Keeper—the one that had been blank for twenty years—began, star by patient star, to shine.

End of Chapter 17.
