## 18. Half-Remembered

Sable had stolen ten thousand memories in her life, and she knew every way a mind could lose something.

She knew the smooth blank of a memory taken whole—clean as a lifted coin, the pocket still holding its shape. She knew the ragged tear of one ripped out by force, all edges and afterpain. She knew the slow erosion of forgetting, the way grief wore names soft as river stones.

What she saw when Rosalind Brightwater opened their eyes on the smoking floor of the throne room was none of these.

It was emptiness. Not absence—a *room*, swept clean, waiting to be filled with someone else's furniture.

"Rosalind," she said, and her voice came out wrong, small, like a child's voice in a large house. "Rosalind, look at me."

They looked at her. Their eyes were the same grey-green they had always been—the color of the Ashvale river in March—but there was no one behind them she recognized. No ledger-keeper's caution. No mercenary's arithmetic. No flicker of the joke they'd made at the toll bridge, three days and one lifetime ago: *If we die out here, I'm haunting you specifically.*

"Who—" Rosalind began, and stopped, because their own voice seemed to surprise them. They pressed a hand to their chest, where the oath-mark sat above the heart, and Sable watched them find it the way a man finds a scar he doesn't remember earning. The mark was dim now. Not dark. Dim, like an ember under ash, breathing.

"You're Rosalind Brightwater," Sable said. She took their hand. It was filthy, burned at the fingertips, trembling. "You're my friend. You're—" Her throat closed. "You're the guildmaster of Ashvale, and you just saved the whole rotting world, and you don't know me at all, do you."

Rosalind studied her face with the careful politeness of a stranger. "I know you're crying," they said. "I'm sorry. I feel I ought to know why."

That was when Sable understood what the god had done—not taken, but *spent*, the way you spend a purse down to nothing, coin by coin, buying fire. Every dearest thing, paid out to kindle Veyra. Childhood first. Oaths next. And last of all, apparently, the people.

The throne room was still burning. It would burn for hours yet; the flame that had unmade the Hollow King did not hurry for anyone. But it burned *clean*, white-gold and silent, and where it touched the black residue of the parasite the stone came up shining, as though the room were being scoured rather than destroyed. Through the great shattered windows, dawn was coming up over the capital in colors Sable had no memory of ever seeing—had there been sunrises like this before? Had she simply never been awake for them?

The child sat cross-legged beside Rosalind's head, humming. The song had no name. It had never needed one; it belonged to tomorrow, and tomorrow had finally arrived to collect it.

"I remember some things," Rosalind said slowly, testing each word like a loose stair. "I remember the ledger. I remember rules. Trade fairly. Record everything." A pause. "Stay invisible." They frowned. "I don't think I like that rule anymore, though I couldn't tell you why."

"Because it got Corvane killed," Sable said, and then wished she hadn't, because Rosalind only blinked—*Corvane* meaning nothing—and she had to turn away and press the heel of her hand against her eyes until the burning stopped.

The child patted Rosalind's hair, once, matter-of-factly, as if this were a thing she had seen coming. Perhaps she had. Perhaps she remembered it happening, which meant, in whatever strange country she kept inside herself, it already had.

"He'll be all right," the child said. Not to Sable. To the room. Or maybe to the future.

* * *

The king lay beneath the window where the light came in, and he was dying the way ordinary men die—slowly, messily, with blood in his mouth and his hands cold.

For thirty years, give or take, something else had lived behind that face. Now the parasite was ash on the wind and the man who remained was old—far older than the crown had let him look—and frightened, and *present*, horribly present, aware of everything he had done and everything done through him, the way a puppet might be aware if the strings were cut and it could finally see its own dangling hands.

Idris Vale knelt beside him with a warden's discipline, doing what wardens do for the dying, which is mostly nothing and letting them know someone is there. His side was bound and soaked through twice since the gates; he should have been lying down himself, and refused to be. The scattered border wardens he'd rallied stood along the walls, uncertain whether they were guards or jailers or mourners. Probably all three.

"Sable." The king's voice was a ruin, but it found her. "You were at the toll bridge. Small Mercies. You paid honest, in the end."

She went cold. "How could you possibly—"

"It saw through your eyes sometimes. When you slept near its wards." He coughed, wet and long. "It kept... accounts. I heard them. All those years, I heard everything it heard, and could say nothing, and by the end I could barely remember which thoughts were mine." His eyes—brown, human, exhausted—moved to Rosalind. "Is that one awake?"

"They're awake," Sable said carefully.

"Good. Good." The king gathered breath the way drowning men gather water. "Help me sit up. If I'm to beg, I'll do it on my feet. Or near enough."

They raised him against the window frame. Up close, without the crown—which lay somewhere in the scorched center of the room, cracked clean through—he was small. A grandfather's body. Someone must have loved him once, before. Someone had taught him to ride, probably. Someone had worried about him catching chill.

He looked at Rosalind for a long moment. Rosalind looked back, polite and empty, a stranger at a stranger's deathbed.

"I don't know you," Rosalind said. "I'm told that matters. Everyone here seems very sad about it."

"It matters," the king said. "More than anything I can say." He turned to Sable instead—to her relief, because meeting those empty eyes any longer felt like standing in a house where the family had just left forever. "Then let me ask you. You keep memories. That's your trade, isn't it? Stealing them."

"Giving them back, mostly," Sable said. "Lately."

"Then hear mine, and judge whether I deserve keeping." His voice steadied. Whatever the parasite had worn him for, the man underneath had been a king once, and it showed now, in the ruin. "Thirty years ago I rode beyond the wards. My father ordered it. There was a thing in the dark past the borders, and my father wanted it measured, named, catalogued. Kings are terrible at leaving darkness alone." A thin smile, all teeth and pain. "It measured *me*. It climbed in through the wound my pride made and it wore me home like a coat, and I watched—from a little locked room inside myself—while it put out stars. While it emptied Marrowgate. While it sent soldiers to kill a guildmaster for knowing too much." His gaze slid toward the wall, toward the east, toward Ashvale far away. "I screamed at it for twenty years. Then I stopped screaming. That's the part I can't forgive myself for. Not what it did. That I stopped screaming."

Silence, except the fire, singing softly in its white key.

"So," the king said. "Ask me anything. Condemn me however you like. Only—don't make them carry it." He nodded at Rosalind. "Whatever hatred is owed for what wore my face, don't hand it to someone who can't remember why they'd hold it. Let it end with me. Let me be the last one who pays."

Sable looked at Rosalind—at the swept-clean room of them, waiting for furniture—and understood exactly what the man was asking. He wasn't asking forgiveness for himself. He was asking that no debt be inherited. He was asking, in the language of ledgers, that the account be closed.

She crouched so their eyes were level. "You want to know if you're forgiven," she said. "Here's what I know about forgiveness, having handled other people's feelings professionally for most of a disreputable career. It isn't a thing you earn. It's a thing you're given, freely, badly, by people who have no business giving it. Which means it's not yours to demand." She glanced at Rosalind. "But it's theirs to offer. Even half-remembering. Maybe especially."

Rosalind tilted their head, considering the dying man with the same attention they'd probably once given a suspicious ledger entry. Then they reached out and took his hand—the hand that had signed the orders, worn the parasite, extinguished the stars—and held it the way you hold the hand of anyone frightened and dying, which is to say: without conditions.

"I don't know what you did," Rosalind said. "But I know what it costs to be locked inside yourself. I seem to be living there now." A pause, and something almost like their old dry humor surfaced, ghost-faint. "So. Forgiven. On the condition that whoever buries you writes down where, properly, in a ledger, because I hate untidy records and I'm told that's one of my defining traits."

The king laughed—it hurt him visibly, and he did it anyway, and it was the first sound from him in thirty years, Sable thought, that the parasite had not shaped. "A fair trade," he whispered. "Record everything. Yes. I was told that once, long ago, by a woman with ink on her fingers." His eyes were going glassy, the light behind them guttering down to the wick. "Tell her... tell whoever keeps her book..."

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The hour ran out the way mortal hours do, quietly, mid-sentence, and the King of Ashvale died on the floor of his own burning throne room as just a man—old, afraid, forgiven, free.

The child hummed a little louder, as if to fill the space he left.

* * *

They buried him at noon in the palace garden, under a pear tree that had somehow survived thirty years of hollow reign, and Sable wrote the location in the back of Rosalind's ledger in her best forgery of Veyra's script, because Rosalind asked her to and because it made them smile, and she would have forged scripture itself to keep seeing that.

Afterward, the world began to come home.

Sable felt it before she understood it—a tingling at the base of her skull, the professional's awareness of memories moving through a crowd, except these weren't moving *toward* her. They were moving *home*. Everywhere in the city, people were stopping mid-stride, pressing hands to their mouths, weeping or laughing or sitting down hard on steps and curbstones. A servant girl dropped a tray of cups and stared at her own reflection in the spilled wine and said her own name aloud, over and over, like a prayer. An old noble in the courtyard suddenly clutched a stranger's arm and cried, *"Marisol—my daughter—I have a daughter—"*

The flood the Hollow King had dammed for a generation was breaking, and it was going to drown the kingdom in everything it had lost.

Not everyone. Not everything. Sable walked the palace corridors and saw the gaps that would never fill—a woman searching a nursery for a child no record could prove existed, a guardsman who knew he'd loved someone whose face was gone even from his grief. The parasite had digested some things past recovery. Victory, Sable thought, was not a full purse returned. It was a partial inventory, honestly counted.

Which brought her back to the room where they'd laid Rosalind, in a chamber off the ruined hall, because no one could bear to move them farther yet.

Idris was there, propped in a chair, grey-faced, arguing weakly with a healer about whether he was dying. The child was asleep against Rosalind's side, one fist curled in their sleeve, and Rosalind—awake, quiet, patient as a stone in a stream—was letting her.

"How much?" Idris asked Sable quietly, when she sat down across from them. The soldier's question, blunt as a blade hilt. "How much did they lose?"

"Childhood. All of it. Wherever they grew up, whoever raised them—gone." Sable counted on her fingers, hating the accounting. "Their oaths' origins. Why they took Veyra's mark, what it meant to them. Corvane—they know the name, nothing else. And..." She stopped.

"And?"

"Their mother's lullaby." Her voice failed on the second word. She tried again. "There was a bottled lullaby, in the first ledger entry, the one Corvane used to tease them about. They sold it years ago—or meant to. Kept putting it off. I think—" She breathed. "I think that was the last coin. I think the god saved it for the final spark, because it was the dearest thing they had."

The fire in the great hall had burned down to embers by then, and through the broken windows the afternoon sky was doing something impossible. Sable saw it first in Idris's face, in the way his eyes went wide and wet without permission.

Beyond the mountains, faint as struck flint, a star was relighting.

Then another.

Then a constellation—she didn't know its name, but Idris did; he said it aloud, reverent, like a password home: *"The Warden's Chain. Gods. The Chain is back."*

Rosalind rose, carefully, disentangling the sleeping child, and walked to the window. Starlight fell on their face—old light, returning light—and they watched it with wonder and no recognition at all, a stranger greeting a miracle.

"I'm told I loved these," they said softly. "The stars. Is that true?"

"Yes," Sable said. "You did."

"Then I suppose," Rosalind said, "we should go home, and learn how again."

Far to the west, over the mountains, over Ashvale, the sky was beginning to remember itself—and Idris Vale, bleeding, grinning, already reaching for his boots, said the words that set the whole found family in motion:

"Then somebody fetch a cart. We're taking our guildmaster home."
