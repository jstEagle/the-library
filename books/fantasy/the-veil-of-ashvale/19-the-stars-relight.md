## 19. The Stars Relight

Idris Vale had spent eleven years learning to read the sky the way other men read ledgers—constellation by constellation, omen by omen, the border wards written in starlight above him like a garrison roster he could recite in his sleep. The Warden's Crown at the northern pole. The Lantern-Bearer trailing east. The Weeping Sisters strung along the meridian where his mother used to say the gods hung their laundry out to dry.

For a generation, those stars had been going out.

Not all at once. That was the horror of it. One by one, season by season, until whole stretches of sky were blank parchment and the wardens' oldest maps became lies. Idris had stood watch over those empty patches for years, telling himself it was weather, telluric ash, some natural dimming that would pass. Telling himself the wards had fallen because of his own failure, because he had been slow, because he had been afraid.

He had been wrong about almost everything. But standing now on the ridge road above Ashvale with Rosalind Brightwater's weight across his shoulders and dawn still an hour away, watching the first star kindle back into the dark—he was, for once, wrong in the direction of hope.

* * *

It began, as far as he could tell, while he was still lying in the mud outside the palace gates.

The second fall. The child had said it plainly, weeks ago, in her flat way of speaking about things that hadn't happened yet: *the soldier falls twice.* He'd carried those words through the mountains like a stone in his boot. He had fallen the first time at the inn, holding the door against Commander Ashe and thirty of his former brothers-in-arms, taking a spear through the meat of his shoulder and a sword-cut down his ribs so the others could slip out the back into the snow. He had woken two days later in a hayloft, stitched and furious at being alive, certain the second fall was waiting somewhere ahead of him like a debt collector.

It had come at the palace gates, when he led forty-odd scattered wardens—half of them deserters, half of them men who'd simply stopped believing what they were told—in a diversion that was never meant to succeed. It wasn't meant to succeed. It was meant to be loud, and it was. He remembered the horns, the torches, the gates groaning open under the weight of men who had finally decided that their oath ran higher than their orders. He remembered the Hollow King's attention swinging toward them like a lighthouse beam, vast and cold and hungry, the sensation of his own name beginning to unravel out of him letter by letter—

And then the fire.

There was no other word for what happened next, though every word he tried failed. Fire without heat. Light without source. A sound like a great bell struck inside his chest, inside everyone's chests at once, and then the thing that wore the crown came apart—not torn, exactly, not killed, but *unmade*, unwoven thread by golden thread, shrieking in a voice made of every memory it had ever swallowed. Idris felt it leave the world the way you feel a tooth come loose: a horrible relief, a sudden strange emptiness, blood where the ache had been.

Then he fell. That was the second fall, and he'd been sure, face-down on the flagstones with the bell-sound still ringing in his bones, that this one was final. He'd even been at peace with it, which frightened him more than dying did.

But peace, it turned out, was premature.

* * *

He woke to birdsong.

That was how he knew something fundamental had changed. There had been no birdsong in the capital for as long as he'd served; the wardens joked grimly that the city ate them. Now the sound poured through the broken gatehouse window like water through a cracked dam—hundreds of voices, ordinary and riotous and utterly indifferent to the affairs of kings.

He sat up. His shoulder screamed. His ribs joined in. He let them, grinning like an idiot, because pain meant the world still worked by its old rules, and he was lying in a cot in the gatehouse infirmary with a warden's blanket over him and a cup of willow-bark tea gone cold on the crate beside him.

An old woman in guild colors sat mending a shirt in the corner—the shirt was his, he realized; someone had cut it off him and someone else had washed it. She looked up.

"You slept three days," she said. "The little one said you would."

"The little one."

"The girl who remembers tomorrow." She bit her thread and tied it off. "She sat with you the first night. Told us you'd wake when the stars started coming back." Her eyes went to the window, and something moved across her face that Idris had not seen on a citizen's face in twenty years—an expression unguarded, unhollowed, entirely her own. "They're coming back," she said softly. "Did you know? All over the kingdom. People are waking up remembering things they lost decades ago. My father's name—I'd forgotten my father's name, can you imagine? Forty years, and last night I woke up weeping because I remembered he smelled of pipe smoke and peppermint."

She said it the way other people spoke of miracles, which, Idris supposed, was precisely what it was.

"Where is she?" he asked. "The child. And the others?"

"Gone home." The woman smiled. "Home to Ashvale. The Brightwater one is—" she hesitated, choosing words carefully, the way people did now when they spoke of Rosalind, "- resting. Carried, mostly. They paid more than anyone should ever have to pay. But they're alive. They're asking for you, when you can travel."

"And the king?"

The needle stilled in her hands. Outside, a cart rattled past, and someone laughed—a real laugh, unforced.

"Died yesterday morning," she said. "A mortal man, at the end. Old, so old, older than any of us knew. Before he went he asked for forgiveness, and—" her voice caught, "- and it was given. By the Brightwater one themselves. I don't pretend to understand it. But there's a lightness in the city now. Like a room after the window's finally opened."

Idris lay back and looked at the ceiling and let himself, for the first time in eleven years, do nothing at all.

* * *

They traveled north through a kingdom remembering itself.

Idris rode in a wagon—his ribs forbade the saddle, and Sable had informed him with terrifying gentleness that if he argued she would steal the memory of his stubbornness and be doing him a favor—while Rosalind lay propped on blankets beside him, awake more often now but quiet, their eyes tracking the countryside with an expression he couldn't read. Half-forgotten, Sable called them, and hated the phrase, and used it anyway because honesty was guild law and grief was no excuse for bad bookkeeping.

Half-forgotten looked like this: Rosalind knew how to speak, how to fight, how to ride, how to swear with impressive creativity. They knew Sable's name and Idris's and the child's. What they did not know was their own childhood—the village they'd come from, the faces of whoever had raised them, why they'd taken Veyra's mark in the first place. Those pages had burned to carry the flame, and nothing in the world could rewrite them.

"They gave them up," the child told Idris one evening, sitting cross-legged on the wagon's tailboard, watching him with those eyes that saw in both directions. "The memories. On purpose. Nobody made them."

"I know."

"Do you?" She picked at the wood grain. "You keep looking at Rosalind like something broke. Nothing broke. Something was *spent*. There's a difference. A bridge isn't broken just because you crossed it."

He had no answer for that. He was learning, slowly, that the child rarely required one.

The villages they passed through were celebrations wearing the skin of ordinary life. In Marrowgate—the river-city that had forgotten its own name—the signs had letters again, and the town crier stood in the square reading actual news, weeping openly between announcements, and the people pressed bread and cheese and flowers on the wagon as it passed, because word had traveled ahead of them of who these travelers were and what they'd done. An old man grabbed Idris's boot as the wagon rolled by and simply held it for a moment, saying nothing, before letting go. Idris thought about that grip for miles.

In the hill country, wardens found them.

He heard the horses before he saw them—disciplined, spaced, the approach of soldiers trying not to seem like soldiers—and his hand went to a sword he was in no condition to use. Then he saw the white armbands. Every man and woman of them wore a strip of white linen knotted around the left arm: the old mourning custom, revived, though none of them could quite say for whom. For the king, some said. For the god, said others. For everything lost, said most.

Their commander reined in beside the wagon. A young woman, weathered, with the look of someone who had been making hard decisions alone for a long time.

"Warden Vale." She saluted—awkwardly, because he was horizontal in a farm wagon, but she saluted. "The order's dissolved. Or remade. Nobody's certain which. But there are four hundred of us who want to swear to something better, and your name keeps coming up."

"My name shouldn't come up anywhere," Idris said. "I let the wards fall."

"No." She said it flatly, without cruelty. "You were the only one who stayed at your post after the wards fell. We've all read the reports now—the real ones. You held a broken line alone for three days." Her jaw tightened. "We want to swear to the guild. If the guild will have us."

Behind him, from her nest of blankets, Rosalind made a small sound. When Idris turned, they were smiling—that new smile they had, quieter than the old one, missing whatever joke their own past used to make.

"Tell them," Rosalind said, "that the Guild of Ashvale trades fairly, records everything, and hides no longer. Tell them we'll be home within the fortnight. Tell them to bring their own ink."

* * *

They crested the last ridge above Ashvale at dusk, and the whole valley took Idris's breath away.

Lamplight everywhere. Not the furtive, shuttered glimmer the guildhouse had kept in the old days, hidden behind heavy curtains lest the wrong eyes notice—but lamplight in windows, in doorways, in lanterns strung between buildings like a netful of caught fireflies. Word had spread through Ashvale the way spring spreads through a valley, unstoppable and slightly chaotic, and the town had decided, apparently by some unanimous unspoken vote, that tonight it would not be invisible.

And above it all—

"Oh," said the child, softly.

The stars were returning.

They had watched it happen in fragments on the road south of the capital, a bright point here, another there, like embers catching in a banked fire. But here, above Ashvale, beneath the place where the veil between the world and whatever lay beyond it had worn thinnest—it was not fragments. It was a tide.

The Warden's Crown blazed first, each star igniting in sequence, and Idris felt tears start and didn't bother hiding them, because eleven years of standing watch under a punched-out sky deserved at least this. Then the Lantern-Bearer, swinging up the eastern dark. Then the Weeping Sisters, one-two-three-four-five, and around him in the wagon Sable made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and said, "My mother used to sing to the Sisters. I don't remember her face anymore, but I remember the song, and I thought I'd lost that too—"

She began, very quietly, to hum it.

And the child—sitting between them, wrapped in a blanket too big for her—picked up the melody without hesitation, without a single missed note, humming a song no one had ever taught her, a song she could only have carried backward out of some tomorrow where Sable sang it often enough for a small girl to learn it by heart.

Sable stared at her. The child shrugged, unrepentant, and kept humming.

"Don't ask me how," she said, around the melody. "I'm not allowed to spoil it."

The constellations came home all night. Idris sat up in the wagon bed—ribs be damned, some nights demanded it—with Rosalind leaning warm against his good shoulder, and named them aloud as they returned, the old warden litany, his voice growing steadier with each one. The Ploughman. The Salt Road. The Hearth-Hound. The Long Watch, last of all, rising over the guildhouse roof just before midnight, and when its brightest star lit, a cheer went up from the town below, hundreds of voices, and someone in the guildhouse yard began ringing the old trade-bell that Corvane had silenced a decade ago.

Rosalind's hand found the oath-mark on their forearm. In the starlight Idris saw it glowing faintly gold—not the burning cold of the dark months, not the searing white of the throne room, but a low, steady warmth, like a lamp left lit in a window for someone coming home.

"It's not talking," Rosalind murmured. "Veyra. Not yet. Maybe not in words ever again." Their thumb moved over the mark, slow, wondering. "But it's *there*. I can feel it the way you feel a fire in the next room. Warmth through the wall." A pause. "Company."

"That's enough," Idris said. "Most nights, company is the whole point."

Rosalind turned their head against his shoulder and looked up at him, and their eyes held the particular clarity of someone seeing the world freshly—which, Idris understood, was literally true, the whole world new-minted to them, every face a stranger's face and every stranger somehow family anyway.

"You survived," Rosalind said. "Twice fallen. Twice risen. The child never doubted, did she."

"She says the ledger's balanced." He huffed a laugh that pulled at his ribs. "Your bookkeeper's already got me filed."

"Of course she does. Everything gets recorded." Rosalind gazed back up at the Long Watch, blazing now in all its ancient certainty. "Everything gets kept."

Below them, the bell rang on, and the stars kept arriving, and the valley filled up with light like a cup filling with wine.

* * *

They reached the guildhouse gates as true dawn broke, gray-gold and clean.

Corvane's ledger lay waiting on the great table inside—or rather, the space where it had lain, for Rosalind had closed it in the capital, formally, with wax and ribbon, and Idris had witnessed the closing with his bandaged hand pressed to the page. On the table now sat something else: a new ledger, bound in pale leather, blank as the sky had been blank, waiting for its first entry.

Rosalind stood over it for a long moment. Then they dipped a pen, and wrote, in a firm hand, the first line of a new book:

*Day one. Everything kept.*

Outside, the child's voice rose over the yard, still humming that song with tomorrow folded inside it, and beyond her, on the eastern road, dust was rising—the vanguard of four hundred wardens coming to swear themselves to something better.

Idris watched them come, and for the first time in eleven years, he was not watching for what might fall out of the sky.

He was watching what might be built.
