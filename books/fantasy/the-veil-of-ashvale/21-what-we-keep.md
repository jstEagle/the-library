## 21. What We Keep

The locket had been the easiest thing Sable had ever stolen.

That was the joke of it, she supposed—the one bitter joke the world had played on her at the toll bridge. She had spent her whole life lifting things that fought back: memories wrapped in grief, secrets sewn into the lining of a man's pride, a dying woman's last summer pried loose with two fingers and a whispered apology. Hard things. Guarded things. Things people would kill to keep.

But her mother's face—her mother's *face*—had come away from her like a leaf from a branch. One moment it was there, autumn-warm behind her eyes: flour on knuckles, a hummed tune half in minor key, the particular gold of lamplight through kitchen smoke. The next, it was folded small and silver-bright inside the locket the toll-keeper had offered, and Sable had walked across the bridge of Small Mercies whole-bodied but hollow-cheeked, carrying the only thing she'd ever given away honestly.

Now, three weeks past the burning of the throne room, three weeks past the stars coming home constellation by constellation like lamps relit in a great dark house, she sat on the guildhouse roof with that locket in her palm and could not open it.

Below her, Ashvale was making noise again.

That was the strangest part—not the stars, not the wards standing tall along the border like healed scars, but the *noise*. For a year the city had been going quiet by degrees, its laughter thinning, its arguments shortening, its songs losing verses until people hummed only the beginnings of things. Now every window spilled sound. Somewhere down Copperlane a fiddler was murdering a reel, and nobody minded. Somewhere east, the fish-market was shouting prices with the full-throated joy of people who remembered yesterday and expected tomorrow. A child laughed—a real laugh, long and ugly and delighted—and Sable felt it land somewhere under her ribs like a thrown coin.

She turned the locket over. Silver, plain, unmarked except for the toll-keeper's sigil on the back: two hands, one open, one closed. She had picked his pocket for it before paying, out of habit more than suspicion, and had never quite found the right moment to give it back. Probably because there was no giving it back. Probably because some trades were one-way roads, and she'd known that when she paid.

*Open it,* said the practical voice in her head, the one that sounded like Corvane. *You didn't sacrifice the memory to admire the packaging.*

She pressed the clasp.

It resisted—not mechanically; it opened fine—but something in her did. Her thumb hovered. The evening wind came up off the river smelling of woodsmoke and the first honest cold of autumn, and it stirred the hair at her temples, and she thought: *what if it doesn't fit anymore.*

Because here was the thing no ballad sang about, no ledger recorded, no thief's apprenticeship covered: a memory kept outside you does not age as you age. It waits. It stays exactly what it was—the kitchen, the smoke, the gold light, the face—and meanwhile the person who paid for it goes on becoming someone else. Rosalind had lost their childhood outright and had woken from the Unbinding diminished but *light*, somehow, the way a house is lighter after a flood takes the furniture and you discover how much floor you actually have. Sable had lost one memory and carried the gap around like a stone in her boot.

What if she opened the locket and her mother's face looked out at her like a stranger? What if the woman in it loved a girl who no longer existed?

*You're stalling,* said the Corvane-voice.

*I'm auditing,* Sable told him silently. Old guild habit. *There's a difference.*

"Are you talking to yourself again?"

Sable flinched so hard she nearly dropped the locket. Peri—the child, the nameless child, though Sable suspected that was about to stop being true—was crouched at the roof's edge, three feet away, having made absolutely no sound arriving there. She did that. She did it to everyone. Idris swore she'd appeared inside his tent once, sitting cross-legged on his bedroll, watching him sleep with the patient interest of a cat deciding whether to stay.

"I'm conferring with management," Sable said. "Corvane. Dead guildmaster. Very chatty."

"Dead people are quiet," Peri said, settling beside her with the boneless ease of someone who had watched the future and knew nothing on this roof was going to kill either of them tonight. "They just echo. You're the loud one." She nodded at the locket. "That's it, isn't it. The toll."

Sable closed her hand around it. "Nosy."

"You're going to give it back," Peri said. Not a question. The child spoke of the future the way other children reported the weather—flatly, without drama, occasionally wrong in ways that taught everyone around her humility. "To yourself. Tonight. You've been working up to it for nine days."

"Have I." Sable leaned back on her palms. The first stars were out—real ones, named ones, the Weaver's Chain strung across the dark where for a year there had been only blankness. Rosalind still went out every clear night to check them, one by one, like a shopkeeper counting stock after a break-in. "Tell me, prophet. Do I do it?"

Peri was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had lost its flat certainty, gone young and careful. "I don't see it. That part's yours now." She glanced sideways, and in the starlight her face was very serious. "The future stopped showing me your bits after the bridge. I think—" She frowned, hunting the words. "I think paying changed you. Made you... unlookable-at. Like a door that locks from the inside."

"That's the most flattering thing anyone's said to me all year."

"It wasn't a compliment."

"It absolutely was."

*

Down in the guildhouse kitchen, the family had gathered without anyone calling a gathering, which was how it always happened now.

Idris was at the table with his arm out of the sling—against healer's orders, against common sense, against the specific objections of Wren the herbwife who had threatened to tie him to the bedpost—and he was teaching Peri to play knucklebones, or rather Peri was teaching him, since she kept winning with bets placed on outcomes that hadn't happened yet and couldn't be argued with afterward. His wounds had knit slow and strange, the second fall the child had promised finally landing as fever rather than death, ten days of delirium in which he'd apologized to people who weren't there—wardens mostly, and once, quietly, to a king. He'd woken thinner and quieter and somehow easier in his skin, a man who had set down something heavy and discovered his own weight underneath.

Rosalind stood at the counter with their sleeves rolled, doing what they always did now: writing. The new ledger lay open beside the bread board, its pages filling in a hand steadier than it had been a month ago. *Remember everything. Charge fairly. Hide no longer.* Three laws, and beneath them entries that would have made old Corvane weep into his ledgers: the widow in Copperline owed two memories returned, taken by a moneylender's charm before the wards fell. The ferryman of Marrowgate owed an apology from a town that had forgotten his thirty years of service. A list of names, gathered street by street, of everyone the Hollow King's hunger had eaten past recovery—kept now not as debt but as remembrance, which Sable had learned was sometimes the same trade wearing different clothes.

"Sable." Rosalind didn't look up, but their mouth curved. They always knew. Oath-mark or instinct or simply a year of learning each other's footsteps—it didn't matter. "Sit. There's stew."

"There's always stew."

"There's always stew because you complain when there isn't." Rosalind ladled a bowl and pushed it toward the empty chair beside Idris. Steam rose off it, onion and barley and the smoked pepper Sable had liberated from a Marrowgate merchant who'd been charging memory-tolls of his own until the guild heard about it. "Eat. You look like you're brooding on a roof."

"I was consulting."

"On?"

"Management."

Idris snorted into his cup. Peri, mid-throw, let the knucklebones scatter and fixed Sable with a look of such transparent hope that Sable's chest hurt.

They knew. Of course they knew. She hadn't told them outright, but the locket lived in her breast pocket now instead of hidden in the floorboard cache, and Rosalind noticed everything, and Peri noticed everything *beforehand*. And none of them had said a word about it, because this family had learned the hard way what it cost to push someone toward a door before they chose to walk through it.

So Sable ate her stew. And when it was gone, she put her hand in her pocket, and took out the locket, and set it on the table between the knucklebones and the ledger, and the room went quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

"For the record," she said, "I hate audience participation in personal crises."

"Noted," Rosalind said. "For the record, we're not participating. We're witnesses. Guild rules."

"We don't have a rule for this."

"We will by morning. I'll write it." They turned the ledger toward her, pen ready. "Entry the first: *Sable of nowhere in particular, keeper of the guild's worse habits, pays herself back what she owes.* Date it properly. Everything gets recorded. That's the whole point of us now."

Sable looked at the locket. Two hands, one open, one closed.

Her hands were shaking. She was a thief; her hands did not shake; she made her living on the exact border between nerve and nervelessness. But they shook now, and she let them, because Idris had gone still and respectful in the way of soldiers at a graveside, and Peri had reached over without asking to rest one small hand on Sable's wrist, warm as a coal.

"If it hurts," Peri said softly, "that's just it fitting."

Sable pressed the clasp.

The locket opened on a breath of air that smelled impossibly of baking bread, of woodsmoke, of a kitchen twenty years drowned. And there—rising out of the little silver case like heat-shimmer off summer stone, like a song resuming on the exact note where it had stopped—came the gold light, the flour dust, the humming in minor key, and the face.

Her mother looked out at her from twenty years ago and smiled, and Sable waited for the stranger.

The stranger never came.

It was not that the memory fit. It was that the memory had *waited*, patient as a buried seed, and all the growing Sable had done in its absence turned out to be roots reaching down toward exactly this. The face was the same face. But Sable saw it with new eyes—saw the tiredness under the warmth, the worry tucked behind the smile, all the things a child cannot read in a parent and an orphan reads forever. She had paid a face at the bridge and gotten back a whole woman, and the grief of it and the gift of it arrived together, holding hands, and she cried the way she never let anyone see her cry: silently, thoroughly, both hands flat on the table as if steadying it.

Nobody touched her. Nobody spoke. The stew pot ticked as it cooled. Outside, the fiddler in Copperlane found the melody at last.

When she could speak, she laughed—one wet, cracked note—and said, "Well. That's embarrassing."

"She hummed," Sable told them, because they deserved to know her, because keeping was the whole point now. "My mother. Always half a tone flat and she never believed me when I told her. She made bread on days she was sad, so the house would smell like something better than she felt." Her thumb traced the locket's rim. "I thought getting her back would fix something. It didn't. It just—" She hunted for it, gave up elegance, settled for truth. "It reminded me what faces are for. You can't keep them in silver. You have to make new ones. Every day, with whoever's at your table."

She looked around the table. Idris, scarred and steady, pretending very hard not to be moved and failing completely. Rosalind, half-forgotten and wholly present, pen poised, watching her with the expression of someone cataloguing a treasure. Peri, whose hand was still on her wrist.

"New ones," Sable repeated, and closed the locket, and did not put it back in her pocket. She slid it across the table to Rosalind instead. "Guild property. First entry in the archive of kept things. If I die stupidly someday, somebody should know what I was willing to pay for."

Rosalind took it with both hands, the way you take an oath. "*Recorded,*" they said, and wrote.

*

Later, when the dishes were done and Idris had lost eleven consecutive games of knucklebones and retired grumbling to the wardens' cot, and Rosalind had climbed to the roof for the nightly star-count, Sable found Peri sitting on the stairs, hugging her knees, watching the front door as if expecting it to do something interesting.

"Couldn't sleep?" Sable sat down beside her. On the stairs was where Peri slept, most nights—refusing beds, refusing rooms, as if permanence were a trap she hadn't decided to step in yet.

"I'm deciding something," Peri said.

"Dangerous hobby."

"The guild needs my name for the ledger. Rosalind keeps asking. Nicely. Every day. Like feeding a shy animal." Peri rested her chin on her knees. "Everyone else got to keep their names. Idris earned his twice. Yours fits you like a glove with knives in it. Rosalind's name grew back around them even after the Hollow King chewed the middle out." She was quiet a moment. "Mine was never mine. The people who left me at the Marrowgate shrine didn't leave one. So I get to choose, and choosing feels like jumping off a roof."

Sable thought about the locket cooling in the archive drawer upstairs. About doors that lock from the inside. About the difference, which she had learned on a bridge, between having a thing taken and giving it freely.

"Here's a thief's trick," she said. "Names aren't like lockets. You don't have to pick just one, and nobody can steal them if you keep handing them out. Spend them freely. They come back."

Peri considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. Then she turned, and in the low lamplight of the stairwell her eyes were bright and certain, and she said:

"Morrow."

Sable tasted it. *Morrow.* Tomorrow's root, tomorrow's promise, worn soft enough for everyday use. A name that belonged to the future and had finally decided to live in the present.

"Morrow," Sable repeated, and stuck out her hand, formal as a contract. "Morrow of the Ashvale Guild. Keeper of tomorrows. Witnessed and recorded—I'll make Rosalind write it in blood if you want."

"Just ink's fine." Morrow shook her hand, solemn, and then ruined it entirely by grinning, quick and crooked, a grin with knives in it, a grin that fit her like a glove. Above them, faintly, came the sound of the roof hatch opening—Rosalind coming in from the stars—and Morrow cocked her head as if hearing something on the wind that hadn't happened yet.

"What?" Sable said. "What do you see?"

"Nothing bad," Morrow said, which from her was practically a celebration. "Just—tomorrow, someone's going to knock on that door with a problem no court will touch. Something about a village that's forgetting things *again*, even with the stars lit. And Rosalind's going to say yes before they finish asking." She stood, stretched, and headed for her stairs-nest like a soldier turning in. Over her shoulder: "Get some sleep, Sable. We'll want it."

Sable sat alone in the lamplight a while longer, listening to the fiddler finally give up, listening to the guildhouse settle around its people like a body around a heart. Upstairs, a page turned in the new ledger. In the archive drawer, a locket kept a face safe for a family that no longer needed it—which was, she thought, exactly what an archive was *for*.

Tomorrow, then. She smiled, and banked the lamp, and went up to bed, spending the name of the day freely, knowing it would come back.
