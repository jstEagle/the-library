## 22. The Veil of Ashvale

The guildhouse had a door now that anyone could open.

That was the change Rosalind noticed most, a year on—not the new roof beams pale as bone where the old ones had burned, not the ledger room rebuilt with windows facing the dawn instead of the dark, but the door. For a hundred years the Ashvale Guild of Impossible Trades had kept its entrance behind a veil-charm and a knock-code known to three living people. Now the door stood wide from morning bell to evening bell, oak worn smooth by the hands of the desperate, and above it hung a sign Sable had painted herself in gold leaf she'd absolutely stolen:

**THE ASHVALE GUILD — KEEPERS OF WHAT MUST BE REMEMBERED.**

Beneath it, smaller, in Idris's blunt soldier's hand: *No charge for the grieving.*

Rosalind stood at the top of the steps with the morning fog rolling up Copperlane Street and watched people come. A miller's widow clutching a locket that had forgotten whose hair it held. Two brothers who could no longer agree on which of them had been born first, or whether there had ever been a third. An old warden—Idris saluted him; the man wept—carrying a scrap of banner he couldn't name. They came because the courts had turned them away, because the temples offered prayers when what they needed was work, because somewhere word had spread down every road out of the mountains: *the guild takes the cases no one else can touch. The guild remembers.*

"Ledger's balanced," said Wren, appearing at Rosalind's elbow with the child's particular gift for silence-then-presence. She was eleven now, or would be tomorrow—she'd declared her birthday fell on the anniversary, "because that's when everything started being real," and no one had argued with a girl who remembered the future. Her own name still sat carefully in the guild rolls: **Wren Tomorrow**, entered in Rosalind's hand, witnessed by three.

"And the impossible column?"

"Three entries. A woman wants us to find the song her mother sang that nobody else heard. A farmer swears his fields remember being a lake and won't grow until someone apologizes to the water." Wren grinned, gap-toothed, fierce. "And someone left a jar on the step labeled *one ordinary Tuesday, 743*."

Rosalind felt something in their chest turn over gently. "We'll take all three."

"You always take all three." Wren bumped their hip with her shoulder and ran inside, and her laughter scattered through the rebuilt hall like birdsong through a morning market.

Corvane's old ledger sat closed now on its shelf of honor, wrapped in grey linen, his name tooled into the cover where Rosalind had restored it letter by letter. Beside it, the new ledger lay open, and its first law was written on the flyleaf in ink Veyra's mark had warmed to gold:

*Remember everything. Charge fairly. Hide no longer.*

* * *

The day went as days went now—full, loud, unhidden.

Rosalind worked the memory-cases at the long table by the window while Sable taught the newest apprentices the thief's catechism, revised: *what is taken without leave must be returned without price.* Idris drilled the wardens who had defected with him and the young ones who had never worn the seal at all, training them not to guard secrets anymore but to guard people—the difference, he told them hoarsely, between a wall and a hearth.

At midday a delegation arrived from Marrowgate, the river-city that had forgotten its name. It remembered now; the signs had filled back in with letters like slow-growing moss, and the town crier had wept for an hour straight simply announcing things again. But they came bearing gifts and one request: the city wanted a guildhouse of its own, a branch, keepers of memory on the river the way Ashvale kept them here. Rosalind read their petition twice, looked up at Sable across the table, and found her already smiling.

"We'll need a thief who's honest," Rosalind said.

"Horrifying," Sable agreed. "I know just the person." She tapped her own collarbone, where the toll-keeper's debt-mark had faded to a pale scar. She had crossed back over that bridge in spring and paid nothing—the keeper had bowed and waved her through, saying the account was settled, paid in full by the only honest coin she'd ever spent. She still didn't talk about her mother's face. But sometimes, watching her hold the locket she'd stolen back from her own past and then chosen not to open, Rosalind thought she saw her building something new behind her eyes, brick by brick, out of this family instead.

The afternoon brought the hard case, as hard cases always did. A man from the borderlands whose daughter had died in the year of the Hollow King, and whose grief had been so total that he'd asked a hedge-witch to cut the memory out—and now regretted it, because he could not remember why he grieved, only that he did, endlessly, without object, like rain falling on a roof with no sky above it. Could the guild give it back?

"No," Rosalind told him gently. "What's cut stays cut. But—" and here they opened the new ledger, the pages Veyra's warmth had blessed, "—we can help you build around the wound. Tell us about her. Everything you remember *about* remembering her. We'll write it all down, and we'll write it true, and you will carry the book instead of the hole."

He wept. They wrote until dusk. When he left, he touched the doorframe the way pilgrims touch shrine-stones, and Rosalind understood, finally, what Corvane had been building toward all those years of hiding: not safety. Readiness. A vault waiting to become a lighthouse.

* * *

Evening came down over Ashvale like a benediction.

One year ago tonight, the stars had begun to return—constellation by constellation, the way dawn comes to a valley, filling the low places first. The kingdom called it the Relighting, and every city celebrated differently. Marrowgate floated lanterns on the river. The capital—governed now by a regency council and a boy-king crowned with deliberate, careful slowness—held a night of open archives, every record unsealed, every citizen invited to read the realm's whole remembered self.

Ashvale lit lamps.

It had started small. On the first anniversary month, Rosalind had set a single lamp in the guildhouse window for Corvane. Then Sable added one for everyone whose memories the Hollow King had eaten beyond recovery—the hollow-eyed refugees who smiled at nothing, the names in the ledgers marked *unreturnable*. Idris lit one for the wardens who had fallen holding the palace gates, and one more, quietly, for the commander he'd once been. Wren lit hers for "everyone who hasn't happened yet," which made no sense and made everyone cry anyway.

Now the whole street did it. Copperlane burned with small flames, window after window after window, a constellation built by hands instead of heavens.

Rosalind climbed to the guildhouse roof as the last light drained from the west, carrying two lamps and the weight of a year. From up here the town spread out golden and imperfect—scaffolding on the east quarter, the scars of the burning still visible on the chapel, the new school's roof bright with fresh shingles. Rebuilt, not restored. That distinction mattered. They had not tried to make Ashvale what it was before. They had made it what it should have been.

Below, voices drifted up: Sable arguing cheerfully with apprentices about lock-picking ethics ("it's *ethics* if you pick the lock of your own heart, that's the whole lesson"); Idris teaching Wren sword-forms even though she insisted she'd already seen how every fight ended ("then show me, prophet-girl, since you're so wise"—and Wren laughing, adjusting his elbow, correcting a man twice her size because she genuinely did know better).

Rosalind set the lamps on the rooftop ledge. One for Corvane, whose last instruction had become a life's architecture: *Unbind him*, and so they had, and so the king had died mortal and forgiven with Rosalind's hand in his, whispering thank-you in a voice scrubbed clean of anything hollow. One lamp remained unlit.

They sat with it a while, knees drawn up, watching the stars come out. The Veil of Stars hung complete overhead now—every constellation returned, even the ones the old star-charts had lost, as if the sky itself had recovered memories no one alive had kept. Astronomers were calling them the Restored Lights. Rosalind called them proof.

"You're stalling," said the mark.

Not in words. Never in words. Veyra did not speak the way people speak—it pressed meaning into the oath-brand on Rosalind's forearm like a thumb pressing into wax, warm, patient, unmistakably *present*. A year ago it had been silence, and silence had nearly broken them. Now it was company. Faint, fragmentary, a god rekindled and slowly gathering itself inside its faithful, but present.

"I'm not stalling," Rosalind said aloud. "I'm observing tradition. You light the last lamp last. That's the tradition. I invented it tonight, but it's still tradition."

Warmth, amused, along the brand. The god had learned teasing from Sable. This was probably a theological crisis somewhere.

Rosalind looked down at the second lamp—the blank one, the one with no name yet. Because there were others to light for, always others. The half-forgotten. The people Rosalind themselves had paid to save the world: the childhood gone like smoke, the origin of the oaths gone, whole seasons of a life traded in the throne room fire. They woke some mornings reaching for a mother's voice they could no longer hear, a home they could no longer picture, and found instead Sable's hand already there, Wren already chattering, Idris already making terrible porridge—as if the family had appointed itself to stand exactly in the shape of everything missing.

Half-forgotten, wholly loved. Wren had said it once, matter-of-fact, the way she said true things. Rosalind had written it in the ledger's margin and underlined it twice.

But the empty lamp wasn't for themself. Rosalind struck the flint, touched flame to wick, and spoke the dedication to the cooling air:

"For everyone the world forgot and no book recorded. For the hollow-smiling ones. For the gaps." A breath. "You are kept. Somewhere, you are kept."

The flame steadied. And on their forearm, Veyra's mark kindled—a soft gold glow beneath the skin, steady as a hearth-coal, brief as a blink. Like a small returning star. Like a god saying *I heard. I keep too.*

They stayed on the roof a long time after that, god and mercenary, watching over the lamplit street.

* * *

Near midnight, footsteps on the ladder—two sets, one light and one favoring the left knee, which meant Idris's old wound and meant he'd been drilling too hard again and meant Sable had sent him up because she was busy pretending not to worry. Wren scrambled onto the roof first and planted herself between them, breathless with the specific urgency of children delivering news.

"It's tomorrow," she announced.

"It's midnight," Rosalind said. "So yes."

"No—I mean it's *tomorrow* tomorrow." She flopped down cross-legged, eyes huge in the lamplight. "I stopped seeing it months ago, you know that. After the Unbinding, the future just... went quiet, like it finally trusted us with it. But tonight it started again. Just a little. Just one thing."

Idris lowered himself onto the ledge with a grunt. "Should I be worried? Last time she started seeing things, we ended up in a burning throne room."

"This one's not fire." Wren's voice dropped, and she looked at each of them in turn, and Rosalind felt the night go still around her small serious face. "There's a road coming. From past the old wards—from *outside*, where the Hollow King crawled in from. Someone's walking it. Someone carrying a lantern that burns cold." She hugged her knees. "And they're looking for the guild. Not to hurt us. To ask us something. And the answer matters more than the war did."

Silence. Below them, Ashvale glimmered, lamp by lamp, a kingdom's worth of kept things.

Then Sable's head appeared over the roof edge, grinning like sin. "Well," she said. "Sounds impossible. Good thing we're taking all three."

Rosalind laughed—really laughed, from whatever place the fire hadn't reached—and put an arm around Wren's shoulders, and together they watched the stars do what stars do best: burn, and keep burning, and light the road for whatever came next.

The veil had lifted. They stepped through it together.
