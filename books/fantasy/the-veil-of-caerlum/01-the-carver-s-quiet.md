## 1. The Carver's Quiet

The knife knew the bone better than Freya did.

That was the trick of it — the thing no one in Greyhollow understood when they brought her their dead and their grief and their coin. The work was not in her hands. Her hands only held steady while the blade remembered what it had always known: where to turn, how deep to bite, the exact angle at which antler or femur or the long pale rib of a whale gave up its grain and became a doorway instead of a wall.

She worked by the window because the light was honest there. Grey light, salt-light, the light that came off the Sundered Sea and through her shutters smelling of kelp and tar and the cold iron of distant storms. Below, the town went about its morning the way it always did — gulls screaming over the fish-cries, the smugglers' carts rattling up Coil Street with their false bottoms full of brandy and unasked questions, old Marta sweeping her step for the fourth time because sweeping was cheaper than weeping.

Freya turned the charm in her fingers.

It was a small thing yet, hardly longer than her thumb, carved from the shinbone of a stag that had died fat and old in Thornfell. She had chosen it deliberately. Bone carried what it grew from; a stag's leg knew running, knew escape, knew the long patience of winter. It would hold a soul gently. Some bones — she'd learned this the hard way, in her first years, with mistakes she still dreamed about — some bones held too tight.

Three lines down the length of it, like a river's course. A spiral at the crown, open, never closed. And at the base, not yet cut, the name.

Names were the hard part. Not the cutting of them — any letter could be learned — but the *choosing*. The widow downstairs would give her the son's true name, and Freya would have to decide which version of it went into the bone: the name his mother shouted across fields, or the name he'd signed on letters home from the fishing crews, or the name he'd been called in the last hour of his life, when the sea took him and no one was there to call him anything at all.

She set the knife down and flexed her fingers until the ache in them quieted.

Below, footsteps on the stairs. Heavy ones, careful ones — Maren Cole climbed stairs the way she did everything now, as if the weight of what she carried might spill if she moved too fast.

"Come in," Freya said, before the knock. "The door's not latched."

Maren filled the doorway the way grief fills a room: completely, and without meaning to. She was a big woman, a fishwife with forearms like mooring rope, but she had shrunk somehow since the drowning. Clothes that had fit her in spring hung loose as shed skin. Her eyes went straight to the charm in Freya's hand and stayed there, hungry and afraid of being hungry.

"You said three days," she said. "Is it — can you—"

"Sit." Freya nodded at the stool by the stove. "Tea first."

"I don't want tea. I want my boy." Maren's voice cracked on *boy* and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth, furious at herself. "I'm sorry. I know you don't — I know it isn't — everyone says you just make them quieter, so the house stops creaking, so I stop hearing him on the stairs. That's all I want. For the house to stop creaking."

*So I stop hearing him.* Freya poured the tea anyway and put it in the widow's hands, because hands needed something to hold or they started wringing. "Tell me about the creaking," she said. "Which stair?"

"The third one. Always the third. He used to skip it, when he was small — jumped it every time, drove his father mad. Now I hear it creak at night and I go to the bottom and I stand there like a fool and there's nothing, nothing, nothing." She drank the tea scalding and didn't notice. "Nothing is worse than something, do you understand? Everyone thinks the hearing-it is the torment. The torment is going to look."

Freya understood. She understood better than any woman in Greyhollow guessed, though they suspected, in the way small towns suspect everything and say nothing. They called her the Quiet Carver, and they came to her back door with coins wrapped in cloth, and they never asked why a woman of thirty lived alone above a shop that smelled of death and juniper oil, and she never told them that the silence she sold was also the silence she lived inside.

Because here was the truth Freya Whitlock had built her life around, plank by plank, like a hull against the sea: the dead did not speak.

Not to her. Not truly. When she carved, she felt *something* — a pressure, a warmth, the sense of a room where someone had recently been sitting — but no voice, no face, no words. The gods were gone; everyone knew that. The Hollow Order had seen to it a hundred years ago, tearing down the temples and burning the priests and teaching the world to pray to nothing, and whatever lingered after them was only residue. An echo in the walls. A warmth in the bone.

She could quiet that echo. She could seal it into carved lines and give the living their sleep back. But she could not hear them. No one could.

"His name," Freya said gently. "All of it. And then tell me which name he loved best."

"Tam." Maren's chin trembled. "Tamsin Cole, though he hated Tamsin, said it sounded like an old woman's cat. Tam, near the end. His crew called him Tam." She laughed wetly. "He'd have wanted Tam on it. He'd have wanted—" She stopped. Set down the cup. Looked at Freya with those hollowed-out eyes. "You'll be gentle with him? You won't hurt him? They say you don't hurt them, that it's like laying a blanket over a bird, but they say things, women say things—"

"Maren." Freya took the widow's hands in hers. They were cold. Everything about Maren was cold lately; grief ran a body's fire down to nothing. "I have done this two hundred times. I have never once hurt anyone, living or dead. I swear it on the bone."

It was an old oath, older than she was — carvers swore it on the bone the way smiths swore on iron — and it settled Maren somewhat. She sat while Freya worked, and the knife sang its small song, and outside the gulls wheeled and the tide breathed against the harbor stones, and everything proceeded exactly as it had two hundred times before.

*Tam.*

The letters went into the base of the charm in the old script, the pre-Hollow script that the Order had outlawed and that survived now only in places like Greyhollow, where the law arrived last and left earliest. Three cuts per letter, no more. The spiral at the crown, already finished, waited to receive him.

And Freya felt it come.

It always felt like this — like a draft from a door opening in an empty house, like warmth passing close behind her shoulder. She braced herself for the familiar nothing. The echo. The presence without a person.

"*Please.*"

Freya's knife stopped mid-stroke.

The word had not come from Maren. Maren was sitting six feet away with her mouth pressed shut, tears sliding silently down her face, watching the knife. The word had come from *inside* — from the bone under the blade, thin and frayed and desperate as smoke drawn backward into a chimney.

"*Please. Someone. Can anyone hear me. It's dark and the water keeps — please, I don't want Mum to hear the stairs, I keep trying to be quiet, I've been trying to be so quiet—*"

The knife clattered onto the table.

Freya stood so fast the stool went over behind her. Her heart slammed against her ribs. She looked at the charm lying there in its shavings, small and white and utterly ordinary, and the voice rose out of it again, fainter now, wounded by her flinching:

"*Did I do something wrong? Please — I only wanted someone to know I'm still—*"

"Stop." It came out of her as a whisper. "Stop, I can hear you, I—"

"What?" Maren was on her feet. "What is it? What's wrong?" She saw the fallen stool, the abandoned knife, and her face went grey. "You heard him. Oh saints, oh mercy, you heard him, didn't you? Is he suffering? Carver, *is my boy suffering?*"

Freya couldn't answer. Her whole body had gone to static. Because the dead did not speak. They had not spoken in years — not since she was a girl, not since the last time, which she had buried so deep she'd nearly convinced herself it never happened. The Order said the gods were dead and the veil between the world and whatever lay beyond it was sealed, silent, *finished*, and every carving she had ever made confirmed it: echoes only, warmth only, the residue of souls like heat lingering in a chair.

But Tam Cole was speaking. Tam Cole was *begging.*

"No," she managed. "No, he's not — Maren, sit down. Sit down and let me finish the work."

Her hands shook so badly she had to press them flat against the table to steady them. Then she picked up the knife again. The voice — Tam's voice, young and scared and trying so hard to be quiet for his mother's sake — hushed itself hopefully as the blade returned to the bone. She closed the spiral. She sealed the lines with a drop of juniper oil, murmuring the closing words her grandmother had taught her, the words that meant *rest, be held, be quiet* —

—and felt the boy settle, the way a frightened animal settles when a blanket comes down over the cage. Gratitude, wordless and enormous, washed through her like warm water.

Then silence. Real silence. And Maren weeping into her apron, saying thank you, thank you, over and over, pressing coins into Freya's hand wrapped in cloth the way they always were, and Freya taking them because refusing would raise more questions than accepting.

She walked the widow down the stairs and out into the street, and stood in her doorway watching Maren Cole go, walking lighter than she'd come, and felt the ground tilt beneath everything she knew.

* * *

She did not sleep.

She tried. She banked the stove, barred the door, laid the day's remaining charms out in their rows and checked each seal twice. She told herself it was exhaustion, a trick of an overtired mind, the same explanation she'd given herself at fourteen when the dead had spoken once before — a drowned girl, a single sentence — before the gift had gone silent again and stayed silent for sixteen years. The mind plays tricks. Grief leaks. Bones are strange material and strange things live in strange materials.

But she lay in the dark listening to her house, and her house did not creak, and somehow that was worse.

Somewhere past midnight she gave up and lit a candle, and that was when she heard the bell.

Greyhollow's market square kept a bell — a squat bronze thing on a post by the well, rung for fires, for wrecks, for the rare arrival of the tax assessor. It rang at midnight for none of these reasons. Freya went to her window and looked down into Coil Street, where doors were opening along both sides, lamplight spilling out in yellow wedges, neighbors in nightshirts drifting toward the square with the wary curiosity of people who smell smoke but see no flames.

She followed.

The square was half full when she reached it, shadows clustered under the eaves of the chandlery and the Salt Cellar tavern. At the center, by the well, stood a man she didn't know — a traveler, road-dusted, mud to his knees, holding his hat in both hands like a man at a graveside. He was praying. That much was obvious even from a distance: the posture, the moving lips, the hat clutched like an offering. People prayed all the time, still, despite a century of the Order's burning — quietly, guiltily, the way they smoked and drank and did everything else the zealots forbade. You prayed to nothing, knowing it was nothing, because the habit outlived the god.

But travelers who prayed did it with their mouths barely moving. This man's lips were shaping whole sentences. And the square had gone quiet enough — the gulls even had settled — that Freya, standing at its edge, began to hear him.

"—I don't ask for much, I never asked for much, but my daughter is fevered in the wagon and the herb-wives are all abed and I am *begging*, I am on my knees inside if not outside, whoever's listening, *please*—"

He stopped. Swayed.

And the well spoke.

Not the man. The *well* — the old stone shaft, dry these forty years, its bucket long since rotted away. Water rose in it with a sound like a great indrawn breath, black and gleaming under the moon, and a light kindled deep beneath the surface, soft and green-gold, the color Freya had only ever seen in her dreams and in the heart of certain consecrated bones. The crowd made a sound — not a scream, not yet; something lower, older, the sound a herd makes.

From the well, in a voice like water over stones, gentle and vast and unmistakably *aware*, a woman's voice — or something wearing a woman's voice — said:

"*Bring her to me, child. Bring her to the water. There is no fever I cannot carry.*"

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed.

Then a door banged somewhere, and a man's voice roared down an alley — *"Order! ORDER! Get the Order!"* — and the square shattered into motion, and the traveler fell to his actual knees and began to weep, and Freya stood rooted at the edge of it all with her heart hammering out a single thought, over and over, like waves against a hull:

*The gods are answering. After a hundred years, the gods are answering.*

And one of them, she was suddenly, terribly sure — the way you are sure of a thing in dreams — was looking directly at her.

In the well, the green-gold light flickered. The voice came again, softer now, meant for no crowd:

"*Priestess.*"

Then it was gone. The water sank. The bell hung silent. And Freya Whitlock turned and walked home through the waking streets of Greyhollow, faster and faster, until she was running, and behind her the town lit up lamp by lamp like a fever breaking.
