## 2. A Voice Like Winter

Freya did not sleep.

She sat at her workbench with the lamp turned low and the widow's charm finished in her hands — a small thing of gull bone and silver wire, its surface etched with the quieting script her mother had taught her before the fever took them both. It should have felt like any other night's work. It should have felt like the hundred nights before it: the rasp of the knife, the smell of tallow and salt, the small mercy of a job well done.

But she could still hear him.

*Help me,* the dead boy had said, from inside his own mother's grief. *It's cold where I am. It wasn't supposed to be cold.*

The dead of Greyhollow did not speak. That was the entire point of what Freya did — she carved the silence into them, stitched the veil shut around each grieving household so the restless could not press through. For eleven years she had worked in perfect quiet, the only carver in the Thornfells whose charms never failed, because Freya Whitlock did not merely carve the symbols. She had always been able to feel them settle, like snowfall, like a held breath released.

She had told herself it was craft. Instinct. A knack, nothing more.

Tonight there was no telling herself anything. She set the charm down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars that were not stars.

Outside, Greyhollow slept the way it always did — fitfully, with the sea muttering against the breakwater and somewhere a drunk singing half a hymn to a god who no longer answered. The old gods of Caerlum had been silent for a century. Everyone knew it. The Hollow Order made certain everyone knew it, their white-and-grey banners hanging in every market square, their preachers saying the same thing they had said since Freya's grandmother's grandmother: *The gods are dead. The gods died for you. Do not pray as though they can hear.*

And yet.

Freya lowered her hands and spoke aloud, to the empty room, because saying it made it smaller.

"I heard a ghost today."

The lamp flame leaned sideways. Not from any draft.

*I heard you too,* said a voice like winter.

It came from everywhere. From the marrow of her own bones, from the space between heartbeats, from the dark beyond the window where snow was beginning to fall though the season was all wrong for it. It was not loud. It was the opposite of loud — vast and still, the way a frozen lake is still, the way the silence after an avalanche is still.

Freya did not scream. Later, she would be proud of that. In the moment, she simply went very cold and very quiet, the way prey goes quiet, and her hand found the carving knife on the bench without her deciding it should.

"You aren't real," she said.

*No one has said that to me in a hundred years.* The voice carried something that might have been amusement, or might have been grief; they sounded the same, at that scale. *You carve my names into bone, little priestess, and tell yourself they are only shapes.*

"They *are* only shapes." Her knuckles were white on the knife. "I copy what my mother copied. I don't know what any of it means. I'm not—"

*You are.* And then the world opened.

It was not a vision. Visions, she imagined, had edges. This had none. One moment she was sitting in her chair and the next she was nowhere at all, suspended in a darkness shot through with light — and she understood, in the way one understands things in dreams, that she was looking at the veil itself. It hung across everything, a shimmering grey curtain stretched between the world of the living and whatever waited behind it. And it was torn.

Not frayed. Not worn, the way she'd always privately believed a century of neglect might leave it. Torn — ripped open in long, weeping gashes, and through those gashes she could see movement. Shapes pressing. Hungry shapes, or frightened ones, or both; at this distance, hunger and fear looked identical.

*Do you see?* the voice asked, and now there was no mistaking the sorrow in it. It was a sorrow with weight and weather, a sorrow that had endured alone for a hundred years. *I have been holding the edges together with my hands. I am so tired, Freya Whitlock. And you are the last pair of hands I have.*

"Stop." Her voice cracked. She was back in her chair, back in her body, shaking, the knife fallen from her fingers. Snow ticked against the window glass. "Stop it. I'm a bone-carver. I carve charms. I keep my head down and my door shut and I pay my taxes and I have never once—" Her throat closed. She started again, quieter. "I prayed to you when I was nine years old. When my parents burned. I prayed every night for a year, and you never answered. So whoever — whatever — you are, you don't get to come now. You don't get to need me now."

The silence that followed had texture. She felt it considering her.

*I know,* the voice said at last, gently, terribly. *I know what your silence cost you. If I could have answered then, I would have torn myself apart sooner to do it.*

"No." She stood up. The chair scraped back. "No, I don't care if you're Caerlum himself, the god of the mountain and the winter sea. I don't care if the veil is tearing. Find someone else."

*There is no one else.* And for the first time, the vastness in the voice bent, and she heard underneath it something almost human: fear. *That is what I have come to tell you, priestess. There is no one else. And there is no time.*

The lamp guttered out.

In the sudden dark, far away, down in the town, a bell began to ring.

* * *

Greyhollow did not have a night bell. That was the first thought Freya had, standing in her doorway with a packed satchel she did not remember filling, watching orange light bloom in the market square below. That was not a fire bell. Fire bells rang fast and ragged. This bell tolled slow, deliberate, ceremonial — and beneath it, voices. Many voices. Ordered voices, chanting in unison.

She knew the chant. Every child in the Thornfells knew it. *One faith, one silence, one crownless sky.*

The Hollow Order had come to Greyhollow.

Freya took the back stairs two at a time, past Mrs. Aldersey's door, past the drying racks hung with fish that would never be collected now, out into the alley where the snow was falling thick enough to muffle the world. From the mouth of the alley she could see the square.

They had erected a pyre in the center of it. Not for burning — not yet. They had erected a *pulpit*, a tall grey scaffold draped in Order cloth, and upon it stood a knight in full white-and-grey plate, faceless behind the visor, holding a censer that smoked with something that stung the eyes even from this distance. Around the square, Order soldiers were going door to door, dragging families out into the snow. Not arresting them. Assembling them. An audience.

The knight's voice carried over everything, amplified by some artifice of the censer-smoke, resonant and calm and absolutely certain.

"People of Greyhollow. Rejoice, for the enemy is near. Last night, a prayer was answered in your square — you all heard it. You all felt it. The old powers stir again, seeking a vessel through which to pour their corruption back into the world." A pause, letting the word *corruption* settle into the crowd like a stone into a pond. "The vessel walks among you. The vessel hears the dead. Deliver up the vessel, and the Order will pass over your town as a kindness."

Freya's blood turned to ice water.

*A prayer was answered.* The stranger in the market square, last night — the old man who'd wept at the shrine and begged aloud for his daughter's safe crossing, and the shrine-light had flared, and everyone had pretended not to see. She had pretended not to see. She had walked home and told herself it was a trick of the wind.

The Order had not pretended.

And they were here within a day. Which meant they hadn't come because of the stranger. They had come because they already knew — had known, perhaps, for longer than Freya wanted to imagine — and the answered prayer had simply told them where to look. *The vessel hears the dead.* The boy in the widow's charm. The boy she had heard.

Her hand went to the satchel at her hip, to the finished charm inside it, warm as a heartbeat.

*I told you,* the winter voice murmured, soft at the edge of her mind. *There is no time.*

"Get out of my head," she breathed.

*Gladly — once you are safe. Take the harbor road to the cliff stair. No. They will watch the harbor. Behind the tannery, priestess. The goat track. You know it better than any soldier born south of the mountains.*

She hated that he was right. She hated more that she was already moving.

Behind the tannery, the reek of lye and rotting hides covered the sound of her footsteps. The goat track began as a crack in the cliff face, a seam of switchbacks barely wide enough for the animals it was named for, ascending through thorn scrub and black rock into the shoulder of the Thornfells. Tourists didn't know it existed. Soldiers from the southern citadels certainly didn't. But Freya had climbed it a thousand times in the dark, carrying charms to hill villages that couldn't afford the road tolls, and her feet knew it the way they knew her own heartbeat.

Below her, Greyhollow shrank to a scatter of lights around the Order's bonfire. She heard, faintly, screaming — someone in the square protesting, someone being made an example of — and forced herself to keep climbing, one hand on the frozen rock, tears freezing on her lashes.

Eleven years, she thought. Eleven years of keeping her head down. Of carving silences and taking no credit and never, ever praying, not once, not even in the worst nights. And it hadn't mattered. She had been marked from birth, or before it, a harvest waiting in its field, and no amount of quiet had hidden her from the scythe.

At the second switchback she stopped, chest heaving, and put her palm flat against the stone of the mountain. Caerlum's mountain. His stone.

"Why me?" she whispered.

The answer came not as words but as warmth, rising up through the rock into her hand, patient and immense and unbearably sad.

*Because someone must hold the veil,* the god of the winter sea said, *and you were born holding it already.*

Above her, against the stars, a signal fire bloomed on the high ridge — red, then white, then red again. The Order's calling-sign. Not searching for her anymore.

*Closing in,* she thought wildly. *They're closing the passes.*

Then another light answered, further east, on the next ridge over. But this one burned green — a color no Order lantern made — and Freya stared at it, snow gathering in her hair, feeling the veil tremble along every mile of mountain between her and that impossible green flame.

Someone else was out there in the Thornfells tonight. Someone the Order wanted just as badly.

The god's voice came again, and this time it sounded almost amused.

*Ah,* said Caerlum. *Her.*
