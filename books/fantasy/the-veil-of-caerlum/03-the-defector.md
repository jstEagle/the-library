## 3. The Defector

Seren Ashvale had been dead for forty-one days.

She knew the exact count because the Order counted everything — breaths, sins, the seconds between a knight's oath and her fall. In life, Seren Ashvale had been a Vigil-Knight of the Hollow Order, third blade of the Veilspire garrison, sworn to hunt what remained of the old gods and those who served them. In death, she was a smear of ash in the ruins of the Kestrel Way chapel, identified by her own pauldron, planted there beside a fire she had built herself from her cloak, her standard, and the letters of commendation she'd once been proud of.

Dead women could go where they pleased. That was the whole point.

She stood now on the shoulder of the Thornfell pass where the road narrowed between walls of black stone, watching the morning fog pour down the mountainside like something spilled. The cold here was an honest cold. It asked nothing of her, forgave nothing, owed nothing. She had spent six weeks learning its language: how to sleep in wet wool without shivering, how to eat pine bark and not dream, how to move so quietly that even the crows forgot to announce her.

The crow above her did not forget. It shifted, rasped once, and looked down the road with one bright, disapproving eye.

Seren followed its gaze.

A figure was climbing toward her through the fog — small, hooded, moving with the careful economy of someone who had walked these passes since childhood. A satchel rode her hip. Her boots were wrong for the altitude, city boots, but she placed each step like a woman reading scripture, certain of every line.

The vessel.

Seren had heard the word spoken only twice before the fires came. Once in the Grand Reliquary, from the mouth of High Confessor Dain Mordane himself, delivered in that voice like oil poured over glass: *A vessel has woken in Greyhollow. The last of the old blood. Bring her to Veilspire unspoiled.* And once in the barracks at night, whispered by a young acolyte who thought no one heard him pray — not to the dead gods, but against them.

Then had come the execution order. Not for the vessel. For three acolytes who had asked too many questions about what the Order actually did with the vessels it found. Seren had been handed the writ because she was reliable, because her blade was clean, because she had never once asked why.

She had read the names. She had read them eleven times by candlelight. And then she had done the only thing her body knew how to do, some animal wisdom older than her oaths: she said no.

They were burning her in effigy somewhere, probably. *Serene Ashvale, fallen to corruption, given to the pyre with honors.* She wondered if anyone had wept. She suspected one person had — a girl named Ivetta whose debts Seren had paid off two winters ago — and she hated herself for wondering.

Now the vessel was walking straight up her mountain, alone, unguarded, carrying whatever terrible divinity the Order wanted back, and Seren Ashvale — dead, disgraced, and freezing — stepped out of the fog into her path.

* * *

The woman stopped four paces away. Up close, she looked less like a priestess than like a carpenter — wind-chapped, ink-stained fingers, a face built for endurance rather than revelation. But her eyes. Seren had seen that look before, in the reliquary, in the eyes of saints' bones behind glass: something looking out through a person who hadn't agreed to be looked through.

"You're blocking the road," the woman said. Her voice was low, roughened by sleeplessness.

"I'm assessing it." Seren kept her hands visible and away from her sword — an old habit of de-escalation, or perhaps just theater. "There's a patrol behind you. Two miles, maybe less. Six knights and a hound-handler. You've been leaving tracks like a confession."

Something flickered across the woman's face — not fear exactly. Recognition of a fear she'd already made peace with. "And you're not with them?"

"What do you think?"

"I think," Freya Whitlock said — because Seren knew her name now, everyone in the Order's hunting coven knew her name, *the carver, the quiet-witch, the vessel* — "that a knight of the Hollow Order doesn't stand alone on the Thornfell pass at dawn unless something has gone very wrong for her. So either you're a trap, or you're worse than a trap. Either way, I should keep walking."

"Through six knights?"

"Through six knights." Freya shifted her satchel higher on her shoulder. "I know this pass better than they do. Better than you do, probably. There's a goat track above the scree—"

"—that ends at a shear drop below Widow's Tooth, yes, I know it, I've patrolled it for nine years." Seren felt the absurd urge to laugh. Nine years of mapping every fold of these mountains for the Order, and now the knowledge belonged only to her, which meant it finally belonged to the right side. Or to no side. She hadn't decided yet. "You'll make it as far as the Tooth, and then the fog will lift, and the hound will find you anyway. They brought Vesper's line. Greyhounds of the crypt-bred stock. They don't lose scent, and they don't tire, and they are very, very good at cliffs."

Freya studied her. Seren let herself be studied — the scar splitting her left eyebrow, the Order's sun-and-veil sigil half-scraped from her breastplate, the hollows under her eyes. Let the witch read whatever she liked. Lies were easy to maintain; exhaustion couldn't be faked at this depth.

"You faked your death," Freya said at last. Not a question. "The chapel fire on the Kestrel Way. They burned a knight in effigy. My widow in Greyhollow lit a candle for you — she does that, lights candles for the Order's honored dead, old habit." Something moved in her expression, bitter and tired. "She prayed for your soul."

"I know," Seren said quietly. "I heard her."

And she had. That was the obscene secret of the past six weeks: since the day she'd died, Seren Ashvale had begun to hear prayers. Faint things, mostly — grief-shaped murmurs drifting up from the valleys like smoke finding a chimney. She had told herself it was madness, altitude sickness, guilt wearing a mask. But she had heard Maren Whitlock-light-a-candle pray for the soul of Seren Ashvale, and the prayer had landed inside her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Which meant the veil really was tearing. Which meant everything she'd refused to kill three acolytes over was true.

"They'll offer you absolution," Freya said. "If you bring me in."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because I want you to know that I've already done the arithmetic." Serne met her eyes. "Six knights, one hound, one defector, one vessel. I've run it every way. The numbers don't favor you alone. They don't much favor us together. But alone is a certainty, and together is only a probability, and I have learned recently that probabilities are all anyone gets." She paused. "Also, you light candles for the dead. That's not nothing. That's the whole religion, as far as I can tell, and the Order burned it out of us for a reason."

Down the valley, faint as a held breath, a hound began to bay.

Freya closed her eyes for one heartbeat — Seren watched her do it, watched the woman gather herself the way one gathers scattered grain before wind — and then opened them. "The goat track," she said. "It's faster if you know the loose stones. Do you trust me?"

"No," Seren said.

"Good," said Freya Whitlock, and turned uphill. "Neither do I. Come on."

* * *

The shear below Widow's Tooth was exactly as treacherous as Seren remembered, which is to say it tried to kill them twice in the first hundred yards. They went up it in fog so thick the world shrank to arm's length — Freya's hand appearing out of the white to point: *there, the flake of granite, weight on your left heel, don't trust the root.* Seren obeyed. It galled her, obeying, after nine years of giving orders, but the mountain did not care about rank and neither did gravity, and the carver knew the stone the way Seren had once known her catechism.

The baying grew closer. Then, horribly, it multiplied.

"They've split the pack," Seren breathed, flattening against the rock. Below them, shapes moved through the fog — lantern-glow smeared yellow, the grey drift of cloaks. Six had become eight. Reinforcements from the waystation at Coldharrow.

"There's a shelf above us," Freya whispered. "Old watch-post. My grandfather used it. There's a way down the far side into the ravine, and the ravine runs to the river, and—" She stopped. Listened. Seren saw her go very still, head tilted, listening to something that wasn't sound. "They know where we're going."

"How could they possibly—"

"Because I can hear them," Freya said, and her voice had gone strange, layered, as though two speakers shared one throat. "Their prayers. Knights pray too, did you know that? Before a fight, after a kill. They're praying to their god right now — the Order's god, the Veil Unbroken — and he's answering them, and I can hear both sides of it." Her hand crept to her satchel. "He's telling them where I am."

Seren's blood went cold. The doctrine she'd swallowed for nine years rearranged itself in real time: the Order didn't serve an absence of gods. It served *one*. One hungry, jealous thing behind the veil, feeding on the prayers of a continent, and calling itself the only truth. Every execution, every burned chapel, every acolyte put to the question — tithes. All of it tithes.

"The shelf," Seren said. "Now. We fight from height or we die in the open."

They gained the shelf as the first lantern cleared the scree. It was a bare crescent of stone beneath an overhang, wide enough for two, walled by a lip of rock to the knee. Seren drew her sword at last — dead women carry swords; it turned out death changed very little about the work — and heard, rising through the fog, the voice she had dreaded for six weeks before she'd ever heard it speak.

"Seren Ashvale." Captain Elara Voss stood at the base of the climb, lantern raised, her face calm as poured wax. Behind her, knights fanned out across the scree, and the hound strained at its chain, singing that low crypt-born note. "We buried you with honors. Come down, sister. The Confessor is merciful to the corrupted. He told me himself."

"He told you," Seren said, "or his god did?"

Voss's smile didn't waver, but something behind her eyes did — a flicker, a door opening onto a room Seren was never meant to see. "Both," Voss said simply. "That's what faith is. Come down, Seren. Bring the vessel. Be forgiven."

"I watched you kneel at Veyra's funeral," Seren called back, and hated how her voice shook. "You wept. Was that faith too? Was that the tithe?"

"Last chance," Voss said, and drew her blade, and the fog filled with steel.

What happened next, Seren would remember forever in fragments: the hound loosed and leaping, impossibly far, jaws closing on air where Freya had been a half-second before — Freya shouting a word in no living language, a carved bone charm held high and *singing*, a note that made the hound whimper and cringe as though struck — Voss climbing, fast and lethal, Seren meeting her blade to blade on the narrow shelf while the mountain dropped away beneath their boots.

"You were the best of us," Voss snarled, pressing her back, sparks streaming. "You threw it away for *her*?"

"I threw it away for three children with names," Seren gasped, and broke the bind, and cut Voss across the thigh — shallow, deliberate, a knight's mercy — and shoved her hard. Voss went down on the scree. The hound howled. And Freya's charm-note rose again, and the fog *answered* — the whole ravine seemed to inhale, lanterns guttering, knights staggering as though the air itself had turned against them — and in that ringing silence Seren grabbed Freya's arm and pulled her over the far lip of the shelf, into the dark throat of the ravine, down and down through falling water and loose shale until the bay of the hound faded to memory and then to nothing.

* * *

They collapsed at the river's edge as the light thickened toward noon. Freya sat with her arms around her knees, shaking — reaction, Seren recognized, the body presenting its bill. Seren washed the blood — hers, Voss's, she couldn't tell — from her knuckles and tried not to think about the look on Voss's face. Not hatred. Worse. *Grief.*

"She'll report that you spared her," Freya said at last. Her voice was hoarse. "Won't that cost her?"

"Yes. And she'll report it anyway, because Voss has never lied in her life. It's her one virtue and it's going to get her broken to the ranks." Seren stared into the green water. "I'm sorry about the hound."

"It's not dead. Frightened half to death, but alive. The charms quiet the restless dead — that hound's been dead before, did you know that? Crypt-bred means they die them once, to bind them. It heard me and it *remembered*." Freya unwrapped a strip of dried fish and offered half. After a moment, Seren took it. It seemed important, somehow, that she take it.

They ate in silence. The river talked instead, patient and cold.

"Where will you go?" Seren asked eventually. "They won't stop. What I saw back there — that thing answering their prayers — it won't stop. It wants you specifically. It's been waiting for you specifically."

"I know." Freya looked up the river, toward the high passes, where the peaks went white against a sky the color of pewter. "There's someone in Thornfell who owes me. Smuggler. Odessa Vane. If anyone can hide a fugitive priestess and a dead knight, it's her — for a price." A ghost of a smile, dry as dust. "There's always a price."

"Vane." Seren turned the name over. Odessa Vane's ships ran the night channels; every knight in the garrison knew her ledger if not her face. "She'll sell you to the Order the moment the number's right."

"Probably," Freya agreed. "But she'll hate doing it, and she'll charge me extra for the guilt, and right now that's the closest thing to loyalty I can afford." She stood, brushing shale from her cloak, and looked down at Seren — really looked, the assessment of a woman deciding whether to carve a name into stone or sand. "You could walk away. Go south, lose yourself. No one hunts a dead woman who stays dead."

Seren considered it. Truly considered it — the warm valleys, the anonymous towns, the long slow forgetting. And then she thought of three names on an execution writ, of a candle lit for her soul by a grieving stranger, of a god behind a veil drinking a continent's prayers, and of the fact that for forty-one days the only voice that had ever answered her own had come from a hound and a heretic on a mountain road.

"Dead women can go where they please," Seren said, standing. "This is where I please."

Freya studied her a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and started up the river trail. "Keep pace, Knight. And when we reach Vane's camp — let me do the talking. She shoots first at people who look like soldiers."

"And what does she do to people who look like priests?" Seren called after her.

Freya didn't turn around. "Worse," she said. "She makes them family."
