## 4. The Smuggler Queen's Price

The road down into Greyhollow smelled of pine resin and old rain, and Freya walked it like a woman returning to a house she'd burned down herself.

Seren kept three paces behind her the whole way — not out of courtesy, Freya had learned, but out of habit. A knight's spacing. Close enough to strike, far enough to see. Even stripped of her white-and-slate armor, even with her Order brand scarred over in a crude attempt at erasure, Seren Ashvale moved like a blade someone had taught to walk.

"You said this smuggler queen owes you," Seren said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I said she owes me *three* things." Freya adjusted the strap of her satchel, feeling the bone blanks inside shift against her hip. "Whether she'll admit to all three is another matter."

"And you trust her?"

Freya laughed — a short, dry sound that startled a jay from the gorse. "No. I trust that she's greedy, sentimental, and smarter than everyone who's ever underestimated her. That's better than trust. Trust is what people ask for when they haven't got leverage."

Ahead, through the thinning trees, the town appeared the way it always did: sudden and grudging, as though Greyhollow had been dropped into the mountain's lap by accident and refused to apologize for it. Slate roofs furred with moss. Chimneys bleeding woodsmoke sideways in the wind. The harbor below was little more than a stone lip cradling black water, where Odessa Vane's ships — and ships that had never been registered anywhere — sat moored like sleeping animals.

And above it all, on the headland, the lantern tower. Not an official light; the Crown had never wasted coin on a beacon for a town that officially didn't exist. This one belonged to the smugglers, and it burned green.

"Green means she's home," Freya said. "Red means don't come in. We've never needed red before."

"There's a first time for everything," Seren murmured, and her hand drifted toward the sword she wasn't carrying. She'd left it wrapped in oilcloth under a cairn two miles back; walking into Greyhollow armed with an Order blade would be like walking into a chapel wearing a heretic's face. Which, Freya supposed, Seren essentially was doing either way.

They came down the switchback road as the light failed, past drying nets and salt-crusted pilings, past the fishwife who spat when she saw Seren's jaw and the widow's son — no. Not the widow's son. Freya caught herself, breath hitching. Three days since the dead had begun to speak, and already her mind reached for ghosts the way her fingers reached for a knife. The boy's voice still lived somewhere behind her sternum: *help us*, he'd said, over and over, while his mother wept over a charm that no longer worked.

*Help us.* As if there were many of them. As if they were all saying it at once.

She hadn't told Seren about that part.

* * *

Odessa Vane received them in the counting room above the Salt Cellar, because Odessa Vane did everything above the Salt Cellar — the tavern below existed mostly to give her a reason to be upstairs listening. The room was small and dense with wealth that pretended otherwise: ledgers bound in oiled leather, a scale for weighing coin, a window overlooking the harbor, and a stove that filled the space with the smell of peat and cinnamon.

She sat behind the desk like a queen behind a throne, which was accurate, though her crown was a plain iron ring in her ear and her scepter was a quill currently dripping ink onto a shipping manifest. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with grey shot through her black hair like frost working its way into a fence post. Her eyes were the color of deep water over sand, and they found Freya's face, then Seren's, then Freya's again, and narrowed.

"Well," Odessa said. "The carver brings me a corpse."

"Seren Ashvale is alive."

"I know who she is." Odessa set down the quill with the deliberation of a woman deciding where to put a knife. "Every Order knight on the Thornfell roads is looking for a dead woman and a bone-witch. You've made my town expensive, Whitlock. Do you know what a bounty does to the price of silence? Silence used to be free here. Now it's a commodity, and commodities attract brokers, and brokers talk."

"Then it's fortunate," Freya said, "that you owe me three debts, and I've only come to collect one."

Something flickered across Odessa's face — annoyance, yes, but underneath it something warmer and more tired. "Sit down, both of you, before you make the room worse by standing in it." She poured three cups of something dark from a pot on the stove and pushed them across the desk without ceremony. "Now. What do you want? Sanctuary, I assume. Passage. New faces. All of which cost money I'll never see again."

"Sanctuary. Until the thirteenth moon."

"That's six weeks."

"I know how moons work, Dess."

"*Don't*—" Odessa stopped. Breathed. Her hand, Freya noticed, had gone briefly to her left breast, pressing flat against her sternum the way people do when a grief shifts position. "Six weeks of hiding the most hunted woman in Caerlum in a town the Order already watches. And why should I spend that? Sentiment?" She smiled thinly. "You know I don't trade in sentiment."

"You trade in it constantly," Freya said. "You just price it honestly. Here's the payment: I'm calling the second debt. The one from the winter the *Kestrel* went down."

The room went very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the tavern roared at some joke or argument, muffled through the floorboards like a heartbeat through skin.

Odessa's face did not change, but everything behind it did — a whole weather system moving behind glass. "That debt is paid," she said quietly.

"It isn't. I never carved the charm. I told you I couldn't — that the drowned don't answer, that the water takes their voices too deep. You accepted that. But you never released the debt, and neither did I." Freya leaned forward. "Three days ago, Dess, the dead started speaking. All of them. The veil between the worlds is torn — I've *seen* it, I've heard what's coming through — and the Order wants me dead for reasons I barely understand yet. I need walls, and food, and time, and people who will lie for me. In exchange, I will carve you the charm I swore I couldn't carve. Your brother will speak to you."

For a long moment Odessa Vane, smuggler queen of the Thornfells, terror of three coastlines, looked like nothing so much as a woman who had been struck.

"You said the drowned don't answer," she repeated.

"The drowned didn't. Everything answers now." Freya hesitated, and then gave her the truth, because Odessa could smell a withheld thing the way sharks smelled blood. "That's part of what frightens me. The voices aren't asking for rest anymore, Dess. They're asking for help. Something on the other side of the veil is — hungry, maybe. Or waking. Or both. And the gods who held it shut are gone."

"Gone," Seren said from her corner. "Or murdered. Depends which history you read."

Odessa looked between them — the fugitive knight, the bone-carver with shadows under her eyes like bruises — and Freya watched her run the arithmetic. Risk against debt. Coin against something that had never once appeared in her ledgers. Finally she drained her cup, stood, and went to the window, looking down at her black harbor and the green light burning on the headland.

"My brother's name was Tam," she said, to the glass. "He was twenty-three. He went down with the *Kestrel* in the Sorrows, carrying cargo I shouldn't have let him carry alone, because I wanted to prove I could run two ships at once and he wanted to prove I could rely on him." Her voice was level in the way that only long-practiced grief can be level. "They never found him. They found his coat. So if your charm works — if I speak to him and hear nothing but cold water and silence — our debt stands unpaid, and you leave my town tonight, and I wish you luck dying somewhere else."

"Dess—"

"Those are my terms, Whitlock. Take them or the road's behind you." She turned. Her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "I'll hide you six weeks. I'll feed your knight and lie to every captain who asks. And in exchange you'll sit at that desk, with my knives and my bone, and you'll carve until my brother talks to me. Even once. Even one word. That's the price."

Freya held her gaze. Below them the tide turned against the pilings with a sound like slow breathing.

"One word," Freya agreed.

* * *

They put Freya up in the loft above the Salt Cellar, among barrels of untaxed brandy and coils of rope that smelled of tar and salt. Seren took the corner by the stairs, naturally, and slept — Freya suspected — with one eye open and one hand shaped around the absence of her sword.

On the third evening, Odessa brought up the materials herself: a bundle wrapped in sailcloth, carried the way other women carried infants.

Inside was whalebone, old and yellowed, dense as ivory, along with a knife with a blade worn thin from years of use. "Tam's carving knife," Odessa said. "He whittled when he couldn't sleep. Little birds, mostly. Bad ones. He had no talent for it whatsoever, and he did it anyway, all the time, because—" Her voice snagged. She cleared her throat. "Because he liked making things. Use his knife. If anyone's going to cut into bone in this room, it may as well be his."

Freya worked through the night.

Whalebone was stubborn stuff — it fought the blade, remembered the sea, resisted fine lines the way driftwood resists fire. She carved the sigils deep instead of delicate: the opening mark, the listening mark, the bridge. Around midnight she realized she was humming an old boat-song she didn't remember learning, and stopped, unnerved, because the tune kept trying to bend itself into something older underneath, something with no words that felt like standing at the edge of a very tall place.

*Not now,* she told the voice in her blood, the god-shaped pressure that had been quiet but never absent since the pass. *I'm working.*

*So am I,* said something that might have been her own exhaustion, and left it at that.

Near dawn, the charm finished — a flat disc of pale bone strung on greased cord — she held it in both palms and spoke the words her grandmother had taught her, the ones that weren't prayers exactly, more like knocking politely on a door.

The bone warmed.

And the loft filled, gently, unmistakably, with the smell of the open sea.

Odessa, who had sworn she wouldn't wait up, was suddenly there in the doorway in her nightclothes with a lamp shaking in her hand. "Is it— did it—"

"I don't know," Freya said honestly. "It's listening. Whether anything's talking back..." She held the charm out across the desk. "Ask it."

Odessa sat down slowly, like an old woman, though she was not old. She took the charm in both hands. For a moment she only breathed. Then, in the smallest voice Freya had ever heard her use:

"Tamsin. Tamsin, it's Dess. Can you—"

The lamp flame leaned. The sea-smell sharpened, brine and kelp and cold deep water. And from everywhere and nowhere — from the bone, from the walls, from the dark beyond the window where the green light burned — came a voice. Young. Amused. Slightly exasperated, in the manner of younger brothers everywhere:

*"Dess. Sweet tides, Dess, it took you long enough. I've been shouting since the veil tore — do you have any idea how loud it is over here? Everyone talking at once. Oh — oh, and there's a knight in the room with you, tell her the cairn two miles north is a terrible hiding spot, the Order scouts it every—*

The voice cut off like a snapped rope.

Silence rushed in. Odessa stared at the charm, white-faced. Then at Seren. Then at Freya.

"He says," Odessa whispered, and had to start again, "he says the cairn. Where you buried your sword. They scout it."

Boots were pounding up the stairs from the tavern below before the echo faded — and beneath the panic rising in Freya's chest, faint and patient and terribly close, she heard the dead of Greyhollow begin, all together, to sing.
