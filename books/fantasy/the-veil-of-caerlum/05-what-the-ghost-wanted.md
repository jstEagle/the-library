## 5. What the Ghost Wanted

Being dead was mostly waiting.

Tam had gotten very good at it. In life he'd been terrible at waiting — a fidgeter, a hummer, the boy who tapped his boot against the pew until the priest glared. But death had taught him patience the way the sea teaches sailors: thoroughly, and against their will.

Now he waited inside a lantern.

Not *in* it, exactly. The lantern was iron and river-glass, hung from the sternpost of Odessa Vane's flagship, the *Kestrel's Grudge*, and it burned with an oil that smelled of juniper and something older, something like the inside of a tomb that had been aired out once and never again. His ghost was bound to its flame the way a moth is bound to a candle — except Tam had never been given the courtesy of choosing to fly into it. Odessa had simply caught him, mid-drift, three nights after his death, and poured him into the glass like she was decanting wine.

"Better than blowing about the harbor," she'd said. "You'll thank me later."

He hadn't thanked her yet. He was keeping a running tally of grievances instead, which was also easier than thinking about the tablet.

*The tablet.* He turned away from the thought the way you turn your face from a cold wind. Below him, on the deck, lamplight moved — lanterns being lit for evening, the crew going about their rounds, and among them two figures he'd been watching for days now with the hungry curiosity of a man pressing his nose to a bakery window.

The carver woman, Freya Whitlock, sat on a coil of rope near the mainmast with her knives spread out before her on an oilcloth like a surgeon's tools. She carved the way other people prayed — slowly, with her whole self, lips moving around words she didn't seem to know she was saying. Beside her, arms crossed, stood the defector knight, Seren Ashvale, wearing her Order leathers stripped of every insignia, looking like a wolf in a coat it had outgrown.

And there — leaning against the rail with a mug of something steaming, watching them both with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like — Odessa.

Tam drifted down through the rigging. This was the one mercy of the binding: within the length of the ship's shadow, wherever the lantern's light fell, he could go. Beyond it, nothing but the gray tug of elsewhere, which he did not care to visit again.

"You're brooding," he announced, settling onto the rail beside Odessa, cross-legged, translucent as breath on glass.

She didn't startle. She never startled; it was one of the many things that annoyed him about her. "Ghosts don't brood," she said. "They *haunt*. There's a difference. Brooding is free."

"I'm pre-haunting. Building anticipation."

"Mm." She sipped her drink. Down the deck, the carver looked up sharply, eyes tracking across empty air toward where Tam sat, and Tam felt the little electric jolt he always felt when the living almost-saw him. Almost. Her gaze slid past. It always slid past. He was a smudge in the corner of the eye, a draft through a shut door.

Except.

Except that three days ago, in this cabin, she'd held a bone charm to her ear while Odessa demanded her brother back, and Tam had heard — through the charm, through the veil gone thin as wet paper — the dead speaking. Not just murmuring. *Speaking.*

And last night, Freya Whitlock had looked directly at the sternpost lantern and said, quietly, so only the night could hear: *"I can hear you in there. If you have something to say, say it."*

He hadn't said it. He'd spent the whole night not saying it, which is a full-time occupation.

"The carver," Tam said. "Your new investment. She hears things."

Odessa went still in the particular way she went still when a deal was turning in her favor and she wanted no one to see her counting. "Go on."

"She heard me. Or she will. And I've got—" Here the old cold came up through him, colder than the grave, colder than the harbor water he'd drowned in. "I've got something she needs. Something *you* need, if you're honest about why you dragged me aboard all these years."

"Careful, Tam."

"You've kept me in a bottle for six years because talking to me is the closest you can get to talking to *him*." The words came out sharper than he meant, the way they always did. Dead men don't get to take things back. "Don't look at me like that. You think I don't know what I am? I'm not company, Odessa. I'm a séance you keep lit."

For a long moment the only sound was the water slapping the hull and the creak of rope. Then Odessa set down her mug, very precisely, and said, "Deck. Now. All of you. Bring the carver's knives if she's finished sulking over them."

"I wasn't—"

"Deck, ghost."

* * *

The cabin below decks smelled of tallow and tar and old maps, and it was crowded in a way Tam found delicious, because he'd spent six years alone with a flame. Odessa behind her desk like a queen holding court, which she was, though the crown was a knitted cap and the throne was a chair missing one arm. Seren Ashvale standing by the door with her hand nowhere near her sword and her whole body screaming otherwise. Freya in the middle, hands folded, looking like a woman who had walked into a trap and couldn't yet tell whose.

"So," Odessa said. "Our resident spirit has information. He's been sitting on it like a hen. I want it out in the open, because if it's worth what I think it's worth, we're renegotiating everyone's contracts."

"That's not how—" Seren started.

"It's exactly how smuggling works, knight. Sit down."

Freya spoke first, and her voice was quiet in the way that made the whole room lean toward it. "Tam." Just the name. Just his name, spoken aloud by living lips, aimed at *him*, and Tam felt it land somewhere ghosts shouldn't feel anything at all.

"Aye," he said, and his voice came out thinner than he liked. He gathered himself. Six years of rehearsing, and here it was, the performance of his afterlife. "Right. So. Before I died — and I'll thank you all not to make faces, drowning jokes are how I cope — I carried cargo for Odessa. Standard run up the Thornfells, nothing special. Except the client was special. Old man, hooded, paid in coin so old it had kings on it nobody remembers. Told me: take this to the shrine at Caerlum's Foot, give it to whoever tends the stones, touch nothing else, ask nothing."

"What was it?" Freya asked.

"A tablet." He let the word hang. "Bone. Carved all over with writing that moved when I looked at it too long. Half a tablet — broken clean down the middle, wrapped in oilcloth. Heavy, for its size. Warm. It was warm the whole journey, like carrying a sleeping bird."

The silence in the cabin had changed quality. Tam had watched a lot of silences from inside his glass, and this one had teeth.

"I never made the shrine," he said. "Order patrol caught us at the narrows. They weren't looking for smugglers — they were looking for *it*. They'd been tipped. Someone sold me." He glanced at Odessa, and had the grace, even dead, to soften it. "Not you. I checked. I've had six years to check. It was the harbormaster."

"He's dead now," Odessa said comfortably. "Very dead. Unrelated."

"Anyway. They took the tablet off my body, or they thought they did. I'd hidden it in the ballast before they boarded — old trick — and when I died, I..." He faltered. How to explain what dying had done? The moment the water closed over him, everything he'd touched, everything he'd *carried*, had become part of him. A ghost is nothing but unfinished business given a shape, and his business had soaked into the grain of that tablet like ink into skin. "It stayed with me. It's *in* me. Wherever I drift, it drifts. Which means it's in that lantern, most days, because that's where I'm nailed."

Freya rose slowly. "May I?"

He nodded, and she lifted the lantern from its hook — the cabin dimming oddly as she did, as if the room were holding its breath — and set it on the desk. She pressed her palm flat against the warm glass and closed her eyes, and Tam watched her go somewhere he couldn't follow, her lips moving, her brow furrowing.

When she opened her eyes they were wet. "There are names on it," she whispered. "Dozens of names. Written in a script that... it's not letters, it's *vows*. It's half a covenant. The gods' half."

"And the other half?" Seren asked, and her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice of a woman reading her own indictment.

Tam laughed — a small, cracked thing. "That's the punchline, knight. That's the part you're really going to love. Because the Order didn't destroy the covenant when they killed the gods. They couldn't. They took both halves to their fortress at Veilspire and locked them in the reliquary vaults, because zealots can't burn what they don't understand, and the tablets wouldn't burn. Wouldn't chip. Wouldn't drown." He tapped his chest, where a heartbeat used to live. "Trust me. I tried the drowning."

"How do you know where they keep it?" Seren said. "No one knows Veilspire's vaults. No one alive."

"No one *alive*," Tam agreed pleasantly. "But dead men gossip, knight. The citadel's catacombs are packed with the restless — everyone the Order ever silenced, all milling around in the dark under those towers like sheep in a fold. And ghosts talk. We talk *constantly*. Eternity is very boring. I know the vaults. I know the wards, the rotations, the tunnel the masons sealed and forgot. I know Veilspire better than anyone breathing, because I know everyone buried under it."

He looked around the cabin — at the smuggler queen gripping her desk edge, at the knight whose whole faith was cracking audibly, at the carver with the lantern-light swimming in her eyes — and something loosened in him that had been clenched for six years.

"Here's what I want," he said. "In exchange. Not gold. Not freedom — well. Freedom eventually, if you're feeling generous. What I want is to come with you. All the way. I've been furniture for six years, Odessa. A lamp. A novelty. I want to be *crew*. I want to matter to something before whatever comes after this after."

Odessa studied him for a long time, and then, softly, in a voice she'd have denied using under torture, she said, "You always mattered, you idiot. That was the problem."

Tam pretended not to hear it. Some gifts you accept by pretending they weren't given.

"Well then," he said briskly, floating up through the ceiling a few inches just to prove he could. "We'd best plan quickly. Because there's one more thing I haven't told you, and you're going to hate it."

Freya's head came up. "What?"

"The thirteenth moon isn't just rising," Tam said. "The Order knows what it's for. They're moving the tablets *out* of Veilspire — deep into the mountain, to a place even the dead won't talk about. We have until the twelfth moon's eclipse. After that, the covenant halves are gone, and so is any chance of—"

He stopped. Through the cabin wall, faint as a whisper through cloth, he could hear it: the sound of oars, many oars, pulling in rhythm up the dark harbor channel. And beneath it, thin and clear, a voice raised in a hymn he knew far too well.

"Hollow Order," he said. "They've found us. And they're not sneaking."
