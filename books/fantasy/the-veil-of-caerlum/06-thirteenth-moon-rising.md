## 6. Thirteenth Moon Rising

The archive of the Hollow Order sat beneath a chapel that no longer had a god.

Freya could feel the absence from across the square — a hollowness in the air, like a tooth missing from the jaw of the world. The chapel of Saint Vess had been deconsecrated a century ago, its altar stones pried up and sold, its windows replaced with plain grey glass. Now it served the Order as a countinghouse, a place where tithes were tallied and heretics were inventoried. And beneath it, if Tam's ghost-memory was true, lay the Archive of Suppression: every prayer ever recorded as answered, every priestess ever named, every scrap of truth the Order had spent a hundred years burying under doctrine and ash.

"You're certain?" Seren murmured beside her. They crouched in the shadow of the bell tower, three streets back, close enough to smell the incense the Order burned to cover the scent of old blood. "Because once we go down there, there's no coming up the same."

"I'm never certain," Freya said. "I carve bones. Certainty is for people who've never held something dead in their hands."

Beside them, Odessa Vane turned her lantern — Tam's lantern — slowly on its hook. Inside, the flame guttered sideways against the wind though there was none, and for a moment Freya caught the shape of a face in the glass, young and crooked-grinned.

*Third door past the vestry,* Tam said, his voice like frost forming on a window. *Down the stair that smells of pennies. And mind the third step — it sings.*

"Sings," Odessa repeated flatly. "The step sings."

*Squeals. Sings is more poetic.*

"We are not doing poetry tonight," Odessa said, but her thumb brushed the lantern glass with something gentler than exasperation, and Freya looked away from the small tenderness of it, the way you look away from a stranger crying.

The moon overhead was a sliver, one night shy of the twelfth. Above it, barely visible, hung the faintest smudge — the thirteenth moon, the hidden one, the moon that only rose when the veil grew thin. Caerlum had shown it to her in dreams: a pale wound in the sky that most eyes slid off like water off oilcloth. Twenty nights until it reached fullness. Twenty nights until the covenant either held or broke.

*You should tell them,* the god said, quiet at the base of her skull, the way he always was now. Winter-voiced. Patient. She hated how patient he was.

"Tell us what?" Seren asked.

Freya realized she'd been staring at the sky. She lowered her gaze. "Nothing. Let's move."

*You cannot carry this alone forever, little carver.*

"Watch me," she whispered, and went over the wall.

* * *

The third step sang. Odessa cursed it in three languages, and somewhere above them a sentry paused, listened, decided the dark was only the dark, and moved on.

The archive smelled exactly as Tam had promised — of pennies, of copper and old iron, the smell Freya had come to associate with the Order's work. Rows of shelving stretched into darkness, laden with ledgers bound in grey leather, each spine stamped with the Order's seal: an eye crossed out. A century of unseeing, catalogued and alphabetized.

Seren lit a single candle stub and held it high, and her face in its light was terrible and still. "I trained in a house like this," she said. "Vows of silence. Vow of erasure. We memorized texts and then burned our own copies so nothing lived outside our heads." Her jaw worked. "I used to think it was holy."

"Ledgers first," Freya said. "Prayers answered. Cross-referenced by year."

They worked in the candle's small circle, pulling volumes, and the dust came down on them like grey snow. Freya found the first ledger within minutes — *Record of Manifest Answer, Years 1–9 After the Silence* — and opened it with hands that wanted to shake.

It was not what she expected. She had expected denial: prayers unanswered, gods declared dead, silence confirmed. Instead the pages were full. Hundreds of entries in a precise clerical hand. Prayers answered. Small ones, mostly — a fever broken, a lost child found, rain on a dying field — but answered all the same, dated years after the Silence began, after the Order's own doctrine said the last god had died screaming.

"They knew," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "The Order knew the gods weren't dead. They wrote it down. Year by year. They *catalogued* it."

"Keep reading," Seren said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "There's more. I can feel there's more."

The second ledger was larger, chained to its shelf, and its lock surrendered to Odessa's picks with a click that sounded obscenely loud. Inside, the handwriting changed — older, hurried, written by someone whose hand shook. The heading read: *On the Nature of the Veil and the True Cause of the Silence. For the High Custodian's Eyes Only. Burn After Reading.*

Nobody had burned it.

Freya read. And the world she had built herself upon — the careful, managed, grief-carved world where the gods were simply gone and the dead were simply quiet and her strange gift was a small secret thing — came apart page by page like wet paper.

The gods had not been murdered.

She read it again, and again the words refused to change. The Order had not killed the gods. No blade had fallen, no heresy had been put down. A century ago, the veil between realms had torn — torn from the *other side*, torn by something that pressed against it the way a starving mouth presses against a window. The old texts called it the Hunger Beyond. It had no name because names were doors, and it ate through doors.

And so the gods — twelve of them, twelve bright powers, Caerlum among them, keeper of winter and mercy — had done the only thing left. They had woven themselves into the tear. All twelve. Their divinity became the stitches; their deaths became the seam. The Silence wasn't murder. It was sacrifice. The Order hadn't ended the age of gods.

The gods had ended themselves, and the Order had spent a hundred years lying about it — because the truth was unbearable. Because the truth had a final page.

Freya turned to it. The candle flame bent toward the paper as if it too wanted to read.

*The seam will fail within a hundred years, for mortal-made thread decays and divine thread, once given, does not renew itself. Renewal requires a vessel: one born with the gift of hearing the dead, of the old priestess line, alive at the turning of the thirteenth moon. The vessel must be brought to the covenant stone and given to the seam. This is not metaphor. The gods did not die. They were spent. The vessel will be spent likewise.*

Given to the seam.

Spent.

The ledger swayed in her grip. Somewhere behind her, Tam was saying something — she saw his light flickering, heard Odessa answer him — but it all came through as if underwater. Because she understood now. Not the theology. The *bookkeeping*. The Order had known for a century that they would need a priestess. Had known the old lines were hunted nearly to extinction. So they had not killed every hearth-child who heard the dead.

They had kept the lines alive. Quietly. Carefully. Moving certain families out of the pyre's path. Recording births. Waiting.

Harvesting.

*That is why they call me the vessel,* Freya thought, and the word finally showed its true weight. Not chosen. Chosen implies someone choosing *you*, some love or fate in it. She had been cultivated. A row of wheat planted a century ago and tended in the dark, waiting for harvest at the turning of the thirteenth moon. Every charm she'd ever carved, every whisper of the dead she'd ever heard, every strange grief-gift that made her what she was — it had all been ripening toward a stone altar and a seam that drank.

"Freya." Seren's hand on her arm. "Your hands. You're bleeding."

She looked down. She was gripping the ledger so hard the edge had bitten through skin. Blood welled along her palm, and — absurdly, horribly — she thought of her carving knife, of the way bone took blood and held it, and she thought: *even my blood knows its purpose. Even my body has been reading the ledger longer than I have.*

Caerlum stirred inside her, and for once he did not sound patient.

*Now you understand why I could not say it plainly. If I told you what you were grown for—*

"Grown for," she repeated aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle. "*Grown.* Like a crop. You let me believe—" She stopped. Pressed her palm hard against her thigh until the pain gave her something to stand on. "How much of it was true? Priestess? Last of the line? Or am I just the last one *alive* because everyone else was collected?"

Silence in her skull. Then, low: *Both. I swear it by the winter that made me. But I will not insult you by pretending the two truths weigh the same.*

"Good," Freya said. "Don't."

Odessa had the lantern raised, Tam's light thrown wide, and her face was grim in a way Freya had never seen on the smuggler queen — no smirk, no price being calculated. "Whatever's in that book," Odessa said, "we burn it or we take it?"

"We take it," Seren said. "Every page. The Order's own hand proves the lie. There are cells full of acolytes who deserve to know what their vows built." Her knuckles were white around her sword hilt. "And then we find the covenant stone before the Order finds another way to do this. Because they'll try again. People who farm priestesses don't stop farming."

"The stone's in the Thornfells," Tam said softly. His light dimmed, the way it did when he spoke of his death, of the tablet he'd carried half across the mountains before the ice took him. "Where I fell. I can take you. It's the one thing I've got left to give."

For a long moment Freya stood among the shelves of buried truth, bleeding quietly into her sleeve, and felt the strangest thing rise in her chest beneath the dread — not despair. Something harder. The widow in Greyhollow had come to her for a charm to quiet her son's ghost, and Freya had carved it, and the dead boy had begged through the bone for help, and she had helped him instead of silencing him. That had been her choice. Hers. Whatever she'd been grown for, no one had yet harvested the part of her that chose.

Twenty nights.

"All right," she said. "We take everything. And then we go north."

Above them, through the grey chapel glass, the thirteenth moon's faint wound glimmered in the sky, patient as a mouth.

And in the dark of the archive, far off, a bell began to ring — the Order's alarm, thin and rising — and Seren swore, and Odessa snuffed the candle, and the four of them ran for the stairs with a century of lies clutched against their chests.
