## 8. Veilspire

The citadel rose out of the cliff like a tooth the mountain had never managed to spit out.

Freya had seen it once as a girl, from her father's fishing boat — a smudge of pale stone against the northern sky, too far away to seem real. Now it filled half the world. Veilspire's walls were quarried from bone-white granite veined with grey, and the Order had carved them with a thousand names: the gods they had killed, each name struck through with a chisel-line so deep she could see it from the road. Caerlum's name was the longest. The strike-through had been carved so many times it was nearly a trench.

*They hated you enough to keep hating you for a hundred years,* Freya said silently.

*I know,* said the god inside her skull, and his voice was very quiet. *Hate is a kind of prayer. They gave me more of it than any temple ever did.*

Above the citadel, the twelfth moon hung swollen and copper-colored, sliding by degrees toward the shadow of the world. When it vanished, the veil would thin to gossamer for one hour, and every ward on those white walls would gutter like candles in a draft.

"That's our door," Seren murmured beside her. She lay flat against the scree in her old armor, sanded free of insignia, her face smeared with ash. She had not spoken much since Thornfell. Since the fire. "Eclipse begins at moonset. We have until it ends."

"And after?" Odessa asked, lounging against a boulder as though they were waiting for a tide rather than an eclipse. Her rings clicked against her belt buckles, all eleven of them, each one a debt or a promise. She wore her red coat tonight instead of grey — *if I'm going to die somewhere,* she'd said, *it won't be dressed like a penitent.*

"After," Seren said, "the wards come back. And everything still inside them belongs to the Order."

"Then let's not be everything," Odessa said.

Tam's lantern swung at Freya's hip, its flame burning that particular shade of blue-green that belonged to no living fire. Inside the glass, the ghost drifted like smoke deciding which shape to be.

"I hate this plan," Tam announced, for the fourth time since Greyhollow. "I want it noted. Historically. Carve it somewhere. *Tam objected.*"

"Noted," Freya said.

"You're taking me back to the place where they bound me to a lantern in the first place. Do you know what that's like? It's like walking into your own funeral and being asked to hold the flowers."

"Yes," Freya said quietly. "I do know something about that."

Tam subsided, the flame dimming contrite. In the weeks since the archive, since the truth — that the gods had not been murdered but had bled themselves willingly into the veil, that a priestess had always been meant to follow, harvested and raised and groomed without ever knowing it — he had been gentler with her. As gentle as a dead smuggler could be.

*You can still turn back,* Caerlum said. It was the first time he had offered. She wondered if it cost him.

"No," she said aloud, and Seren glanced at her, and Odessa smiled, and nobody asked her what she'd said, because they all knew by now that some conversations were not for sharing. That was the strange grace of them, Freya thought. Four people who had survived alone, learning the grammar of together.

The moon touched the shadow.

Darkness poured over Veilspire like ink into water, and far below, along the white walls, the wards flickered — great runed sigils that had burned steady for a century suddenly stuttering, guttering, dying to embers.

"Now," Seren breathed.

* * *

They went in through the cisterns.

Seren knew the way — she had helped design the water-ward rotations before her fall, and shame sat strangely on her as she led them through passages she'd once walked in white and gold. The cistern tunnels stank of mineral cold and old incense; the Order burned frankincense even in its plumbing, apparently, sanctifying everything, admitting nothing was ordinary. Freya's boots found each foothold in the dark, and the lantern light made ghosts of their shadows on wet stone.

Two acolytes stood watch at the cistern gate. Seren took them without a sound — a hook behind the knee, a hand sealing mouth and nose, lowered gently down. She laid them side by side and pulled her cloak over their faces.

"They'll wake with headaches," she whispered, "and questions they can't answer."

"You've gotten soft," Odessa teased, but there was warmth under it.

"I've gotten *tired*," Seren said, "of killing children who were lied to."

Beyond the gate, Veilspire opened upward — stairs spiraling through the rock, halls of white stone lit by everburning votive lamps, every surface carved with scripture Freya could now read, though she had never been taught. The god's language lived in her blood. *We seal. We suffer. We remember. We wait.* A century of zealots praying beneath words they thought were triumphs and were actually elegies.

*They built a tomb,* Caerlum said, *and called it a fortress.*

The Reliquary was at the heart of everything, naturally. Of course it was. Zealots kept their wounds close.

The doors were black iron worked with silver chains, and the wards around them did not gutter with the eclipse — these were fed differently, fed on something that made Freya's skin crawl to look at. Old prayers. Dead ones. Names of priests long burned, ground up into warding like bones into meal.

"It will hurt," Caerlum warned.

"Add it to the list," Freya said, and pressed her palms to the iron.

The pain came like winter flooding in through her wrists. Every name in those walls woke at her touch and screamed — not in hatred, but in *longing*, a hundred dead voices recognizing something living in her, clawing toward her the way drowning men claw toward light. Freya held on. Her nose bled freely. Somewhere far away Seren was shouting her name, and Odessa's hand was braced between her shoulders, solid and warm and stubbornly present.

*You are not alone in this either,* Odessa had told her once, drunk on plum wine in the Thornfells. *That's the whole trick of it, love. Nobody has to be.*

The chains fell away link by link, rusting into nothing, and the doors groaned open onto darkness that smelled of dust and myrrh and centuries.

* * *

Inside, the halves of the covenant waited on separate pedestals, as though the Order could not bear to let them touch.

The first half was the tablet — bone, ancient, yellowed to ivory, covered in script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it. This was the piece Tam had died carrying, the piece that had bound his ghost to the lantern when the Order's knights cut him down on the Salt Road and stuffed his lingering spirit into a ship-lantern to interrogate. He drifted out of the glass now, hovering above it, and for once he had nothing funny to say.

"Yeah," he said finally, thickly. "That's it. That's the thing that got me killed." A pause. "Worth it, though. Always hoped it would be."

The second half was worse. The second half was a knife — a curved blade of grey metal, and set into its spine was a sliver of bone that sang when Freya came near. Not with sound. With recognition.

"That's the cutting edge," Caerlum said, and for the first time since the voice had entered her life, he sounded afraid. "The rite requires a severance. Something must be given up whole. My siblings used the knife on themselves, at the stone, on the thirteenth moon. What they gave—" He stopped. Started again. "What they gave was not small."

Freya wrapped both relics in wool and oilcloth and bound them against her chest with the same care she used for charms that had to survive drowning. The moment the cloth settled, the eclipse-light beyond the high windows began to change — the shadow retreating off the moon's face, copper bleeding back into the dark.

"The wards," Seren said. "We're out of hour. Move. *Move.*"

* * *

They almost made it.

The trouble was the gate — the cistern gate, where two sleeping acolytes should have been and instead stood six knights in white-and-gold, drawn up in formation, blades bare. Their captain stepped forward, helm under his arm, and Freya saw his face and understood why Seren made a sound like something breaking.

"Commander Veyra's orders," the man said. "Bring the Ashvale woman and the vessel. No harm needs doing, Seren. Come home. Be absolved."

"I burned my home," Seren said. "It's ash. You're standing in the smoke."

The captain sighed, genuinely sorrowful, and raised his sword. "Then the smoke it is."

The fight in the cistern gate was ugly and close and lit only by Tam's lantern swinging wild on its chain. Seren met three knights at once and was a marvel in it — all economy and fury, the Order's own training turned back on itself. Odessa fought dirty, knives low, laughing in the way she laughed when she was most frightened. Freya put her back to the wall and did the only magic she trusted: she *carved*, quick strokes of her knife into her own palm's callus, whispering the old words, and the air went cold and the dead of Veilspire — a hundred years of them, all the names on the walls, all the priests burned to feed the wards — came up through the stones like water through cracked earth.

The knights broke. Men who had spent their lives denying the dead do not handle meeting them well.

But the eclipse ended, and the wards woke, and the cistern gate slammed shut with a sound like a coffin lid, iron bars dropping from the ceiling, sealing the corridor in sections. Separating them.

Freya, Seren, and the tablets were on the inner side of the falling grate. Odessa and Tam were on the outer side. On the wrong side. On the side filling with knights.

"Well," Odessa said through the bars, cheerful as a market-day greeting, and already pulling her knives. "This is inconvenient."

"Odessa." Freya gripped the iron. It was warming already as the wards drank power back into themselves. "Odessa, stand back, I can—"

"You can't, love. You'd bring the whole ceiling down and we both know it." Behind her, boots were thundering closer, dozens of them. Odessa looked over her shoulder, then back, and her smile changed — softened into something Freya had only seen once before, across a table in Greyhollow, over a charm for a drowned brother. "Eleven rings," she said. "You know what they are? Debts. Promises. Things people owed me and things I owed them. Only one I never collected." Her hand pressed flat against the bars, mirroring Freya's. "You get to live, Freya Whitlock. That's the payment. Consider us square."

"*No—*"

"Tam!" Odessa shouted, not turning. "Lantern! Now!"

And Tam — brave, ridiculous, dead-for-fifty-years Tam — flung himself out of the glass.

She felt it happen more than saw it: the ghost pouring out of the lantern like a wave leaving the shore, spreading himself thin and wide and blazing, wrapping Odessa in a shroud of blue-green fire that turned the knights' blades aside and bought her seconds, whole seconds, while she fought her way toward the maintenance sluice Seren was screaming directions to — the old flood-tunnel, the one way out that wasn't a gate—

The lantern hit the floor.

Whether it was knocked, whether it simply fell, no one ever established. But glass burst across the cistern stones in a spray of glittering shards, and Tam's flame — his self, his anchor, fifty years of drifting tied to that little prison of glass — shattered with it.

He came apart like mist in wind.

Freya heard him, just barely, in the last instant — not screaming, never screaming; he was laughing, breathless, delighted, the laugh of a man diving off a ship's rail into warm water —

"—worth it," the wind of him said, everywhere and nowhere. "Told you. Worth it—"

And he was gone. Scattered. Not destroyed, perhaps — ghosts were stubborn things, and Tam had been stubborn past all reason — but strewn across a citadel's worth of stone and shadow, unfindable, unknowable, unless someone went looking shard by shard.

On the other side of the bars, Odessa stood alone in the sudden silence, surrounded by fallen knights, blood sheeting dark down her left side from a wound Freya could see was deep, was *wrong*, was mortal in the slow terrible way. She looked at the broken lantern. She looked at Freya.

"Well," she said again, and her voice cracked clean through the cheerfulness. "Bugger."

Then the flood-tunnel swallowed her, running, limping, alive — for now — and the grate sealed, and Seren dragged Freya backward into the dark because the wards were waking fully now and the whole citadel was about to become a furnace of white fire.

* * *

They ran.

Through the cisterns, up through the hidden ways, out through a postern gate Seren had bribed a mason to leave unmortared ten years ago and had prayed he still had. Out onto the mountain road under the re-risen twelfth moon, full and copper and indifferent.

They did not stop until the citadel was a pale smear behind them. Then Seren collapsed against a boulder and shook, silent tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face. Freya stood at the road's edge with the covenant wrapped against her heart and did not cry at all, because something in her had gone past crying into a vast ringing quiet.

Both halves of the covenant. Recovered. The price paid in glass and blood.

*One moon remains,* Caerlum said softly. *Thirteen days.*

Behind them, far below, a single red spark moved along the mountain road — slow, stubborn, following. Someone had walked out of Veilspire's flood-tunnels and refused, absolutely refused, to die on schedule.

Freya watched the spark climb toward them and found, to her astonishment, that she was smiling through the grief.

"Come on, then," she whispered to the night, to the god, to the scattered ghost, to the bleeding queen crawling up her mountain. "We finish this. All of us. Whatever it costs."
