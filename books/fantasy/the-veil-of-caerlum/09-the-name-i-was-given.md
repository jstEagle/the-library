## 9. The Name I Was Given

The covenant stone stood at the crown of Caerlum's Rest, where the mountain forgot it was a mountain and became something older — a shoulder, perhaps, or a kneeling figure worn smooth by ten thousand years of weather and worship. Freya had climbed through the last hours of the twelfth moon with Seren's arm braced under hers and Odessa's blood drying stiff on both their coats, and when she crested the final ridge and saw the stone rising black against the stars, her first thought was not awe.

It was: *it looks like a bone.*

Of course it did. She had spent eleven years carving bone, reading the grain of femur and rib the way other women read letters. The covenant stone was vast — thirty feet of it at least, thrust up from the summit like a splinter of some enormous creature — and its surface was veined with channels that might have been carved or might have grown, grooves that once, she understood now, had run silver with godsblood. The old prayers called it the Anvil of the First Silence. The place where nine gods had laid down their names to hold shut a door that hungered.

The thirteenth moon was already climbing.

It came up over the eastern peaks swollen and wrong-colored, the color of milk left too long in a copper bowl, and beneath its light the shadows on the summit lay at angles that disagreed with one another. The veil was thin here. Thin everywhere tonight. Freya could feel it the way she felt weather in her bad knee — a pressure, a pulling, the world's skin drawn taut over something that pressed back.

Behind her, Seren made a low sound in her throat.

"It's real," Seren said. "Gods. All those years reciting the litany of the Sundering, burning effigies, singing about how the dead gods rotted because they deserved it — and it's *real*, and they were holding the door shut with their own—"

"Seren." Freya turned. "Breathe."

Seren Ashvale had lost her armor somewhere above the treeline, along with most of her left sleeve and, Freya suspected, whatever remained of her faith in anything except the woman standing in front of her. There was ash in her cropped hair. Her hands were still trembling from the gate — from holding it, from what it had cost — and there was a burn scar wrapping her forearm like a dark bracelet where Veilspire's collapsing wards had licked her as they fled. But she was alive. That was the miracle Freya kept having to relearn. They were all, against every arithmetic, alive.

Mostly.

Odessa Vane sat propped against the base of the stone with her back to the wind, one hand pressed flat over the wound in her side, and even now — even gray-lipped, even with the smuggler queen of the Thornfells reduced to a woman breathing in careful, rationed sips — she managed to look like she owned the mountain. "Don't fuss," she said, before Freya could speak. "I've been stabbed worse by better people."

"You haven't."

"I've been stabbed *comparably*." Her grin cost her something; Freya watched the price of it move across her face. "How long until the moon clears the ridge?"

"An hour. Maybe less." Freya knelt beside her, unwrapped the field dressing, and did not let herself flinch at what lay beneath. The Order's blade had gone deep under the ribs. The charm Freya had carved for her on the road up — rowan wood and wolf-bone, quick work, ugly work — was doing what charms could do, which was buy time and nothing else. Time was the only currency Freya had ever truly dealt in. Tonight she intended to spend all of it.

"Then we'd better get you comfortable," Freya said. "And I'd better get to work."

* * *

She carved by the light of the wrong-colored moon, seated cross-legged at the stone's foot with her kit open before her and the two halves of the covenant tablet laid out on cloth like relics — which was, she supposed, exactly what they were.

The tablet had been broken a century ago into two halves so that no single hand could renew the rite alone. The half Tam had died carrying was dense with the language of offering: *a name given freely, a self surrendered wholly.* The half recovered from Veilspire's deepest vault held the counter-verse, the part the Order had spent a hundred years pretending didn't exist: *and the burden shall be borne by many, lest one soul be ground to nothing.*

Freya read both halves until the moonlight swam. Then she read them again.

"That's not how they told it," she said quietly. Not to anyone. To herself, mostly. To the habit of a lifetime.

"Nobody tells it right." The voice came from the lantern.

They had salvaged the lantern from the wreck of the gate — or rather, Seren had gone back through fire and falling stone for it, because none of them could bear otherwise. The glass was crazed with cracks, the brass scorched, and inside it the flame burned small and stubborn and faintly blue. Tam. What remained of Tam. When he spoke, his voice came thin and doubled, like a man talking down a long stairwell.

"I carried my half for sixty years and I never once read the whole thing. You know why?" A flicker, wry even now. "Because I was afraid of what it asked for. And then I got to be dead, and being dead taught me the punchline. It doesn't want your soul, carver. Souls are cheap. The veil doesn't run on souls." The flame leaned toward her. "It runs on *belonging*. Always has. That's why the gods did it together. Nine of them, hand in hand. One alone would've been eaten in a heartbeat."

Freya's knife stilled over the blank bone blank she'd chosen — a shoulder blade from an old ox, wide as two spread hands, smooth as held breath.

"The rite says surrender yourself wholly," she said.

"The rite was written by frightened people translating frightened gods," said Tam. "You've rewritten worse scripture than that. I've seen your workshop."

* * *

She worked while the moon climbed, and the others kept watch around her without being asked, because that was the thing about found family — nobody assigned the watches anymore, they simply happened, the way breath happens.

Seren stood at the summit's edge with her sword drawn and her ruined cloak snapping, watching the passes below, where the Hollow Order's signal fires had begun to bloom like red flowers. They would come. Of course they would come. High Warden Maren Voss did not forgive, and did not forget, and had learned tonight that her runaway knight and her escaped vessel were climbing toward the one thing the Order existed to prevent. Let them climb, Seren had said. I've buried enough of my past this month. I can bury a few more.

Odessa dozed against the stone and woke every few minutes to check her own pulse with a smuggler's unsentimental fingers, and between checks she talked — low, rambling, funny — telling Freya about her drowned brother Callum, about the charm that had let him speak once more before the end, about how he'd sounded *exactly* as annoying as he'd been at fifteen. "He forgave me," she'd said, in the middle of a story about stolen rum. "Can you believe that? I never forgave me, and he forgave me in four sentences." Then she'd slept again, one hand curled around the little bone charm at her throat like a talisman, which, Freya thought, was precisely what it was.

And Freya carved.

She carved away from the old rite the way a river carves away from a mountain — patiently, inevitably, grain by grain. Where the tablet demanded *one who surrenders all*, she cut *many who share*. Where it demanded *the self erased*, she cut *the name offered*. She carved the living into it — a woman with ash in her hair and a sword; a queen bleeding laughter into the dark. She carved the dead into it — a ghost in a lantern, sixty years faithful, finally going home. She carved room for a returned god, because Caerlum's voice had been silent since the gate, and silence from him frightened her more than the Order did, and if he was spending himself to hold the tear closed from the other side then he belonged in the covenant too, written in, bound in, *held*.

The bone took each line reluctantly and then completely, the way good bone always did, drinking the knife-strokes until they looked like they'd always been there.

Near midnight the moon cleared the ridge, and the light fell on the summit like a held note, and everything went quiet at once — the wind, the watchfires below, the ringing in Freya's ears that had lived there since the first night a widow's dead son had spoken through her carving.

The stone began to sing.

Not loudly. A hum in the veins of it, in the channels where godsblood had run, a sound felt in teeth and sternum more than heard. The torn places in the world pulled tight around the summit like fabric gathering toward a seam. And out of the hum rose the voice Freya knew better than her own heartbeat.

*Priestess.*

"I'm not your priestess," Freya said. It wasn't defiance anymore. It was just true, and she'd finally earned the right to say it.

*No,* the god agreed, warm as winter sun on snow. *You are something I did not dare hope for. You are the rewrite.*

She stood. Her legs shook. She walked to the stone anyway, carrying the carved scapula in both hands, and Seren fell in at her left shoulder and Odessa — white-faced, upright, refusing all help — at her right, and Tam's lantern floated behind them because ghosts, it turned out, could be carried forward by love even when they couldn't walk.

At the stone's heart the channels converged in a shallow basin, and in the basin lay a groove shaped exactly like a name waiting to be spoken into it.

"Old rite," Freya said, loud enough for all of them, for the mountain, for the hungry thing pressing at the veil, for the god inside her head. "One gives everything and is unmade. So here is the new one, and I'm speaking it whether the stone likes it or not."

She set the scapula in the basin. It fit as if it had been measured for it.

"We give a name. Only a name. Mine — *Freya Whitlock, daughter of Greyhollow, carver of quiet things* — freely offered, freely released. And in exchange—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding grief had built the walls and sharing it was how they'd come down— "in exchange, these four carry the keeping of it. The knight who chose us. The queen who bled for us. The ghost who waited sixty years to matter. The god who came home. Not one soul ground to nothing. Five hands on the door. Take it or leave it."

The moon hung full and terrible overhead. The humming deepened. For three long heartbeats the whole summit held its breath —

— and then the basin flared silver, and the covenant poured itself into the new shape like water finding a channel, and Freya Whitlock gave away her name the way you give away bread: gladly, knowing it will be eaten, knowing it will keep someone alive.

It didn't hurt. That was the astonishment of it. It felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing. It felt like the moment after a fever breaks. The name lifted out of her gently — *Freya* — and went into the stone, into the seam, into the five-handed keeping of the door, and the veil drew closed across the sky with a sound like the longest-held note in the world finally resolving home.

When it was done, the moon was only a moon. White, and ordinary, and kind.

Seren caught her as her knees folded. Odessa laughed — a real laugh, reckless, agonized, delighted. The lantern-flame leapt and spun and shouted something joyful and unintelligible. And the woman who had been Freya Whitlock opened eyes that were wet and clear and empty of nothing at all, because she had not been erased; she had been *shared*.

She smiled at them — at her family, her five-fold covenant, her keeping.

"Well," said the woman with no name. "Someone should probably tell me who I am."

Far below, in the passes, the first grey light of morning touched the road toward Greyhollow — and on that road, small and patient, a rider in Hollow Order white had stopped, dismounted, and was looking up at the summit, at the place where the thirteenth moon had come and gone without ending the world.

High Warden Maren Voss began, very slowly, to climb.
