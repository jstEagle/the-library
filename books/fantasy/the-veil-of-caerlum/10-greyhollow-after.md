## 10. Greyhollow, After

Seren Ashvale woke before the bells, the way she always did, and lay in the narrow bed above the chandlery listening to Greyhollow stir. Gulls argued over the fish market's leavings. Somewhere below, a shutter banged and a woman cursed it fondly. The harbor smelled of brine and tar and woodsmoke, and beneath all of it — faint as a thread pulled through cloth — the sea-god Marenne was singing to the tide.

She could hear him now. That was the thing that still undid her, some mornings, lying there with the ceiling beams crossing overhead like the rafters of a church no one had built: the gods were audible again. Not the thunder-and-terror of that last night at Veilspire, not Caerlum's voice cracking the sky like river ice. Just this. A low singing under the world, patient and old, like a mother humming while she works.

A century of silence. And now this.

Seren dressed in the grey wool she'd taken to wearing — no more white, never white again — and went down into the street where the morning fog was burning off in gold ribbons. The town had changed. Not its bones; smuggler towns keep their bones. But people spoke differently now. They prayed openly, carelessly, the way you greet a neighbor across a fence. A fisherwoman stood knee-deep in her nets murmuring thanks to the river god for a good catch. Two children had chalked a crude spiral on the cobbles — the sign of the covenant, remade — and were arguing over which of them it belonged to.

It belongs to all of you, Seren thought. That was the whole point. That was what Freya—

That was what *she* had understood, standing on the covenant stone with the thirteenth moon bleeding light down over them all. Seren had been there. She had held Odessa's hand and watched a woman give away her name the way you'd hand someone bread — freely, gladly, as if identity were never meant to be hoarded in the first place.

The Order was gone. Not destroyed — Seren doubted anything built on fear was ever truly destroyed — but broken open, its archives unsealed, its citadel surrendered to the crown of Thornfell and then, slowly, to pilgrims. She had walked through its halls one last time in the spring and felt nothing but the cold of stone. Her mentor's face still came to her sometimes, in the hour before waking, offering absolution like a coin he expected her to bite. She let the face come. She let it go. That, too, was a kind of prayer now.

At the corner of Rope-Walk and Tide Street there was a small shop with a carved lintel: bone spirals and moon-thirteen, and beneath them a single word painted fresh every season by whoever loved the place enough to do it. *Whitlock.* The name stayed. Names, it turned out, were things a family could carry for you.

Inside, apprentices worked at long benches — orphans mostly, and runaways, and one reformed acolyte who flinched at loud noises but had steady, gifted hands. Seren taught them footwork three mornings a week. Defense of the defenseless; how to stand between a frightened person and a frightened mob. It was not redemption. She had learned there was no such thing, only the next right thing, done over and over until the doing became who you were.

Today, though, she wasn't teaching. Today she climbed the cliff path above the harbor with a bundle of white flowers cut from Odessa's garden — Odessa grew flowers now, which still made Seren laugh if she thought about it too long — and she sat awhile on the flat stone where the covenant had been renewed, and she said nothing, because there was nothing left unsaid.

The wind came up off the water carrying Marenne's song, and Seren found she was smiling.

* * *

Odessa Vane convalesced badly, by all accounts, including her own.

"The scar," she announced from her chaise in the great room of the Vane house, "is magnificent, and I will hear no arguments. It runs from my collarbone to my hip like a bolt of lightning deciding I was worth striking." She lifted her glass — wine, though the physiker forbade it, though everyone forbade it — toward the window, where autumn light lay across the harbor in long warm bands. "Veilspire's price, paid in full. I intend to spend the rest of my life being insufferable about it."

"You nearly died," said Marta, her quartermaster, not looking up from the ledgers.

"I *did* die, briefly, and let me tell you the paperwork on the other side is nonexistent. Very disappointing. No ledgers at all." But her voice caught on something, and she covered it with the glass.

The truth was quieter than her jokes. Some mornings the wound ached so deeply she couldn't rise, and she lay staring at the ceiling beams thinking about a lantern shattering on Veilspire's stones, about a ghost scattering into a hundred bright fragments of light, about the moment she had understood — with a clarity that hurt worse than any blade — that she had loved him. Not as cargo. Not as debt. Tam, who had haunted her ship and her heart for six years, gone in a rain of glass and moonlight, and his last look had been at *her*, and it had been grateful.

He came back. That was the miracle nobody in Greyhollow quite stopped marveling at. When the covenant was rewoven — living hands and dead hands and a god's own voice braided together — the veil hadn't merely sealed. It had softened. The dead could return now, gently, briefly, the way light returns after an eclipse: not to stay, but to visit. Tam arrived on the first night of every new moon, coalescing out of the ship-lantern's flame — a new lantern now, iron-bound, that Odessa carried everywhere and pretended was merely sentimental.

"Evening, captain," he'd say, flickering green at the edges, translucent as sea-glass.

"Vane," she'd correct. "Captain Vane. I earned the whole name."

"Course you did." And they'd talk until dawn, and he'd fade out with the tide, and she would sit alone in the big empty house and find, to her ongoing fury, that she was weeping and laughing at once — grief and gift tangled past any hope of separating.

Because that was the shape of it now. Everything cost, and everything was given. The dead visited and departed. The gods answered prayers, but softly, the way you answer a child — never taking the work from your hands, only warming them while you do it.

Her brother came too, sometimes. Drowned forty years and forgiven thirty of those. He sat with her in the garden among the ridiculous flowers and told her the sea kept no grudges, only memories, and that their mother's lullaby was still sung somewhere in the deep places, and that he was sorry, and that she was allowed to be sorry too.

Odessa Vane, smuggler queen, terror of three coastlines, learned to say *I miss you* out loud. She practiced it on the flowers first. Then on Marta. Then, one impossible night, on the flickering green shape of a man who had died holding half a tablet of bone and half her heart.

"I miss you," she said, and the lantern burned brighter, just for a moment, like a held breath released.

* * *

The apprentice named Freya was nine years old when the voices began, and ten now, and she had never once been afraid of them.

They came to her the way birds come to a feeder — because she had made herself a quiet, kind place, and word got around. The old woman who'd frozen in the winter of the hard year, wanting someone to know she'd been happy anyway. The drowned boy from the *Kestrel*, homesick for the sound of his sister laughing. A soldier so old his war had passed out of living memory, who only wanted to hear what bread smelled like now.

Mistress Seren said the girl's gift was rare even in the old days — not carving charms, not sealing doors, just *listening*. "The veil didn't only need guarding," Seren told her once, kneeling to the girl's height in the workshop that still smelled of bone dust and beeswax. "It needed tending. Someone on both sides who wanted to hear. You're the hearing kind."

The girl took the name seriously. Everyone knew whose name it was — there was a statue in the market square now, a woman with a carver's knife and her head tipped back toward the thirteenth moon, and children left bone spirals and wildflowers at its feet. The girl visited it sometimes and talked to it the way she talked to the dead, easily, without ceremony.

"I don't know why they gave me your name," she confessed one evening, sitting cross-legged at the statue's base while gulls wheeled overhead. "I'm not brave like you. I just listen."

And the wind moved through the square, and the god Caerlum — who answered gently now, who had learned gentleness the hard way, from a priestess who refused to be consumed — said, in the space between one wave and the next:

*Listening is the bravest thing there is.*

The girl grinned. "You always say that."

*It is always true.*

She ran home along the harbor road with her satchel bumping, past the chandlery where Mistress Seren was locking up, past the Vane house where a green light sometimes glowed in an upstairs window on new-moon nights, past the shop with the carved lintel where the older apprentices were lighting lamps against the dusk. Behind her the town settled into evening, lamplit and salt-smelling and safe, the way a town is safe when its dead are neighbors and its gods are kind.

She heard only kind voices. She intended, with the whole fierce seriousness of a ten-year-old, to keep it that way.

* * *

On the mountain road above Thornfell, where the passes opened onto country no map in Greyhollow bothered to draw, a woman walked south with the frost silvering the gorse and the sun coming up behind her like a promise kept.

She wore a carver's coat with pockets full of bone blanks, and boots good for another thousand miles, and no name at all. When travelers met her on the road they found themselves telling her things — griefs, hopes, the small unbearable weights people carry — and she listened the way spring listens to ice, and something in them loosened. In the villages she passed through, charms appeared on doorposts overnight, delicate spirals that quieted nightmares and eased dying and called back, gently, the faces of the beloved dead. No one saw her carve them. No one ever quite caught her face. By morning she was gone, and the town was warmer, somehow, and stayed that way.

The gods knew her. When she slept beneath hedges, Marenne sang her to sleep with the tide-song. When she crossed high bare fells, Caerlum walked beside her as a wind with weight and warmth, and neither of them needed words. She had given her name into the covenant and the covenant had given her back to the world — not erased, not consumed, but *shared*: held now by a knight who taught children to stand their ground, a queen who kept a lantern lit, a listening girl in a borrowed name, and a god who had finally learned what she'd tried to teach him.

Belonging, she had written once, in a life that felt like a story someone else had lived, means letting others carry you.

She tested it every day. It held.

Near midday she crested a ridge and saw, in the valley below, a town she'd never visited — smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, a river running bright through it, and on the bridge a crowd gathered around something she couldn't yet see, though she could already hear the singing.

The woman with no name smiled, shifted her pack, and started down toward them, her boots finding the path as surely as if the path had been waiting for her all along.
