## 1. The Night Without Stars

The trick to selling magic you don't have is confidence, and the second trick is never touching the merchandise twice.

Sable Okafor had both tricks down cold. He worked the evening market of Cinderhold with a lacquered tray slung from his neck, six rows of charms catching the lamplight — knotted cords, river-stones wrapped in copper, little glass vials of what was, if anyone pressed him, mostly rainwater and optimism. Love-luck. Safe-passage. A fever-breaking knot his gran would've laughed at. Five coppers apiece, or three for a family.

"Protection charm?" he said to a woman hauling a basket of eels past his pitch. "Wards off bad dreams, keeps the night honest."

She didn't stop. Nobody ever stopped for Sable. He had his mother's dark skin and her long fingers but none of her gravity; she'd been the kind of woman who made rooms reorganize themselves around her, and he was the kind who got shooed out of them. Maerin Okafor, cartographer, gone eleven years now, leaving him nothing but a locked map-case he couldn't open and a talent for forgery he'd taught himself out of hunger.

He was mid-spiel with a nervous apprentice cooper — *yes it's real, no it doesn't need blood, that's a different school entirely* — when the case went warm against his hip.

Sable stopped talking.

The map-case hung under his coat on a leather strap, where it had hung every day since he was fourteen, because he was superstitious about exactly one thing in his life. Iron-banded, triple-locked, small as a bread loaf. It had never done anything. It had never even gotten damp, which in Cinderhold was its own miracle. And now it sat against his ribs like a sleeping cat that had decided to wake up.

"—so does it work on headaches too, or just—" The cooper's apprentice was still going.

"Just headaches," Sable said absently, and put a hand flat over the case.

Warmth spread through the iron bands, then heat, then something finer than heat — a pulse, rhythmic, patient. Like something under the locks was breathing. His mouth went dry. Eleven years of nothing, and tonight, of all nights—

The first star went out while he was staring at his own hand.

It didn't flicker. It didn't dim. It simply stopped being there, a hole punched clean out of the sky above the tanners' quarter. Sable had time to think *that's odd* and then a second star vanished, and a third, and then the whole eastern sky went out like a snuffed candle-row, darkness rolling westward across the heavens faster than any cloud, swallowing stars whole.

The market noticed all at once. Conversation died in patches, like rain starting in reverse. Somewhere a pot clattered. The lamplighter stood on his ladder with his pole half-raised, looking up as if the sky had personally insulted him.

"What is that," the cooper's apprentice whispered.

"I don't know," Sable said, and hated how honest it sounded.

Against his hip, the case pulsed again — harder now, urgent, and beneath his palm he could have sworn he felt the locks shifting, testing themselves, the way a horse tests a gate.

Then the border wards broke.

Everyone in Cinderhold felt it. You grew up feeling the wards the way you felt the ground under your feet — a faint pressure at the edges of things, a sense that the world ended politely where it was supposed to. When they shattered it was like a door slamming somewhere inside his own chest. Every dog in the city began howling at once. The lamps guttered though there was no wind. And down at the south gate, where the road came in from the marsh country, torches flared to life in a panic of motion — gates swinging open, horns blowing, Wardens in their grey coats streaming toward walls that suddenly needed defending for the first time in living memory.

"Get home," someone shouted. "Curfew! Warden curfew!"

Nobody moved. Everybody was looking up.

The last star in the western sky held alone for a breath — one stubborn ember over the harbor — and then it, too, went out, and Cinderhold stood under a sky with nothing in it at all. No moon. No stars. Just black, total and close, pressing down on the city like a lid.

Sable's fake charms were warm.

All of them. Every cord, every stone, every vial of rainwater on his tray had gone gently, unmistakably warm, glowing with a faint amber light like coals under ash. The cooper's apprentice was staring at the love-luck knot in his own fist, which was shining.

"You said this wasn't real," the boy said.

"It isn't," Sable heard himself say. "I mean — it wasn't. I made it myself. There's nothing in it. There's never been anything in—"

The case *knocked*.

One sharp rap from inside the iron bands, deliberate as a fist on a door, and Sable's whole body went cold despite the heat pouring off his tray. He knew that knock. Not the sound of it — the *manner* of it. Impatient, precise, three beats when two would do. His mother used to knock on doors like that. On tables. On the top of his head, when he wasn't listening.

"Maerin," he said, very quietly, to a dead woman. "Maerin, that isn't funny."

The market was coming apart around him. People were running now, real running, trays abandoned, a fruit cart overturned and bleeding oranges across the cobbles. Down by the south gate the first refugees were coming through — and Sable saw them because he was frozen at his pitch while everyone else fled, saw them stagger into the torchlight: mud to their thighs, faces wrong, some of them weeping without any expression at all, like people who had cried so long the crying had become mechanical.

A soldier was half-carrying an old man between the stalls. "They came out of the ground," the old man kept saying, to no one, to everyone. "Out of the ground where we buried them. It comes walking and where it walks, you forget. My wife — I know I had a wife — I can't remember her *face*, boy, I can't remember her *name*—"

"It eats memories," the soldier said hoarsely, to the crowd, to the night. "The dark out past the wards. It eats memories and names and it doesn't stop walking. Get inside. Bar your doors. Don't look at it."

Sable stood in the emptying market with a tray of glowing fakes and a knocking coffin of a case, and felt something terrible settle over him that wasn't quite fear.

It was recognition.

Because here was the thing he'd never told anyone, the thing he barely admitted to himself at four in the morning: sometimes his fakes *worked*. Small things. A knot that kept a headache away longer than chance should allow. A passage-stone that seemed to keep pickpockets oddly uninterested. He'd told himself it was luck, coincidence, the power of suggestion. But standing under a starless sky with his counterfeit magic lit up like festival lanterns, Sable finally understood what his mother must have known when she left him the case and never explained it:

The magic hadn't been missing from his charms. It had been waiting. Sleeping, maybe — folded down small, hiding the way everything hid in the years after the Purges, after the Wardens outlawed the old arts and burned the practitioners' names from the rolls. Hiding in copper wire and river stones and a boy who didn't know what he was carrying.

And now something had called it awake. Or something had broken whatever was holding it asleep.

The case knocked again. Twice. Insistent.

Around him, Cinderhold screamed and ran and barred its doors against a dark that ate names. Above him, the sky was a blank page. And against his ribs, his mother's last gift knocked a third time — patient now, almost gentle, the way she used to knock when she wanted him to open up and let her in.

Sable looked down at his tray of shining lies, at the only magic he'd ever sold honestly described as worthless.

"Right," he said to nobody, to the dark, to her. His hands were shaking. He set the tray down on an overturned crate, charms still glowing, and drew the map-case out from under his coat into the open air for the first time in eleven years.

The iron was hot as a hearthstone. The three locks clicked — one, two, three — before his fingers even found them.

Inside, on folded cloth older than the city: ink. Moving ink. And a voice like his mother's, thin as thread through the keyhole, saying words he'd spend the rest of his life trying to understand.

"*Sable. Open the map. Highmoor is waking.*"

* * *

He didn't open it in the market. Some instinct older than thought told him not to — that whatever was in the case shouldn't be unfolded under torchlight with Warden horns still blaring and refugees babbling about hungry dark. So he ran, clutching the case to his chest, taking the back ways he knew from years of dodging toll-collectors, down Tanner's Stair and along the drainage cut, until he crouched breathless under the arch of the old ferry bridge where the drowned quarter began.

Here the water was black and still, mirroring a sky with nothing to mirror. Sable knelt on wet stone and laid the case on his knees.

The locks were already open. Of course they were.

He lifted the lid.

Cloth, yellowed, embroidered at the corners with a sigil he'd never let himself study closely — a circle crossed by a line, the mark that got people hanged in the Purge years. Inside the cloth lay a map. Not paper. Something between vellum and skin, and *alive*: rivers sliding across it like slow silver worms, streets of a city rearranging themselves, borders drawn and redrawn by an invisible pen. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and it was whispering.

Not in words, at first. In the rhythm of a voice he'd spent eleven years forgetting and could never actually forget — the cadence of Maerin Okafor reading aloud late at night, pen scratching, humming to herself. The map hummed his mother's hum.

"Oh no," Sable said softly. "Oh, you're real. You've been real this whole time."

The ink gathered itself along the map's edge and formed letters, quick and sure, in handwriting he knew better than his own:

**NOT HIGHMOOR. THE HEART UNDER IT. AND IT HAS NOT BEEN GUARDED FOR ELEVEN YEARS, LITTLE SPARROW.**

Little sparrow. Her name for him. His eyes stung, and he laughed, a cracked, awful sound, because grief and terror had apparently decided to arrive together.

"Guarded from what? The dark they're talking about?"

The ink swirled, erased itself, wrote again:

**FROM WHAT WAS NEVER BURIED DEEP ENOUGH. LISTEN. THERE IS NO TIME.**

Downstream, torchlight bloomed — a patrol, grey coats, Warden lanterns sweeping the water. Voices: *"Charm-sellers were seen near the market—"* and *"the Curator wants every peddler of false sigils brought in tonight, tonight, do you hear me—"*

Sable looked at the map, at his mother's handwriting crawling across living skin, at the Warden lights closing on the only thing in the world he had left of her.

The map wrote one line more, fast, the ink almost tearing:

**RUN, SABLE.**

Boots on stone behind him. Lantern-glow licking the archway. He wrapped the map against his chest, case and all, and did the only thing his legs remembered how to do.

He ran.
