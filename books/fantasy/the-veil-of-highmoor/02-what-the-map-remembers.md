## 2. What the Map Remembers

The case was warm when Sable finally touched it, and that was wrong, because the case had been cold for six years.

He'd carried it down from the loft of his mother's shop — his shop, now, though he still thought of it as hers, the way he still thought of her as merely away — and set it on the counter between a tray of love-charms (dyed river stones) and a jar of fever-warding salts (salt). The lock had never yielded to him. Not to picks, not to pry bars, not to the one memorable afternoon he'd tried to burn it with lamp oil and succeeded only in scorching his own eyebrows. His mother had locked it three days before she died and told him, with that half-smile she used for things she wasn't going to explain, *"When it wants you, it'll open."*

He'd assumed she meant the lockbox where she kept the good silver.

Now the whole sky was black above Cinderhold, no stars, no moon, just a low bruised ceiling of nothing, and the case lay under his palm warm as a sleeping animal. Warm like skin. And when he pressed his thumb against the lock, it didn't resist at all. It opened the way a hand opens when someone takes it.

Inside, folded into layers of oilcloth gone soft with age, was a map.

Sable had seen maps all his life. He'd grown up over a cartographer's shop; he could draw the Cinderhold docks from memory, could name every drowned street of the old quarter. This was a map of Highmoor — the whole of it, city and borderlands, rendered in ink so fine it looked stitched rather than drawn. But it was moving.

Not flickering. Not an illusion, the way his own wares were illusions. The map was *redrawing itself*. Rivers shifted their courses by a hair's width and settled. A road near the eastern wards unraveled thread by thread and re-stitched itself along a new line. Tiny letters surfaced in the margins like fish rising to feed, held for a moment, sank again.

And beneath the rustle of it, faint as breath through a wall, a voice.

*"—the northern boundary is wrong, it's always been wrong, they drew it from fear instead of—"*

Sable dropped the corner of the oilcloth. The voice cut off. The rivers stilled.

"No," he said out loud, to the empty shop, to the starless window. "No, no."

But his hands went back to the cloth, because his mother's voice had been dead six years and grief is not a rational creature. He lifted the edge again.

*"—if you're hearing this, then it's already begun. Don't go to the Wardens. Whatever they tell you, whatever they promise—"* The voice frayed, looped, began again from a different place, as if the map were less a recording than a woman pacing a room and talking to herself: *"—the heart remembers what the surface forgets. That's the first law. Sable. Sable, if this is you, the compass is under the floorboards, third nail is loose, and you must not let them bind you before you understand what binding means—"*

The voice stopped. All of it stopped. The ink went still, and Sable realized he was crying, which was inconvenient, because tears and old ink are natural enemies, and he snatched the map up and away from his own face with a care that surprised him.

Third nail. Loose floorboard. Of course. She'd hidden things under those boards his entire childhood and he'd never once thought to look, because looking felt like admitting she was really gone.

He knelt behind the counter, worked the nail free with his thumbnail, and lifted out a brass instrument no bigger than a pocket watch: a compass, except the needle wasn't pointing north. It was pointing at the counter. At the map. At him.

The needle swung once, twice, and settled on his chest like a small patient finger.

Somewhere out in the market square, a whistle blew. Then another. Then the deep bronze voice of a Warden horn, and the sound of boots — many boots — and someone screaming *run.*

* * *

The raid came down Tanners' Row like weather.

Sable had survived raids before by being small-time: hide the charms, light a candle, look bored and honest while grey-cloaked Wardens turned over your stock and found nothing but rocks and salt. But tonight there were lanterns lit against the starless dark, and lanterns mean shadows, and shadows mean the Wardens were searching faces, not shelves. He heard a charm-seller two doors down — old Pemmy, who sold luck-knots — shouting that she had a license, she had papers, and then he heard the sound that meant the papers hadn't mattered.

He wrapped the map around the compass without letting himself think about it, shoved both inside his coat, and went out the back.

The alley behind the row emptied onto the canal stairs, and the canal stairs emptied onto the water, and on the water, moored against the rotted pilings, sat a flat-bottomed ferry with a single lantern hung at the prow and a single figure standing in it like the lantern's keeper.

She was broad as a door, wrapped in an oiled coat the color of wet slate, and she had one eye — the left socket sunken and seamed with old scar tissue. Her remaining eye found Sable in the dark the way herons find fish.

"You're the charm-boy," she said. "Okafor's whelp."

"I'm a licensed vendor of curiosities."

"Sure." She jerked her chin at the alley behind him, where the boots were getting louder. "And I'm the Duchess of Vell. In or out, boy. Boat leaves when the first Warden reaches the stairs, and not one breath after."

Everything in Sable said don't — don't get in boats with strangers, especially strangers who know your mother's name, especially tonight. But the map against his ribs had gone warm again, warmer, almost urgent, and the compass needle through the fabric was pressing against him like something trying to point harder. And the boots reached the top of the stairs.

He got in.

The ferry slipped away from the pilings without any visible push, sliding into the current of the drowned quarter, where the canals widened and the buildings leaned together overhead until the lantern-light barely touched the water. Behind them, horns. Ahead of them, black water and the smell of rot and river-mint.

"Ferrywoman's called Brannagh," she said, poling with a long oar that made no splash. "You'll want to remember that, since you're about to owe me."

"Am I?"

"Passage out of a raid costs coin. Passage during the Night Without Stars costs more." She looked back at him, one eye catching the lantern. "Where are we going, charm-boy? Because you climbed into my boat like a man who knows where he's going, and I've poled this water thirty years, and I can smell a man being steered."

"I'm not—" Sable started, and stopped, because the map chose that moment to whisper.

It was quiet enough in the boat, quiet enough in the world, with the stars dead and the city holding its breath, that he heard it clearly. His mother's voice, muffled through wool and coat-lining:

*"—south channel, past the bell tower, the water remembers the old crossing—"*

Brannagh's oar stopped mid-stroke.

For three full seconds neither of them moved. Then she leaned forward, slow, and pulled the lapel of his coat aside with two fingers, careful as a surgeon, and looked down at the oilcloth bundle against his ribs. The ink had begun to glow faintly through the wrapping — or perhaps it wasn't glow, perhaps it was simply that everything else had gotten darker since the stars died.

"Well," Brannagh said softly. "Six years I've wondered what happened to her kit."

Sable's blood went cold. "You knew my mother."

"Everyone on the water knew Ishikawa." She resumed poling, but her eye never left him. "Knew her better than most. Knew what she ran from, too, and what she was running *as*, if you take my meaning." The ferry slid under a collapsed archway, past a bell tower rising out of the flooded street, its bell green with forty years of rot. "So. Either you stole the Cartographer's case off a dead woman's shelf — in which case I drown you here and feed the canal, no offense — or it opened for you. Which did?"

"It opened for me," Sable said, before he could decide whether lying would be safer. It probably wouldn't have been. Something about the woman's one eye made lies feel like throwing pebbles at a mountain.

"And it talks."

"It... whispers. Fragments. My mother's voice."

Brannagh nodded slowly, and there was something in her face that Sable couldn't read — not surprise, exactly. Recognition wearing surprise's clothes. "That's how it starts," she said. "It learns your ear first. Then your hand. Then your blood." She spat over the side, an old habit, or a warding gesture, or both. "My sister had a mark come in on her palms once. Lines like a chart. She said it tickled."

"Once?"

"Once," Brannagh said, and poled them around a bend where the canal split three ways, and the lantern showed nothing ahead but more black water. "There's a crossing south of here the maps stopped drawing twenty years ago. The water still goes there. Things that get erased leave holes, boy, and holes hold their shape." Her voice was level, but her knuckles were white on the oar. "Your mother's map will know the way. It remembers everything they unwrote."

The map whispered again, and this time Sable didn't flinch, and that frightened him more than anything else that night — how quickly he was getting used to it. How easily the voice fit into the silence, like a key into a lock that had been waiting.

*"—ask the ferrywoman about the erased crossing, she'll pretend not to know, she always pretended—"*

Brannagh's head came around. "What did it say."

"Nothing," said Sable Okafor, liar, counterfeit-charm merchant, son of a woman who apparently spoke to maps from beyond wherever dead cartographers go.

The ferrywoman smiled with only half her mouth, and poled on into the dark.
