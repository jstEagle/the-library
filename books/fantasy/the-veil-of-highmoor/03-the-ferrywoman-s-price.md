## 3. The Ferrywoman's Price

Brannagh had ferried liars across the Drowned Quarter for eleven years, and she had learned to weigh them the way she weighed cargo: by how much water they displaced.

The boy with the map-case sat in her boat like he was afraid of it. Good. Men who trusted boats were usually men who hadn't drowned yet. He kept one hand pressed flat against the case's lid as if it might open without him — and knowing what lived inside that leather, Brannagh thought it might.

"Rule one," she said, pushing off from the stair where Cinderhold's cobbles gave up and became black water. "You don't touch anything under the surface. Rule two, you don't sing. Rule three—"

"You charge double for questions," Sable said.

She almost smiled. "So you've been told."

"By a man missing an ear. He wouldn't say why."

"The singing." She shipped the oar and let the current take them. The Drowned Quarter was old Highmoor, the part the city had swallowed rather than mourned — rooftops just beneath the skin of the water, weathervanes standing up out of the flood like drowned men reaching for weather that would never come again. On clear nights you could see the whole street grid down there, lamplit by the pale glow of the wards' corpse-light. Tonight there were no stars, no glow, nothing but the lantern at Brannagh's prow and the boy's face trying not to look down.

He looked down anyway. Everyone did.

"So," she said. "Charm-seller."

"That's me."

"Selling fakes."

His jaw tightened. "They work about as well as most magic does these days."

"Cute." She turned the oar lazily, keeping them mid-channel. "Here's my problem, charm-seller. Last night every star went out. This morning the border wards fell like a drunk off a wall, and something came through the gap that eats memories. And today, of all days, a Warden raid hits the charm market — which they haven't bothered with in years — specifically the stall where a boy was seen carrying his dead mother's map-case. Wardens don't raid for fakes. Wardens raid for *that*." She nodded at the case. "So either you're bait somebody set swimming, or you're exactly what I think you are, and both options end with me feeding fish."

"I'm not bait."

"Everyone's bait, love. The only question is whose hook."

* * *

She tested him the way she tested everything: quietly, and while he wasn't looking.

First, the route. Instead of taking the straight channel toward the Ember Stair, she took the turn past the Old Customhouse, where the water ran shallow over a sunken bell tower. If he were a Warden plant, he'd flinch — Wardens hated the drowned places, said they smelled of the old war.

He didn't flinch. He leaned over the side instead, and she watched his reflection in the black water watch him back, and she watched something in the case against his chest pulse once, slow, like a heartbeat under a coat.

Second, the question. "Your mother," Brannagh said. "What did she do?"

"She drew maps." His voice went careful, the way voices go around graves. "Survey maps. Trade maps. She worked for the city, before—" He stopped. "Before they decided she'd drawn something wrong."

"They didn't decide she drew something wrong," Brannagh said. "They decided she drew something *true*. There's a difference, and it got your mother killed and my sister unmade, so mind it."

That landed. She saw it land — saw him go still the way people do when a stranger says a name they thought was buried. "Unmade," he repeated.

"Keep rowing your mouth and maybe I'll tell you." She didn't intend to tell him. She intended to find out whether he already knew, because that was the real test, the third test, the one that mattered: what the map had already shown him, and what he'd done with the knowing.

Third, the hands. She called for the oar — "Take it, my shoulder's gone" — and when he reached across her, she caught his wrist and turned his palm up to the lantern light.

There it was.

Not ink. Not scar. A fine tracery across the lifeline and up between the fingers, like the veins of a leaf, like the lines of a chart no surveyor ever drew — moving, faintly, the way water moves when it wants somewhere to be. She'd seen those lines once before, on another pair of hands, younger and surer, holding a pen over a map that could rewrite the ground it described.

The Cartographer's mark. The office the Wardens had spent forty years insisting was extinct, because extinction was tidier than admitting what they'd done to stop it.

Sable didn't pull away. That surprised her. He just looked at his own hand like a man reading a letter addressed to someone else. "It showed up last night," he said quietly. "When the stars went. I thought — I sell fake charms, ferrywoman. I know what pretending looks like. This isn't pretending."

"No," Brannagh said. "It isn't." She let go of his wrist and took back the oar, and for a long moment the only sound was water against hull and the far-off, directionless groaning of the city's broken wards.

"My sister held that mark," she said, because apparently eleven years of silence had been waiting for this boy all along. "Aoife. Court-trained, blood-sworn, the finest cartographer Highmoor ever produced and never thanked. When the Wardens outlawed the office, she refused to burn her maps. They came for her. And they couldn't kill her — killing a Cartographer is like tearing a page; the book remembers. So they did worse. They wrote her *out*. There's a rite for it. Erasure." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used for pricing funerals. "No grave. No record. No name in anyone's mouth. I watched them do it and then I woke up the next morning unable to remember what I was grieving. It took me four years to even learn I'd *had* a sister. Four years of a hole in me shaped like someone, with nothing to fill it."

Sable was very quiet. Then: "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She swung the boat hard around a submerged chimney stack. "Because here's what I know that you don't, charm-seller. That map in your case — it's not a map. It's the *office*. The living record of every boundary ever drawn, every veil ever woven. And the thing wearing its ink knows how to write things back in as surely as the Wardens knew how to write them out."

"You want me to redraw your sister."

"I want you to promise." She shipped the oar and turned to face him full, letting the lantern throw her shadow over him, letting him see the eye-patch and the scars and all the rest of the price list written on her skin. "I don't guide you out of charity. I don't guide you out of hope. Hope's for people with time. You're going to be hunted by Wardens within the day and courted by worse within the week, and every soul who helps you will want something, so let's have mine on the table first and cleanest: you get me across the Drowned Quarter alive, you get me wherever that map is going, and when you finally put pen to whatever it puts under your hand — you draw Aoife back. Name, bones, memory, all of it. Before you fix any star. Before you save any city. Her first."

"And if I can't?" he asked. "If it doesn't work? If writing a person back into the world costs more than the world will pay?"

"Then I'll have lost nothing I hadn't already lost." She picked up the oar again. "But you'll have lied to me, and I'll know, and I'm very patient, and the water here keeps secrets nobody asks it to keep."

* * *

They passed the Bell Tower shoal in silence. Under the hull, thirty feet down, the drowned bell hung in its flooded belfry, and as they crossed above it the water shivered — once — though there was no wind.

Sable's hand crept to the map-case. "It's doing that," he murmured. "Since we entered the quarter. Like it's... reading."

"It grew up down there," Brannagh said. "Everything true about Highmoor is drowning down here. That's rather the point."

"It whispered to me. Last night, after the raid." He said it like a confession. "In her voice. My mother's. Telling me which alleys were safe. And the awful thing is—" He stopped. Started again. "The awful thing is it was right every time. And I followed it. I didn't even hesitate. What kind of son follows a voice out of a burning market without hesitating?"

"The kind who misses his mother," Brannagh said. "And the kind the map chose. Both at once. Sit with it."

He sat with it. She watched him do it, and revised her weighing: heavier than he looked, this boy. Liars displaced more than their size, and he was lying about something — she could smell it on him the way sailors smell weather — but he was lying badly, guiltily, the way honest people lie, and that was a rare and useful flaw.

Ahead, the channel opened onto the Ember Stair: a half-submerged colonnade where the water glowed faintly warm, lit from below by fires that had been burning since before the flood. The door to underneath. The door to the Court that had exiled his mother and now wanted her son.

Two figures waited at the top of the stair, robed in ash-grey silk, faces hidden behind ember-lit masks. They had been waiting, Brannagh realized, since before she pushed off. Which meant someone had known his route before she'd chosen it.

Which meant the map was talking to more people than the boy.

"There's your welcome party," she said, low. "Last chance, Cartographer. Once you set foot on that stair, you're theirs until you give them a reason otherwise. So give them one. Give me one. Promise me Aoife — properly, on the mark in your hand — or I turn this boat around and you can swim to your destiny."

Sable looked at the stair. At the masked figures. At the case against his chest, pulsing now in time with the drowned bell below, quickening like a thing eager to come home.

Then he held out his right hand, palm up, the living lines catching the ember-glow, and said, "On the mark. Aoife first."

The lines moved. Brannagh would swear on any god still listening that the lines *moved* — settling into a new shape, a new oath, like a river accepting a bridge.

"Done," she said, and rowed for the stair, and tried not to think about how easily he'd promised, or how easily the map had accepted it, or what it meant that the office bound itself faster than any mortal could think.

At the top of the steps, the taller of the two masked figures inclined its head.

"Cartographer," it said, in a woman's voice, warm as banked coals. "Her Majesty has waited forty years to meet you. And she has such *stories* about your mother."
