## 4. Court of Ash and Embers

The ferry docked beneath the city the way a knife slides between ribs.

One moment Sable was on the black water of the drowned quarter, Brannagh poling them past rooftops that rose like drowned men's hands, and the next they were inside — a cavern lit by veins of ember-light running through the stone like something alive, like the city had swallowed a sunset and never digested it.

"Ember Court," Brannagh said, tying off. She didn't look at him. "Try not to sign anything."

"You know these people?"

"I know everyone, boy." She swung her pack over her shoulder. "That's my curse and my trade."

The tunnel opened into a hall that took his breath and held it hostage. Pillars of fused glass, melted long ago in some fire he could only imagine, arched overhead like frozen flames. Hundreds of people moved through it — not the hollow-eyed refugees he'd seen pouring into Cinderhold's gates, but people who *worked*. Forging. Weaving. Writing on walls with fingers that left trails of faint gold light.

Magic. Real magic, casual as breathing, done by people who looked like they'd been doing it since before the Wardens were born.

A girl no older than him walked past carrying a lantern that burned without oil, its flame bending toward her like a curious bird. She caught him staring and smiled.

"Don't gawk," Brannagh muttered. "It's rude, and it marks you as fresh."

"I'm not gawking. I'm cataloguing."

"Cataloguing what?"

"What I'm worth here." He kept his voice flat. "Because everyone down here is about to pretend it's me, and I'd like to know the truth before the lying starts."

Brannagh gave him a sideways look — the first real one she'd given him since the docks. "Maybe there's hope for you yet."

They were expected. Of course they were expected. A woman in ash-grey robes met them at the foot of the glass pillars with the practiced warmth of someone who greeted strangers for a living, and led them through corridors where the walls remembered heat, up a stair carved from a single piece of obsidian, into a chamber where a woman sat on a throne of cooled lava.

She was not what he expected. He'd imagined the Queen of the Ember Court as old, terrible, wreathed in literal flame. Instead: silver hair cropped short, a plain dark dress, hands folded in her lap like a scholar waiting for a student to finish talking. Only her eyes gave her away. They moved like fire moves — always toward whatever would burn best.

"Sable Okafor," she said. His name in her mouth sounded like an inheritance being read aloud. "Son of Adaeze Okafor. Cartographer of Highmoor."

"My mother sold thread," Sable said. "And before you start, I know what the map is. I've had it four days. I haven't agreed to give it to anyone, and I'm not going to be flattered into it either."

Something flickered across Vess's face. Not anger. Delight.

"Oh," she said softly, "you *are* hers."

* * *

They fed him first. That was the trick of it, he realized later — you couldn't distrust someone while eating their bread. The hall filled with courtiers who asked nothing about the map and everything about him: his mother's stall, his charms, whether he'd really once sold a luck-charm to a Warden sergeant (he had; it was a bead of painted glass; the sergeant had survived three raids and credited the bead). They laughed in the right places. They touched his shoulder like he belonged there.

And the worst part — the part he would not admit until much later, alone, staring at the ceiling of the guest cell they gave him — was that some traitor part of him *did* belong there. Four days ago he'd been a fraud selling painted glass to frightened people. Here, his fraud was suddenly pedigree. Here, the thing in his mother's locked case wasn't contraband; it was a crown.

Vess found him on the second evening, alone on a balcony overlooking the ember-veins.

"You haven't touched the map since you arrived," she said. "I've had you watched. I apologize for that, but I don't retract it."

"It sleeps when I'm not scared," he said. "Your court makes me comfortable. It's very annoying."

She laughed — a real laugh, quick and surprised out of her. Then it faded, and she turned to face him fully, and he saw how tired she was underneath. Centuries tired, maybe.

"Let me tell you about your mother," Vess said. "Not the version you knew. The version we did."

* * *

She led him to a vault below the throne room. No guards — just doors of black iron that opened to her hand, which told him everything about how few people came down here.

Inside, on a table of stone, lay artifacts arranged with museum precision. And in the center, a portrait.

His mother. Younger than he'd ever known her — sixteen, maybe seventeen — in robes of deep red stitched with sigils he recognized now because they crawled across the living map every night. Standing beside her, a man with Vess's jaw and none of Vess's tiredness.

"My brother," Vess said. "Her teacher. Her *friend*." She let the word sit. "Adaeze Okafor was born to this Court, Sable. Trained in the cartographic arts from the age of six. She could redraw a ward-line in her sleep. When the old veil began to fray — when the first star went out, forty years ago — she was the one who mapped the damage. She stood where you're standing and swore she would hold the boundary."

Sable couldn't speak. His mother, who had smelled of dye and cheap soap, who had hummed off-key and haggled viciously over ribbon prices — this girl in red robes was a stranger wearing her face.

"She was going to inherit the office," Vess continued. "But then the Wardens came. The Purges. And Adaeze made a choice." Vess lifted the portrait aside to reveal a document beneath — official seal, Warden script, the ink browned with age. "She testified against us. Names, locations, the vaults, the hidden enclaves. Everything she knew, she gave them, and in exchange they let her walk away. Let her live mortal. Let her have *you*."

"That's a lie." His voice came out small.

"Is it?" Vess tapped the paper gently, so gently. "Read the signature. Read the date. Nine months before you were born."

He read it. The signature was unmistakable — he'd seen it a thousand times on market permits, on his school forms, on the letter she'd left him with the map-case: *Keep it locked. Keep yourself small. Trust no one who calls you special.*

Adaeze Okafor. In Warden ink.

"They killed my brother with what she told them," Vess said quietly. "They burned three enclaves. Two hundred souls. And your mother walked into Cinderhold, married a dyer, and sold thread for twenty years while we hid under her city like rats." She straightened. "So understand me, Sable. I am not offering you sanctuary out of kindness. I am offering it because you are the last Cartographer, and the veil is failing, and the dark outside is eating *names* — and because somewhere in that map of yours is the power to unmake the order that destroyed mine."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you leave, and we grieve, and the dark comes for all of us equally." She smiled, and it was almost gentle. "I would rather you said yes. But I have learned, these forty years, that wanting a thing does not make it come."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the guest cell with the map-case open on his knees, watching the living ink crawl, listening to his mother's whisper rise off the parchment like steam.

*Don't trust the ones who call you special,* the whisper said. Or maybe it said: *don't trust the ones who called me traitor.* The ink kept shifting. He couldn't tell anymore which words were hers and which were the map's own hunger.

Because that was the poison in it, wasn't it. Vess had shown him a portrait, a signature, a story that fit every wound he'd ever carried — the locked case, the smallness, the fear. And it fit *too well*. Twenty years of thread-selling and haggling and never speaking of the past, and now one document explained it all neatly, tied with a bow, delivered by a queen who needed him to believe it.

If his mother had been Court-born, then he was Court-born, and the belonging he'd felt in the ember-lit halls wasn't a lie — it was *his*, stolen back. That was the seduction. Not the flattery. The permission to stop being small.

Or maybe Vess had simply forged the one thing that would make a lonely counterfeit-charm-seller hand over the most dangerous object in the world.

He pressed his palm flat against the map. It warmed under his hand, eager, and whispered something new — not his mother's voice this time, but layered voices, dozens, murmuring like a crowd behind a door:

*Choose. Choose. Choose.*

"No," he said aloud, to the map, to the Court, to the dark beyond the city walls. "Not tonight."

But the map pulsed anyway, and far above, through miles of stone, the last stars over Highmoor guttered — and in the corridor outside his cell, he heard boots. Many boots. Warden-issue, by the sound. Coming down.

Someone had followed them into the dark after all.
