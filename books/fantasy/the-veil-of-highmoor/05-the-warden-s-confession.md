## 5. The Warden's Confession

The holding cells beneath the Wardenhouse smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Ilsa Reyne had spent enough of her life in them to know that smell meant confessions came slow.

She sat across a scarred table from a charm-seller named Doss, a wiry man with burn scars on his knuckles and the flat eyes of someone who'd been arrested often enough to find it boring. Between them lay the contents of his confiscated satchel, laid out with forensic care: eleven charms, each one a fraud. Dried herbs sewn into leather. River stones painted with sigils that meant nothing. Glass beads sold as solidified starlight at three coppers apiece, back when there had been stars to sell.

"Walk me through it," Ilsa said.

Doss shrugged. "You've done this before, Captain. You know how it goes. People are scared. Scared people buy things. I sell things."

"The stars went out four nights ago."

"So they did." He turned his hands palm-up on the table, an old prisoner's gesture: nothing here, nothing hidden. "You going to charge me with that too?"

Ilsa didn't answer. She was looking past him, at the eleventh charm — the one that didn't belong.

It was a compass rose pressed into a disc of tin, no bigger than her thumbnail. Cheap work. Sloppy edges. But the sigil at its center —

She'd seen it before. Not in any of the Warden archives, not on any warrant or wanted bill, but once, years ago, in a room she had spent two decades trying not to remember. A map-case open on a table. A woman's hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle. And drawn in the corner of a living map, small as a signature, the same mark: a rose whose petals were made of roads.

*Cartographer's mark.*

No. That was impossible. The Cartographer's mark belonged to a dead office held by a dead line, extinguished so thoroughly that the Wardens' own histories called it a myth the old regime had invented to frighten children. She herself had signed the order. She had written *line extinct* in her own careful hand, twenty-two years ago, and slept poorly for a month and then never again.

"Where did you get this one?" she asked, and her voice came out quieter than she intended.

Doss glanced down at the tin disc and, for the first time, looked uncertain. "Traded for it. Off a boy who sells at the Cinderhold market. Sable something. Sells the same rubbish I do, only prettier." He smirked. "Why? Is it real?"

"No," Ilsa said. "It's worse than real."

* * *

They gave her a cell alone, which was a courtesy, and she took it standing up because sitting meant thinking and thinking meant the night the stars died over Harrow Fen.

She had been nineteen. Her father had been the ferry-warden there, a minor post, a minor man, and she had grown up believing the wards around Highmoor were like the walls of a house: solid, permanent, built by better hands than theirs and needing nothing from anyone. Then one autumn evening the veil had thinned — no one ever explained why, though later she would read the sealed reports and learn the phrase *Cartographic drift* and understand that somewhere, a dying office had failed at its last duty — and the dark had come through.

Not soldiers. Not monsters, exactly. Just absence, moving with appetite. It took her mother first, and it did not take her body; her mother had stood in the doorway one moment and the next moment Ilsa could not remember her face, could not remember whether she'd had a sister, could not remember why the third chair at the table made her cry. Her father had walked into the fen after it, calling names he no longer knew were names, and the water had kept him.

Ilsa had survived because she'd been on the far side of the fen delivering dispatches, and when she came home there was nothing left to grieve because grief needs a shape and the dark had eaten all the shapes.

So she had done what survivors do. She had found the people most afraid, and the people most afraid were the ones who had built the old order, and together they had written the Edict. *Magic is the wound through which the dark enters.* They had outlawed the Colleges, exiled the Ember Court beneath the city, burned the grimoires, hunted the hedge-witches and charm-mongers and cartographers-of-record until the words themselves became contraband. She had been young and righteous and hollowed out, and she had believed — needed to believe — that if no one touched magic again, no veil would ever thin again, and no girl would ever again come home to a house where her mother had never existed.

For twenty-two years it had worked. Or rather, for twenty-two years nothing had come to prove it hadn't.

Then the stars went out.

She had stood on the Wardenhouse roof the first night, watching the sky go black patch by patch, and felt the oldest fear in her body wake up and stretch. Because she had read the sealed reports. She knew what the stars actually were — not lights, but *anchors*, each one a pin holding a piece of the veil taut against the dark. The Cartographer had hung them. And when the last Cartographer vanished without successor or ceremony, the pins had begun, slowly, patiently, one by one, to pull free.

The Edict hadn't saved Highmoor. The Edict had just made sure no one was left alive who remembered how the veil was mended.

And now a charm-seller's counterfeit bore the true mark of the one office that could.

* * *

"You're certain?" Warden-Sergeant Ovett asked. He was a solid, careful man, the kind she promoted precisely because he never got excited. He stood in her office now while she turned the tin disc under her lamp.

"Look at the petals," she said. "Roads. Every road out of Highmoor, drawn from memory of roads that haven't existed since the city was remapped. No forger alive knows these routes. This is inheritance, Ovett. Not imitation."

"Then we have a name?"

"Sable. Surname unknown. Charm-seller, Cinderhold market." She paused. "Find out everything. Quietly. If he's what the mark says he is, then half the Court will want him crowned and the other half will want him bled, and I want to reach him before either happens."

Ovett frowned. "Captain — with respect. If he's the Cartographer, then the Edict applies. We arrest him. We hold him. The office dies with him, cleanly, the way it should have—"

"And the veil dies with it." She set the disc down. "The stars aren't going out because magic exists, Sergeant. They're going out because magic has no keeper. I helped write a law that amputated the city's hands and now the wound won't close."

Ovett said nothing. He was too disciplined to argue and too honest to agree.

"There's more," she said, and hated how much it cost her to say it. "Pull the sealed reports from Harrow Fen. All of them. I'm declassifying my family's case file."

"Captain, that's—"

"I know what it is. Do it anyway. If I'm going to stand in front of the Council and tell them the Edict was a mistake, they'll want to know why I changed my mind, and the truth is that I changed it twenty-two years ago and was too much of a coward to say so." She looked at the little tin rose, cheap and false and truer than anything she'd forged in her career. "A boy has been selling fake charms to frightened people. Every one of those fakes is a confession that the real thing is gone. And I'm the reason it's gone."

* * *

Near midnight, the reports came up from the archive, and Ilsa read them alone.

Harrow Fen, twenty-two years past. Veil-failure, cause undetermined. Casualty count revised upward three times. And on the final page, in a clerk's cramped hand, a note she had never been cleared to see before: *Witness statement attached — see Addendum C, Cartographer's Office, prior to dissolution.*

Addendum C was a single page, copied badly, the original long burned. A deposition from the last recorded deputy of the Cartographer's office, taken days before the Edict passed. Most of it was legalese. But near the bottom:

*"…the office cannot simply be abolished. The veil requires a bearer. If none is named, the boundary will fail by degrees — first the stars, then the outer parishes, then memory itself. I beg the Council to understand: outlawing the Cartographer does not end the office. It only ensures the next bearer inherits it untrained, unwarned, and alone."*

Ilsa set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate.

Untrained. Unwarned. Alone.

Somewhere in Cinderhold tonight, a young man who sold glass beads as starlight was carrying the weight she had spent two decades insisting nobody should carry. And every hour she spent drafting warrants was an hour the dark ate another parish, another memory, another mother out of a doorway.

She thought of her father walking into the water, calling names.

She thought of the boy, whoever he was, running through streets she had made lawless for him.

Duty, she had always believed, was a wall. You built it stone by stone and you stood on it and you did not move. But walls, she understood now, could be built facing the wrong way.

Ilsa Reyne picked up the tin compass rose, closed her hand around it until the edges bit, and gave the order that would end her career one way or the other:

"Double the watch on Cinderhold. And Ovett — when we find him, we don't arrest him. We warn him. Whatever else I owe the dead, I owe the living the truth about what's hunting them."

Because the reports held one more thing, buried in a supply requisition she'd nearly skimmed past: three days ago, a ferrywoman had carried an unidentified passenger across the drowned quarter, toward the old Ember tunnels.

Toward the Court.

Toward Vess.

Ilsa reached for her coat. If the Ember Queen got to the Cartographer first, the boy wouldn't be warned at all. He'd be *used* — bound by blood and flattery into a rite she was only beginning to understand, a rite the sealed reports called, in a phrase that had chilled her even through twenty years of dust:

*the bloodright of the veil.*

She blew out the lamp and went down into the dark to find him before the dark did.
