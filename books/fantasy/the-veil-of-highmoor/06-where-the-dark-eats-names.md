## 6. Where the Dark Eats Names

The dark had a border. That was the first thing that surprised me.

I'd imagined it the way the refugees described it — a wall of nothing, a place where light went to die. But standing at its edge on the drowned causeway above Old Cinderhold, I saw it was gentler than that, and worse. It didn't end sharply like nightfall. It faded. Streetlamps burned orange, then amber, then a color I had no name for, and then no color at all. Like ink diluted in water until the water forgot it had ever held anything.

The map-case against my chest was warm as a sleeping animal.

"You don't have to go in," Brannagh said. She stood beside me with her one eye fixed on that fading edge, her boat-hook planted on the stones like a spear. "Nobody's making you. The Court wants you to. That alone should give you pause."

"The Court doesn't know what it wants," I said. "That's why they want me."

She snorted. But she'd come anyway — that was the thing about Brannagh. She'd spent three chapters of my life pretending she was only ferrying me for payment, and every mile of it she'd been watching me the way you watch a wound you're afraid to touch.

"My mother's voice comes from this thing," I said, patting the case. "It's been calling something for years and never told me. If the dark is answering, I want to hear what's being said."

"And if it says come closer?"

"Then I'll be polite and decline."

Brannagh looked at me for a long moment. "You lie badly, Sable Okafor. It's one of your few virtues."

We went in.

* * *

Inside the dark, sound changed first.

The city noises — gulls, the far bell of the tide-bell, the creak of the Wardens' patrol-boats out on the black water — all of it thinned, like cloth pulled over a drum. Then our own sounds thinned. My footsteps made less noise than they should have. Brannagh's breathing came to me muffled, as if from another room.

And then memory began to slip.

It wasn't dramatic. That's what nobody tells you. It wasn't pain or screaming or visions of horror. It was small. I reached for the name of the street we were walking down and found a blank where the name should have been — a shape of a word with nothing inside it. I looked at Brannagh and knew her face, knew her laugh, knew the way she spat when she was disgusted, but for three full seconds I could not have told you her name.

Then it came back. *Brannagh.* And my whole body shuddered with relief so violent I nearly sat down.

"It takes names first," Brannagh said quietly. Her hand had gone to the knife at her belt, though there was nothing to fight. "My grandmother told me. In the last war. It eats names because names are the smallest spell anyone ever casts. You say a thing's name and you hold it still. Take the name away—" she gestured around us at the formless grey pressing in from every side, "—and everything starts coming apart."

"How do you know your grandmother told you?" I asked. "If it eats memories."

Her jaw tightened. "Because I wrote it down. On the inside of my left forearm. Before we crossed." She pushed back her sleeve.

Ink, in her own cramped hand: *GRAN — MARGIT — TOLD ME ABOUT THE DARK.*

"Cheating," I said.

"Ferrywoman's privilege."

We walked deeper. Or I thought it was deeper; distance meant nothing here. The drowned quarter rose around us in shapes without edges — rooftops softened into suggestion, doorways into mouths that weren't quite mouths. Everything was *almost* something else. A lamppost that was almost a gallows. A cat that was almost a shadow that was almost a child.

The map-case grew hotter.

"Mother," I whispered, and hated how young my voice sounded. "What am I walking into?"

The case didn't answer in words. It never did, exactly. But warmth spread up through my fingers, and behind my eyes came a feeling I recognized from the market nights of my childhood — the feeling of her hand on the back of my neck, steadying me before a crowd. *Steady. Look at them properly. Most frightening things are just frightened.*

"Comforting," Brannagh muttered, watching my face. "Or ominous. With mothers it's usually both."

Then she stopped walking.

"Sable," she said. Her voice had gone strange. Flat and careful, the voice of someone stepping around broken glass. "My sister. When we were girls, she used to—"

She stopped again.

"She used to—" 

Her mouth worked. Her good eye stared at nothing, at some interior darkness far more complete than the one around us. I watched her reach for something and find her hands closing on air.

"She used to what?" I asked gently. "What was her name, Bran?"

"I know her name." But her voice cracked in the middle of it, and I watched her lips move silently, shaping syllables that wouldn't come, fishing for a word in water that kept closing over it. "I know her name. I *know* it. It started with—" Her hand flew to her forearm, scrabbling at the sleeve, and I saw her find the next line of ink she'd written there, the line below her grandmother's: her sister's name, copied out in letters she now read like a stranger's handwriting. "*Sorcha.*"

She said it aloud, twice, three times, each repetition buying the name back for a moment before it began sliding again, and I understood with a horror that went all the way through me: she hadn't written it down because she was clever. She'd written it down because she'd known — known since the last war, known since her sister vanished — that someday she would walk into this dark looking for her, and she would need the name to carry her home.

And even written, even read, it was slipping. Each time she spoke it, it sounded a little less like hers.

"Bran." I took her arm. "Bran, look at me. We can go back."

"No." The flatness cracked, and underneath was fury, and underneath the fury was something so old and raw it made my throat close. "No. She's here. Somewhere in this. Twenty years I've ferried this river telling myself she was dead clean and proper, buried under honest dirt, and she's *here*, Sable. This thing ate her and it's wearing her absence like a coat." She wiped her eye with the heel of her hand, rough, angry. "I'm not leaving without asking it what it did with her."

The map burned against my ribs.

And the dark answered her.

* * *

It didn't come out of any direction. That was the worst part. One moment the grey was empty, and the next it was full — faces, hundreds of them, drifting in the murk like leaves on still water. Not ghosts. Ghosts have edges. These had been worn smooth, like stones in a river, like things the water had been holding too long.

They didn't speak all at once. They spoke through one — a figure that might once have been a soldier, armor melted into his skin so it looked grown rather than worn. His mouth moved and the dark moved with it, and the voice arrived inside my head rather than my ears, in a tone that was not menacing. That was the second-worst part. It was tired. It was the most tired voice I had ever heard.

***Cartographer.***

"That's not my name," I said.

***It is your office. You opened the case. You carried the map into the deep places. Every step you walked, it called us.***

"We didn't call anyone," Brannagh snarled. "Whatever game this is—"

***No game. No cruelty. Only hunger, which is not the same thing, though men rarely learn the difference.*** The worn faces drifted closer. Up close I could see them clearly, and I wished I couldn't. Some wore Warden colors, rotted to lace. Some wore the ash-grey of the Ember Court's exile. Some wore nothing at all, because clothes are one of the first things the dark digests.

***We are the unburied dead of the old war,*** the soldier-thing said. ***Those who fell at the veil-failures. Those whose names were struck from the rolls so their families would not grieve openly. Those who died holding breaches shut and were never thanked, never named, never laid down. The ground would not take us. So we learned to take instead.***

"Take what?" I asked, though I knew.

***Names. Memory. Land. All the same substance, Cartographer. All drawn lines. You of all people should understand — your trade is deciding where things begin and end. Your mother drew the boundary that left us outside it.***

My stomach dropped. "My mother?"

***Your mother fled rather than finish the work. Left the veil half-bound, held together by blood-oaths thinning year by year. Now the last of them has failed, the stars are out, and the map calls to its maker's heir to redraw the lines. So we come to the heart of Highmoor to be redrawn ourselves — or to pour through what remains and unmake everything, boundary by boundary, until there is no *inside* anywhere and our hunger finally rests.***

"You'd drown the whole city," Brannagh said. "Everyone in it. Everyone who ever—"

***We drowned long ago, ferrywoman. Ask your sister.***

Brannagh made a sound I will never be able to describe as long as I live. Half grief, half rage, all animal. Her knife was in her hand and pointless and she knew it and raised it anyway, and I caught her wrist.

"They're not lying," I said. "Look at them, Bran. Really look."

I did look. Past the horror of them, past the wrongness, I looked — and what I saw was not an army. It was a crowd at a funeral nobody had held. It was every refugee who'd poured through Cinderhold's gates, hollow-eyed, clutching things that used to mean something. Grief given hunger. Hunger given purpose. And underneath it all, so faint I almost missed it, a question:

*Will anyone remember us?*

"They're not marching," I said slowly. "They're *walking*. Like a procession. Like mourners." My voice shook. "They're going to the heart because the heart is where the lines get drawn, and they want—"

***To be on the map,*** the soldier-thing said. ***To be somewhere. To be held. Even a prison is a country, Cartographer, if you have been nowhere long enough.***

The map against my chest pulsed, hot, insistent, and for the first time I felt what it actually wanted — not to fight them, not to banish them. To *draw them*. All of them. And I felt, threaded through that wanting, my mother's old steadying warmth, and beneath the warmth something that frightened me more than the dark did:

Guilt. Her guilt. Twenty years of it, soaked into the leather like rain.

"Vess will tell you to bind them out," Brannagh said hoarsely, reading my face. "The Wardens will tell you to burn them. What will *you* tell them?"

I looked at the worn faces drifting in the grey. I looked at Brannagh's white knuckles around her knife. I thought of my mother, wherever she was, carrying whatever she'd refused to carry.

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "But I think I'm going to need help. Both kinds. The kind that burns and the kind that arrests."

The soldier-thing's ruined face did something that might, in a living man, have been a smile.

***Then hurry, Cartographer. The heart of Highmoor has begun to open. And we are not the only ones who feel it.***

Behind us, far off toward the living city, bells began to ring — not the tide-bell's slow count, but the Wardens' alarm, fast and frantic, over and over. And beneath the alarm, rising like heat off stone, a sound I'd heard only once before, in Vess's court beneath the city:

Singing. The Ember Court, in full voice, marching to war.

Brannagh grabbed my shoulder. "Sable. Whatever's happening up there — it's starting without us."

We ran for the edge of the dark, and the worn faces watched us go, patient as the drowned, and the last thing I heard as we crossed back into starlight-less grey was the soldier's voice, soft inside my skull:

***Bring the map to the heart, little heir. Someone must decide who gets to exist.***
