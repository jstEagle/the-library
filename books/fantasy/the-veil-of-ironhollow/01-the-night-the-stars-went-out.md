## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The charm stall smelled of beeswax, dried lavender, and the particular desperation of a man pretending very hard to be ordinary.

August Villalobos arranged his wares with the care of a jeweler and the prices of a man who wanted to be forgotten by closing time. A row of luck-charms knotted from river grass. Bottles of "genuine moonwater" that was, in truth, well water he'd whispered at until it felt embarrassed to be anything else. Love charms that worked exactly as often as falling in love worked anyway, which was to say: often enough to keep the customers coming.

"Storm-caller," his old master had called him once, with pride. "Weather in his fists."

"Charm-seller," Ironhollow called him now, without any feeling at all. That was the point. Twelve years of being nobody had taught August that invisibility was not an absence but a craft, practiced daily like scales on a fiddle. Keep your hood up. Keep your voice flat. Never fix anything too well — mended roofs attract attention. Sell small magics to small people, and let the big magic rot in you like a tooth you've decided not to name.

He was re-tying a bundle of protective knots when she arrived.

Mrs. Hetta Brum, widow, champion complainer, and his best customer, planted herself before the stall like a ship coming to dock. She was seventy if she was a day, and she had been buying anti-ache charms from him for three years, and for three years she had told him they didn't work, and for three years she had come back every fortnight.

"August." She said it the way everyone did — as though it were a word for a man behind a stall, nothing more. "My knees are singing again. The bad song."

"The one in D minor?"

"Don't get clever. It's going to rain."

"It's always going to rain, Mrs. Brum. We live under a sky."

She snorted and dug in her purse while he wrapped two knot-charms in brown paper. Around them the night market went about its honest business — lanterns strung over the fishmongers' row, someone frying onions, children darting between stalls stealing plums with the dedication of apprentices. Ironhollow sat in the crook of two hills like a coin in a palm, and its market was the warmest place in the kingdom after dark. Ordinary. Blessed, boringly ordinary.

That was when August felt it.

It began as a wrongness in his teeth. Then a tightening along his spine, the way a musician feels a string go sharp just before it breaks. He set down the paper twist slowly, because his hands wanted to shake and shaking hands were memorable hands.

The border wards. He'd helped raise them once — no. Careful. He'd *heard* of them, the way any citizen had heard of them, great woven shields of intent stretched across the mountain passes, humming night and day, keeping out whatever the crown feared. They were supposed to last another century.

They snapped.

Not all at once. Like harp strings cut one by one, somewhere far to the north and east, each break arriving a half-heartbeat after the last, and August stood in the middle of the friendliest market in the kingdom and counted them the way you'd count gunshots. Seven. Eleven. Fourteen. Each snap left a silence behind it, and the silences were worse than the sound, because a ward's hum is a thing you stop hearing after a while — until it stops, and then the quiet where it used to be roars.

*They're down,* thought the part of him that had once held lightning in cupped hands. *All of them.*

Above the market, above the lanterns and the onion smoke, the stars began to go out.

Not clouds — there were no clouds; the sky was clean as swept glass. The stars simply extinguished, in ones and twos and then whole constellations, like lamps being carried away room by room through some vast dark house. The Shepherd went first. Then the Sisters. Then the long pale river of stars that sailors steered by, dimming from the top down as though something were drinking it.

Around him, nobody noticed. That was the horror of it. Mrs. Brum was still rummaging in her purse. The plum-thieves kept thieving. A fiddler sawed away at a reel, oblivious, and the crowd danced to music played beneath a sky with holes being punched in it.

*Good,* said the careful part of him, twelve years practiced. *Let them not notice. Let me not notice.*

But his hands knew what star-death meant. His hands remembered things his mouth would never say.

"Forget the knees," Mrs. Brum announced, having found her purse. "Give me a forgetting charm."

"A what?"

"One of those little bottles. For my sister-in-law, who will not stop talking about her gallstones." She looked up, and for the first time in their three-year acquaintance, August saw that her eyes were frightened, though her voice wasn't. "There's a fog come off the low fields tonight. My dog wouldn't go out. Dogs know."

A fog off the low fields. Wards snapping like cut strings. Stars going out overhead like candles pinched by a passing hand.

"Mrs. Brum," August said, and heard his own voice do something dangerous, which was to become gentle. "Go home. Lock your door. Say your family's names out loud tonight — yours, theirs, all of them. It's an old custom. Humor me."

"What custom?"

"Exactly."

She squinted at him, decided charm-sellers were eccentric the way cats were eccentric, paid him, and shuffled off into the crowd.

And that was when the boy came to buy a luck-knot.

He was maybe ten, gap-toothed, sent by his mother to spend a copper on fortune because his father had a job interview at the quarry. Ordinary business. August chose him a good knot, tied the last loop with a flourish he allowed himself precisely once per market night, and took the boy's copper.

Then he saw what else the boy held out — change, a few coins gathered loose from a dish by the door, and on top of them, catching the lantern light, a silver piece.

A king's face was stamped on it. Every coin in the realm bore the king's face; August had seen Aldric's profile ten thousand times, the strong jaw, the laurel crown, the nose that cartographers flattered.

This face was blank.

Not worn smooth, not scratched, not defaced. *Blank* — smooth as an egg, as though the metal had never decided whose face to be. And where the king's name should have curved around the rim in proud lettering, there was only empty space, a groove where letters had been and had stopped being.

August held the coin and felt the ground tilt under the world.

Because here is what the careful part of him, the twelve-years-invisible part, understood instantly and completely: wards keep things out. That is what everyone believes, what he had believed, what he'd built his exile upon. But a ward is also a promise to keep something *in*, and fourteen strings snapping in the north meant that whatever the crown had sealed — whatever had been pressed down and locked away with such tremendous effort that it required *his* ruin to hold the lock —

was loose.

The stars weren't being put out. Something was eating the light, and the memory of light, and probably — he watched Mrs. Brum pause halfway through a goodbye to her neighbor, brow furrowing, mouth opening around a name she suddenly couldn't find — probably other things besides.

"Sir?" said the boy. "Is the coin bad?"

August looked at the blank face in his palm. Somewhere under his ribs, weather was gathering — pressure, ozone, the old ache of a storm-caller who hadn't let himself storm in twelve years. If the wards were down, then the thing they'd held was looking for the lock. And the lock, whether he liked it or not, whether he moved or stayed perfectly, exquisitely still, was a man selling love charms in the Ironhollow night market for three coppers apiece.

His invisibility had ended the moment the first string snapped. Running wouldn't help. Standing still wouldn't help. There was no more hiding inside the ordinary, because the ordinary itself was being unmade, coin by coin, star by star, name by name.

"No," August said, and gave the boy back the silver, keeping only the copper. "Coin's fine. Here's your luck-knot. Go home quick, tell your mother to say everyone's names out loud tonight."

"Why?"

"Family tradition."

The boy shrugged the shrug of the young, who have not yet learned which instructions save their lives, and ran off into the crowd.

August closed his fist around the blank coin. Above the market the last bright star in the east guttered, hesitated — and went out. The fiddler played on. The onions fried. Nobody screamed, because nobody yet remembered what they'd lost.

He pulled his hood lower, blew out his lantern, and left the stall standing open behind him for the first time in three years.

* * *

He made it four streets before the girl found him.

She stepped out of an alley with a ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, small and wiry, maybe fifteen, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that were too awake for the hour. She looked at him the way no one had looked at him in twelve years — as though she could see straight through the charm-seller to the weather underneath.

"You're August Villalobos," she said.

Every hair on his arms stood up. The name hit him like cold water, like a struck bell, like a door thrown open onto a room he'd bricked shut. He hadn't heard it spoken aloud since the day the decree was read.

"That's a shame," he managed, voice flat, heart hammering like hail on a roof. "I'm afraid you've got me confused with—"

"You're the storm-caller," she said, stepping closer, clutching the ledger tighter. "My father kept the wards. Your exile paid for them. And tonight they fell, and the stars are dying, and I remember *everything*, and nobody else does — so you're going to listen to me, Mr. Villalobos, whether you like it or not."

Behind her, over the rooftops of Ironhollow, the sky hung black and starless as the inside of a closed fist.

August sighed the sigh of a man whose careful life had just ended.

"Start talking," he said.
