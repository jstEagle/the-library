## 2. The Girl Who Remembers Everything

The morning after the stars went out, Ironhollow did what Ironhollow always did: it pretended nothing had happened.

August watched it happen from behind his counter, a cup of chicory going cold in his hands. The baker across the lane opened his shutters and called a cheerful good-morning to the fishmonger, who answered with the same words he'd used every morning for eleven years, though neither of them could have told you the other's name. A woman walked past with a laundry basket balanced on her hip, humming a tune that stopped halfway through and started over, stopped halfway through and started over. Nobody noticed. That was the terrible part. Not the forgetting itself—people forgot things all the time, forgot where they'd put their keys, their griefs, their grudges—but the smoothness of it. The city was forgetting itself the way water forgets a stone, without any sense of loss at all.

And August Villalobos, charm-seller, purveyor of lucky buttons and love-knots of dubious efficacy, sat in his little shop with the door locked for the first time in twelve years.

He hadn't slept. He'd spent the night doing what he always did when the world cracked: inventorying. Counting what he had. Three hundred and forty-one charms, give or take the ones that actually worked. Fourteen years of a life folded small enough to fit in a cellar. And one coin bearing the blank face of a king, sitting on the counter like an accusation.

*The wards snapped,* he kept thinking. *I felt them snap.*

He had felt border wards before—he'd helped raise three of them, back when raising things had been his trade instead of selling trinkets to people who wanted to feel protected. A ward snapping sounded like a harp string cut under tension. This one had been more like forty strings, all at once, and underneath them something vast turning over in its sleep.

The bell above his door chimed.

"I'm closed," August said, not looking up.

The bell chimed again, which was odd, because it only rang when someone pushed the door, and the door had a bolt on it that August had thrown himself at dusk—

He looked up.

A girl stood in the middle of his shop. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, thin as a winter branch, with iron-gray hair pulled back in a knot so severe it looked like it might be holding her head together. She wore a man's oilskin coat three sizes too big and she was breathing hard, as though she'd run a long way or argued with something invisible on the way over. Her eyes went around the shop—the shelves of charms, the bundles of dried rowan, the little brass bells—and then fixed on August with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of someone checking whether a stranger was dangerous.

"You locked the door," she said.

"Yes."

"I picked it." She held up two bent pins, almost apologetically. "Your lock's rubbish, by the way. You should get that seen to."

"Most burglars don't critique the lock."

"Most burglars aren't in a hurry." She crossed to the counter and set down a book with both hands, the way you'd set down something wounded. It was a ledger, leather-bound, its corners worn soft, its spine mended twice with different-colored thread. August knew that mend-work. He'd seen it on half the civic bindings in Ironhollow's old quarter, back when there was an old quarter worth walking through.

"That's a ward-keeper's ledger," he said carefully.

"It's my father's ledger." The girl's chin came up. "Ward-keeper Calloway. Tammas Calloway. Dead four months now, drowned in the reservoir, except he couldn't swim, and everyone says that's just how it goes sometimes, and I say—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again in a flatter voice. "I say my father didn't drown by accident in a reservoir he maintained for nineteen years."

August said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill; he'd practiced it daily since the night he'd walked out of the capital with nothing but his name changed and a price implicitly on anything else.

"The whole city is forgetting," the girl went on. "You've seen it. Don't pretend you haven't—your shutters were open last night when the stars went out, and you stood in your doorway for a full minute looking like a man watching his own funeral. Mrs. Hetch next door forgot her husband's name this morning and made him porridge anyway, smiling at him like he was a lodger. The baker can't remember the blessing over the first loaf. Children are losing their grandmothers' faces." Her voice cracked on that and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "Everyone's forgetting everything. Except me."

"Except you," August repeated.

"I remember all of it." She said it simply, the way you'd report weather. "Every name. Every face. Every street that isn't there anymore—I remember those too, the ones that got forgotten right out of the map. I remember the third bell tower that burned when I was six, and the ferryman Ostrec who had a laugh like a cart on gravel, and I remember—" she jabbed a finger at the ledger, "—that page. Page ninety-one. Read it."

"Wren," August read, because the ledger was already open to a marker and the top of the page said *Property of Wren Calloway — if found, return to keeper's daughter, who will nag you properly,* and despite everything, despite the dread sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone, something in him warmed toward whoever had written that. "That's you."

"My father wrote that when I was nine. Read further down."

August looked down the page. His eyes found the entry and the world tilted very slightly on its axis, the way a floor does when you realize it has been sloped all along and your whole life you thought you were standing straight.

*Expenditure, extraordinary. One (1) binding-grade storm-caller, exiled per royal decree, estate forfeit to crown. Proceeds applied in full to construction and maintenance of the Ironhollow border wards. Note: the crown does not pay for wards. The crown pays to make examples. We paid for ours with a man's whole life. Keeper's note to self: find out who he was. Find out what they took from him. Owe him that much.*

Below it, in smaller writing, cramped and hurried:

*Name supplied under seal: VILLALOBOS, AUGUST. Do not speak aloud. Do not write again.*

August set the ledger down very gently, as if it might go off.

"Well," he said. "That's awkward."

"That's you." The girl—Wren—was staring at him with an intensity that would have stripped paint. "You're the storm-caller. You're the reason the wards exist. My father spent twelve years trying to track down what happened to you, and you've been *next door* the entire time, selling love charms to the tanner's wife."

"They work about forty percent of the time," August said. "I never promised miracles."

"You're joking." Her voice rose. "You're standing there making *jokes*. The wards fell last night. Whatever they were keeping out is coming in, and it's eating the whole city one memory at a time, and the man whose exile *paid* for the wards is selling lucky buttons!"

"Lucky buttons are honest work."

"They're hiding!" She slammed her palm flat on the counter, and the blank-faced coin jumped. "Don't tell me they're not. I know hiding when I see it. I've been doing it for four months—wearing my father's coat, keeping my head down, pretending I'm nobody's daughter because being the ward-keeper's daughter got my father killed and might get me killed too. I know exactly what it looks like when someone makes themselves small enough to disappear." She snatched up the ledger and hugged it to her chest, suddenly younger, suddenly just a tired kid in a dead man's coat. "So don't you dare tell me lucky buttons are honest work."

The shop was quiet. Outside, the humming woman passed again, the tune stopping halfway through, starting over. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing—or perhaps at something; August no longer trusted nothing.

He looked at the girl and saw, uncomfortably clearly, himself at twenty-four: furious, frightened, certain that the world could be fixed if only one specific coward would stand up and do the brave thing. He'd been wrong then. He was probably wrong now. But he remembered what it cost to be that person, and what it cost to stop being them, and which of the two prices he regretted paying.

"Sit down, Wren Calloway," he said. "I'll make tea. Real tea, not chicory—it's the one luxury I allow myself, so appreciate it. And then you're going to tell me exactly what your father wrote in the rest of that ledger, because I suspect you already know, and I suspect you've known for months, and I suspect that's why you came here the moment the stars went out."

She hesitated. Suspicion warred with exhaustion on her face, and exhaustion won by a nose.

"You're going to help?" she asked. "Just like that?"

"No," August said honestly. "Not just like that. First tea. Then I'm going to argue with you for a while, because arguing is free and helping is expensive. But yes. In the end. Help."

"Why?"

Because the wards were mine, he didn't say. Because I built the walls around this town out of the wreckage of my own life and I felt them break. Because a king's face went blank on a coin in my hand and I have been afraid every hour since, and fear shared is at least fear halved.

"Because you picked my lock," he said, "and anyone with standards that low deserves a chance."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might someday build.

They drank the tea. She talked, and the ledger lay open between them, and August listened to the shape of the thing coming for Ironhollow and felt the last twelve years quietly folding themselves up, packing away, preparing to be carried somewhere new.

It was near noon when Wren turned to a page near the end and went pale.

"This entry," she said slowly. "It's dated the day my father died."

*

The last line in Tammas Calloway's hand was not about wards at all. It read:

*If the crown is hollow, the kingdom was built to keep it that way. God forgive us, we weren't guarding the borders. We were guarding the throne.*
