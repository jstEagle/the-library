## 3. What the Wards Were For

Wren Calloway had read her father's ledger a hundred times before the stars went out. She had read it the way other people said prayers—half attention, half habit, the words worn smooth as river stones. *Entry 411: north anchor holding. Entry 412: replaced the salt in the western cairn. Entry 413: Wren's birthday, bought honeycakes, ledger closed early.*

It had never once occurred to her to ask what the wards were *for*.

She supposed that was the trick of it. You don't interrogate the roof over your head until it leaks.

Now she sat cross-legged on the counter of Villalobos Charms & Sundries—which smelled of beeswax, dried lavender, and something faintly scorched that August Villalobos kept pretending not to notice—with the ledger open across her knees and lamplight pooled around them both like a held breath.

"Read it again," August said. He was standing very still by the window, the way he'd been standing since dawn, one hand resting on a shelf of luck-knots as if for balance. "The entry from the night you arrived."

"'Entry nine hundred two,'" Wren recited. "'The border anchors failed at moonrise. Not broken. *Released.* Someone on the inside let go of their end.'" She looked up at him. "My father didn't write that part. It was added later, in different ink. Darker ink. I used to think it was just his hand getting tired."

"And now?"

"Now I think my father wrote the truth down only once, in the dark, where he hoped no one would ever need to find it." She turned pages backward, past years of salt tallies and cairn repairs, back and back, until the handwriting changed—until it became younger, sharper, angrier. Twelve years ago exactly. "Here. 'The crown must remain sealed within the palace bounds. The king is not the danger. The king's *head* is.'"

The shop went very quiet. Outside, Ironhollow's morning market murmured along without them—a woman arguing about the price of eels, children running underfoot, all of it ordinary, all of it oblivious. Wren envied them fiercely.

August crossed the room slowly and sat down on an overturned crate across from her, which put them nearly eye to eye. Up close, she could see how tired he looked. Not old-tired. *Carrying*-tired.

"You knew," she said. It wasn't quite an accusation. It wanted to be one.

"I knew some things," he said. "I've spent twelve years making sure I didn't know the rest. There's a difference between ignorance and—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the shop, at the whole careful architecture of his small gray life, "—this."

"Then say the things you know. Start anywhere."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he reached out and turned the ledger toward himself, careful not to smudge the ink, the way you'd touch something sacred or dangerous. Possibly both.

"Your father," he said, "was the finest ward-keeper the border ever had. Better than the ones who trained me."

"*Trained* you." Wren sat forward. "You were ward-trained?"

"I was many things before I was a charm-seller." A ghost of a smile crossed his face and vanished, embarrassed to be seen there. "Storm-caller. Which sounds grander than it is. Mostly it means you stand in bad weather on purpose and tell it where to go."

"That's *exactly* as grand as it sounds."

"It's less fun than it sounds. There's a great deal of paperwork." He tapped the ledger. "Twelve years ago, the crown of Aldric the Second began to—leak. That's the word your father used, and it's the right one. Whatever sits inside that crown doesn't eat flesh. It eats remembering. Names first. Then faces. Then the reasons you loved someone. And the wards along this border weren't built to keep raiders out, Wren. They were built to keep that thing *in*. Containment. Your father reinforced the seal every season of his life because everyone who understood what the crown truly was had either died, fled, or—"

He stopped.

"—or been exiled," Wren finished softly.

August nodded once, like a man accepting a sentence.

* * *

The rain started while they talked, a thin gray drizzle against the windows, and August got up to light the stove without being asked, which Wren decided was a point in his favor. Charm-sellers, in her experience, were either frauds or cowards, and he seemed to be neither, though he was working very hard at both.

"My name isn't August Villalobos," he said, crouching by the stove. "That's the fourth name I've had. The first one I gave up on purpose. The others were given to me by necessity and a forger in Lowmarsh who charged me two goats."

"What was the first one?"

"No." He said it gently, but firmly, like closing a door on a cat. "Not yet. If I say it aloud in this town, with those wards down and that thing loose in the world, I might as well ring a bell over my own head. You'll have it when you need it. I promise you that much."

"That's a terrible promise. It means I need it eventually."

"Yes," August said, feeding a knot of kindling into the stove. "It does."

Wren hugged her knees. The ledger lay open on the counter behind her, and she found she couldn't look at it anymore—not the entries, not the careful columns of salt and stone, not the last page, where her father's handwriting had gone shaky and strange: *If anyone reads this, the girl remembers too much. Keep her away from the capital. Keep her away from me. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. The wards will hold as long as one of us holds them.*

He had died reinforcing them. Everyone in Ironhollow said fever, said bad lungs, said the wet spring. But Wren had sat beside his bed and watched him press his palms flat against the floorboards, muttering coordinates under his breath like a man lost at sea, and she had known even then that whatever killed him hadn't been in his chest.

"He poured himself into it," she said. "Didn't he. The last anchoring. When the others failed or fled, he fed the wards with—" her voice caught and she pushed through it, angry at it, "—with everything he had left."

August came and sat back down on his crate. He didn't offer comfort, which she appreciated; he just sat there, present, solid, letting the silence be a place to stand instead of a hole.

"The night they snapped," he said, "he wasn't there to hold his end."

"No one was."

"And the thing in the crown felt it. Felt *him* go." August rubbed his face with both hands. "Wren, I need to tell you something and you're going to hate me for it, and you should know upfront that I hate myself considerably more, so we can skip that step."

"Just say it."

"The exile decree. Twelve years ago—the one that emptied the border of every storm-caller and ward-sworn soul who could have stood where your father stood." He looked at the fire, not at her. "I signed it."

"You—" Wren blinked. "You're a charm-seller. You sell *love knots*."

"I sell love knots at a markup of four hundred percent, yes. But twelve years ago I was asked to put my name to a decree. I was told it was administrative. I was told the border wards were being restructured, modernized, that the old guard would be compensated." His laugh had no humor in it whatsoever. "I was twenty-three and flattered that anyone wanted my signature. By the time I understood what I'd done—who I'd sent away, what I'd left undefended—I was already gone. Exiling myself was the closest thing to penance I could afford."

The stove ticked. The rain thickened against the glass.

"My father starved himself into the stones of that border," Wren said, "because of a piece of paper you signed when you were young and stupid."

"When I was young and stupid and *lied to*, yes. Though I've learned the second part doesn't make the first part weigh any less." He finally met her eyes. "I won't ask you to forgive me. I'll ask you to come with me to the capital anyway, because you remember everything this city is forgetting, and I can't do what has to be done without someone who remembers. Those are two separate requests and I'm aware of how they sound stacked together."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Grief and guilt, hers and his, sat in the little shop together like two damp dogs shaking off the same rain.

"If you betray me," she said at last, "I will remember it forever. Literally. Forever. Every detail. I'll bring it up constantly."

"That may be the most frightening threat anyone has ever made to me."

"Good." She unfolded herself from the counter and went to fetch her coat and her father's ledger. "Then we understand each other."

* * *

They were wrapping the ledger in oilcloth when the knock came—three sharp raps, then a pause, then three more, like someone who had rehearsed the rhythm of seeming normal.

August glanced at Wren. Wren glanced at August. Neither of them moved.

"Charm-shop's closed," August called.

"Excellent," said a muffled voice through the door, crisp and slightly offended. "Then I'll say this quickly. My name is Odell Brack. Formerly royal cartographer, currently erased from all records, currently standing in the rain, currently fading—rather literally—and I believe you two are going to the capital, because everyone who knows anything is going to the capital, and I would like to come, provided you agree to say my name at least once daily. Consider it rent."

Wren looked at August. August looked at the ceiling as if petitioning it directly.

"He heard us," Wren said. "How did he hear us? We whispered."

"People who are forgetting themselves hear everything," August muttered. "It's the one compensation." He raised his voice. "Mr. Brack. How did you find us?"

A pause. Then, slightly sheepish: "Your shop has a storm-bell above the door that hasn't stopped ringing since moonrise Tuesday. It's ringing *now*. It's been ringing at your entire street for three days. Half of Ironhollow thinks it's haunted. I think it's pointing." Another knock, more urgent. "Also I'm extremely wet and beginning, I believe, to forget my mother's face, so if we could continue this interview indoors?"

August opened the door.

On the step stood a lean man in a soaked traveling coat the color of dishwater, clutching a leather map-case to his chest the way other men clutched infants. Rain dripped off his nose. Behind him, above the lintel, the little brass storm-bell—one August had sworn to Wren was purely decorative—shivered and rang and shivered again, straining toward the stranger like a dog at the end of its rope.

"Well," said Wren, shouldering her pack, ledger safe against her spine. "Odell Brack. Welcome aboard. That's one."

The cartographer's whole face changed—lit up from somewhere deep, the way a house lights window by window.

"One," he agreed hoarsely. "Thank you. Now—may I come in? And do either of you happen to know the way to a city that keeps rearranging itself? Because I have maps of it," he patted the case, "from memory. And lately, memory is the only road that stays put."

The storm-bell rang on behind them, insistent, as August reached for his own coat—and somewhere far to the east, beneath a sky missing eleven of its stars, the road to the capital waited, hungry, unrolling itself mile by mile toward whoever was brave enough, or foolish enough, to walk it.
