## 4. The Cartographer of Missing Roads

The drowned quarter smelled of old rain and older secrets, which was fitting, because August had come looking for both.

He had not wanted to return to the low streets. Twelve years of careful, deliberate invisibility had taught him a simple arithmetic: every person who knew your face was a door someone could kick open. But Wren had walked into his shop two days ago with her father's ledger and turned his whole quiet life into a countdown, and the ledger's margins—crowded with the ward-keeper's small, furious handwriting—kept returning to one name.

*Brack. If Brack still lives, Brack knows the way.*

"Who's Brack?" August had asked, and Wren had looked at him the way she looked at everything: as though daring it to be more complicated than it appeared.

"The royal cartographer," she said. "Except he isn't anymore. Papa said the court struck him from the records so thoroughly that even his own mother had to think twice before saying his name." A pause. "Papa thought that was very funny. I never did."

"And you think he's in the drowned quarter."

"Papa thought he'd go where the water does the forgetting for people." She had shrugged then, shouldering her father's ledger like a soldier shouldering a pack too heavy for her frame. "So that's where we're going."

*We.* August had noticed she'd started saying *we* without asking permission, the way young people annex territory they've decided is theirs. He hadn't corrected her. There seemed little point in defending borders that were already falling everywhere else.

Now they picked their way through streets where the canal had swallowed the cobblestones a generation ago and simply never given them back. Houses leaned over the water on stilts like tired old men gossiping at a rail. Laundry hung between them, dripping. Somewhere a fiddle played, badly, and stopped, and started again, worse.

"You're sure about this?" August asked for the third time.

"I'm sure about nothing," Wren said. "That's rather the point of everything lately."

They found the cartographer's house by accident, which felt appropriate. It was the only structure on its street still standing square, wedged between two ruins that had fallen toward each other like drunkards leaning on one another. The door was covered in maps—not framed, not preserved, just *pasted*, layer upon layer, until the wood beneath had vanished entirely and the door had become a kind of paper skin, coastlines and mountain ranges and trade roads overlapping in a palimpsest of ink.

Wren raised her hand to knock.

"Don't," said a voice from above.

August looked up. In the upper window, half-hidden behind a curtain made from what appeared to be an enormous nautical chart, sat an old man with a spyglass pointed directly at them. He had the look of a heron that has decided, after long consideration, not to fly away.

"You've been followed since the fish market," the man said. "Not by anything dangerous. By a dog. But a dog today could be something else tomorrow, and I don't open my door for anyone who arrives in company, canine or otherwise."

"We're not here to hurt you," Wren called up. "We need a map."

"Everyone needs a map. That's the trouble with people." The spyglass did not waver. "What sort?"

"A road," August said. "To the capital."

The curtain twitched. For a long moment there was no sound except the fiddle resuming its crimes somewhere across the water. Then, slowly, the window opened, and the old man leaned out, and August saw his face properly for the first time—and felt the strangest sensation, like reaching for a stair that wasn't there.

He knew this man. He was almost certain he knew this man. There was something in the set of the jaw, the sea-gray eyes, that rang a bell whose clapper had been stolen.

"Do I know you?" August asked.

"No," said the old man, too quickly.

"You do," Wren said. She had gone very still, the way she did when her memory snagged on something. "You're Odell Brack. You mapped the northern passes when my father was still an apprentice ward-keeper. He kept your survey of the Graywatch line pinned above his desk his whole life."

The change that came over Odell Brack's face was not joy. It was something rawer than joy—the expression of a man who has been handed back a piece of himself he'd stopped expecting to see again.

"He *pinned it up?*" Brack said. "Forty years of service and the court strikes me from every ledger, every registry, every stone inscription, and Calloway pins my map above his desk?"

"He said it was the truest thing ever drawn."

Brack disappeared from the window. There came sounds of considerable commotion within—a crash, a muttered oath, the unmistakable slither of many papers. The door, when it finally opened, revealed the old man slightly damp and holding a lantern he did not appear to have lit.

"Come in before the fog finds you," he said. "And wipe your feet. I know it's all water, but standards are standards."

* * *

Inside, the house was a country unto itself. Maps covered every surface—walls, ceiling, the undersides of tables. Rolled charts stood in barrels like pickled eels. In one corner, a kettle sat atop a small stove, and Brack attended it with the ceremony of a man performing a sacrament.

"Tea first," he said. "Then impossibilities. I find tea makes the impossibilities go down easier."

August took a seat on a stool that crinkled suspiciously, as though it too were laminated in cartography. "You know why we're here."

"I know why you *think* you're here." Brack poured three cups with hands that trembled only slightly, which for a man his age and profession was practically steady. "The stars went out. The wards fell. People are losing their names in the market districts—you hear it in how they talk now, all roundabout, everyone circling words like gulls circling a boat that won't sink." He fixed August with those gray eyes. "And you want to walk to the capital, into the teeth of whatever's eating this kingdom, because you've read a dead man's ledger and decided you're the ones who can stop it."

"That about sums it up," August said.

"Good. Honest. I can work with honest." Brack lowered himself into a chair upholstered, genuinely, in old harbor charts. "Here's my difficulty. I can draw you the road to the capital. Any road. All roads. I drew them once for a king who rewarded me by unmaking me, because I put a certain valley on a certain map, and a certain crown did not care to be reminded that valleys exist." He sipped his tea. "But my maps have changed since. Or rather—" he smiled, and it was not entirely a comfortable smile, "*I* have."

"What does that mean?" Wren asked.

Instead of answering, Brack reached beneath the table and produced a roll of vellum, which he spread across the floor with both hands. August looked down at a map of the western reaches—and felt his stomach turn over, gently, like a boat in a swell.

The roads on the map were *moving*. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But where a highway ran bold and black toward the capital, its far end was faint, sketched in lines so light they might have been drawn in breath. And where villages should have been, some were rendered in fine detail while others were mere ghosts—outlines of outlines, places dissolving even in ink.

"They fade," Brack said quietly. "Every road, every town, every bridge I ever charted. When the people who remember them stop remembering, the ink goes pale. I've watched my life's work evaporate for three days now, stroke by stroke." He tapped the ghosted end of the highway. "This road exists right now because a toll-keeper two hundred miles east still recites his route in his sleep. When he forgets, it goes. And I—" he spread his hands, "—I am made of the same stuff. The court erased me so completely that I exist mostly on paper. Paper that's fading."

The stove ticked. Outside, the fiddle had stopped again, this time for good.

"So speak my name daily," Brack said. "Both of you. Morning and night, if you can manage it. *Odell Brack, royal cartographer.* It doesn't matter that the title's a fiction now; the name is the anchor. Say it, and I stay solid enough to walk. Forget, and—" he shrugged, a small, dry motion, "—well. You've seen the villages."

August studied the old man across the steam of his tea. He recognized the shape of this bargain. It was his own bargain, worn by a different body—the charm-seller's stall, the false name, the long discipline of being nobody. Two men who had survived by erasure, sitting in a house made of fading paper, discussing a journey into the mouth of the thing doing the erasing.

It should have been hopeless. It should have felt like the end of the road before the road even began.

Instead—and August examined the feeling carefully, the way one examines a coin of uncertain mint—it felt almost like hope. Fragile, secondhand, patched at the seams. But hope nonetheless. Because for the first time in twelve years, he would not be walking toward danger alone, and neither would the man across from him.

"One condition of mine," August said.

Brack raised an eyebrow. "You're negotiating with me?"

"I'm negotiating with the situation. My condition: you don't lie to us on the road. If the way is impossible, say so. If I'm the reason any of this happened—" he held Brack's gaze, and watched the old man's eyes flicker with recognition he clearly wasn't ready to voice, "—say that too."

A long silence. Brack looked at Wren, at the ledger strapped to her back, at August's face, and August had the distinct impression of being *read*—surveyed, measured, and entered onto some interior map with a small annotation in the margin.

"Liar's Road runs through Hollow Country," Brack said at last. "Three hundred miles of it, and every mile stranger than the last. The capital sits at the end like a knot at the end of a rope." He extended a hand, liver-spotted and steady. "I'll take you by the only road that still remembers itself. And you'll say my name each morning. *Odell Brack.* Like a prayer, but grumpier."

August shook it.

"Pack light," Brack added, already bustling toward a cabinet stuffed with rolled charts. "And eat something. Nobody thinks clearly hungry. I learned that mapping the Saltmarsh campaign, which incidentally is also where I learned that generals are just cartographers with worse tempers and better tents—"

The knock came at the door then. Three slow raps, evenly spaced, like something counting.

Brack stopped mid-sentence. The lantern he'd never lit flickered anyway, though no flame burned in it.

"That'll be the fog finding us," he said softly. "Or something wearing the fog's coat." He looked at August, and for the first time the heron-stillness in him cracked, just barely, showing the fear beneath. "You said you were a charm-seller, Master Villalobos. Tell me true—what else are you?"

Outside, the knock came again. And beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, August felt the first distant grumble of a storm answering a call it had been waiting twelve years to hear.
