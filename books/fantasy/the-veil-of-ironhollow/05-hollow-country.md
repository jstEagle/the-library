## 5. Hollow Country

The road out of Ironhollow smelled like rain that had never fallen. Wren noticed it first—the way the air hung thick and expectant, as if the whole country were holding its breath between one word and the next.

Her father's ledger rode in her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, pressed against her hip like a second heartbeat. She caught herself touching it every few minutes, just to feel its corners. As long as she remembered what was written there, she told herself, she couldn't lose it.

Odell Brack walked at the front with his maps spread across a wooden board slung from his neck like a market tray, muttering to roads as he went.

"Keep saying them," he'd explained that morning, in the drowned quarter, while water dripped off the eaves of houses no one lived in anymore. "A road exists if someone remembers where it goes. Stop remembering, and—" he'd made a small, apologetic gesture at the fog rolling over the rooftops, "—well."

So Wren said the roads aloud. *The Millway runs east by the alder stand. The Shepherd's Cut climbs toward Hollowmere.* It felt absurd for the first hour. By the third hour it felt like prayer.

August brought up the rear. He hadn't spoken much since they left the city walls. He kept flexing his right hand, opening and closing it, the way a man does when he's deciding whether to pick something up or put something down forever.

"You're going to wear grooves into your own fingers," Wren said, falling back beside him.

"Old habit." He didn't stop.

"It's not an old habit. I've known you three days. You started doing it when we passed the boundary stones."

He glanced at her then, and there was something almost amused in it, tired as it was. "You really do remember everything, don't you."

"Everything I've seen," Wren said. "Which is why I'm asking. What happens when you use it again? The storm-calling?"

The flexing stopped.

"Nothing," August said, which was the least convincing word Wren had ever heard anyone say, and she had spent her childhood listening to ward-keepers explain away their mistakes.

* * *

They reached the village of Marrow's Bend just past midday, and Wren knew before they entered it that something was wrong, because the wrongness had a shape: it was quiet in the specific way a house is quiet when everyone inside is pretending to sleep.

There should have been dogs. There should have been a smith, or at least the smell of a smith. There should have been someone yelling at children, because in every village Wren had ever passed through, someone was always yelling at children.

Instead there was a well with the bucket still turning on its rope, around and around, nobody drawing it up.

And there were people.

A woman stood in her doorway holding a loaf of bread, halfway through offering it to someone who wasn't there anymore. A man knelt in his garden with his hands full of soil, smiling at nothing. An old fellow sat on a bench outside the chapel, and as they passed him he turned his head with perfect courtesy and said:

"Good morrow to you, and your name is—"

And then he stopped. His mouth stayed open. The smile never moved. He was stuck there, Wren realized, mid-name, like a clock with a bent tooth. He would ask forever and never finish asking.

"Don't answer him," Odell whispered. He'd gone gray around the mouth. "I've seen this. If you give them your name and they can't hold it, the forgetting reaches back along it. Like pulling a thread."

Wren looked at the old man's open mouth, his patient, pleasant smile, and her chest hurt in a place she couldn't point to. Her father had died reinforcing wards against whatever did this. He had died, and these people hadn't even been allowed to grieve properly, because grief needs a name to hang itself on.

"Hello," she said carefully, giving the old man nothing. "We're just passing through."

The smile held. The question held. They walked on.

It was Odell who found the girl.

She was behind the chapel, wedged into the gap between the wall and a rain barrel, knees pulled up, maybe seven years old, with mud dried in pale tracks down her cheeks where tears had run. When Odell crouched near her she flinched so hard she cracked her elbow against the barrel, and then she screamed—not loudly, but continuously, the way a kettle screams, without any plan of stopping.

Wren went down beside her slowly. "Hey. Hey. We're not going to touch you."

"I don't—" The girl's breath hitched. "I don't know who my mother is."

Wren waited.

"She was here." The girl's hands made shapes in the air, grasping at something invisible. "I know she was *here*, I can feel where she was, there's a—a warm spot where she was, and I don't know her face, I don't know her name, I don't even know if 'mother' is the right word, maybe it's *mama*, maybe it's—" Her voice climbed. "I'm scared of her! Why am I scared of her? I'm scared of her and I don't know who she is!"

Because fear, Wren thought, goes deeper than memory. Because the body remembers what the mind lets go of. She thought of her own father's ledger and felt suddenly, fiercely lucky, and hated herself for feeling lucky while this child sat in the dirt with a hole where her whole world used to be.

"What's your name?" Wren asked.

The girl blinked. And then, wonderingly, like reporting a miracle: "Pell. My name is Pell."

"Good. Hold onto that. Pell. Say it again."

"Pell." Stronger.

"Pell, we're going to walk you somewhere safe, and while we walk, you're going to tell us about the warm spot. Everything you can feel about it. Can you do that?"

Pell could. It took some doing—she fought the whole way, small fists and furious dignity—but by the time they reached the edge of the village she was riding on Odell's shoulders, describing her mother's hands ("rough, like bark, but kind-rough"), her mother's humming ("low, wrong notes, all wrong notes, she sang everything wrong on purpose"), and the smell of their kitchen ("burnt onions. Always burnt. She said burnt onions meant love").

"See," Odell said gently, "you remember more than you think. You're carrying her fine."

And that was when Wren saw the fog.

* * *

It came up the valley road like weather, except weather doesn't move with intent. It was low and silver-grey and it flowed rather than drifted, pouring over the hedgerows and filling the ditches, and where it touched the standing stones at the crossroads, the moss on them turned brown and crumbled as though years were passing in seconds.

"The forgetting-fog," August said. He had stopped walking. His face had gone very still, the way a pond goes still right before a stone hits it.

"That's a fog," Wren said stupidly. "That's actual fog, we can outrun fog—"

"We can't. Look at its edges. It's circling." He was right; the grey was bending around them, closing like a hand. "It heard her. Not Pell specifically—it hears *remembering*. Someone in there is paying attention, and we've been talking nothing but memories since Marrow's Bend."

Pell, on Odell's shoulders, had begun that continuous scream again.

"Wren." August turned to face her fully, and she saw that he had already decided something, days ago probably, and had only been waiting for the moment to arrive. "When I do what I'm about to do, I need you to hold the ledger and tell me what's in it. Out loud. All of it. Don't stop."

"Why?"

"Because I'll need something true to stand in." He rolled up his sleeves, and Wren saw the scars there for the first time—old ones, deliberate ones, thin lines drawn in patterns she recognized from her father's warding diagrams. Binding scars. Self-binding scars. "And because after, I might not remember why any of this mattered. So remind me."

"August—"

"Wren." Almost gentle. "I've been saving this for twelve years. Apparently I was saving it for you."

Then he stepped forward into the fog's path, planted his feet, and raised his hands, and Wren watched twelve years fall off him like a coat.

The storm came *up*. That was the only way she could ever describe it afterward. It didn't roll in from anywhere; it rose out of the ground beneath his feet and out of him, thunder cracking skyward instead of downward, lightning crawling along the hedgerows in bright blue veins. Wind screamed through the valley—and the fog *flinched*. She watched it flinch, watched it recoil from August like a hand from a hot stove, and for one glorious moment she thought: *we're winning.*

Then she saw his face.

He was mouthing words. At first she thought it was spellwork, but no—he was saying names. *Mara. Tomas. The blue door on Chandler's Row.* Home things. Family things. Each name came out of him and went into the wind and did not come back, and she could see him feeling each loss land, one by one, like coins dropping out of a torn pocket.

"*The ledger,*" she shouted, and yanked it out, and began to read.

She read her father's careful handwriting into the teeth of the storm: the ward measurements, the supply lists, the note in the margin about August Villalobos, *the storm-caller whose exile paid for the border wards.* She read until her throat was raw. Odell had gotten Pell down and wrapped his coat around her, and the two of them were shouting the roads—all the roads, every road Odell had ever mapped—into the wind, a chorus of names against the grey.

And the fog broke.

It didn't retreat so much as *dissolve*, shredding into rags, streaming away up the valley like smoke chased by a hearth-fire. The pressure lifted. The air went clean and cold and ordinary, so ordinary that Wren wanted to laugh at how beautiful ordinary was.

August swayed. Wren caught his arm.

"August? August Villalobos. Storm-caller. Charm-seller. Terrible haggler—I watched you sell a luck-charm for half its worth yesterday."

He looked at her. And for a heartbeat—one long, terrible heartbeat—his eyes were polite and empty, the eyes of the old man on the bench, mid-question, mid-everything.

Then something behind them caught, like a flint striking.

"...Half its worth," he repeated. Faintly. Testing the shape of it. "You said I was a terrible haggler."

"Yes."

"And you read me the ledger." He frowned, reaching. "My exile paid for the wards. That's—that's in there. In ink. Your father wrote it." He looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, and perhaps, Wren thought, they partly did now. "I can't find my sister's face," he said quietly. "Mara. I know I have a sister named Mara. There's a blue door. I can't—I can't see the blue door anymore, Wren."

"No," Wren said. "But I read it to you once, and I'll read it again. That's how this works now. You'll carry what we carry for you."

He laughed then—a broken, wet, real laugh—and leaned on her shoulder, and let her take some of his weight.

Behind them, Pell tugged Odell's sleeve and pointed back down the valley road, toward the direction the fog had fled. Toward the capital.

"Mister," she said, in the matter-of-fact way of children announcing disasters, "there's more of it coming. Lots more. And it's all going the same place we are."

Wren looked. On the horizon, under the stars-that-weren't, the grey was gathering—not scattered now, but organized, rivering toward the distant spires of the capital like iron filings toward a lodestone.

All of it flowing home to the crown.

"Well," said Odell Brack, unrolling a fresh map with shaking hands. "Then we'd best be sure we remember the road better than it does."
