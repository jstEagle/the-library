## 6. The Mentor in the Mirror

The academy had been called the Vellum Spire once, back when it had a spire and, presumably, vellum. Now it was a broken tooth of masonry jutting from a hillside of wild thyme, its roof caved in like a collapsed lung, its windows staring blind at the road they'd been following for three days.

"We could keep walking," Odell said, though he said it the way a man says *we could keep walking* when his feet have already voted otherwise. He stood at the base of the hill with his satchel clutched to his chest, squinting up at the ruin. "There's a village past the ridge. Probably. If I'm remembering the map correctly, which—" he paused, honest as ever, "—I am not, because the map keeps changing and so do I."

"The fog is coming," Wren said simply.

She didn't point at it. She never pointed at it anymore; she just named it, the way you name weather or death, standing on the eastern rise with her father's ledger hugged under one arm like a shield made of paper. Behind her, far off across the hollow country, the forgetting-fog moved through the valleys slow as poured milk, patient, unhurried, certain. It had been gaining on them since dawn.

August looked at the ruin, then at the fog, then at the two of them—the girl who remembered too much and the mapmaker who was forgetting himself one day at a time—and made the kind of decision he'd spent twelve years practicing how not to make.

"Inside," he said. "We shelter. We rest. We leave before light."

"You're learning decisiveness," Odell observed, already climbing.

"It's temporary."

* * *

The Vellum Spire's great hall still had its bones: ribbed arches, a hearth big enough to roast an ox, rows of reading desks hunched under drifts of plaster dust like sheep under snow. Someone had loved this place once. Someone had argued philosophy here until the candles guttered, had carved initials into desk corners, had left ink-stains shaped like continents on the tabletops.

Wren got a fire going in the hearth while Odell laid out his maps and did his evening ritual, tracing the roads with one finger and speaking them aloud—*the Lowmill road, the ford at Catter's Bend, the shepherd's cut over Thorn Hill*—each name offered up like a coin dropped into a well, buying him another day of existing. August watched him do it and thought, not for the first time, that he had never seen anything so brave performed so quietly.

He told himself he would sleep. He told himself a great many things.

But the fire burned low and the others slept—Wren curled around her ledger like a hedgehog around its heart, Odell murmuring place-names into his dreams—and August found himself drawn down the corridor behind the great hall, toward the old library wing, because the building had a smell he knew. Candle smoke and binding glue and the particular dust of books nobody had opened in years. He had lived inside that smell for six formative years of his life. A body remembers what a mind tries to bury.

The library had fared better than the hall. Half the shelves still stood. Rain had gotten in somewhere and bloomed a black constellation of mold across the astronomy section, which August felt was fitting—he'd never trusted the stars anyway, and now look at them, all gone out like snuffed candles.

And there, on a reading desk near the window, squared neatly as if its owner had only stepped away to fetch more tea, sat a journal.

Bound in grey leather. Corners worn soft. A brass clasp shaped like a key.

August knew that journal the way you know your own front door.

His hands were shaking before he touched it. That annoyed him. Twelve years of careful, practiced nothing—not feeling, not hoping, not being *seen*—and his hands betrayed him over a book.

The clasp opened without resistance. Of course it did. It had always been meant to open. That was the whole horrible shape of it, he realized much later: the lock had been waiting for the key to come home.

*My name is Aldous Vane,* said the first page, in the handwriting August had once tried so hard to copy that his own script still bent toward it, like a plant toward light. *Master of the Vellum Spire, storm-teacher, and—if this works—a liar of the highest order.*

*If anyone is reading this, it means the wards have fallen. Which means I failed. Which means I need you to understand why, because I suspect I know exactly who is holding this book, and August, if it is you, put the kettle on first. This will take a while.*

A sound came out of August that was half laugh, half something else entirely. He looked over his shoulder at the dark hallway. No kettle. No tea. No Aldous Vane striding in with his ridiculous beard and his habit of assigning essays as punishment for breathing too loudly.

Just the page. Just the voice of a dead man, patient as the fog.

*You remember the war, though I did my best to make sure you wouldn't. The border lords had crowned a pretender, and the pretender had learned—God help us—to unmake things. Not break. Unmake. There is a difference, and the difference was eating villages. Whole parishes, gone, and worse than gone: *unremembered*. Mothers couldn't recall their children's faces. Soldiers marched back through towns they'd defended and were turned away as strangers.*

*The court asked me to kill the pretender. Killing, I told them, would only scatter what he'd become. Such things don't die; they move house. So instead I proposed a vessel. A container of state, worn openly, worshipped daily, fed on ceremony and attention—the most remembered object in the kingdom. A crown. Bind the entity into the crown and seal it there, and let the whole nation's love of its king be the walls of its cell.*

*A clever plan. It worked. Here is what I did not tell the court:*

*A binding needs a key. Not a physical key—an act. A wound in the world, specific and singular, that the seal can anchor itself to. And it must be a wound that will never heal, because if it heals, the lock opens.*

*I searched a long time for the right wound. Then the court handed me one, gift-wrapped, in the person of my own finest student.*

August stopped reading. The fire crackled faintly in the other room. Somewhere in the walls, wind found a gap and sighed through it like a student bored by a lecture.

He read it again. Then a third time, moving his lips like a child, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something survivable.

*You remember your trial, August. You remember the charge: reckless storm-calling, endangering the city of Ironhollow. You remember that you confessed. I know you confessed, because I wrote the confession myself and you—loyal, furious, heartbroken boy—recited it word for word because I asked you to. You believed you had done something wrong. You hadn't. The lightning strike that toppled the harbor tower killed no one; I arranged the evacuation personally. But your exile decree needed to be real, irrevocable, written into law and stone and grief, because a wound everyone mourns is a wound that never heals. Every year Ironhollow remembers the storm-caller who betrayed them. Every year the memory renews the seal. Your disgrace was the mortar in the crown's prison.*

*I chose you because you were strong enough to survive it. I tell myself that. Some nights I even believe it.*

*Twelve years the crown held. But seals starve, August. The court forgot to feed it ceremony. The kings stopped wearing it. And a starving thing in a cell begins to thin the walls. When the wards finally fail—and they will fail, they are failing now, your exile bought them twelve years and not a day more—it will come looking for the wound that anchored it. It will come looking for you. You are not merely the key, my boy. You are the hinge of the whole door.*

*I am sorry. I have written this apology forty times and every version is cowardice dressed in calligraphy. So I will say it plainly: I took your life and used it as a lock, and I told myself it was for the greater good, and both those things are true, and neither one excuses the other. If you find this, do not try to fix what I broke out of loyalty to me. Loyalty to me is what broke it.*

*Go home, August. Whatever that word comes to mean for you.*

*—A.V.*

August sat down on the floor. Not gracefully. His legs simply resigned their post.

*You are the lock.*

He thought about the blank-faced coin. The stars going out like a lesson dismissed early. The wards snapping—*he'd felt them go*, twelve harp strings cut at once—and he had assumed, all these years, that his exile had *built* something. That his punishment had purchased protection. He had hated Ironhollow for exiling him and hated himself for deserving it, and both hatreds had given his life a shape, a terrible tidy symmetry: *I sinned, therefore I suffered, therefore the border holds.*

It wasn't symmetry. It was sacrifice. Someone else's.

And the worst of it—the part that made him press the heel of his hand against his eyes in the dark of a dead library—was that it explained everything. Why Vane had visited him in his last month at the Spire, teaching him ward-craft with a gentleness August had mistaken for favoritism. Why the trial had moved so fast. Why Vane hadn't come to say goodbye at the gates, and how much that absence had hurt, and how August had carried that hurt like a stone for twelve years, polishing it nightly, and the whole time—

The whole time the old man had been watching his student walk out the gate carrying a kingdom on his back, and had not been permitted to say a word, because a key that knows it's a key might start asking questions. Might start trying to turn.

"August?"

Wren stood in the doorway, silhouetted against the faint glow of the banked fire, the ledger clutched in both hands. She'd heard him, then. Or felt the silence change. She had a talent for that—for noticing the exact weight of things unsaid.

"You've been gone three hours," she said. "Odell's talking in his sleep again. All place names." She hesitated. "You're crying."

"I'm not."

"You are, but I won't make it a thing." She came and sat beside him on the dusty floor, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, the way people sit when they're offering contact but not demanding it. She looked at the open journal for a long moment. "Whose is it?"

"My teacher's."

"The one whose name is in my father's ledger? Aldous Vane?" She'd read the ledger cover to cover, of course. She read everything. Forgetting was the one luxury she'd never been able to afford. "The ward-keeper's records say Vane designed the original border seals. My father used to curse his name. Said his work was brilliant and impossible to maintain and probably haunted."

"He was right on all three counts."

Wren waited. She was good at that too. August stared at the journal, at the brass key-clasp glinting dully, and understood that he could carry this alone—he'd carried everything alone for twelve years, he was very accomplished at it—or he could do the harder thing, which was to set it down where someone else could see it.

"He bound the entity into the crown," August said. His voice came out flat, strange, like a stranger reporting weather. "To stop the war. But a binding needs a key. An unhealing wound." He turned the journal toward her, toward the line, and watched her read it, watch her lips move slightly the way Odell's did over place-names. "Me. My exile decree. Every year this kingdom remembered the traitor of Ironhollow, it tightened the lock. I wasn't punished for a crime, Wren. I *was* the crime. Or the—"

He stopped. His throat had closed.

"The mortar," Wren said quietly.

They sat in the dust of the Vellum Spire, two orphans of other people's secrets, and above them through the broken roof the sky showed black and starless, and somewhere eastward the fog kept coming, patient as a debt.

"I spent twelve years being invisible," August said at last. "Careful. Small. Because I thought I'd earned it." A laugh escaped him, cracked and humorless. "Do you know what the funny thing is? Vane writes *go home*. As if I have one. As if 'home' isn't the city that hates my name—which, incidentally, is the only reason any of this worked. My shame was load-bearing."

Wren reached over and took the journal from his hands. Gently, the way you take a hot pan from someone who doesn't notice they're burning.

"So we stop feeding it your shame," she said.

"That's not—it doesn't work like that. The seal is already failing. The wound has to be *closed*, not reopened, and the entity's loose regardless, it's eating names right now, it ate the *stars*—"

"Then what closes it?" she asked. Simply. Terribly. The way she asked everything, because she was her father's daughter and her father had died reinforcing walls built on lies and she had inherited his habit of looking directly at the structural flaw.

August didn't answer. Because he had already begun, in some cellar of himself he hadn't known he possessed, to see the answer's shape. A binding needs a key. A key is an act, a wound, a name. And the entity—the Unremembered, the thing in the crown—could eat anything except the one thing freely given. Vane had taught him that, once, in a lecture he'd slept through: *Nothing devours a gift, August. Even hunger has manners.*

An unhealing wound, willingly healed. A name, freely spoken.

"Get some sleep," he said instead, taking the journal back. "Long road tomorrow."

Wren looked at him for a long moment—looked at him the way she looked at everything, as if memorizing it against future loss—and then rose, brushed the dust from her skirt, and paused at the doorway.

"For what it's worth," she said, "my father's ledger lists every ward-anchor in the kingdom. Dates, strengths, failures. He was thorough." Her voice went carefully level. "The crown's seal is listed last. Under maintenance notes, he wrote one line. *Anchor requires the living presence of the key.*"

She left him alone with that.

August sat in the ruined academy until the grey beginning of morning crept through the broken roof, Vane's journal open on his knees, and he read the old man's words until they wore smooth as river stones. *Loyalty to me is what broke it.* Outside, the first birds started up, tentative, testing whether song was still allowed.

And far away to the west, past the emptied villages, past the smiling vacant faces frozen mid-name, the capital waited with its hollow crown and its hungry throne—and the road to it ran straight as a sentence being written, and August Pemberton Villalobos, storm-caller, traitor, lock, finally spoke aloud into the empty library, to a man eleven years dead:

"All right, old man. I'll bring you the key."

He closed the journal, and began to pack.
