## 7. The City That Forgot Its King

I have been drawing maps since I was six years old, and in thirty-one years of cartography I have learned one iron law of the trade: the land does not lie. Rivers may flood, roads may crumble, but the bones of a place—the way a city sits in its valley, the stubborn angle of its streets—those things endure. A map is an act of faith that the world will hold still long enough to be known.

The capital broke that law before we even reached its gates.

"Odell," Wren said, tugging my sleeve for the third time since dawn. "The river."

I looked up from my traveling atlas, quill still tucked behind my ear like a carpenter's pencil, and felt my stomach perform a slow, dignified revolution. The Silverwash should have curved east around the city walls. On every map I had ever drawn—every map *anyone* had ever drawn—it curved east. It was why the harbor sat where it sat. It was why the fish market smelled the way it smelled.

The river now ran straight as a ruler's edge, cutting through what my memory insisted was the Tanners' District.

"That's not possible," I said, which is a phrase I had been saying approximately once per hour for two days, and which had lost all power to stop anything.

"It's been possible all week," August said quietly. He rode with his hood up, hands buried in his sleeves like a man attending his own funeral. "It just wasn't *your* problem until now."

Everything about the man suggested he wished to be smaller than he was. I had spent four days in his company and could count on one hand the number of times he'd looked directly at me. But when he spoke, people listened—not because he commanded it, but because there was something in his voice like distant thunder deciding whether to bother.

"The palace," I said, and unrolled my sketch across my saddlebags. "The palace is built on bedrock. Granite shelf. It cannot reshape."

Wren leaned over my shoulder. She had her father's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and she touched it the way other people touch amulets. "Then explain the towers."

I looked. There were indeed more towers than there ought to be. The palace of Aldric the Third had eleven spires—I had drawn them so many times in my youth that I could have done it blindfolded, upside down, in a snowstorm. There were now somewhere between fourteen and nineteen, depending on how you counted the ones that seemed to be arguing with each other about whether they existed.

"I can't," I admitted.

"Good," said August. "Honesty. Rare quality in a guide."

"You hired me because I know the capital better than anyone alive."

"I hired you because you offered, and because Wren trusted you, and because you promised to be useful." He paused. "Also I paid you nothing."

"A point in your favor, economically speaking," I conceded.

* * *

We entered through the River Gate at midday, and this is where I must confess something that has troubled me since: I did not recognize my own childhood.

I grew up in the capital. My father was a chandler; our shop stood on Bellows Lane between a pie house and a woman who repaired violins and argued with both. I knew the city the way you know your own handwriting. And yet walking those streets was like reading a letter from myself that someone else had rewritten. The words were mostly right. The meaning kept sliding.

Bellows Lane was still there. The pie house was still there. The violin woman's shop was now a candlemaker's, then a cobbler's, and then—while I watched—a milliner's, the sign repainting itself letter by letter in a hand suspiciously like mine.

"They're using your memories," Wren whispered. "The streets. They're wearing them."

"That is deeply rude," I said, because if I said anything else I would have had to sit down in the road.

August caught my arm. His grip was careful, precise, the grip of a man who had learned exactly how much force the world required and refused to use a drop more. "Don't look too hard at any one thing. Keep your eyes moving. A map-maker's trick?"

"As it happens, yes."

"Then make us a map," he said. "Out loud. Quietly. Walk us through what should be here, and maybe what should be will remember itself faster than what isn't."

So I did. I narrated the capital under my breath like a nervous tour guide: *the granary on the left, the fountain with the leaping carp, the shrine to Saint Ferrow where children leave buttons.* And here is the strange mercy of it—wherever my voice went, the city steadied. The fountain appeared. The shrine flickered into being, buttons and all. The moment I stopped talking, things began to drift again, like furniture on a tide.

"You're anchoring it," Wren breathed. "Your remembering is heavier than their forgetting."

"For now," August said. He did not say what he was clearly thinking, which was *until it notices you.*

* * *

The pageant found us rather than the reverse.

We heard drums first—too many drums, a celebration with no occasion—and followed the sound to the Grand Processional Way, where the entire street had turned out for the Festival of the King's Glory, which I can assure you appears on no calendar ever devised by mortal hands.

There were floats. There were banners in royal blue and gold. There were dancers, acrobats, a choir of forty singing an anthem whose lyrics changed slightly every verse, as though the words couldn't quite commit. And at the center of it all, borne on a litter by eight bearers, was an enormous effigy of King Aldric the Third—

—which had no face.

Not a blank face, understand. Not a smooth oval, which would have been eerie enough. No: the effigy had the suggestion of features, constantly rearranging. A nose would form, thoughtful and aquiline, then dissolve. An eye would open, close, migrate. Watching it was like watching a crowd from above—individual details appearing and vanishing while the whole remained stubbornly, cheerfully present.

The crowd adored it. That was the worst part. Thousands of people cheering, throwing flowers, weeping with love for a face they could not describe.

"Hail the King!" shouted a baker beside me, tears streaming down his cheeks.

"What does he look like?" Wren asked him gently. "The king. Can you tell us?"

The baker beamed at her with radiant, uncomplicated joy. "Like a king, miss! Like a king!"

"He looks however a king ought to look," added a washerwoman. "You know. Kingly."

"Dark hair? A beard? Tall?" Wren pressed.

They considered this with the serene patience of the very happy. "Yes," they agreed, together, and meant it, and it told us nothing.

Only one person in that whole shining crowd seemed uneasy: a small girl near the front, perhaps seven years old, clutching her mother's skirts. As the faceless effigy drifted past, she looked directly at it, and her lip trembled, and she said—clearly, in the ringing silence only children achieve:

"Mama, that's not what he looks like."

Her mother laughed and hushed her. But the effigy's half-face *turned*. Just slightly. Just toward the child. And every bell in the capital rang at once though no ringer stood in any tower.

We left the procession at speed.

* * *

The palace guards let us in.

This requires explanation. We had no invitation, no credentials, and—in my case at least—an outlawed profession. Yet when we approached the Servants' Gate, the guard on duty straightened, saluted a set of orders that existed nowhere I could see, and waved us through with the vacant courtesy of a sleepwalker.

"All visitors are expected," he announced, to us and to no one. "All visitors have always been expected. The court thanks you for your attendance."

"Attendance at what?" Wren asked.

"The audience," he said warmly. "The audience is always beginning."

Inside, the palace was worse than outside, because inside I *knew* the layout, and the layout knew I knew, and it took offense.

Corridors I had mapped at nineteen now bent where they had always run straight. The Gallery of Kings stretched twice its proper length, portraits ripening and rotting along its walls—the same king, over and over, faces smearing like wet paint. Staircases climbed politely to landings that declined to be there. Twice we walked through a door marked GRANARY and emerged in the chapel. Once we walked through a door marked CHAPEL and emerged in the granary, which I felt was almost honest of it.

"Stop," August said at last. We stood in a corridor that was, I am nearly certain, trying to become a different corridor while we watched. "Odell. You said the palace couldn't change. Tell it that."

"With respect, I don't think it cares about my opinion."

"It cares about *memory*," Wren said suddenly. She had the ledger out, pages fluttering as though in wind that wasn't there. "My father wrote it down—the wards, the binding, everything keyed to true remembrance. If the palace reshapes itself out of forgetting, then—" she looked at me, eyes bright and frightened and absolutely certain— "then it can't move against someone who remembers it properly. Draw it, Odell. Here. Now. Out loud."

So I stood in a shifting hallway of a mad palace, produced my journal, and began to do the hardest work of my life: mapping a building that was actively disagreeing with me.

I drew the throne room as I had last seen it at twenty-three, when I'd presented His Majesty's Survey of the Northern Reaches and received, in return, a handshake I have treasured and a dismissal I have resented. Long hall, seventy paces. Dais of white marble, three steps. Windows of colored glass on the eastern wall, saints and seasons. I spoke every measurement aloud, and as I spoke, the corridor around us shuddered, groaned like a ship in weather, and *obeyed*—grudgingly, the way a cat obeys—until we stood at the end of a hall that matched my lines.

The guards lining it did not match anything. They stood at attention with perfect discipline, facing an empty dais, presenting arms to a throne room that contained no king, no crown, and no audience except three tired travelers and whatever else was waiting behind the marble.

"Orders from the throne room," murmured the nearest guard, eyes forward, voice pleasant as tea. "His Majesty commands: prepare the audience. His Majesty is looking forward to meeting the storm-caller after so very long."

The words went through our little company like cold water. Wren grabbed August's sleeve. August had gone very still, in the way of a man hearing a voice he'd hoped never to hear again, from a mouth that had no business knowing anything.

"How long," August said carefully, "since His Majesty issued orders?"

"Why, twelve years," said the guard, smiling. "He's been giving them the whole time. He says you'll remember the way in."

* * *

That night—we assumed it was night; the sky over the capital held stars the way a sieve holds soup—we sheltered in the map room, the one chamber in the palace that still made sense to me, possibly because I had bled over it for a decade.

By candlelight I redrew the palace entire, from memory, correcting for everything that had shifted. Wren read her father's ledger by my elbow, annotating my draft with ward-lines I hadn't known existed. And August stood at the tall window, looking down at the sleeping, drifting, rewriting city, and said nothing for a long time.

"Odell," he finally said. "Tomorrow, when we go in there—whatever happens—will you do me one kindness?"

"Name it."

"Don't stop saying my name," he said. "Whatever happens tomorrow. Whatever it offers, whatever it takes. Names are the one coin it can't counterfeit." He almost smiled. "You asked me to speak yours daily so you wouldn't fade. Consider us even."

I looked at this man who had spent twelve years being nobody on purpose, and I understood—for the first time, truly—that he was not asking for safety.

He was asking to be carried.

"August Villalobos," I said, testing it, tasting it, committing it to ink in the margin of my map where it would keep. "I'll write it in every chart I own. Every border, every coast. Sailors will curse your name in harbors you've never seen."

"That," said August Villalobos, "is the nicest threat anyone's ever made me."

Beyond the window, deep beneath the palace, something vast turned over in its sleep, and every clock in the capital struck an hour that does not exist, and the doors of the throne room—seventy paces away, three steps of white marble—swung silently open, as if to say:

*We've been expecting you. We've always been expecting you.*
