## 8. Audience with the Hollow Crown

The doors of the throne room opened on their own, which was the first wrong thing. The second was that they were warm.

August had expected cold. Palaces in stories were always cold—marble and draft and the breath of dead kings—but the doors yielded to Wren's push like the flank of a sleeping animal, and the air that rolled out smelled of beeswax and bread and, beneath it, faintly of rain on hot stone. A storm's smell. His smell.

"Stay behind me," he said, and hated how much it sounded like a thing a man says in a story right before everything goes badly for him.

"You said that at the fog," Wren whispered. "And the bridge. And the inn with the suspicious stew."

"And were you ever once behind me?"

"No," she admitted. "But I appreciate the ritual."

Odell said nothing. He hadn't said much since the gates, since the pageant where ten thousand people had cheered a king whose face no banner could hold—a smudge of gold paint where features should have been, and every celebrant weeping with love for it anyway. Odell had stood there mapping the palace from memory, muttering elevations under his breath like prayers, because the palace kept rearranging itself around them. Corridors lengthened when unwatched. Stairs went down twice. But the throne room, Odell had said grimly, drawing it in the dirt of his memory with his finger: *the throne room never moves. It's the anchor. Everything else drifts off it.*

They crossed the threshold together anyway.

The hall was vast and lit by candles that burned without shortening, and the light did not so much illuminate as *remember* the room into being—the columns solidifying as August's eyes passed over them, the banners stitching themselves out of suggestion. At the far end, on a throne of grey iron veined with something darker, sat King Aldric.

August knew the king only from coins and wanted posters, and even now, at forty paces, the resemblance was approximate. The man sat perfectly still, hands on the armrests, head tilted with an attentiveness that was almost kind. His face was *there*. That was the horror of it. After weeks of blank coins and unnamed crowds, here at last was a whole human face—weathered, middle-aged, tired around the eyes—and it moved when its mouth moved, blinked when it needed to blink, smiled when—

It smiled when August flinched.

"There you are," the king said, and his voice was layered, two voices in one throat, one of them Aldric's own and worn thin as old parchment, and the other one August knew. Knew the way you know a lullaby you'd rather forget. Warm. Dry. Amused. *Aldous Vane's voice.*

"Master Villalobos," the second voice continued, folding itself comfortably around the first. "Twelve years. You haven't aged nearly as well as your grudges."

"Aldous." The name came out of him like a splinter. "Let the king go."

"The king?" The body on the throne looked down at its own hands with mild curiosity, turning them over, examining the royal signet. "He's still in here. Somewhere in the back rooms of himself. Very tidy back rooms—he was a methodical man, your Aldric. I've tried not to disturb the furniture." The smile widened. "You may speak with him, if you like. He remembers you, incidentally. You're the reason his crown has been so *hungry* all these years. He carries that guilt beautifully."

Behind August, Odell made a small sound—the sound of a cartographer realizing his map is wrong. "August," he breathed. "The wards. Your exile decree. It's the binding, isn't it? You're not the key. You're the *lock*, and locks don't get to walk away."

"I know what I am," August said quietly.

"Then you know why I'm offering you terms," said the thing wearing the king.

* * *

It rose from the throne, and it did not walk so much as arrive, each step simply relocating it closer, the distance between them surrendering without negotiation. Up close, Aldric's face was worse than blank—it was *inhabited*, the way a house is inhabited, and August could feel something looking out through the eyes the way a spider sits at the center of a web, feeling every tremor.

"Here are my terms," it said. "Walk away. Turn around. Take your girl and your mapmaker and go back to Ironhollow and sell your little charms. In exchange—" it raised one hand, elegantly, a conjurer's flourish "—I restore what was taken from you."

"I was taken from myself," August said. "Not the reverse."

"Were you?" And the throne room changed.

The candles guttered and came back golden, and the walls were gone, and August stood in a kitchen he had not seen in twelve years—a kitchen in a village called Emberlow, with copper pots hanging from hooks and a scarred oak table and a woman laughing at something, flour on her hands, flour on her cheek, flour in her dark hair, saying his name—

"*August.* Supper's getting cold, you disaster of a man."

Mara. His wife. Dead these twelve years—or not dead, no, look, alive, annoyed, beautiful, *alive*, and behind her a boy of maybe nine chasing a cat under the table, and the boy turned and grinned up at him with a gap where a tooth had been, and said, "Father, she scratched me again, can we eat her?"

"Only if you say grace first," August heard himself answer, in a voice he'd forgotten he owned.

His knees nearly went. Wren caught his arm—Wren, who was somehow still beside him, though the kitchen was realer than she was, more solid, more *now*—and he realized distantly that his legs were shaking and that tears were running freely down his face and that some part of him, some large and shameful part, was already packing its bags.

"This is not real," Wren said firmly, to him, or possibly to the room. "My father taught me about this. The fog shows you what you want. It fishes with your own bait."

"It is real," said the Unremembered gently, from everywhere. "That is the cruelty of it. This happened. Every moment of it happened, and I kept it, because I keep everything that is forgotten. They forgot you, August—not by my hand, but by the decree's. Exile is erasure; your council knew that when they wrote it. The binding required it. So Emberlow woke one morning and could not recall why the extra chair sat at the table, and Mara told herself she'd always been alone, and your son grew up calling another man *father* because the shape where you had been would not hold a name." The kitchen flickered—the boy aged, became a young man, broad-shouldered, a stranger with August's grin. "But *I* remember. I remember all of it. And if you walk away, I will give it back to them. All of them. Every villager who lost you, restored. Mara will wake tomorrow knowing twelve missing years. Your son will know his father's face. The exile revoked, the lock unmade—gently, without breaking anything but your silence."

"And the king?" August managed. "And the stars? And every village full of smiling hollow people?"

"Ah." The Unremembered spread Aldric's hands, apologetic. "There is always a price, isn't there? There was a price when Vane bound me. There was a price when your council signed the decree. Everyone in this long story has paid someone else's bill, August. Why should you be the first honest man?" Its voice softened, and that was the worst weapon it had, that gentleness, because it was *Vane's* gentleness, the voice that had once talked a frightened apprentice through his first storm. "I was a man once too. I remember being one. Barely. I remember wanting what you want—to matter somewhere, to be known in one small room. Take it. Take the kitchen. Let me finish eating the rest, slowly, and none of it will ever be yours to mourn."

Silence. The kitchen held its breath around him—Mara waiting at the table, the cat under the chair, supper going cold exactly as it always had, preserved forever at the temperature of his longing.

"August," Wren said. Small. Steady. "Your charms. Back in Ironhollow. The luck-knots, the fever-charms. Half of them didn't work, did they? You sold them anyway."

"They worked sometimes," August said hoarsely.

"You gave people hope with broken thread and called it kindness. Don't you dare buy hope from *this*."

And August laughed—wet, cracked, surprised out of him—and looked one last time at his wife's flour-dusted cheek, memorizing it against his heart, and said, "Mara, love. Supper was always cold by the time I got home. Some things don't change."

The kitchen shattered like ice on a pond.

* * *

He was on his knees on the throne room floor, and the king stood over him, and the double voice said, with genuine curiosity now, "Why? Explain it to me. I have forgotten wanting things honestly. Convince me."

"Because a memory given back by a thief is stolen twice," August said. He rose, slowly, his old bones complaining. "Because my family deserves their grief clean, not gift-wrapped. And because—" he met the borrowed eyes "—you asked Vane's question wrong, old man. You said *why should you be the first honest man.* I'm not trying to be honest. I'm trying to be *useful*, which was always the better trade."

The entity regarded him for a long moment. Somewhere deep behind Aldric's eyes, something else stirred—something small and methodical and drowning, pressing against the glass.

"Then we proceed to the unpleasant portion of the evening," said the Unremembered, and sighed, and the candles began to go out one by one, and the fog came seeping up through the marble like water through a hull.

At the far end of the hall, the great doors boomed shut. Through them, muffled and distant, came Wren's voice—she and Odell had been driven back, he realized, sealed outside—and she was shouting his name, shouting it over and over like a ward, like a rope thrown across dark water.

"August! *AUGUST!* Remember it! Whatever it takes you—remember that we—"

The fog took the rest.

August rolled up his sleeves, the way he had for storms, the way he had for supper, the way he had for every small stubborn act of repair in a life spent being erased, and began to recite his true name.

The darkness leaned in to listen.
