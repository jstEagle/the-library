## 9. The Unbinding

The gates of the palace were bronze, taller than three men standing on each other's shoulders, and covered in reliefs of victories nobody could name anymore. Wren pressed her palms against them and felt the metal humming like a kettle about to boil.

"Odell," she said. "How do we close a gate that doesn't have doors?"

"It has doors." Odell Brack was crouched at the hinge-side pillar, chalk stub in hand, drawing lines on the stone with the fierce concentration of a man defusing something. "They're just *elsewhere* right now. The palace reshapes around whatever the Unremembered wants forgotten. Right now it wants these doors to be a wall." He blew chalk dust off his fingers. "So we remember them into being."

"That sounds like a children's story."

"Most true things do," Odell said. "That's why they survive."

Wren looked back across the plaza. The fog was coming.

She'd seen forgetting before—had watched whole neighborhoods in Ironhollow go soft-eyed and smiling—but she had never seen it move like this: a slow silver tide sliding up the palace steps, eating sound as it came. Their footsteps on the flagstones went quiet first, then the wind, then the distant bells that had been ringing wrong all day. It was like watching snow fall upward over the world, covering everything in white silence.

And behind her, up the great stair, through the throne room doors that stood open onto darkness, August Villalobos was already walking toward the crown.

*Hold the gate,* he'd said, and had almost smiled—the crooked half-smile he did when he was terrified and pretending not to be. *I'm rather good at storms. Terrible at goodbyes. So don't let there be one.*

"Right," Wren said aloud, because saying things aloud was the whole trick now, wasn't it? Saying things aloud was how you kept the world from dissolving. She planted herself in front of the bronze gates. "Odell. Tell me what to say."

"The doors," Odell said, standing beside her, chalk-white hands trembling only slightly. "Describe them. Everything. Every scratch and rivet. I'll map as you speak. Between us—" he managed a grin that was mostly bravado and entirely beloved— "we are going to be insufferably thorough."

* * *

The fog reached the bottom step at moonrise, or what would have been moonrise if any of the stars had bothered to come back.

Wren talked.

She described the gates' bronze dragons whose scales were actually tiny hammered feathers, because the smith who made them had apprenticed to a feather-worker first—a detail her father's ledger mentioned and which she suspected no living person but her knew. She described the rust stain shaped like a running hare on the left door, which Odell swore he'd drawn in his court maps twenty years ago. She described the way the hinges sang a low note in high wind, like a drunk humming through his teeth.

As she spoke, the metal *listened*. Lines drew themselves along the blank wall where a seam should be. A handle swelled out of nothing like a bud forcing open.

The fog slid closer. Where it touched, the plaza stones forgot they were stones and became smooth gray nothing, and the fountain forgot its water, and a guard standing at the stair's base turned slowly toward them with an empty pleasant face, and opened his mouth—

"—and the third dragon from the left is chipped," Wren said loudly, "because a cartographer named Odell Brack climbed it when he was nine years old and fell, and everyone laughed, and he pretended not to mind but he drew the chipped scale extra carefully ever after, out of spite—"

"I did *not* climb it out of—well. Yes. Fair," Odell said.

The guard stopped. Blinked. Something flickered behind his vacant eyes—not memory, maybe, but the *shape* where memory lived—and he sat down heavily on the steps like a man waking from a long nap, and began, wonderingly, to cry.

The fog recoiled a handspan. Then kept coming.

"Wren." Odell's voice had gone thin. "It's learning. Look—it's not touching the places we've named. It's flowing *around* them. It's mapping us."

"Then we talk faster."

* * *

Above them, the storm began.

Wren felt it before she heard it—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like copper coins and rain-on-hot-stone. Twelve years of swallowed weather breaking loose all at once. Lightning lit the open throne room doors from within, blue-white and furious, and thunder rolled down the palace steps and *shook the fog*, and for one glorious moment the silver tide shredded like smoke in wind.

Then the silence pushed back.

That was the worst part, Wren thought later. Not the fog. The silence. It came off the throne room like cold off ice, and where it met the thunder both simply *stopped*, and in the gaps between them she could hear—faintly, horribly—a voice. Not words. Something older than words. Something that sounded like a library burning underwater.

"He's doing it," Odell whispered. He had his map-book open against the gate, drawing blind, fast, lines crossing lines. "The unbinding. Can't you feel it? The whole palace just—*inhaled*."

"What does an unbinding feel like?"

"Like the moment after you sneeze," Odell said solemnly. "Only for reality."

Wren laughed—a wild, cracked laugh, the kind you laugh while holding a gate closed against the end of remembering—and threw more words at the fog. Names. All the names she could carry. Her father's name. Her own. The names of streets in Ironhollow, the names of charms August sold with ridiculous names he'd invented himself, *Widow's Patience* and *Traveler's Little Lie* and one called *Come-Home-Soon* that he'd never charged anyone for, because he said homesickness was the one ailment that shouldn't cost anything—

The fog broke against their voices like surf against a lighthouse. And still it came.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight—or what passed for midnight under a starless sky—the voice in the silence found words.

Wren heard them through the bronze, through the stone, through her own ribs:

***Villalobos.***

Then, softer, almost tender, the way a knife is tender:

***August.***

"No," Wren breathed.

"It's talking to him." Odell had gone gray. "It never talks. It *eats*. If it's talking, it's—"

"Bargaining." Wren's stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, into whatever dark thing the palace was built over. Of course it was bargaining. That was how you unmade a man who had spent twelve years wanting one simple thing—to be allowed home again. You didn't attack him. You *offered*.

She gripped the gate handle—the real one now, warm and solid under her hand because she and Odell had remembered it into being—and held on.

*Say no,* she thought, with everything in her. *You stubborn, self-deprecating, secretly generous old storm-crow. Say no.*

* * *

What happened next, she would tell a hundred times in the years after, and every telling would be true and none would be complete.

The storm above the palace gathered itself into a single column of light and rain and roaring air, and inside it—she was somehow certain of this, though she couldn't see him—August Villalobos stood at the foot of the hollow throne, and he spoke.

Not lightning. Words.

His true name.

She didn't hear it. Nobody who survived that night could repeat it afterward; it slipped from the mind like water from a closed fist. But she heard the *effect*: the silence shrieked. Actually shrieked, a sound like tearing silk the size of the sky, because here was the one meal the Unremembered could never digest—a name given freely, owned completely, offered up as the key to its own prison. Aldous Vane had bound himself into the crown using his student's exile decree. And now that student stood in the same room, undoing the lock by turning himself into the key.

"You can take my memories," Wren imagined him saying, because she knew him, and this was exactly the sort of theatrical nonsense he'd say. "Every last one. But you can't take my *name*, because I've just spent it. Spent things can't be stolen."

The fog stopped moving.

All of it. Everywhere. The silver tide hung on the stairs like a held breath, and then, gently, the way frost melts in morning sun, it began to fall—as ordinary rain. Just rain. Warm, clean, smelling of the sea it had crossed and the stone it had missed.

And the memories went up.

Wren felt it happen the way you feel a fire take hold of a house you're standing outside of. Somewhere above her, August was feeding himself to the thing piece by piece—his childhood in the storm-country villages, his mother's lullabies, the academy, Aldous Vane's face, the taste of the tea he drank every morning in his little charm shop, twelve years of careful invisible days in Ironhollow, the night market, the stars going out, the girl with the ledger, the cartographer with the chalk—

"Don't take us," Wren whispered. "Take anything else. Take the tea. You hated the tea anyway."

But of course he'd already decided. He'd decided back at the gates, with that crooked half-smile. *Don't let there be a goodbye.* He was trusting them. Trusting the ward-keeper's daughter who remembered too much and the erased man who drew the world to hold what he couldn't.

The light peaked. The thunder cracked once more, so vast that every window in the capital rang in sympathy, every bell swung silent in its tower, every citizen sat up in bed knowing, without knowing why, that something lost was coming home.

Then—quiet.

Real quiet. The kind quiet is supposed to be.

* * *

Wren found him at the top of the stairs, crumpled at the foot of the dais like a coat dropped carelessly. Beside him lay a crown, split cleanly in two, and inside it—nothing. Just old gold and the faint impression of a man who had loved a country enough to become its prison warden, and was finally, finally allowed to stop.

On the throne itself, King Aldric stirred, blinked, and said, in the small bewildered voice of a man waking from a very long nap, "...Was I asleep? I feel as though I've been asleep for *years*."

"You have, Your Majesty," Odell said, kneeling, already sketching. "We'll bring you up to speed."

Wren knelt by August. His chest moved. That was something. His face was slack in a way faces shouldn't be—every expression wiped, every line smoothed, a page washed clean.

"August?" She took his hand. "August Villalobos?"

He opened his eyes. They were the same eyes—gray-green, storm-colored, always faintly amused at a joke the world hadn't told yet. But there was nobody behind them looking out. No wariness, no wit, no grief.

He looked at her hand holding his, and smiled politely, the smile you give a stranger.

"Hello," he said. "Should I... know you?"

And above the palace, through the parting clouds, the first star came back.

Then a second. A third—bright and cocky, right over the eastern towers, as if showing off.

Wren stared up at them, and August's hand loose in hers, and felt something settle in her chest: not despair. *Work.* The best kind. The kind you'd walk through fog to do.

"No," she said, wiping her eyes with her sleeve and laughing despite everything. "But you're going to. Sit still. This is a long story, and I remember all of it."
