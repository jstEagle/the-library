## 10. Carried in Story

*Wren*

The king woke like a man surfacing from deep water, and the first thing he said was not a royal decree or a prayer of thanks but simply, "I'm hungry," which Wren thought was probably the most honest thing any monarch had ever uttered in that throne room.

She stood at the foot of the dais with her father's ledger clutched to her chest, watching King Aldric press his palms against his own face as though checking whether it still fit. Behind him, the crown sat on the throne's armrest, empty now. Just gold. Just metal, dull as a kitchen pot. Whatever had lived inside it was gone—or rather, it had gone somewhere else, somewhere it could be fed by other means.

Somewhere, Wren thought, looking sideways at the man lying crumpled on the marble steps.

August Villalobos breathed. That was all she could say for certain. He breathed, and his chest rose and fell, and when Odell knelt beside him and said his name—said it carefully, the way you'd set down something breakable—August had looked up with the polite blankness of a stranger at a market stall.

"Do I know you?" he'd asked.

And Odell, who had known him for less than a month, had wept openly onto a royal cartographer's coat, which Wren suspected had never happened to that coat before.

Now they walked out of the palace together, the three of them and a king who kept stopping to touch doorframes as if reassuring himself walls were real. Outside, the sky was doing something Wren would remember for the rest of her life, which was saying something, because remembering things for the rest of her life was rather her specialty.

The stars were coming back.

Not all at once. They returned one by one, like lamps relit by a careful lamplighter working down an endless street. A bright one over the eastern towers. Then two more, shy, near where the moon should be. Each time a star reappeared, someone in the city below shouted, and the shout rolled across the rooftops like a wave, and another star seemed to answer it.

"Look," she said to August, pointing up. "That's the Harrow. My father taught me its stars when I was six. The plough-star, the ox-star, the two that make the handle."

August looked where she pointed, squinting. "Pretty," he offered, the way a man compliments weather he has no opinion about.

Wren's chest did something painful. But she'd made a decision on the palace steps, kneeling in the dust while storm-silence drained out of the air, and decisions were the one kind of memory she trusted absolutely.

"That's all right," she told him. "You'll learn them again. I'll teach you."

He gave her a small, puzzled smile—the smile of a man being handed a debt he didn't recall owing—and followed her down into a city that was slowly, star by star, remembering itself awake.

*Odell*

Odell Brack had been erased once. Formally, administratively, with ink and wax and a clerk's bored signature. He knew exactly how it worked: first your name went from the rolls, then your office, then your portrait in the gallery, until finally there was a shape-shaped absence where you used to be and everyone stepped around it without noticing.

Which was why, three days after the unbinding, sitting in a rebuilt map-room in the palace (the old one had reshaped itself into a linen closet sometime during the festivities), Odell began drawing August's face in the margin of every map he made.

"You can't keep this up forever," Wren said, watching him sketch. She was cataloguing her father's ledger for the royal archivists, who had received it with the reverence usually reserved for saints' bones. "People will think it's a signature."

"It is a signature." Odell shaded the line of the jaw—that stubborn jaw, always tilted slightly upward, as if listening for weather. "Every map I draw from now until I die will carry his face. Coastlines change. Borders crawl around like drunkards. But maps get copied, and margins survive, and someday some apprentice will wonder why every chart in the kingdom has the same man scowling out of it, and they'll ask, and someone will answer."

"Answer what?"

"Who is that?" Odell tapped the little portrait. "And the answer will be: that is August Villalobos, the storm-caller, who gave everything he was so the rest of us could keep what we have. And the apprentice will write it down, because apprentices always write everything down—it's their single redeeming quality—and so it will go on."

Wren was quiet a moment. "You talk about him like he's already a legend."

"He is a legend. He just doesn't know it yet." Odell blew a fleck of graphite off the page. "Which is, frankly, my favorite kind. Legends who know it become insufferable. See: most kings."

As if summoned, King Aldric wandered past the open door, wearing a plain wool coat and carrying his own breakfast, stopped, and backed up. "Brack. Is that—" He leaned in, squinting at the marginal portrait. "Is that the fellow? The one from the throne room?"

"The very same, Majesty."

"I keep trying to thank him," the king said, troubled, "and he keeps thanking *me*, and I don't know what for, and it's driving me mad." He rubbed his chin. "There's a hole where the gratitude should go. Like a tooth knocked out. You keep poking at it."

"I know, sire."

"What do you do about a thing like that?"

Odell considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "You tell the story anyway, Majesty. Even to him. Especially to him. Stories don't need the listener to remember anything. They only need him to be listening."

The king nodded slowly, as if this were the wisest statecraft he'd heard in years—which, given the state of the court lately, it may well have been. "Then I'll tell it at supper tonight. And every supper after." He paused at the door. "Does he like mutton?"

"No one likes court mutton, sire."

"Hm. I'll have them roast something properly, then." And he went off, humming, a king rehearsing a story the way other men rehearsed speeches.

*August*

August Villalobos—who had been informed, gently, repeatedly, that this was his name—woke each morning in a guest room of the palace to find that the world had continued existing without his permission, and that this was apparently fine.

He remembered nothing before the throne room. Nothing before the silence and the storm and the sensation—still vivid, the only vivid thing he owned—of being *poured out*. Everything else was secondhand. His hands were described to him as a charm-seller's hands; the calluses said so. His accent was said to be Ironhollow market-dialect with something older underneath. He was told he had been brave, which embarrassed him, and that he had been exiled, which confused him, and that the woman who visited him each morning with tea and a ledger full of notes had loved her father very much and had decided, for reasons he couldn't fathom but didn't dare question, to love August by proxy.

It was a strange arrangement, being a memory he wasn't allowed to keep.

"So," Wren said this particular morning, settling into the chair by the window, opening the ledger to a page soft-cornered from handling. "Where were we?"

"The drowned quarter," August guessed. It was always a guess. "The cartographer."

"Close. We'd already got the cartographer. Yesterday was Hollow Country." She smoothed the page. "Right. There was a village called Merrow's Bend, and everyone in it had forgotten their names mid-word—you'd see people smiling at each other with their mouths half-open, stuck between syllables. And there was a little girl, maybe seven, who'd forgotten her mother's face but not her fear of the fog coming down the hill. She grabbed your coat and wouldn't let go."

August looked at his hands. One of them, he noticed, curled slightly, as though it remembered holding something small and terrified.

"What did I do?"

"You did what you swore you'd never do again." Wren's voice went quiet, reverent, the way she always went quiet here. "You called a storm. First time in twelve years. You put yourself between that fog and everyone behind you, and the lightning drew a circle, and inside that circle nobody forgot anything. But it cost you. Every storm-calling takes from the caller, and yours took—" she hesitated, then finished honestly, because honesty was her whole religion— "it took your memories of home. Your mother's kitchen. The sound of rain on the roof of the house you grew up in. By the time we reached the capital, you were paying for that shield out of what was left."

August sat with that. Outside the window, a star that hadn't existed last week burned steadily over the eastern wall, and somewhere below, the city was shouting about it again.

"And then the throne room," he said.

"Then the throne room." She turned the page. "But that's tomorrow's chapter. One a day. Doctor's orders—Odell's orders, actually. He says if we pour it all in at once it won't hold. Says memory's like mortar. Sets better in layers."

"Wise man, the cartographer."

"Insufferable man. Also wise." She smiled and tapped the ledger. "One more thing before breakfast. Repeat it back to me. Who are you?"

August took a breath. This part was strange still, and would be strange for a long time—he understood that now—but it was also, in a way he couldn't explain and didn't try to, the best part of his day. Better than the mutton, certainly.

"My name is August Villalobos," he recited. "I was a storm-caller, exiled twelve years ago for a crime that was actually a kindness. I hid in Ironhollow selling charms that mostly didn't work. I crossed Hollow Country with the bravest girl in the kingdom and the only honest mapmaker in the realm. I faced the thing in the crown, and I gave it everything I had, and it wasn't enough to destroy me, because—"

He paused. This was the part Wren insisted on. This was the part she'd written in the ledger in letters larger than all the others.

"—because the people I saved carried me forward in story instead of memory," he finished, "and a story, unlike a memory, belongs to everyone."

"There," Wren said, satisfied, closing the book. "See? It's taking root. Last week you stumbled on 'kindness.' Now look at you."

Down in the courtyard, Odell's voice drifted up, arguing cheerfully with a stonemason about the proper orientation of a commemorative sundial. Above the eastern tower, another new star flickered into being, and the city roared its nightly approval, and somewhere in the palace kitchens a king was instructing cooks on the correct roasting of a celebratory goose.

August felt all of it land on him like light rain—unfamiliar, gentle, soaking slowly in.

"Wren," he said, as she gathered the ledger. "Tomorrow. When you tell me about the throne room. Will you stay a while after? There's a question I've been meaning to ask."

"Ask it now."

"No." He shook his head, and found—to his surprise, to his enormous, inexplicable relief—that he was smiling. "Tomorrow. Some things should be remembered properly the first time."
