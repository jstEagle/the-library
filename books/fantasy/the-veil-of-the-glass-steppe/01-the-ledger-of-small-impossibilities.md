## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The trouble with cataloging impossible things, Sebastian Winterbourne had learned in her six years as the Guild's last apprentice, was that they refused to sit still long enough to be counted.

She stood at the third-floor inventory room with a clipboard, a stub of pencil, and the particular patience of a woman who had been overlooked so thoroughly and for so long that she had begun to mistake it for a vocation. Rain ticked against the windows in a rhythm she suspected was not entirely natural—the Guild of Impossible Things occupied the top four floors of a building that had, according to every map and deed in the city, been condemned in 1931—and somewhere below her, the building itself creaked like an old man shifting in his chair.

"Item one," she said aloud, because the ledgers preferred to be spoken to. "One jar of borrowed thunder. Provenance: the storm of March the eleventh, borrowed from the sky over Hessel Bay, never returned."

The jar sat on the shelf where it had sat for forty years: a squat preserve jar, cloudy with age, containing what appeared to be nothing at all until you looked directly at it—then something moved inside, slow and heavy, like a cat turning over in its sleep. When Sebastian pressed her palm against the glass, the thunder pressed back.

*Still there,* she wrote. *Grumpy.*

The Guild of Impossible Things had once kept three hundred apprentices, nine masters, and a waiting list two years long. It had negotiated with weather, recovered lost years from locked attics, and once—though this was disputed—taught a river to hold a grudge. Now it had Master Oren Voss, who forgot more each week; Mistress Alda Fenwick, who had retired from impossibility in all but name and spent her days growing tomatoes that were, she insisted, perfectly ordinary, though they ripened out of season out of sheer stubbornness; a cook named Bram who believed he worked in a boarding house and could not be persuaded otherwise without upsetting him; and Sebastian, who did the counting.

Nobody had appointed her to do the counting. That was rather the point. In a guild full of people who could walk through walls or trade their shadows, the girl who showed up early and kept meticulous records became, paradoxically, invisible. Sebastian had learned to wear invisibility the way other people wore coats: initially uncomfortable, eventually warm.

"Item two," she said, moving down the shelf. "One key to a house that burned."

The key was iron, old-fashioned, with a bow shaped like a bird mid-flight. The house it opened—a crooked yellow house on Lantern Row—had burned to its foundations twenty-two years ago, taking the family inside it into whatever comes after houses. And yet the key still turned, if you held it up to any door and thought hard about yellow paint and someone singing off-key in the kitchen. Sebastian had tried it once, on the Guild's own door, on a night when she was lonelier than usual. For half a second she had smelled woodsmoke and bread and heard, unmistakably, a woman laughing at her own joke.

Then the key had gone cold in her hand, embarrassed, and she'd put it back on the shelf and written *functional, handle gently* in the ledger, which was what she always wrote when what she meant was *this hurts a little, but beautifully.*

That was the thing nobody told you about impossible things. They weren't fireworks. They were mostly small, and mostly sad, and mostly needed someone to look after them.

* * *

By noon she had reached the middle shelves, where the inventory thinned out in a way that made her chest ache if she let it. The ledgers from the Guild's great years filled an entire wall—forty-seven volumes in green leather, their spines stamped in gold, recording wonders the way other institutions recorded grain shipments. The current ledger, Volume Forty-Eight, was barely an inch thick after eleven years.

She ran her finger down today's page. *Borrowed thunder: stable. Key to Lantern Row: stable. One umbrella that opens onto yesterday: unstable, leaks.* The umbrella had begun leaking recently—not rain, but Tuesdays. Anyone standing under it came away damp with a day they'd already spent, and Alda had complained of arriving at breakfast with the taste of a lunch she hadn't eaten yet. Sebastian had quarantined it in a closet with a sign reading DO NOT OPEN ON WEEKDAYS, which was, she admitted, only half a joke.

"You're talking to the shelves again."

Sebastian didn't jump. She'd learned that much. She turned and found Alda Fenwick in the doorway, holding a tomato like a scepter, her silver hair escaping its bun in the manner of hair that had won several arguments with combs over the decades.

"I'm conducting the inventory," Sebastian said.

"You're gossiping with a jar." Alda came in anyway, because Alda always came in anyway, and peered over Sebastian's shoulder at the ledger. Her eyes, watery at the edges but sharp at the center, moved down the entries. "Hm. You've given the thunder a temperament."

"It has one."

"Everything has one. Doesn't mean we write it down." But Alda didn't tell her to cross it out, and Sebastian noted, with the quiet satisfaction of a person collecting small victories, that the older woman read the entry twice.

"How is Master Voss this morning?" Sebastian asked, because it was safer than asking how are you, a question Alda answered with weather reports or tomato statistics.

Alda's face did the thing it always did at the master's name—a tightening around the mouth, like a drawstring pulled. "He asked me twice today where the east wing went."

"There is no east wing."

"I know that. You know that. He built the east wing himself, in 'sixty-one, out of a Tuesday afternoon and some very good intentions." She set the tomato down on the table, where it sat radiating disapproval. "This morning he stood where it used to be and wept at the wall. Then he thanked me for my kindness and asked if I was new."

Sebastian looked down at her ledger. The pencil felt suddenly very small in her hand, a toothpick offered against a tide.

"He was lucid last week," she said. "He corrected my Latin."

"He's lucid most weeks. That's the cruelty of it. If he faded all at once we could grieve properly and get on with things." Alda picked up her tomato again, cradling it. "Instead he surfaces and dives, surfaces and dives, like something drowning slowly in clear water. And we stand on the bank making notes."

Sebastian said nothing. There was a rule in the Guild, unwritten but iron-clad, that you did not speak of the war. Not because it was forbidden—nothing here was forbidden anymore, that was part of the problem—but because whenever anyone tried, Oren would surface from wherever his mind had gone and say, in a voice stripped of everything but urgency: *Don't. Don't write that down.* And then he would forget why he'd said it, and everyone would pretend he hadn't, and the conversation would die quietly in a corner like something smothered.

She wrote instead: *Master Voss — memory unstable. See attached concerns.* There was no attachment. There never was. But writing *see attached* made the concern feel official, filed, held by something sturdier than her own chest.

"Alda," she said, before the woman could shuffle out. "Do you ever wonder—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Wondering was dangerous; wondering led to questions, and questions led to the wall where Oren had wept. "Never mind."

"No," Alda said. "Go on. Do I ever wonder what?"

"Do you ever wonder why the Guild is dying? Not just—old. Dying. We used to broker impossibilities for half the continent. Now our inventory fits in one room, and half of it is unstable, and the rent—" She stopped again, because the rent was another thing that didn't bear thinking about. The Guild paid its rent, every quarter, promptly and without question, in currency that no bank in the city recognized and no shopkeeper would take. Nobody knew who cashed it. Nobody asked. The landlord, whoever he was, had never once complained.

Alda was quiet for a moment. Outside, the unnatural rain continued its ticking, patient as a clock that wanted something.

"I stopped wondering in 1954," Alda said finally. "It was easier." And she left, tomato under her arm, her footsteps fading down a corridor that had been longer yesterday. Sebastian was almost sure it had been longer yesterday.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the deep shelves—the ones against the north wall, where the objects nobody wanted to be responsible for lived in the permanent dusk of a window that showed a view of a garden that had never existed anywhere. Sebastian liked the deep shelves best, though she'd have bitten her tongue before admitting it. Up here, among the misfit miracles, she wasn't the invisible girl with the clipboard. She was staff. Possibly management.

There was the mirror that showed you as a child saw you—taller, braver, smelling faintly of adventure. There was the doorbell that rang for visitors who meant to come but died on the way; it had rung three times in Sebastian's tenure, and each time she had lit a candle in the stairwell and said, "I'm sorry, they're not home," which was the truest lie she knew. There was a bottle of a sea that had been drunk dry by a drought in another century, one swallow remaining. There was a pair of gloves knitted by a woman for a son who never came home from the border, which stayed warm forever, and which Sebastian was not permitted to touch, though she touched them every time, briefly, the way you touch a bruise to check it's still yours.

And at the very end of the deep shelves, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with twine gone soft as ribbon: the folios. The prophecies.

Sebastian had cataloged them her first year—eleven volumes, dated between 1602 and 1899, donated by a dying seer who'd specified only that they be kept *dry and unloved*. They were, as far as anyone knew, nonsense. Most prophecy was. A seer sees ten thousand futures and writes down the ones with good imagery, and the result is poetry that occasionally frightens people. Sebastian had entered them in the ledger with the brisk neutrality she brought to all her work:

*Prophecy folios, eleven. Contents: unverifiable. Condition: fair. Recommendation: keep dry, keep dusted, do not consult.*

*Do not consult* had been her own addition. It had seemed professional at the time.

Now, six years later, she untied the twine on the last of them—the 1899 folio, the newest and the least impressive, full of the florid doom-mongering typical of the century's end—because it had developed a wobble in its binding, and a wobbling book in a damp room was a book with a deadline. This was the other half of her devotion: not just the counting, but the keeping. Books outlast their readers only if somebody intervenes.

She carried it to the worktable by the false window, where the light was good and the garden-that-wasn't bloomed in perpetual late summer, and began, carefully, to cut the broken stitches.

The Guild was silent around her. Bram was downstairs burning lunch with the serene confidence of a man who believed he was feeding boarders. Alda was in her greenhouse arguing with the tomatoes about November. Oren was wherever Oren went these days—surfacing, diving, surfacing. Rain ticked at the glass. The thunder jar, two floors up, grumbled in its sleep.

It should have been a perfect afternoon. It was, Sebastian would think later, looking back across the whole bright terrible year that followed—the kind of ordinary, dusty, unremarkable afternoon that a person gets exactly one of.

Because as she eased the spine open to rebind it, pressing the brittle pages flat, a loose leaf slipped free and fluttered to the table like a molting bird. She caught it before it fell further. A single page, older than the rest, inserted sometime after the folio's original binding—she could see the stitch holes didn't match.

She almost set it aside. Inserted pages were common; seers were notorious amenders. She almost smoothed it back in and got on with the work, because the work was safe, the work was hers, the work was a life built entirely out of small dutiful motions that never required her to look up.

But the handwriting caught her eye. Not the seer's hand—that tight, self-satisfied script she'd seen on a hundred pages. This was different: hurried, slanted, urgent, the letters of someone writing faster than wisdom advised.

And the date at the top of the page was a date Sebastian knew intimately, because she had written it herself that morning at the head of the inventory ledger, the way she wrote it every morning.

Tonight's date. Written in a book bound before her grandmother was born.

She told herself it was coincidence. Dates repeat; calendars cycle; a hundred years makes a strange arithmetic. She told herself that while her finger slid down the cramped lines to the middle of the page, where the hurried handwriting steadied for one phrase, inked darker, pressed deep, as though the writer had wanted it carved rather than merely written:

*...and the Door Out shall be found by S. Winterbourne, who counts what remains.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, in the same desperate hand:

*Tell her to start before the clocks agree.*

Sebastian stood in the silent inventory room with the page trembling in her hands, and outside the false window the garden that had never existed went on blooming in its endless, patient summer, and somewhere above her the rain changed its rhythm—just slightly, just for a moment—to something that sounded, unmistakably, like footsteps crossing a ceiling where there was no floor.

She did not tell anyone. That, too, was a decision, though she wouldn't understand until much later that it was the first one she'd ever really made.

But that night, alone in her narrow room under the eaves, she took out the inventory ledger and, beneath the day's neat columns of jars and keys and umbrellas, wrote a single line in her smallest, steadiest hand:

*Item unnumbered. One date, returned after sixty-six years. Provenance unknown. Condition: mine.*

And beneath that, because she was who she was—diligent, thorough, invisible—she added: *Begin tomorrow.*

The clocks, as it happened, had other ideas.
