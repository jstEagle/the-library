## 2. Tonight's Date, Written Long Ago

The folio arrived on my desk the way all the worst jobs did: by way of Master Voss, who set it down with both hands and the particular expression of a man handing someone a live wasp.

"The Aldermoor bequest," he said. "Came in this morning. Water damage along the spine. You'll want to rebind before it goes into the deep archive."

I looked at it. It was a prophecy folio — that much I could tell from the catalog card clipped to its cover, which read *Aldermoor, E., comp., 1899. Prophecies, miscellaneous. Handle as per Section Nine* — and Section Nine of the Guild of Impossible Things' handling manual had been written by a man who clearly believed prophecies were flammable, vindictive, and possibly listening.

"Couldn't Tam take it?" I asked. "Tam likes the old ones."

"Tam is cataloguing the thunder jar," Oren said, "because last week he shook it." He paused at my door, one hand on the frame, and for a moment he wore the expression he sometimes got when the guild's purpose slipped through his fingers like water — that brief, terrible blankness, followed by the fierce reassertion of duty over understanding. "We keep what we're given, Winterbourne. That's the whole of it."

"That's not the whole of anything, Master."

"It's enough," he said, and went down the stairs to wherever it was masters went when they couldn't remember why they'd come up.

I turned the folio over in my hands. It was small — smaller than I expected, given its reputation. Guild rumor held that the Aldermoor collection contained a prophecy concerning the end of the world, another concerning the return of the king across the water, and at least one concerning a pie that would never go cold, which Tam maintained was the only genuinely frightening one. The binding was cracked calfskin gone soft as an old glove, and when I lifted it, something inside shifted with a dry whisper, like sand deciding which way to settle.

"Well," I told it, because I talked to the work the way other people talked to cats, "let's see what you've got."

* * *

Rebinding is slow work, and I love it for exactly that reason. There is a honesty in it. A book comes to you broken and you make it whole, or as near to whole as honest materials allow, and nobody asks you to explain yourself while you do it. For four hours that morning I was not the apprentice who fetched the tea and signed for the deliveries and was forgotten in three conversations out of five. I was simply hands, needle, linen thread, and paste.

The water damage was worse than Oren had let on. The spine glue had failed entirely, and the sewing supports were rotted through, so I had to take the whole thing apart — lifting each gathering of pages free, keeping them in order with brass weights, washing the residue of the old adhesive from the spine folds with a sponge barely damp. The paper itself surprised me. It was good stock, rag-based, the kind that outlives its owners, but every page carried a faint tide-mark of stain along its lower edge, as if the book had once stood in shallow water for a long time. Or as if the water had come up to meet it.

The text was hand-copied, which was common enough for the period — printing a prophecy felt, to most compilers, like shouting it — in a cramped, patient hand that slanted slightly uphill from left to right, as though the scribe had been climbing as he wrote. The prophecies themselves were the usual fare: riddling, grandiose, obsessed with kings and borders and comets. *When the twin rivers forget their names.* *The crown that is weighed and found borrowed.* I copied none of them down. Prophecies are like weather; noting them changes nothing and makes you feel foolish later.

It was past noon when I reached the final gathering, and found the tide-stain there darkest, soaking up into the text from below like something rising to be read.

And there — beside a date written in the same cramped, climbing hand — I stopped.

Not a riddle-date. Not *when the comet returns* or *in the reign of the third successor*. An actual date, the kind a person writes on a letter: day, month, year.

Today's date.

Except the year was wrong. The year was sixty years ago.

I sat very still, the way you do when a floorboard creaks somewhere it shouldn't. Then I did what any sensible person does with an impossible document: I checked my work. I checked it twice more after that. I counted the months against the guild calendar hanging crooked by the window, the one Tam had annotated with birthdays and the anniversary of the thunder jar incident. I verified the date conversion tables in Marguerite's *Concordance of Calendars, Reformed and Otherwise*, because Vellmark had reformed its calendar twice and half the archive ran on the old reckoning. I checked until checking became its own small madness, and the answer did not change.

Sixty years ago, a man named Aldermoor — or whoever copied for him — had written today. Not approximately. Not in some prophetic rounding-off. Today.

My name was beneath it.

Now, understand: *Sebastian* is not so rare a name that finding it in an old book should stop a heart. And *Winterbourne* appears in the census often enough. But there, in the folio, in that patient climbing hand, was *S. Winterbourne*, and beside it, in the same ink, no gap left for doubt:

*S. Winterbourne walks the Glass Steppe to find the Door Out.*

I read it perhaps forty times. I will admit that freely. I read it the way you press a bruise — not because it feels good, but because you need to confirm it's real, and then confirm again that confirming didn't help.

The Glass Steppe. Every child in this quarter grew up on the Glass Steppe the way children elsewhere grow up on bogeymen. It lay east of everything, past the salt flats, a plain of glass where nothing grew and nothing lived and the light came up wrong out of the ground. Cartographers marked it with the little convention reserved for hazards: a scatter of dots and a warning word. *Unsurveyed.* Nobody went there. Nobody had ever, in living memory, gone there, and the reason nobody gave was that nobody knew one, which is how a country teaches itself not to ask questions.

And the Door Out. That phrase rang no bell at all — and yet it rang *somewhere*, low, like a note struck in a room upstairs. I had the peculiar sensation of having known it once and filed the knowing somewhere I could no longer reach.

"S. Winterbourne," I said aloud, to the empty workroom. My voice sounded wrong in it. Too loud and too small at once.

Because here is the thing about being me, here in the Guild of Impossible Things: I am the one who watches. I am the one who catalogs, who signs for parcels, who notices that we are down to eleven jars of preserved impossibility and that Master Voss has begun writing the same entry twice. Watching is my whole occupation. I watch the ledgers; the ledgers do not watch me. No entry anywhere in the guild's hundred years of records concerns me beyond *apprentice, third grade, wages quarterly*.

And now a book older than my grandmother had written my name next to a date, and the date had waited sixty years with the patience of all paper, and arrived.

* * *

I finished the rebinding. I want that on the record, whatever record there may be: even with my hands not entirely steady, I sewed the new supports, pasted the spine, and set the boards true, because work is work and a book deserves better than to suffer for my nerves. By the time I trimmed the last of the paste squeeze-out, the afternoon light had gone long and amber across the workbench, and the folio sat closed under its brass weights looking exactly as innocent as any object that has ever ruined a person's evening.

Section Nine said prophecies went directly to the deep archive upon treatment. No reading. No copying. *Handle as per Section Nine.*

I did not take it to the deep archive.

I am not proud of that, and I am not ashamed of it either. Both of those would require a decision, and I hadn't made one. I had merely — misplaced my enthusiasm for compliance. Temporarily. I wrapped the folio in its cloth and put it in the bottom drawer of my own desk, under the spare thread spools, which is where things go in the guild when they are waiting for someone to become brave.

Then I went and made tea, because ritual is what holds a person together at the seams, and I am, professionally speaking, very interested in seams.

* * *

The trouble with secrets, I discovered that evening, is that they change the acoustics of a building.

The guildhouse is old — older than the guild's own founding ledger claims, which is a sentence I had always found charmingly sloppy and now found ominous — and it talks. Pipes knock. Stairs report footsteps. In six years I had learned its vocabulary so thoroughly that I could tell you whether Tam or Marguerite was on the landing by the particular complaint the third step made.

That night, every sound seemed aimed at me. When Oren came up to bank the study fire, his tread sounded like a question. When Marguerite called goodnight down the corridor, her voice seemed to pause on my door a half-second longer than usual. Ridiculous, all of it. Nobody knew. The secret sat in a drawer under some thread, minding its own business.

But secrets don't mind their own business. Secrets sit in drawers and *radiate*.

I stayed late in the workroom on the pretense of conditioning leather. What I actually did was take out the guild's oldest catalog — not the ledgers, which are accounts of trade, but the accession book, the great vellum-paged register of everything the guild has ever taken custody of — and I looked up Elias Aldermoor.

He appeared exactly twice. Once in 1899: *Bequest received. One (1) folio, prophecies, misc. Donor deceased. No kin.* And once, thirty years earlier, in a list of subscribing members whose annual dues kept the guild's lamps lit: *E. Aldermoor, surveyor, retired.*

Surveyor.

Of what? The entry didn't say. Surveyors map things. Things that exist have coordinates. The Glass Steppe was famously unsurveyed — famously, insistently, in the way of things everyone has agreed very hard not to look at.

I closed the accession book and sat in the dark of the workroom with the lamp turned low, and I thought about the shape of the coincidence until thinking stopped helping. A man surveys something no one surveys. Thirty years later he leaves behind a book that names a stranger not yet born, walking toward that same something, on a date the man could not have known. Unless he did know it. Unless knowing dates was part of what he surveyed.

There is a particular loneliness in being watched for. Being watched *at* — that I understood; I'd spent years watching at other people, and I knew it required nothing of its object. But to be watched *for* meant that somewhere back down the years, someone had expected me. Had counted on my arriving. Had written me into a plan the way you write an appointment, and had then died comfortably in 1899 without leaving instructions, leaving me instead with a drawer full of prophecy and no idea whether I was the hero of the arrangement or merely the labor.

I blew out the lamp. In the black, the workroom settled around me, pipes knocking their old complaints, and I realized I was whispering to myself, which I only ever do when the guild is empty and I believe myself unobserved.

"*The Door Out*," I whispered. Out of what?

* * *

Morning arrived grey and smelling of rain that hadn't committed. I came down early, earlier than anyone, and stood at my desk with my hand on the drawer handle for a long moment — long enough that I had to admit I was deciding, and decide.

Here is what I decided, and I stand by it still: a prophecy is a claim, and the guild does not act on claims. The guild acts on evidence. Sixty years ago a man wrote my name; fine; men write many things. Before I took one single step toward the salt flats and the unsurveyed plain, I would know more. Who Aldermoor surveyed. Why a guild of impossible things pays its rent, as I had noticed once and never managed to stop noticing, in coins stamped with the crest of a country our own treaty archives describe as dissolved.

So I did not carry the folio to the deep archive. Instead I carried it deeper — down past the archive proper, to the cellar stacks where the guild keeps the documents too awkward to catalog, and I began, quietly, methodically, with all the diligence of a woman who has spent six years perfecting the art of being overlooked, to search.

I did not tell Oren. I did not tell Tam, who would have shaken something, or Marguerite, who would have told Oren. I told no one.

Which is why it unsettled me so badly, an hour later, climbing back up into the grey daylight with dust on my sleeves and nothing yet found but a growing list of questions — to hear Master Voss's voice from the study, raised just enough to carry, saying to someone I couldn't see:

"No. She hasn't said anything to you either? ... Then she doesn't know yet. Good. Let her come to it in her own time."

A pause. Then, softer, in the tone of a man agreeing to something he'd rather not:

"Yes. I remember what the book says happens tonight."
