## 3. The Master Who Forgets

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its master the way a drafty house keeps a candle: carefully, anxiously, and with the constant expectation that any moment would put him out.

Sebastian climbed the stairs to Oren Voss's study at half past eight, carrying his tea on a tray with the particular concentration of someone transporting nitroglycerin. Not because the tea was dangerous — though it was, in its way; she'd brewed it from leaves that had been picked, according to their label, "on a morning that has not yet happened" — but because mornings were when Oren was best, and she'd learned not to waste them. By evening he could still recite the guild's founding oath in four dead languages. But ask him what he'd eaten for lunch and he would look at you with the polite, straining attention of a man trying to remember a dream through a window that kept fogging over.

She knocked with her elbow, because knocking with her hand meant setting down the tray, and setting down the tray meant the tea went cold, and cold tea made him sad in a way he couldn't name, which was worse than sadness with an address.

"Enter," said a voice from within. It was a good voice still, deep and worn smooth as a stair rail, though these days it sometimes lost the thread of a sentence halfway through, like a kite string slipping from a fist.

Oren Voss sat behind a desk built from the door of some older, larger building — he'd never say which one, and lately Sebastian suspected he no longer knew — surrounded by the organized chaos of a man who had once been the most meticulous person in Vellmark. Ledgers fanned around him like sleeping birds. A magnifying glass lay across an open folio. His spectacles were pushed up into white hair that seemed to be staging a quiet rebellion against the comb.

"Ah," he said, looking up. His face did the thing it always did when he saw her: a flicker of search, a half-second of *who is this girl*, and then the arrival — warm, certain, relieved. "Sebastian. My last apprentice."

"Your only apprentice," she corrected, setting down the tray.

"My *last* apprentice." He tapped the desk with one knuckle, as if the distinction were load-bearing. "There were others. Eleven of them, once upon a time. You're the last, which makes you the most important by simple arithmetic of scarcity." He smiled, and the smile was genuine, and something behind it flickered like the candle she'd been thinking about. "Sit. Sit. Or don't. Do we have chairs?"

"We have four chairs, Master. One of them hums."

"Does it? Which one?"

"The green one. It hums a note that isn't anywhere else in the building."

"Ah, yes." He nodded slowly. "We acquired it from a widow whose husband tuned pianos. After he died, all the music left the instruments and went into the furniture. She couldn't afford to keep a house full of humming." He paused. His brow furrowed, gently, like weather moving in. "Or perhaps I invented that. It's getting difficult to tell, these days. The stories sit so close together they lean on each other."

Sebastian poured the tea. The steam rose and briefly spelled out a word in a language she didn't recognize before dissipating. Neither of them remarked on it. This was another thing she'd learned: at the Guild of Impossible Things, you let the impossible pass without commentary, or it got self-conscious.

"Master," she said, settling into the humming chair's silent sister, "I rebinding the 1899 prophecy folio yesterday. The stitching was rotten all the way through."

"Mm." He drank his tea. Outside the window, the city of Corvane went about its morning — carts rattling, gulls arguing over the fish market, the customs house clock striking the hour with two extra strokes it had been adding for as long as anyone could remember and nobody could explain. "And did it say anything interesting?"

"It's prophecies, Master. They mostly say fog."

"Fog," Oren agreed, "is the national literature of prophecy." He set down his cup. And then, because he was Oren Voss, and because some part of him — some stubborn, load-bearing part — needed to do this every morning the way a man with a bad leg needs to test it, he folded his hands and said:

"The Guild of Impossible Things exists to—"

He stopped.

Sebastian waited. She had learned to wait well. Waiting was most of apprenticeship; the rest was dusting objects that objected to it.

He tried again, slower, feeling for the words like a man crossing ice. "The Guild exists to... preserve. To preserve those things which..." His hands came apart and reassembled themselves. "Which the world has decided it can do without."

"Yes," Sebastian said softly.

"No—" He shook his head, frustrated, and the frustration aged him ten years in a breath. "No, that's the motto. That's what we say. But why, Sebastian? Why must the world's discards be kept? What are they *for*?" He looked at her, and his eyes — gray, sharp, formerly the sharpest instrument in the building — were doing something terrifying. They were asking honestly. He genuinely didn't know. "You spend your days among the inventory. The jar of borrowed thunder. The key to the burned house. Tell me: what is the purpose of the shelf?"

"To keep things safe until they're wanted again."

"And who wants them?"

"Someone. Eventually. That's the premise."

"The premise." He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only the hollow ring of a word dropped down a well. "Yes. We have premises. Premises are what remain when the reasons have been carried off." He pressed his fingertips to his eyes. "Forgive me. Some mornings the whole shape of it slips. I know we must exist — I feel it in my chest like a second heartbeat, *must*, *must*, the way a compass feels north — but I cannot remember for the life of me *why*. As if the answer were written somewhere and the page torn out. As if—" He stopped again. His gaze drifted toward the window, toward the east, where beyond the rooftops and the harbor cranes the land rose into hills that the maps called, with bureaucratic blandness, the Unassigned Territories.

And Sebastian watched it happen. She had watched it happen maybe forty times now, and it never got easier, and she had begun, guiltily, to take notes.

Whenever Oren spoke of the war — and he hadn't said the word yet, not once in her three years at the guild, not ever, not even in the oldest ledgers' margins did anyone write it plainly — whenever he came near it, his memory frayed. Like cloth rubbed thin. Like a recording scratched at the same groove. He would reach for a fact — a date, a name, the reason the guild's eastern wall bore scorch marks nobody discussed — and the fact would simply *not be there*, replaced by a smooth, glossy blankness, like the surface of—

Like glass.

"The war," Sebastian said, very carefully, testing.

Oren blinked. "What war?"

The question landed in the room and just sat there. He wasn't being evasive. She watched his face and understood, with a cold sliding sensation under her ribs, that he was asking sincerely. Somewhere in the last three years, the word had stopped meaning anything to him. Somewhere in the last fifty years, it had stopped meaning anything to *everyone* — but everyone else had never had it to begin with, and Oren had, and she could see the socket where it used to live.

"I misspoke," she said. "The — the border troubles. Long ago. Before either of us."

"Border troubles." He frowned, reaching. She could almost hear the sound of his mind walking down a long corridor and finding doors painted onto the wall. "There was... something. There was a treaty. There was a signing — I have the pen! We keep the pen, it's in inventory, case eleven, the pen that signed—" His voice picked up speed, hopeful, sprinting toward the memory — and then slowed, and stalled, and died. "...the pen that signed. Something. Somewhere. To somebody." He looked down at his own hands as if they might confess on his behalf. "I remember the weight of it. I remember my brother standing beside me. I remember that it was raining and that I thought the rain meant something, and I cannot tell you what we signed or why or what it cost, and Sebastian—" His voice cracked, gently, like old binding glue. "Sebastian, I am the *Guildmaster*. If I cannot hold it, no one can. And it isn't age. I want to be clear about that. Age takes memories one at a time, politely, like a pickpocket. This took them *all at once*, whole rooms of them, and left the wallpaper."

*This.* He'd said *this*, not *that*. As if the forgetting were a thing with location. As if it were ongoing.

Sebastian's mouth was dry. "When did it start? The — taking."

Oren considered the question with the gravity he gave everything, which was one of the things she loved about him, and which was also, she suspected, why the forgetting hurt him so much more than it would hurt a sloppier man. He had been a curator of memory. Now he was a museum with the exhibits removed.

"It started," he said slowly, "when the guild started dying. No — before. With. Alongside. I've never been able to establish the order of events, because establishing order requires a sequence, and sequences require—" He waved a hand. "You see the difficulty."

"The guild started dying," Sebastian repeated. "Master, when I joined, there were nine members. Now there are five. The rent comes due in a currency I can't find on any exchange. Half our funding letters arrive postmarked from towns that—" She hesitated. "That I can't find on any map, either. I assumed the maps were cheap."

"The maps," Oren said, "are excellent. That's what frightens me." He turned in his chair and looked out the window again, and Sebastian followed his gaze, and for a moment — just a moment, the light had to be exactly right, slanting off the harbor through morning haze — the city looked *wrong*. Not damaged. Not ruined. Wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the eyes have been repainted by someone working from a written description instead of the face. The streets lay in patterns that almost repeated. The gulls wheeled in loops that felt rehearsed. Somewhere below, a fishmonger cried his wares, and the cry was beautiful and old, and Sebastian realized she could not have said, if asked, what fish he was selling. None of them could. It wasn't a thing anyone checked.

"You've noticed it too," Oren said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Noticed what, Master?"

"Don't do that." He turned back to her, and for a flash his eyes were young and fierce and absolutely lucid, the Guildmaster of legend looking out through the ruin of himself like a lamp through fogged glass. "Don't learn the guild's oldest trick. Don't look directly at the wrongness and then look away and call it noticing nothing. I trained eleven apprentices before you, and every one of them learned to *not-notice* faster than they learned anything else, and I let them, God forgive me, because not-noticing kept them safe. But you—" He pointed at her, finger trembling slightly. "You read the ledgers. All of them. Even the ones in the sealed cases. Especially those. You notice things and then you write them down in that little book you think I don't know about."

Sebastian felt her ears go hot. "It's inventory notes."

"It's *evidence*," Oren said, "and it's the finest instinct you have, and I am about to give you the worst advice of your career, which is: keep noticing, and stop writing it down where I can find it." He sagged back in his chair. The fierceness guttered. "Because here is what happens to people who notice too much here, Sebastian. Here is what happened to the other eleven." He spread his hands. "They noticed. They wrote. And then one morning they didn't come to work, and their desks stood empty, and when I went to ask after them, their neighbors described men and women I had never employed, and their rooms had been let to other tenants since 'the year before,' and I stood in the street with their names in my mouth and watched the names turn to smoke between my teeth. I could not hold them. I could not even *grieve* them properly, because grief requires the shape of the loss, and the shapes had been taken." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Do you understand? They weren't killed. Killing leaves a body. Killing leaves a wound. This was *tidier*."

The tea had gone cold in the pot. Steam no longer spelled anything. Down in the inventory, Sebastian knew, the jar of borrowed thunder sat in case three, and lately — she had checked twice, telling herself she was mistaken both times — the thunder inside sounded less like thunder and more like a recording of thunder. Like something remembering being loud rather than being it.

"Master," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "If people can be taken out like that — cleanly, completely — and if the guild is losing its members, its money, its *reason* — then what's happening to us is happening to..."

"To everything," Oren finished. "Yes." He smiled at her, and it was the tenderest, saddest expression she had ever seen on a human face. "Welcome to the advanced course. The Guild of Impossible Things is not merely a museum of discarded wonders, child. It is a *symptom*. We are what remains of something much larger that was folded away — and the folding is not finished. It never stops. Veils don't rest. Every year the edges come in a little further, the way a tide comes in, patient as arithmetic. First the outlying towns. Then the roads. Then the currency. Then the members. Then—" He gestured vaguely at his own head, at the elegant, emptied architecture of it. "Then the reasons. Then the names. It eats outward-in and inward-out simultaneously, and it has *excellent manners* about it. Nothing screams. Everything simply becomes slightly less, each year, and everyone agrees, comfortably, that it was always thus."

"Can it be stopped?"

"Everything can be stopped. That's practically our motto's footnote." He reached beneath the blotter — an automatic motion, worn smooth by decades of repetition — and touched something there. Sebastian glimpsed metal, old metal, a seal perhaps, before his hand covered it again. When he spoke next, his voice had changed register, dropped into something formal and liturgical, words that clearly predated his forgetting, stored somewhere deeper than memory, in marrow or muscle:

"*The Guild keeps what the world releases. The Guild holds what the world folds away. When the last door stands open, the last door will be found.*"

"That's the founding oath," Sebastian whispered. "You say it in four dead languages on the bad evenings."

"I say it because saying it is the one act of memory they never managed to take." He looked at his hands. "*When the last door stands open, the last door will be found.* Fifty years I've recited that line without knowing what door it means. But I know this much, and I know it the way you know fire is hot: there *is* a door. And I know that the guild was not founded to mourn what was lost. We were founded to be findable. To stand in the dark holding a light, so that whoever finally goes looking will have a place to start from." He raised his eyes to hers. "And lately, child — lately I have begun to suspect that the reason I can't remember why we exist is that the *why* was never supposed to stay with the likes of me. It was supposed to leave. It was supposed to end up in the keeping of someone the folding never quite managed to finish. Someone with roots on both sides of whatever it was we agreed to forget."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the customs house clock struck a quarter hour that lasted slightly too long.

"Both sides," Sebastian repeated. "Of what, Master?"

And she watched him try. She watched him gather everything he had left — the oath, the pen in case eleven, the scorch marks on the eastern wall, the rain, his brother's face beside him at the signing, the weight of a country in his cupped hands — and reach, and strain, and

nothing.

His shoulders dropped. The lucidity drained out of his face like water through fingers, and when he looked up at her again he was only a tired old man in a room full of books, blinking, kind, adrift.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Where were we? Is that tea? You spoil me, child. What time is it — is it nearly the hour when you go down to the archive? You spend such long hours down there among the old folios. Mind the damp. Paper hates the damp." He smiled at her, warmly, wholly unaware. "Now — remind me. Remind an old man. Why is it we exist?"

And Sebastian, with dread sitting in her stomach like a swallowed stone and tenderness sitting right on top of it, heavier, rose and gathered the tray, and lied to him with love:

"Because someone has to keep the things the world can do without," she said. "Until they're wanted again."

"Ah, yes." He settled back, comforted, the compass finding north without knowing why it pointed there. "Yes. Precisely so. Off you go, then. And Sebastian—"

She paused at the door.

"—the green chair hums," he said, with sudden, absolute certainty. "Whatever else goes — however thin the world wears — that chair hums. Check it for me sometimes. Tell me it's true."

"I'll check it today, Master."

She went down the stairs with the tray and her heart hammering, past case three where the borrowed thunder muttered its fading rumor of itself, past case eleven where a pen waited under glass with nothing left to sign, down, down, to the deep archive where the oldest folios slept — because there was a date coming, she could feel it, a date circling her like a moth around a lamp she hadn't lit yet, and if her little notebook of evidence was going to mean anything, she needed to know what the 1899 folio had said on the nights Oren Voss forgot.

She did not yet know that tonight was the night.

But the archive did.

* * *

Down in the cold stacks, the 1899 folio lay where she had left it, mid-rebind, its covers off, its spine exposed like something under anesthesia.

Sebastian set down the tray. Washed her hands. Tied back her hair. Did all the small rituals of the craft, because ritual was a kind of courage you could practice.

Then she opened it to the page she had been avoiding all morning, the page with tonight's date written in faded iron-gall ink a century before she was born, beside a single word in a careful, scholarly hand:

*Beginning.*

And beneath it, smaller, in different ink, added later, in a hand she recognized with a jolt that went through her like lightning finding ground —

Oren's handwriting, younger, steadier than she had ever known it:

*God forgive me for what I must do tomorrow.*
