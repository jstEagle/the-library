## 4. A Country That Isn't

The deep archive of the Guild of Impossible Things was located three flights below street level, which Sebastian had always felt was appropriate. Impossible things, like mushrooms and grudges, preferred the dark.

She descended the spiral stair that morning with a lantern in one hand and a heel of bread in the other, because Oren Voss had a standing rule that no one entered the archive hungry. "Hungry archivists make careless decisions," he'd said once, back when he still remembered saying it. "They start alphabetizing by feel." Sebastian had never learned what disaster had prompted the rule, but she honored it. She ate her bread on the third step down, as tradition demanded — well, as she demanded; after four years she supposed that amounted to the same thing.

The archive smelled of cold stone and old glue and, faintly, of rain that had fallen somewhere else. That last one was the jar of borrowed thunder on the top shelf, which leaked weather when it rained in whatever place the thunder had been borrowed from. Sebastian had checked the borrowing ledger once. The thunder came from a village called Aldenmoor, borrowed in 1911, due back — according to the ledger's own cramped hand — *when the owner asks nicely*. No one had asked nicely in over a century. Sebastian rather hoped they never would. It was nice, having weather in the basement that belonged to someone else.

She lit the lamps along the reference wall, hung her coat on a hook shaped like a question mark (the guild's founder had commissioned it; no one knew why), and pulled down the folio she'd been avoiding for two days.

It was a treaty document. Or rather, it was what the guild kept instead of treaty documents: an accession record, the kind of dry administrative thing the Guild of Impossible Things collected precisely because everyone else found it too boring to be suspicious of. Sebastian had learned early that the truly impossible rarely announced itself with dragons. It arrived in triplicate, stamped, and filed under M for Miscellaneous.

The folio was titled, in faded iron-gall ink: **ACCESSION AND RESOLUTION OF THE VELLMARK QUESTION, 1874–1924**.

Fifty years ago this autumn. The war she'd grown up hearing about in the way one hears about weather in other countries — real, presumably, but never touching you directly.

Sebastian settled onto the reading stool, which creaked its familiar complaint, and began to read.

* * *

Here is what she had always known about Vellmark, the way everyone in the city of Grausend knew it:

There had been a country called Vellmark. It had sat between two larger neighbors like a wedge of cheese between two large dogs. There had been a border war — the Steppe War, they called it, though no one could tell you why, since nobody fought on any steppe; the battles had all been in river towns. The war had ended badly. The treaty had dissolved the country. Its territory had been divided between the two neighbors, its people issued new papers, and its name removed from maps with the brisk efficiency of a stain being treated.

That was the story. Everyone knew it. There were memorials, even — small ones, municipal, the kind with a plaque and a bench and a litter bin, so that remembering the dead could be combined with throwing away your apple core.

And here is what Sebastian knew, as of two nights ago, from a book written in 1899: that tonight's date appeared beside her own name and the phrase *the Door Out*, and that she had told no one, and that she had slept badly, and that she had decided — in the way one decides to look at a bruise — that the only cure for a prophecy was context.

So: the folio.

She read the preamble. Diplomatic language has a texture all its own, smooth and bloodless, like stones in a river. *The undersigned parties, desiring a permanent resolution to the Question of Vellmark...* Sebastian mouthed the word *question* to herself. Not *problem*. Not even *war*. A question. As though the country had been an exam item, and the answer had been *no longer applicable*.

Then she reached the annexes, and stopped breathing for a moment.

Because the annexes were not diplomatic at all. They were guild documents. She recognized the format instantly — the double-ruled margins, the inventory numbering, the particular slant of the old clerk's hand that she saw every day in the ledgers upstairs. Her guild. Her dusty, dwindling, rent-behind guild had drafted the legal machinery that erased a country.

Annex Three was titled **ON THE FOLDING OF TERRITORIAL MEMORY**, and it read less like law than like a recipe. There were ingredients. There were quantities. *Sufficient forgetfulness, procured locally, to blanket a population of 2.3 million.* *One boundary of glass, to hold the fold.* *A Door, for those who must go out.* And then a line that made Sebastian set down the folio and press both hands flat against the cold table, just to feel something solid:

*The Resolution shall be maintained by a standing Custody, funded in perpetuity.*

*Custody.* The word snagged in her like a fishhook. She didn't know why. It was an ordinary word. Custodians swept floors. Custodians looked after museum pieces. But somewhere behind her sternum, a door she hadn't known was there rattled on its hinges, and she made a note of the feeling the way she made notes of everything — carefully, in pencil, in the margin, where it couldn't hurt anyone.

* * *

By late afternoon she had filled eleven pages of notes and developed a headache with architectural ambition. She went up for tea.

Oren was in the front room, ostensibly cataloguing but actually asleep sitting up, his spectacles slid down his nose, a pen still poised over a ledger he'd been pretending to write in. On the desk beside him lay the day's post: a demand for rent, a flyer for a lecture on modern drainage, and a letter addressed to *The Keeper of Small Miracles, Grausend* — which was not their name, but mail found them anyway, the way water finds low ground.

Sebastian lifted the rent demand. The Guildhall landlord was punctual the way winter is punctual. But it was the second page — the accounting slip the guild's treasurer, old Mrs. Havel, attached to every payment — that caught her eye today.

Their rent was paid in marks. Not the neighbor's currency, not the empire's coin. In *Vellmarkish* marks. Old ones. Pre-war issues, with the little mountain-and-wheat engraving on the back that every schoolchild used to trace with a finger.

Everyone knew the currency had been withdrawn fifty years ago. Everyone knew it was worthless. Everyone except, apparently, the landlord, who accepted it monthly without comment, and Mrs. Havel, who paid it without comment, and the bank — if you could call it that — where Mrs. Havel drew it from, which was a narrow building on Candlemakers' Row that Sebastian had walked past a thousand times without ever once seeing a customer enter or leave.

"Oren," she said gently, because he was snoring now, softly, in a minor key.

His eyes opened. For a moment — a long moment, three heartbeats maybe — he looked at her with complete blankness, the polite blankness of a man trying to place whether she was a niece or a burglar. Then something reassembled itself behind his face.

"Ah," he said. "Apprentice."

"Sebastian."

"Of course you are." He straightened his spectacles and looked down at the ledger, at the pen still hovering over nothing. "Was I writing something?"

"I don't think so."

"Good. I've a suspicion I only ever pretend to, these days. Keeps the ledgers honest." He smiled at her, warm as a stove, and she felt the familiar ache underneath the smile — the tenderness and the dread braided together, dread wrapped in tenderness like a hot coal in a cloth. He was forgetting. Not all at once; in flakes, like paint. Last month he'd called her by the name of an apprentice dead forty years. Yesterday he'd stood at the window for an hour watching the street as though waiting for someone to come home from a war.

"Oren," she said, before she could stop herself, "what do you remember about the Veiling?"

Something crossed his face. Not confusion exactly. More like a man reaching into his pocket and finding the pocket sewn shut.

"The Veiling," he repeated. His hand drifted toward his temple. "There was... there was an accord. We signed—" He stopped. Blinked. "We signed *something*. Why would we have signed something?" He laughed, a small bewildered sound, and shook his head as if clearing water from his ears. "You'll have to ask the ledgers, child. They keep better than I do."

He returned to his catalogue. Sebastian stood a moment longer, watching the top of his white head, and then went back downstairs, because there are questions you can only bear to ask of paper.

* * *

Dusk came early to Grausend in autumn, and it came strangely that evening.

Sebastian noticed it first from the archive's high slit of a window, while she was reshelving the treaty folio and arguing with herself about whether to pull Annex Three again. The light outside did something wrong. Not dramatic — nothing so useful as thunder or darkness. It simply *stuttered*. The amber of sunset held, and held, and then skipped, the way a needle skips on a scratched record, and for half a heartbeat the rooftops across the lane were lit by a sun that had already set.

She blinked. The light behaved. The rooftops were ordinary roofs, chimney pots against a violet sky, a cat walking the ridgepole with the confidence of a landlord.

But her skin had gone cold, and she stood very still at the window, watching, the way one watches a person who has lied to you, waiting to see if they'll lie twice.

The cat reached the end of the roofline and stepped off onto the neighboring eaves — and for a flicker, less than a blink, there was no neighboring eaves. There was open air and a drop of four stories and the cat stepping off into nothing—

—and then the eaves were back, and the cat landed on them, unbothered, and continued its rounds.

Sebastian gripped the windowsill. Her breath fogged the cold glass. She wiped it with her sleeve and watched until full dark, and the city stayed put, brick by brick, lamp by lamp, a city holding very still because it suspected it was being observed.

*Home is a forgery,* she thought. And then, more honestly: *No. Home is real. Something else is the forgery. Find out which.*

* * *

She worked through the evening by lamplight, cross-referencing, and what she assembled by midnight was a picture so orderly it could only be madness:

The treaty resolved the Vellmark Question in 1924. The guild drafted the mechanism — the Folding, the veil, the Door Out. A standing Custody maintained the Resolution, funded in perpetuity. The country's memory was folded out of the world the way you fold a letter out of sight, not destroyed, merely *put away*, held behind a boundary of glass on the old Steppe.

And here was the part that turned her blood to cellar-cold: the folding had never ended. It wasn't a historical event. It was a maintenance schedule. Fifty years of payments, of procurements, of forgetfulness bought and delivered — and the ledger of those transactions ran through her guild like a main pipe through a house. Every entry in the Ledger of Small Impossibilities, she suddenly understood, might be a withdrawal from some citizen's stock of remembering. Her beloved books. Her cages.

She sat back on the stool. Above her, muffled through three floors of stone, the city went about its night: cart wheels, a drunk singing, the clock tower striking ten — striking ten twice, actually, with a gap between the strikes like a stutter, like the sunset had stuttered, like the whole world was a record with a scratch running through it.

Sebastian thought of the rent paid in dead currency. Of a bank on Candlemakers' Row that no one entered. Of a landlord who accepted money from a country that officially did not exist, and thought nothing of it — or worse, *could* think nothing of it, because thinking about it had perhaps been purchased from him, decades ago, and filed in a ledger, and shelved in a basement.

She thought of the plaque by the memorial bench, which gave the dates of the war but not, she realized now, a single name of the dead. Just numbers. Numbers were easier to file.

And she thought of the prophecy. Tonight's date. Her own name. *The Door Out.*

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the east, toward the flat lands where the Steppe began, the sky flickered once — a pale seam of light along the horizon, there and gone, like a door opening briefly in a dark house to let out a thread of lamplight.

Sebastian wrote one final line in her notebook, in pencil, pressing hard enough to tear the page:

*Tonight. If the date means anything, it means tonight. Go and see.*

She put on her coat, took the lantern, and climbed the stairs toward the ordinary, forged, flickering world — and at the top of the stairs she paused, because through the guild's grimy front window she could see the street below, and beneath the customs house clock across the lane, a woman in a grey coat stood perfectly still, looking up at the guildhall windows.

Looking up, Sebastian was almost certain, at hers.
