## 5. The Woman at the Customs House

The Customs House of the Veil did not appear on any map, which was fitting, because its entire purpose was to make sure that certain things did not appear on maps.

Ilse Marchetti had worked there for eleven years, and in that time she had learned three truths. First: the coffee was terrible, and would remain terrible, because the Custodian budget allocated funds for surveillance equipment but not for anything a human being might actually enjoy. Second: the veil over Vellmark was fraying, and everyone above her pay grade knew it and said nothing. Third—and this was the one she kept folded small behind her ribs, like a letter never sent—she had been in love with Sebastian Winterbourne for six years, and had never once spoken to her.

"Marchetti." Warden Kessler's voice came through the intercom, dry as old paper. "Audit rotation. Northern districts. Take the ledger of anomalies."

"The whole ledger?" Ilse asked.

"The anomalies are multiplying," Kessler said. "Or our instruments are failing. Either way, count them before they count us."

She took the ledger. It was heavier than it should have been, the way all Veil documents were heavier than they should have been—paper remembers what it records, and memory has weight.

* * *

The audit itself was simple work, or should have been. Ilse walked the northern districts with her brass compass-chronometer—a device that measured not north but *presence*, the density of remembered reality in a given street—and noted where the needle trembled.

It trembled everywhere now.

On Coriander Lane, a bakery that had stood for forty years flickered like a candle in wind: present, present, absent, present. A woman buying bread didn't notice. The baker didn't notice. Only Ilse stood on the corner with her instrument humming against her palm, watching a shopfront stutter in and out of existence, and felt the particular loneliness of being the only sober person at a party where everyone else believes the punch is fine.

"Morning," said the baker, when the shop solidified long enough for him to exist in it. "Foggy one today."

"It's clear," Ilse said.

"Is it?" He frowned at the window, at the pale light. "I could've sworn I closed up last night in fog thick as wool. Funny." He laughed, gently, at his own confusion, and handed her a roll she hadn't asked for. "On the house. You've got an honest face."

Ilse ate the roll walking. It was good bread. That was the thing nobody warned you about, working for the Custodians: the world they were erasing was *good*. It was warm rolls and honest faces and bakers who misremembered the weather and laughed about it. Somewhere in a filing drawer in the capital there was a form that described all this as *maintenance of regional stability*, and Ilse had signed dozens of them, and every signature had felt a little less like ink and a little more like a small knife.

By afternoon she had catalogued eleven structural failures, two temporal inconsistencies (a church bell that rang at noon and then again at noon, twice), and one incident she classified only as *resonance*: a stray dog on Tanner's Row who stopped mid-street and stared directly at Ilse's compass as if he knew exactly what it was measuring, and disapproved.

"You don't know anything," she told him.

He wagged his tail once, slowly, in a manner she chose to interpret as skeptical.

* * *

The Winterbourne problem began, as most of Ilse's problems began, with paperwork.

Rule Seven of the Custodian Charter: any citizen whose name appears in a pre-Veiling document must be flagged for observation. Rule Seven existed because names are hooks; a name written down somewhere is a handhold for memory, and memory is the enemy. The Custodians had spent fifty years pulling names out of books the way you pull splinters out of skin—carefully, thoroughly, and without asking the skin how it feels about it.

Sebastian Winterbourne's name appeared in a book.

Ilse had found it four years ago, during a routine sweep of the guild quarter's archives. She'd been twenty-six, newly badged, eager in the way that people are eager before they understand what their eagerness will be used for. The folio had been a prophecy register from 1899, water-damaged, half-legible, scheduled for incineration. Standard procedure: log it, burn it, file the ash.

She had opened it anyway. Curiosity was a firing offense in the Custodians, technically. In practice it was just a habit nobody had managed to stamp out, least of all in Ilse Marchetti, who had joined the order at nineteen because she wanted, more than anything in the world, to know why her grandmother had wept whenever anyone mentioned the word *north*.

And there, in faded iron-gall ink, in a list of dates and names that read like someone's private weather forecast:

*Tonight's date, sixty years hence — S. Winterbourne — the Door Out.*

Sixty years hence had seemed impossibly far away, then. It was not so far anymore. Ilse had done the arithmetic more times than she could count, usually at night, usually instead of sleeping. And the date matched. And the name—

Well. The name belonged to a young woman who worked at the Guild of Impossible Things, who bought apples from the greengrocer on Wednesdays, who walked home along the canal with her collar up and her nose in a book even in the rain, who had no idea—not the faintest shadow of an idea—that she was written down somewhere, foretold, filed, flagged.

Flagged. That was the official part. Ilse was supposed to report her. There was a form. Form 12-C, *Notification of Prophetic Resonance*. Ilse had taken the form out of the drawer four years ago. She had held it. She had even filled in the date field once, in pencil.

Then she had erased the pencil, put the form back, and gone home and lain awake until dawn listening to her own heartbeat, which sounded, in the dark, like footsteps walking away from something.

* * *

"You're doing the face," said Petra Vogel, dropping into the chair across from Ilse's desk.

"What face?"

"The face where you look like you're doing arithmetic and losing." Petra was the closest thing Ilse had to a friend in the Customs House, which meant she brought Ilse contraband biscuits and asked no questions that couldn't be answered with lies. She was also, Ilse suspected, sharper than she let on, which was its own kind of friendship. "Kessler's got you on anomaly counts?"

"Eleven structural failures in the northern districts alone. Last year the annual total was nine."

Petra whistled softly. "The veil's getting thin."

"The veil isn't thin," Ilse said automatically. "The veil is stable. The veil is permanent."

"Say it louder, maybe the veil will believe you." Petra slid a biscuit across the desk like a bribe. "You know what I heard from Records? They pulled a treaty folio last month and the ink had *healed*. Rewritten itself. 'Resolved' where 'destroyed' used to be. Somebody downstairs fainted."

"That's a rumor."

"It's a rumor with a fainting witness." Petra bit into her own biscuit and studied Ilse over the top of it. "You've been odd lately. Odder. You take the northern route every week and come back looking like you left something there."

"I leave forms there," Ilse said. "It's called work."

"Mm," said Petra, in the tone of a woman filing something away for later, and Ilse understood that she had just been audited herself, by someone much better at it than she was.

* * *

Thursday was apple day.

Ilse told herself it was coincidence. Ilse told herself a great many things, and had gotten quite good at telling them with a straight face, which was after all the primary professional skill of a Custodian. But Thursday found her at the greengrocer's stall on Canal Street at half past five, ostensibly verifying market attendance figures, actually holding a bag of pears she did not want, waiting.

Sebastian Winterbourne arrived at ten past, exactly as she always did, with ink on her fingers and her collar crooked and a battered canvas satchel slung across her body like a bandolier of books.

"Two apples," Sebastian said to the greengrocer. "The bruised ones. For stewing."

"The bruised ones are cheaper because they're *bruised*, miss, not because I'm running a charity."

"They have more character."

"Everything has more character when it's discounted," the greengrocer said, but he was smiling as he bagged them, and Sebastian smiled back, and Ilse stood eight feet away holding her unwanted pears and felt something in her chest turn over with the slow inevitability of a page being turned.

Here was the thing Ilse had never been able to explain to anyone, least of all to the woman in question: she had watched Sebastian Winterbourne for six years, and it had started as duty and become something else entirely, and she could point to almost the exact day it changed. Three years ago. Rain. Sebastian had stopped on the canal bridge to rescue a gull tangled in fishing line, cursing at it with tremendous fluency while it stabbed at her thumbs, and finally freed it, and then sat down on the wet stones of the bridge and laughed—laughed at herself, alone, delighted, unobserved—or so she thought—and Ilse, standing in the shadow of the customs kiosk with her compass humming in her pocket, had felt the observation turn into something that had no form number and no procedure and no place in any charter she had ever sworn to.

You were not supposed to love the things you surveilled. That was practically Rule One. The whole architecture of the Custodians rested on the principle that the watched were material, not people—inventory, not neighbors. And here was Ilse Marchetti, eleven years badged, knowing the exact Wednesday rhythm of a stranger's grocery habits, knowing she took her tea too strong and read walking and talked to stray dogs in a low apologetic voice, and knowing—this was the worst part, the guilty part, the part that kept her up at night—that none of this was surveillance anymore. Surveillance was supposed to be *for* something. This was just attention. Attention, sustained for six years, which is a word that rhymes with devotion and weighs considerably more.

"Excuse me—you've dropped one."

Ilse looked down. A pear lay on the cobbles between them. She looked up, and Sebastian Winterbourne was looking at her, directly, for the first time in six years of Thursdays, and her eyes were brown and sharp and slightly amused, and Ilse's entire professional composure performed an act of structural failure worthy of the northern districts.

"Oh," Ilse said. Brilliant. Eleven years of training.

"You've been standing there a while," Sebastian said. Not accusing. Observing. Which was, Ilse thought wildly, *her* job. "Deciding on the pears? They're better than they look. Everything here is."

"Yes," Ilse said. "That's—" She stopped, because the true ending of that sentence was *that's what I keep telling myself about my entire life*, and instead she finished, weakly, "Yes."

Sebastian picked up the pear, dusted it on her sleeve, and handed it back. Her fingers brushed Ilse's palm for perhaps half a second. Ilse would remember the temperature of that half-second for years: warm, ink-stained, ordinary, unbearable.

"You're the customs auditor," Sebastian said. "I've seen you around. You always look like you're counting something."

"I am," Ilse said. "It's my job."

"And do you ever finish?"

"Not yet," Ilse said, and heard, too late, everything that sentence contained.

Sebastian smiled—the same crooked, self-delighted smile from the bridge, three years of rain ago—and hoisted her satchel. "Good luck with the counting."

She walked off along the canal, collar crooked, eating an apple she'd claimed was only fit for stewing, and Ilse stood in the fading light with her pears and her badge and her six years of quiet treason, and understood with total clarity that she was going to do it again tomorrow, and the day after, and every day until the counting finished or she did.

* * *

Back at the Customs House, past midnight, Ilse opened the forbidden drawer.

The 1899 folio lay where she had hidden it—in violation of Rule Nine, in violation of Rule Four, in violation, frankly, of several rules whose numbers she'd deliberately forgotten. She turned to the page. The ink had shifted since spring. She was almost sure it had shifted; the instruments downstairs said ink didn't do that, but the instruments also said the veil was permanent, and the bell on Coriander Lane rang noon twice.

*S. Winterbourne — the Door Out.*

Below it, new since summer, in the same ancient hand or a hand pretending to be ancient, one more line had surfaced from the water damage:

*The Door opens for the one who belongs to both sides. The Custodian who watches her will choose.*

Ilse read it three times. Then she sat very still in the dark of the records room, listening to the building settle, feeling the words sit in her chest like a swallowed stone.

*Will choose.* As though it hadn't already happened. As though the choice weren't six years old and sitting right there in the shape of a crooked collar and a bag of bruised apples.

Somewhere below her, in the deep archive, a bell rang noon for the second time.

Ilse closed the folio, returned it to the drawer, and went to find Form 12-C. She filled in the name field in careful block letters—*Winterbourne, Sebastian*—and then she fed the form into the incinerator chute and listened to it burn, and reported, officially, in the morning log:

*Northern district audit complete. No prophetic resonance detected.*

The lie settled onto the page in her neat, obedient handwriting, and Ilse Marchetti signed beneath it, and outside the window, over the rooftops of a city that was forgetting itself one bakery at a time, the dusk flickered—just once, just briefly—like a candle deciding whether to go out.

She had three days' leave accrued. She intended, starting tomorrow, to spend all of it on Canal Street.
