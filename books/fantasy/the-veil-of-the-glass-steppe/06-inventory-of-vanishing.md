## 6. Inventory of Vanishing

The morning Sebastian Winterbourne discovered that Tanner's Row had stopped existing, she was thinking about eggs.

This was not unusual. Eggs were one of the few reliable pleasures left in her life—the guild's kitchen produced them in a state of perfect, unremarkable adequacy—and she had built a small ritual around acquiring them from Frau Lindqvist at the corner shop on Tanner's Row: two eggs, one conversation about the weather, and a standing invitation to a dinner party that neither of them ever expected her to attend but both pretended might happen.

She turned onto Tanner's Row and found herself on Wexler Street instead.

Not *found herself lost*. That would have been forgivable, ordinary, the sort of thing that happened to everyone after a long night rebinding folios. No—she had walked the route her feet knew by heart, and the city had simply declined to provide Tanner's Row. Where the Row should have curved off between the cooper's yard and the old bathhouse, there was instead a continuation of Wexler Street so smooth and self-assured that it seemed to have been there for centuries. The cobbles matched. The drain grates matched. A cat sat on a windowsill looking profoundly unbothered, as cats do when reality rearranges itself around them.

Sebastian stood very still.

"Good morning," said Herr Brandt, passing with his paper under his arm.

"Morning," said Sebastian. "Herr Brandt—Tanner's Row. Is it—did they close it? For repairs?"

He lowered his paper and looked at her over his spectacles with the mild concern of a man assessing whether he needed to remember this interaction later. "Tanner's Row," he repeated, as though tasting the words. "You mean where the cooper's is?"

"Where the cooper's *was*, possibly."

"Ah." He nodded slowly, the way people nod when they've decided not to think about something. "They did some work there years ago. Filled it all in, I expect." And he went on his way, satisfied, while Sebastian's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

Because here was the thing: she had bought eggs from Frau Lindqvist **yesterday**. She could still feel the coins leaving her palm. She could still hear the woman saying *rain coming, mark me* in the tone of a person who considered weather a personal acquaintance.

Except—

Hadn't it been sunny yesterday? When she mentioned it to the cook that evening, the cook had insisted it had poured all afternoon, and Sebastian had let it go, because letting things go was what you did with small disagreements. But now the small disagreement sat up and looked at her with teeth.

She tried the other direction, cutting through Cooper's Lane—which was either the same street or a cousin of it, the geography of the district having always been more suggestion than fact—and found the corner shop after all, sitting exactly where it should be, with Frau Lindqvist arranging apples in the window.

"Two eggs," Sebastian said, slightly breathless.

"You look like you've seen a ghost."

"I've possibly seen a street. Or failed to. It's unclear." She counted out the coins. "Frau Lindqvist—yesterday. Was it rainy?"

The woman paused, an apple hovering. "Rained from noon straight through supper. Ruined my washing." She handed over the eggs wrapped in brown paper. "Why? You were out in it?"

"I was," said Sebastian, who had absolutely been out in brilliant sunshine, and felt the ground tilt gently beneath her certainty. "Thank you. Enjoy your... apples."

Outside, she unwrapped an egg and held it up to the light, half-expecting it to be impossible too. It was merely an egg. That was somehow worse.

* * *

Back at the Guild of Impossible Things, Sebastian climbed to the inventory room with her eggs and her dread, because if the world was going to dissolve politely outside, she wanted witnesses of a sort. The inventory room occupied the top floor of the headquarters, a long room under a leaking slate roof where forty generations of apprentices had catalogued objects that defied the general agreement about how things behaved.

There was the jar of borrowed thunder—third shelf, blue glass, humming faintly even now, though lately the hum had developed a stutter, like a man clearing his throat before deciding against speaking. There was the key to a house that burned down in 1841; it still turned locks, provided the locks belonged to houses that had also burned, a niche talent that had never once proven useful but which the ledgers recorded with pride. There was the umbrella that opened only into yesterday's rain, the clock that ran backwards on Sundays, the mirror that showed its viewer at their most honest (banned from common use after an incident involving the Guildmaster and a haircut).

Sebastian took down her clipboard and began the morning count, and it took her four shelves to notice what was wrong.

The thunder jar wasn't humming.

She lifted it. Blue glass, lead seal, the little brass plate engraved *THUNDER, BORROWED, 1 UNIT, DO NOT SHAKE IN ANGER.* Inside: nothing. Not silence exactly—silence has a texture, a held-breath quality—but absence. She shook it, gently at first and then, betraying every warning on the label, not gently at all. The jar remained stubbornly full of nothing, like a promise someone had quietly decided not to keep.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one. "Oh, that's not good."

She moved down the shelf. The backwards clock was running forwards—she watched it for a full minute, ticking in the correct and deeply boring direction. The umbrella, shaken experimentally over the sink, declined to open into anything at all. Only the burned-house key retained its gift: she tried it in the lock of the disused coal cellar (which had never burned, admittedly) and then, on a whim, carried it three streets over to the gutted shell of the old Harmonist Chapel, where it turned sweetly, as it always had. One impossibility still breathing. The rest going quiet, one by one, like lamps being snuffed along a corridor by someone walking away.

Sebastian sat down on a crate and made notes, because notes were what she did when the world frightened her. *Thunder jar: dormant. Clock: conventional. Umbrella: refuses. Key: functional.* Her handwriting grew smaller as she wrote. At the bottom of the page she added, in letters barely bigger than a confession: *Objects losing impossibility. Cause unknown. Duration unknown.*

Then, after a moment, she crossed out *unknown* twice and wrote: *Cause: the city. Duration: ongoing.*

She didn't know why she wrote that. She only knew that it felt true in the way the flickering at dusk had begun to feel true—that sense, arriving more often now, that the world around her was a stage set viewed briefly from the wings, all paint and canvas and careful angles, and behind it something vast was holding very still.

* * *

That evening, she tested the neighbors.

She told herself it was diligence. The guild's charter—such as anyone remembered it—obliged members to observe and record anomalies, and streets failing to appear certainly qualified. But honesty required admitting, at least to the ledger, that she also simply wanted someone else to say it first. To say: *yes, I noticed too, yes, Tanner's Row went missing on Tuesday, yes, we're all holding hands while the furniture drifts.*

She began at the bakery, where Herr Ostergaard was scraping trays.

"Herr Ostergaard, do you remember the storm last week?"

"What storm?"

"The one Wednesday night. Thunder over the river quarter, hail the size of—"

"We haven't had hail since I was a boy." He said it comfortably, without suspicion, and that comfort was the frightening part. "Lovely week, actually. Clear skies."

Clear skies. Sebastian had spent Wednesday night wedged under her desk with her ears ringing, certain the thunder jar had finally escaped its shelf. Two memories, irreconcilable, and his wore the easy confidence of the majority.

At the fountain square, she tried Frau Behm, who knitted through wars, funerals, and presumably the end of the world. "Did you see the new lamplighter on Cooper's Lane?" Sebastian asked. "Red coat?"

"Lamplighters wear grey," said Frau Behm, needles clicking. "Always have."

"They wore red until quite recently, I'm nearly sure."

"Nearly sure." Frau Behm smiled at her kindly, the smile reserved for young people who argued with knitting. "Memory's a slippery fish, dear. Better to let it swim."

*A slippery fish.* Sebastian walked home turning the phrase over. It was such a *convenient* philosophy. Memory slips, so don't chase it. The street fills in, so don't grieve it. The weather changes overnight, so carry no umbrella. Every discrepancy came pre-packaged with a soothing explanation, the way bruises come with the body's own numbing. The city wasn't fighting its own dissolution. It was *anesthetized* to it.

And then, near the guild steps, she met the boy.

He was perhaps nine, sitting on the low wall opposite the cooper's yard—or where the cooper's yard would have been, in the version of the morning she trusted—and he was drawing in chalk on the flagstones. Sebastian slowed. He'd drawn a street: crooked houses, a shop sign, a figure in the doorway with a round face and an apron.

"That's Tanner's Row," Sebastian said.

The boy looked up. He had the particular gravity of children who have recently learned something adults refuse to know. "It comes back sometimes," he said. "Early. Before everyone wakes up. Then it goes." He pointed the chalk at the empty air where the Row should curve. "My mum says I dreamt it. But dreams don't smell like bread." He frowned at his drawing, then rubbed out the figure in the doorway with his sleeve—not cruelly, just practically, the way children edit reality to match what they're permitted. "Don't tell my mum I said."

"I won't," Sebastian promised. "What's your name?"

"Piet." Then, quickly, as though the information cost him: "I think. It's Piet today, anyway."

*Today,* thought Sebastian. As though names were weather. As though identity itself had begun to keep banker's hours.

She gave him a coin for the drawing—he protested, accepted, pocketed it with the swift grace of the professionally bribed—and climbed the guild steps with the chalk picture burning in her memory. In the entrance hall, the founding ledgers sat in their case, and beyond them, dim in the evening, the long shelves of the archive waited with all their patient, terrible knowledge.

Sebastian lit her lamp, climbed to the inventory room, and added a final line to the day's report:

*Witness: Piet, approx. 9 yrs. Remembers accurately, briefly. Children retain longest. Adults forget fastest and most gratefully. Conclusion: this is not decay. This is maintenance.*

She stared at the word *maintenance* for a long time. Maintenance implied a maintainer. Decay was indifferent, accidental, sad—but maintenance was *purposeful*. Someone, somewhere, was doing this. Doing it carefully, thoroughly, and—she thought of Herr Brandt's mild nod, Frau Behm's kind smile—doing it with everyone's grateful consent.

The lamp guttered. Outside, dusk came down over the city, and Sebastian watched through the grimy window as the rooftops across the way shimmered—just for a heartbeat, just at the edge of vision, like heat-haze in winter—before settling back into solid, obedient brick.

She looked down at the inventory list, at the little graveyard of dead impossibilities, and understood suddenly, with the clarity that arrives only at the worst times, that the guild's objects hadn't failed on their own. They were part of the same ledger. Whatever force was smoothing Tanner's Row out of existence, correcting the weather, greying the lamplighters—it was drawing from the same account. The impossible things weren't dying of old age.

They were being *spent*.

By whom, and for what, and why the payments were accelerating—all of that lived in the archive below, in ledgers she had dusted for six years without truly reading. Sebastian closed her report, tucked it under her arm, and turned toward the stairs, toward the deep shelves, toward whatever the guild's books had been too polite to say out loud.

Behind her, on the third shelf, the blue glass jar sat silent among its neighbors—and if you had listened very closely, in the pause between one heartbeat and the next, you might have sworn it hummed a single low note. Like thunder remembering itself. Like a country, far away, clearing its throat.
