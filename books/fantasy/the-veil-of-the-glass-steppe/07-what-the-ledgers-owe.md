## 7. What the Ledgers Owe

The trouble began, as most troubles at the Guild of Impossible Things did, with a filing error.

I had spent the morning reorganizing the third-floor stacks—someone had shelved a bottled eclipse under M for *Midnight*, which was charming and also wrong—and while shifting a crate of obsolete weather, I noticed that Ledger 44 had been misbound. Its spine read *Ledger 41*. Small thing. The sort of thing I was made for finding, the way some people are made for whistling or baking bread.

But when I pulled it free to correct the label, the binding cracked along a seam no binder should have sewn: not thread, but hair. Fine, silver-brown human hair, stitched through the spine like a signature.

I sat down on the step-ladder and looked at it for a long time.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the ledgers before that morning, which is to say here is what I had carefully never asked:

There were four hundred and twelve of them, dating back to the guild's founding in 1899. Each contained entries in a dozen different hands across a century. And each entry followed the same maddening format—a name, a date, an object or event described with bureaucratic tenderness, and a price paid in the old Vellmarkish currency, the kruna, which had been worthless since the treaty.

Example, from Ledger 201, entry 3,091:

*Marta Ellingsen, fishwife, Halden Row. Sells: the sound her daughter made laughing on the ferry, aged six. Bought: two kruna and one winter without grief.*

I had read ten thousand entries like that. I had found them beautiful. That is the confession I have been circling for three days now, the one I keep trying to file under something gentler: I found them beautiful, the way you might find a birdcage beautiful if you were very fond of cages and had never much considered the bird.

Because here is what I understood, sitting on that ladder with a ledger bound in human hair: nobody sells the sound of their daughter laughing unless they are trying not to hear it anymore. Nobody sells a first kiss, a dead brother's voice, the smell of bread in a house they can't afford to keep—unless keeping it has become unbearable. The guild bought grief by the pound. We called it impossible inventory. We were, as far as I could reconstruct it, a pawnshop for pain.

And the kruna—the worthless currency we still paid our rent in, that the landlord still accepted without comment, that appeared on no banknote list anywhere in the world—that currency had to come from somewhere.

Ledgers don't mint money. Ledgers record debts.

I climbed down. My legs felt like borrowed equipment.

* * *

The deep archive opened only to a founding seal, which I did not have, and to Oren, who had forgotten where he kept his teeth twice that month. But there was a third key, and it was me—or rather, it was my particular talent for being overlooked, which until that morning I had regarded as my single flaw and was beginning to suspect was my job description.

The archive door was enchanted in the laziest possible way: it simply declined to notice anyone standing in front of it. Most people, being noticed creatures, could not get past this. I stood in front of it, breathed slowly, thought about dust, and the door yawned aside like an old man dozing through a sermon.

Inside: the true ledgers.

Not the four hundred and twelve upstairs, which I now understood to be receipts—customer copies, essentially, the polite fiction we let ourselves read. The deep archive held the master books, and the master books were arranged not by date or object but by *weight*.

That word stopped me. Weight. I ran my finger down the index and found columns I had no framework for: tonnage of forgetting, per district; seasonal yields; attrition rates. The guild had kept harvest records. We had kept harvest records for a hundred years, and I had dusted the shelves they sat on, humming.

I took down Volume One and opened it to the first page, expecting grandeur, a manifesto, at minimum a dramatic epigraph. Instead I got minutes. Actual meeting minutes, dated 14 March 1899, in a hand so cramped it could only belong to someone being paid by the word:

*Present: Oren Voss (junior clerk), A. Brandt, the brothers Kesselring, Madame Ilves. Discussed: the Treaty terms require the country to be forgotten, and forgetting does not come cheap. Discussed: where memory goes when it is taken. Resolved: it must go somewhere. Resolved: somewhere will be us.*

Below that, in fresher ink, a line added decades later:

*Resolved again, every year since. God forgive the bookkeeping.*

I turned pages with hands that would not stay steady. Here was the architecture of my whole beloved crumbling building laid out plain: the Veiling Accord hadn't erased Vellmark. It had *folded* it—out of maps, out of treaties, out of the world's attention—but folding requires weight on the crease. Something had to hold the country shut. And the cheapest material for holding anything shut, the material that binds tightest and weighs least and is easiest to extract from people who are desperate, is grief deliberately surrendered.

Every citizen who sold a sorrow to the Guild of Impossible Things was feeding the veil. Every jar of borrowed thunder in our inventory was a container; the thunder was incidental. What we actually stored, shelf after shelf after shelf, in bottles labeled with charming nonsense—*bottled eclipse*, *obsolete weather*, *a key to a house that burned*—was other people's pasts, pressed into service as ballast for a lie.

The key to a house that burned. I had catalogued that key myself, last autumn, and admired its craftsmanship. Now I saw the entry behind it, in the master ledger: *J. Aldous, carpenter. Sells: the house itself—not the walls, the living of it. Eleven years. Reason given: cannot afford to remember what the fire took.* And beneath, the clerk's note: *Heavy lot. Handle with care. He wept at the counter and thanked us.*

He thanked us. Of course he did. That was the genius of the whole arrangement, the part that made me want to be sick into the archive's wastepaper basket: we helped people. Genuinely, truly helped them. Who among us, offered two winters without grief, would say no? The veil wasn't built on cruelty. It was built on mercy, harvested at scale, and I—I had spent seven years polishing the jars.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat in the deep archive. Long enough for the candle to drown. Long enough that when I finally climbed back up into the ordinary dusty light of the reading room, my eyes had adjusted to darkness and the afternoon looked overexposed, unreal, a photograph of a room rather than the room itself.

Oren was at the front desk, doing what he always did at that hour: staring at a ledger he couldn't finish reading, tracing lines with his finger and losing his place.

"Sebastian," he said, pleased, certain, and then, after a pause in which something flickered behind his face like a lamp in wind: "Sebastian."

"You said it right the first time," I told him gently.

"I did? Good." He tapped the ledger. "This entry. Marta Ellingsen. Do you know her?"

"She'd be dead fifty years, Master."

"Ah." He frowned at the page as though it had contradicted him. "She came in every spring, you know. Sold something small each time. A season's worth of missing someone. She said—" His brow worked. "She said it was like paying rent on being alive. Why do I remember that? Why do I remember *her*, and not whether I ate breakfast?"

"Maybe she mattered more than breakfast."

"Everything matters more than breakfast," Oren said, "that's the whole tragedy of the profession," and then he looked up at me with sudden terrible clarity, the lucidity that visited him lately the way weather visits, unannounced and briefly total. "You've been downstairs."

My blood went cold in a thoroughly professional manner. "Master—"

"The archive doesn't open for just anyone. It opens for people it can't see." He said it without alarm, almost fondly. "It's how I got in, at your age. I was very good at not being seen too. It's a prerequisite, really. You cannot run a guild of impossible things if you insist on being perceived."

"Oren." I hadn't called him that in years; he blinked. "What happens to the memories we buy?"

"What happens to rain?" He shrugged, and for a moment he was only a tired old man in a cardigan with holes at both elbows. "It falls somewhere. It pools. It—" He stopped. His face did something complicated, the expression of a man reaching for a word and finding the shelf empty. "It feeds something," he finished quietly. "We feed something. We've always fed something. I signed the paper myself, and every year I understand less why, and every year I'm more certain that stopping would be worse, and I don't know why *that* either, and it is going to drive me mad, girl, this certainty without content, this—" His voice cracked, and he pressed his palm flat against the ledger as though steadying it, or himself. "Check the rent," he said abruptly. "The kruna. Count what's in the strongbox against what the ledgers say we've paid out."

"Why?"

"Because if the numbers ever match exactly," he said, "then nothing is left over, and if nothing is left over—" He shook his head hard, like a dog shedding water, and the lucidity went with it, draining out of his face until all that remained was mild confusion and the ghost of a smile. "Was I saying something? Never mind. Have you eaten? Everything matters more than eating, that's the tragedy."

* * *

I checked the rent. Of course I checked the rent; obedience was the one skill I had never needed to practice.

The strongbox held eleven thousand kruna. The ledgers said we had disbursed four hundred thousand over the last fiscal year alone. By any arithmetic known to banking, the guild should have collapsed in 1974 and every year since. Instead the landlord took our worthless coins, the grocer took them, the lamplighter took them, and none of them could tell you, if you asked, what they'd spent them on.

Unless the kruna weren't currency at all. Unless the whole city was running on the same exchange the guild was: memory for forgetfulness, substance for shadow, and everyone politely declining to audit the account because the alternative was noticing that the ground under Vellmark—if there was a Vellmark, if there had ever been anything under anything—was made of other people's surrendered pasts.

I stood in the counting room with the strongbox open and the evening coming on, and I did the thing I am best at, the thing I had done my whole invisible life: I watched, and I understood, and I told no one.

Then I went back up to the third floor, to the shelf where Ledger 201 lived, and I took it down, and I held it, and I hated it, and I loved it, and I understood at last that these things were not opposites. Every book I had treasured here was a cell block. Every elegant entry was a sentence handed down. Somewhere in the city, a woman named Marta Ellingsen had once laughed on a ferry with her daughter, and the laugh lived in a bottle on my shelf, doing labor, holding shut a border, and the woman had gone home lighter and poorer and had thanked the clerk.

I put the ledger back precisely where it belonged, spine aligned, because even in horror I am tidy—it is my worst quality and my best one.

And that was when I heard the knock at the guild's front door.

Three knocks. Even, unhurried, official. The kind of knock that comes with paperwork. In seventy years of records, the Guild of Impossible Things had received exactly four visitors who knocked like that, and three of them had been tax assessors, and the fourth—

The fourth had been a Custodian.

Through the window, in the blue failing light, I could see a figure on the step. A woman. Tall, coat buttoned to the throat, carrying a leather case the way soldiers carry rifles: comfortably, and like it might go off.

She looked up—at the window, at the glass, directly at me.

Nobody looks at me. It is practically my title. But this woman's gaze found mine through two panes and forty feet of dusk as easily as a hand finds a light switch, and she held it, and she did not look away, and I realized with a lurch that I knew her face—from where? From years ago, from a customs post, from a dream I'd never had?

From everywhere, said the feeling in my chest. From nowhere, said my caution. From the forbidden files, said the deep archive's index, entry 3,091, cross-referenced: *Custodian liaison, assigned to observe the guild. Note: subject watches the watcher. Recommend rotation.*

Recommended, and never carried out. For years.

She knocked again, patient as arithmetic, and Oren shuffled toward the door saying, "Visitors! How unusual," and I stood frozen on the stairs between the caged books and the opening door, holding in my head the entire secret economy of a stolen country, watching my future walk up the steps in a buttoned coat.
