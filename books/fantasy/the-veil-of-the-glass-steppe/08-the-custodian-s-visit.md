## 8. The Custodian's Visit

*Ilse*

The notice had gone out on Tuesday: a routine integrity audit of licensed memory-trading establishments in the Lower Vell district. Ilse Marchetti had signed the routing slip herself, which meant she had signed her own ambush.

She stood outside the Guild of Impossible Things at half past nine in the morning, holding a leather folio she did not need, wearing the grey coat of her office, and hating how carefully she had dressed beneath it. The guild building leaned against its neighbors like a tired uncle at a wedding—narrow, soot-colored, its brass sign so weathered it read now as *GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THIN S*, which Ilse privately thought was more accurate.

She had audited forty-one establishments. She knew the choreography. Announce yourself, request the ledgers, note discrepancies, leave a carbon copy, depart before anyone offers you tea. The choreography existed precisely so that no one would ever have to be a person to anyone else.

The door opened before she knocked.

"You're early," said Sebastian Winterbourne. "Custodians are always punctual, but you're *early*. That's either zeal or nerves."

"Inspection," Ilse said, and produced the folio like a shield held slightly too low.

"Of course." Sebastian stepped back to admit her, and something in the gesture—the small courtesy of it, the way her hand hovered near the small of Ilse's back and then thought better of it—made the whole morning tilt two degrees off true.

The entry hall smelled of paper dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Shelves climbed the walls in defiance of several principles of engineering. A jar on the third shelf hummed with contained thunder, sulking. Ilse catalogued all of it automatically, the way she catalogued everything, and then catalogued Sebastian instead: ink on the left middle finger, a fresh bandage on the thumb, hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual pencil, which was either carelessness or a message about how little Ilse's visit mattered.

It was not a message. Ilse knew this because she had spent six years learning to read Sebastian Winterbourne the way Customs officers read manifests—looking for the one item declared incorrectly.

"The ledgers are in the reading room," Sebastian said. "There are eleven hundred and forty of them. Would you like them chronologically or by weight?"

"Ilse."

"Sorry. *Inspector.* Would you like them chronologically or by weight, Inspector Marchetti."

"Chronologically," Ilse said gravely, "and I'll want the heaviest three separately."

Something flickered at the corner of Sebastian's mouth—a suppressed laugh, quickly filed away—and Ilse felt the old dangerous warmth arrive right on schedule, punctual as a Custodian, early as a woman in love.

* * *

*Sebastian*

The problem with Inspector Marchetti, Sebastian reflected while leading her down the corridor, was that she inspected things even when she wasn't inspecting them. Her eyes moved across the shelves the way a lighthouse moves across water—steadily, impartially, and with the implication that anything surfacing would be seen.

Sebastian had prepared for this visit for two days. She had dusted the public ledgers, hidden the prophecy folio from 1899 inside a hollowed-out atlas of shipping routes, and rehearsed eleven different casual opening lines, of which she had deployed the worst one.

Now the inspector sat at the reading table with the first ledger open before her, gloved hands turning pages, and Sebastian sat opposite with a second ledger she was not reading, performing diligence.

"You've reorganized," Ilse said without looking up.

"We reorganize constantly. It's part of impossible maintenance."

"Mm. The 1870s ledgers used to be on the east wall. Now they're south, under the window."

"That," Sebastian said, "is a remarkable thing to remember."

"I remember things. It's my profession." Ilse turned a page. "You have a new bandage."

"A paper cut."

"From what?"

"*Ledgers*, Inspector. They're sharp-edged objects. That's practically our motto."

Ilse looked up then, and for a moment the official grey of her eyes went somewhere warmer and more complicated, and Sebastian's heart performed a small illegal maneuver. Then the moment closed like a file.

"Your acquisition records," Ilse said, tapping the page, "show a withdrawal last month. One memory, category: domestic, provenance: the Salt Gate quarter. Purchased by whom?"

"The guild buys forgetfulness from citizens who ask to sell. It's in the charter."

"Purchased by *whom*, apprentice Winterbourne."

And there it was—that slight drop in temperature, the voice going flat as a customs stamp. Sebastian made herself meet it evenly. "Guildmaster Voss. He signs for most acquisitions."

"Voss." Ilse wrote the name down, though Sebastian would have bet the entire inventory of borrowed thunder that she already knew it. "He's been guildmaster long?"

"Fifty years, more or less. He'd remember exactly, but—" Sebastian stopped.

"But?" Ilse asked softly, and it was the softness that undid her more than the question.

"He forgets things," Sebastian said. "Increasingly. We tell ourselves it's age."

Ilse wrote nothing for a long moment. The lamp between them hissed. Somewhere upstairs a shelf settled with a sigh, the way old buildings do when they think no one is listening.

"My grandmother forgot things too," Ilse said at last, not looking up. "At the end she called me by my mother's name, and my mother by her sister's, and once she called me *little bird*, which no one had ever called me. I used to sit with her and let her get me wrong, because being gotten wrong was still being gotten." She turned another page. "I'm noting your guildmaster's condition as a staffing concern. Not a violation."

"Thank you," Sebastian said, meaning something much larger, and both of them pretended otherwise.

* * *

*Ilse*

By noon they had worked through sixty years of ledgers, and Ilse had found nothing—which was itself suspicious, because everyone hid something, and finding nothing meant the hiding was good. Or it meant the guild kept its real books elsewhere, and she could not afford to wonder where, because she suspected she already knew, and knowing would obligate her.

They broke for lunch, if it could be called that. Sebastian produced bread, hard cheese, two apples, and a jar of pickles labeled in faded ink: *CONFISCATED, 1911, DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT WITNESS.*

"Is that a joke?" Ilse asked.

"Everything here is a joke eventually. That's what impossibility decays into." Sebastian opened the pickles anyway, witnessless, defiant, and pushed the jar across the table. Their fingers met on the glass.

It should have been nothing. Two hands on a pickle jar, for heaven's sake. But the touch ran up Ilse's arm like a current finding ground, and she saw it land in Sebastian too—a stillness, a caught breath, the particular paralysis of someone standing on ice and hearing it sing.

Neither pulled away immediately. That was the error. A Custodian would have pulled away instantly. An innocent person would have pulled away after a natural beat. They held the jar between them for three full seconds, which is enough time to remember a customs post on a border that officially does not exist, and a young officer with chapped hands checking papers, and a girl with a satchel full of contraband novels who had argued with her about the translation of a single word until the queue behind them mutinied.

*Vellmarkish has no word for 'homeland' separate from 'hearth,'* the girl had insisted. *So when your form asks for 'country of origin,' it's really asking where your fire is.*

*That's a poetic interpretation of a bureaucratic field,* Officer Marchetti had said.

*All fields are poetic if you read them honestly,* the girl had said, and stamped herself through with a smile, and walked into the country that wasn't there, and Ilse had watched her go and understood that her own fire had just been declared at customs and never reclaimed.

Sebastian took her hand off the jar.

"More pickles?" Sebastian asked, voice only slightly wrecked.

"No, thank you." Ilse withdrew her own hand and consulted her folio with tremendous professionalism. "I have questions about the 1954 acquisitions."

"Everyone does. 1954 was a strange year."

"In what way?"

"In every way available at the time," Sebastian said.

* * *

*Sebastian*

The afternoon audit proceeded. Sebastian answered questions with the truth wherever the truth was boring and with bureaucracy wherever it was interesting, and Ilse accepted this arrangement with the grace of someone running the same con from the other side. They were, Sebastian realized around four o'clock, having the most honest conversation of her life conducted entirely in code, each of them fluent, each of them pretending not to notice the fluency.

"Item," said Ilse. "Your thunder jar. License class?"

"Borrowed. It goes back."

"To whom?"

"A storm in the hills above Kesselring. We borrowed it in 1902 for a funeral. The family wanted the sky to mean it."

Ilse's pen paused. "Did it work?"

"It's still working. Thunder keeps better than you'd think. It's grief-adjacent; they store well together."

"And when you return it? To a storm fifty years dispersed?"

Sebastian looked at the jar, humming its low sulky note. "Then we'll have kept something alive past its warranty. The guild considers that the entire job description."

Ilse wrote something. Sebastian craned, shamelessly. Ilse tilted the folio away.

"Inspector."

"Apprentice."

"You wrote either 'no violation' or 'she's lovely when she talks about storms.' I'd like to know which, for the record."

"The record," Ilse said, closing the folio, "is sealed."

But her ears had gone pink at the tips, and Sebastian carried that image away like contraband of her own, to be examined later by lamplight, repeatedly, with commentary.

* * *

*Ilse*

At five o'clock the audit ended, as audits must, with forms in triplicate and a carbon copy left on the hall table. Ilse packed her folio. Forty-one establishments, and in none of them had she lingered like this, inventing questions about thunder, stretching sixty years of ledgers to their absolute interpretive limit.

At the door she stopped. Procedure required a closing statement: *This establishment remains subject to further review.* She had said it forty times.

"This establishment," she began, and faltered, because Sebastian was looking at her with the late light behind her and the pencil still in her hair, and procedure died quietly in Ilse's throat.

"Remains subject to further review," Sebastian finished for her, gently. "I know. You said it to the thunder jar on your way in."

"I did not."

"You absolutely did. It appreciated it. It hums louder for authority figures—it's very insecure since 1902."

Ilse laughed. It escaped before she could file it, a real laugh, unprofessional in every particular, and she saw it strike Sebastian like weather. For one unguarded instant they simply stood there, two women in a doorway of a building that shouldn't exist, in a country that didn't, holding a laugh between them the way they had held the pickle jar.

Then Sebastian reached out and, with two fingers, straightened the collar of Ilse's grey coat—an inspection of her own, brief and devastating—and said, "You had a smudge. From the ledgers. They mark everything they touch."

"I know," Ilse said. "It's why people fear them."

She went down the steps into the ordinary street, where nothing hummed and nothing was borrowed and every object stayed exactly what it claimed to be, and she found that she hated it, the perfect honesty of things that never touched you.

Behind her, the door did not close immediately. She heard it wait. She counted to ten before it shut, and she chose to believe those ten seconds were dust in the hinges, and she knew better, and she wrote neither fact in any report.

* * *

*Sebastian*

Sebastian bolted the door, leaned against it, and allowed herself exactly sixty seconds of being a person with feelings before resuming her duties.

Then she went to the hollowed atlas, drew out the 1899 folio, and set it on the reading table beside the lamp, beside the cold teacup Ilse had left, which Sebastian had no intention of washing and every intention of keeping, a fact she examined with mounting alarm.

Tonight's date sat on the folio's brittle page in a century-dead hand. Beside it: her name. Beside that: *the Door Out.*

An audit, today of all days. A Custodian whose eyes remembered where the 1870s ledgers stood. Whose fingers had known the weight of a pickle jar as if jars were sacred texts. Whose collar had smelled, absurdly, of cedar and cold air and something Sebastian refused to name because naming things gave them jurisdiction over you, and she had learned that from the ledgers too.

Coincidence, she told the empty room. Bureaucracies audit everyone eventually. The world is large and mostly indifferent.

The room declined to agree. Upstairs, Oren Voss was pacing his study, talking to someone who wasn't there, in a language Sebastian had begun, with dread, to recognize.

She put the folio away, and was reaching for the lamp when the sound came again—Oren's voice, thin and urgent through the floorboards, saying clearly: "*I signed it. God help me, I signed it,*" and then nothing at all, a silence with edges on it.

Sebastian picked up the lamp.
