## 9. Oren's Last Entry

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its dying master in the room above the counting house, where the ceiling sagged like a tired eyebrow and the window looked out on the one street in Vellmark Quarter that still reliably appeared each morning. Oren Voss had asked for that room forty years ago, they said, because he liked to watch people go to work. Lately he watched them and could not say what work was.

Sebastian climbed the stairs with a tray she had assembled with more care than the task strictly required: barley tea, honey from a jar whose label read *borrowed from summer, return by autumn*, and two butter biscuits shaped — through some long-dead apprentice's joke — like keys. The guild's inventory was thinning. Its masters were thinner still.

"Oren?" She knocked with her elbow, tray balanced. "It's Thursday. I think it's Thursday."

From inside came a voice like a door opening in an empty house. "Then I owe someone an apology for Tuesday."

She smiled before she opened the door, which was a habit she'd learned early: smile first, so your face is ready when his isn't.

Oren Voss sat in his chair by the window wrapped in a quilt that had once been impossible — it had supposedly been woven from the pause between lightning and thunder, and had kept three generations of guildmasters warm without ever being washed. These days it was just a quilt, soft and gray, losing its impossibility thread by thread like everything else in the building. He was a tall man gone stooped, white-haired, with hands that had once signed documents that rearranged reality and now trembled lifting cups. His eyes were the problem. They were clear. That was the cruelty of his particular forgetting: he didn't fade into vagueness like the neighbors did when they misremembered the weather. He faded *selectively*, whole decades dropping out of him at random, while the present stayed sharp as a ledger line.

"You're the girl with the ledgers," he said, as he said most mornings.

"I'm the girl with the ledgers," Sebastian agreed. She set down the tray. "And you're the master who owes Tuesday an apology."

"Do I know you?"

"Every other day," she said gently.

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and took his tea. "Ha. Good answer. Sit, ledger-girl. You have ink on your chin."

She did not have ink on her chin. She checked anyway, because it gave him pleasure to be right about something small.

* * *

For an hour they made the morning together, the way they did on his good days. He quizzed her on the inventory — a ritual he insisted on even now, though half the time he forgot why the items mattered.

"The jar of borrowed thunder," he said. "Status."

"Corked. It grumbles if you look at it sideways. Third shelf, behind the rain we haven't paid for yet."

"And the key?"

"To the house that burned." Her voice went careful around that one; it always did. "Still works. We checked last month. It opens any door in any wall, but only onto that kitchen, that morning, the kettle just going on. Mistress Aldery used to visit it every Sunday until—"

"Until she couldn't find her way back out," Oren finished quietly. "Yes. I remember that part. That's the trouble with doors to the past, ledger-girl. The past remembers you better than you remember yourself, and it holds on."

He drank his tea and looked out the window at the clerks and bakers going by below, and for a moment his face did something complicated.

"What were we, exactly?" he asked. "This guild. I know the words. Impossible things. Catalogued, brokered, preserved. I've said those words ten thousand times, I expect, the way you say grace or a national anthem. But today the words are just shapes in my mouth. What *were* we?"

Sebastian hesitated. Since the archive — since the treaty that 'resolved' a country that was somehow still collecting rent in its own currency — she had been carrying questions like stones in her pockets, and every day they got heavier. She wanted, badly, to ask him. *Master, what happened fifty years ago? Why does the city flicker at dusk? Why do our ledgers buy forgetfulness?*

But his good days were fragile. She knew what too many questions did to him: the war would come up, or the Accord, or whatever lay underneath all of it, and his memory would fray at the edges like wet paper, and he would sit staring at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger who had done something terrible. Once he had wept for twenty minutes without being able to say why, and she had held his hand and hated whatever it was he couldn't tell her.

"We were keepers," she said instead. "Of things the world wasn't ready to lose."

He nodded slowly, satisfied, and for a while there was only tea and biscuits and the ordinary music of the street below. Sebastian let herself pretend, the way she did most mornings, that this was the whole truth of her life: an old man, a crumbling guild, a tray of biscuits shaped like keys. Loneliness wore diligence very well. Nobody ever suspected it.

* * *

It was nearly noon when the change came.

She was gathering the tray, already composing her afternoon in her head — the deep archive again, the treaty folios, the rent receipts stamped with a dead country's crown — when Oren set down his cup with a click of absolute finality.

"Close the door," he said.

She looked up. Something in his voice had shifted registers entirely. The vagueness was gone; the trembling was gone. He sat straighter than he had sat in a year, and his eyes were not the eyes of a man losing himself. They were the eyes of a man who has been waiting, patiently, for his own mind to come home for a visit, knowing the visit would be short.

"Master—"

"Close the door, Sebastian Winterbourne." Her full name, in his mouth, landed like a coin dropped down a well. "We have perhaps two hours before I go under again, and I have spent eleven months of bad days saving up for these two hours. I will not waste them being gentle. Close the door."

She closed the door.

"Now sit where I can see you clearly. Good. Look at me. Do you know how difficult it is, forgetting? Not the losing — anyone can lose things, losing is easy, the world does it constantly. The difficulty is knowing what you've lost. Waking up every morning missing a shape in yourself and not being permitted to name it." He pressed his palms flat on the arms of his chair, steadying himself. "They took my memory of the war, ledger-girl. Piece by piece, year by year, the way you'd eat a loaf so no one notices it shrinking. And I let them. I *signed* for it. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Her heart had begun to knock against her ribs. "No," she said honestly.

"Good. Honest. You'll need that." He drew a breath. "Fifty years ago there was a border war. You won't find it in any book now — I made sure of that myself, in the end, God forgive me — but there was a war. Two countries, Vellmark and its neighbor across the Salt Steppe, grinding each other down for nine years. Nine winters of it. My brother Tomas was a cartographer on the northern front. Do you see? A *cartographer*. He drew maps of places that then stopped existing, and he wrote to me that he had begun to dream in erased lines."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "I found the treaty," she whispered. "In the archive. Vellmark was 'resolved.' But the rent—"

"—is still paid in vellmarks, yes, because the country was never destroyed." Oren's voice was flat and precise, the voice of a man reciting a confession he had rehearsed through a thousand foggy mornings. "It was *veiled*. Folded out of the world's attention. There is a craft for such things — older than this guild, older than the Custodians who inherited it. A veil laid over a whole nation so that both armies would simply... lose interest. Forget why they were fighting. Forget there was anything on the other side of the line worth a single grave."

"But the war ended," Sebastian said. "That's — that's a mercy, isn't it? However it was done—"

"The war ended." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she had ever heard from him. "The war ended and nobody remembered it had been fought. The dead were buried by parents who couldn't recall having children. The veterans woke in their own beds to wives they loved and could not place. And the ones who built the veil discovered it needed feeding. Memory, ledger-girl. It eats memory. Every entry in every ledger downstairs is a recollection somebody sold so that the forgetting would hold. The Guild of Impossible Things has spent fifty years buying the raw material of a lie and calling it preservation."

The floor seemed to tilt. All those entries in her beloved books — *purchased: one summer afternoon, fair condition*; *acquired: a mother's lullaby, seller wept, price negotiated* — she had thought them whimsical, tender, the guild keeping safe what grief would otherwise destroy. Cages. Every page a cage, and she the devoted keeper of cages, oiling their locks.

"Why are you telling me this?" she managed. "Why now?"

"Because I am the only signer of the Veiling Accord still alive, and my mind is leaving, and when it goes the last honest witness goes with it." He leaned forward. "I signed it to save my brother. That's the whole ugly arithmetic of it. They came to me — the men who devised the veil — and they showed me the casualty projections, and they said, Master Voss, your guild trades in impossibilities; here is the greatest possible thing anyone will ever ask of you: peace, purchased with remembering. And they told me Tomas would die in the spring offensive unless the war stopped *now*. So I signed. And they brought Tomas home safe, and he lived forty years, and he never once remembered that he had ever been away." Oren's hands had stopped trembling. That frightened her more than the shaking had. "I have had fifty years to decide whether I am a monster or a savior, and I will tell you the answer: I am a man who saved one person and unmade a country, and the bill has come due."

* * *

He rose, then — rose easily, the way he must have risen in his prime, crossing rooms full of ministers and marvels — and went to the wardrobe in the corner. From beneath a false bottom he lifted a box of dark wood, plain as a coffin for a sparrow, and carried it back to her.

Inside, on faded velvet, lay a seal.

It was smaller than she expected: a disc of some metal she couldn't name, neither silver nor pewter nor anything that quite consented to be looked at directly, mounted on a handle worn smooth by centuries of hands. The face bore the guild's mark — the open book, the impossible star above it — but cut deeper than any stamp she'd seen, and around the rim ran letters in an alphabet that kept almost resolving into sense.

"This is the founding seal," Oren said. "Older than the Accord. Older than the Custodians' charter. With it, a guildmaster can open what the guild has closed — and close what the guild has opened. There are exactly two keys to the deep cellar of this building, ledger-girl. One is the lock on the door. The other is this. Take it."

"Master, I can't—"

"You can, and you will, and here is why." He pressed the box into her hands; it was warm, absurdly warm, like something recently alive. "I watched you this morning. On my good days I watch everything, I hoard observations the way a drowning man hoards air. You check the inventory like it's a rosary. You rebind folios that don't need rebinding. You walk streets that are disappearing and count the lampposts to prove they existed." His voice gentled, and somehow that was worse. "You have known something is wrong for weeks. You went looking. And you came back up those stairs every morning and made me tea anyway, and never once asked me to carry it for you. That is either devotion or dread, child, and in my experience the two grow on the same vine."

Sebastian realized she was crying only when a tear hit the velvet beside the seal.

"There is a Door," Oren said quietly. "Out on the Glass Steppe, where the two borders used to meet. The founders of the guild wrote about it before there was a guild. The Custodians guard the road to it and call the guarding *maintenance*. And somewhere in that prophecy folio you've been rebinding — yes, I know which one, I may forget faces but I never forgot which books you carried — somewhere in it, your name is written next to a date. Tonight's date. Am I right?"

She could only nod.

"Then listen to me, because my time is draining and I want to spend the last of it well." He gripped her wrists, firm, fever-hot. "The veil is failing. You've seen it — the streets, the weather, the objects going ordinary on the shelves. It was never meant to be permanent. No veil is. It feeds until there's nothing left to feed on, and then it comes for its keepers, and then it comes for everything. The Custodians know. Some of them want to renew it at any cost. Some of them—" his mouth twisted "—want worse. Whatever is written in that folio, whatever the Door asks of you, remember one thing: the men who built the veil chose a shortcut through other people's lives. Don't take the same road. An impossible thing done honestly is worth a hundred miracles done by theft."

"Come with me," she said. It came out young and foolish and she didn't care. "When I go — come with me. We'll find it together."

Something moved across his face like weather. For a heartbeat he was fully, terribly present, and he looked at her the way a father looks at a daughter he has loved in stolen increments.

"Oh, my girl," he said. "I would give anything. But I already gave it. Fifty years ago, in ink." He released her wrists and stepped back, and she watched — actually watched — the lucidity begin to leave him, like tide going out of a harbor. His shoulders softened. His gaze drifted toward the window, toward the clerks going home in the late light. "Is it Thursday?" he asked pleasantly. "I believe I owe Tuesday an apology."

* * *

She stayed with him until dusk.

She made more tea he wouldn't drink. She told him stories he'd heard a hundred times — about the jar of thunder escaping down the stairs in 1911, about the apprentice who traded his shadow for a season of perfect pitch — and he laughed in the right places, because the stories lived deeper in him than the dates did. Twice he called her by names that weren't hers: Margit, once, and once, softly, *Tomas*. She answered to all of them.

Near midnight he slept, quilt to his chin, breath whistling faintly, his face in sleep smooth and unburdened as a boy's. Sebastian tucked the seal-box inside her coat, against her ribs, where it beat its slow warmth like a second heart.

At the door she paused. "Goodnight, Master Voss," she said. "It's Thursday. Everything's forgiven."

"Night, night, ledger-girl," he murmured, eyes still closed. Then, drifting, almost gone: "Mind the Steppe. Mind the glass. It remembers..."

By dawn, when she crept back up with the breakfast tray, he smiled at her with complete, courteous blankness and said:

"Forgive me, miss — have we met?"

She set down the tray. She did not correct him. Outside the window, over the rooftops of the quarter, the dawn light flickered once — twice — like a lamp running low on oil, and in her coat the founding seal pulsed warm against her heart, and somewhere far off across the flatlands, on a plain of glass, a Door that had been shut for fifty years dreamed of the sound of her name.
