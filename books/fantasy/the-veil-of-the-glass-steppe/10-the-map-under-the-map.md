## 10. The Map Under the Map

The seal was smaller than I expected.

I had imagined—after a sleepless night turning it over in my mind—that the founding seal of the Guild of Impossible Things would be some grand thing: a medallion the size of a saucer, heavy with gilt and consequence. Instead Oren had pressed into my palm a disc of dull brass no wider than a coin, worn smooth as a river stone, stamped with an emblem so faint I had to hold it to the lamp to read it at all.

A door. Standing open.

"He kept it in his boot," I whispered to the empty ledger room. "Fifty years, he kept it in his *boot*."

That was Oren all over. The guild's grandest treasures were always hidden in the least ceremonial places—the jar of borrowed thunder shelved between pickle crocks, the key to a house that burned filed under K for "kitchen, former." A man who understood that impossible things survive only when nobody thinks to look at them. And now that man sat upstairs by the window with his hands folded, watching the street with polite incomprehension, unable to tell me his own name before noon.

He had been lucid exactly once since dawn. Once was enough.

"Cellar," he'd said, gripping my wrist with surprising strength, pressing the seal against my palm and closing my fingers over it like a man hiding a bird from a cat. "Under the ledgers. It's been waiting for someone who'd ask it properly. Not *open*, Sebastian. Ask." His eyes had cleared then, blue and terrible, and he'd added, almost gently: "And whatever you find down there—remember that everything true has a bill attached."

Then the fog had rolled back over him, and he'd smiled at me the way one smiles at a kind stranger on a train, and asked if I knew where his brother had got to, because dinner would be getting cold.

*Everything true has a bill attached.* I turned the seal over and over as I descended the stairs behind the archive, past the shelf of folios nobody dusted, down steps that grew colder underfoot with each landing. The guild had seven floors above ground and—I had always assumed—a shallow root cellar for coal. I had worked here eleven years. I had never once thought to wonder why the coal cellar door was locked with three locks, or why the locks were made by different smiths in different centuries, or why the lowest lock bore an inscription I'd never bothered to translate because it was in Old Vellmarkish and I had told myself, in the way we tell ourselves small comfortable lies, that it probably just said *keep out.*

It did not say keep out.

It said: **ASK PROPERLY.**

* * *

I knelt on the cold flagstones with the lantern beside me and the seal in my hand, feeling profoundly ridiculous. This was the moment, I realized, where every sensible person would turn around. I had spent two weeks discovering that my city might be a forgery, my guild a cage, my beloved ledgers full of stolen lives—and my response had been to keep cataloging inventory, because cataloging was the only prayer I knew. Now here I was about to knock on a door in a coal cellar with a dead man's boot-button.

"Right," I said aloud, to hear a voice in the dark. "Asking properly."

I pressed the seal to the lock.

For a long moment, nothing. Then the brass grew warm—not hot, but warm the way a hand is warm—and the inscription lit from within like embers breathed upon. The three locks did not open. They *withdrew*, folding back into the door itself the way a snail folds its eyes into its head, and the door swung inward without a sound, revealing not coal, not darkness—

Revealing light.

Not lamplight. Not daylight. A soft gold radiance rising from below like the glow of a city seen from a hill at dusk, and up through it came a smell I had no business recognizing and recognized instantly, completely, helplessly: cut hay and river water and woodsmoke and the particular green smell of rain about to arrive over farmland. The smell of a country I had never visited. The smell of home, if home were somewhere I'd lost before I could walk.

"Oh," I said. It was not a word so much as an exhale that had given up pretending to be language.

I went down.

* * *

The cellar was larger than the guild above it. Considerably larger. I stopped trying to do the arithmetic after the first hundred steps of spiral stair, because the arithmetic kept insisting the room below was bigger than the entire building, and the room below kept agreeing, and I have learned that when impossibility presents its credentials you simply check them and move along. That was the whole trade, after all.

At the bottom, the light resolved, and I stood at the edge of Vellmark.

Not a picture of it. Not a model. Vellmark herself—or what remained of her—laid out across a floor of dark glass that stretched farther than my lantern could argue with. Rivers ran there, actual rivers, threads of silver water moving with real current though they were thin enough to step across. Forests stirred. Mountain passes wore their snow like old lace. And the roads—Oren had said *the roads are still warm*, and I hadn't believed him, because how could I—but I crouched at the edge of a country lane no wider than my hand and laid two fingers against it, and it was warm. Warm as pavement at evening after a hot day. Warm as something alive.

"It's a map," I said to myself, uselessly, because it wasn't. Maps don't breathe. This did. I watched a tiny weather front drift across a valley of glass-green fields, watched it rain there, watched the rain darken the little fields, and felt tears startle me by arriving without permission.

Because I knew this place. Not from books—from somewhere deeper and less trustworthy. That bend in the river, where the water went silver and slow. Those hills with their crowns of pine. I had dreamed that bend in the river my whole life and assumed dreams invented such things. Dreams don't invent. They *remember carelessly*, my grandmother used to say—except I didn't remember having a grandmother who said things, which was precisely the sort of hole in me I had learned not to poke.

The map remembered for both of us.

I walked the border of the country like a pilgrim, careful not to let my shadow fall across any village longer than courtesy allowed. Here was the western march, where the war had started—here the glass went cloudy and scarred, and the roads cooled under my fingers, and one town near the old frontier flickered like a candle in wind, present, absent, present, holding on. And there, dead center, glowing.

I had been walking toward it without deciding to, the way water finds the low place. At the heart of Vellmark lay a plain that was not painted like the rest. It was pure white-glass, featureless, radiant, and it threw its light up at me with the patient insistence of something that has been waiting a very long time to be looked at.

The Glass Steppe.

I knew the name the way I knew the river's bend—from nowhere, from everywhere, from the prophecy folio with tonight's date written beside my own name. On the great living map, the Steppe glowed like a lamp under silk, and at its center stood a shape so simple it took me a moment to understand what I was seeing.

A doorway. Just a doorway, standing alone on the empty plain. No wall around it. No building. A frame of light with nothing through it but more light.

"The Door Out," I whispered, and the Steppe brightened at the words, and every warm road in the country pulsed once, all together, like a heartbeat answering its name.

I sat down right there on the cold stone floor, because my legs had opinions and none of them involved remaining vertical.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat. Long enough that the lantern burned low. Long enough that I talked to the map, quietly, the way you talk to the sick—to the flickering frontier towns, to the cool roads, telling them somebody had come, somebody was asking questions now, somebody intended to be inconvenient about all of this.

It was during this conversation that I noticed the price tag.

I want to be honest about how I found it, because I found it the way I find most things: by being fussy about bookkeeping. The map was beautiful, but beauty makes me suspicious—it's too easy a place to hide things. So I crawled along the Steppe's glowing rim looking for seams, for joins, for the places where a forgery shows its work, and instead of flaws I found writing.

Old Vellmarkish again, inscribed around the Steppe's edge in letters fine as stitching. I translated slowly, lips moving, and the translation went through me like cold water:

**THE DOOR OPENS FOR THE DOUBLE-BORN AND DEMANDS WEIGHT IN EXCHANGE. WHAT IS GIVEN CANNOT BE KEPT BY THE GIVER. LET THE ONE WHO ASKS COUNT FIRST WHAT SHE CAN SPARE, FOR THE DOOR DOES NOT MAKE CHANGE.**

*Does not make change.* A shopkeeper's phrase. A phrase from market stalls and coin-counters, from the ordinary world of haggling and receipt. The Door Out was a merchant, and its currency was memory, and it gave no credit.

I sat back on my heels. Around me the little country breathed—rivers running, forests stirring, roads keeping their warmth the way a sleeper keeps warmth, stubbornly, without knowing why. Somewhere up above, Oren was watching a street he couldn't name, and the fog was eating him one afternoon at a time, and I understood suddenly, completely, that the fog and the map were the same weather. Every memory that frayed upstairs was a road cooling down here. My mentor's mind was being spent, coin by coin, and I had never known I was spending it.

And if the Door demanded weight—if it wanted a life's worth of remembering in exchange for a country's worth of returning—then the arithmetic was simple and obscene. One person's whole remembered self against several million people's forgotten ones. The Door did not make change, and neither, apparently, did history.

"Fine," I told the Steppe. My voice cracked on it, which undercut the bravado somewhat, but the Steppe was polite enough not to mention it. "Fine. I'll count what I can spare."

I tried. Right there in the dark, I sat and took inventory of myself, the way I take inventory of everything, because it is the only prayer I know. The jar of borrowed thunder: mine. The taste of the guild's terrible coffee: mine. Eleven years of ledgers, of Oren calling me "my girl" on his good days and "young woman" on his middling ones: mine. Ilse Marchetti's face across a customs-house desk, and the way she had touched my hand over a ledger last week and left a warmth on it that I had refused, professionally, to think about for six entire days: mine, apparently, whether I liked it or not.

But underneath all of it, when I reached for the earliest shelves of myself, I kept finding gaps. Rooms in the house of my childhood with the furniture missing. A mother whose voice I could describe but not hear. A father I could locate in space but not in time. I had always assumed everyone's past was moth-eaten like this. Sitting on the cold stone beneath a breathing country, I began to suspect otherwise.

The double-born. Born of both sides of a border that officially never existed. Of course my past had holes in it. Holes were the family resemblance.

I laughed—one short, unlovely bark of a laugh that echoed strangely off a country that wasn't there—and the Steppe pulsed at the sound, and every warm road pulsed with it, and far away on the flickering frontier a little town held steady for a moment longer than it should have, as if my laugh had been a log thrown on its fire.

"All right," I said. "You've made your point. You're worth saving. Everyone down here is worth saving. But I'm going to need help, and I'm going to need time, and I am *terrible* at both."

The map, being a map, offered no reply. But as I climbed the long spiral stair with my lantern guttering, I felt the warmth of the roads at my back like a hand between my shoulders, gentle and insistent, pushing me upward toward the world of clocks and coffee and Custodians, where I would have to explain to a guild of dwindling eccentrics that our coal cellar contained a nation, and that the nation had named its price, and that the price was me.

On the top step I paused. Below, faint as a nightlight under a door, the Steppe still glowed.

And I noticed—for the first time, with the particular dread of a bookkeeper spotting an entry that doesn't balance—that the light was not steady. It flickered. Slowly, patiently, at the pace of decades rather than seconds, the Door Out was dimming.

Whatever window the prophecy meant, it was already beginning to close.

Someone else, I realized, climbing into the cold ordinary air of the archive, was going to have to be told tonight.
