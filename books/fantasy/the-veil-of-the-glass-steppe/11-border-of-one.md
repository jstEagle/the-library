## 11. Border of One

The audit form had eleven lines, and Ilse Marchetti lied on nine of them.

She sat at her desk in the Custodian annex—a building so deliberately unremarkable that pigeons refused to nest in it—and filled in the blanks with the tidy, blameless handwriting she'd practiced for fifteen years. *Site inspected: Guild of Impossible Things, Vellmark Quarter. Veil integrity: nominal. Personnel questioned: none of consequence. Anomalies: none.*

The ninth lie was the hardest. *Apprentice Winterbourne: no irregular activity.* Her pen hovered over it the way a hand hovers over a stove you already know is hot.

"Nominal," she said aloud, tasting the word. It was such a small word for such an enormous treason. Somewhere in the capital, Warden Kessler was probably using it about his breakfast.

She signed, stamped, and filed the audit into the morning courier pouch, then sat very still and listened for the sound of her soul leaving her body. It didn't leave. It just shifted its weight, uncomfortably, like a cat asked to move off a warm chair.

*In increments,* she reminded herself. That was how she'd learned to do impossible things—before she'd ever heard of the guild that traded in them. You didn't betray your order all at once. You did it the way a river takes down a bridge: patiently, drop by drop, until one morning the whole structure is downstream and everyone acts surprised.

Her report would buy Sebastian perhaps two weeks. Two weeks in which no second audit would be scheduled, no watchers assigned to the Vellmark Quarter, no questions asked about why a guild apprentice had been pulling prophecy folios from the deep archive with hands that shook.

Two weeks. It sounded like nothing. It felt like everything.

* * *

Five years ago, at the customs post at Grayfen Crossing, Ilse Marchetti had been very good at her job and quietly miserable about it.

The post was a wooden hut with a stove that smoked when the wind came from the east and sulked when it came from anywhere else. Her duty was to check papers against lists, to stamp what matched, and to log what didn't. What never appeared on any list, because officially there was nothing there to appear, were the travelers who crossed the border without crossing it—people who walked through the fog line and came out the other side with their memories neatly trimmed, like hedges.

Ilse logged them anyway, in a private notebook, because someone had to remember that these people had once been whole. She hadn't yet understood that this impulse—the compulsion to write things down before they vanished—was the exact opposite of everything the Custodians stood for.

It was raining sideways that day, the Grayfen special, rain that seemed personally offended by umbrellas. And through the door of the hut came a young woman soaked to the ribs, holding over her head not an umbrella but a large leather ledger, which she had clearly decided was more valuable than dry.

"I need to declare something," the woman announced, water streaming off her chin with tremendous dignity.

"This is a border post," Ilse said. "You're going the wrong direction entirely."

"I'm always going the wrong direction. It's a professional condition." She set the ledger on the counter with a wet, reverent thump. "I'm Sebastian Winterbourne, apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I am declaring—" she peeled back her sodden coat to reveal a birdcage covered with a cloth "—one thunderstorm, borrowed, due back Thursday."

Ilse looked at the cage. Something inside it grumbled, low and petulant, like a dog that had been asked to wait outside a bakery.

"You can't declare weather," Ilse said.

"It's not weather. Weather is free. This is *borrowed* thunder. There's paperwork." Sebastian produced, from somewhere inside her coat, a document so waterlogged that the ink had begun to migrate toward the edges, seeking freedom.

"That's soup," Ilse said.

"It's a *manifest.*"

"It's a manifest that has become soup."

Sebastian looked at her then—really looked, with an expression of such frank, exhausted curiosity that Ilse felt audited herself, thoroughly and not unpleasantly. "You're not going to stamp me through, are you," Sebastian said. "You're going to stand there and be *correct* at me until I give up."

"The thunder is unlicensed," Ilse agreed. "And your paperwork is inedible."

"And you're going to enjoy every second of it."

"I might," Ilse admitted, "enjoy some seconds of it."

What she did not say—and did not know yet, would take her three years and one burned file to say—was that the seconds stretched. That Sebastian stayed two hours waiting out the rain, drinking terrible tea from the post's dented kettle, asking questions no one asked Custodians (*what do you guard, exactly? and don't say 'the border,' borders don't need guarding, they need grieving*), and that when she finally left, taking her caged storm and her souped manifest with her, Ilse wrote her name in the private notebook. Not as a violation. As a keepsake.

Under it, after some consideration, she wrote: *declared one thunderstorm. Due back Thursday.*

It was not due back Thursday. But Ilse checked the crossing logs every Thursday for a month anyway, telling herself it was diligence.

Diligence. There was that word again. It kept showing up wherever she was lying to herself.

* * *

Back in the present, Kessler's office smelled of lamp oil and certainty.

He stood at his window with his hands clasped behind him, watching the capital the way a man watches soup he suspects is plotting against him. On his desk lay the week's integrity reports, and Ilse watched his finger travel down them, column by column, stopping at hers.

"Guild of Impossible Things," he read. "Nominal. Again." He turned. "Do you know what I find interesting about the Vellmark Quarter, Marchetti?"

"No, Warden."

"It's the only district where the veil doesn't cost us anything to maintain. Everywhere else, we feed it daily. Rationed memory, processed forgetfulness, the whole apparatus. But the Quarter—" he tapped the report "—the Quarter runs quiet. Like a room where somebody's already asleep." His eyes came up. "Or like a room where somebody's pretending to be asleep."

"Perhaps it's simply well-made," Ilse said. "The original Accord work was better than ours. They built it to last."

"They built it to last fifty years, Marchetti. The mortgage comes due soon, and I intend to be standing on the right side of it when it does." He smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that had been issued with the uniform. "Keep auditing. If anything stirs in that Quarter, I want to hear its heartbeat before it hears mine."

"Yes, Warden," Ilse said, and discovered she could still say it without her voice changing. Fifteen years of practice. Some skills you keep forever, like swimming or lying.

Walking back to her office, she passed the Hall of Schedules, where the veil's maintenance calendar hung in illuminated glass—the feeding times, the rationing rotations, the great slow pulse of forgetting laid out in gold ink. And near the bottom, marked only with a small administrative glyph that most clerks skimmed past, the anniversary notation:

*Veiling Accord: executed [date]. Annual reconciliation: [date].*

Three days' difference between the anniversary and the reconciliation audit. Three days in which the veil would be thinnest, busiest, most distracted—three days in which, if a person were inclined to walk out onto a plain of glass looking for a door, they would have their best chance in fifty years.

Ilse noted the dates. She did not write them down. Some things you carry in the body, where audits can't reach.

* * *

That evening she went to the Vellmark Quarter, which was against protocol, and stood across the street from the guildhall, which was worse, and watched the light in the third-floor window, which was practically a confession.

The window glowed amber. A silhouette moved behind it—bent over a desk, the particular hunch of someone reading something that is reading them back. Ilse knew that hunch. She had spent years learning silhouettes; it was a Custodian skill, reading people at distances where faces fail. But she had never needed the training with this one. She could have identified that shape in fog, in firelight, at the bottom of a well.

*I have been watching you for six years,* she thought at the window. *I have written forty-one reports about you, and thirty-nine of them were lies, and I have kept the two honest ones in my boot.*

Because that was the arithmetic of it, the ugly beautiful sum. Every falsified audit, every omitted name, every *no anomalies* —each one had been a brick in a wall between Sebastian and the order, and Ilse had laid every brick gladly, and each one had also been a brick in a wall between them, because a woman you protect by lying cannot afford to come close enough to catch you doing it.

Love against loyalty. Everyone assumed it was a war. Nobody warned you it was a siege you ran from the inside.

A lamplighter passed, touching flame to the Quarter's old gas lamps—lamps that shouldn't have worked anymore, on streets that were having trouble remembering to exist. One lamp flickered twice, guttered, and then steadied, as if it had briefly forgotten combustion and talked itself back into it.

"Hold on," Ilse murmured to it. To the street. To the whole stubborn, flickering Quarter. "Hold on a little longer."

From the third-floor window, faintly, came the sound of pages turning. Then—she would swear this later, and swear it cheerfully—a sneeze. Sebastian's sneeze was famous in Ilse's private taxonomy: less a sneeze than an argument the nose was losing.

Ilse laughed, alone in the dusk, a short helpless sound, and turned up her collar, and went home to write tomorrow's lie.

* * *

At midnight she took out the private notebook—the old one, the Grayfen notebook, kept now behind a loose brick in her quarters where no inspection had ever thought to look. She turned past years of entries. *Declared one thunderstorm.* Past the coded observations dressed as inventory notes. Past the page where, three winters ago, she had written a single sentence and then spent a week deciding whether to burn the book:

*If she asked me to choose, I would choose before she finished asking.*

She hadn't burned it. Instead she had done the Custodian thing, the incremental thing: she had filed the sentence under surveillance and told herself that knowing your own heart was simply intelligence-gathering.

Tonight she turned to a fresh page and wrote, in the smallest handwriting she could manage:

*Audit filed. Two weeks bought. The veil reconciles in three days—anniversary. If she's going to the Steppe, it must be then. I can slow the order but I cannot stop Kessler. He smells the mortgage coming due. God help whoever stands between him and that Door, because I've read his file and it isn't a man anymore, it's an ambition wearing a man.*

She stopped. Looked at the sentence. Decided, incrementally, to mean it.

Then, beneath it, because the notebook deserved honesty even if her reports didn't:

*I am going to help her. Not watch. Help. And when they ask me at the tribunal—I have imagined the tribunal four hundred times, I should get a hobby—I will say I did it for the country. Both countries. Any country. I will say a lot of true-sounding things, and underneath all of them will be one true thing, and it has a name, and she doesn't know it's hers.*

She closed the book, returned it behind the brick, and blew out the lamp.

Outside her window, far to the north, the sky above the Glass Steppe held a faint wrongness, a shimmer like heat-haze in cold air—the veil breathing in its sleep. Three days. Somewhere out there was a plain of glass that remembered everyone, and a seam of light older than treaties, and a woman with a talent for going the wrong direction professionally.

Ilse began, quietly, methodically, to pack.

The first thing into the bag was the old Grayfen manifest—the soup-stained original she had never filed, kept five years the way you keep a first wound. Underneath it went a change of clothes, a knife, and the boot-file: two honest reports, folded small.

The last thing she packed, though it weighed least, was the decision.

By dawn, if anyone checked—and someone eventually would—the audit trail would show a routine inspector traveling north for seasonal verification. By dawn, Ilse Marchetti, model Custodian, fifteen years of impeccable lies, would be a defector with a head start measured in hours.

She paused at her door and allowed herself one look back at the neat little life, the issued furniture, the lamp, the brick.

"Increments," she told the empty room. "Right up until suddenly, all at once."

She pulled the door shut behind her, and the sound of it closing was the best sound she had ever made.
