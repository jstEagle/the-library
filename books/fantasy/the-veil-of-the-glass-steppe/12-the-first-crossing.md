## 12. The First Crossing

The border of Vellmark was not where the maps said it was.

I had learned this over three days of walking with the founding seal in my coat pocket and the map-under-the-map folded into my memory like a letter I kept rereading. The official border ran along the River Senn, marked by customs posts and a fence the government had never bothered to take down after the treaty, because a fence is cheaper than an apology. But the cellar map had shown something else: a seam, faint as a hairline crack in teacup glaze, running north of the river through country that officially belonged to nobody and unofficially belonged to whoever had stopped asking questions about it.

That was where I stood at dawn on the fourth morning, my boots in wet grass, looking out at the Glass Steppe.

I had expected—though I would never have admitted expecting anything—to see a plain. Grass, maybe. Scrubland. The kind of terrain that exists between two countries precisely because neither wants it. Instead the land simply *stopped*, the way a sentence stops when the writer loses their nerve. Grass ended in a clean line, and beyond it lay glass.

Not a lake frozen over. Not a mirror laid flat. Glass the color of early morning, pale green-grey at its edges, deepening toward blue in the middle distance, stretching all the way to a horizon that seemed less like an edge than a suggestion. It looked solid. It looked like it might not be. Both impressions arrived simultaneously and refused to resolve, which, I was beginning to understand, was how everything about Vellmark worked.

"Well," I said, to no one, because Ilse was still a day behind me and Oren was a hundred miles back in his chair and the only other living creature in sight was a lark who had the good sense to stay on the grass side of the line. "You've catalogued stranger things."

This was true. I had catalogued a jar of borrowed thunder. I had catalogued a key to a house that burned down before the key's owner was born. I had spent six years filing impossibilities in alphabetical order, and none of them—not one—had ever asked me to step onto them.

I took off my left boot and set my bare foot on the glass.

It was cold. That was the first thing, and somehow the most reassuring: whatever else this place was, it was cold the way real things are cold, without philosophy. I put my weight forward. The glass held. I brought my right foot across the line, and the grass let go of me, and I was standing on the Steppe.

For about four seconds, nothing happened. I remember counting them, because counting is what I do when the world misbehaves. One: the glass held. Two: the light did something odd. Three: I understood what the light was doing. Four: I sat down very suddenly, because my knees had made the decision without consulting me.

Here is what the light was doing. The sky above me was ordinary—a grey-gold wash of dawn cloud, a few stars not yet gone. But the glass underfoot was reflecting something else entirely.

It reflected memories.

I don't mean it showed pictures. I mean that when I looked down, the surface gave back a Sebastian Winterbourne standing in a kitchen that did not exist, watching a woman with flour on her hands laugh at something I had apparently just said. The woman's face turned, in the reflection, toward the woman standing above her—and for one vertiginous moment our eyes met across a gap of fifty years and one total absence of recollection.

I knew her. That was the unbearable part. I didn't remember her—I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had left at that point—but I knew her the way you know a word you've read a thousand times but never once spoken aloud. She had my jaw. She had my habit of pushing hair behind the left ear while thinking. And she was laughing at me with the particular exasperated delight of someone who has laughed at me ten thousand times before.

"Mother," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable like ice on a puddle.

The reflection went on being itself. The flour-woman handed small-Sebastian something—a bowl, I thought, or a nest of some kind, something with a living thing in it—and the small girl in the glass took it with both hands, solemnly, the way children accept things that adults have told them matter.

I have no memory of any kitchen. I have checked. In the days since the folio, since the seal, since the map, I had gone through my own past the way I'd go through an inventory, shelf by shelf, looking for gaps. My childhood was there: the foundling ward in the capital, the Guild taking me in at nine, Master Voss teaching me ledger-hand, the long grey years of diligent invisibility. It was all there, complete, tidy, every entry accounted for.

It was too complete. That's what I hadn't let myself think until now. No child has a past that neat. No orphan remembers every year of the ward equally, without seams, without the soft lost stretches where ordinary life pools and blurs. My memory wasn't a memory. It was a *record*—and records, I now understood better than anyone alive, can be written by somebody else.

The guild traded in memories. I had known that for a week. What I hadn't understood—not truly, not in the marrow—was that I might be among the traded. Born of both sides of the border, the Echoes' logic required it; the prophecy's logic demanded it; and somewhere in some ledger, in some hand steadier or crueler than mine, someone had purchased the whole of Sebastian Winterbourne's first nine years and filed them away to feed a veil.

The glass showed me more, now that I had stopped flinching. It was patient. It had been waiting a long time, and it could wait out my terror too.

A boy—no, a girl, small, maybe five—running through a market street hung with drying peppers, both sides of the border visible at once in the stalls, Vellmarkish and Meridian shouted in the same breath by vendors who saw no contradiction. A man with my nose lifting a child onto his shoulders so she could see over a crowd gathered to watch—not soldiers, not a war, just fireworks, just a festival. A bedroom with two beds because there were two daughters, except in the glass there was only ever one daughter in focus and the other bed stayed empty in a way that made my chest hurt in a place I couldn't name.

"Stop," I whispered.

The glass did not stop. But it gentled, the way Oren gentled when he caught me crying over an inventory error in my first year—he hadn't scolded, he'd simply sat beside me and begun reading the ledger aloud in a boring voice until the crying became embarrassing and therefore impossible. The reflections slowed. They stopped demanding. They simply were, the way weather is, and I knelt on the cold glass in my stockinged feet and let them rain over me.

Here is the thing I discovered, kneeling there, that I have never told anyone quite correctly because there are no correct words for it: grief for a memory you don't have is not like other grief. Ordinary grief has an object. You mourn a person, a house, a year. This was mourning a hole shaped exactly like myself. Every scene in the glass fit me the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. That was my laugh. That was my stubborn chin. That was me, aged seven, refusing to come inside out of the rain because I had decided the garden needed me—and I remembered deciding things like that, remembered the *texture* of being that kind of child, and yet the specific child, the specific garden, the specific rain, were gone, requisitioned, filed under some accession number in a vault beneath a dying guild.

They had taken everything except the shape of me. They'd left the mold and poured out the casting.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, but it came out, because there was something darkly funny in it, something Master Voss himself would have appreciated: they had given me to a Guild of Impossible Things as if daring me to notice. They had hidden the theft inside the one profession equipped to detect it. Either tremendous carelessness or tremendous cruelty, and I genuinely did not know which.

* * *

I don't know how long I knelt there. The sun climbed. My knees complained. Eventually I did the only reasonable thing, which was the thing I always did: I got out my notebook.

If the Steppe wanted to show me things, then fine. I was a cataloguer. Let it be catalogued. There is a peculiar comfort in method even—or especially—when the subject matter is your own dismembered childhood. I wrote: *Surface: glass, load-bearing, temperature approx. cellar-cold. Reflective property: mnemonic, not optical.* Then, because honesty is a discipline too: *Contents of reflection verified against subject's known biography: zero matches. Contents emotionally consistent with subject's person: complete.*

Underneath, in smaller writing, I added: *Conclusion: I am a contested territory.*

It sounds dramatic written that way. It felt dramatic kneeling there, too, so we were even. But it was also simply accurate, and accuracy has been my religion since I was nine years old and learned that the world keeps its promises only in ledgers. Some power had claimed my past. The glass claimed it now, faithfully preserving what the thieves had discarded. And somewhere in the middle, walking around on borrowed certainty, was the current administration: me, Sebastian, holding the seal and the notebook and a flask of tea going cold in my pocket.

I drank the tea. It helped. Tea usually does; it is the guild's true founding miracle and I will defend that position.

The reflections shifted as the sun moved. Morning scenes now: the flour-woman again, younger, hanging washing; the same street of peppers at opening time; a schoolroom where children recited something in two languages, the words sliding off the glass like water off oil, unrecoverable. And then—one scene that stopped my pen mid-word.

A customs post. Small, shabby, the kind that dots the unofficial edges of official borders. A young man in a Meridian uniform stamping papers, and across the counter a Vellmarkish family, and standing slightly behind the family a girl of perhaps sixteen with ink-stained fingers, waiting her turn, watching the young man the way you watch someone when you haven't yet decided whether he's interesting or merely present.

The scene was not mine. I knew that immediately. The angle was wrong—the glass showed it from outside, from the perspective of someone standing near the counter, someone whose shoulder occasionally drifted into the frame's edge. These were not my memories in the glass. They were *everybody's*. The Steppe was not reflecting me specifically; it was reflecting everything the veil had swallowed, and I happened to be standing in the middle of the swallow.

But the girl with the ink-stained fingers—

I leaned closer. My breath fogged the glass, absurdly, uselessly, as though fog could obscure what was already underneath.

The young customs officer stamped her papers and said something, and she answered, and they both smiled the careful smiles of strangers negotiating a border, and the scene faded. And I sat back on my heels with my heart doing something irregular, because I had seen that smile before. Not hers. His. Older, greyer at the temples, worn by a face I'd studied across ledgers and candlelight and one terrible confession at dawn.

Oren Voss had been a customs officer. He had told me that much, once, in one of his lucid hours—the only fact about the war years he could hold onto, the single stone left dry in his flooding mind. *I stamped papers, girl. That's all I did. Whatever they tell you I signed, remember that mostly I stamped papers.*

In the glass, a girl with ink-stained fingers had walked through his border post carrying something wrapped in cloth. I couldn't see what. The reflection had chosen, gently or cruelly, to keep it turned away.

* * *

I walked. There was nothing else to do, and walking is the second-best cure for vertigo after tea. The glass rang faintly under my boots now—I'd put them back on, dignity being recoverable even when childhood isn't—and each step sent a low chime rolling out ahead of me, and each chime stirred the reflections like a stick through pond water.

Scenes rose and sank. Weddings. Funerals—the funerals were strange, joyless and brief, as though even grief had been rationed near the end. Harvest festivals. A parliament building with people streaming out of it, faces upturned, mouths open in a shout I could not hear but whose shape I recognized from engravings: the sound a crowd makes when it learns the worst news of its life. I stepped carefully around that one. Some reflections deserve privacy.

By midday I had worked out three rules, and I wrote them down, because rules are how I love things:

*One. The glass shows what was veiled, not what is hidden. Everything here was taken openly, by treaty, however criminal the treaty. Nothing here lies.*

*Two. It responds to attention. Look hard at a scene and it holds; look away and it sinks. The Steppe is not a museum. It is an animal that lives on being witnessed.*

*Three.* I hesitated over the third, pen hovering, because writing it would make it true and I wasn't sure I could afford another true thing. I wrote it anyway, in the smallest handwriting of my life:

*Three. It knows me here.*

Because it did. That was the day's final discovery, the one I'd been circling since dawn. The nearer I walked toward the center of the Steppe—toward the pale glow on the horizon where the cellar map promised the Door—the more the reflections organized themselves. Random scenes gave way to ordered ones. Strangers gave way to family. The glass was sorting itself around me, presenting its collection in the order of relevance, the way an archive presents its holdings to a researcher it recognizes.

The Steppe thought I had business here. More than business. It behaved like a bank that has been waiting fifty years for the heir to arrive and produce identification.

I did not feel like an heir. I felt like a clerk who had wandered into the vault. But I had learned, cataloguing impossibilities, that feeling is not evidence, and the glass did not care how I felt. It cared that I was born on both sides of a line drawn by men who needed the line more than they needed the people on it.

The glow brightened. The chimes under my boots changed pitch—deeper now, slower, like bells heard through water. And the reflections began arriving in a new category altogether: not scenes anymore, but figures. People. Standing in the glass the way the flour-woman had stood, but facing outward now, facing *me*, dozens of them, then hundreds, rising through the blue depths of the Steppe like divers ascending from somewhere very far below.

They were translucent. They were patient. They were looking at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors, from Oren, from Ilse Marchetti's careful face across a ledger: the look of someone who has waited a long time to say something and is determined to say it correctly.

One of them raised her hand—an old woman, small, with my stubborn chin—and beckoned.

My notebook fell open in my hands. My pen touched the page and wrote, without consulting me, the only possible entry:

*Inventory, item one: company.*
