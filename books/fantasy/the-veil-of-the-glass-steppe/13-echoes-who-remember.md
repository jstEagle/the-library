## 13. Echoes Who Remember

The trouble with walking on memories, Sebastian discovered, was that her feet kept disagreeing with her eyes.

The Steppe stretched before her in every direction—a plain of glass, smooth and pale as the inside of a seashell, going on until the horizon gave up trying to describe it. But underfoot, each step landed somewhere slightly different from where it appeared to be. She would look down at a stretch of empty glass and step onto cobblestones. She would see a wheat field shimmering ahead and feel gravel crunch beneath her boots. Once, for three full strides, she was fairly certain she was wading through a river, though her trousers stayed perfectly dry.

"It's not lying," she said aloud, mostly to hear a voice. "It's just remembering out loud."

Her own voice came back to her strangely, doubled at the edges, as if the glass were whispering along with her a half-second late. She had been walking since what she guessed was morning, though the sky overhead refused to commit to any particular hour. It hovered in a perpetual amber, the color of lamplight through whiskey, and occasionally—she could not say why—it flickered, like a candle deciding whether to bother.

She had brought supplies. That much of her guild training held: a satchel with bread, a flask of water, chalk, twine, Oren's seal wrapped in flannel against her ribs like a second heart, and—the one indulgence she'd allowed herself—a jar of borrowed thunder she'd liberated from the inventory shelf on her way out. It wasn't useful. But there was something about carrying thunder in your bag that made you braver than you actually were, and Sebastian had decided, somewhere around the third mile, that she was entitled to every cowardly advantage available.

"Right," she said, stopping. "If I were a door, where would I be?"

The glass did not answer. Of course it didn't. Doors never answered when you asked nicely; they waited until you were exhausted, damp, and emotionally compromised, and then they appeared behind you.

She turned around. There it was—not the Door Out itself, but something else: a village.

It sat perhaps fifty paces behind her, which was impossible, because she had walked that ground twenty minutes ago and there had been nothing but glass and the amber sky. Yet here it stood: a scatter of low houses with steep roofs, a well with a crooked crank, a chapel whose bell tower leaned like a man listening hard. Smoke rose from two chimneys. Laundry hung between windows. And figures moved among the houses—walking, sweeping, carrying baskets—as if they had always been there and had merely been waiting, patiently, for someone to turn around.

Sebastian stood very still and took inventory of herself, as the guild taught. Heartbeat: elevated but functional. Knees: holding. Urge to run: significant, overruled.

The guild trained its apprentices in impossible things, after all. It had simply never occurred to her that the first impossible thing she'd meet on the Steppe would be hospitality.

A woman emerged from the nearest house, wiping flour from her hands onto an apron, and looked directly at Sebastian with the expression of someone who has been expecting a guest since roughly the previous century.

"You're late," the woman said. "But then, everyone who arrives here is late. Come in before the light shifts. The light gets sentimental around midday, and I won't have you standing in it."

"I—" said Sebastian.

"There's tea," the woman added, in the tone of a checkmate.

There was, Sebastian reflected, no protocol in any ledger for this situation. She followed the woman inside.

* * *

The house smelled of rye bread and woodsmoke and, underneath both, something faintly metallic, like the air just before a storm. Inside it was larger than outside, which Sebastian chose not to remark upon, having learned long ago that commenting on architectural impossibilities was considered poor manners in her profession.

Other people were already there. A man with a farmer's forearms and a clerk's spectacles sat mending a fishing net nowhere near water. Two children played a game with buttons on the floor—but they moved slowly, dreamily, their laughter arriving slightly after their mouths opened, like sound traveling a great distance. An old man dozed by the fire with a newspaper dated, when Sebastian dared glance at it, to a year that officially had never happened.

And by the window, in a chair worn smooth by decades of sitting, an old woman was shelling peas into a bowl that never filled.

"Sit," said the flour-handed woman, pushing her toward a bench. "You'll want to eat. Walking the glass hollows a person out. It takes more than steps, crossing the Steppe. It takes *agreeing* to be somewhere."

Sebastian sat. Bread appeared. Tea appeared. The tea tasted of chamomile and, distantly, of a summer she couldn't place—which was, she was beginning to understand, the local seasoning.

"Forgive me," she said carefully. "You know who I am?"

"We know what you are," said the net-mending man without looking up. "That's different."

"The last apprentice," said the old woman by the window, not pausing her shelling. "Of the Guild of Impossible Things. Vellmark branch. Which is to say, the only branch, which is to say—" she smiled thinly, "—the only thing left of either."

"You're Echoes," Sebastian said. It came out half-question, half-confession. "The ones folded into the veil. The ledgers call you residual citizens. I'm sorry—that's a horrible way to put people. I've read it a hundred times and never once heard how it sounds."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when a stranger accidentally tells the truth.

"It sounds accurate," said the flour-handed woman, whose name, she offered, was Marta. "Residual. Like a stain. Like the ring a cup leaves on a table." She said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "We're what didn't fit in the fold, child. When the rulers of this country folded it shut—when they tucked Vellmark away like a letter nobody wanted to answer—some of us caught in the creases. Not destroyed. Not remembered. Kept. The way you keep a coat you can't wear but can't throw out."

"The veil needed anchors," said the man with the net. His name was Tomas. "You can't fold a whole country without something holding the corners. We hold the corners."

"How many?" Sebastian asked softly.

Marta and Tomas looked at each other, and it was the old woman by the window who answered.

"Enough," she said. "More than enough. Fewer than there were." She set down the pea pod, finally, and looked at Sebastian fully for the first time—and Sebastian felt the look go through her like a knitting needle through wool, catching on everything, rearranging nothing, taking exact measure. "Come nearer to the light, girl. Let me see your face properly."

Sebastian moved her bench closer. Up close, the old woman's face was all planes and patience, brown-eyed, with a scar through one eyebrow shaped like a comma—as if her life had paused there once and never quite resumed.

"You have your mother's chin," the woman said. "And your father's habit of apologizing to furniture."

Sebastian's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "I beg your pardon?"

"You apologized to the bench when you sat down. Under your breath. Your father did exactly that his whole life. Chairs, doors, ladders—he treated the entire material world like a cousin he owed money to." The old woman's eyes crinkled. "I'm your grandmother, Sebastian. On your mother's side. My name is Anka Jelenová, and I died—or whatever we do here—eleven years before you were born, which is why you've never heard my voice except in your sleep, and why you've never known why you hum that tune when you're worried."

Sebastian's mouth went dry. Because she *did* hum a tune when she was worried. Four notes, descending, looping. She'd asked her mother about it once and her mother had gone quiet in a way that closed the subject like a drawer.

"What tune?" Sebastian whispered, though she knew.

Anka hummed it. Four notes, descending, looping. And the room—the whole glass house—seemed to lean in, the way a family leans in when a story begins that everyone knows by heart.

"That's a Vellmarki lullaby," Sebastian managed. "It can't be. Vellmark hasn't existed for fifty years. My mother was born after—"

"After what?" Anka asked gently. "Finish the sentence, girl. After the war? After the treaty? After the country was *resolved*?" She said the word the way one spits out a fruit stone. "Your mother was born in a country that was told, politely, by everyone around it, that it had never existed. And she believed them, because believing is easier than grieving, and because the forgetting had already been poured into the milk, so to speak. Into the ledgers. Into the rent paid in currency from a nation that isn't on maps. You found that already, didn't you? In the archive."

"Yes," Sebastian said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "The city flickers at dusk."

"Flickers." Anka nodded. "That's the seams showing. The veil is old, and it was never built to last—it was built to *end* something, and things built to end things make terrible architecture. Your Guildmaster knows. Half-knows. Knowing hurts him, so he forgets, and the guild lets him, because the guild was always the veil's janitor and janitors learn not to look too closely at the stains."

"You know about the guild."

"We remember the guild." For the first time, something moved across the old woman's face that was not patience. "Someone had to. They bought our memories to feed the veil—did you know that yet? Yes, I see it in your face, you do. Every entry in those beloved ledgers of yours is a piece of somebody's life, sold cheap because the alternative was worse. My sister sold the day of her wedding. Said she couldn't bear to carry it anymore, not with the border running through her own kitchen table. So the guild took it, and fed it to the fold, and now some corner of this Steppe is paved with her wedding day, and she walked off into the flat part of the world and forgot she'd ever loved anyone, and called that peace."

The fire popped. One of the children looked up from the button game, and for a moment their eyes were ancient and terribly sad, and then they were children again, laughing a half-second late.

"I'm sorry," Sebastian said. "For all of it. For the ledgers. I catalogued them for six years and I never—I dusted the cages, that's what I did. I kept the cages tidy."

"Yes," said Anka, and there was no cruelty in it. "And now you're here. Do you know why you're here, Sebastian Winterbourne? Truly? Not the prophecy-folio version. Not 'born of both sides of the border.' Tell me what you think it means."

Sebastian set down her cup. She thought about the folio with her name in it, tonight's date written a century ago. She thought about Oren's fraying memory, the seal against her ribs, Ilse Marchetti's face in the doorway of her mind, uninvited and persistent.

"I thought it meant I was a key," she said slowly. "Born on both sides of the line—my mother's family from the Vellmarki valleys, my father's from across the border, the side that won the war and got to keep existing. I thought the Door Out opens for me because I'm... split down the middle. Half erased blood, half remembered blood."

"And?"

"And now I'm sitting in a dead woman's kitchen being served tea by people who don't technically exist, so I suspect I understood less than I thought."

Anka laughed—a real laugh, rusty, delighted—and the sound did something to the light in the room, warming it a degree.

"The Door doesn't open for halves," she said. "That's what everyone gets wrong, including, I'm afraid, whoever wrote that prophecy you've been carrying around like a ticket. The Door doesn't care about bloodlines. Bloodlines are just the paperwork. The Door opens for *belonging*. Both kinds. It opens for someone who belongs to the country that was erased *and* belongs to the country that did the erasing—not by birth, child, by *choice*. By love, if you want the embarrassing word. Someone who has stood on both sides of the fold and refused to pick one. Someone who carries the whole border inside them and doesn't try to amputate either half."

The room had gone still. Even the fire seemed to listen.

"Do you understand what that means?" Anka said softly. "Everyone else who ever sought the Door wanted it for one side. The restorationists want old Vellmark back and the rest of the world damned. The Custodians want the veil eternal and Vellmark buried forever. Kings want kingdoms. But a person who loves both—who grieves for the erased *and* pities the forgetters, who sees that the winners of that war also lost something, lost their neighbors, their other language, their better selves—that person is the only one the Door will trust with itself. Because the Door doesn't lead out, girl. Everyone thinks 'Door Out' means escape. It doesn't. It means *out of the argument*. Out of either-or. It only opens inward, in a manner of speaking. It opens for wholeness."

Sebastian sat with that. Around her, quietly, the household resumed its ghost-chores: the net mended, the buttons clicked, the peas fell into a bowl that stayed empty, and none of it was sad exactly, or none of it was only sad. There was bread. There was tea. There was belonging, offered by strangers—no. Offered by family, by people who had been waiting in the creases of the world for her specifically, when her actual family had failed her by forgetting her before she was born.

"Why tell me all this?" she asked finally. "Why help me? If I open the Door, what happens to you? To the Steppe? To—" she gestured at the kitchen, the leaning bell tower, the children, "—all of this?"

Anka was quiet a long moment. Outside, the amber sky flickered, and for half a heartbeat Sebastian saw through it: a gray plain under a normal sky, and far off, a smudge of movement. Figures. Many figures. Moving with purpose.

When she blinked, the amber returned, and Anka was watching her with sharp attention.

"You saw that," the old woman said. It wasn't a question.

"Saw what?"

"Don't lie to your grandmother, it's unbecoming. You saw something moving beyond the fold. Something in uniform." Anka leaned forward. "Then we have less time than I hoped. Listen to me, and listen with both halves of yourself. The Door stands at the center of the Steppe, a day's walk, maybe less if the glass likes you. It will ask you for a price—I won't tell you what, because the telling changes it, and because some prices must be chosen with clean ignorance. But know this: you will not walk it alone."

"I came alone."

"No." Anka shook her head, and the comma-shaped scar seemed almost to smile. "You came *first*. There is a difference. Someone follows you even now—I felt her cross the edge of the Steppe an hour ago. A Custodian. One of the keepers of the fold. She walks badly, favoring her left side, and she walks *toward* you, which is the most dangerous direction a Custodian has ever walked in a hundred years."

Sebastian's heart performed an operation she hadn't authorized. "Ilse," she said, before she could stop herself. Then, quickly: "How do you—"

"The glass remembers footsteps," Anka said, rising, taking the teacup from Sebastian's hands with surprising tenderness. "Especially the ones that hesitate. Now eat the rest of that bread. Grandmothers are legally required to send people off fed; it's in our founding documents, and unlike your country's, ours were never dissolved."

* * *

They walked her to the edge of the village—all of them, even the children, even the sleeping old man, who apparently slept-walked with remarkable directional sense. The houses began to thin, then to fade at their edges, becoming suggestions of houses, then sketches, then a shimmer, then only glass again. Marta pressed a parcel into Sebastian's hands: bread and cheese wrapped in cloth that smelled of thyme.

"It won't run out," Marta said. "Nothing here runs out. That's rather the problem with us."

Tomas shook her hand solemnly, as though concluding a business arrangement of great antiquity. "Tell the world we held the corners," he said. "That's all. Tell it plainly. No speeches."

At the last house, Anka took Sebastian's face in her hands—hands that felt like warmth and paper and the specific weight of things that have been waiting a long time.

"Four notes, descending," she said. "Hum them when you're afraid. I put them in you before you were born, through your mother's milk of dreams. They're not just a lullaby, Sebastian. They're a map. The first four turns of the road to the Door. Sing them on the glass, and the glass will sing back, and where the songs agree—that's the true road. Everything else is the Steppe trying to be helpful."

"Will I see you again?"

"Oh, certainly." Anka smiled, and it was the smile of someone folding a letter carefully, to be opened later. "Everything here happens again. That's what we are. Go now. And girl—one more thing."

Sebastian paused, already turning toward the endless amber plain.

"Whoever wrote that prophecy with your name in it," Anka said, "loved you. Prophecy is just memory with its shoes on the wrong feet. Somebody remembered you before you existed. Think about who could do such a thing, and be kind to them when you find out."

Before Sebastian could ask—and there were approximately forty questions crowding her throat—Anka kissed her forehead, and the village let go of her all at once, gently, the way a tide releases a shell.

She stood alone on the glass, holding bread that wouldn't run out, humming four notes under her breath. And far away behind her—at the rim of the Steppe, where the amber met the ordinary gray of the world—she saw a small figure begin to cross, limping slightly, favoring the left side, walking toward her with the stubborn, terrified courage of someone who had chosen, at last, the most dangerous direction of all.

And further off still, at the very edge of sight, something else was crossing too. Something in formation. Something that did not limp, and did not hesitate, and carried banners.

Sebastian began to walk.
