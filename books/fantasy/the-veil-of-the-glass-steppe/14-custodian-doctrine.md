## 14. Custodian Doctrine

The Ministry of Continuity did not appear on any map of the capital, which Ilse had always considered a small administrative joke: an office devoted to keeping things on maps, absent from one itself. To reach it you took the tram to Terminus Square, walked past the customs house where she'd spent six unremarkable years stamping papers for goods that had never crossed any border worth naming, and turned down a street that most pedestrians' eyes slid over like water off wax. The door was green. It was always green. Ilse had once spent a full afternoon trying to remember whether it had ever been another color, and had come away with the uneasy sense that the question itself was a symptom.

She signed the daybook at nine o'clock sharp. The clerk—a pale young man named Brandt who had the gift of looking apologetic even when asleep—gave her the usual nod and the usual stamp, and Ilse climbed three flights of stairs to the Warden's office, rehearsing her report the way other people rehearsed apologies.

The audit of the northern district was clean. That was the problem. Clean audits were supposed to be comforting, and instead they sat in her chest like swallowed stones, because she had written every word of this one herself, and not one word of it was true.

* * *

Warden Kessler received her in a room that smelled of ink and cold tea. He was not what strangers expected. People imagined Custodian wardens as tall, gray, ceremonial—figures carved from the same stone as their doctrines. Kessler was short, round-faced, with spectacles he polished constantly and a laugh that, in the canteen, could be heard from two tables away. He told jokes about paperwork. He remembered birthdays. He had once given Ilse his own umbrella during a downpour and refused to take it back, saying the Ministry had plenty of umbrellas even if it kept them all classified.

That was the thing about Kessler that made him frightening, if you thought about it long enough, which Ilse tried very hard not to do. He was kind. He was kind the way a well-run abattoir was clean.

"Ilse Marchetti," he said, without looking up from her file. "Sit, sit. You look like you slept in your coat."

"Close enough."

"The northern district." He turned a page. "Veil integrity nominal. Memory decay within tolerance. No anomalies reported." He looked up then, over the rims of his spectacles, and smiled his warm smile. "You've audited that district four times in eighteen months. More than any other officer. Any particular fondness?"

"Fondness for thoroughness," Ilse said. "The northern districts sit closest to the old border. I like to check them often."

"Mm," said Kessler, which was not agreement, or disagreement, or anything at all. "And yet your reports have gotten shorter. Fourteen pages last spring. Nine now. Six this week. A woman who loves thoroughness writing thinner reports each time—that's either efficiency or fatigue, and you don't strike me as efficient."

Ilse kept her face pleasant and empty, a skill the customs house had taught her well. "I could pad them back out, sir, if the format requires it."

"No, no." He closed the file and set it down with a small pat, the way one might settle a sleeping cat. Then his voice changed—not louder, not colder, just closer, the way a room gets smaller when someone lowers their tone. "I'm going to tell you something, Marchetti, because you're good at your work, and because I think you've already guessed pieces of it, and guessing is corrosive. Guessing left alone eats through an officer like rust through a pipe. Better to hand you the truth and let you hold it properly."

He rose and went to the cabinet behind his desk—the tall one, the one junior officers were told contained surplus forms. It did not contain surplus forms. It never had. From its depths he drew a slim ledger bound in gray leather, and set it between them, and did not open it.

"Do you know why the Veiling has held for fifty years?" he asked.

"Discipline," Ilse said. "Constant maintenance. The schedules, the audits, the trades."

"Those are how. Not why it holds." Kessler sat again, folding his hands. "It holds because it eats. Everything that feeds does." He said it the way a man describes weather. "A veil of that size—half a country folded out of the world's memory—is not a wall. Walls stand on their own. This is more like a fire, and fires need fuel. The fuel is memory. Bought memory, traded memory, surrendered memory. Every entry in every guild ledger across the border feeds it. And do you know what happens, Marchetti, when the fire runs low?"

"It dims," Ilse said carefully.

"It reaches for the hearth." Kessler took off his spectacles and polished them, and for a moment without them his eyes looked strangely young and terribly tired. "The veil consumes its keepers. That is doctrine, Marchetti. Actual doctrine, filed under classification you'll receive tonight. The Custodians are not merely maintaining the Veiling. We are its diet. Slowly. By degrees. Why do you suppose the turnover rate in this ministry is what it is? Why do you suppose officers transfer out after eight years, ten, and spend their retirements unable to say what they did? They haven't retired, most of them. They've been digested. Politely. With pensions."

The clock on Kessler's shelf ticked. Ilse discovered her hands were cold around her teacup, though she couldn't remember accepting tea.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "Sir, that's an extraordinary claim."

"It is. It's also arithmetic." He opened the gray ledger at last and turned it toward her. Columns of names. Her colleagues. Officers she'd trained with, drunk with, covered for. Beside each name, dates. Beside the dates, a single column headed *Residual Retention*, filled with numbers that declined down each page like water finding its level. "Officer Halven. Joined at thirty-one. Sharp as a tack; she once caught a smuggling ring by noticing a town's church bell rang in a key nobody remembered teaching it. Look at her retention curve. At year twelve she forgot her mother's face. Year fifteen, her wedding. Last month she asked me who won the war we exist to keep buried—and then laughed and said, *silly me, there wasn't one, was there.*" He tapped the page gently. "The veil doesn't distinguish between the memories we feed it and the memories of the people feeding it. Eventually it eats its way to the cook."

Ilse looked at the columns and felt something in her stomach turn over slowly, like a boat changing direction. Because she knew this. Not as doctrine—as weather. She knew it in the way she sometimes stood in front of her own wardrobe in the morning and could not, for three whole seconds, remember why she owned the blue dress, or whether the woman who'd bought it had liked being the sort of person who wore blue. She knew it in the gaps where her childhood should have been, soft-edged absences she'd always assumed were ordinary forgetting, everyone's forgetting, the universal tax of living.

Everyone forgot things. Everyone. That was what she'd told herself.

"You feel it, don't you," Kessler said quietly. It was not a question, and his kindness was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. "You've felt it for years. That's why your reports get shorter. Not fatigue. Conservation. Some part of you is rationing."

"I want to do my job, sir," Ilse said, and heard how thin it came out.

"And you will. Because here is the second thing." He closed the ledger. "The veil is failing."

The words dropped into the room and did not echo, as though the office itself declined to repeat them.

"Failing," Ilse repeated.

"Accelerating decay along the entire western line. Districts flickering at dusk—you'll have seen it. Objects losing their impossibility. Whole streets failing to render on schedule." He ticked these off on his fingers like groceries, and Ilse thought, absurdly and horribly, of a certain guild headquarters and a jar of borrowed thunder, and made her face stay still. "Fifty years of doctrine says the Veiling is permanent, stable, self-sustaining. Fifty years of doctrine is wrong. It was never meant to be permanent. The original Accord's architects built a temporary measure and called it eternal because no one would sign a treaty admitting the alternative was indefinite cannibalism of the national memory. We have been eating ourselves to keep a secret, and the meal is nearly over."

"What happens when it fails entirely?"

Kessler put his spectacles back on, and behind them his eyes became warden's eyes again, flat and bright. "Unfolding. All at once. Half a million people waking up inside a country that officially doesn't exist, remembering a war everyone agreed to forget. Treaties voiding. Borders reasserting themselves like bones knitting badly." He shook his head. "There are those in the government who would welcome it. There are those who would burn everything rather than allow it. My job is to make sure neither group gets to choose by accident. Which brings me to you."

"Sir?"

"A leak." He slid a photograph across the desk. Ilse looked down at it and felt the floor perform a small, silent rotation.

It was the customs house. Years ago—younger Ilse, younger by half a decade, standing at her post with her stamp in hand. And beside her, leaning on the counter with that particular insolent patience she had never quite lost, a girl with ink-stained fingers and a guild apprentice's sash worn like an apology. Sebastian Winterbourne. Eighteen years old, maybe nineteen, asking questions about import duties on impossible objects with a straight face while Ilse tried desperately not to laugh.

"Why do you have this?" Ilse asked. Her voice was steady. She was almost proud of it.

"Routine surveillance archive. Every officer's public interactions are retained." Kessler watched her with mild interest. "You knew her?"

"She's a guild archivist. The Guild of Impossible Things pays duties on some very strange inventory. I processed her paperwork perhaps a dozen times over the years." Each word laid down carefully, like stones across a stream. "An unremarkable acquaintance."

"Mm. Except." He produced a second sheet—a memo, typed, unsigned. "Six months ago, someone accessed the restricted files on the Veiling Accord using a clearance belonging to this office. The access trace terminates at a terminal in the northern district. The same terminal used for your last three audits. And in the weeks following that access, the Guild of Impossible Things—which has spent fifty years being harmlessly decrepit—began moving. Seals unlocked. Cellars opened. One of their members, an elderly guildmaster, was seen lucid for the first time in years." He laced his fingers together atop the desk. "So. Either the veil is decaying on its own schedule and coincidence is decorating the margins—or someone fed the fire's secrets to the people who built the stove, and the leak is sitting in my office drinking my terrible tea."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, a tram sang past on the square, carrying citizens who did not know they were breakfast.

"What would you have me do?" Ilse asked.

"Investigate," Kessler said simply. "Discreetly. Start with the guild. You have a legitimate pretext—your audits, your history with their paperwork. Find me the leak, Marchetti. Find me whoever opened that cellar." He smiled, warm as ever, and Ilse thought: *he knows. He must know. Nobody assigns a person to hunt themselves unless they're watching to see what the person does.* Or—and this was worse, and possible—maybe he didn't know at all, maybe her guilt was simply painting suspicion onto a kind man's ordinary request, the way anxiety painted faces onto wallpaper.

Either way, the trap had the same shape.

"I'll begin tomorrow," she said.

"Tonight, if you can bear it." He stood, and offered his hand, and held hers a moment longer than protocol required. "Marchetti. One more thing, officer to officer." His voice dropped, and for a heartbeat the warden vanished and only the tired man remained, the one who gave away umbrellas. "Whatever you find—whatever you learn about what this ministry truly is—remember that everyone in this building is being eaten too. The ones who betray the veil are rarely villains. Mostly they're just people who noticed the teeth before the rest of us managed not to." He released her hand. "It makes them sympathetic. It doesn't make them right. Can you hold both of those truths at once?"

"I'm a customs officer, sir," Ilse said. "Holding contradictory truths is the entire job."

Kessler laughed—his real laugh, the canteen laugh—and waved her out.

* * *

She did not go home. She walked.

The capital at dusk was performing its usual trick of being beautiful in a way that required cooperation. Lamplighters moved along their routes. A baker's boy argued cheerfully with a fishmonger about whose stall had been on which corner since time immemorial, both of them wrong, both of them certain. On the corner of Vosser Street an old woman was telling two children about the winter the river froze solid from bank to bank, and Ilse stopped to listen, and realized after a moment that the winter she described was not any winter that had happened, or not only one—it was three winters braided together into one good story, the way the veil braided, smoothing seams, choosing warmth.

*Conservation,* Kessler had said. Rationing.

Ilse stood under a lamp and took inventory of herself, the way she'd once inventoried seized cargo: declared contents versus actual contents.

Declared: a loyal officer with a fading memory and a clean audit trail.

Actual: a woman who had falsified four reports to protect a guild she had no official reason to care about; who had accessed restricted files on a terminal she was authorized to use precisely so the trace would point at her and nowhere else; who had fallen in love, years ago, across a customs counter, with a girl who asked impossible questions, and had never once said so, and had built an entire quiet architecture of small treasons around that unsaid thing the way a tree builds rings around a nail.

And now the ministry had handed her a leash and pointed her at the nail.

They think I'm hunting a leak, she thought. They may even be right. The trouble with a leak is that you can't plug it without putting your finger in it, and then you're part of the pipe.

She thought of Sebastian—of ink-stained fingers and that crooked half-smile, of the way she'd leaned over the counter to read her own stamped forms upside-down, muttering figures, catching errors Ilse had planted deliberately just to see if she would. She had. Of course she had. Ilse had known from the third visit that this girl would be the death of her doctrine, and had gone back anyway, eleven times, fourteen times, manufacturing reasons—duties owed, discrepancies to reconcile—until the visits needed no manufacture at all and became simply the best hour of every week, until the guild girl started bringing her tea, until the war between loyalty and wanting ended not with victory but with occupation.

And now Sebastian was unlocking cellars and reading Accords and walking toward something that Kessler's own gray ledger said would eat her alive.

The lamplight buzzed. The old woman finished her impossible winter and the children clapped, having no idea they'd applauded a forgery, loving it anyway.

Ilse turned up her collar and began, unhurriedly, to plan her investigation—outward. Inward, she was planning something else entirely: how to warn a guild archivist that the ministry suspected her, without exposing that the warning came from the ministry's own hands; how to buy time with forged numbers; how to stand in the fire's path holding a bucket that was, technically, stolen from the fire brigade.

Tomorrow she would open a fresh audit file on the Guild of Impossible Things.

Tonight, first, she had a letter to write, unsigned, undeliverable by any official channel, addressed to no name at all—to *the archivist who asks impossible questions*, care of a crumbling headquarters on a street that half the city's eyes slid past.

Some leaks, Ilse thought, stepping into the dusk, you don't plug.

Some leaks you follow down to whatever ocean they're trying to reach—and you pray, with everything the veil hasn't eaten yet, that you get there before the warden does.
