## 15. The Price of Passage

The grandmother's Echo poured tea that was not tea.

It looked like tea. It steamed like tea. When Sebastian lifted the cup, it had the exact weight of her grandmother's good china — the blue willow pattern she'd somehow never actually seen, and yet knew down to her bones — but when she drank, nothing passed her lips at all. The warmth simply arrived inside her, as if her body had agreed to pretend.

"You're frowning," said the Echo, whose name was Marta, who claimed to be her mother's mother, who flickered at the edges when the glass plain breathed. "Your mother frowned exactly like that when I told her the price of things."

"The price of *things*," Sebastian repeated. Around them, the camp of the Echoes shimmered on the Steppe like a city remembered by someone who hadn't paid attention: houses with doors in plausible places, a well with no bucket, a church whose bell rang a half-second before it was struck. "You mean the Door."

The Echoes had gone quiet around their cookfire — not a fire, really, but the idea of one, giving light without heat or smoke, arranged in a circle of eleven figures who were more lantern than person. All day they had been leading her across the glass toward the place where the Door Out stood folded into the horizon like a crease in a letter. All day they had been kind to her. Kindness, Sebastian had learned in the guild's ledgers, was often the wrapping paper on terrible news.

"Sit properly," Marta said, which was such a grandmothers' sentence that Sebastian obeyed before she could stop herself. "There is no gentle way to say this, so I will say it the way I would have said it to you over a kitchen table, if kitchens had been allowed to keep us. The Door Out opens for you. Only for you. You are stitched from both sides of the border, and the veil cannot decide what you are, and so the Door cannot refuse you." She set down her cup. "But nothing crosses out of Vellmark for free. Not even its last daughter."

"Then name it," Sebastian said. "A toll. A fee. The guild has accounts; I can—"

"It doesn't want money, child. It wants weight."

Sebastian waited. The fire that wasn't fire crackled anyway, out of politeness.

"A memory," Marta said, "of equal weight to a country."

The words landed in her chest and sat there like a stone dropped into a well, falling and falling and refusing to hit water.

"That's absurd," Sebastian said finally. "No memory weighs as much as a country."

"One does," said an Echo near the edge of the circle. He had been a schoolteacher, they said, in the town of Brast, which no longer appeared on any map including the ones in people's heads. He wore spectacles with no lenses in them, because his eyes had been among the first things the veil took. "One memory always weighs as much as a country. The Door is not greedy. It is only honest. It asks for the life you would live *after* you cross."

* * *

They let her walk after that, which was either mercy or strategy. The Steppe stretched ahead, glass underfoot going soft and amber where the sun struck it, and beneath her feet — she tried not to look, failed, looked — memories moved like fish under ice. A wedding she'd never attended. A harvest festival in colors that didn't exist anymore. Once, horribly, a flash of a kitchen: blue willow china, steam off a pot, a woman humming — and Sebastian wrenched her gaze up at the sky, which offered nothing back, being only weather.

*The life you would live after.*

She did sums, because sums were what she did when the world went wrong. She was twenty-six years old. She had, if she was average about it, another fifty years. Fifty years of mornings. Fifty years of Ilse —

She stopped walking.

The Echoes, patient as furniture, stopped with her.

"There must be another way," she said, turning. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which she counted as the single useful inheritance from a guild that taught you to catalog disasters without flinching. "There is always another way. I've read four hundred years of impossible transactions. Every contract has a loophole, a rider, a clause written small enough to hide in. Who brokered the Veiling Accord? Someone drafted terms then. Terms can be renegotiated."

Marta's flicker-steady face did something complicated. Pity, mostly. "The Accord was signed by men who wanted the war to end and didn't much care what else ended with it. They weren't looking for loopholes, child. They were looking for the smallest door they could shut quietly."

"Then I'll find the largest one and open it loudly."

A ripple went through the circle — the Echo equivalent of laughter, or perhaps of grief wearing laughter's coat. The schoolteacher adjusted his lensless spectacles. "In fifty years under the veil," he said, "no one has ever stood where you're standing and refused. They weep. They bargain. One man offered the Door his entire childhood, hour by hour, like coins. The Door weighed it and found it light. But refuse? No one refuses."

"I'm a guild apprentice," Sebastian said. "'Impossible' is our whole inventory."

"Was," murmured someone gently, and she chose not to hear it.

* * *

They made camp — if a camp made of remembered lamplight could be called that — in the lee of a hill that had once been a fortress, according to the schoolteacher, and had surrendered so thoroughly during the war that even the hill had forgotten how to be steep. Sebastian sat apart, taking inventory, because inventory was prayer for people like her.

Item: one apprentice, last of her order.
Item: one founding seal, warm in her pocket, given by a master who could no longer remember her face.
Item: one woman, somewhere behind her, committing treason in careful increments, whose name Sebastian wrote nightly in the margin of her own notebook and erased each morning, superstitious as a child.
Item: one Door, requiring payment of everything that hadn't happened yet.

She caught herself doing it — the thing she'd done since she was small, since the orphanage, since she'd learned that wanting things loudly meant losing them loudly. She was rehearsing the loss. Practicing the shape of a life without Ilse in it, without mornings, without the small dumb future she'd begun, shamefully, secretly, to imagine: a flat above the guild hall, two cups on the shelf, arguing about whether rain counted as weather or as an event.

*Fifty years,* whispered the rehearsal. *Gone in one signature.*

"No," Sebastian said aloud, to the Steppe, to herself, to whatever book of prophecies was presumably still watching her with its patient century-old ink. "No, I don't accept the premise."

The nearest Echo — a young man, maybe, though Echoes aged strangely, like photographs left in windows — tilted his head. "The premise?"

"The Door assumes my after-life is *mine to give*. That it belongs to me, sitting here, unspent, like coin in a purse. But it doesn't exist yet. You can't tax a country on wealth it hasn't earned. You can't collect a debt against a house not built. Any guild clerk worth her salt would reject the ledger entry on its face." She heard herself speeding up, the way Oren used to speed up when he was frightened and covering it with procedure. "I want to speak to whoever keeps the Door's books."

The Echoes exchanged a look that contained an entire parliament.

"There are no books," the young one said carefully. "The Door isn't a merchant, apprentice. It's more like... a wound that learned arithmetic."

"Then I'll teach it better mathematics."

* * *

Bargaining, it turned out, was harder when the other party was a seam of light older than the country it guarded.

They reached it at dusk on the third day — or what the Echoes called dusk, the glass going rose and gold as if embarrassed by how beautiful it was. The Door Out stood alone in the middle of the plain, and it was, infuriatingly, modest. A doorway. Free-standing, frameless, tall as a tall man, filled not with anything but with *almost* — the way a word sits on the tip of the tongue. Looking at it too long made Sebastian's teeth ache.

She planted herself in front of it the way she'd once planted herself in front of Guildmaster Voss to argue for a larger stationery budget, and she felt, absurdly, the same vertigo: small person, large institution.

"Here are my terms," she announced.

The Door did nothing, magnificently.

"Term one. I will not pay in advance. No guild pays in advance; advance payment invites fraud. Term two. If I pay at all, I pay in installments, and I retain the right to audit the collection. Term three—" and here her voice did wobble, and she hated it, and pushed through, "term three. Whatever I give, it cannot include her. Whatever the future holds, however much of it the Door takes, the part of it that is *us* — that's not mine alone to sell. It's joint property. Find another asset."

The glass under the Door caught her reflection and held it, and for a moment her reflection looked less like her than like a page waiting to be written on, blank and patient and slightly smug.

"She loves you, you know," said Marta, coming to stand beside her. Not unkindly. "The Door knows it too. That's why your refusal interests it. Love makes heavy collateral."

"Then it should love me right back and waive the fee."

"Child." Marta's voice went soft in the way that preceded the worst sentences. "Do you understand what you'd be giving up? Not death. Death leaves a body, a grave, mourners. This is different. If you pay, you cross, and the country wakes, and you will stand in it — alive, breathing, *there* — and there will be nothing after. No next morning. No next argument. You will arrive at the end of your own story and simply... continue to stand in the last line. Forever. Watching everyone you love go on without you, unable to follow, because you spent your following."

The wind moved across the Steppe with a sound like pages turning.

Sebastian had imagined many versions of the price since the cookfire. She had imagined forgetting. She had imagined dying. She had imagined some clean trade, memory for memory, pain for country — the kind of transaction the ledgers prepared you for. She had not imagined *this*: standing in the new Vellmark she built, watching Ilse grow old beside her, and being unable to take a single step into tomorrow. A life with all its pages present and none of them turnable.

Her knees informed her, quietly, that they would like to stop holding her up now.

She sat down in front of the Door, in the amber glass, and did not cry, because she was a Winterbourne apprentice and also because she suspected the Door collected tears at a very unfavorable exchange rate.

"All right," she said, when she could trust her voice. "All right. New negotiation. Stall tactic number one: I need time."

"Time," Marta echoed. "The anniversary approaches. The Door will be at its most open soon — the veil thins, the old wound reopens. After that window closes, it may be another year."

"Then I have until the anniversary. And until then, I have questions, and I intend to ask every one of them, and the Door can just wait there being smug while I do due diligence." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, businesslike, fooling no one. "Also I'm hungry, and your tea is a lie, and if I'm going to negotiate away my entire future, I would like to do it with actual food in me. Does anyone here remember bread? Real bread?"

The circle of Echoes rippled again, and this time it was definitely laughter, warm and startled, the first she'd heard from them. The young one grinned. "We remember bread the way you remember a dream of flying. We can show you, but you'll wake up hungry."

"Show me anyway."

That night, sitting among the lantern-people in their lamplight-that-wasn't, eating bread that wasn't, Sebastian laughed twice — genuinely, helplessly — and understood, with a lurch, that she was doing it again. Rehearsing. Memorizing them. Filing the exact pitch of Marta's cackle and the schoolteacher's dry chuckle into the part of herself that kept records of things she couldn't bear to lose, because some traitor corner of her had already begun to believe she would lose them — or worse, that she would keep everything except the ability to move forward into keeping it.

Terror wore defiance well. She'd learned that from the guild's oldest ledgers, where the bravest entries were always written in the shakiest hands.

Later, alone at the edge of the camp, she took out her notebook and, instead of writing Ilse's name and erasing it, wrote it and left it. Then, underneath, she wrote:

*Door demands: the life after. Status: under dispute.*

And beneath that, because she was her master's apprentice and the guild had taught her that every impossibility begins its life as a clerical error someone refused to correct:

*Possible avenues: (1) find what the Door values more than futures. (2) learn what became of those who paid before. (3) ask the market.*

She paused over the third line. She'd heard the Echoes mention it that afternoon, half in warning, half in longing — a market at the Steppe's edge, appearing when the veil thinned, selling back the things the veil had confiscated. Letters. Lullabies. Small stolen hours.

And if the veil confiscated things, and the market sold them, then somewhere in its stalls there might be a record of the Door's past customers. What they'd paid. Whether anyone had ever found a discount.

Whether anyone had ever walked away.

Sebastian closed the notebook, looked once more at the Door standing patient and smug in the dark, and pointedly turned her back on it.

"Due diligence," she told it, and went to sleep.
