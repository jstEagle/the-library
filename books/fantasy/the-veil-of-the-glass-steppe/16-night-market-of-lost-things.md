## 16. Night Market of Lost Things

The Steppe did not end so much as it *hesitated*.

Sebastian had walked since the grey hour before dawn across glass that remembered other people's summers, and now the plain gave way to something softer—a shelf of dark earth where the reflections underfoot grew shallow and then, mercifully, stopped being anyone's at all. Beyond that shelf, in a fold of land the old maps would have called nowhere, lights were strung between poles of pale wood: hundreds of them, amber and green and one stubborn blue that flickered like it was trying to remember how to be fire.

A market.

She smelled it before she saw it properly—fried dough, woodsmoke, and underneath those, something stranger: the smell of a specific rain on a specific street, bottled and uncorked by someone who missed it.

"You'll want your wages," said a voice at her elbow, "in whatever currency you carry. They take everything here. That's rather the point."

The speaker was an Echo, or nearly one—a woman made mostly of suggestion, her outline soft as breath on a window. She wore an apron that said, in faded embroidery, FOR GOODNESS' SAKE, WASH YOUR HANDS, which struck Sebastian as the most heartbreaking sentence she'd read all year, because it was clearly the sort of thing a person embroidered when they loved somebody enough to nag them.

"I don't have wages," Sebastian said. Then, honestly: "I have guild scrip."

The Echo laughed, and the laugh had weight to it, actual weight, like coins dropped into a bowl. "Guild scrip! Oh, they'll weep with joy. Half of what's sold here was confiscated from your ledgers in the first place. You'd be repossessing." She tucked her arm through Sebastian's with the ease of someone who had done it ten thousand times to ten thousand nervous visitors. "Come. Mind the pickle stall. The pickles are fine; the vendor is sentimental and will tell you his whole life story if you make eye contact."

"What is this place?" Sebastian asked, though she was beginning to guess, and the guess made her throat tight.

"Officially? Nothing. A market cannot exist inside a veil unless the veil permits it, and the veil permits nothing." The Echo steered her past a stall of clocks, all of them ticking at slightly different speeds, like a room full of people agreeing to disagree. "Unofficially, everything the Veiling took had to go somewhere. You can't destroy a memory, dear. You can only misplace it. So it got misplaced here, and where memories gather, merchants follow. Merchants are like cats that way."

The stalls resolved out of the lamplight one by one, and Sebastian understood why the place needed darkness: everything for sale glowed faintly, the way things do when they are loved too much to be entirely inert.

There was a letter stall—actual letters, hundreds of them, pinned under glass like butterflies. A man behind the counter was reading one aloud to a customer in a low voice, and the customer was crying in the efficient way of someone who has budgeted for tears.

There was a stall of lullabies, kept in jars. Not recorded—*kept*, humming softly against the glass, each lid labeled in a different handwriting: *For putting down a baby who fights sleep. For mothers. For fathers pretending not to cry in kitchens.* One jar simply read, *Mine, do not touch,* and had been touched so many times the label had worn to lace.

There was a stall selling first kisses. Sebastian looked away from that one quickly, ears hot, and the apron-woman chuckled.

"And there," the Echo said, gesturing with her chin toward a long, quiet tent at the market's heart, "is the voice stall. If you've come for what I think you've come for."

Sebastian stopped walking. "I haven't told you what I came for."

"No," the Echo agreed kindly. "But you walk like a woman carrying a question shaped exactly like a voice."

---

Inside the tent it was dim and warm, and the walls were hung with curtains of beads that were not beads but syllables, each one a small polished sphere of sound waiting to be strung back into speech. A broker sat at a desk that had been mended so many times it was less furniture than biography. He was old in the way Echoes were old—old the way a photograph is old, all his aging happening elsewhere—and he looked up at Sebastian with the weary kindness of a man who has watched thousands of people decide whether they can afford their own hearts.

"Sit," he said. "Or stand. Standing is cheaper. Sitting costs a memory of sitting down, and nobody ever thinks about that until it's too late."

Sebastian stood.

"How does it work?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The buying."

He opened a ledger—her stomach clenched reflexively at the sight of a ledger, even here, even now—and turned it toward her. "Everything the veil took was weighed before it was stored. Sentiment has mass; grief especially, grief is practically geological. When you buy something back, you pay its weight in equivalent coin. Guild scrip works—" he tapped the page, "—because guild scrip is already denominated in forgetting. Every note you carry bought somebody's silence once. Spending it here just... completes the circuit."

"So I'm trading someone's stolen memory for mine."

"I prefer 'facilitating a return,'" the broker said. "It sounds better at parties. But yes." He folded his hands. "Now. What are you looking for?"

And here was the thing Sebastian had not let herself think plainly since she'd crossed onto the glass, because thinking it plainly would mean wanting it plainly, and wanting was a door she couldn't afford to leave open while the real Door waited three days' walk away, demanding prices she hadn't agreed to pay.

"A voice," she said. "My mother's."

The broker didn't write anything down. He just looked at her for a moment, and then he rose, unhurried, and went to the back of the tent, where the deepest shelves stood—the ones closest to the ground, the way heavy things settle.

"We get very few requests for voices," he said, running his fingers along the shelf edges. "People ask for letters, because letters can be reread. People ask for faces, because faces can be imagined. But a voice—" he pulled out a small box, plain wood, no label, "—a voice you can't keep in your head honestly. Memory smooths voices flat. Within a year of losing someone, most people can't hear them anymore at all. They remember *that* the person spoke. Not the speaking." He set the box on the desk between them. "So when someone asks for a voice, I know they're not decorating a nostalgia. They're missing someone specifically."

Sebastian's hands had gone cold. "How much?"

"That depends." He lifted the lid.

The sound that came out was not loud. It was barely a sound at all—an hour of a life compressed into a hum, the way a seed compresses a tree. But Sebastian knew it instantly, the way you know your own name shouted from another room. Her knees actually buckled; she caught the desk edge.

Her mother. Laughing. Saying something Sebastian couldn't yet parse but whose shape she recognized the way you recognize the shape of home through fog.

"One hour," the broker said quietly. "Harvested during the Veiling. Standard confiscation—voices near the border were taken first, they carried the most... border in them." He paused. "You're the Joubert girl's daughter."

It wasn't a question. Sebastian stared at him.

"My grandmother," she managed, "has been telling people about me."

"The whole Steppe knows about you, child. You're the most interesting thing to happen here in fifty years, and I include the time a horse got loose on the glass and skated for a mile." He almost smiled. Then the smile settled into something more careful. "The price. An hour of a mother's voice, harvested fresh, pre-war vintage—that's a heavy item. In guild terms..."

He named the figure.

Sebastian's stomach dropped through the floor, through the Steppe, through every reflection beneath it. It was everything. Not everything she had—everything the guild had. Three years of wages, the scrip in Oren's strongbox she'd taken without asking, the sealed envelope marked EMERGENCIES that she was fairly sure had never once been opened because the guild had never once believed anything was worth it.

"That's not a purchase," she said. "That's a robbery."

"Yes," the broker agreed placidly. "Grief made purchasable is still grief. We just put a till in front of it." He slid a slip of paper across the desk. "You can take a partial hour. Thirty minutes. Twenty. Voices divide cleanly; it's one of their virtues. Most people take twenty minutes and spend the rest of their lives regretting the other forty."

Sebastian looked at the box. From inside it, faintly, absurdly, her mother was humming—the tuneless, wandering hum she'd always done while kneading bread, a hum that never found its melody because it wasn't looking for one.

*What the ledgers owe,* she thought. *What they took. What they owe me.*

She counted out the scrip—all of it, the strongbox money, the emergency envelope, the coins sewn into her coat lining that she'd forgotten about until her fingers found them now like old friends. The broker weighed each note on a small brass scale, nodding, and when the scale balanced he pushed the box across the desk with both hands, formally, the way you hand someone a country.

"For the record," he said, "the market doesn't usually do refunds. But if you find yourself back this way, short of coin—" he glanced at her face and softened, "—come see me anyway. Some accounts I keep open."

---

She didn't open the box right away.

She carried it out past the letter stall, past the pickle merchant (who told her his life story anyway; apparently eye contact was optional), up the low rise at the edge of the market where a bench faced the Glass Steppe, and there she sat with the box on her knees while the market noise washed below her like tide.

Somewhere behind her, the apron-Echo had materialized again, keeping a tactful distance that wasn't very distant.

"You don't have to listen alone," the Echo offered. "First listens are heavy. That's why we built benches facing outward. Everyone needs somewhere to put the overflow."

Sebastian almost said yes. Then she shook her head. "I need—I want to hear it the way I would have heard it. In a kitchen. Alone. Doing something with my hands." She looked down at her empty hands and almost laughed. "Which I don't have. Well. Close enough."

She opened the lid.

The hour poured out.

It began mid-sentence—her mother mid-argument with someone off-harvest, laughing while she scolded, because that was how her mother fought, joyfully, as though anger were a dance partner she refused to take seriously. There was the scrape of a chair. A door. Then closer, suddenly intimate, the harvest catching up with her: *Sebastian, love, if you eat that now you'll spoil your supper, and I mean it this time, I mean it the way I meant it Tuesday—*

Sebastian pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and let it happen.

There was bread being kneaded—the rhythmic slap and fold of it, the tuneless hum riding on top. There was a moment, around minute eleven, where her mother stopped everything and said, quietly, to no one the recording could see: *She has his stubbornness and my timing. God help everyone.* And then, softer, half to herself, in the voice mothers use when they believe themselves unheard: *Whatever happens, whatever they decide up in those offices—she gets to be from somewhere. I'll fight God himself for that.*

Sebastian sat on the bench and cried the inefficient way, the unbudgeted way, while the hour spent itself beside her.

Because that was the cruelty of it, the beautiful unbearable engineering of it: an hour is an hour. It played at its own pace. She could not slow it. She could not skip to the good parts, except that all of it was the good parts, including the part where her mother burned the bread and said a word that would have gotten her fined at any respectable market, including the part where she sang four lines of a song and forgot the rest and said *ah, well, your grandmother knew it, ask her sometime,* which—

Which Sebastian could now do. Which was possibly the reason the hour had felt, in her hands on the walk over, less like a purchase than a message.

At minute fifty-eight, her mother said, clear as a bell across fifty years and one impossible plain:

*If you're listening to this someday, little one—it means they took it, which means it mattered, which means hold on to what matters. Loudly. Out loud. Things held silently get misplaced.*

Then the hour ended, and the box was only a box, and the Steppe lay quiet under its stars, holding its million borrowed memories the way a lake holds rain: patiently, and without promising to give any of it back.

Sebastian closed the lid. Wiped her face. Discovered the apron-Echo had come to sit at the far end of the bench at some point, offering a handkerchief without comment, which was the single most civilized act Sebastian had witnessed in either version of her country.

"She fought for you," the Echo said. It wasn't a question either. Word seemed to travel oddly here.

"She tried." Sebastian's voice was wrecked and she let it be. "They veiled the whole country anyway."

"Mm. And yet here you sit, on the outside of the veil, holding her voice." The Echo patted the bench. "I'd call that a draw going into extra time."

Sebastian huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. Below them the market glowed, commerce in the currency of loss, and she thought: *this is what my ledgers became. This is where the entries went.* Somewhere down there, probably, were entries she herself had catalogued with loving care, filed under accession numbers, never once wondering whose kitchen they'd been stolen from. The thought didn't bring horror tonight. Tonight it brought something more dangerous: resolve with a face on it.

She stood. Tucked the box inside her coat, against her heart, which was where it had apparently been all along.

"Thank you," she told the Echo.

"Don't thank me. Tell your grandmother the song. She's been humming the missing verses for fifty years and driving the entire eastern ridge mad."

Sebastian was halfway down the rise when she saw it.

At the far edge of the market, where the lamplight thinned into ordinary dark, a figure stood apart from the stalls—too still, too deliberately placed, wearing a coat cut with a precision no merchant or Echo would bother with. Even at this distance Sebastian knew the posture. She'd know it anywhere, in any light, across any border: the particular way Ilse Marchetti stood when she was trying to decide whether her duty and her heart could fit in the same room without breaking something.

Except Ilse was supposed to be in the capital. Except Ilse should have no reason to be here, on the Steppe's edge, at a market that officially did not exist.

And she wasn't looking at the stalls.

She was looking—at Sebastian.

* * *

The space between them was thirty paces of trampled grass and five years of unsaid things, and Sebastian covered none of it. She stood where she was, one hand still resting on the coat pocket that held her mother's voice, and waited.

Ilse moved first. Of course she did. Ilse always moved first; it was one of the many things Sebastian had catalogued about her in the years when cataloguing her had been indistinguishable from loving her.

"You shouldn't be here," Ilse said when she got close. Her voice was low and rough, scraped raw by whatever ride or lie or treason had brought her. Up close, in the amber spill of the nearest lamp, Sebastian could see the details that frightened her more than the presence did: the mud on Ilse's boots that wasn't Steppe-mud, the shadowed exhaustion under her eyes, and—worst—a thin dark stain on her sleeve that she was holding slightly away from her body, the way one holds a wound one hasn't admitted to.

"Neither should you," Sebastian said. "This market isn't real. Officially."

"Neither am I. Officially." Ilse's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the scaffolding of one. Then it fell. "Sebastian. I don't have much time, and I need you to listen without asking questions, because the answers will only slow us down and some of them will hurt you."

Something cold walked up Sebastian's spine. "Ilse—"

"He knows." Ilse glanced once over her shoulder at the dark beyond the lamps. "Kessler knows you're on the Steppe. He's known since the cellar map. He's not sending auditors anymore, Sebastian. He's sending soldiers. And he isn't coming for you."

The market noise went on cheerfully behind them—the pickle man, the crying letter-buyer, the jarred lullabies humming against their lids—as if the world hadn't just tilted.

"He's coming for the Door," Ilse said. "And he leaves at dawn."
