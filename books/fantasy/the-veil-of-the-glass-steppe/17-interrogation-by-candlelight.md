## 17. Interrogation by Candlelight

The room they gave the instrument had once been a chapel, which Ilse thought was either very funny or very honest, depending on how much you trusted the Custodians' sense of irony. She did not trust it at all.

There was no altar anymore. In its place stood a small table, and on the table a candle — an actual candle, beeswax, because the truth-instrument disliked electric light the way some people disliked being photographed. Beside the candle sat a shallow dish of water no larger than a saucer. That was all. No wires, no needles, no levers. The Ministry had learned long ago that the most convincing instruments were the ones that looked like nothing at all.

Warden Kessler sat across from her with his hands folded, a man built entirely out of patience and good posture. He had the face of someone who had been told he looked trustworthy so often that he'd decided to make it a professional asset.

"Ilse Marchetti," he said pleasantly. "Thank you for coming."

"You said it was a routine audit debrief." She took the chair opposite him. It was the only other chair in the room, which meant there would be no witnesses, and she noted that the way one notes weather turning.

"It is routine," Kessler said. "Everything is routine until the moment it isn't. That's rather the philosophy of our entire institution."

He nodded to the dish of water. "You know what this is?"

"A reflection pool. Pre-Veiling technology. We're not supposed to have them anymore."

"We're not supposed to have a great many things." He turned the dish a quarter-turn, the way a man might straighten a picture frame. "It doesn't read thoughts. I want to be clear about that, because rumors get out of hand. It reads *alignment*. When you speak, the water shows whether your words and your heart are pointing in the same direction. Lie outright, and the surface clouds. Tell the truth, however painful, and it stays clear as glass." He smiled. "You'll be relieved to know it cannot detect omission. The Ministry tried for years to build one that could. It kept breaking. Apparently even the instruments find evasion more interesting than falsehood."

"That does sound like us," Ilse said, and immediately regretted the *us* — too warm, too familiar — but Kessler only laughed softly.

"Good. You're relaxed. That helps the readings."

*That helps the readings.* She filed the phrase away like evidence.

* * *

He began, as they always did, with the boring questions. Where had she been on the fourteenth. Whom had she interviewed. What condition were the western veil-anchors in. She answered each one cleanly, and the water in its little dish stayed flat and bright, holding the candle's flame like a held breath. It was almost meditative, the first twenty minutes. Almost pleasant. She caught herself thinking she could do this every week, and then hated herself for it, because that was exactly how they got you — they made the cage comfortable before they measured you for it.

Then he set down his notes.

"Let's talk about Vellmark headquarters," he said. "The old Guild of Impossible Things building."

Ilse did not let anything happen to her face. This was a discipline she'd practiced since childhood, when her mother's moods required a daughter who could look serene while calculating exits.

"What about it?"

"It's on your audit route. You've visited it — what, nine times in the past year? More than any other site. Curious, given that the guild has been formally classified as dormant for two decades." He tilted his head. "Tell me about the guild, Marchetti."

"Its records are disorganized," she said, and the water stayed clear, because that was true. "Its membership has dwindled to a handful of elderly members and one apprentice. Its funding is irregular. Its building leaks." All true. All beside the point. She was beginning to understand the shape of the evening.

"And yet you keep going back." Kessler folded his hands again. "Why?"

"Because dormancy is exactly when veiled assets go unmonitored," she said. "A leaking roof can damage archive material. Archive material can contain pre-Veiling references. Pre-Veiling references are my department."

The water shimmered — just slightly — and settled. True enough. Kessler watched it settle and made a small satisfied noise, and Ilse understood that the dish was not the trap. The dish was the *audience*. The trap was whatever question he asked after she'd grown confident.

She resolved to never grow confident. It was, she reflected, advice that would have served her well in several other areas of her life.

* * *

"Tell me about the apprentice," Kessler said.

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"Which apprentice?" she asked.

"There's only one. Winterbourne. Sebastian." He said the name the way he said everything, evenly, without emphasis, which somehow made it worse. "Born forty miles inside the erased border. Father from the eastern side, mother from the western. Both bloodlines of a country that officially does not exist. And yet here she is, employed by the last guild in the capital, cataloging objects that should have lost their impossibility years ago." He opened his folder — an actual paper folder; he liked paper, paper couldn't leak — and slid a photograph across the table.

Sebastian, taken from across a street, head bent over a ledger. Her hair falling forward. The particular way she bit her lower lip when a number didn't come out right.

Ilse looked at the photograph for exactly as long as a Custodian would look at a photograph: three seconds, clinical, forgettable.

"What about her?" she said.

"The Veiling Accord specifies that individuals of dual border lineage are to be flagged for observation," Kessler said. "Standard precaution. A person who belongs to both sides belongs to neither, and persons who belong to neither are historically the ones who go looking for doors." He tapped the photograph once. "Your observation reports on Winterbourne are thorough. Exemplary, even. But they're also — and I say this with genuine admiration — remarkably *fond*. You write about her coffee habits. You note when she laughs. In your February report you spent an entire paragraph describing how she talks to the ledgers when she thinks no one is listening."

"They're very old ledgers," Ilse said. "They respond to tone."

The water stayed clear. Barely. She saw the tremor in it, the faint ring spreading from nowhere, like a stone dropped somewhere far beneath the surface.

Kessler saw it too. Of course he did. He'd been watching the dish the whole time while pretending to watch her.

"Interesting," he said mildly. "The ledger theory."

"Custodian doctrine holds that archived objects retain residual sympathy," Ilse went on, warming now, committing fully to the performance, building the lie out of true bricks the way you build a fire out of true wood. "An object that has absorbed memory responds to cadence, to affection, to repetition. An archivist who speaks kindly to a ledger preserves it better than one who merely dusts it. My reports document Winterbourne's methods because her methods are *effective*, Warden. If we lose the guild archives, we lose the best early-warning system the veil has."

The water calmed. Settled. Went glass-bright.

And Ilse felt something she had not expected to feel in this room: *pride*. Not relief — pride. Because everything she'd just said was true, or true enough, and because she had just discovered that love, properly framed, was indistinguishable from diligence. Every tender thing she knew about Sebastian Winterbourne could be entered into the record as surveillance. The way she hummed when shelving. The ink stain on her left middle finger. The fact that she checked the door twice before locking up, always twice, as if hoping the second check would find something different.

All of it reportable. All of it, in the language of the Ministry, *asset monitoring*.

They had trained her for fifteen years to see everything and feel nothing, and instead she had learned to feel everything and describe it in their vocabulary. Let them read her reports. Let them read them aloud at assemblies. They would hear a model Custodian. Only she would hear the love underneath, intact, smuggled through customs in plain sight.

Kessler studied her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was his real smile, which she had seen perhaps twice, and both times shortly before something bad happened to someone.

"You're good at this," he said.

"Thank you, Warden."

"No — I mean you're genuinely good. Better than your file suggests. Which raises a different question." He closed the folder. "If a Custodian this careful has produced reports this fond, I have to wonder what the *object* of those reports is doing to earn them. People don't observe coffee habits out of duty, Marchetti. Believe me. I've tried."

The candle flame leaned again. Ilse kept her eyes on Kessler and her heart on a very short leash.

"With respect," she said, "you've never met her. She makes excellent coffee. It would be negligent *not* to report it."

A pause. Then Kessler laughed — a real laugh, brief and startling in the chapel-dark — and for a moment he seemed almost young, almost human, almost someone she might have liked under any other sky.

"All right," he said. "One last question, and then you can go home. It's off the record. Consider it curiosity between professionals." He turned the dish toward himself, so that the water faced him and its reflections faced her, and she realized she could no longer see the surface — only him, framed in it, doubled and dark.

"When the veil finally fails — and it will fail, Marchetti, sooner than the Ministry admits — whose side will you be standing on?"

Silence. The candle burned straight up, indifferent.

Ilse thought carefully. She thought about the word *side*, and how sides were drawn by men who drew maps, and how she had spent her whole career on the side of the map and her whole heart on the side of the country it erased. She thought about literal truths, and how many of them she owned.

"I stand where the work is," she said. "I always have."

The water — wherever it was facing — stayed clear. She would bet her life it stayed clear. She was, in fact, betting her life.

Kessler nodded slowly, and rose, and gathered his folder, and blew out the candle between them with a single unhurried breath, so that the last thing she saw in the dark water was his reflection dissolving.

"Go home, Marchetti," he said from the darkness. "Sleep well. You've earned it." A pause, and then, lightly, almost kindly:

"Oh — and tomorrow morning I'm reassigning the Vellmark district. Effective immediately. Fresh eyes on the guild. Yours have been... thorough enough."

* * *

She walked home through streets that flickered at the edges, lamps guttering on before she reached them, doors remembering themselves shut a half-second after she passed. The city was fraying. She had known it for years the way you know a loved one is ill — refusing the arithmetic, doing it anyway at 3 a.m.

In her flat, she lit no lamp. She sat at her desk and pulled out the drawer where she kept fifteen years of observation reports, and she found the February one, and she read her own paragraph about Sebastian talking to the ledgers — *she tells them they're doing well; she means it* — and she pressed her palm flat against the page as if checking it for a fever.

Fresh eyes on the guild. New watchers, hungry ones, with none of her careful blindness. Days, perhaps. Hours, if Kessler already suspected more than he'd shown — and he had shown her that photograph for a reason. Men like Kessler showed you things for reasons.

Ilse took out a blank sheet of Custodian letterhead and began, in her neatest administrative hand, to draft a false audit finding — a fabricated anchor-decay report in the western district, the kind of paperwork error that would pull inspection resources away from the guild for weeks.

It was treason. She noted this the way she noted weather. Then she noted that it wasn't nearly enough treason to matter, and reached for the master schedule — the true rotation of the veil, the calendar of its weakening — which she had copied months ago and told herself she'd copied for reference.

Outside, a clock struck eleven, hesitated, and struck it again, unsure.

Ilse wrote faster.
