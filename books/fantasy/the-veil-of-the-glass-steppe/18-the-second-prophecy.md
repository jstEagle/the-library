## 18. The Second Prophecy

The second folio arrived the way most impossible things do: by accident, and slightly damp.

I had spent three days in the deep archive since unlocking it with Oren's seal, and I had learned its rhythms the way one learns a temperamental stove. The archive breathed. It preferred candlelight to lamps. It hid things not out of malice but out of what I can only describe as shyness—the same quality that made certain ledgers slide themselves behind others when a Custodian walked past, and made the jar of borrowed thunder hum contentedly whenever anyone was lonely enough to sit with it.

So when I went looking for the 1899 prophecy folio's companion volume—because no book that important travels alone, my mother had said once, in a voice I had to take on faith—I did not search. I tidied.

This is a guild secret worth writing down: the fastest way to find a lost thing is to stop looking for it and start putting other things away. The lost thing cannot bear disorder. It surfaces like a cork.

I was reshelving a crate of municipal impossibilities—petitions from the 1940s asking the guild to make rain fall only on Sundays, a ledger of every door in Vellmark that had ever been locked against a returning son—when the shelf behind me exhaled. Not creaked. *Exhaled*, a soft papery sigh, and a slim volume dropped from a gap I had searched twice already, landing spine-up on the flagstones with the particular thud of a book that has been waiting a long time to be picked up.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the guild's old wax seal—the same seal now warm in my pocket—and across the wrapping, in Oren Voss's handwriting but a hand decades younger than his current one, someone had written:

*DO NOT REBIND. DO NOT READ ALOUD. DO NOT LET HER FIND IT.*

*Her* being underlined. Three times.

Well. I have never claimed to be good at doing what I'm told, especially when the instructions are this specific. I untied the knot with hands that were steadier than I expected them to be, which frightened me more than shaking would have. You know you've changed when dread arrives as calm.

Inside was a folio bound in grey calfskin, thinner than the first, its pages the color of weak tea. The title page read, in the same cramped copperplate as the 1899 volume: *A Second Accounting of Things to Come, Being Corrections and Warnings Upon the Former Prophecy, Set Down by the Same Unhappy Hand.*

The same unhappy hand. I turned back to the first folio in my memory, to the page with my name on it, tonight's date, *the Door Out.* Whoever had written my fate a century before I was born had apparently felt bad about it, because here were the footnotes.

* * *

I read until the candles burned down to puddles, and then I lit more candles, because some things you do not stop reading halfway through even if the building catches fire around you. Especially if the building catching fire is possibly part of the text.

The second folio did not repeat the first prophecy. It *argued* with it. The whole book was structured as a correction—a scholar quarreling with their own younger self across the gulf of pages. Where the first prophecy spoke in the lofty, weather-forecast tones I'd come to distrust (*on this date the Door Out shall be sought*), the second answered in a voice tight with fear:

*You wrote that the Door opens for the child of both borders. You wrote it as though it were a gift. It is not a gift. It is a load-bearing wall. Understand what you have described: the Door does not open FOR her. The Door opens BECAUSE OF her. She is not the key. She is the hinge.*

I sat back at that. My candle guttered and steadied. Somewhere above me, through however many feet of stone and shelving, the guild went about its evening—Maren arguing with the coal scuttle, old Piotr winding clocks that no longer agreed with each other—and I sat in the dark reading a dead person's apology and thinking: *hinge.* A hinge doesn't get to choose which way the door swings. That's rather the point of hinges.

The middle chapters were marginalia wars, the unhappy hand correcting itself, crossing out, re-crossing out. But near the end the handwriting settled, and the writer set down, with terrible clarity, the two endings. Two endings, side by side, the way a physician might set down two diagnoses—one survivable, one not—and ask a future reader to please, *please* pay attention to which symptoms present.

*The First Ending. The Door Out opens willingly, for one who belongs wholly to neither side and therefore to both. Through it comes not the old Vellmark but the possibility of a new one—the Steppe itself made country, the erased given soil again. The cost falls on the opener. This is written plainly so that no one may claim surprise.*

*The Second Ending. The Door Out is opened by force—by many hands, or by one hand holding many others' claims. Then the Door does not distinguish between restoration and robbery. It gives what is asked. And what a conqueror asks for is the past entire: the old borders, the old grievances, the war resumed at the exact point where veiling interrupted it. The Door opened by force returns Vellmark as it was on the last day before the Veiling—which is to say, mid-war, mid-mistrust, with every debt unpaid and every grave fresh. The erased do not come home. They come back angry.*

Below this, underlined so hard the pen had torn the page:

*If you are reading this and the Door has not yet opened: know that there will be others seeking it. There are always others. The veil was built by frightened people, and frightened people leave keys lying about, and patient people pick them up. Watch for the ones who speak of restoration but count the doors and measure the fields. A savior asks how to open the Door. A thief asks what is on the other side.*

*A savior goes alone, or with those who love her. A thief brings soldiers.*

I closed the folio.

Then I opened it again immediately, because closing it felt too much like agreeing to something, and read the final page, which was short:

*I have tried to warn her across a hundred years and I do not know if warnings travel that distance intact. So let me put it in the smallest words I own: Little hinge, they will tell you the Door is a door. It is also a mouth. Choose carefully whose hunger feeds it.*

* * *

I don't remember climbing the stairs. I remember arriving in the guild's common room with the folio under my arm and both hands full of candle stubs I had no memory of collecting, and Maren looking up from her mending with the expression of a woman who has seen apprentices return from the deep archive before and knows better than to ask questions before tea.

"You look," she said, threading her needle without looking at it, "like the ledger fell on you."

"The ledger fell on me," I agreed.

"Was it a heavy entry?"

"Two endings."

She snorted. "Amateur. We've got seven for the harvest festival alone." But her needle had stopped moving, and when she glanced up her eyes were kind and sharp at once, the way they always were, as though kindness and suspicion shared a lodging and split the rent. "Sebastian. What did you find down there?"

And here is the trouble with belonging to a guild of impossible things: everyone in it can smell a withheld truth the way horses smell weather. I could have lied. I've lied to Maren before, small lies, the polite lies of a person keeping her one precious secret close. But I thought of the unhappy hand's smallest words, and of the phrase *a thief brings soldiers,* and of Ilse Marchetti standing in the customs house light saying *the veil is failing* with a face that wanted very much to be believed, and I decided that if the Door was a mouth, I didn't want to stand at it hungry and alone.

"Sit down," I said. "Both of you."

Piotr looked up from his clocks. "I am sitting."

"Sit down *more.*"

They sat. I unwrapped the folio on the common table, between the teapot and Maren's darning, which struck me as exactly the right altar for it—prophecies belong among ordinary objects or they grow ideas above their station. And I told them. Not everything; the parts with my name I kept, selfishly, the way you keep a wound covered not because it's shameful but because you're not done deciding what it means. But I read them the two endings, and the warning about thieves, and the line about soldiers, and I watched their faces do what faces do when the floor of the world tilts a few degrees—not panic, not yet, just the careful recalibration of people who have spent their lives around impossible inventory and are grimly experienced at absorbing one more item.

Piotr spoke first. He is the oldest of us, older than Oren, and he has the habit of answering questions nobody asked, which is occasionally exactly the right skill.

"I knew a man in the customs service," he said. "Before my knees. He used to say that every border has two kinds of people waiting at it: the ones who want to go home, and the ones who want to own the road. He said you can always tell them apart by their luggage. Homesick people carry little. Owners carry maps."

"Charming," said Maren. "What's your point?"

"My point," said Piotr, "is that the girl should find out who else has been carrying maps."

* * *

Here is what I knew, laid out on the common table alongside the teapot, the darning, and a century-old warning:

Someone had been in the deep archive before me. This I had assumed—Oren's note proved it—but assumption hardened into evidence when I went back down that night and looked properly, with the seal's permission humming faintly in my fingertips like a struck tuning fork. The gap where the folio had hidden bore marks: dust disturbed in clean rectangles, the shape of boxes removed and returned. Someone had catalogued that shelf within the last decade. Catalogued it, and taken items.

I found the catalogue. Of course I found it; the guild keeps records of its records the way anxious families keep photographs of photographs. The last full inventory of the deep archive was dated nineteen years ago, signed by Guildmaster Oren Voss, in the steady hand he no longer possessed. And appended to it, in the same hand but newer, a supplementary list titled simply *Discrepancies*—items noted missing at subsequent counts.

Most were small. A compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost. A letter that delivered itself. A bell that rang once for every lie told in its hearing—that one stung; imagine the uses a dishonest institution would have for such a bell, and imagine it walking out the door.

But the last three entries on the discrepancy list were not small, and they were not random, and whoever had taken them had known precisely what they were gathering.

Item: *One surveyor's glass, guild-made, which shows land as it was before any change made to it.*

Item: *One treaty copy, unsigned draft, of the Veiling Accord, including the clause describing the Door Out's location.*

Item: *One key, provenance unknown, which the 1899 folio describes as fitting "no lock built, but perhaps one folded."*

I read that last line four times. *No lock built, but perhaps one folded.* Folded. The word the ledgers used for what had been done to Vellmark—folded out of memory, folded flat like a map, tucked into the pocket of a treaty. Which meant that somewhere in the world there was a key to a folded country, and it had not been in the guild's cellar for at least fifteen years.

I sat down on the cellar steps with the discrepancy list trembling in my hands, and I made myself think it through, because terror makes sloppy arithmetic and I could not afford sloppy arithmetic.

The first folio had named me. Tonight's date, my name, *the Door Out.* If I had found it, others could find it—or already had. The second folio warned of thieves who measured fields and counted doors. And here was an inventory proving that someone had spent years quietly assembling exactly the toolkit a Door-opener would need: a glass to see the old land, the clause that located the Door, and a key for locks that weren't built.

Three items. Three separate thefts, spread over years, none reported—because reporting them would mean explaining what they were for, and the guild had forgotten what it was for. Whoever took them hadn't needed to break in. They'd only needed the guild to be asleep inside its own walls, which it had been, graciously, for half a century. We had mistaken our decay for safety. A vault no one believes in is a vault no one guards.

And one more thing, the thing that made me put the list down and press my palms flat against the cold steps until the stone's indifference soaked up through my skin: the thefts predated me finding anything. Predated Oren giving me the seal. Whatever was coming for the Door had started moving years ago, patiently, while I catalogued thunder and rebound folios and felt sorry for myself in the pleasing, harmless way of the invisible. I had thought I was the watcher, discovering the watched-for. I had been wrong. I was the watched-for, discovering the watchers.

*Little hinge,* the unhappy hand had written. *They will tell you the Door is a door.*

"They," I said aloud to the empty cellar, and the empty cellar, which prefers candlelight and shyly hides things, said nothing back, which I chose to interpret as agreement that "they" was the correct pronoun.

* * *

Upstairs, dawn was doing its uncertain best. The city outside the windows flickered at its edges the way it had begun to lately—a chimney hesitating, a rooftop remembering itself a half-second late—and I stood watching it with the discrepancy list in my pocket next to the seal, two pieces of paper and a lump of wax standing between a country and whatever came for folded doors with soldiers and maps.

Maren found me at the window. She handed me tea without comment, which is her highest form of speech.

"So," she said. "Thieves."

"Possibly. Probably."

"And you're going to do something reckless about it."

"I'm going to *find out* something reckless about it. There's a difference. Reckless doing comes later, once I've identified the correct direction."

"Mm." She drank her own tea and looked out at the stuttering skyline with me. "You know who you need, then."

I did know. There was exactly one person I knew of who stood on the border between the guild and whatever moved in the dark on the other side of it—one person who audited veils, who recognized my name in forbidden files and chose silence, whose touch over a ledger last week had lasted a half-second too long and meant something neither of us had said aloud. One person who might know who else carried maps.

"She'll arrest me," I said.

"She bought you an hour of your mother's voice at the Night Market and pretended it was mislabeled inventory," said Maren. "In my professional opinion, she is not planning to arrest you. In my personal opinion, you should bring flowers, because you are hopeless, and hopelessness requires props."

"That was confidential."

"Nothing is confidential in a guild of four people and a haunted coal scuttle."

I laughed, and the laugh surprised me—it came out steady, unafraid, the laugh of a person who has read two endings and chosen, provisionally, to believe in neither of them yet. Then I finished my tea, and took the second folio off the table, and wrapped it back in its oilcloth, and beneath the old warning in Oren's young hand I added a line of my own, in ink still wet:

*Noted. Watching. —S.*

Because prophecies are correspondence, when you think about it. Somebody a hundred years dead had written me a letter full of fear and care, and it seemed only polite to answer.

I was sealing my reply into the archive's drop-slot—because yes, the deep archive has a drop-slot; every shy thing needs a way to be spoken to—when Piotr came down the stairs faster than his knees usually permit, holding a scrap of paper that had come, he said, with the morning post, though the morning post had not yet run.

It was a single line, no signature, the handwriting pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*THEY HAVE THE KEY. THEY LEAVE AT MOONRISE. COUNT THE SOLDIERS.*

And underneath, smaller, in handwriting I knew better than my own:

*Come alone. Come armed with the truth. Come to the customs house. —I.*
