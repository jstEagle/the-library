## 19. Kessler's Ambition

Warden Anselm Kessler kept his office at the top of the Custodian tower in the capital, on the floor where the windows had been replaced with frosted glass so that no one working there would be distracted by a city that did not officially exist. He had always found this arrangement sensible. Sentiment was a leak, and leaks were what he was paid to find.

He was finding one now.

The file lay open on his desk: *Marchetti, Ilse. Senior Auditor. Eleven years of service. Commendations for discretion.* And beneath the commendations, arranged with the patient neatness of a man who read other people's lives the way accountants read columns, Kessler's own annotations. He had been annotating Ilse Marchetti's career for three years now, ever since he'd noticed the small arithmetic of her reports — audits that took a day longer than they should, anomalies logged just often enough to look diligent, and never, not once, an anomaly that led anywhere.

Diligence without consequence was a signature. Guilty people were either careless or immaculate, and Marchetti was immaculate.

"Sir?" His adjutant, a young man named Pell whose enthusiasm Kessler had so far failed to grind down entirely, stood at the door with a tray of tea. "You rang?"

"I didn't ring."

"You touched the bell-pull twice, sir. That's the signal."

"That was an itch." But Kessler gestured him in anyway. There was no harm in tea, and Pell made it correctly — black, scalding, bitter enough to punish whoever drank it, which Kessler considered the only honest quality a beverage could have. "Pell. What do you know about the Veiling Accord?"

"The treaty that ended the border war, sir. Fifty years ago. Vellmark agreed to be dissolved, both sides signed, everyone went home sad but alive."

"Everyone went home," Kessler said, "sad but *edited*. Sit down."

Pell sat. He was twenty-six and still believed that when his superior invited him to sit, it meant trust rather than ambush. Kessler found this touching in the way one finds a candle touching — pleasant, warm, doomed.

"There is a document," Kessler said, "older than the Accord itself. The Custodians call it the master schedule. It describes the mechanism of the Veiling — how a country can be folded out of memory without a single death, provided someone stands outside the fold and holds the seam. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That we're the seam-holders, sir."

"We are the *seam*," Kessler corrected. "Holding implies we might let go. We are the seam. The veil is not a wall, Pell, it is a garment, and garments wear out." He turned the file toward Pell, though not far enough for the boy to actually read it; Kessler believed information should be shown the way a butcher shows meat, close enough to smell. "Our instruments say the fabric is thinning. Streets flicker. Objects forget themselves. Somewhere inside the fold, something has begun to move toward the Door Out."

"The Door Out, sir? From the prophecy folios?"

"From the *inventories*, which is where prophecy goes when it stops being poetry and starts being logistics." Kessler rose and crossed to the map on his wall — not the official map, which showed two tidy nations and a blank space politely labeled TERRITORY RESOLVED BY TREATY, but the Custodian map, inked over decades by men who had walked the edge of the erased country and come back lighter than they left. In its center, where the Glass Steppe lay under fifty years of enforced forgetting, he had drawn, in pencil, a single circle.

A door drawn as a circle. Even his own hand had flinched from drawing it properly.

"For eleven years I have managed the decay of this veil," Kessler said. "I have filed the reports, balanced the memory-tithes, signed off on the quiet disappearances of citizens who remembered too well. I have done this because stability is a virtue and because the alternative to the veil was a war that would have burned both nations to their foundations. I am not a monster, Pell, whatever the junior clerks say about my tea."

"No one says anything about your tea, sir."

"They will. My point is this." He tapped the pencil circle. "If the veil dies — if some apprentice or defector or sentimental ghost walks through that Door and drags old Vellmark back into the world — then everything the Accord bought will be repurchased in blood. Unless the Door opens onto the past *under management*."

Pell frowned, which was the correct response, and then, being Pell, kept frowning until he arrived somewhere worse. "Under management, sir? You mean—"

"I mean that pre-war Vellmark is sitting behind that Door like money in a vault," Kessler said, and heard his own voice go quiet, the way it did when he spoke of things he had wanted for a long time. "A whole country, preserved at the moment before ruin. Its institutions intact. Its people unbroken. A nation with no factionalism, no war debt, no history of betrayal — because we will arrive *before* all of that. Do you see? Every government dreams of founding itself on virgin soil. Here is soil older and richer than any colony, and all it requires is that we reach it first."

"With respect, sir — the Door opens for people who belong to both sides. The Echoes' doctrine, the folios, all of it—"

"The Door opens for whoever makes it open," Kessler said. "Doctrines are written by the people who got there first and want to be left alone. I have spent thirty years reading rules, Pell, and I have learned that every rule is a fence around something somebody was afraid of losing. This—" he touched the circle again, gently, almost fondly, "—this is worth building a ladder for."

He watched the idea take root in the young man's face. It was, Kessler thought, a good face for planting in: earnest, loyal, slightly undernourished by doubt. In another life Pell might have made a fine schoolteacher. In this one he would make a fine instrument.

"Retrieval squad," Kessler said. "Twelve handpicked. Men who've done veil-work, who won't panic when the ground shows them their childhoods. Provisioned for nine days. We move within the week."

"The week, sir? The schedule said the anniversary window—"

"The anniversary is three days after that. Yes. I can read the same schedule Marchetti can." He let that sit in the air between them, watching Pell work out who else could read the schedule, and what it meant that the Warden knew she could. "Which brings me to her."

"Marchetti." Pell's voice dropped, obediently conspiratorial. "You think she's the leak."

"I think," Kessler said, pouring his own tea because Pell had let it go cold in the excitement, "that Ilse Marchetti has spent eleven years being exactly competent enough, and that competence is like sugar — easy to fake in small amounts. I think she falsified an audit last month, and did it well, and that doing it well was her mistake, because it means she cared enough to try." He sipped. Bitter. Good. "She has gone to the provinces. She has requested access to sealed files she should not know exist. And yesterday, a customs officer in the eastern district reported a woman matching her description asking questions about the Glass Steppe."

"What are your orders, sir?"

Kessler looked out through the frosted glass, at the pale blur where the capital ought to be. Somewhere beyond it, a country that wasn't was dissolving politely into a country that might be, and in between stood a door, and before the door stood either a traitor he had once considered promoting or a fool he had once considered harmless, and either way the future was arriving faster than his paperwork.

"Surveillance first," he said. "Gentle. If she's simply gone soft, we bring her home and retire her with honors; sentiment responds well to pensions. If she's working against us—" he set down the cup, precisely, on its saucer, the click very small and very final, "—then we follow her to the Steppe. She'll lead us to the Door faster than any survey. Let the traitor carry the map."

"And if she leads us to the apprentice? The one from the prophecy records — Winterbourne?"

"Then we bring them both in." Kessler returned to his desk and drew a fresh sheet toward him, and began, in his small, immaculate hand, the mobilization order that would put twelve armed men on the glass within the week. "Winterbourne is half of each side of the border, Pell. By the Door's own rules, she may be the only key that fits. Which makes her the most valuable object in this hemisphere." His pen paused. For one moment — brief, quickly filed away, never to be examined — something that was almost envy crossed the Warden's face. "And the most valuable objects in any hemisphere end up in the custody of the state. It's practically patriotism."

Pell gathered the tea tray and went, whistling faintly, a young man carrying orders he believed were about protection.

Alone, Kessor finished the order, sealed it, and then unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk — the one with no lock, because no thief in history had ever robbed the drawer that was obviously empty. Inside lay a photograph, fifty years old, of a street in a city that no longer appeared on maps: cobbled, lamplit, ordinary, a woman laughing on a corner with bread in her arms. His mother, in Vellmark, the year before the Accord. The year before she stepped across a border that was about to stop existing, to visit a sister, and arrived on the other side unable to say why she was crying.

They had told him she'd died in the war. The Custodians had told him, carefully, kindly, when they'd recruited him at nineteen — told him so that he would hate the veil, and instead he had come to understand it, and then to serve it, and then, slowly, across thirty years of frosted windows and bitter tea, to want what it hid.

"She's still in there," he said to the photograph, in the tone of a man explaining an inheritance to a lawyer. "All of it. And I am going to bring her home."

It sounded, even to him, almost noble. That was the trick of ambition done properly: you dressed it in your mother's face and called it duty.

He locked the drawer he did not need to lock, out of habit, and rang for Pell — once, deliberately, this time — and began writing the names of twelve men who would walk on glass.

By morning the squad would be provisioned. By evening, surveillance on Marchetti confirmed what he already suspected: her trail bent east, toward the Steppe, moving fast, and beside her — traveling separately, converging like streams toward the same sea — the faint, flickering signature of a girl the prophecy books had named a century before she was born.

Kessler read the report twice, smiled the way ice smiles, and gave the order.

"Move up the timetable. We leave tonight."

* * *

That night, packing his field kit with the economy of a man who had rehearsed this moment for years without admitting it even to himself, Warden Kessler paused at the frosted window. Below, the capital glittered, unaware. Beyond the blur, a door waited in the glass, and behind the door, a country held its breath — and somewhere in that held breath, a woman with bread in her arms was still laughing on a corner, waiting fifty years for someone to come and get her.

He buttoned his coat. He took the photograph.

He went down into the dark, twelve men falling into step behind him like beads on a string, toward the border of a place that wasn't — and toward the girl who, if the old pages told true, was already walking the other way.

The race, though he did not yet know it, had already begun without him.
