## 20. What Ilse Buried

The Custodian archives smelled of cold iron and older lies.

Ilse Marchetti had walked these corridors for eleven years, and she knew their moods the way a sailor knows weather. Tonight the air held that particular stillness that meant someone had recently been through the restricted stacks—someone careful, someone frightened. The lamps along the wall burned with the steady, unblinking flame that Custodian doctrine called *vigilance light*, though Ilse had long privately renamed it *insomnia light*, since no one who worked beneath it ever slept well again.

She carried her clearance folio under one arm and her guilt under the other. Only one of these was heavy.

The file she wanted was filed under M. Of course it was. Marchetti, Ilse—Custodian Second Grade, appointed by examination, commended twice for diligence. The name on the spine was hers. The contents were also, in a sense, hers: eleven years of reports, observations, marginal notes in her own tidy hand. She had written every word. She had also, she now admitted to herself in the flat honest voice the archives demanded, written almost none of what was true.

She took the file to the reading alcove at the end of Row Nine, the one with the lamp whose shade was chipped in the shape of a small bite, as if the building itself had once tried to eat its own history and found it unpalatable. She sat. She did not open the file immediately. There was a ritual to this, she felt—one owed to the woman she had been before.

* * *

It had begun, as most catastrophes do, with paperwork.

Nine years ago, newly badged and terribly earnest, Ilse had been assigned routine observation duty at the Vellmark border customs post—a post which, technically, bordered nothing, since Vellmark officially did not exist, and which therefore spent its days stamping documents for travelers going nowhere in particular. It was the kind of assignment given to junior Custodians to teach them patience. It taught Ilse something else instead.

Her subject was a guild apprentice. A young woman from the Guild of Impossible Things who came to the customs post every second Thursday to collect a delivery of archival paper—the guild still used Vellmark currency, an anomaly Ilse was supposed to log and did not, because logging it would have triggered an audit, and an audit would have closed the account, and the account's closure would have meant the end of the deliveries, and—

Well. That was the official reason she gave herself at the time. *Preserving the anomaly for study.* Custodians were very good at giving themselves official reasons. It was practically the entrance exam.

The apprentice's name was Sebastian Winterbourne. She had ink on her left ring finger always, in the same place, as if she rested her hand against the page while reading. She argued gently with the customs clerk about import tariffs on thunder. She said thank you to the door hinge when it squeaked, apparently out of habit or kindness or some private theology Ilse never learned but thought about far more often than doctrine permitted.

The first report Ilse wrote read: *Subject observed collecting materials. No irregularities.*

This was false. There had been one irregularity, and it was that when Sebastian dropped a box of paper in the mud, Ilse had left her post—left her post, an offense punishable by reassignment—to help her pick it up, and Sebastian had looked at her with rain in her eyelashes and said, "You're the first person from your side of the border who's ever touched anything of mine," and Ilse had felt something in her chest fold shut like a ledger closing, and she had known, with the terrible clarity of the young, that she was ruined.

*No irregularities,* she wrote anyway.

After that it became easier. That is the thing about treason that no one warns you about: it does not arrive as a storm. It arrives as a shortcut. Each report was only slightly kinder than the truth, each omission only slightly convenient, and by the fifth year Ilse Marchetti was commended twice for diligence on the strength of files that documented everything except the one thing happening inside her.

She had watched Sebastian for nine years. She knew the way she hummed when rebinding folios—always slightly flat, always the same folk song neither country claimed. She knew she took her tea bitter enough to stand a spoon in. She knew she talked to ledgers, actually talked to them, low apologies and encouragements, as though books were shy animals. And she knew that Sebastian Winterbourne had appeared in a prophecy folio printed in 1899 beside tonight's date, because Ilse had read the forbidden file three years ago and had chosen—had *chosen*, with her whole cowardly devoted heart—not to report that either.

Tonight the veil's integrity readings had finally made lying insufficient. The flickers at dusk were no longer deniable. Warden Kessler had begun asking questions in his soft, patient voice, the voice he used on things he intended to take apart. And somewhere across the city, a woman with ink on her ring finger was walking toward something the Custodians would kill to contain.

Ilse opened the file.

* * *

Eleven years of herself lay in the folder, neat as teeth.

She read it the way you read a diary left behind by a stranger who happens to share your handwriting. *Subject appears content in her work.* (Sebastian had cried that week; Ilse had heard it through the guild's thin cellar window and had stood in the rain outside for an hour, unable to knock.) *No significant contacts.* (Ilse herself, forty-one Thursdays, uncounted.) *Subject poses no threat to the veil.* (False. Subject was the threat. Subject was the prophecy. Subject was the only person alive who could walk the Glass Steppe, and Ilse had buried that sentence so deep in bureaucratic language that even she had nearly lost it.)

Near the back, folded into a report from six years ago, she found the worst of it: a draft observation she had written and never submitted, in a moment of weakness she'd forgotten until now. It read, in full:

*Subject laughed today at something the customs clerk said. Noted for completeness: I would give up my grade, my post, and the entire fiction of my neutrality to hear that sound again. Recommend no action. Recommend no action ever.*

She had been twenty-four and honest for exactly one paragraph. Then she had folded the page away like a letter to someone she didn't dare become, and gone back to writing *no irregularities* until the words wore grooves in her soul.

Ilse sat in the chipped lamplight and let the shame come, because she had decided on the walk over that she would not flinch from it. Shame was the toll. You paid it or you carried it forever, and she was done carrying.

Then she did the arithmetic that changed everything: every falsified report had bought Sebastian time. Nine years of small mercies, stacked like sandbags against a flood. Whatever else she was—coward, traitor, fraud—she had kept one person safe with her lying, and there were worse uses for a life. Her grandmother had smuggled flour across this same border during the war and had called it *feeding people, not politics*. Ilse had smuggled time. Same trade, different goods.

She stood, went to the brazier the archivists kept for disposing of damaged documents, and fed the file to it page by page.

The reports burned quickly. Bureaucratic paper is designed, Ilse thought, to be forgotten; it goes up almost gratefully. But the old folded draft—the one honest paragraph—resisted for a moment, curling around itself, and she had to hold it into the flames with two fingers while the words *recommend no action ever* blackened and lifted as sparks.

"Action," she told the fire quietly, "is taken."

* * *

The master schedule lived in Warden Kessler's outer office, which was either arrogance or a trap, and with Kessler it was generally both.

Ilse had audited the office twice before, officially. She knew the schedule was kept in a locked drawer whose key hung on a hook behind Kessler's portrait of the Founding Wardens—a portrait in which all four men looked deeply constipated, a detail that had sustained Ilse's morale through many grim years. She knew the night clerk dozed between the second and third bell. She knew, because Kessler himself had boasted of it at last year's vigilance dinner, that the office's alarm ward responded only to unauthorized *entry*, not to authorized personnel doing unauthorized things. "Loyalty is our best lock," he'd said, and the room had applauded, and Ilse had thought: *you have just described exactly how I will rob you.*

She went at the third bell, wearing her own badge, carrying her own clipboard, looking like exactly what everyone believed she was: a diligent woman doing diligence.

The clerk slept with his cheek on a manual of interrogation techniques, which Ilse found poetically apt. She lifted the key from behind the constipated Founders. The drawer opened with a sound like a small cough.

The master schedule was thinner than she expected. That was the horror of it—all that power, all those vanished streets and rewritten Tuesdays, summarized on fourteen pages in a Warden's tidy hand. She read fast. The anniversary of the Veiling fell in nineteen days. On that date, the veil's anchoring ritual required renewal, and for one hour—*one hour*, marked in red ink—the Door Out could be held open by anyone holding the founding seal. Kessler had annotated the margin in pencil, in handwriting that tried to look administrative and failed: *Window confirmed. Asset acquisition pending.*

Asset. He meant Sebastian. He meant the woman with ink on her finger, reduced to a word in a margin.

Ilse copied the schedule onto her clipboard pages in her own tidy hand—it seemed only fair that treason should also be legible—replaced the original, returned the key, and adjusted the sleeping clerk's manual so it propped his head more comfortably. This last act served no strategic purpose whatsoever. Some habits of decency survive even defection.

At the office door she paused. On Kessler's desk lay tomorrow's orders, already drafted: a retrieval squad, mobilization for the Steppe, and a list of names. The fourth name down was *Winterbourne, S.* The fifth was *Marchetti, I. — observe.*

Observe. Even now, even tonight, she was still only an observation in someone's file.

"Not anymore," Ilse said softly, and let herself out.

* * *

The burning of her own career was, she reflected on the walk east, remarkably anticlimactic. No alarms. No pursuit. Just a woman with a clipboard full of stolen country walking through streets that flickered faintly at their edges, like paintings left out in the rain.

She stopped on the bridge over the canal—the same bridge where, six years ago, she had watched Sebastian feed pigeons with the seriousness of a head of state—and she took out the small tin she'd carried for eleven years, the one containing her badge, her commendation seals, and the single pressed flower Sebastian had once dropped without noticing, which Ilse had rescued from the customs house floor like a relic.

She dropped the badge and seals into the canal. The flower she kept.

Treason, it turned out, felt less like falling and more like setting something down that she had been carrying so long she'd mistaken it for a spine. Her shoulders ached. Her step, she noticed with mild surprise, did not.

Ahead of her, eastward, past the failing streetlamps and the politely dissolving neighborhoods, lay the Glass Steppe, and somewhere on it a Door, and somewhere nearer than that a woman who talked to ledgers and thanked door hinges and had no idea—none, not yet—that the person coming to find her had spent nine years loving her in the margins of surveillance reports.

Ilse touched the pressed flower through the tin.

"Hold on," she told the dark, to the city, to the woman, to whatever remained of the girl who'd once written *recommend no action ever*. "I'm done watching. I'm coming."

Behind her, in the capital, the first of Kessler's messengers was already mounting the stairs to his office, carrying the news that a file was ash, a drawer stood unlocked, and a shadow of eleven years' standing had stepped out of the Custodians' ledger—into the one country they could never follow her into: the truth.
