## 21. Reunion on the Glass

Ilse had crossed the border of Vellmark eleven times in her career as a Custodian, and every crossing had felt like stepping through a curtain of cold water—unpleasant, brief, official. This was nothing like that.

This was walking into the sea.

The Steppe began where the last farm road gave up pretending to go anywhere, and it did not announce itself. One moment her boots were on packed earth; the next they were on glass, and the glass was not cold, and it was not exactly there. Below her feet—or perhaps above them; the distinction had grown ambitious—memories moved like weather. She saw a wedding she'd never attended, seen from the angle of someone standing near the cake table. She saw a dog, brown and ridiculous, chasing something that might have been a ball or might have been the concept of a ball.

"Right," Ilse said aloud, because saying things aloud made them slightly more real, and she needed all the reality she could get. "Don't think about the dog."

She thought about the dog for a quarter mile.

She was carrying three things: the veil's master schedule folded into her coat lining beside her heart, a knife she hoped never to use, and a letter of resignation she had written at two in the morning on Kessler's own stationery and then burned, unread, because some gestures are for the writer alone.

The storm found her an hour in.

* * *

Sebastian felt it first as a change in the light.

She had been walking the glass for two days, following roads that the living map insisted still existed, guided by the grandmother-Echo's instructions, which were less directions than opinions. *Keep left when the horizon smells like bread,* the old woman had said. *If you see your mother's kitchen, do not go in. You will want to. Don't.*

The horizon smelled like bread now, which meant nothing good.

The sky over the Steppe went strange—not dark, but crowded, as if every cloud had invited relatives. And then the memories came down like rain.

They fell in sheets. A hailstorm of somebody's first day of school. A downpour of a funeral, gray and slow, soaking everything in the specific grief of a woman named Anselmina whom Sebastian had never met and now knew intimately. She put up her arms as if that would help, which it did not, and stumbled toward the only shelter the flat plain offered: a single structure rising out of the glass ahead, impossible and unmistakable.

A bandstand.

It stood in the middle of nowhere, white-painted iron and a scalloped roof, the kind of thing that belongs in a park where lovers walk and children drop ice cream. It cast no reflection in the glass below it, which meant, by the logic she was slowly learning, that it belonged to no one—that it was a memory so widely shared it had become weatherproof. Public infrastructure of the heart.

She ran for it and dove beneath the roof just as the storm broke fully overhead, a deluge of lantern-light and brass-band music falling from a sky that had decided, all at once, to remember a festival.

And under the bandstand, already there, dripping with other people's joy—

"Ilse," Sebastian said.

The name came out before permission was sought from any sensible part of her. Ilse Marchetti stood with her back against the far rail, soaked in falling light, a cut on her sleeve and the expression of a woman who has rehearsed forty openings and watched all of them desert her.

"Sebastian Winterbourne," Ilse said. "You're shorter than your file says."

"My file?"

"I've read your file." A pause. The storm hammered the roof with someone's wedding night. "Extensively. For years. Professionally."

"Professionally."

"You keep repeating my adverbs."

"You keep using them to hide behind." Sebastian's heart was doing something unbecoming of a guild apprentice—something arrhythmic and hopeful and deeply impractical. "How did you find me?"

"The way water finds a low place." Ilse pushed off the rail and came closer, and the falling festival lit her face in colors Sebastian's ledgers had no column for. "I've been walking toward you since I was twenty-two years old. Tonight the walking finally matched the direction."

* * *

The storm showed no sign of stopping. Above the bandstand roof, the sky replayed the festival in fragments: a ferris wheel turning against fireworks, a mayor whose speech went on so long that the memory itself seemed embarrassed, children with paper lanterns, a whole town square that Sebastian recognized with a lurch—it was the customs square at Halden Crossing, the border town where she'd been born, rendered in the collective memory of everyone who had ever loved it on a summer evening.

Except neither of them had ever been to this festival.

"That's the Midsummer Fair of 1949," Ilse said, following her gaze. "The year before the Veiling. Every Custodian trains on it. We memorize the festival as a case study in what was lost." Her mouth twisted. "Propaganda, really. Look how lovely. Look what peace cost you. Now stop asking questions."

"And you?"

"I've sat through the lecture four times." She looked up at the falling lanterns with an expression that was almost tender and entirely furious at herself. "I know this festival better than any festival I actually attended. I danced at it once, in a training exercise. They project the memory and you move through it, learn its streets." She glanced sideways. "Did you dance at it?"

"No. But my—" Sebastian stopped. The word *grandmother* caught in her throat like a fishbone. "Someone told me about it. Someone who was there. She said the lemonade was terrible and everyone lied about it."

Ilse laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped custody. "That matches the archival record. There's a complaint filed about the lemonade. In triplicate."

"You're joking."

"Custodians file complaints about everything. It's how we pray." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone to her sleeve, where the cut was, and Sebastian saw the blood had soaked through in a line, neat and recent.

"You're hurt."

"It's a scratch."

"It's a scratch in the shape of a story. Sit down."

"There's nowhere to sit. This is a memory."

"There's a bench right there."

There was indeed a bench—bandstands have benches, even remembered ones—and Ilse sat on it with the wary obedience of someone who has decided, provisionally and against all doctrine, to be taken care of. Sebastian knelt and unwound the makeshift bandage she'd been carrying since leaving the guild, the one Oren had pressed on her along with the seal, muttering that a Winterbourne should always travel with linen and a grudge.

Under the cloth, the wound was a clean slice across the forearm. A Custodian blade's signature. Sebastian's hands went very still.

"Kessler's people."

"One of them. He was faster than his paperwork." Ilse said it evenly, watching Sebastian's face rather than her arm. "It's why I'm late. I had to be creative about leaving. Creativity, in the Custodians, is a disciplinary offense."

"What happened? Tell me properly."

"He suspected the leak was me. He was right, but he couldn't prove it, so he sent me on an audit instead of an arrest—which is Custodian for *I am giving you rope and enjoying the view.* His man followed me to the edge of the Steppe. We disagreed about whether I should continue." She flexed her fingers. "I won the disagreement. Narrowly."

Sebastian tied off the bandage. Her fingers lingered a half-second past medical necessity, and Ilse looked at them, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them noted it.

"You could have stayed," Sebastian said quietly. "You could have let him win. You'd be safe tonight, in a barracks, filing complaints about the coffee."

"Yes," Ilse agreed. "And you'd be out here alone in a storm made of dead people's happiness. So you can see my dilemma."

Above them, the festival fell harder. The music thickened—the brass band swelling until the notes came down like warm rain, and the air under the bandstand began to shimmer, and Sebastian realized with a jolt that the memory was not staying above the roof. It was seeping in. The lanterns drifted down between the iron struts like fireflies. The smell of fried dough and cut grass arrived without any dough or grass to account for it. Somewhere just beyond the bandstand's edge, a crowd murmured in the particular murmur of a crowd waiting for fireworks.

"We should move," Ilse started.

"Don't." Sebastian caught her sleeve—the uninjured arm. "Look."

The glass around the bandstand had gone soft and bright, and in it—not reflections now, but invitations—an image formed: the fair itself, whole and walkable, the ferris wheel turning, stalls lining the square, the terrible lemonade stand with its hand-lettered sign promising FRESH LEMONADE, FRESHLY. The memory-storm, having fallen, was pooling. And pooled memories, the grandmother had told her, can be entered.

"It wants us in," Sebastian breathed. "Storms on the Steppe don't pass over you. They pass *through* you. Unless—" she groped for the old woman's phrasing— "unless you give them somewhere to go. Unless you share the shelter."

"Share it how?"

"I don't know. I think—" Sebastian laughed, helpless. "I think it has to be a memory we hold together. That's what a bandstand *is.* It's not anyone's memory. It's everyone's. It only shelters people who agree to be under it at the same time."

Ilse stared at her. "That is either beautiful or completely fabricated."

"I'm a guild apprentice. Those are the same thing."

And because Ilse had spent seven years reading a file and calling it distance, and because the file had never once mentioned that Sebastian Winterbourne laughed like she was apologizing for it, she held out her hand.

"Together, then," Ilse said. "On three."

"On three," Sebastian said, and took it.

* * *

They stepped into the fair.

The transition was not like walking through a door. It was like being *remembered*—the sensation, briefly overwhelming, of being an event in someone else's mind, warmed and slightly idealized. Then the square resolved around them: cobbles worn smooth by generations, bunting in the old Vellmark colors (green and rust, before the treaty replaced them with gray), the ferris wheel groaning its slow circumference against a sky gone golden with invented evening.

"Oh," said Ilse, in a very small voice.

Around them the festival went about its business. Ghost-crowds drifted past—translucent at the edges, solid at the center, laughing at jokes whose punchlines arrived half a second before the setup, because memory is an impatient storyteller. A child ran between them chasing a hoop, and passed *through* their joined hands like light through water, and giggled anyway.

"This is the training projection," Ilse whispered. "But it's never—I've walked this square a dozen times and it never did *this*." She turned in a slow circle. "It's never had me in it. It's never looked back."

The fair did seem to be looking back. Stalls turned their wares toward the two women as they passed. The ferris wheel paused its groan. Even the ghost-bandsman nearest them lifted his ghostly trumpet in something like a salute.

"They know we don't belong," Sebastian said softly. "And they don't care. That's the difference, isn't it? That's what the veil took. Not the country—the *not caring.* Everyone belonging whether or not anyone checked their papers."

Ilse said nothing. She had gone pale in the golden light, and her hand had risen unconsciously to her collar, where the Custodian sigil used to sit before she'd pried it off and dropped it in a river.

They walked. The square was not large, but memory squares never are—they fold to fit whatever the rememberer needs. They passed the lemonade stand, and Ilse, with the gravity of a scholar confirming a thesis, bought two cups from a vendor whose coins were also lemonade, and handed one to Sebastian.

"Well?" Sebastian said.

Ilse drank. Her face performed a small, controlled catastrophe. "Terrible."

"Liar. Your face says it's wonderful."

"My face is off duty." But she was smiling, and the smile was doing things to the evening that the evening welcomed.

They came, inevitably, to the dancing. A wooden floor had been laid in the square's center, and ghost-couples turned upon it in a slow waltz that owed nothing to any actual orchestra and everything to the general consensus of ten thousand remembered evenings. The floor had room for exactly two more.

"That wasn't in the training module," Ilse said.

"Everything's in the training module. You just weren't allowed to notice."

"Sebastian."

"Ilse."

"You know what I am. What I was. I built the thing that erased all this. I audited its walls. I signed off on confiscations—" her voice caught on the bureaucratic word and cracked it open— "on *letters*, Sebastian. Sacks of letters. I logged them. I initialed the sacks."

"I know."

"And you'll still—" She gestured at the dance floor, at the whole forgiving evening, unable to finish.

Sebastian set down her terrible wonderful lemonade. "My guild's entire inventory," she said, "is things that shouldn't exist anymore. A jar of borrowed thunder. A key to a house that burned. I spent six years dusting them and thinking they were sad." She looked up. "They're not sad. They're *patient.* That's the whole job, Ilse. Keeping things past all reason, on the chance someone comes back for them." She extended her hand, palm up, in the exact gesture of a ledger awaiting an entry. "I'm keeping you. Past all reason. Sign here."

For a moment Ilse simply looked at the offered hand as though it were a document in a language she'd studied but never dared speak.

Then she took it.

The waltz took them the way storms take rooftops. Neither of them was a good dancer—Sebastian counted under her breath and got the counting wrong; Ilse danced the way she did everything, precisely and three beats ahead of the music, which is another way of saying not with it—but the floor didn't mind. The floor was made of consensus. Around them the ghosts made room, and somewhere the brass section, sensing narrative obligation, swelled.

"I never attended anything like this," Ilse said into the space between their shoulders. "My childhood was schedules. My adulthood was schedules with knives." A breath. "This isn't my memory. I need you to know that. Whatever this is, I'm—"

"Borrowing it. I know. Me too." Sebastian steered them through a turn, badly. "But look around. Nobody here is checking provenance. That's rather the point of the place."

Ilse was quiet for the length of a phrase of music. Then, in the tone of a woman filing the truest report of her career:

"I used to watch your window. From the customs post. Tuesdays, you'd put a lamp in the window while you worked, and I told myself I was monitoring a security risk." Her voice went rough. "Seven years of surveillance, and the most classified thing I learned is that you hum when you rebind books. Badly. With great commitment."

"I do not hum badly."

"You hum like a door that needs oil. I have it documented."

"You *documented* my humming?"

"In the margin. Margins aren't subject to audit." Ilse pulled back just far enough to look at her, and the golden invented light caught the cut on her arm, the burn-scar on her jaw from a veil malfunction years ago that the official report blamed on a stove, every small damage of a life spent guarding a lie. "I'm telling you badly. I've drafted this confession nine hundred times and it always came out as a form. *Observed: subject. Duration: prolonged. Recommendation:*—" she stopped, and started again, plainer: "I love you. I think I have since the second Tuesday. The first Tuesday I was professional. The second Tuesday you waved at the lamp."

Sebastian stopped dancing. The music, obligingly, held its breath.

"You waved back," she said. "Every week. I thought the customs post had a friendly clerk."

"I *am* the customs post's friendly clerk."

"You're the Custodian Empire's most wanted traitor."

"I contain multitudes. Several of them are clerks."

And Sebastian laughed—really laughed, the unguarded kind, the kind that had gotten her labeled *insufficiently solemn* in her own guild file—and kissed her, there on the borrowed dance floor of a festival neither of them ever attended, surrounded by ghosts who politely looked elsewhere, because even memories understand privacy.

When they parted, the storm outside the bandstand had begun to lift. The fair dimmed around them by gentle degrees, the ferris wheel slowing, the ghosts thinning into lantern-glow, everyone folding themselves away with the courtesy of guests who know the hour. But before it went, the memory did one last thing.

The crowd parted. Across the square, unhurried, came an old woman in a green shawl, translucent at the edges and absolutely solid at the center, and her face was the face from the cellar-map, the face from the Echo camp, the face Sebastian had begun, dangerously, to think of as home.

Grandmother Maren stopped an arm's length away and looked at the two of them—at their joined hands, at Ilse's Custodian blade still belted at her hip, at the whole improbable picture—with the expression of a woman revising a prophecy.

"So," she said. "You found each other. Good. Because I've just heard what the Door is charging these days, child, and we need to talk about the price—before Kessler's army reaches the Salt Gate."

Behind her, the last of the festival went out like a held breath released.
