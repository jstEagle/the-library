## 22. The Grandmother's Bargain

The Echoes had a way of arranging themselves around Sebastian that reminded her of cats who had decided, collectively, that she was furniture worth sitting on. They did not demand anything of her. They simply gathered—drifting near when she sat by the cold hearth in the memory-house they kept on the Steppe's edge, settling into chairs that were only suggestions of chairs, and waiting with the particular patience of people who had already waited fifty years and could afford a little more.

Her grandmother's Echo—Maret Vell, though she insisted on *Oma*, said with the crisp authority of someone who had once run a bakery and never fully surrendered the title—had been circling the subject for two days.

Sebastian knew the signs. Oma circled subjects the way weather circled mountains: visibly, ominously, and with an eventual certainty that made preparation pointless.

"You're not eating," Oma said now, watching Sebastian push a bowl of soup across the table. The soup was real enough to steam; it had been someone's favorite meal once, folded into the veil along with everything else, and the Echoes cooked it from memory so faithfully that Sebastian could taste the dill and feel vaguely guilty about it.

"I'm eating."

"You're performing the concept of eating." Oma reached over and turned the bowl back toward her. "In my house, we do not perform meals. We eat them. Sit properly."

"I am sitting properly."

"You are sitting like a woman reading bad news in her own head instead of saying it out loud where someone can argue with her." Oma folded her hands—hands that were slightly translucent at the edges, like paper held up to a lamp—and looked at Sebastian with eyes the same gray-green as her own, which was an unsettling experience Sebastian still hadn't gotten used to. Looking into her own future, if the family resemblance ran true, and finding it disapproving of her posture.

"The Door wants a memory," Sebastian said. "A life. Mine. Everything I'd live after crossing—the whole unwritten part. That's the price of opening it."

"Yes," said Oma.

"And I've been trying to find a way around it. A substitution. Something of equal weight." She laughed, without much humor in it. "I've been doing what the guild does, honestly. Trying to trade my way out of grief. And then last night I thought—" She stopped.

"Say it," Oma said. "Whatever you thought in the dark, say it in the light where it can be ashamed of itself properly."

Sebastian set down her spoon. "You offered. Last night, while I slept. You spoke to me in the dream the Steppe gives us, and you offered to take my place. To be the payment instead of me. Your peace instead of my future."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the glass plain caught the afternoon light and threw it back in long amber panes, and somewhere among the other Echoes a man was humming a song he no longer had words for, just the tune, worn smooth as a river stone.

"That wasn't a dream," Oma said at last. "That was an offer. I made it while you were asleep because I know you, girl. You have your mother's face and your grandfather's stubbornness, and either one alone would refuse me. Together they might hesitate long enough for me to get the words out."

"It doesn't work that way. I asked the Door—I went back out to the seam this morning, before dawn, and I asked it directly. It accepts a life freely given. But it weighs them." Sebastian's voice caught and she steadied it. "It would take your final peace, Oma. The rest you've earned. Fifty years folded into this place, holding onto each other, holding onto yourselves—that's not nothing. That's the whole country's inheritance. If you paid my toll, you wouldn't die. Echoes don't die. You'd just... unravel backward. Into nothing. No reunion, no rest, no sitting in this kitchen arguing about soup. Just gone, and the Door fat and satisfied, and me walking through on your account."

"Good," said Oma.

"*Good?*"

"Efficient. One old woman's rest against a young woman's whole life. Any accountant would call that a bargain." She said it briskly, the way she must have once announced that a loaf had come out wrong and would be given to the birds rather than sold, no use crying about it.

Sebastian stood up. Sat down. Stood up again, because staying seated felt like agreeing. "No."

"No?"

"No. Absolutely not. It's not efficient, it's not a bargain, and I'm not letting you do it. I came here to say no to your face, which apparently requires me to sleep first, but consider yourself formally refused."

Oma regarded her for a long moment. Then, to Sebastian's complete surprise, she smiled—not the sharp bakery smile, but something older and softer underneath it, the smile of a woman looking at proof of something she'd hoped was true.

"There it is," she said quietly.

"There *what* is?"

"Your mother did the same thing. Did I ever tell you? During the war, when the rations came through, she'd give hers to the neighbor children and swear she'd already eaten. I caught her at it four times before she got clever about it. Generosity as sleight of hand. Love that won't let itself be witnessed." Oma leaned forward. "You want to save everyone by paying their bills secretly. It's a lovely flaw. I'm telling you it's a flaw."

"It's not a flaw to refuse to spend another person's soul!"

"It is when the other person has *asked*."

Sebastian pressed her palms flat against the table. The wood under her hands was memory too—somebody's kitchen table, somebody's rings ground into the grain—and it held her weight as faithfully as any real thing. "Why?" she asked. "Why would you even offer? You waited fifty years. You told me—you told me the waiting was almost over, that when the Door opens, all of you step out of the veil and finally rest. Why would you throw that away for me? You barely know me. I'm a stranger who wandered onto your plain carrying a guild seal and a head full of borrowed prophecies."

Oma was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had lost its briskness entirely.

"Because you are not a stranger, Sebastian. You are my granddaughter, and I have known you were coming for fifty years, and knowing has been both the hardest and the easiest part of the waiting." She touched her own chest, where a heart would be. "We Echoes are what the veil couldn't digest. The too-loved parts. The parts that fought being forgotten. And do you know what we loved hardest, all of us, through all of it? Not our houses. Not our borders or our flags or the war itself, gods knows. We loved the *after*. The grandchildren we'd never meet. The country our children might build if anyone ever found the door." Her eyes glittered. "You're not the price of my peace, child. You're the reason for it. Spending myself on you isn't a sacrifice. It's the point. It's what the whole fifty years was *for*."

The words landed in Sebastian's chest like stones dropped into deep water—she felt them sink, felt the ripples spread outward into places she hadn't known were dry.

And it would be easy. That was the terrible thing. It would be so easy to say yes. To let love do the arithmetic. To walk through the Door with her future intact and her grandmother's peace spent like coin, and tell herself forever after that it had been Oma's choice, freely given, and who was she to dishonor it?

She thought of Ilse then—Ilse lying in the memory-house two doors down with a Custodian blade-wound in her side, refusing strong tea on principle, pretending not to wince when she laughed. Ilse, who had burned her own file. Ilse, who had taught her, without ever lecturing, that the truest things are the ones you do in daylight, with witnesses, at cost to yourself.

If she took Oma's bargain, she would carry it the way the guild carried its ledgers. Every entry a stolen recollection. Every kindness a cage. She would walk into her new country with her grandmother's rest jingling in her pocket like stolen silver.

"No," Sebastian said again, and this time the word came out steady, rooted. "I mean it. And I'll tell you why, and then you're going to sit there and listen, because I'm the granddaughter and you started it."

Oma raised an eyebrow but, remarkably, listened.

"If I let you pay, I don't arrive at the Door clean. I arrive bought. And a country founded on a purchased passage is just the old Veiling Accord wearing new clothes—we'd have traded one generation's forgetting for another's, and called it mercy because the math was smaller." She took a breath. "More than that—if I accept your peace as payment, then I've agreed that some people's existence is currency. Yours. The Echoes'. That's what Kessler believes. That's what the Custodians built. I will not build my country out of the same material they built theirs."

"So what will you pay?" Oma asked softly. "The Door does not open for good intentions."

"I don't know yet." Sebastian laughed—a real laugh this time, startled out of her. "That's the honest answer. I don't know. Maybe there isn't a way. Maybe I stand in front of that seam of light and it takes my whole unwritten life and I let it, because that's the actual price and I'm actually willing. Or maybe I find something it wants more. But whatever I do, I'll do it with my eyes open, owing nobody, spending only what's mine to spend." She reached across the table and covered Oma's translucent hand with her own solid one. "You gave me fifty years of your love already, Oma. It's the richest thing I own. Don't make it a debt."

For a moment Oma didn't move. Then she turned her hand palm-up and gripped Sebastian's fingers, and her grip—though made of memory, though light at the edges—was strong enough to hurt.

"You sound like your mother," she said, and her voice cracked cleanly down the middle. "She argued exactly like that. Right up until the border closed and I never saw her again." She wiped her eyes with her apron, briskly, businesslike, as though tears were flour that had gotten where flour shouldn't. "Well. Refused, then. Formally. I suppose I should have expected it. Nobody in this family has ever accepted help gracefully."

"We're a disgrace," Sebastian agreed, blinking hard.

"Worse than a disgrace. We're *consistent*." Oma stood, and the kitchen seemed to settle around her, reassured. "Now. Since you've refused the sensible solution, you'd better tell me what you're planning instead, and I'm going to poke holes in it, and then we're going to improve it together. That's how we do things in this family. Badly, loudly, and with witnesses."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and it was the strangest council Sebastian had ever attended: herself, a translucent grandmother, and a rotating parliament of Echoes who drifted in and out of the kitchen offering opinions. An old ferryman who remembered every current of the rivers that no longer existed. Two sisters who had been schoolteachers and could not stop correcting each other. A boy of perhaps ten who had died—or been veiled—at fourteen and remained fourteen, sharp-eyed and merciless, who kept asking questions like *"But why does the Door want the future instead of the past? Isn't that backwards?"*

It was the boy's question, eventually, that cracked something open.

"Why *does* it?" Sebastian repeated, turning it over. "Everything else on the Steppe runs backward. The memories underfoot, the storms, the market. But the Door wants what hasn't happened yet." She stared at the table. "Unless... unless it doesn't want a payment at all. Unless it wants a *seed*. A life after crossing, carried through intact—that's not a toll, that's cargo. It's not asking what I'll give up. It's asking what I'll bring."

The schoolteacher sisters exchanged a look of professional vindication. "See," said one, "this is why one listens to children."

"One listens to *prepared* children," said the other. "The rest is chaos."

But Sebastian was barely hearing them. Because if the Door wanted a future brought through it—not surrendered, *carried*—then Kessler's plan made horrible new sense. He meant to pour his soldiers through into the past, to loot the old country with an army. And the Door, fed on a genuine future willingly carried, would meet his stolen past at the threshold, and—

She stood up so fast her chair fell over.

"Oma. The anniversary. Ilse said the Door can be held open during the anniversary of the Veiling—in three days. Kessler knows that date too. He's not going to wait politely."

"He never did have manners," Oma said dryly. "His family bought flour from mine. Paid late, always."

Sebastian was already moving toward the door, toward the memory-house where Ilse lay healing, toward three days that had just become the whole width of the world. At the threshold she stopped and looked back at her grandmother, who stood in the amber kitchen light with flour-dusted hands that weren't quite there.

"Thank you," Sebastian said. "For the offer. For letting me refuse it."

Oma smiled the soft smile again. "Go," she said. "And girl—when you stand in front of that Door, remember whose future you're carrying. It isn't only yours anymore. It never was."

Outside, the glass plain had begun to darken toward evening, and far off across the Steppe, at the seam where the world was stitched shut, the light was flickering—fast, and wrong, like a pulse counting down.

Three days. Maybe fewer.

Sebastian ran.
