## 23. Skirmish at the Salt Gate

The Salt Gate had been a customs post once, back when Vellmark still bothered to have borders. Now it was two pillars of white stone and a lintel crusted with mineral bloom, standing alone on the glass like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die.

Sebastian felt it before she saw it—a wrongness in the Steppe's hum, the way the glass underfoot began to sing at a pitch that made her teeth ache.

"They're here," she said.

Ilse was already crouching, one palm flat against the plain. Sebastian had learned, in the days since the reunion, to read Ilse's silences the way she'd once read ledgers: fluently, and with growing alarm.

"How many?" she asked.

"Twelve." Ilse's voice was flat, professional, terrible. "Retrieval squad. Kessler didn't send them through the veil—he sent them *under* it, along the old salt road. Which means he knows the route. Which means—"

"Someone told him."

Ilse didn't answer. She didn't have to. The betrayal was still an open wound between them, three days old and festering. Tomas had wept when they found his bunk empty, his son's memory returned to him in a sealed envelope like a bribe wrapped in love. Sebastian understood it. That was the worst part. She would have done the same thing, for her mother's voice.

"Don't," Ilse said quietly, catching her expression. "Don't forgive him yet. We need you angry."

"I'm not angry."

"You're cataloguing your anger. It's what you do instead of feeling things. I've watched you do it for six years."

Sebastian almost smiled despite everything. "You *have* watched me for six years."

"Yes, well." Ilse stood, brushing glass dust from her knees. "One of us had to be thorough about it."

The Echoes came out of the horizon like weather. Sebastian had stopped being startled by them—the way they condensed from nothing, figures of translucent light wearing the shapes of coats and shawls—but this time there was something new in their bearing. Purpose. The grandmother was among them, unmistakable, her hands folded in front of her the way Sebastian's mother used to fold hers when delivering news that would not be argued with.

"They're moving toward the Door," the grandmother said without preamble. "Twelve soldiers and a warden's instrument. They will reach the seam by nightfall unless they are slowed."

"Slowed how?" Sebastian asked. "There are twelve of them and—" she gestured at the Echoes, "—however many of you, but you can't be hurt, can you? And they can't hurt you?"

The grandmother's smile was thin as frost on a windowpane. "Child, we are made entirely of things people wanted to forget. Do you imagine we have no teeth?"

* * *

Ilse saw the squad from the ridge of a memory-storm's aftermath—a hill of frozen recollection, all amber light and half-formed faces—and her stomach did what it always did before violence: nothing. Calm. The Custodian training held even now, even with treason in her pockets and the woman she loved thirty meters behind her.

Warden Kessler was not with them. Of course not. Kessler never bled first; he arrived after, to take credit or take custody.

Sergeant Brecht led the column, and Ilse knew him—knew his wife's name, knew he kept a photograph of his daughter in his breast pocket because regulations forbade photographs on duty and Brecht had never once followed a regulation that inconvenienced him. Good man. Bad assignment. Twelve good men and women walking into a country made of grief.

She turned to the Echoes gathered around her. "They'll have truth-instruments and salt-iron. The instruments won't work out here—the Steppe doesn't recognize Custodian authority. But the salt-iron will. It cuts light as well as flesh."

"And what do you propose?" asked an Echo who had been, in life, a schoolteacher named Anselm, and who retained both his pedagogical patience and his habit of adjusting spectacles he no longer possessed.

"We don't fight them," Ilse said. "We *remind* them."

Anselm's translucent eyebrows rose. "Ah," he said, in the tone of a man who has just been handed a very interesting examination question. "Yes. I see. And you're certain you want to unleash that?"

"No," Ilse said honestly. "But I'm asking anyway."

* * *

Sebastian watched the first soldier fall into a loop and would remember it, afterward, as the strangest mercy she had ever witnessed.

His name was Corporal Voss—no relation to Oren, though the universe clearly enjoyed its small jokes—and he was twenty meters ahead of his squad when the Steppe reached up and gave him back a day. Sebastian saw it happen: the glass beneath him shimmered, and suddenly the corporal was elsewhere, kneeling in a kitchen that existed only in the folds of the veil, watching a woman—it must have been his mother—pull bread from an oven. His rifle clattered to the glass. He started to laugh, then to cry, then to laugh again, caught in the rotation of a perfect afternoon that had ended decades ago and been traded away, sold to the guild by some desperate citizen, fed into the veil, and now spent like ammunition.

"That one's mine," Anselm said with grim satisfaction. "I taught him arithmetic, you know. Before. He was hopeless at long division." He paused. "He's crying."

"They usually cry," the grandmother said. "It's the ones who don't cry you worry about."

The squad broke formation. How could they not? Each of them walked into a pocket of their own surrendered past—a wedding, a christening, a last conversation with a father, a summer that smelled of cut grass and someone's name they'd promised never to say aloud again. The Steppe didn't distinguish between enemies and mourners. It simply recognized hunger.

Only Sergeant Brecht kept walking.

He had tied a cloth over his eyes.

* * *

"Brecht," Ilse called out, stepping onto the open glass where he could hear her. "Sergeant. Take off the blindfold."

"I know your voice, Marchetti." His hand stayed on his sidearm. He moved forward by counting steps, a trick she'd seen demonstrated once in training and privately considered a party trick until this exact moment. "Traitor's voice."

"Probably. But think about what you're doing. You're marching blind across a battlefield made of memories, toward a door that eats conquerors, on behalf of a man who will feed *you* to the veil the moment you stop being useful. I've read the maintenance schedules, Brecht. I know what keepers cost."

"The Warden says—"

"The Warden says whatever gets the Door open." She moved laterally as she spoke, drawing him off his bearing, buying seconds. Behind her she could feel Sebastian and the Echoes falling back toward the seam, and every second mattered now, and she was aware—with the strange double-vision of combat—that she was also aware of how Brecht's blindfold had slipped slightly, showing one eye, and that eye was frightened.

"Sergeant," she said, softer. "Your daughter. What's her name?"

The blindfolded head turned toward her voice like a compass needle.

"You don't get to ask that."

"You're right. I don't. But the Steppe already knows it. And right now, forty meters back, there is a kitchen with bread in it and a woman in it, and if you take off that cloth you can stand in that kitchen until the world ends, and no one will ever write a report about it."

For a moment—one full breath, two—Brecht wavered.

Then the flare went up.

It came from the direction of the Salt Gate, red and wrong, burning a color that didn't belong in any sky: Kessler's signal. *The instrument has found the seam.* And the Steppe itself flinched. Sebastian felt it through her boots—an entire country recoiling—and the loops shattered all at once, twelve kitchens and weddings and summers collapsing like soap bubbles, and twelve soldiers stood blinking on bare glass, disoriented and armed and very much awake.

"There!" someone shouted, spotting the Echoes.

"Sebastian," Ilse said quietly. "Run."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not *leaving* me. There's a difference, and it involves me arriving later, extremely annoyed, and you apologizing profusely." Ilse drew her blade—the Custodian issue, salt-iron, the kind that cut light—and for the first time since the customs house, since the candlelit interrogations and the burned files and the master schedule hidden against her ribs, she looked entirely like what she had been trained to be. "Go. Now. That's an order from a traitor."

* * *

Sebastian ran.

She hated every step of it. The glass sang under her feet, the Door's light growing ahead of them like a seam of dawn torn into the ground, and behind her she heard the ugly business of actual combat—the ring of salt-iron against whatever weapons Echoes could improvise from nostalgia, shouts, the wet sound she refused to let herself identify. Anselm fell beside her path, or rather unraveled, his translucent edges fraying where a blade had passed through him, and his last words were characteristically pedantic: "Note the date, girl! Note it! History rewards the—"

Then he was scattered light, and Sebastian ran harder.

She heard Ilse shout—not words, just a sound, a sound Sebastian would carry in her chest for the rest of her life, whichever length that turned out to be—and she turned, because of course she turned, because she had never once in her life managed to walk past a closed book or an open wound—

Ilse was down. Brecht stood over her, blindfold gone, sidearm raised, and between them lay Ilse's blade and a spreading darkness on the glass that was not shadow. One of the other soldiers had gotten past the Echoes' line, and his knife had found her below the shoulder blade while she blocked Brecht's shot with her own body.

Sebastian did the impossible thing, which was the only thing the Guild of Impossible Things had ever actually trained her to do: she stopped running toward safety and ran toward the danger, screaming words she didn't plan, and the Steppe answered her. The glass rose in a wave—not sharp, not cruel, just *insistent*, a wall of remembering—and threw Brecht backward into a memory of his daughter's birth, and the knife-man stumbled as his own childhood flooded up around his ankles like rising water.

Sebastian dropped to her knees in the spreading dark.

"Ilse. Hey. Hey, look at me. Look at me, Marchetti, that's an order from a bookkeeper."

Ilse's eyes found her, unfocused, then focused, and she managed—God help them both—half a smile. "Told you. Arriving late. Extremely annoyed."

"You're bleeding."

"Custodians bleed professionally. It's in the manual." Her hand found Sebastian's wrist and gripped it with startling strength. "The schedule. Against my ribs. Three days, Sebastian. The anniversary. The Door holds only then, and only for—" Her breath hitched. "Take it. Don't read it here. Read it when my hands aren't shaking."

"Your hands aren't shaking."

"They will be. I'm very brave, but I'm not stupid."

The Echoes closed around them like a tide coming in, the grandmother foremost, and beyond them Sebastian could see the remaining soldiers regrouping at the Salt Gate—and beyond *them*, on the far ridge, a figure in a warden's coat, motionless, watching, patient as arithmetic.

Kessler had arrived to take custody after all.

The grandmother knelt beside Ilse, and her translucent hands hovered over the wound, doing whatever Echoes could do, which was not healing but perhaps *stalling*—memory pressed against mortality like a cold compress. "She'll live," the old woman said, in the tone of someone reporting weather. "Barely, loudly, and with complaints. Now pick her up, child. We have three days, a wounded woman, a dying map, and a Door that is starting to notice us."

Sebastian slid her arms under Ilse and lifted. Ilse weighed almost nothing, which terrified her more than the blood did.

"For the record," Ilse murmured against her collarbone, "this counts as arriving together."

"For the record," Sebastian said, turning toward the deep Steppe, toward the Door, toward the three days that were all that remained of the world's patience, "you're heavy as sin and I'm putting it in Ledger Zero."

And somewhere behind them, unhurried, Kessler began to walk.
