## 24. Triage and Truths

The safehouse was a bookbinder's cellar two streets from the Salt Gate, chosen by an Echo named Marta who had died—or been folded—before Sebastian was born and who nevertheless remembered that the cellar's owner had kept a well-stocked first aid kit behind the false spine of a Bible. The Bible was still there. The kit was too. Vellmark's habit of preserving the useless details of ordinary life had never seemed so much like mercy as it did at half past midnight, with Ilse Marchetti bleeding gently but persistently into a chair.

"It's not deep," Ilse said, in the tone of someone reporting the weather from inside a hurricane.

"You were stabbed."

"I was *grazed* by a blade with *aspirations*."

"Sit down before you fall down, and let me look at it, or I swear I will trade your memory of this argument to the veil myself."

Ilse sat. It cost her something—the wince she tried to hide by turning toward the candle—and Sebastian pretended not to see it, because pretending not to see things was apparently the language they'd been speaking for eleven years and old habits died harder than countries did.

The wound was on her left side, below the ribs, where a Custodian blade—a thin, ugly instrument called a *severance*, designed not to cut flesh so much as cut a person's *continuity*—had caught her as she shoved Sebastian through the Salt Gate. The gash itself was shallow. But the edges of it were wrong. They didn't bleed quite normally; they bled in small pauses, like a sentence losing words. Where the blood dried, the skin beneath looked faintly unfinished, the way a page looks when the ink has been lifted off it.

Sebastian had seen what severance blades did to whole streets. She had seen them do it to whole *days*.

"Hold still." She knelt, opened the kit—gauze, iodine, a spool of surgical thread gone amber with age, and, inexplicably, a tin of peppermints—and got to work cleaning the wound while Ilse gripped the arms of the chair with the discipline of a woman who had been interrogated by candlelight and considered this nothing.

It should be noted, for the record, that Sebastian Winterbourne was competent. She could rebind a folio in quarter-bound goatskin without a bubble in the paste. She could mend a torn map so the roads matched. Her hands knew how to do delicate work under bad light. What her hands did *not* know how to do was touch Ilse Marchetti and remain merely professional, and so every pass of the cloth was a negotiation between urgency and the terrible awareness of warm skin, of breath held just slightly, of eleven years of coded courtesy finally running out of code.

"You're staring," Ilse said.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring *affectionately*."

"I'm assessing whether you'll live, which requires affection, because if you die I will have to carry you out of here, and you are heavier than you look, and also I would be very annoyed."

Ilse laughed—a short, pained, real laugh—and then hissed when the iodine found her. "Sorry," Sebastian said, not sorry at all in the way that meant *I'm sorry and also this is your own fault for taking a blade meant for me.*

They worked in silence for a while. Outside, muffled through the floorboards, the city made its stuttering sounds: clocks arguing about the hour, a tram passing twice with different conductors' bells. Somewhere far off, the Steppe sang its low glass note, the one the Echoes said meant the borders were dreaming.

Sebastian threaded the needle.

"Ilse."

"Mm."

"When you stepped in front of that blade—" She stopped. Started again, the way she did when rebinding a broken spine: find the true line of the fold first. "When you stepped in front of it, you didn't hesitate. You didn't even look surprised. You looked like someone doing something she'd decided on a long time ago."

Ilse was quiet. The candle moved their shadows up the wall, over shelves of blank ledgers the bookbinder had never filled because he'd been folded mid-career, mid-life, mid-sentence.

"That's accurate," Ilse said finally.

"How long?"

"What?"

"How long ago did you decide?"

Ilse looked at her then—really looked, the way she almost never allowed herself, because looking was surveillance and surveillance was her profession and she had spent a decade making sure her eyes always reported duty instead of want. But she was wounded and it was midnight and the world was ending politely, street by street, and she was tired all the way down to whatever a person is underneath their orders.

"Do you remember the customs post at Ferren Bridge?" she asked.

"No. Should I?"

"No," Ilse said softly. "That's rather the point. I remember it enough for both of us."

She told it plainly, the way she told everything—as if filing a report—but her voice went quiet in the middle the way reports never did. Eleven years ago. A rainy autumn inspection at a border crossing that officially did not exist, because neither side of it officially existed. A young apprentice from a guild nobody had heard of, presenting papers for a crate of impossible objects—a jar of borrowed thunder, if Sebastian wanted the exact manifest, and Ilse had memorized the manifest because she had no talent for forgetting anything about that day. The apprentice had argued with her. Not angrily—*scholarly*. She had cited guild regulations at a Custodian, in the rain, with thunder in a jar rattling faintly on the counter between them like an impatient cat.

"You told me the jar was mislabeled," Ilse said. "You said it wasn't borrowed thunder, it was *lent*, and there was a difference, and the difference mattered, and I stood there getting wet and thinking: this is the most dangerous person I have ever met, because she believes paperwork can be kind."

Sebastian laughed despite everything. "That does sound like me."

"I wrote my report that night. First honest sentence I'd written in years: *Subject argues about labels in the rain. Recommend continued observation.* And then I observed you," Ilse said, "for eleven years. I falsified every file. I learned your walking routes, your tea preferences, the way you talk to books when you think no one is listening. I told myself it was surveillance. I was extremely thorough at lying to myself. I'm a professional."

The needle hovered. Sebastian's hands, so steady over torn maps, were not steady now.

"Why tell me this tonight?"

"Because tonight I bled on a salt gate for you," Ilse said, simply, "and if I wait for a better moment there may not be one. The veil eats its keepers, Sebastian. It eats days and streets and grandmothers and customs posts at Ferren Bridge. I am done letting it eat things I haven't finished saying."

Sebastian tied off the last stitch. Her fingers were shaking. She pressed the bandage flat with her palm, over Ilse's heartbeat, and left it there, because leaving it there was easier than taking it away.

"I love you," she said.

It came out smaller than it had lived inside her for years—eleven years, if they were counting, and they were, apparently, both of them, in separate locked rooms of the same archive. Not a declaration so much as a ledger entry, finally made: *one (1) love, held in trust, unclaimed, condition: excellent, provenance: a rainy bridge.* She said it again, louder, because the first time had been for herself and the second time was for Ilse.

"I love you. I have loved you since—I don't know when. Since before I knew your name was real. Since the letters you left in returned books, which I know were you, Ilse, marginalia doesn't lie, nobody annotates a shipping manifest *that tenderly* by accident."

"The margins were *operational notes*—"

"They were *flirtation in cipher*—"

"—fine, they were flirtation in cipher," Ilse admitted, and then, wreckingly, she smiled, and Sebastian understood that she had survived the skirmish at the Salt Gate only to be killed outright in a bookbinder's cellar by the smile of a woman with a stitched-up side and iodine on her collarbone. "For the record," Ilse said, "I love you too. Since the rain. Possibly since the label argument. Definitely since you apologized to the jar of thunder when you dropped it."

"I didn't apologize to the—"

"*You curtsied to it.*"

"It has feelings! It's *thunder!*"

And then they were laughing, both of them, helplessly, the unhinged laughter of people sitting in a cellar under a dissolving city with a knife-wound and a war coming, and Ilse pulled Sebastian close with her good arm, careful of the stitches, careless of everything else, and kissed her like closing a ledger that had been open far too long.

* * *

Afterward they sat on the floor against the shelves, sharing the peppermints from the first aid kit, because the bookbinder had believed no emergency was complete without mint, and honestly he might have been right.

Sebastian had her head on Ilse's uninjured shoulder, cataloguing the moment with the desperation of a woman whose profession had taught her exactly how perishable moments were. The candle. The stitch-line rising and falling with Ilse's breath. The taste of peppermint. *Keep this,* she told herself, the way she'd walked her neighborhood two nights ago, memorizing doorways. *Whatever the Door asks. Keep this.*

"I have something for you," Ilse said eventually. "Besides the obvious."

"Oh?"

Ilse reached into her coat—wincing, refusing help, being insufferable about it—and produced a folder. Plain grey, Custodian issue, stamped with the seal of the Office of Continuity. But the seal had been broken deliberately, cleanly, the way you break a seal when you've decided the office no longer has authority over you.

"This is the master schedule," she said. "Every timing the veil runs on. Feeding cycles, memory harvests, reinforcement rotations. And on the last page—" she opened it to a diagram Sebastian recognized with a lurch: the Glass Steppe, rendered in Custodian survey-lines, with a single seam of light marked at its heart— "the maintenance window for the Door Out."

Sebastian sat up. "There's a *maintenance window?*"

"Everything has a maintenance window. Even miracles. Especially miracles—they're the highest-maintenance things we have." Ilse tapped the seam. "The Door can't simply be opened, and it can't simply be held. Once it starts opening, it draws on the veil itself, and the veil fights back—it tries to heal over the Door like skin over a wound. There's exactly one stretch of time each year when the veil is too busy holding itself together to fight. The night it renews the original Accord. The anniversary of the Veiling."

"And when is—"

"Three nights from now."

The word landed in the cellar like a stone in a well. Three nights. Sebastian counted backward automatically, the way she counted pages, the way she counted everything: three days to gather the guild, cross the Steppe, reach the seam, and stand at the Door when it opened—with Kessler's retrieval squad somewhere out there in the dark, hunting them, and a traitor's information possibly already in Kessler's hands.

Three days. She thought of Oren, lucid and heartbroken, pressing the founding seal into her hands. She thought of the Echoes singing the old borders. She thought of the price waiting at the threshold, the one she hadn't let herself name yet.

"You're doing the thing," Ilse said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes all ledger. Where you turn the future into columns." Ilse tilted her head to look at her. "Say what it is."

Sebastian exhaled. "I'm trying to figure out how to save a country in seventy-two hours."

"Don't." Ilse took her hand—the bandaged side complained; she ignored it. "Don't save a country in seventy-two hours. Countries take centuries and mostly fail. Do what your guild actually does."

"Our guild trades in stolen memories and calls it impossible things."

"Your guild does *small things carefully*," Ilse said. "You told me that on the bridge. You said impossibility isn't scale, it's attention. So: three days, one road across the glass, one Door, and everyone we can convince to walk with us. That's not saving a country. That's just the next small thing. Then the next."

Sebastian looked at her—at this impossible woman who had spent eleven years committing treason in increments, one falsified report at a time, all of it leading here, to a cellar, to a folder full of the enemy's clockwork handed over like a bouquet.

"When did you become the hopeful one?" Sebastian asked.

"I'm not hopeful. I'm *scheduled*." Ilse tapped the folder. "Hope is your department. I just make sure you arrive on time."

Sebastian laughed, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand—there were tears, apparently; there had probably been tears for some time—and bent over the master schedule with Ilse warm against her side, reading Custodian survey-lines by candlelight, planning a crossing.

The Steppe's edge was a night's walk. The crossing itself, with the glass reshaping and Kessler's squad surely moving, was another two days if the Echoes' songs held true. Which left no margin at all—which meant, Sebastian realized, her stomach tightening even as something steadier settled beneath it, that they couldn't go alone, and couldn't go quietly, and couldn't go without the one thing they hadn't yet asked for.

"We need the guild," she said aloud.

"The four remaining members of a dying institution?"

"Yes. And the map under the map. And Oren's seal. And every impossible object left in the inventory—including," she added, with dawning, slightly hysterical resolve, "the jar of lent thunder."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the city stuttered; the Steppe sang; somewhere in the dark a retrieval squad sharpened its doctrine. And in the cellar, Sebastian Winterbourne closed the master schedule, kissed the woman who had stolen it, and stood up, already reaching for her coat.

"Then we'd better get home," she said. "The guild has a country to trade."

But as they snuffed the candle and climbed toward the street, neither of them saw the thin grey figure slip away from the cellar's outer door—nor knew that the route across the Steppe, murmured in love and lamplight, was already traveling toward Warden Kessler faster than any echo could sing.
