## 25. The Guild Rises

I walked back to the guild through streets that were trying very hard to be streets.

That is the only way I can describe it. The cobblestones under my feet were cobblestones; the lamplighters' poles stood where lamplighters' poles ought to stand. But there was a hesitancy to it all, a flicker at the edges of things, like an actor who has forgotten his line and is waiting for someone in the wings to whisper it to him. A woman watering her geraniums paused mid-tilt and stared at the watering can as though she could not remember what it was for. Then she remembered—or something remembered for her—and went on.

Three days until the anniversary of the Veiling. Three days until the Door could be held open. And between me and that Door: a dying guild full of people who did not know what they had been feeding all these years.

I climbed the steps of headquarters—the ones that creaked in the key of E-flat, because Master Voss had once traded a composer's perfect pitch for a dry roof, and the stairs had never forgiven anyone since—and pushed open the doors into the great hall.

The lamps were lit. Someone had made tea. The remaining members of the Guild of Impossible Things sat around the long table like the last guests at a wedding nobody wanted to admit was over.

There were seven of us now. Seven, from a guild whose founding roster ran to four hundred names. I counted them the way you count your own heartbeat after a fall: Mrs. Aldous, who had catalogued the archive for sixty years and knew every folio by its smell; Fenwick, our locksmith, who could open anything except, he liked to say, his own heart, which was not true but he enjoyed saying it; the twins Perpetua and Sable, who kept the inventory of borrowed weather and argued constantly about whether fog should be filed under H for humidity or F for feelings; old Tomas, who repaired broken vows and had not taken a new commission since the war; young Wren, sixteen, apprenticed for barely a year, who still believed everything we told her; and at the head of the table, Master Oren Voss, who was this morning lucid enough to pour the tea, which was both a mercy and a warning. Lucidity in Oren came like sun through cloud—brief, beautiful, and a sign of weather changing.

"Sebastian," he said, and my name in his mouth was steady. "You look like a woman who has been reading things she shouldn't."

"I've been reading everything I shouldn't," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

Mrs. Aldous sniffed. "Sit down before you deliver whatever speech you've clearly been rehearsing on the walk over. You have your mother's tell. You press your thumb against your forefinger when you're about to be earnest."

I hadn't known my mother's tells. I pressed my thumb against my forefinger anyway.

"I need to show you all something," I said. "And I need you to hear all of it before anyone speaks. Especially you, Fenwick."

Fenwick raised both hands. "I haven't said a word."

"You were composing one. I could see it behind your eyes."

* * *

I brought out the ledgers—all of them, the whole debt of them, stacked on the table until the wood groaned. Fifty years of entries. Fifty years of small impossibilities, each one purchased with a memory, each memory fed into the veil that kept Vellmark folded out of the world.

I showed them the columns. The left-hand column, which we had always read as inventory: *one jar of borrowed thunder; one key to a house that burned; one apology, undelivered, still warm.* And then I showed them the right-hand column, the one written in the older hand, the one Mrs. Aldous's predecessor had kept and hers before that, going all the way back to the Accord itself.

*Paid for with:* and then a name, and then what the name had surrendered.

"The jar of thunder," I said. "Bought with a fisherman's memory of his brother's voice. He lives two streets from here. He thinks he never had a brother. There's a woman on Cordwainer Lane who sold her wedding day so the city could keep its fountains running. She passes the fountain every morning and wonders why she cries."

The hall was quiet. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged, and the sound went through the room like a shot.

"It's worse than that," I went on, because I had decided on the walk over that I would not soften it, that softening was how the guild had died in the first place. "It isn't fifty years of transactions. It's one transaction, renewed. Every entry feeds the veil. Every memory we buy makes the country harder to remember from outside and easier to forget from within. We are not a guild of impossible things. We are the maintenance crew of an erasure. We are the reason Vellmark isn't on any map."

Perpetua spoke first, which surprised no one. "Then why do we feel it?" she asked. Her voice was smaller than usual. "When a street goes missing overnight. When the twins—" she gestured at Sable, who was staring at the ledger "—when we notice the fog acting strange. If we're the ones maintaining it, why does it hurt us?"

"Because you can't hold the scissors without feeling the cutting," I said. "The Custodians built the veil. We keep it stocked. And neither of us gets to be numb."

Sable had gone very pale. She was holding the ledger open to a page near the end, and her finger was resting on an entry dated eleven years ago.

"This is my mother," she said.

Nobody moved.

"She sold—" Sable's voice caught and started again. "She sold the memory of my father. He didn't die. He walked out. And she couldn't bear remembering him, and there was a notice pinned to the guild door, the way there always is, offering fair trade for painful recollections, and she—" Sable looked up at me, and her face was the face of someone watching a building they loved collapse in slow motion. "She came here. To this table. And one of you took it."

"We all took it," Mrs. Aldous said quietly. "In turn. It was called rotation. It was called fairness." She took off her spectacles and cleaned them with a handkerchief, though they were clean. "I took the memory of a man's daughter. He'd lost her to fever and wanted the ache gone. I carried it for a week before it went into the veil. I can still tell you the color of her ribbon." Her hands were perfectly steady, which is how I knew how much it cost her. "Fairness," she repeated, with contempt she had clearly been saving up for decades.

Old Tomas laughed—a single, terrible syllable of a laugh.

"What?" Wren asked. She was the youngest of us and the bravest, which is often the same thing.

"I repaired broken vows," Tomas said. "Forty years. Wedding oaths, partnerships, treaties between neighbors. And half of them broke again, and I could never understand why, and I used to blame myself." He laid one scarred hand flat on the ledger. "You cannot stitch together what someone has been made to forget. Half those couples weren't falling out of love. They were falling out of *material*. And I kept sewing, and charging my little fee, and the guild took its cut, and somewhere underneath it all—" he tapped the ledger twice— "*this*. This is what we were sewing with."

Oren had not spoken. He sat at the head of the table with his hands around his teacup, and I watched him read the oldest pages—the ones in his own handwriting, from fifty years ago—and I watched him arrive, page by page, at himself.

"I signed this," he said.

"You signed it to save your brother," I said gently. "Ilse Marchetti found the record. The Custodians gave you a choice, and you chose him."

"That doesn't make it right."

"No. But it makes it human. There's a difference, and I intend to insist on it."

He looked up at me then, and his eyes were clear—clearer than they had been in months, clear the way the sky is clear just before it decides to do something dramatic. "Sebastian. What do you want?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the others. Not *how could we*, not *who is to blame*, but the guildmaster's question, the one the guild had been founded to ask four hundred years ago when some forgotten optimist first wrote *The Guild of Impossible Things* above a door.

What do you want?

* * *

I want you to understand what I almost said first, because it matters. I almost said: *I want to fix it.* I almost promised restoration, the old borders redrawn, the old country handed back whole, everyone's memories returned and every wound unmade. It would have been a lovely speech. I had most of it drafted somewhere below my ribs.

But three days on the Glass Steppe teaches you things. One of them is that the past is heavy in a way that crushes whoever tries to carry it alone. Another is that the Echoes—the folded citizens, the people we had been buying memories *from* without ever once asking them—do not want the old country back. They want a country. Any country. One with their names in it.

So instead of the speech I'd drafted, I said something else.

"I want to make one last trade."

Fenwick snorted. "With what? Look at us. Our inventory is a jar of thunder and a key to a house that burned down before Wren was born."

"With exactly that," I said. "Our entire inventory. Everything the guild has ever held in trust. All of it, poured into the Door Out at the anniversary hour, as payment—not to restore Vellmark as it was, but to found a new one. On the Steppe. Open to both bloodlines, both sides of the old border, and the Echoes. A country where no memory is ever traded away again. Where the guild does what it was always supposed to do, which is make impossible things *happen*, not disappear."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, mixed.

"A country," Mrs. Aldous said at last. "You're proposing to found a country. With seven guild members, a jar of thunder, and—" she checked her watch— "seventy-two hours."

"Yes."

"And the Custodians? They'll come. They always come. They'll bring soldiers."

"They'll bring Kessler," I said. "Yes. Ilse is holding the master schedule and buying us what time she can, and the Echoes will fight for it—they've been fighting for it for fifty years, they just haven't had anywhere to put the fighting. But yes. It will be dangerous, and possibly fatal, and there is a real chance that in four days' time none of us will exist in any ledger anywhere."

Perpetua and Sable looked at each other across the table—the twins, who finished each other's sentences and stole each other's pens and had never once, in thirty years of shared employment, been seen to agree about anything in under an hour.

"We're in," they said, simultaneously, and then immediately began arguing about which of them had agreed first.

"Tomas?" I asked.

The old vow-mender was looking out the window at the city—the flickering, forgetting, forgery of a city—and when he turned back his face was wet, and he did not trouble to hide it. "I spent forty years repairing things people were being prevented from keeping," he said. "One more job. The big one. Then I'm retiring."

"Fenwick?"

He was turning the burned house's key over in his fingers. "You know," he said, "I've opened every lock in this city. Vault doors, prison gates, hearts, allegedly. Never once opened a *door out*." He grinned, and it was the grin of a much younger man, or perhaps the same man finally honest. "I'd hate to die wondering."

"Mrs. Aldous?"

She put her spectacles back on and drew the master ledger toward her, squaring it against the table's edge with the precision of sixty years' habit. "Someone will need to keep the accounts," she said, "if we're going to commit the largest fraud in the history of commerce—trading a fiction for a country and calling it fair exchange." A pause. Her voice softened by one degree, which from Mrs. Aldous was a landslide. "My granddaughter lives in the capital. She thinks I write insurance filings. I should like her, someday, to know what I actually did."

"Wren?"

The youngest of us had tears running freely down her face and had, I noticed, already pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward herself. "I don't have any memories worth trading yet," she said. "I thought that made me useless. But if we're making a *new* country—" she looked up, and her chin set in a way I suspected we'd all be grateful for before the week was out— "then somebody needs to start writing things down that are worth keeping."

"And Master Voss," I said last. "Lucid as you are this morning—I need to ask you while I can. Because I may need you tomorrow, and tomorrow you may not know my face."

Oren looked around the table, at the seven of them, at the ledgers, at the dust motes turning gold in the light of lamps that had burned in this hall for four hundred years. When he spoke, his voice was the one from the old photographs, the guildmaster's voice, the one that had sworn oaths in this room before the war took most of the swearers.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," he said, "has one rule older than its charter: *that which cannot be done must be.*" He rose, and his knees cracked like the stairs, and he stood straighter than he had stood in years. "A country, Sebastian Winterbourne. You propose we spend everything we have to buy a country that has never existed."

"That's the proposal."

He smiled. It was, I thought, the first time I had ever seen the whole man inside the smile—no fog, no forgetting, just Oren Voss, apprentice of the old world and, if we lived, founder of the new.

"Well," he said. "Then we had better begin. Impossible things take preparation."

* * *

We worked through the night, and it was the strangest night of my life, and I include the night I learned my own name was written in a hundred-year-old book.

Mrs. Aldous began the great withdrawal—every item in the guild's inventory, called in from wherever it rested, catalogued for its final entry. The jar of thunder arrived by courier at midnight, humming faintly, eager. Fenwick went down into the cellar and stood before the living map of Vellmark with his hat in his hands, like a man visiting a grave, and came back up talking fast about hinges and thresholds and what a door needs besides a frame. The twins inventoried the weather: two storms, a fog, and a sunset we'd been holding in escrow since 1931, which Perpetua declared essential and Sable declared sentimental, and which they packed together without further argument, which told me everything about how the night was going.

Tomas sat apart, sharpening nothing, mending nothing, simply being present the way old men can be present, like furniture that loves you.

And Wren wrote. Page after page, in the new ledger she had ruled herself, recording not what was lost but what we meant to keep: the creak of the stairs in E-flat; Mrs. Aldous's way of saying *hmph* when proven right; the exact angle at which Oren tipped his head when an idea pleased him. A country, she seemed to understand before any of us, is mostly a list of small things that people have agreed to love in common.

Near dawn, Oren found me at the window, watching the city flicker through its false sunrise.

"Your grandmother's echo," he said. "On the Steppe. Did she truly offer to pay your price?"

"She did. And I refused. It would have cost her peace, and mercy that costs another isn't mercy—it's accounting."

"Hm." He settled beside me, hands clasped behind his back, and for a while we watched the sun pretend to rise over a country pretending to exist. "Your mother would have liked you," he said suddenly. "I knew her, you know. Before. She used to sneak into the archive to read the prophecy folios, and I let her, because she asked better questions than the apprentices."

My throat closed. "Master—"

"Ah—" He waved a hand, gentle, rueful. "Don't. I can feel the fog coming back, and I'd rather you remember this version of me asking for something." He turned, and his eyes were wet and clear at once. "Whatever happens at the Door, Sebastian. Whatever price it asks. Don't pay it alone. That's the request. That's the last thing I'll ever ask of anyone."

Before I could answer, the doors of the great hall slammed open hard enough to rattle the lamps.

Wren stood in the doorway, white-faced, a letter crushed in her fist, her chest heaving from running.

"It's from the Custodians," she said. "It's addressed to you, Sebastian. By name. And it's signed—"

Her voice failed, so she simply held it out, and I unfolded the heavy paper, and read the signature at the bottom, in ink so black it looked wet:

*Warden Kessler.*

And beneath it, in the body of the letter, words that stopped my heart mid-beat:

*We have your guildmaster's brother. Terms enclosed.*
