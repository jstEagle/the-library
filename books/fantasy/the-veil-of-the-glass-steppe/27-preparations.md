## 27. Preparations

The guild hall had never held so many people, which was to say it had never held more than four, and now there were nine.

Sebastian stood at the long table that had once been reserved for inventorying impossible things and watched the members of the Guild of Impossible Things inventory something considerably more difficult: themselves.

"Right," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We have three days. The Door opens on the anniversary of the Veiling and stays open for—Ilse, how long exactly?"

"Hours," Ilse said. She stood at the window with her arms crossed, silhouetted against a city whose clocks had begun stuttering in earnest. "Perhaps fewer. The schedule calls it 'a window.' Custodian documents are vague about windows because vagueness is easier to defend at tribunal."

"Wonderful," muttered Fenwick, the guild's oldest surviving member, who was ninety-one and had survived three guild purges by being too unremarkable to purge. He was sharpening a pencil with great concentration, as though the pencil were going to fight beside them. "So we're storming a country that doesn't exist through a door that's open for an afternoon."

"We're not storming anything," Sebastian said. "We're *opening* something. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire point."

Fenwick considered this, then went back to his pencil.

Around the table sat the rest of them: Marta Oduya, who kept the guild's correspondence and could write in eleven hands, seven of them forged; the twins Piotr and Anka Sorel, who between them remembered every corridor of the headquarters better than the corridors did; old Bramwell, who had joined the guild forty years ago for the pension and stayed for reasons he'd never articulated; young Corin Ash, who was nineteen and had been an apprentice for six months and therefore believed impossible things happened every day; and Sister Yeva, who had been a nun before she'd been a ledger-keeper and brought to all meetings the calm of someone who had already made peace with worse.

Nine people. One seal. Three days. A country.

"First order of business," Sebastian said, unrolling the living map across the table. It breathed under her hands—the warm roads pulsing faintly, the Glass Steppe at its center glowing like a lamp behind frosted glass. "The map needs power. It runs on memory; it always has. That's what the ledgers were for." She said it plainly now. They had all read Chapter Seven of their own history two nights ago, and the horror of it had settled into something harder: purpose. "The route from here to the Door crosses terrain that reshapes itself. The map can show us where the glass will shift—if we feed it enough."

"How much is enough?" Marta asked.

Sebastian hesitated.

"A life," Ilse said quietly. Everyone looked at her. She didn't flinch. "That's what the veil eats. That's the exchange rate. A significant memory per mile of clarity."

The silence that followed had texture to it. Sebastian watched each face around the table do arithmetic with their own pasts.

Then Sister Yeva folded her hands and said, "I'll do it."

"No—" Sebastian started.

"I am seventy-three years old," Yeva said, with the mild firmness of a woman used to being obeyed in matters of the soul. "My memories are mostly of other people's confessions, which I am professionally obligated to forget anyway. But there is one I've kept." Her eyes went somewhere far away and came back. "My wedding day. Forty-eight years ago. I married a man named Tomas in a chapel with a leaking roof, and it rained the whole ceremony, and everyone danced in the puddles afterward. It rained, and nobody minded." She smiled. "It is the single happiest day of my life, which is precisely why it should be spent. Happiness hoarded does no one any good. I've thought about this longer than you've been alive, child."

"You'd lose it entirely," Sebastian said. "You wouldn't know you'd ever been married."

"I would know I'm married to a cause worth my wedding," Yeva said. "And if we succeed, perhaps someone will tell me about it someday, and I'll get to hear it fresh." She leaned forward and pressed her palm flat against the edge of the map. "Tomas would have liked this. He always said I was wasted on quiet."

The map drank. Where her hand touched it, color bloomed—the roads around the Steppe sharpening from suggestion into certainty, rivers finding their courses, the first three miles of glass resolving into readable terrain. Yeva swayed slightly. Piotr caught her elbow. She waved him off, then frowned faintly, as if searching for a word she'd misplaced, then let it go with visible grace.

"There," she said. "Three miles bought. Someone else had better be sitting on something good."

"I'll give it my stamp collection," said Bramwell.

"That's not how it works," Sebastian began—

"It absolutely should," said Corin.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and it was strange, Sebastian thought, how quickly dread becomes furniture. By the fourth hour they were arguing over logistics the way any family argues over seating at dinner.

"The decoy problem," Ilse said, standing at the head of the table now, in her element. She had pinned up a rough sketch of the Steppe's approaches, drawn from Custodian patrol schedules she'd memorized during eight years of service. "Kessler knows the Door's timetable as well as we do. He'll expect movement along the eastern causeway—it's the only approach that doesn't cross open glass. So he'll hold it."

"So we go west," Piotr said.

"So he expects you to think that, and holds the west," Anka countered.

"So we need him looking nowhere," Ilse said. "Which means we need someone being loudly, unmistakably somewhere else. A decoy. Someone convincing enough to pull a retrieval squad off our path for at least six hours." Her eyes moved around the table and Sebastian saw her doing the same terrible arithmetic everyone else was—not just *who*, but *who could afford to lose what it might cost*.

Bramwell raised his hand.

Everyone stared at him. In forty years, Bramwell had never once raised his hand.

"Bramwell," Sebastian said carefully, "you don't have to—"

"I know exactly what I'm offering," Bramwell said, and for the first time since Sebastian had known him, he sounded like a man rather than a shrug. "I'm sixty-eight years old. No family. No remarkable memories—I checked, twice, last night, lying awake going through everything like a man taking inventory before moving house." He tapped the table. "And here's the thing none of you want to say: I'm also the most boring man in this room. Kessler's people will look at me and see exactly what I am. Which means when I walk into the eastern customs house tomorrow night carrying a forged transfer order in Marta's finest hand, claiming the guild seal is being moved east under escort—they will believe me, because I am constitutionally incapable of lying, and liars are the only people anyone believes."

"He's right," Marta said reluctantly. "I've tried to teach him forgery for twenty years. He's hopeless. His honesty is load-bearing."

"And if they detain you?" Sebastian asked.

Bramwell shrugged—the old shrug, but warmer now. "Then they detain a boring old man with nothing to hide, which is the safest thing in the world to be. And while they're filling out paperwork about me, you're crossing the glass." He looked around the table, almost shy. "Forty years I took the pension and did the filing. Let me be useful at the end. It's not a sacrifice if it's what you wanted all along and were too modest to ask for."

Sister Yeva reached over and squeezed his hand. Bramwell went pink to the ears.

"Motion carries," Sebastian said, before her voice could betray her.

Corin raised his hand next. "Can I do something?"

"You can stay here," Sebastian and Ilse said simultaneously.

"That's not fair, I'm nineteen, I'm practically indestructible—"

"You're nineteen," Ilse said, "which means your whole life is ahead of you, which means it is the one thing at this table we cannot afford to spend. Guard the headquarters. Guard the ledgers. If we fail, someone has to remember we tried." She softened. "That's not a small job, Corin. That may be the biggest one."

Corin subsided, mollified in the way of the young when handed a burden dressed as an honor. But later, passing through the hall, Sebastian found him copying the ledgers by candlelight, all of them, in his careful apprentice's hand, and understood he had accepted after all.

* * *

Night came down over the headquarters like a held breath. In the cellar, by lamplight, Sebastian and Ilse bent over the living map together, planning the crossing inch by inch, and discovered that planning a route across shifting glass with the woman you loved was either the worst or best way to spend possibly your last evening, and was certainly both.

"Here." Ilse set two fingers on the western margin. "The salt flats. Kessler's maps show them as solid ground. They aren't anymore—the Steppe has been eating outward for years. The glass under the salt reflects whatever the walker fears most. First squad they sent in, thirty years ago, came back refusing to describe it. Two resigned."

"What do you fear?" Sebastian asked, before she could stop herself.

Ilse was quiet a moment. "That this is surveillance," she said finally, wry. "Old habit. Asking questions to build a file." Then, softer: "Losing you before I've had you. That's the honest answer. Put it on the map and watch it squirm."

Sebastian laughed despite everything, and Ilse smiled—that rare, unguarded smile—and for a moment the cellar was just a warm room with two people in it, and the country waiting outside might have been any country.

"Route says here," Sebastian managed, tapping the map. "We skirt the flats north, follow the old pilgrim road—the Echoes can sing us the parts the glass moves. Yeva's memory bought us the first three miles clearly. After that—" She exhaled. "After that we navigate by trust."

"Trust of whom?"

"The Echoes. My grandmother." Sebastian traced the glowing center of the Steppe, where the Door waited like a held note. "Ilse, when we get there—the price. You know what it asks."

Ilse straightened. In the lamplight her face was very still. "I know what the files say it asks."

"I won't let you pay it. Whatever you're thinking—whatever bargain you're drafting in that Custodian brain of yours—no. The Door takes one payment. Mine. That was decided before you arrived and it isn't negotiable."

"You can't decide that alone."

"I can. I'm the one it wants. Born of both sides of the border—that's the whole prophecy, that's why my name is in a book older than I am. It doesn't want your memories, Ilse. It wants mine, and specifically the ones I haven't lived yet." Sebastian heard her own voice flatten and made herself look up. "So here's what I want instead. When I cross, I want to know someone's keeping the books. Real ones. Not cages—records. What we keep. Who we loved. Starting with—" her voice did betray her, just slightly— "starting with you."

Ilse looked at her for a long time. Somewhere overhead, the city's clocks stuttered midnight, or tried to.

"All right," Ilse said at last, roughly. "But I'm writing it in ink. None of that pencil nonsense Fenwick loves."

"Fenwick's pencil has served this guild honorably for sixty years."

"Fenwick's pencil," Ilse said, "is the only weapon in this building, and I intend to requisition it."

They were still arguing pleasantly about it when they climbed back into the main hall and stopped.

The others were gathered at the window. Not alarmed—gathered, the way people gather around a hearth. Outside, the veiled city flickered at its edges, streets remembering and forgetting themselves in slow pulses, and above it all the sky had gone strange and clear, stars coming out over districts that hadn't seen stars in fifty years because the veil couldn't quite manage sky.

"It's starting," Yeva said softly. "Tomorrow's anniversary. The whole country feels it, even the parts that don't remember there's a country." She turned to Sebastian, and her face was serene and slightly unfamiliar without the wedding in it, and no less kind for the loss. "Whatever happens on the glass—we're with you. All of us. Even Bramwell, especially Bramwell."

"Especially me," Bramwell agreed. "I've rehearsed being detained. I'm very good at it."

Laughter moved around the room, thin but real, the sound of frightened people choosing to be brave in shifts.

And then the front door of the Guild of Impossible Things creaked open, though no one had touched it, and a cold wind came down the cellar stairs from a direction the cellar didn't have, and every lamp in the building guttered at once except the light rising from the map below—which had begun, all on its own, to glow brighter, and to pulse, in rhythm, like a heartbeat quickening.

Sebastian looked at Ilse. Ilse looked at the stairs.

"That's not scheduled," Ilse said.

Down in the dark, the map of Vellmark was calling them early.
