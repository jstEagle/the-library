## 28. The Anniversary Eve

The clocks began to stutter at dawn.

Not all of them, and not in any way you could prove to a magistrate. The clock over the baker's door hesitated between the seven and the eight, the way a person hesitates on a threshold, as if it had forgotten which room it was walking into. The cathedral bells rang noon four minutes early and then, embarrassed, rang a small apologetic fifth chime that no one had ever heard them ring before. In the guild kitchen, the old regulator with the cracked pendulum simply stopped at 6:12 and refused to be argued with.

"Anniversary eve," said Marta, the guild's bookbinder, watching it with her arms crossed. "It did this last year too."

"It did?"

"No." She frowned. "I thought it did. I remember thinking it did. That might be the same thing."

That was the trouble with the eve of the Veiling, I was learning. Memory went soft at the edges, like bread left out overnight. Not broken — softened. You could still eat it, but it didn't hold its shape, and if you weren't careful, yesterday tasted suspiciously like fifty years ago.

I had three days. Ilse had said so with her hand pressed to her bandaged shoulder and her voice stripped down to the bare wires: *The Door can be held open only during the anniversary. Three days, Sebastian.* And now the first of those days was ending, and the whole city could feel it coming the way animals feel weather — not knowing what it was, only that something enormous had turned over in its sleep.

* * *

I went out at dusk because I couldn't stand the ledgers another hour, and because — though I would not have phrased it this way even to Ilse, especially not to Ilse — I wanted to memorize my neighborhood.

In case I lost it.

The plan we'd built in the guild hall was a good plan, or as good as plans get when they involve walking across a plain made of other people's memories while a Custodian warden hunts you with an army. But every good plan had a shadow-plan folded inside it like a letter in an envelope, and mine read: *if this goes wrong, if the price is higher than we think, if I have to give up more than I've promised myself I will — then let me at least know what I'm giving up. Let me hold it properly first.*

You cannot grieve for something you never bothered to look at. I intended to look.

So: Lamplighter's Row, where my lodgings were, above the chandler's shop with the crooked green awning. The awning had been crooked for as long as I'd lived there, sagging on the left where the bracket rusted through, and Herr Brandt the chandler refused to fix it because, he claimed, customers could find his shop by the crookedness alone. *"Turn left at the honest crooked awning,"* he liked to say. Tonight he was sweeping wax drippings off his step and he looked up at me and said, "Evening, miss," and then, before I'd passed him entirely, added — quietly, puzzled at himself — "Sebastian. Your name is Sebastian, isn't it? Funny. I've known you six years and tonight it came back to me all at once, like a sneeze."

"Good evening, Herr Brandt."

"Funny," he said again, and went on sweeping, and I walked on with my throat tight, because that was the eve working. Fifty years ago, someone had paid this street's memory away — or the veil had taken it as toll — and now, with the anniversary turning beneath us like a key in a lock, things were leaking back through. Names. Small kindnesses. The particular way a neighbor takes his tea.

Strangers were greeting each other with half-remembered names all along the Row. I saw the fishmonger's wife stop dead in front of the tailor and say, "Aldous?" with such naked hope that the tailor — who had been Aldous once, apparently, before everyone agreed without discussing it that he was Mr. Pell — stopped dead too, and put his hand flat against his own chest as though steadying something inside it.

"Pell," he said. But gently. Like a man correcting a child about Santa Claus, sorry to do it.

"Aldous," she insisted, and then both of them laughed, uncertainly, and stood there a moment longer than strangers ought to, and I understood I was watching two people try on a country the way you try on a coat found in an attic: *this fits better than anything I own, but whose is it?*

* * *

At the corner by the pump, children were playing a game none of them had been taught.

I know how that sounds. I want to be precise about it, because precision is the only gift the ledgers ever gave me. The game involved one child standing blindfolded in the middle while the others circled her singing — singing, actual words, in a language I recognized from the deep archive as Old Vellmarkish, the border tongue, the language of the country that officially did not exist. The words meant, roughly, *the door remembers, the door remembers,* though the children didn't know that. They knew the tune the way you know a tune you heard before you were born.

When the song ended, the blindfolded girl pointed at random, and whoever she pointed at became the next door.

I stood there longer than was dignified. A woman beside me — Mrs. Ostrava, third floor, who had never once in five years spoken to me beyond nodding — said, without looking away from the game, "My grandmother used to sing that."

"Your grandmother sang Vellmarkish?"

"She wasn't my grandmother." Mrs. Ostrava frowned at her own sentence, turned it over, tried again. "She was. She is. I don't—" She laughed, short and unhappy. "Listen to me. I mean, I remember a grandmother who sang it. Whether she was mine or somebody else's, tonight I couldn't swear."

"That's the eve," I said. "Things come loose."

"Is that what it is?" She finally looked at me, and there was no fear in her face, which surprised me; there was something more like homesickness pointed at nothing. "Because I'll tell you what's strange, Miss Winterbourne. All day I've felt like the night before a name day. You know that feeling? When you're a child and tomorrow's your feast day and you can't sleep, and you don't even want the presents particularly, you just want the morning to come because the morning is *yours?*" She shook her head slowly. "I feel like that. At my age. About tomorrow. And I don't know why, and I don't know what's tomorrow, except the baker says his flour delivery is late, which hardly explains it."

"The morning is yours," I said. It was all I could safely give her. "Whatever else happens, Mrs. Ostrava, the morning is yours."

She nodded as if this were a perfectly ordinary thing to say, thanked me for the nodding-at over five years, and went inside, and I stood at the pump listening to children sing a dead language alive, and I confess I cried a little, standing up, in public, like a fountain with feelings. Nobody minded. It was that kind of evening. Everyone's eyes were already wet about something they couldn't name.

* * *

By full dark the stuttering had spread from clocks to lamplight. The gas lamps along the Row would flare, dim, flare — breathing, almost. Down at the tram terminus the destination board flickered between its proper routes and, twice, briefly, a single word in old script that the conductor swore he'd painted over himself fifteen years ago. I didn't go close enough to read it. I knew what it would say. Everything in this city was quietly, politely trying to remember itself, and I was the one person carrying the knowledge of why, and the weight of that is a strange weight: lighter than grief and harder to set down.

I did my rounds the way Oren taught me to do inventory — methodically, affectionately, item by item.

The crooked green awning. *Held.* Herr Brandt's step, worn concave by forty years of boots. *Held.* The pump handle's squeak, a rising minor third that every child on the street could whistle. *Held.* The cat — the enormous gray tom who belonged to no one and everyone, whom different households called by four different names and fed accordingly — sat on the wall outside my window and watched me take mental notes like a small furry auditor.

"What," I asked him, "would you trade for a country?"

He blinked. Cats are the only creatures in Vellmark who were never fooled by the veil; they simply declined to participate, which is why they always stare slightly past you, at the version of the street they prefer. He stared past me now, at some earlier evening, tail curled around his feet like a comma in a sentence that hadn't ended.

I went in. I lit the lamp. I took out paper — real paper, guild stock, the good rag paper we used for records that mattered — and I began to write.

Not a ledger entry. Something worse and better. An inventory of the ordinary.

*Lamplighter's Row, this evening, the last eve of the old counting:* I wrote, and under it:

*The chandler's awning leans left and must never be mended.*
*The pump sings E-flat when it means to sing E.*
*Mrs. Ostrava, 3rd floor, geraniums, keeps them too wet out of kindness.*
*The cat answers to everything and belongs to the street.*
*Bread smell begins at 4 a.m., reaches my window by 5, forgives everything by 6.*
*Someone practices violin badly two doors down, Tuesdays, and it is the most human sound in the world and I would fight anyone who tried to improve it.*

On and on. My hand cramped. I kept writing. Because here is what I knew that the city only felt in its bones: tomorrow — or the day after — I would walk onto the Glass Steppe, and the Door Out would ask its price, and the price might be exactly this. Not the dramatic things, not the prophecy and the seal and the war. The *ordinary*. Bread smell at five. Geraniums watered too generously. A badly practiced violin. Countries are not made of treaties; they are made of Tuesdays, and if I was going to lose my Tuesdays, then by every god the guild had ever failed to believe in, I was going to lose them *knowingly*, with my eyes open, having loved each one by name first.

That is the whole secret of the impossible trades, if you want it. You cannot pay with what you haven't held.

So I held. I wrote until midnight, and then I did the truly unforgivable thing: I went back out and walked the streets again, slower, touching things. Railings. Doorposts. The cold iron lip of the pump. The baker's boy delivering the first loaves gave me one, hot, wrapped in paper, refusing payment because "you look like a woman owed a loaf," which is either charity or prophecy, and on the eve of the Veiling I chose not to investigate which.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when the feeling arrived — the one I'd been waiting for, dreading, rehearsing.

It came as a kind of pressure behind the eyes, and then as sound: very far away, under the ground, a long groan like ice shifting on a lake. Every lamp on the Row guttered at once. The cat stood up, arched, and stared hard at a point in the empty air about four feet above the cobbles, and for one heartbeat — just one — I saw what he saw: the street doubled, translucent, older. Cobbles laid differently. A customs post where the pump stood. Lanterns of a make I'd only seen in the archive drawings, and people — faint as breath on glass — walking through each other's evenings.

Then it closed. The lamps steadied. The present reasserted itself, slightly rumpled, like a man straightening his coat after a gust.

And in that heartbeat, in that doubled street, I had seen a figure looking back at me. Faint. Familiar. A woman with my jaw and my mother's stance, standing at the ghost-customs-post with a travel case in her hand, and she had mouthed something — two words, maybe three — before fifty years sealed over again like water over a stone.

I couldn't hear her. But I knew, the way you know a tune before you were born, the way children circle a blindfolded girl singing a language nobody taught them.

I knew what she'd said. Grandmothers say it across any distance, through any veil, over any number of years.

*Come home.*

I stood in the middle of Lamplighter's Row with a warm loaf going cold in my hands, and above me every window on the street glowed with people who couldn't sleep either, and somewhere across the sleeping city Ilse was keeping watch over a stolen schedule and a wounded shoulder, and in three days the Door would open, and it would ask, and I would answer.

But first — one more thing, and it was this that sent me home uneasy, that kept me awake till the bread smell rose at four:

As I turned up my stairs, I passed the guild's night messenger coming down, pale, out of breath, carrying a note for Master Fenwick. Our Fenwick — steady, kind, gray-templed Fenwick, who had volunteered for the decoy route without hesitation and hugged me afterward like a brother. The messenger wouldn't meet my eye. The note was sealed with wax I didn't recognize, and when I asked who sent it, the boy said, "I don't know, ma'am. A man met me at the bridge. Paid double not to look at his face."

Fenwick's window, when I glanced up at the guild hall from the corner, was dark.

But the candle stub beside it was still smoking.
