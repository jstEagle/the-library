## 30. Flight Across the Steppe

### Sebastian

The betrayal caught up to them before the soldiers did.

Sebastian had known something was wrong the moment the map in her hands went cold. For two days it had been warm as a sleeping animal, humming against her palms, but when she'd unrolled it at dawn on the third morning—the morning of the anniversary itself—it lay inert as any dead thing. The route they had planned, the winding path through the shifting glass toward the Door, had simply stopped being true. A salt flat that should have taken a day's walk now stood three miles east of where the map insisted.

"He told them," she said.

Ilse didn't ask who. She looked at the horizon for a long moment, at the thin gray line of Custodian lanterns moving through the fog beyond the Steppe's edge, and said only, "How long do you think we have?"

"An hour. Maybe less."

"Then let's not waste it standing here admiring their punctuality."

They ran.

The Glass Steppe did not forgive running. It was a plain of translucent stone that stretched to every horizon, and beneath its surface—not reflected in it, *contained* in it—lay everything the veil had swallowed. As Sebastian sprinted across it, her boots striking sparks of pale light, she ran over fields she remembered and fields she didn't, over a wedding party frozen mid-toast, over rain that had fallen fifty years ago and never stopped falling down there in the dark. Her reflection was everywhere and belonged to no one. Sometimes it was a child. Sometimes it was an old woman. Once—only once—it was her mother's face, and Sebastian stumbled so badly that Ilse caught her arm without breaking stride.

"Don't look down," Ilse said.

"It's looking up."

"Then don't look up either." There was a breathless half-laugh in it despite everything, the particular gallows warmth Ilse carried like contraband. "Honestly, Winterbourne, you cataloged a jar of borrowed thunder without flinching. You can manage a little existential weather."

Behind them, the fog split. Lanterns became torches; torches became men. Kessler's retrieval squad moved across the glass in formation, and where their boots struck, the memories underfoot dimmed, as though the glass itself were flinching from their touch.

"They're herding us," Sebastian panted. "The route's wrong. They know where we have to go."

"Then we go somewhere else."

"There is nowhere else. The Door is the Door."

Ilse glanced back over her shoulder, and what Sebastian saw in her face then wasn't fear. It was calculation—the old customs-house arithmetic, weighing cargo against time against lives—and it frightened Sebastian more than the soldiers did, because she recognized it as the look of someone preparing to pay.

* * *

### Ilse

Ilse had run from Custodians exactly once before, eleven years ago, and that time she'd been carrying nothing but a stolen pear and a guilty conscience. This time she carried the veil's master schedule sewn into the lining of her coat, a woman she loved three strides ahead of her, and the growing certainty that she was going to die on this beautiful, terrible plain.

She found she minded less than expected. What she minded was the schedule. If it burned with her, the Door would close unopened, and everything—all of it, the grandmother-echoes, the ledger-truths, the whole folded country—would gutter out like a candle pinched between Kessler's fingers.

So: arithmetic.

"Singing," she called ahead. "Do you hear singing?"

Sebastian slowed, head cocked. And yes—there it was, threading through the wind. Voices. Dozens of them, hundreds, rising not from the horizon but from *below*, from under the glass, muffled as music heard through a floor. An old marching song, the kind schoolchildren sang in Vellmark before there was anything to march about. Ilse knew it. Of course she knew it; the Custodians made memorizing erased songs part of training, the way surgeons memorize anatomy.

"The Echoes," Sebastian breathed. "They're—they're showing us the border."

Because that was the thing about the song: it wasn't one song. It was a round, each voice entering where another ended, and the entrances marked the old boundaries. North province here. The river provinces there. The disputed marches where Sebastian's parents had been born on opposite sides of a line that no longer officially existed. Underneath them, the dead-and-folded country was singing itself back into shape, and the glass was listening.

The Steppe began to change.

It happened slowly at first, then all at once—a shudder through the plain, like ice deciding whether to hold or break. Ridges rose where none had been, walls of clear stone thirty feet high, curving and branching. Within moments they were running through a labyrinth that was assembling itself around them, and Ilse realized with a lurch that it was assembling *deliberately*: the Echoes were building corridors, closing passages, steering.

"Left!" Sebastian shouted, because she could hear the song better than Ilse could—of course she could; she belonged to both sides of it—and Ilse cut left, shoulder grazing a wall that hummed faintly against her coat like a struck bell.

Behind them, the first squad hit the maze and stopped being a threat and started being a noise. The glass reshaped behind them too, sealing routes, and somewhere in the fog of it Ilse heard shouting, and once, horribly, a man screaming in a voice full of unbearable happiness—caught, she understood, in a loop of some remembered festival day, trapped by weaponized nostalgia while his comrades tried to drag him free by the ankles.

"Are they—" Sebastian began.

"Some of them will walk out in an hour thinking they've been to the loveliest parade of their lives." Ilse didn't soften it. She'd decided years ago she wouldn't lie to this woman even to comfort her. "Some of them won't walk out at all. That's Kessler's doing, not yours."

"I know." A pause, breath ragged between words. "I just wanted to be sure you knew I know."

God, Ilse thought. Even now. Even running for her life, the woman needed to be *precise*. It was the most infuriating and most dear thing about her.

* * *

### Sebastian

The maze led them wrong twice on purpose, which took Sebastian a while to understand.

The second time, they emerged onto an open shelf of glass overlooking a valley that did not exist—could not exist, because valleys required weather and the Steppe permitted none—and in the valley stood a farmhouse with smoke rising from its chimney. A woman waved from the doorway. She wore an apron Sebastian's hands remembered tying, though Sebastian herself remembered nothing.

"Grandmother," she whispered.

"She can't hear you," Ilse said gently. "Or she can, but it doesn't work the way—"

"I know how echoes work, Ilse."

But she raised her hand anyway, and the echo-woman raised hers, and for three seconds—which is a very long time when you are being hunted—nothing else in the world existed except a wave exchanged across a valley made of memory.

Then the song changed key, urgent now, and the valley collapsed into mist, and the glass tilted beneath their feet and *pushed*, actually pushed, a slope rising behind them like a wave deciding to become land. They ran downhill along a corridor of singing walls, and Sebastian finally grasped what the Echoes were telling her: the maze wasn't random. It was the old border, rendered in stone. Every turn followed a boundary line. They were running through Vellmark's skeleton, and the skeleton was trying to carry them home—or to the Door, which amounted to the same thing.

The corridor narrowed. The walls closed in until they ran single file, close enough to touch both sides at once, and the singing was so loud here that Sebastian felt it in her teeth, her ribs, the backs of her eyes.

Then the corridor ended.

It opened onto a wide clearing of flawless glass, and standing in the middle of it, alone, unhurried, was Warden Kessler.

He had come through the maze faster than he should have been able. Later, Sebastian would understand that the Echoes hadn't stopped him because he carried no memories worth trapping—he had traded them away decades ago, all of them, in exchange for certainty—and that a man with nothing inside him casts no shadow on a plain made of memory. But in that moment she only felt the temperature of the place drop, the way a room drops when someone important enters, and the song faltered, voices dropping away note by note like birds going quiet before a storm.

"Apprentice Winterbourne." His voice was mild. He sounded tired more than menacing, a bureaucrat at the end of a long quarter. "And Marchetti. Your file has grown quite thick, I'm afraid."

"Thank you," Ilse said. "I worked hard on it."

Something almost like amusement crossed Kessler's face. Then he lifted his hand, and six shapes detached from the fog behind him—Custodians, blades drawn, blades that shimmered with that wrong silver light Sebastian had seen once in the archive, the light that could cut memory as cleanly as flesh.

"Give me the seal," Kessler said, "and I will let the guild live. Give me the schedule, Marchetti, and I will let your treason be classified as a clerical error. Everyone in this transaction keeps their future." He smiled thinly. "I'm told that's rare, these days."

Sebastian's hand found the founding seal in her pocket, warm against her palm, warmer than the map had ever been.

"No," she said.

Kessler sighed, genuinely, deeply sighed, the sigh of a man whose paperwork had gotten out of hand. "Take the schedule," he told the Custodians. "Leave the apprentice breathing. She's more useful intact."

What happened next happened fast, the way catastrophes do—no grand slow-motion, no time to think, just bodies and decisions arriving simultaneously.

The nearest Custodian lunged for Ilse. Ilse, who had spent eleven years learning exactly how Custodians moved because she had trained beside every one of them, sidestepped beautifully—perfectly—one quarter-turn too elegant, and Sebastian saw the problem a half-second before it resolved: the sidestep put Ilse directly between the second blade and Sebastian.

"Down!" Ilse shouted, and shoved her.

Sebastian went down hard on the glass, hard enough to crack her elbow, and above her there was a sound she would spend the rest of her life hearing: not a scream, not exactly. A short, shocked exhale, like someone sitting down heavily after a long day.

When she turned, Ilse was on one knee, one hand pressed to her side, and between her fingers the blood came bright and steady, shockingly red on all that colorless glass. The Custodian stood over her, blade drawn back for a second stroke.

Sebastian had catalogued a jar of borrowed thunder. She knew, in the technical sense, exactly what thunder was for.

She took it out of her coat—she had carried it for weeks without knowing why, the way the guild's inventory always seemed to choose its own custodians—and she opened it.

The sound that came out was not thunder. It was older and stranger than that: it was every storm the veil had ever confiscated from Vellmark, all at once, given permission after fifty years of polite captivity. The glass plain lit white. Kessler's Custodians were thrown like dolls. The Echoes' song surged up through the floor with something that could only be called joy, and the walls of the border-maze rang like ten thousand bells, and Sebastian knelt in the center of it with her hair standing on end and the empty jar ringing faintly in her fist.

When she could see again, she crawled to Ilse.

"Hey," she said. "Hey. Look at me. You absolute idiot, you perfect, impossible—"

"Careful," Ilse managed. Her face was gray. Her smile was not. "You'll make me blush. Blood loss makes me vain."

"We're nearly there. The Door—it's close, I can feel it, the glass is warm—" It was true; the plain under her knees had begun to pulse with heat, gentle as a heartbeat. Ahead, through the settling haze, a seam of light had appeared in the distance, thin and gold and impossibly patient. "We just have to get you there. Can you stand?"

Ilse tried. Failed. Tried again, and got one foot under her, leaning all her weight into Sebastian's shoulder.

"Schedule's still dry," she murmured. "Sewn lining. Told you I plan for contingencies."

"You planned for getting stabbed?"

"I planned for *everything*. Getting stabbed was merely... likely."

Sebastian laughed, and the laugh came out wet and broken, and she didn't care. She got her arm around Ilse's waist, and together—three-legged, staggering, ridiculous—they began to move toward the light, while behind them Kessler rose slowly to his feet in the wreckage of his formation, and his face, for the first time, held something other than mild bureaucratic patience.

Ahead, the Door waited. Behind, the warden gathered what remained of his men, and his voice carried across the glass, calm as a ledger entry:

"Whatever it costs. Bring me the door."

Sebastian tightened her grip on Ilse and ran.
